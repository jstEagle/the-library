## 4. Roads Out of the Vale

Mother Ilsen died smiling, which was more than anyone could say for the rest of us.

By dawn the snow had stopped, and by mid-morning the word *riders* had started moving through Frostfall the way water moves through cloth—not announced, just everywhere at once, seeping into every conversation and staining it. The miller's boy had seen them on the high road: six horses, royal blue cloaks, the ash-grey livery of the king's sorcerous corps. They were a day out. Maybe less, if the roads held.

A day. Two hundred years of silence, one dead priest, one impossible child, and the crown gave me a day to decide what to do about all of it.

I decided with my hands, because that's how tasters decide everything. My hands packed a satchel while the rest of me stood in the middle of my kitchen feeling like a woman who has just been told the floor she's stood on her whole life was painted onto open air.

Dried meat. Hard cheese. The little tin of antidotes I'd compounded myself—willowbark and charcoal and a dozen things whose names would make an apothecary weep. My mother's knife. The letter from the capital, because it seemed suddenly important not to leave evidence of who I was lying around for strangers to read. And then, after a long moment, Mother Ilsen's notes, wrapped in oilcloth against the wet. She'd pressed them into my hands before she died, along with the name of a man she called "a liar, a fraud, and the only cartographer worth the title." Osk, of somewhere. A mapmaker of places that didn't exist.

Well. I knew something about those now.

The child sat on my hearthstone, watching me pack with the terrible patience of the very young and the very old. She hadn't spoken since last night—not since that single word, *Kavanagh*, spoken in a dead language by a living mouth, the word that had made my scars ache and my fire burn green and my entire understanding of myself collapse like a badly built arch. She just watched. Her eyes were dark and still as wells, and when I looked at her too long I got the vertigo again, the falling feeling, the sense that I was looking at something the world had agreed to forget and she had refused.

I needed to call her something. You can't run for your life alongside someone you're thinking of as "the child" or, worse, "it."

She'd come to me on the wind, small and wild and out of place in the warm. There's a bird for that. It nests in the eaves of the taster's hall back home—or what I'd thought was home—and it sings at night in winter when every other sensible creature has gone quiet. Small brown thing, unremarkable until it opens its beak, and then you wonder how so much sound fits inside so much bird.

"Wren," I said aloud, testing it.

Her head tilted. Not recognition exactly. Consideration. As if she were tasting the name the way I tasted soup, holding it on the tongue, checking for bitterness.

"Wren," I said again. "It's yours if you want it. Names are like that. Nobody can really give them; they can only offer."

She considered a moment longer, and then she nodded once, gravely, the way a queen might accept tribute she already owned. And that was somehow more unsettling than everything else, which I would not have believed possible.

* * *

We left through the kitchen garden, because the front door felt like a stage exit and I've never trusted stages. The snow came up past our ankles in the lane, blue in the shadow of the houses, blinding where the sun hit it. Frostfall was doing its morning business—smoke from chimneys, the smith's hammer going like a slow heartbeat, old Marta beating her rugs with the rhythm of a woman settling scores—and none of them knew that magic had spent the night in my kitchen drinking tea.

Or maybe they did. Maybe that's why nobody waved goodbye.

I'd lived in the Vale of Ashenmoor my whole life, all twenty-six years of it, and I had never once thought about its edges. That sounds stupid written down, but there it is. The vale is the world. Beyond the passes there are other vales, other towns, the capital with its gilded rot, but the vale itself simply *is*, the way weather is, the way hunger is. Magic cannot enter it. Everyone knows this the way everyone knows stone sinks. It's not a border; it's a fact.

Facts, I was learning, have edges too.

The eastern pass out of Frostfall climbs through pine and scree for most of a day before it tops the ridge where the valley finally exhales. I'd walked partway up twice in my life—to gather mountain sage that wouldn't grow lower, to bury my mother's ashes where she asked—and both times I'd turned back well short of the top because the air got strange up there, thin and humming, and I'd told myself it was altitude.

It was not altitude.

Wren rode on my hip like she weighed nothing, which she increasingly did not, and I climbed with my satchel banging against my back and my breath coming out in white flags of surrender. Halfway up, I heard the bells of Frostfall change pattern—the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one—which meant someone had talked, or someone had searched my cottage, or the riders had simply arrived ahead of the rumor. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that the sound carried up the valley with horrible clarity, and Wren's arms tightened around my neck, and we ran.

Not gracefully. I'm a food-taster, not a soldier; my training consists of swallowing things and dying slowly enough to warn others. But terror does wonders for the cardiovasculature, and I scrambled up that mountainside like a goat with a grudge, lungs burning, boots slipping on iced scree, the child clinging and silent and lighter with every step in a way I refused to think about yet.

The ridge came sooner than it should have. One moment the pines were thick around me and the slope was still climbing, and the next the trees simply stopped—stopped as if drawn, in a line too straight for nature—and beyond them the land fell away into a country I had never seen, rolling brown and gold toward a horizon with actual distance in it, real distance, the kind that makes your eyes water.

And the sound.

That's the only way I can describe it. Sound—but nothing was making any. The world beyond the tree-line was *loud*. Loud with nothing audible. I stood at the edge of the pines with my ears ringing and my skin prickling and every hair on my body standing up to attention, and I understood, in the bone-deep way you understand a poison rather than merely identify it, what I was feeling.

It was the vale. Or rather, it was the absence of the vale. All my life I had lived inside a silence so total I'd mistaken it for quiet. And now, standing one step from the boundary, I could feel the noise of the world pressing against that silence like a sea against glass—a vast, layered, singing pressure, the hum of everything alive and enchanted and other, all the music that had never been allowed through. Magic wasn't entering the vale. Magic was everywhere else, all the time, and the vale was the deaf spot, the dead note, the hole in the song.

I had grown up inside the hole and called it the whole world.

"Saints' teeth," I said, or something equally dignified.

Wren wriggled down from my hip and stood at the line where the pines ended, bare feet on the snow—she had never once consented to shoes; we'd have to negotiate that later if there was a later—and looked out at the roaring silence beyond. Then she looked up at me. And her mouth opened, and I braced myself for another word in the tongue of the erased, another syllable that would rearrange my insides—

Nothing came out.

She tried again. Her lips moved. Her small throat worked. And no sound arrived, as though the words were being posted to an address that no longer existed. Her face did something I will spend the rest of my life trying to un-see: not surprise. Recognition. The look of someone who expected the door to be locked, and found it locked, and hated herself for hoping.

"Oh," I said softly. "Oh, sweetheart."

Because I understood. Of course I understood. She was made of Vhalen—all of her, every inch, the last spell of a vanished kingdom wearing the shape of a child—and Vhalen did not exist outside the vale. Inside the vale, where magic couldn't reach, she was protected from her own nature, held together by the same deadness that had sheltered her these two centuries of travel and hiding. Outside it, in the singing loudness of the world, she was a message being read. And messages wear out.

She'd stop speaking entirely out here. Perhaps worse than speaking.

Behind us, faint but rising, hooves on the switchback below.

I took her hand. Her fingers closed around mine, cold as river stones, and we stepped over the line together.

* * *

People say drowning is peaceful, near the end. I wouldn't know; my whole profession is the opposite of swallowing water. But I know what it is to have a sense return after a lifetime without it, because that afternoon I drowned in light.

The first hour beyond the boundary I spent mostly on my knees, which was undignified but honest. Every color was wrong—wrong meaning *more*. The brown of the winter grass was a chord of forty browns. The sky went on being blue in a manner I can only call aggressive. When the wind moved the dead stalks of the meadow-grass I saw, unmistakably, briefly, the shapes of the green that had been there in summer, ghosting under the present like handwriting under palimpsest. Time was visible here. Everything left its echo.

And underneath it all, the singing. Not with the ears. With whatever organ you'd use to taste a lie, or feel someone watching your back, or know—before the broth ever touches your tongue—that today is a day someone wants the king's envoy dead. Tasters have that sense honed to a razor. Out here the razor was a cathedral bell being struck continuously. Every hedge had opinions. Every stream was saying something. A crow watched us from a fencepost with an interest I could only describe as professional, and I caught myself glaring back at it and thinking, quite clearly, *yes, and?*

Wren pulled me onward. She couldn't speak, but she communicated plenty—tugs on my sleeve, points, the imperious little frowns of a child who knows exactly where she's going even if she's translucent at the edges. Because she was, now. Translucent. In direct sunlight I could see the shape of a fencepost through her shoulder, faint as a watermark. She was fading out here like ink in rain, and every hour we stayed in the loud world cost her something I couldn't name and couldn't replace.

Which presented a problem of elegant cruelty: the only place she could survive was the place we'd just fled, and the people hunting her were already in it.

We made camp that night in the lee of a fallen dolmen—an old standing stone, tipped and half-buried, older than any kingdom I could name. I got a fire going with the flint I'd had the foresight to pack and the lack of foresight to be proud of, because the moment the flame caught, it burned green. Not the sickly green of copper salts. A deep, glad green, like spring remembering itself. The fire liked Wren. Everything out here seemed to like Wren, or notice her, or lean toward her the way plants lean toward windows.

I fed her dried meat softened in melted snow and watched her eat with the automatic thoroughness of the starving, and I did arithmetic I didn't like.

One day out of the vale, and she was thinner. Paler. The watermark had spread. At this rate—no. I put the arithmetic away. Arithmetic was for grocers and coroners.

"You'll have to talk," I told her instead, gently, uselessly. "In the vale, I mean. We'll have to go back eventually, and when we do, you're going to tell me everything—where this door is, why I matter, what word comes next after Kavanagh. I warn you, I ask excellent questions. It's the one part of my job that transfers."

She looked at me over the meat. And then, deliberately, she reached out and touched the fire—put her whole hand into the green flame and held it there, unhurt, watching me watch her—and withdrew her hand and pointed at the flames, then at the east, then at her own chest.

Fire. East. Herself.

"I don't speak charades," I said. "Try again when you have a voice."

But I lay awake a long time after she slept, staring at the green firelight on the underside of the dolmen, turning it over. Fire and the east. Somewhere east, something burned that was hers—or that she was. Mother Ilsen's notes were a jumble of marginalia and dying shorthand, but one phrase kept surfacing when I closed my eyes, in the priest's careful hand:

*The Door of Thirteenth Moons stands where the moon has always counted differently.*

East. Where the moon counts differently. Where a kingdom used to be, before it taught the maps to forget.

I should have felt despair. Instead—and I want to be honest in this account, because honesty is the one luxury I never got to practice as a taster—I felt awake. Terribly, dangerously awake. For twenty-six years I had swallowed small deaths for a man I'd never met, in a valley that swallowed everything, and called the numbness peace. Now the world was deafeningly, unbearably alive, my companion was evaporating, armed men wanted us both, and I had never in my life been less numb.

Freedom, it turns out, feels like drowning. But drowning, I'm told, is also what being born feels like, from the baby's side of things.

Near midnight, Wren woke screaming—soundlessly, horribly, her mouth wide and nothing coming out, her small body thrashing like a hooked fish—and I gathered her in and rocked her until the shaking stopped. When it did, she gripped my wrist and turned my arm over and pressed one finger, very lightly, to the faded white scar on the inside of my elbow. The oldest one. The one I've had since before memory.

Then she pointed east again. Fire. East. Scar.

And far behind us, back toward the pass, toward the vale, toward everything I'd been—dogs began to bellow. Not hounds. Something with more discipline and less soul. The riders weren't waiting for daylight.

"Well," I said to the child, to the fire, to the enormous listening night. "Up we get, then."

I kicked dirt over the green flames, shouldered the satchel, lifted Wren onto my hip—and we went east, into the singing dark, with the moon above us counting toward some number I didn't yet know how to fear.
