## 5. The Gilded Cage of Aldric's Court

Everyone knows you flee a hunter by running away. Everyone is wrong.

You flee a hunter by standing very still inside his own house, where he will never think to look, because no one is stupid enough to hide in the wolf's den. I had spent eleven years being paid to swallow things that might kill me; I was, if nothing else, an expert in stupidity of a controlled and deliberate kind.

Besides, Wren could not walk far. That was the truth I dressed up as strategy. The child had stopped speaking the moment we crossed the vale's boundary—stopped entirely, as though the road out of Ashenmoor had taken their words as toll—and now moved through the world like something half-drawn, a sketch of a child waiting for its artist to finish. They ate little. They slept much. And when they slept, they faded at the edges, the way a fire dims before it dies.

So: Serelle. That was my name now, or close enough to it that I answered without flinching. Serelle Voss, a taster from the southern hill country, references forged in Osk's careful hand during our three days hiding in his sister's cellar while riders combed the roads behind us. Osk had wanted to come with us all the way to the capital. I'd told him no. Someone had to stay in the vale and keep Mother Ilsen's grave swept, and someone had to remember where her notes were buried, and mostly I could not afford to love anyone else just then. Love was becoming an expensive habit.

"You're mad," Osk had said, not unkindly, packing dried plums into my satchel like a man arming a soldier with fruit. "The king's own sorcerer pulled your name from the guild rolls two days after the child appeared. You think a false name will stop him?"

"No," I said honestly. "But Corvane will be looking for a fugitive on the roads. He won't look for a taster in his master's kitchen. People don't look for things they're already holding."

He hadn't liked it. He'd kissed Wren's forehead anyway, and pressed his maps against my ribs until I promised—promised—that I would send for him when I found the Door. The Door of Thirteenth Moons. Even saying it felt like handling a blade by the edge.

And so, on a gray morning thick with river fog, I walked through the gates of Carreth with a sleeping child bundled on my back like contraband wool, and presented myself at the servants' gate of the royal kitchens with forged papers and a dead woman's trade.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about the palace: it smells like every other great house, only more so. Tallow and wet stone, onions and old smoke, the sweet rot of roses too far from their gardens. Underneath it all, the particular sourness of fear, which every large household exudes the way a cheese sweats whey. I knew that smell. I had lived in it my whole life. The Vale of Ashenmoor simply wore it more quietly.

I was hired within the hour. This surprised me less than it should have. Tasters die like mayflies—the profession's turnover is brutal—and the head cook, a mountain of a woman called Mistress Hark, looked at my hands, then my tongue, then the line of my jaw, and grunted the grunt of a woman who has judged a thousand carcasses and found this one acceptable.

"Swallow first," she said, pushing a cup of wine across the scarred oak. "Then we'll talk wages."

I drank. It was clean—no bitterness under the plum, no numbness crawling at the gumline—and I set down the cup with the small nod that was my whole résumé. She raised an eyebrow at how fast I'd assessed it.

"Hill country?" she asked.

"Southern vales." Let the geography blur. "My mother trained me. Her mother before."

Something flickered in Hark's face—not suspicion exactly, but the wariness of a woman who has heard family histories used as weapons. "We lost our last taster in spring," she said. "Fell ill. Not poisoned," she added quickly, which is what everyone says about tasters who die, right before they lower their voices.

"I'm sorry," I said, because there was nothing else to say, and because some part of me wondered—with a cold, professional curiosity—if the last taster had fallen ill of anything, or merely of being near the wrong secret at the wrong time.

Wren was the harder problem. A child cannot sit silent in a kitchen for months without questions. But the palace has a genius for absorbing children the way a river absorbs stones—it simply assumes they belong somewhere. Hark took one look at Wren's hollow cheeks and muteness and declared them an orphan cousin of mine, deaf since a fever, useful for scrubbing and sorting, and assigned them to the scullery with a gentleness she would have denied under torture. Wren scrubbed pots with the solemn diligence of someone performing a sacred rite, and if the other kitchen children noticed that the quiet one sometimes stared at the moon through the high windows as though listening to it, well—kitchens are full of oddities, and oddities who work hard are left alone.

At night we shared a pallet in the tasters' narrow cell below the east stair, and I held Wren's cooling hand and counted the thinnings of them the way a miser counts coins leaving his purse. Their skin had begun, in certain lights, to show the wall through it. Not transparent—translucent. Like wax. Like paper held to a flame but not yet burning.

Thirteen turns of the moon, Mother Ilsen's notes said. The impossible month. It had begun the night the child spoke to me in my kitchen, and winter solstice would end it, and everything between now and then was a race I did not yet know the course of.

But I knew where to start looking. Kings keep archives. And kings owe debts to those who feed them safely. I only had to survive long enough to earn one.

* * *

I saw King Aldric for the first time nine days into my service, at the evening meal, and I nearly dropped the tasting spoon.

Not because of anything he did. Because of what he didn't do.

The histories and the heralds paint Aldric of Carreth as a conqueror's son, tall and bright, a man carved by sculptors who were paid in advance. And he was handsome—I'll grant the coin spent on his portraits wasn't wasted—but what struck me, watching him from three places down the high table, was how tired he was. Not bored-tired. The tiredness of a man carrying something heavy up a long staircase who cannot put it down and cannot say what it is.

He laughed at his chamberlain's joke. He complimented the venison—he had no idea I had tasted it forty minutes earlier, standing alone in a stone pantry with a candle and a spoon, reading the dish the way Ilesen read manuscripts—and he touched the shoulder of the youngest page with an easy kindness that made the boy glow for a week. Charm came off him naturally, the way warmth comes off a hearthstone. But underneath the charm, his eyes kept doing a thing I recognized because I had seen it in mirrors: they kept checking the doors. All of them. Constantly. In a rhythm so practiced it looked like nothing at all.

I knew that rhythm. It was the rhythm of a man who expected something to come through one of those doors, someday, and had decided long ago that the expecting itself must remain invisible.

A king afraid. Of what? I turned the question over that night while Wren slept against my side, and found no answer, and found the not-knowing strangely worse than any answer.

* * *

Lord Corvane I saw sooner, because Lord Corvane made sure to be seen.

He appeared in the kitchens on my third day, unannounced, in a grey coat the color of weather coming, and the entire room rearranged itself around him the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. Cooks developed sudden fascinations with their pots. Scullery boys evaporated. Only Hark stood her ground, arms folded over her chest like siege works.

"Lord Corvane. We've naught for your table today but bread and honesty, and you'll find both stale."

"I've come about the new taster." His voice was mild. Mild voices are the ones you watch. "Serelle Voss. From the southern hills."

"That's the one."

"May I?"

She stepped aside with the reluctance of a drawbridge lowering for an army, and Corvane crossed the kitchen to where I was grinding pepper, and looked at me.

I want to record precisely what that look was, because later—much later—I would understand it, and understanding changes the memory retroactively, the way knowing a friend betrayed you recolors every kindness they ever did. At the time, it seemed like appraisal. A sorcerer inspecting the newest mouth between his king and death: reasonable, even responsible. His eyes were pale and patient, the eyes of a man who reads slowly because he intends to finish the whole book. They went over my hands—taster's hands, scarred faintly along the fingertips from years of pricks and burns meant to look accidental—and lingered there a half-second longer than courtesy required.

"Voss," he said. "An old name."

"There are many of us."

"In the south?"

"Everywhere there are hills and bad cooks," I said, and ground the pepper harder, and gave him the flat, slightly stupid stare of a woman whose ambitions extend no further than the next meal.

He smiled. It reached his eyes, which unsettled me more than if it hadn't. "Welcome to court, Mistress Voss. Guard the king well. He is—" and here the pause was so brief most people would have missed it, but I tasted pauses the way I tasted broth, hunting the note beneath the note—"precious to all of us."

Then he left, and the kitchen exhaled, and Hark muttered something anatomically creative about sorcerers, and I went back to my pepper with my heart tapping out a slow alarm against my ribs.

He knew. I couldn't say how I knew that he knew—or rather, I could say exactly how: eleven years of poison-training had taught me to detect a toxin at concentrations no sane palate could catch, and Corvane's whole manner had carried that trace, that half-part-per-million wrongness, the flavor of a thing pretending to be another thing. He had not come to inspect me. He had come to confirm me. The question was whether he confirmed me as Serelle Voss, competent stranger, or as something else—and either way, why he had walked away smiling instead of calling the guard.

Unless, whispered a voice in the dark of my skull that night, unless the hunt hasn't been announced. Unless he wants whatever you are for himself, first.

I lay awake listening to Wren breathe, each breath a little shallower than the last, and thought: I have hidden in the wolf's den, and the den has a second wolf, and the second wolf is smarter than the first.

* * *

The weeks found their shape, as weeks do. I tasted. I survived my own competence. I learned the palace's rhythms: which steward watered the wine, which lord sent gifts that needed triple-checking (all of them, always), which dishes arrived at the royal table already tasted by protocol and which came raw with risk. I learned that King Aldric dined lightly, drank sparingly, and had dismissed his father's old fool for reasons no one would name. I learned that he walked the eastern gallery alone at dusk, every dusk, past the portraits of his dead fathers, and that the guards had orders not to follow.

I learned, above all, that the palace was afraid in a way it refused to articulate. Fear moves through a great house like damp through stone—you can't see it, but everything grows cold in patches. Servants spoke of omens in the lowered voices people reserve for illnesses. A hawk had died falling out of a clear sky onto the tiltyard. The moat had frozen in a pattern some swore spelled letters in a language no one could read. The bells of the chapel rang a half-tone flat and no bell-founder could correct them. Small things. Stupid things. But they accumulated, and I had spent my life among people who believed that safety was a substance you could store, and I watched that belief eroding in real time, day by day, like a shoreline before a storm.

And once—only once—I saw the king break.

It was late, near midnight, and I had been summoned to taste an unscheduled supper: the king, I was told, had taken a fancy to working through the night and wanted plain food brought to the library. So I climbed with the tray myself, because I trusted no one else's caution and my own paranoia had become, by then, a sort of religion, and I came around the corner of the library stairs and stopped.

Aldric stood at the window with a goblet untouched beside him, and he was talking—to no one. To the dark glass. And though I caught only the last of it, I caught enough:

"—every year, the same dream, and every year it's closer to the door. Father dreamed it too. You wrote that it was nothing. You wrote that and then you died, and I have read your hand a hundred times and I am asking you, *what was behind the door*—"

His fist came down on the sill, softly, a king's fist, trained never to make noise. Then he stood very still, breathing, a big man reduced by exhaustion to something almost boy-shaped, and I withdrew before he could turn, and delivered the tray through the proper servant as though I had never been anywhere near the library at all.

That night I lay staring into the dark of our cell, Wren's hand curled in mine, and assembled the pieces the way I'd assemble the profile of an unfamiliar poison: a king who dreams of a door. A king whose fathers died young. A sorcerer who hunts a vanished kingdom's heirs. A mute child fading like ink in rain, carrying a language that should not exist.

The door. Everything, all of it, pointed at a door.

And somewhere out in the world—or under it, or behind it—was the Door of Thirteenth Moons, and I had until the solstice to find it, and the only mapmaker alive who might know the way was three hundred miles back in the vale, sweeping a dead woman's grave and waiting for word I could not yet safely send.

Wren stirred. In sleep, the child's lips moved, shaping soundless syllables, and for a moment—one heartbeat, no more—I felt warmth pulse through their fingers into mine, deliberate as a handshake, and in my blood rose a word I had never learned and understood completely:

*Soon,* it said. Or perhaps: *Hurry.*

Both, probably. With Wren it was always both.

Outside the arrow-slit window, the moon hung over the capital, white and patient and counting.
