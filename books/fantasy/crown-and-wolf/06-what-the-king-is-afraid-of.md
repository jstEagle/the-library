## 6. What the King Is Afraid Of

My father used to say that a king should be the last man in his kingdom to know fear, because fear is like smoke: it drifts downward, and everyone below you chokes on it. He said this while drinking himself blind every night in this same chamber, so I have always taken his counsel with the appropriate grain of salt. Several grains. A whole salt cellar, some nights.

Tonight the fire is lit, the wine is poured, and I am talking to a painting again. There are worse habits. I could be talking to Corvane.

"You would laugh at me," I tell him. The portrait says nothing, which is the quality I value most in a confessor. My father's face gazes down from the canvas with the expression he wore for state occasions—dignified, weary, faintly constipated—and beneath the gilded frame the inscription reads: *ALDRIC THE SECOND, BY THE GRACE OF THE VALE AND THE VALOR OF ITS PEOPLE*. He was neither graceful nor valorous, my father. He was frightened, and he was thorough about it, and the historians have mistaken one for the other, as historians do.

I am his son. I am thorough too.

* * *

It began, as these things always begin, before I was born. Before my father was born. There is a book in the sealed vault—no, I won't pretend I've read the book; Corvane reads it and tells me what I need to know, which is a arrangement I have never entirely trusted and have never once ended—but there is a story every heir of my line learns, usually from a dying parent, usually too late to be useful.

Every king of Ashenmoor dreams of a door.

Not a metaphor. Corvane was very clear on this when I was fourteen and had just woken screaming from the first of them. *Not a metaphor, Your Highness. A door. Describe it.* And I described it: a door standing alone in snow, no wall around it, made of pale wood gone silver with age, and on its lintel thirteen notches like the marks a prisoner carves to count her days. And Corvane had gone very still, the way he does, which is his version of weeping, and said, *Yes. That is the one.*

My grandfather dreamed it. My father dreamed it. His mother, who was regent for eleven years and never crowned, dreamed it anyway, because the door apparently does not care about technicalities. And every one of them who wore the crown died within a year of their coronation. Grandfather: a fever that burned him to a husk in six weeks. Father: a fall from a horse in a flat meadow, at a walking pace, with no stone or root or reason anywhere in sight. The regent-mother: she simply stopped, in her chair, between one breath and the next, with her sewing still in her lap.

A year. The door gives you a year. I have been king for nine months and eleven days, and I have taken to counting the way a condemned man counts, which is to say constantly and with no benefit whatsoever.

"You'd tell me it was nonsense," I say to the portrait. "You'd say, *Aldric, kings die of fevers and horses, that's the whole of the trade.*" I drink. The wine is good; it is always good; I have never once tasted it, because tasting is what I pay other people to do. "But you dreamed it too. You told me the night before your coronation. You held my hand and you said, *If you dream of a door, boy, don't open it. Whatever knocks, don't answer.* And then you went and died of a horse, and left me the crown and the dream and the counting."

The portrait maintains its dignified silence. My father was better at silence than at anything else.

* * *

Here is what I know, or what I have assembled from Corvane's briefings and the vault's sealed shelves and the nightmares I no longer bother describing to anyone: two hundred years ago there was a kingdom called Vhalen. It stood where the Salt Flats now stand, or perhaps the Salt Flats stand where it stood; geography is one of the casualties of what happened next. Vhalen was old, and rich in a way that had nothing to do with gold, and it practiced a kind of magic that my ancestors' chroniclers described in the tones men use for beautiful, dangerous weather.

And then it was gone. Not conquered. Not burned. *Gone*—unwritten, unmapped, unnamed. The chronicles of the era skip from one year to the next like a stone across water. There is a two-hundred-year-old treaty in the vault whose third clause simply ends mid-sentence, and the sentence it ends with, according to Corvane, is the name of Vhalen's capital. Whatever unmade the kingdom unmade the words for it too. The treaty doesn't trail off because the scribe died. It trails off because the scribe, writing the name, forgot what he was writing.

Corvane says the Vhalenics did it themselves. That when conquest came—and something was coming for them, something even my ancestors feared—they chose erasure over surrender. That they folded their whole nation closed like a hand, and the door is the seam.

I say: then why does the seam dream itself into the heads of my family every generation like an unpaid debt?

"You'd have an answer," I tell my father. "You always had an answer. They were usually wrong, but you delivered them beautifully."

* * *

There was a woman in the kitchens today. A taster, new to the capital post, though her papers said she'd served in the Vale for years—Kavanagh, some name like that, I don't retain the small ones, it's a flaw of mine and Corvane lectures me about it. I passed her in the corridor outside the banquet hall and she did the thing all tasters do, the small bow, the eyes down, the whole choreography of a person whose profession is to be unremarkable.

Except her hands.

A taster's hands are steady. That is the entire art of it—Corvane taught me this in my first month, when I flinched at a cup and he looked at me with something between pity and contempt and said, *Sire, the poison is in the flinch. A taster who fears the cup has already failed it.* This woman's hands were steady as a surgeon's. But when I passed her, when my shadow crossed hers, her hands went white at the knuckles, the way hands do around something they're holding very hard. Fear, I thought. Or fury. With tasters it's usually fear, and who could blame her; I am the man whose food she may die for.

But I have been raised on fear the way farmers are raised on weather, and I know its varieties, and this was not the fear of a servant near a king. It was the fear of a person who has seen something in the room that no one else has seen yet.

I mentioned it to Corvane this evening. He said he would look into her. He always says he will look into things. I have begun to suspect that Corvane's looking into things is rather like my talking to portraits: a ritual that soothes the performer and changes nothing.

* * *

The dream came again last night. It always comes the same.

I am standing in snow, in a country that is somehow both the Salt Flats and a garden, and the door is in front of me, silver-wooded, thirteen-notched, patient. And from behind it—because in the dream the door is closed, it is always closed, and I am grateful to whatever mercy keeps it closed—there comes a sound. Not knocking. Not voices. It is the sound a house makes at night when it settles: the small honest creaks of a thing that is lived in. The door sounds *inhabited*. And every time, in the dream, I understand with total, wordless certainty that on the other side of that door is everything my family took from the world, and it has been waiting, and it is not angry.

It is worse than angry. It is *reasonable*. It knocks politely.

I wake with my heart going like a siege drum and I lie in the dark doing arithmetic. Nine months, eleven days. The dream has been coming every night this month. In my grandfather's time, the household records say, the dreams came nightly only in the final weeks.

"Corvane says you were right to erase them," I tell my father's portrait. "He says a wound that size doesn't close on its own, and that if anything of Vhalen is leaking back through—through dreams, through whatever else—it should be burned at the seam. He says it with such conviction. He's the only man I've ever met who is more afraid than I am, and he's dressed his fear so well that everyone calls it wisdom."

I turn the cup in my hands. I don't drink. Somewhere below, in the kitchens, a woman with steady white-knuckled hands is tasting my dinner, and I am betting my life on her mouth, which is the most honest arrangement in this entire palace.

"Here's what I can't say to Corvane," I say. "Here's what I've never said to anyone. If the door opens—if it's *already* opening, the way the dreams suggest—then perhaps it isn't Vhalen coming for us at all. Perhaps it's Vhalen coming *home*. And perhaps the reason every crowned head of our line dies within the year is not that Vhalen kills us. Perhaps it's that we were never meant to sit on this throne at all. Perhaps the crown itself is the theft, and the door is only the collection notice."

The fire pops. The portrait says nothing. Outside, the winter wind worries at the shutters like a dog at a bone, and somewhere in the tower Corvane is awake—I know he is awake, he is always awake, I have never once seen the man sleep and I have stopped believing he does it—doing whatever he does with his sealed books and his sealed vault and his sealed face.

Nine months and eleven days. If the pattern holds, I have weeks, not months. And Corvane's answer to that is fire at the seam, and my father's answer was a warning I can't follow, and my own answer, so far, has been a cup of wine and a conversation with paint.

There is a knock at the chamber door. Three soft raps. Corvane's knock—he has exactly one knock, the way he has exactly one expression—and I feel my spine straighten into the posture kings are taught, the one that says *nothing frightens me*, which is the most useful and most exhausting lie I own.

"Enter," I say.

He comes in with the candlelight behind him and something in his hands wrapped in grey cloth, and he is smiling. In nineteen years I have seen Corvane smile perhaps four times, and every one of them has preceded the worst news of my life.

"Sire," he says, "I've found something. About the taster."
