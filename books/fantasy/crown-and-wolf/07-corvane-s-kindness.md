## 7. Corvane's Kindness

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a palace at night, and I had learned to read it the way I once read the surface of a suspect broth. The Vale of Ashenmoor had taught me that everything dangerous announces itself eventually—a bitter edge on the tongue, a film on the wine, a servant who suddenly won't meet your eyes. Silence was no different. A palace that was merely asleep sounded like a snoring dog: heavy, honest, forgettable. But a palace that was *listening*—that was a different vintage altogether.

For three weeks as "Maren Ash," under-taster of the second kitchen, I had been listening back.

I told myself it was strategy. Hiding in the wolf's den was the oldest trick there was; no one hunts the mouse in the cat's own bed. Wren slept in the false-bottomed linen chest in my narrow room above the laundry, breathing slow and small, and every morning I checked that breath first the way other people check for rain. The child spoke almost nothing now. Outside the vale, the words had gone out of her like air from a punctured lung. She communicated in tugs on my sleeve, in the way she arranged pebbles on the windowsill into patterns I didn't understand but which made my scars ache in a way I was learning to distrust.

The patterns were getting simpler. Fewer stones each week.

I tried not to do the arithmetic that implied.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday, because catastrophe has always had a taste for ordinary days. I had been sent to the archive corridor to deliver the evening's tasting ledger to the steward's office—the king's household kept records of everything that passed anyone's lips, and mine was considered unusually thorough—and I took a wrong turn. That was true, actually. I want that on the record. I genuinely took a wrong turn, down a service passage I hadn't used before, past a door some careless clerk had left ajar.

And there, lit by a single guttering lamp, was a wall.

Not a wall of stone. A wall of glyphs, carved deep into the plaster and then plastered over and then carved again, the way a scar forms over an old wound and the wound breaks through. I knew perhaps one word in twenty from Mother Ilsen's fevered lessons, but Ilsen had taught me the alphabet the way she'd taught me everything else—by making me write it until my hand bled, saying, *A taster must know poison's name even when it's written prettily.*

I stood there like a fool with my mouth open, reading.

Most of it was administrative. Grain tallies. Names of harbors. A complaint about the price of glass—I smiled at that, some long-dead Vhalenic clerk grumbling about imports two centuries after his kingdom had been unwritten from the world. And then, near the bottom, half-erased by newer plaster, a phrase I did know, because Ilsen had made me copy it forty times:

*The door remembers what the moon forgets.*

My finger touched the carving before my brain could stop it.

"You read Vhalenic."

I did not jump. Twenty years of tasting had burned the flinch out of me; you cannot startle when your mouth is full of something that might kill you. But every muscle I owned went to iron, and I turned around slowly, arranging my face into the pleasant blankness of a kitchen servant who has been caught doing nothing worse than loitering.

Lord Corvane stood in the corridor with a candle in one hand and no expression at all.

He was not what the rumors promised. Court gossip painted the king's sorcerer as a vulture in silk, all hooks and hunger, and there was certainly something carrion-bird about the stillness of him—but he was also tired. Deeply, structurally tired, the way a house is tired when its foundations have shifted. His eyes went from my finger on the wall to my face and stayed there with the patience of a man who had all night and knew it.

"The ledger says Maren Ash," he said. "Transferred from a valley posting three months ago. References from a steward named Dossen, who, I have since learned, died of a fever two years ago." He tilted his head. "Forged references are usually better. Yours were lazy."

"Sir—" I began, in my best valley accent, flat and humble.

"Don't." He said it without heat. "Please don't. I've spent eleven years listening to people lie to this court, and I can hear the seams in it now, the way you'd hear them. You read High Vhalenic. There are perhaps four people alive who can, and I've had two of them killed and one is me." He stepped closer, and the candle flame leaned away from him, which I noted and filed under things to worry about later. "So you will understand my interest."

Here is what the vale teaches you that no court can: how to stand very still while something decides whether to eat you. I let the silence stretch. I let my shoulders drop into apology. And I thought, with a clarity that would have made old Mother Ilsen proud, *He hasn't called the guards. Guards are loud, and this man wants quiet.*

"I read a little," I said finally. "Old family letters. My grandmother's people came from the north, before the erasure. It was a nursery thing. Songs, mostly."

"Sing one."

The trap was elegant. Any song I produced would be wrong, or right in the wrong way, or right in a way that proved too much. So I did the only honest thing available to a liar of my quality: I sang a nonsense lullaby in fake-Vhalenic cadence, syllables shaped like the real tongue the way wax fruit is shaped like pears, and watched his face while I did it.

Corvane listened all the way through. Then he said, quietly, "That was beautiful and completely meaningless, and the fact that you thought it would work tells me you're frightened rather than stupid. Frightened I can use."

"Use for what?"

"For nothing, yet." He turned and walked, unhurried, and after a moment's argument with myself I followed, because the alternative was standing alone in a haunted corridor with a wall full of dead language and a man who might return with soldiers. "You should know," he said over his shoulder, "that I reported none of this. No one knows I found you here. Consider what that tells you about my intentions."

"It tells me your intentions have a price tag," I said. "Everything in this palace does. I've seen the market rates."

Something moved across his face that on another man I'd have called amusement. "You'll do."

* * *

He took me down.

I mean that in every sense. We descended stairs I hadn't known existed, below the archive levels, below the cellars where the coronation wine slept in the dark, into a cold that had a mineral smell to it, old stone and older water. The lamps down here burned a color I recognized from the worst night of my life—the night Wren arrived at my door and the fire went green—and I hated that I recognized it.

"Where are we?"

"The Undercroft." Corvane's voice had changed. It was quieter now, and careful, the way people speak in sickrooms. "Every kingdom keeps the things it's ashamed of. Aldric's grandfather built this. His father expanded it. The king himself—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat, "—the king prefers not to know it exists. Which is a luxury, not knowing. Someone has to know."

"Someone being you."

"Someone always being me."

We stopped before an iron door banded with lead. Corvane produced a key—not metal, I realized, but a sliver of something pale and dense, like moonlight that had been pressed into a bar—and the lock opened with a sound like a held breath released.

"Before we go in," he said, "you should understand why I'm showing you. I could threaten you. I could have you arrested within the hour; your forged papers wouldn't survive my attention, let alone a tribunal. But threats make people run, and running people go to ground in places I can't follow, taking things I need." He looked at me, and for one unguarded moment the tiredness in him was bottomless. "So instead I'm going to show you the truth, and let you draw the obvious conclusion. Kindness, Mistress Ash. Or the shape of it. It opens more doors than keys do."

"That's the most honest sentence anyone's said to me since I left the vale," I said, "and I hate it."

"Good. Hate keeps people sharp."

He opened the door.

The cell beyond was clean. That was the first horror—that someone *cleaned* it. A pallet, a basin, a window slit too high and too narrow to matter. And in the corner, sitting with its back to us, was a thing that had once been a person.

I say *once* because I don't know how else to say it. It had the outline of a woman—shoulders, spine, the fall of hair—but the details had been worn off it the way lettering wears off a coin passed through ten thousand hands. Its skin was the gray of paper left in rain. When it turned toward the light, its face was smooth where features should have been, except for the mouth, which was very detailed indeed, and very much in use.

It was singing. In Vhalenic.

The same nonsense lullaby I had just performed in the corridor above—except this was the original, the true melody, and hearing it was like hearing a bell after years of someone humming an imitation. My scars lit up. My teeth ached. Somewhere far above us, I would have sworn, the whole palace shivered.

"She came through eighty years ago," Corvane said softly. "Near the coast. Fishermen found her walking out of the shallows, singing. She was a girl then. They brought her to the capital because she wouldn't stop walking east, and she's been walking east ever since—in circles, in this cell, when we let her. Toward something that isn't here anymore." He closed the door gently. "She is the luckiest of them. The others—" He shook his head. "Things come through, Mistress Ash. Small things, broken things, pieces of a country that was taken out of the world without being allowed to finish dying. And they come through *wrong*. Because there is no longer any place for them to be right."

"And you keep them here. In the dark. Singing."

"I keep them here so they don't end up in a village square with pitchforks, or in some hedge-witch's cellar being bled for charms. Yes. In the dark. Singing." He met my eyes. "You think I'm their jailer. I am also the only person who visits them."

I wanted to hate him cleanly and completely, and the maddening thing was that I couldn't. That was the hook of him, I understood even then—he was kind in the exact places where cruelty would have been easier to refuse. Every kindness was a barb dressed in wool.

"Why show me this?" I asked. "Truly."

"Because of the child."

The floor did not move, but I felt it move anyway, the way the deck of a ship moves in a swell.

"There is no child," I said.

"Maren Ash arrived in the capital with an extra ration chit drawn against the laundry stores. Laundry stores feed staff, not staff's imaginary cousins. Someone in the provisioning office owes me a favor and noticed the discrepancy three weeks ago. I've been waiting to see if you'd be clever enough to come to me before I came to you." He folded his hands. "You weren't. So here we are."

Every escape route in my mind slammed shut at once, and I stood in the wreckage doing sums. He could take Wren whenever he wished. He had known for weeks and had done nothing—which meant he wanted something he couldn't simply seize, something that required my hands, my cooperation, my willingness.

"What do you want with her?" I asked. "If you say 'to help her,' I will scream, and this close to your cells, I suspect everything down here would scream with me."

That earned me the ghost of the amusement again. "To study her. To learn what she is before the thirteenth turn completes—yes, I know about the moon, I've known about the moon since before you were born—and to decide, with evidence rather than fear, whether she is a door or a wound." His voice dropped. "The king dreams of a door, Mistress Ash. Every king of this line has dreamed of it, and every one of them has died inside a year of his crowning. I have served two of those kings and buried both, and I am telling you plainly: I will not bury a third. If the child is the key to whatever kills my kings, I need her. And you—" he stepped closer, and the lamplight made his shadow climb the wall behind him like something climbing out of a well, "—you need someone between her and everyone else in this palace who will be looking for her, once they realize what she is. I am offering to be that someone. Protection. Food. A roof that isn't a linen chest. In exchange for access. That is all. Access."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I continue knowing what I know, alone, without allies, and we both discover how long my patience and your luck hold out." He straightened. "But you saw her." He nodded at the iron door, behind which the worn-away woman was still singing. "You saw what comes through wrong. Is the child speaking? Still herself? Still *solid*?"

I said nothing, and my silence said everything, and Corvane received it like a man receiving confirmation of a diagnosis he'd already suspected.

"She's fading," he said quietly. "Of course she is. They always fade. Whatever she carries, she carries alone, in a world with no room for it, and it is eating her alive from the inside. You can watch that happen in a linen chest, alone, with your forged papers and your valley stubbornness. Or you can let me try to stop it." He extended his hand. "Kindness, Mistress Ash. Or the shape of it. I'm told it opens more doors than keys."

I looked at his hand. I thought of Wren's pebbles, fewer each week. I thought of the worn-away woman singing a lullaby toward a country that no longer existed, walking east in circles for eighty years because no one had ever thought to ask her *where*, only to lock the door behind her.

I thought: *this man is offering to save her the way a collector saves a moth—pinned, labeled, preserved forever.*

I put my hand in his anyway, because a hooked kindness is still a rope, and drowning people are not famous for inspecting knots.

"One condition," I said. "When you study her—you tell me everything. Every test, every result, every theory. No locked rooms between us."

"Agreed," said Corvane, and shook once, firm and dry, and I felt the bargain settle over me like snowfall, soft and cold and covering everything.

* * *

I climbed back up through the palace with the lullaby lodged in my skull like a fishbone. The true version. Eighty years in a lead-banded cell, and she still remembered every note of a song from a country that had been unwritten before her grandmother was born.

Wren was awake when I reached my room. She sat up in the linen chest, hair sleep-tangled, and looked at my face the way children look—as if faces were books written specifically for them—and whatever she read there made her reach out and touch my cheek with one small, cool hand.

Then she held up her other fist, and opened it.

Three pebbles. Last week there had been five.

I took her in my arms and held her while she trembled, and over her head I stared at the wall where, faint as breath on glass, a pattern of cracks had appeared overnight in the plaster—thin lines crossing, meeting, spreading outward.

They formed a glyph.

I knew it from Ilsen's lessons. From the wall in the corridor. From the bottom of every map Osk's kind had ever been imprisoned for drawing.

It meant *door*.

And it was pointing down.
