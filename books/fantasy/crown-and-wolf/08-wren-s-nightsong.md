## 8. Wren's Nightsong

I dream in the old country because I have nowhere else to put it.

That is what I am, though I did not know the word for it until now. Not a girl who dreams. A place that dreams. A kingdom with nowhere left to be, so it folds itself small and hides inside a child, the way a snail carries its house, the way my mother — no. Not my mother. The way *someone* carried me across a border that was already closing, running through snow that fell upward, and pressed me into the world like a seal pressed into wax.

I am the wax. Someone else made the stamp.

* * *

Tonight the towers are glass again.

They always are, at the start. Glass towers full of warm light, and inside each light a life: a baker pulling loaves shaped like sleeping birds, a soldier polishing armor that has never seen a dent, two old men arguing over a game of stones in a courtyard where the fountain runs with honey instead of water. I know all of them. That is the strange part. I have never met any of them and I know all of them, the way you know your own hand without having to look at it.

The baker looks up as I pass her window and she waves. Her name is Maretta. She has flour on her cheekbone and a dead son she does not remember grieving, because grief was one of the things the kingdom folded away when it folded itself into me. She waves anyway. Everyone here waves. It is very hard to be sad in a city where everyone waves, which is why the sadness lives somewhere else, somewhere below, down under the glass foundations, in the dark where the door is.

I go there every night. I do not want to. I go anyway.

The streets of Vhalen are cobbled with moonstone, and when I walk on them they hum a little, like a cat deciding whether to purr. The humming gets louder near the center of the city, where the palace stands — no, not a palace. A lantern. That is what the Vhalenic word means, the one the mute-mouthed priests used to sing: *lantern-house*, the building that holds the light for everyone else. Its windows are tall and narrow and full of a color I don't have a word for in the tongue I'm thinking in. In High Vhalenic it is called *selleth*. It is the color of the moment just before someone you love says your name.

Inside the lantern-house, on a throne that is really just a chair with good light behind it, sits the queen.

She has Sable's face.

I didn't know that the first hundred times. I only knew that looking at her felt like being held, and that her voice sounded like bread smells, and that when she said my true name — my real one, the long one, the one with the river in it — the whole city hummed along. But then I woke up in the vale, in the kitchen that smelled of iron and thyme, and I saw the taster woman by firelight, and my chest did something painful and wonderful, like a fist unclenching after years.

She has the queen's face. She doesn't know it yet. Or maybe she does, deep down, the way I know Maretta's loaves and the soldiers' dents-that-never-were. Some knowledge isn't learned. It's inherited, like eye color, like scars.

The queen rises when I come in. She always rises. Queens are not supposed to rise for anyone, and this queen rises for a child every night, and that tells you almost everything about why Vhalen had to die.

"You're fading," she says. She says it gently, the way you'd tell someone their shoe is untied.

"I know," I say.

"Show me your hands."

I show her my hands. They are still hands. Mostly. If you look through them toward the window-light, the light comes through in places it shouldn't, little holes opening up between my fingers like the gaps in frost ferns. Last week the holes were smaller. I count them sometimes, in the morning, back in the real world, holding my fingers up against Sable's kitchen candle. Nine holes last week. Eleven yesterday.

"Ink in water," the queen says, and smiles, and her smile is the worst thing in the dream, worse than the dark under the city, because it is kind and it is sorry and it does not stop what is happening to me. "That's what we are now, all of us. A drop of ink in a great grey glass of water. Beautiful while it lasts."

"How long?" I ask. I ask every night. Every night she answers differently, and every answer is true, which is the cruelest arithmetic I know.

"Thirteen turns," she says tonight. "The moon will lie thirteen times before it tells the truth once. When it finishes its lying—" she spreads her hands, and the throne-room light bends toward her palms like plants toward sun, "—the ink finishes spreading. Either the water becomes us, or we become the water. There is no third thing. There never was. That was our sin, in the end. We thought there was always a third thing."

* * *

Here is what the dream does not let me forget, no matter how many towers of glass it builds for me first:

Underneath everything lovely, there is the door.

You reach it by going down. Down past the honey-fountain courtyards, down past the roots of the glass towers where the moonstone cobbles give way to raw black stone that remembers being a mountain, down and down until the humming of the streets fades and what replaces it is a sound like breathing. Slow breathing. Patient breathing. The breathing of something that has been waiting two hundred years and is very, very good at it.

The Door of Thirteenth Moons is not made of anything. That is the first thing everyone gets wrong about it, if anyone ever thinks of it at all, which almost no one does, because erasing a kingdom means erasing its doors too. It is not wood or stone or metal. It is an absence shaped like a threshold. It is the shape of the space where a country used to be. Standing in front of it feels like standing in front of a mirror that shows the room behind you instead of your face.

And set into the nothing of it, like jewels in a band of shadow, are thirteen moons. Small ones. Silver-white, each a little dimmer than the last, arranged in an arc from brightest to nearly gone. Twelve of them are closed eyes. The thirteenth is barely a sliver, a fingernail paring of light, and every night when I come down here it is thinner than the night before.

When the thirteenth sliver goes out, the door goes with it. And everything the door is holding — which is everything, which is Maretta and her bird-shaped loaves and the soldiers and the stone-players and the queen and the humming streets and the color *selleth* — everything goes out with it.

Unless someone opens it from the other side first.

That's me. I'm the other side. I'm the knock.

I stand in front of the door and I press my palm against the absence and it presses back, soft as a forehead, and somewhere far away — in a kitchen that smells of iron and thyme, in a valley where magic is not allowed — I feel my own hand twitch against a blanket in my sleep.

"Soon," the door breathes. Doors shouldn't talk. This one only says one word, and it says it the way the tide says *shore*.

"Not soon," I tell it. "Someone has to find the way. She's finding it. The one with the queen's face. She's slow but she's stubborn, and stubborn is better than fast for this kind of walking."

The door breathes. The thirteenth sliver dims by a hair's width. Somewhere above us, twelve closed moons keep their counsel.

* * *

There are other things in the dream besides the beautiful ones. I should say that. The erased kingdom is not only glass towers and waving bakers. Erasure has a texture, and the texture is teeth.

Sometimes the streets empty. Not gradually — all at once, mid-wave, mid-laugh, the way a candle goes out when you pinch it. And then the city belongs to the things that were folded away along with the grief. The kingdom hid its worst memories in the same place it hid its best, because there was only one hiding place, and the hiding place was me.

I meet them in the empty streets. They look like people who have been described badly. A man whose face is a rumor. Women walking backward with their eyes shut, singing a song that makes the cobblestones bleed light. Once, something enormous moving between the towers, too big to see all at once, wearing the dark like a coat it hadn't finished growing into. It stopped when it sensed me. It inclined what might have been its head.

"*Little house,*" it said. "*Little lantern. How much longer can you hold us all?*"

"As long as I have to," I said, in the brave voice I use in dreams, which is braver than my waking voice because dreams haven't taught me better yet.

It laughed, and the laugh shook snow off roofs that weren't there anymore, and it said: "*Every vessel breaks. The question is only what spills.*"

Then the people came back, mid-wave, mid-laugh, and nobody remembered, and neither did I, mostly, except dreams keep a cellar where waking minds don't think to look, and I know the thing in the dark is down there right now, waiting under the city with the door, patient as tide.

* * *

I wake the way you wake from cold water.

Not all at once. First the edges. First the kitchen comes back in pieces: the tick of the stove cooling, the smell of thyme and iron, the particular dark of a valley where magic cannot follow. Then the weight of blankets. Then — last, always last — my own body, reporting in like a soldier after a bad patrol. *Present. Barely.*

Sable is asleep in the chair by the stove. She sleeps sitting up, one hand curled near the poker, even here, even in the safest room in the vale. Tasters, I am learning, do not get to stop being tasters. Their bodies stay at the table forever.

I watch her breathe and I do the counting. Fingers up against the banked coals' faint glow. Twelve holes tonight. The light comes through my left hand like a colander now, little stars punched in a child's skin. I put my hands away quickly, under the blanket, the way you hide a broken cup before company arrives.

Here is the thing I have not told her. Here is the thing pressing on my ribs like a swallowed stone:

Each night I spend in the dream, I bring less of myself back. It is not that the dream takes something. It is that I *am* something being spent. Every tower of glass I walk through costs a coin I cannot mint more of. The kingdom folded itself into me to survive, yes — but survival was never the plan. Delay was the plan. A message tucked into a bottle and thrown across two centuries, and bottles are not meant to last, and the sea is patient, and the sea is *me*, dissolving myself from the inside, ink in water, ink in water.

I am not a messenger. I finally understand that, in the small hours, watching Sable sleep. A messenger delivers a thing and walks away whole. I am the message itself. The words are written on my skin and the skin is running out. When the last of me fades, the message dies unread, and Vhalen doesn't get a second death — it gets a forgetting so complete that even its dying will never have happened.

Two hundred years ago, my grandmother — no. Not mine. *Hers*, the taster's, the Kavanagh line's — carried a spell-child out of a burning border and into a magicless valley, because magicless was the one place the hunters would never think to look. She traded everything for one chance: that someday, someday, someone with the queen's face would be standing in a kitchen when the bottle washed ashore.

The chance is standing in the chair, asleep, with her hand near the poker.

But chances are not certainties, and doors need finding, and moons need turning, and I need to last long enough for both, and tonight I counted twelve holes in my hands where last week there were nine.

* * *

In the morning I try to say something about it. I open my mouth and what comes out is Vhalenic, the old tongue, the dead tongue, the language that lives in me the way blood lives in a body — and Sable goes still over the porridge pot, and her eyes do the thing where they go somewhere else for a moment, somewhere with a priest and a dying smile in it.

"What did you say?" she asks. Her voice is careful. Careful voices are how she says *afraid* without spending the word.

I don't know how to make my mouth shape her tongue for this part. Some meanings are too heavy to carry across. So I do the next best thing. I take her hand — the taster's hand, the poisoned, disciplined, scarred-at-the-knuckles hand — and I press it flat against my chest, over the sternum, where a heartbeat should be steady.

She frowns. Counts. I watch her count.

My heart skips. Not the skipping of a frightened child. The skipping of a clock winding down. Beat, beat, beat, *nothing*, beat — a rest in the music, a hole in the cloth, growing a little wider every morning, keeping time with the thinning sliver of the thirteenth moon.

"Oh," Sable says. Very quietly. The porridge burns behind her and she doesn't turn around. "Oh, *no*."

And far away, under a city of glass, in front of a door made of nothing, the thirteenth moon loses another hair's width of silver, and something enormous in the dark between the towers lifts its head, and smiles with whatever smiles are made of down there, and begins — softly, patiently, in a voice like snow falling upward — to hum.
