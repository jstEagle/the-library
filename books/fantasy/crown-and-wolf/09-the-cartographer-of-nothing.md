## 9. The Cartographer of Nothing

Mother Ilsen's notes were written in three hands, and none of them were hers.

I'd worked that out on the second night of the road, by firelight, with Wren asleep against my ribs like a coal I was afraid to drop. The first hand was the priest's own—cramped, pious, the letters of a woman who'd spent sixty years copying scripture and resented every minute of it. The second hand belonged to someone older; the ink had gone brown and the phrasing had the musty confidence of a person quoting things they believed everyone knew. *The Door stands where the mapmaker wept,* that hand said. *Ask the one who drew the nothing.*

The third hand was mine.

I hadn't meant to write in a dead woman's notebook. But somewhere past the vale's edge, on the night Wren stopped speaking entirely—outside the boundary, magic flooding back into the world like sound after deafness, and the child going silent as a held breath—I'd found myself scratching questions into the margins because the silence needed answering. *Where is the door? What is a thirteenth turn? Why her?* My handwriting looked frightened even to me.

Wren hadn't spoken since we crossed the line. Inside the vale, where magic couldn't follow, the child had babbled Vhalenic like water over stones. Outside it, nothing. Not muteness—I'd checked, the way you check, holding a candle to small lips, watching for breath. Just silence. As if the words themselves were made of something this world wouldn't carry.

So: the mapmaker who wept. Ask the one who drew the nothing.

It took me four days and most of my remaining coin to find him, and when I did, he was exactly where the notes said he'd be, which told me Mother Ilsen had visited him too, and recently enough that the trail was still warm.

They kept Osk Merrow in the debtors' wing of the Greyward gaol in Corrowmere, which is not a prison so much as a waiting room for people the law has finished with but hasn't yet decided how to embarrass. Debtors could receive visitors. Debtors could, in theory, work off their debts. And debtors, unlike felons, could be bribed, which is why I stood at the warden's desk sliding across a purse I could ill afford, wearing the face I wore for kings' envoys—the pleasant, forgettable one—and saying, "I'm his niece."

"You don't look like his niece," the warden said, weighing the purse in his palm without looking at it. He was a man built like a door left slightly ajar.

"I take after my mother's side."

He grunted, which in Corrowmere apparently constituted a birth certificate, and led me down a corridor that smelled of wet stone and old cabbage to a cell that had, at least, a window.

Osk Merrow sat on the cot with his back against the wall and his legs crossed like a man meditating, except that what he was actually doing was drawing. On the wall. In charcoal he'd clearly stolen from somewhere, or been given by someone kind, or ground from the fire-blackened stone itself with the patience of the truly obsessed. He didn't look up when we came in. He was drawing a coastline.

"Uncle," I said.

"It's a girl," he said, to no one, still drawing. "The warden always sends girls. He thinks I'll be embarrassed." He finished a curve, blew charcoal dust off it, and finally turned his head. He was perhaps fifty, with a beard that had given up any pretense of grooming and eyes the pale grey of paper left out in weather. They went to Wren first. They stayed on Wren a long moment. Then they came to me, and something in them sharpened.

"You're not his niece either," he said. "You're the one Ilsen wrote to me about. Three years ago. A letter asking whether I'd ever charted a valley that eats roads." He smiled, and it was not a friendly smile; it was the smile of a man who has been right for so long that being right has become a wound. "I told her yes. She never wrote back."

"She died," I said. "Last month. She died smiling, if that helps."

"It doesn't," he said. "But thank you for the precision. Cartographers appreciate precision." He set down his charcoal and folded his hands. "Who's the child?"

"A message," I said. "Running out of time."

Something moved behind his eyes—not pity; I'd have distrusted pity—but recognition. The look of a man hearing his own language spoken badly by a stranger.

"The notes say you drew the nothing," I said. "That you mapped places that don't exist."

"They exist," Osk said mildly. "That was rather the whole problem."

* * *

Here is what the guild records will tell you, if you can get a clerk drunk enough to recite them: forty years ago, a junior cartographer named Osk Merrow submitted for guild certification a survey of the northern coast—a stretch of shoreline between Cape Verity and the Salt Flats that every other map in the kingdom rendered as blank space, marked only with the cartographer's traditional confession: *here be nothing.*

Merrow's survey showed a bay. A harbor town. A road running inland toward mountains that appeared on no other chart. He named the town, provisionally, Thirteenthmoon, because he claimed he'd arrived there on a night when the moon was wrong—full, and then fuller, impossibly so, a moon making a turn the sky doesn't allow. The guild examined his instruments. They examined his eyes. They examined, eventually, his sanity, and finding all three suspect, they struck his name from the rolls, revoked his certification, and fined him into the debtors' wing, where he had remained ever since, drawing coastlines on walls.

"That's the official version," Osk said. We were sitting on the floor now, because there was nowhere else, and Wren was asleep again in my lap, breathing that thin, deliberate breath. "Would you like mine?"

"Yes."

"My version is that I was twenty-six years old, I had a good theodolite, better eyes, and a patron who wanted the northern coast surveyed because he suspected the Crown was hiding a harbor there for smuggling purposes. Which, incidentally, they were. I walked eleven days up a coast that every map says isn't there, and on the twelfth day I came over a headland and looked down at a town with white towers and a harbor full of ships that flew no flag I knew, and the moon overhead was full, and then—" he paused, choosing the word with a craftsman's care, "—*more* than full. It had a ring around it like a coin seen edge-on. A thirteenth turn. I stayed three days. I drew everything. The people were kind. They spoke a language I didn't know and understood mine perfectly. And on the fourth morning I woke up on the beach at Cape Verity with dry boots, an empty inkwell, and three days of drawings that the guild declared impossible, forged, or mad, in ascending order of politeness."

"And were they? Forged?"

"I traced them myself under guild supervision," Osk said. "Every line. If they're forged, I forged them in my sleep, twice, with different hands." He leaned forward. His voice dropped, not for drama—for accuracy. "Here is what I learned in that cell, girl, and it took me thirty years to learn it, so listen: the guild wasn't wrong that the place doesn't exist. They were wrong about what that means. There are two ways for a thing not to exist. One is that it never was. The other is that it was *made* not to be—and the second kind leaves scars. Edges. Places where the world was sewn shut too fast. I've spent my life mapping those seams. Do you know what they call me in the guild annals?"

"I've heard," I said. "The Cartographer of Nothing."

"They think it's an insult," he said, with enormous satisfaction. "It's the most accurate title anyone's ever given me."

* * *

I told him then. Not all of it—I kept back the vault, the name Kavanagh, the word the child had spoken to me in Mother Ilsen's kitchen—but most of it. The child speaking a dead tongue inside a valley where dead tongues should be impossible. The dying priest naming it High Vhalenic. The kingdom struck from every map and memory two centuries ago. The thirteenth turn beginning, and ending at winter solstice, and a child fading like ink in water with each night between.

Osk listened the way I imagine he listened to the sea: completely, without interruption, cataloguing.

When I finished, he was quiet for a long time. Then he got up, went to the far wall of his cell—the one the warden's corridor light didn't reach—and began scrubbing at the charcoal with his sleeve. Underneath, I realized, there were older layers. Years of them. Coastlines under coastlines. And beneath all of them, drawn so many times the stone itself was grooved with the lines, a shape I recognized from Mother Ilsen's notes: a door. Tall, narrow, arched at the top like a crescent, standing alone on a featureless plain.

"Thirteenthmoon," he said, tapping the grooves. "The town I found. Or the shell of it—the town is the antechamber. The door stands beyond it, on the flats, where the tide goes out farther than the eye can follow. I saw it once, at distance, on my last morning there. I thought it was a monument." His finger traced the crescent arch. "Now I know better. Now I know it's a *door*, and I know why the moon above it was wrong, and I know—" he stopped.

"What?"

"Why they erased it." He turned around. In the dim light his grey eyes looked almost colorless, like a map with the legend torn away. "Your kingdom-that-wasn't, girl. Your Vhalen. Someone erased it from the outside in, or it erased itself from the inside out—Ilsen's letter suggested she believed the latter, and priests are usually right about self-inflicted wounds—but either way, the erasure had to be anchored. You cannot unmake a country everywhere at once. You unmake it at its edges and pull inward. And the anchor, the last point of contact, the place where the seam was tied off—" he tapped the door again, "—is that. The Door of Thirteenth Moons. It's not a way in. It's the knot."

"Can it be untied?"

"Oh, knots can always be untied," Osk said. "The question is what happens to the rope."

Wren stirred in my lap. Opened her eyes—those eyes that had looked at me with two centuries of patience behind them—and looked at Osk's wall, at the grooved outline of the door, and made a sound. Small. Almost nothing. The first sound she'd made since we crossed the vale's edge.

Osk went very still.

"That," he said quietly, "is Vhalenic."

"You know it?"

"I know the *sound* of it. From Thirteenthmoon. Three days of a language I couldn't parse and never forgot." He crouched down slowly, the way you approach a bird, until he was at the child's level. "What did she say?"

"I don't know. She doesn't speak outside the vale. That might be the first sound she's made in weeks."

"Then it wasn't speech," Osk said softly. "It was recognition. She knows this room." He looked up at me, and for the first time the wry armor dropped, and underneath was just a man, tired and certain. "She's been here before, hasn't she. Or something in her has. This is a message running out of time, you said. Messages need routes. I spent forty years being punished for insisting that my route was real." He straightened, wincing, and stuck out a charcoal-blackened hand. "Get me out of here, and I'll take you to the door."

"There's a condition," I said. "I read your file. The guild offered you reinstatement years ago—quietly, through an intermediary—if you'd sign a retraction. Recant the survey. Admit the coast was nothing. You refused. You chose the cell."

"I did."

"So here's my price, and it's the only one I'll pay: I'm not going to flatter you, or thank you, or tell you you're brave. I'm going to do the thing the guild wouldn't do." I took his hand. It was warm, and stained, and steady. "Your maps were true, Osk Merrow. The coast exists. The town exists. The moon turned thirteen times over it while you watched, and you drew what you saw, and you were right, and they were wrong, and they broke your life for it. Your maps were true."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that I heard the warden cough somewhere down the corridor, and a rat relocate its household, and Wren breathe.

Osk Merrow, disgraced cartographer of nothing, sat down heavily on his cot and put his face in his clean hand, and his shoulders shook once, twice, and then were still.

"Forty years," he said, muffled. "Forty years, and it takes a poison-taster and a mute child nine minutes." He wiped his eyes with the heel of his palm, leaving a grey smear across his cheekbone like a border on a chart. "Right. Well." He stood, and something had changed in how he stood—some load redistributed, some keel corrected. "We'll need the original survey plates. They weren't destroyed, whatever the guild claims—they're in the guildhall archive, sealed, because Master Aldous Vey couldn't bring himself to burn work that good. Vanity. The Lord Cartographer has always had vanity where I'm concerned." He was already pacing, already drawing routes in the air. "And we'll need to reach the coast before solstice, which means leaving tonight, which means bribing the warden twice, which means—"

"Which means you'll need more than my remaining coin."

"Which means," Osk said, turning, and the grin that split the grey smear on his face was young and feral and forty years overdue, "that we're going to rob the guild that ruined me, and I confess I have never in my life been happier."

Behind us, soft as snowfall, Wren laughed—one syllable, in a dead tongue, in a debtor's cell—and both of us turned to look at her, and neither of us asked what she'd said.

Some messages, I was learning, you carry without opening.

We left Corrowmere before the moon rose. It hung low over the eastern hills as we took the coast road north, and I could have sworn—it was probably exhaustion, probably nothing—that it looked a little rounder than it had the night before.

A little closer to full.

A little closer to thirteen.
