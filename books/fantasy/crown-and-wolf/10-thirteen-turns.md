## 10. Thirteen Turns

Osk kept his prison cell the way other men kept libraries, which is to say: badly, obsessively, and with an alarming quantity of ink.

They'd been released into a borrowed room above a chandler's shop in the low town of Greyharbor, three days' walk from Mother Ilsen's grave, and Osk had spent the first hour of freedom not weeping with joy or kissing the ground but requisitioning paper. He'd charmed the chandler's widow out of her late husband's account books, scraped them clean with a knife, and filled them again in a hand so small and dense it looked like the work of a very literate ant. Wren sat on the floor amid the shavings, drawing moons. Dozens of moons. Circles within circles, all of them wrong in a way that made my teeth ache to look at.

"Right," Osk said, and stabbed the knife into the table to hold his place in a ledger. "You want to know how the door works. So I'm going to tell you how the door works, and you're going to want to interrupt, and I'd ask you not to, because I have rehearsed this speech in a cell for eleven years to an audience of rats, and the rats never once interrupted me. I miss them terribly."

"Osk."

"Point made. You interrupt like a rat too. Very well." He flipped the ledger open. "First lesson. What is a month?"

I stared at him. "I'm a food taster, not a scholar."

"You're a Vhalen-blooded heir to an erased throne, but yes, let's start smaller. A month. What is it?"

"The moon goes around. It waxes, it wanes, it comes back to where it started. That's a month."

"Beautiful. Wrong, but beautiful. The moon doesn't come back to where it started." He drew a circle, then a smaller circle crossing its path. "The moon's road is a ring, and the sky is a road too, and they don't sit still for each other. So when the moon returns to the same shape—full to full, new to new—it hasn't returned to the same *place*. Not quite. It's a little behind. A little short." He tapped the gap between his two circles. "Every month, the moon falls short of closing its own circle. Every month, a little gap. Do you see?"

"A gap in what?"

"In the sky, girl. In the *seam* of the sky. And a door, as any fool who has ever built one knows, is only as good as the gap you cut for it."

He said it the way I'd say *the poison is in the wine, not the bread*—the flat certainty of a man stating his trade. I looked at Wren. Wren looked at the moons on the floor and, very slowly, drew one more.

"Thirteen," the child said. It was one of the few words Wren still had, in my tongue. The others were all Vhalenic, all locked away beyond the vale's edge like birds in a covered cage. Outside the valley, Wren barely spoke at all. Outside the valley, Wren was fading—thinner every day, paler, the way a coal goes grey before it's ash. I'd stopped letting myself measure it.

"Thirteen," Osk agreed. "Twelve turns, and the moon has fallen short every time, and the seam stays shut. But a thirteenth turn—" his knife-hand traced the ledger, circle over circle over circle, a spiral of falling-short gaps, "—a thirteenth turn doesn't fall short. The errors cancel. The arithmetic closes. Once in a very great while, the moon comes home to the exact place it left, and the seam of the sky is a perfect gap, and for one night—*one*—the Door of Thirteenth Moons has something to open into."

"And when is that night?"

He looked up at me, and for once the mad cartographer was gone and there was just a tired man who had spent eleven years being right in a place where being right was a crime.

"It's begun," he said. "The thirteenth month. It began the night the child knocked on your door."

* * *

I want to say I took it well. I want to say I stood tall and asked sensible follow-up questions like a heroine in a ballad.

What I actually did was sit down on a crate of candle-wicks and put my head between my knees, because I am a woman whose entire life has been built on the principle that harm arrives in small, measured doses, and Osk had just handed me the whole bottle.

"Say the rest," I said, into my knees. "All of it. I'd rather be gutted once."

"The thirteenth month ends at the winter solstice. Thirty days from tonight, give or take what the sky feels like giving. The door opens that night and no other. Miss it, and you'll wait—" he did arithmetic with his lips, "—nineteen years for the next one. And the child won't be here in nineteen years. The child may not be here in *thirty days*."

"Why not?" I knew why not. I'd watched it happen every morning: Wren's wrists thinner, Wren's eyes going from brown to a brown so pale it was nearly the color of paper. But I made him say it. I have always believed you should make the world say the cruel things out loud, because unsaid, they grow teeth.

Osk was gentler than I expected. "The child *is* the spell, Sable. The last spell Vhalen ever cast. A whole kingdom folded down into one small body and sent through the seam before it closed. But a spell needs a country to anchor to, and Vhalen is—" he waved the knife at the air, at everything, "—*withheld*. Erased. Not dead, mind. Dead things rot and feed the ground. Vhalen was never allowed to die. It was folded shut, and it's been holding its breath for two hundred years, and the child is the breath it's holding." He set the knife down. "The spell is running out of the strength to be a child. When the thirteenth turn completes, either the door opens and the spell goes home—or the spell simply... stops. Like a candle in a room with no air."

"And if I open the door," I said slowly, "and walk through, and take my crown, and restore Vhalen whole—then Wren lives."

"Then Vhalen lives," Osk corrected. "And Wren is Vhalen. And so are you, though you've had the good fortune of being too ignorant to fade." He smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. "Ignorance is the finest preservative, my lady. Ask any pickle."

* * *

That night I sat by the chandler's narrow window and watched the moon rise over Greyharbor's crooked roofs, and I hated it a little, which was new. I'd always liked the moon. It was one of the few reliable things in a taster's life—no poison yet invented that could sour moonlight.

Now it was a clock. It had always been a clock. It had been counting down since the night a barefoot child stood in my kitchen speaking the language of the dead, and I'd been too busy being terrified to notice the sky ticking.

Wren climbed into my lap without asking, the way children do when they've decided you belong to them. The child had grown lighter. That was the detail I couldn't shake—lighter, as though something were being subtracted nightly, spoonful by spoonful. I knew about subtraction. I'd spent my life subtracting myself, small dose by small dose, so that a king I'd never met could eat his supper in safety. I knew exactly what it looked like when a person was spent in service of something larger.

"Tell me a story," Wren said. One of the stock phrases, learned from me, in my tongue. The child's Vhalenic was locked away beyond the vale, but Wren had begun hoarding my language like a starving thing hoards bread, and every new word felt less like progress and more like a trade—something gained here, something lost there.

"Once," I said, "there was a taster who was very good at her work, and very bad at everything else."

"Bad," Wren agreed solemnly, and I pinched the child's ear, and we both pretended that was a whole conversation.

I thought about the arithmetic of it. Thirty days. I'd survived on thirty-day rations in my head for years—thirty days of stores, thirty days between letters from the capital, thirty days of any danger before it either passed or killed you. Thirty days was a workable number. Thirty days was a number you could put your shoulder against.

The trouble was the shape of the work. To open the door, I needed Osk's maps, and Osk's maps needed the coast, and the coast was a fortnight's hard travel with a fading child in winter. And between here and there stood a king who dreamed of a door and died of it, and a sorcerer who had smiled at me with too much knowledge in his face, and a whole apparatus of riders and wards and law built by men who had decided, two centuries ago, that Vhalen must never come back—and who had, I suspected, very good reasons I hadn't heard yet.

"Osk," I said through the dark. He was asleep sitting up, the way prisoners sleep, ready to wake. "Your maps. The ones that got you imprisoned. The door—did you find it because you were looking, or did it find you?"

A long silence. Then, wry as ever: "Does it matter? You've already decided to go. I can hear you deciding. It sounds like a woman arguing with a window."

"I'm not arguing with a window."

"You're always arguing with a window. It's how you think. I've watched." He shifted. "For the record: I was mapping the salt coast, and there was a place where my instruments all agreed on a nothing. A perfect nothing, a hole in the world's arithmetic, the same way the moon's months are a hole. I drew it. They called it madness. Eleven years, they called it madness." A pause. "The child drew it too. Did you see? On the floor. The moons with the gaps. Wren drew my nothing without ever being taught it. So either we're both mad, or the door has been calling its own witnesses."

"And which do you believe?"

"The one that keeps me employed," he said, and slept.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat with Wren breathing against my collarbone and did what tasters do, which is to say I took inventory of the poison.

Here is what I had: a disgraced cartographer with a hole in the world's arithmetic and eleven years of grievance to burn as fuel. A child who was a kingdom's last breath and who had, for reasons I still didn't fully understand, chosen my door out of every door in the vale. A name—Kavanagh of Vhalen—that sat in my chest like a swallowed coal. And thirty days.

Here is what I did not have: a plan past the city gates, a weapon, money beyond a taster's savings, or any certainty that opening the door would save Wren rather than simply relocate the dying. Osk had been very careful with his words. *Either the door opens and the spell goes home—or the spell stops.* He had not promised that going home meant living. He had not promised anything, because cartographers map the world; they do not negotiate with it.

And beneath all of it, the thought I kept turning over like a stone with something underneath: *Vhalen was erased on purpose.* Mother Ilsen had said it with her dying breath, smiling. The kingdom had done it to itself, or been done, and either way the erasure had held for two hundred years, which meant someone had wanted it to hold. Kings had died within a year of their coronations, dreaming of doors. That wasn't accident. That was a wall with bodies hung on it, a warning written in the only language kings read.

Every instinct I had—the instinct that had kept me alive through twenty years of small controlled harms—said: *this is the dish that kills. Walk away. Let the child fade gently somewhere warm. Grieve and live.*

But I had also spent twenty years watching other people eat first.

I looked down at Wren, asleep, translucent at the edges in the moonlight—actually translucent, the window's glow coming faintly *through* the child's hand where it lay on my arm, and my heart did something that wasn't beating so much as breaking in rhythm.

"Thirty days," I told the moon. "Fine. I've eaten worse on a schedule."

The moon, being a moon, said nothing. But it was full that night, round and white and punctual, and I found I couldn't hate it after all. It was falling short, month after month, century after century, and it kept going anyway. Kept circling. Kept trying to come home to the exact place it had left.

I understood that, suddenly, completely.

I woke Osk an hour before dawn, because if the clock had started, I saw no reason to let the man sleep through his own deadline.

"Draw me the coast," I said. "All of it. Every nothing on the way."

He was already reaching for the ink.
