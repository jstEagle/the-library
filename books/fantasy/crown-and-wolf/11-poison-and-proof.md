## 11. Poison and Proof

There is a particular silence a banquet hall makes just before the king is served. Two hundred courtiers holding their breath without knowing it, the clink of silver stilled by ceremony, and underneath it all the low hum of my own heartbeat, which has been my metronome since I was nine years old and swallowed my first dish on purpose.

I had been at court under the name Merrin Vale for eleven days. Long enough to learn that the under-steward of the west tables watered the wine when he thought no one watched, that the Duchess of Harrow laughed only with her teeth, and that King Aldric ate sparingly at state functions—two bites of each course, three if he was nervous. Tonight, watching his hands around the stem of his goblet, I made it four courses' worth of nervous.

Wren was two floors up in my narrow servant's room, sleeping under a ward-sigil Osk had drawn for me in charcoal, half-remembered from a map margin. I told myself the distance was safety. I told myself a great many things these days; lying to yourself is a skill, like poison-tasting, that improves with practice.

"Kitchen's sent up the quail in ash-glaze," muttered Breck, the head taster, appearing at my elbow. He was sixty, gouty, and had survived three reigns through the simple strategy of never being interesting. "You're on it. My knee's acting."

Breck's knee acted whenever work appeared. I'd have found it endearing if I weren't about to eat his dinner.

The quail arrived on a platter of tarnished silver, glazed dark as wet slate, smelling of rosemary and something sweeter beneath—honey, maybe, or plum. I took the small ceremonial spoon, the one they gave tasters so the audience could watch us die politely, and I did what I had done ten thousand times before.

I put it in my mouth and waited.

* * *

You should understand something about immunity. It is not armor. Armor stops the blade at the surface. Immunity is more like knowing every beggar in the city by name—the poisons don't shock me anymore because my body has met their cousins, their brothers, their old war friends. Arsenic and I are nodding acquaintances. Hemlock owes me money. When a toxin touches my tongue, I feel it arrive like a stranger stepping into a lit room, and my blood says, *oh, you*, and closes around it.

What touched my tongue with the second spoonful was not a stranger.

It was not even, properly speaking, a poison.

The hall stayed golden and loud. The king said something to the ambassador from Cael Harrow that made the man's beard twitch. And inside me, something coiled against the back of my throat and said, in a voice like warm oil poured into my ear:

*Serve him the next portion. Your hand will not shake. You will be proud of your hand.*

I set down the spoon very carefully, the way you do when the floor has become a rumor.

Compulsion. A charm baked into the glaze—not enough to sicken, dosed for effect rather than death, riding in on some alchemical vehicle my stomach had never been asked to host. And yet my body did what sixty years of Kavanagh discipline had trained it to do: it met the thing, catalogued it, and *refused delivery*. The voice pressed again—*serve him, serve him*—and I felt it slide off me the way rain slides off waxed canvas. Whatever the charm wanted, it wanted it from someone whose blood could be argued with. Mine had been arguing back since childhood.

Which meant, I realized with a coldness spreading through my chest like spilled well-water, that whoever had done this had expected it to work on a normal taster. Or—

Or they had known exactly who would taste it, and this was a test.

I looked up. Lord Corvane stood at the king's left shoulder, robed in dove-grey, his hands folded, his face arranged into its usual expression of patient intelligence—the look of a man reading a document slightly ahead of everyone else in the room. He was not watching the king.

He was watching me.

* * *

Let me describe the arithmetic of the next ninety seconds, because it is the finest calculation I have ever performed, and I once calculated whether a suspected broth was worth spitting into a napkin while a duke watched.

If I said nothing: the charm presumably worked on whoever served the king next, or on Aldric directly through some mechanism I couldn't see, and a king died at a banquet, and the vale of suspects would include one false-named taster from nowhere.

If I cried poison: I saved him, and every eye in the room turned to me, including the grey ones already counting my breaths.

If I was wrong, I ended my career and probably my neck.

Aldric reached for the quail.

There is a moment in every taster's life—and most of us get only one—where the whole profession compresses itself into a single act. We spend decades swallowing harm so that this exact fork-lift never happens. My grandmother, I now knew, had done it for a queen in a country that no longer existed. Her mother before her. A dynasty of women whose entire art was dying invisibly so that someone else could eat in peace.

I did not die. I did something, for me, far more dramatic.

I dropped the platter.

Silver hit stone with a sound like a bell being murdered. Quail and glaze spread across the dais steps in a dark slick, and the music stopped, and two hundred faces turned with the beautiful synchronized stupidity of startled birds.

"Poison," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me; inside I was a kettle at full boil. "In the glaze. Not toxin—or not only. It turned my head the moment it touched me. Whoever tastes clean portions after this, search the kitchen's honey stores and the spice cellar both, and arrest whoever ordered tonight's menu changed. It was changed. Someone paid for it to be changed."

Silence. The kind with weight.

Then Corvane moved, and I understood why kings keep sorcerers the way ships keep anchors—you hope never to feel them working, but God help you when you need them. He raised one hand and the air over the ruined quail *shivered*. The glaze lifted off the meat in threads of faint violet light, curling upward like steam, forming briefly—a shape. A mouth. A sigil I didn't recognize but felt in my teeth anyway, the way you feel a word in a language you almost speak.

"A compulsion weave," Corvane said. His voice carried effortlessly without rising. "Third-rank work. Expensive." He turned his head toward me, slowly, and his eyes were doing something I didn't like at all: not surprise. Confirmation. "The Lady Merrin is to be commended. Few tasters could have caught a charm at all. Fewer still could have refused it."

*Fewer still.* The words sat in my stomach beside the poison.

"How few?" I asked, and heard too late how sharp my voice came out.

Corvane smiled the way winter smiles at the last leaf. "Fewer than are standing in this room."

* * *

They arrested the sous-chef, Pellam, a soft-bodied man who wept so thoroughly during questioning that even the captain of the guard looked embarrassed on his behalf. Whether he was guilty or merely purchasable, I couldn't say; purchased men rarely know what they're purchasing. The honey stores were seized. The kitchen was turned over like a pocket.

And I became, for one evening, the most famous woman in the palace.

Courtiers who hadn't acknowledged my existence in eleven days suddenly remembered my name—which was, of course, the wrong name, which made the attention its own special torment. A countess pressed a ring into my hand. The ambassador from Cael Harrow toasted "the vigilant Miss Vale." Breck clapped me on the shoulder hard enough to bruise and said, "Knew you had it in you," which was a lie, delivered with such warmth I nearly forgave it.

Aldric summoned me himself.

I was led up through corridors that grew quieter and better-lit the higher we climbed, until we reached a study that smelled of ink, woodsmoke, and sleeplessness. The king stood at his window with his back to me, and up close, without the crown and the forty feet of dais between us, he looked exactly like what he was: a tired man in his forties who did not eat enough.

"You knew," he said, turning. "Before anyone. Before the plate hit the floor. Your face when you stood—it wasn't fright. It was certainty."

"Tasters are trained to be certain quickly, Majesty. Certainty is the whole trade. A hesitant taster is an unemployed taster."

"That isn't what I asked."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who spends his life surrounded by people answering questions he hasn't asked, meeting the rare person who declines to do it.

"Corvane says you shouldn't exist," Aldric said quietly.

"Lord Corvane says a great many things. That's what counselors are for. Kings exist, though. I've seen one recently. Rather memorable chin."

The corner of his mouth moved. Just barely. But it moved, and I thought: *there is a person in there, under the crown, and he is drowning.*

"Eat with me tomorrow," he said. "Not at banquet. Here. Small table. I want to taste my own food again without performing gratitude to a room full of people who want my chair." He paused. "And I want to ask you things, Miss Vale, that I can't ask in front of my sorcerer."

"Majesty." I bowed, heart hammering out a rhythm that had nothing to do with toxins. "I live to taste."

* * *

I did not sleep that night.

Partly this was Wren, who woke crying from a dream she couldn't describe except to press her small hands flat against her own arms as though checking she was still there. I held her until her breathing evened, and her skin—which had worried me for days, gone faintly translucent at the wrists, like paper held to light—warmed back to ordinary child-flesh. She whispered something in High Vhalenic as she drifted off. I've had two months of lessons from a dead priest's notes and a mad cartographer, but I didn't need translation for that one. Ilsen had taught it to me early, because it was the first word of Vhalen any child learned:

*Mother.*

Partly, I confess, it was the honey-colored thread of the evening replaying itself behind my eyes. *Fewer than are standing in this room.* Corvane had looked at me across that ruined quail and he had not been surprised, and a man who is never surprised is a man who has already read to the end of the book.

So near dawn, when the knock came—three soft raps, the rhythm Osk used—I was already dressed and had a knife in my sleeve, because paranoia, like immunity, is a discipline.

It wasn't Osk.

It was a page, perhaps twelve, holding a folded note sealed with grey wax. No crest. He handed it over and fled as though the note might bite him too.

I broke the seal. Inside, a single line, in a hand so elegant it was nearly hostile:

*A Vhalenic charm cannot bind a Vhalenic tongue. You tasted it and lived because it recognized its maker's blood. Come to my chambers at the ninth bell, and we will discuss what else your blood recognizes.—C.*

I read it twice. Then I read it a third time, and the wry commentary that usually narrates my life went very quiet, because beneath the courtesy, the message was plain as a knife laid on a table:

*I know what you are. I have known since the night you walked through my gate. And now I also know you can survive things meant to break ordinary people—which means you, Sable Kavanagh, last heir of a dead kingdom, are the only person in this palace who could have eaten that quail and kept her wits. Which means either you just saved my king by accident,*

*—or you are exactly the key I have been looking for.*

Through the frost-feathered window, the moon hung low and wrong, a hair further along its arc than any honest moon should travel. Thirteen turns, Osk had said. Eleven remaining.

Somewhere below, in a cell, a weeping sous-chef was taking the blame for a plot that needed no chef at all—only a sorcerer, a glaze, and a girl who could not be charmed.

Ninth bell was in three hours.

I put on my shoes and went to find out what my blood recognized.
