## 14. Betrayal at Moonrise

I had been in the capital long enough to learn its rhythms—the way the palace exhaled at dusk, servants streaming down to the lower city like water finding its level; the way the guards changed at the ninth bell and for exactly one hundred heartbeats the corridors belonged to no one; the way Lord Corvane's boots never made a sound on marble, which should have been my first warning, because a man who moves quietly is a man who has practiced being unheard.

The night it all came due, I was carrying Wren's supper up from the kitchens myself. I had stopped trusting anyone else's hands weeks ago. Rice, salted fish, a pear cut into moons—harmless food, food with no history, because Wren ate like a bird that had learned not to trust branches. The child sat cross-legged on the floor of our borrowed room, drawing shapes in spilled flour. Vhalenic glyphs. The same one over and over.

"That's the word for *door*," I said, setting down the tray. "You've drawn it forty times."

Wren looked up at me with those eyes that were too old for the face they lived in, and drew it a forty-first time.

The knock came then. Three soft raps, courteous as a debt collector.

I knew before I opened it. Some part of me had known since the banquet, since the moment Corvane watched me spit out the compulsion charm and something behind his eyes had gone very still and very pleased, like a fisherman feeling the line go taut. You don't survive years of tasting by ignoring the flavor of a trap. But knowing a trap and not walking into it are different skills, and I have only ever been good at the first one.

"Mother Kess," Corvane said when I opened the door, using my false name with a small smile, savoring the lie of it. "May I come in? Or shall we conduct this in the corridor, where the walls are thinner and less loyal?"

Behind him stood four of the king's gray-cloaked wardens, and behind them, held by the wrist by a woman with kind eyes and a cruel grip, was Osk.

He looked terrible. He always looked terrible—it was practically his coat of arms—but tonight he looked terrified, and Osk frightened is a sight that curdles the stomach. His mouth was moving, shaping words at me. I read them off his lips the way I'd once read poison off the surface of wine.

*Run.*

"Lord Corvane," I said, and kept my voice level the way I kept my hand steady over a suspect dish. "It's late for social calls."

"It's late for many things." He stepped past me into the room without invitation, the way powerful men do, as if doors were suggestions. His gaze found Wren in the corner and lingered there with an appetite I recognized. I had seen men look at delicacies that way. I had seen kings look at dishes that way. It is the look of someone deciding whether to eat now or save it for a better occasion.

"The child," he said. "You'll hand her over."

"Will I."

"You will." He turned, and the lamplight caught his face, and what I saw there wasn't cruelty, which I could have fought. It was certainty. Cruelty doubts itself sometimes. Certainty never does. "Let us dispense with the theater, Sable Kavanagh of Vhalen."

My name. My true name, spoken aloud in a palace where saying it was supposed to be impossible, where Ilsen had died with it on her lips, where the archives were sealed and the vault locked. The floor did not move, but I felt it tilt anyway.

"How—" I began.

"The banquet," he said, gently, horribly gently. "Did you truly believe that a charm aimed at King Aldric would be so crude? So detectable? That it would sit in a sauce like a beetle in soup, waiting for a taster to find it?" He shook his head, almost fondly. "I wrote that dish myself. Every ingredient. Every measure. A compulsion charm doesn't work on the man it's fed to, taster—it works on the woman who tastes it. It was meant to bind you. To open you. So that when the moment came, you would walk to me and give me the child and thank me for the honor."

The pear moons on the tray had gone cold. I stared at them as if they might explain themselves.

"But you didn't bind," he continued, circling the room slowly, and the wardens closed in behind him like a hand closing. "You *resisted*. Do you know how rare that is? How delicious? Your little immunity, your years of swallowing death in spoonfuls—it burned the charm out of you before it could take root. And in burning, it left a mark. A resonance. I've been able to find you anywhere since. Anywhere in this palace. Anywhere in this city." He smiled. "You thought you were hiding in the wolf's den. You were glowing in it."

Osk made a sound—not a word, just a noise, low in his throat—and the woman holding him twisted his arm until it became a grunt instead.

"Why?" I asked. Not *why me*. I was past why me. Why any of it. "Aldric trusts you. You have everything."

"I have nothing," Corvane said, and for one unguarded instant something moved under the certainty, something old and starved. "I have a kingdom that is dying of safety. Magic cannot enter the vale, and everywhere else it gutters like a candle in a draft. The sorcerers' art grows weaker every generation. We are priests of a god who has stopped listening." He gestured at Wren, and his voice went reverent, which was worse than hungry. "But there is a country that was erased rather than ended. A kingdom that folded itself out of the world while it was still full of power. All of it—every spell, every secret, every drop of Vhalen's light—is still in there. Preserved. Waiting. Behind a door."

"And you want me to open it."

"I want the child to open it. You are merely the key that turns the key." He extended one white-cuffed hand toward Wren. "Come along, little message. Your kingdom has been patient long enough."

And Wren—who had not spoken a word outside the vale, who had gone silent as a held breath the day we crossed the boundary—looked at Corvane's outstretched hand and said, in a voice like a bell cracking:

"No."

The lamps guttered. The flour glyphs on the floor flared green, every one of them, forty-one little doors burning cold fire, and the temperature of the room dropped so fast my breath came out silver. Magic. In the magic-dead capital, in a stone room three floors above the king's own bedchamber, magic answered a child's refusal.

Corvane didn't flinch. He'd expected this too. Of course he had. He'd had longer to prepare than I'd had to panic.

"Take them both," he said.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way disasters do, each fragment sharp and separate:

The nearest warden reached for me and I drove the supper tray's edge into his throat—not the steel corner, I'm not a killer, just the wood, hard, where the breath goes—and he folded with a sound like a dropped sack.

The second warden grabbed my hair and I remembered, absurdly, my grandmother's letters in the vault, *a Kavanagh dies for her queen*, and thought: *not tonight, and not for you,* and stamped his instep and tore free, losing more hair than dignity.

Someone shouted for silence and got none, because Wren was screaming now—a high, glass-breaking note that made the window frost over from the inside—and the third warden covered his ears and the fourth actually reached the child, actually got a hand on Wren's shoulder—

—and Osk broke free.

I will never know how. He was sixty years old and built like a question mark and he had spent eleven years in a cell for drawing coastlines that weren't there. But he threw himself across the room with a cartographer's fury, the fury of a man who had been told his whole life's work was lies, and he hit the fourth warden like a falling bookcase, and they went down together into the flour, and the green glyphs smeared under them into a single blazing smear that looked, for one strange heartbeat, like a map.

"Window," Osk gasped. "Sable. The *window*."

Three floors up. Winter. I looked at him. He looked back with the expression of a man who had already done the arithmetic and hated the answer.

"There's a ledge," he said. "There's always a ledge. I mapped this building when I still had a reputation. Trust the map."

Corvane had not moved through any of it. He stood by the door with his hands folded, watching, and that frightened me more than the wardens did, because a man who doesn't chase you is a man who knows where you're going.

"The solstice is in nine days, taster," he said, over the chaos, calm as a man discussing weather. "The moon completes its thirteenth turn whether you run or not. And I have your scent now—your blood-mark, your resonance. There is nowhere on this continent you can be that I cannot follow." He inclined his head, courteous to the end. "Do give my regards to the Door of Thirteenth Moons. I'll want it open when I arrive."

Then the room filled with smoke—not ordinary smoke, but the gray-cloaks' sleep-mist, sweet and heavy—and I made the choice that every choice in my life had been rehearsing for: I grabbed Wren, I grabbed Osk by the collar, and I went through the window into the dark.

* * *

The ledge was real. Osk's map was real. These facts saved our lives, and I intend to remind him of both at every opportunity for the rest of whatever life we manage to keep.

We went along the palace's shoulder like thieves, like spiders, like three fools bound together by necessity and frostbite. Below us, the courtyard rang with shouts. Above us, the moon hung fat and wrong, a day too full, swelling toward its impossible thirteenth turn like a bruise ripening.

Wren clung to my back with arms around my neck, lighter than a child should be. Lighter than Wren had been last week. I didn't let myself think about what that meant. There would be time for grief after we stopped falling.

We dropped the last ten feet into a hay cart—which I maintain was skill and not luck—and ran.

Not out the gates. Gates have names and lists and wardens. We went down, into the city's guts, through the tannery quarter where the stink covers all other traces, through the drain-tunnels Osk had mapped in his arrogant youth ("They asked me to chart the harbor approaches; I charted everything; that was the crime"), out through a culvert that debouched into the reed marshes beyond the walls like a secret being told.

Only when the palace lights were a smudge behind us did I stop, hands on knees, lungs full of marsh rot, and take inventory.

One fugitive taster. One half-blind disgraced cartographer wheezing beside me. One fading spell-child, silent again, staring back at the city with those ancient eyes.

And burning behind us—or so it felt, though nothing visible burned—every protection I had built. The false name. The post in the kitchens. The king's gratitude, that fragile coin I'd banked after the banquet. The law's boon, unspent. Aldric himself, who had granted me the vault against Corvane's counsel, who had begun—God help me—to *trust* me. By morning, Corvane would have shaped the story: the Vhalen witch, exposed at last, fleeing with the abomination she'd smuggled into the heart of the court. And Aldric, who feared the door more than anything alive, who dreamed of it every night of his reign, would believe it. Believing it would be a mercy. It would let him feel that his fear had been wisdom all along.

Every alliance, come due at once. This is the thing no one tells you about trust: it isn't a wall you build. It's a debt you carry. And debts get called.

"Sable." Osk touched my arm. His fingers were shaking—from cold, I told myself, though his eyes were fixed on the moon. "Nine days. He said nine days. Is he right?"

"He's right." I straightened. My legs ached. My scalp ached where the warden had pulled. Somewhere under my ribs, the old immunity hummed, the poison-tempered blood that had betrayed my hiding place and might yet save my life. "The door stands on the coast past the salt flats. Eleven days' hard travel if the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold."

"The weather never holds. We leave now."

Osk looked back at the palace one last time—at the library he'd never see again, the maps, the life. Then he sighed the sigh of a man adding a final annotation to a doomed manuscript.

"You know he'll follow us," he said. "Corvane. He wasn't bluffing about the blood-mark. He can taste you the way you taste poison."

"Then we'll give him indigestion." I lifted Wren onto my hip—so light, so terribly light—and turned south, toward the flats, toward the sea, toward a door that wanted opening and a man who wanted it opened for all the wrong reasons. "Keep walking, Osk. And if you ever map another building, map the exits first."

"I did map the exits first," he said, hobbling after me into the dark. "That's how we're alive."

Behind us, far away and getting farther, the thirteenth moon climbed, and somewhere beneath it a door began, patiently, to remember how to open.
