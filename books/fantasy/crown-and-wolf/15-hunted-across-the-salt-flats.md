## 15. Hunted Across the Salt Flats

The horses died on the third day.

I want to be precise about that, because everything afterward is measured against it. On the first day we were fugitives with horses, which is a romantic thing to be. On the second day we were fugitives with exhausted horses, which is merely a practical problem. On the third day the gray mare lay down in the salt grass, sighed like an old woman taking off her boots, and did not get up, and the black followed her within the hour, and after that we were just three small warm things crossing an enormous white nothing with the king's riders somewhere behind us and the sea somewhere ahead, and I learned that the difference between an adventure and a disaster is mostly a matter of horses.

"Cheerful thought," Osk said, tightening the straps of the pack he'd made from the saddlebags. "We could have been eaten by wolves instead. There's a dignity in wolves."

"There's a dignity in you noticing that," I said, and he grinned, and it cost him something to grin, and I loved him a little for spending it anyway.

Wren rode against my chest in a sling made from my old taster's coat, the one with the deep pockets where I used to carry antidotes. The child weighed almost nothing. That was the first thing I noticed that morning, before the mare even stumbled: Wren had weighed something yesterday, and today weighed almost nothing, the way a loaf of bread weighs less after the steam goes out of it. I told myself it was my own exhaustion. I had been telling myself a great many things lately, and my credibility with myself was wearing thin.

The salt flats began where the grass gave out. They were not flat, exactly—that was a cartographer's lie, and Osk, to his credit, winced every time I used the word. They were a shallow basin of crusted white, miles of it, cracked into polygons like the skin of something ancient that had dried out from the inside. The sun came off them with a violence that made your eyes water and your thoughts go simple. *Walk. Breathe. Walk.* The coast was four days away, Osk said. Maybe three, if the weather held and the riders didn't find us and Wren—

Well. If Wren.

I looked down at the child, and the child looked up at me, and I saw the horizon through the place where Wren's cheek should have been solid.

I did not scream. Twenty years as a food-taster teaches you to swallow your screams with everything else. But my hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind, and Osk saw my face and stopped walking, and then he saw what I saw, and his grin died the death it had been dreading all morning.

"Ah," he said, very quietly, in the voice of a man whose maps had just told him the truth again. "That's new."

* * *

Osk here, since Sable has gone to walk the perimeter and someone with an honest profession ought to keep the record.

Let me say what I know about fading, because I have studied it, in my way. I spent eleven years in a cell for drawing coastlines that the Crown insisted did not exist. They burned my maps. They burned my notes. They burned, at one memorable point, my chair, which I had grown attached to. And in all that time, the thing that kept me sane—not comfortable, mind, sane—was the certainty that a thing which is real cannot be unmapped by burning paper. The world keeps its own ledger. Erasure is a lie told loudly enough to be believed.

But I am looking at a child who is becoming a lie, and my certainty is not helping either of us.

The fading had a pattern. I noticed it that first evening on the flats, when we camped in the lee of a salt ridge and Sable built a fire from dried grass and the last of our wood, and Wren sat close to the flames and did not get warm. The child faded fastest at the edges—the fingers first, then the outline of the ears, the tips of the hair, as though Wren were a drawing being erased from the outside in. And the fading came in waves. It was worst at moonrise. I checked. Every night, when the moon came up—that impossible thirteenth moon, still two turns short of its solstice, hanging fat and wrong in the sky like a debt collector at a funeral—Wren went dimmer. Like ink in water. Like a voice running out of breath.

Sable did not sleep that night. I know because I didn't either, and because at some hour past midnight I heard her talking to the child in that language she pretends she doesn't know, the one that makes the fire burn green at the edges when she speaks it, and the child answering in the same tongue, in a voice getting smaller and smaller, like a room being closed off in a house you used to live in.

I am a cartographer. I map what is. And what is, is this: we have four days of salt, riders behind us, no horses, food for two people for two days, and a child who is being unmade at the rate of one moonrise per night. I have drawn maps of places that don't exist. I never thought I'd watch one of them be a person.

But here is the other thing I know, the thing I learned in eleven years of a cell: people will spend anything for the ones they love, and the spending is never in coin. I watched Sable that night, watching Wren, and I saw her doing sums. I have seen that look before. It is the look of a woman pricing her own blood.

I should have said something. I am telling you this so that the record is honest: I saw the sums, and I said nothing, because I did not know what I could offer in exchange, and because I was afraid, and because—this is the shameful part, write it down, Osk, write it plainly—I thought perhaps the sums would come out in our favor, and I wanted them to.

The flats do not forgive. But they keep excellent records.

* * *

It was the second night on the salt when I did it.

I want to be clear that I knew what I was doing. People will tell you afterward—Aldric, if he ever speaks of it; the priests, if any of them bother—that I acted in desperation, in the dark, without understanding. That is a kindness, and it is a lie. I understood perfectly. Mother Ilsen's notes had a rite in them, buried in the middle of a page about funerary customs, described in the flat tone of a woman cataloguing horrors she had witnessed only in books: *the taster's tithe, by which the sworn may lend their prepared blood to the spell-born.* The Kavanaghs had done it before. That was, if you want the whole ugly truth of it, part of what we were *for*. We built our immunity poison by poison, drop by drop, year by year, not only to protect a queen but to hold a reservoir of it—blood that had learned to survive what would kill anyone else. Blood with room in it. Blood that could carry a passenger.

Wren was made of a spell that was running out of vessel. And I was made of twenty years of small controlled survivals, and I had never once asked what all that surviving was *for*.

I did it at moonrise, when the fading came. I cut my palm with Osk's map-knife—the one he'd kept hidden in his boot through eleven years of imprisonment, because of course he had; I was beginning to understand that everything about Osk was a thing he'd kept hidden in his boot—and I held my hand over the child's heart and spoke the words from Ilsen's page, badly, in an accent that would have made a Vhalenic grandmother weep.

The blood did not fall. That was the first impossible thing. It rose—beaded up out of the cut like mercury, hung in the air in a thread of dark red, and sank into Wren's chest the way rain sinks into ground that has been waiting a long time for it.

The second impossible thing was what it felt like. It felt like being *drunk from*. I don't know how else to say it. Not like losing blood—I've bled before; bleeding is just your body complaining. This was something being *taken that had been paid for*. Every drop of poison I'd ever swallowed, every hour of numb-tongued discipline, every morning I'd woken sick and gone to work anyway—it all went through my palm and into that small fading body, and I felt each payment leave me, and I want to tell you it hurt, and it didn't. It was worse than hurt. It was *spending*, the way the poor spend, knowing exactly what each coin was and what it had cost and that there would be no more where it came from.

Wren solidified. I watched it happen—color coming back into the child like dawn coming into a valley, the edges sharpening, the eyes going from that terrible translucence to a brown so dark it was nearly black. And Wren looked at me and said, in the common tongue, in a voice like a bell that has decided to be a voice:

"You didn't have to."

"Yes, I did," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

Then I fell over, because apparently heroism has a physical limit, and mine was the edge of a salt ridge in the wind.

* * *

Sable will tell you she fell over with dignity. I am the record-keeper here, and I say she fell over like a sack of turnips, and I caught her, mostly, and the sack of turnips weighed almost nothing herself by then, which frightened me more than the riders did.

Here is what I saw over the next two days, and I set it down plainly because someone must: she gave too much. The rite is not meant to be done once, I think—I have no notes on it beyond what she showed me from Ilsen's page, and Ilsen is dead, and the dead are notoriously unavailable for follow-up questions—but Sable did it twice more. Once at the second moonrise, when Wren began to go gray at the fingertips again, and once at the third, and by the third time her own hands had begun to do the thing Wren's had done. The edges of her. The tips of her hair. When she smiled at me on the last morning of the flats, I saw the horizon through her cheek, and I understood that the rite did not create anything. It only moved the debt. The child's erasure was being paid for out of the taster's account, and the taster's account, however deep, was not bottomless.

"You're doing it wrong," I told her, on the last day, when we could see the coast—a gray line, a promise, the place where Osk's true maps and the Crown's false maps finally agreed that land stopped.

"Enlighten me," she said. She was walking with a stick she'd cut from salt-bleached scrub, and she was leaning on it more than she'd admit, and she was still managing to sound like a woman discussing the correct way to fold linen.

"You're supposed to spend it on yourself," I said. "The immunity. The surviving. That was the whole design, wasn't it? The Kavanaghs paid in poison so that *they* would be safe. You've inverted the ledger. You're spending your own protection on a child who was never in your contract."

She stopped. The wind came off the flats and pushed her hair sideways, and through the flying strands I could see the place where her ear was going faint, and it made me want to break something, and there was nothing to break but salt.

"Osk," she said, "I spent twenty years tasting death in small doses for a king I'd never met, because it was my job, because someone had to, because the alternative was letting it reach someone who didn't choose it. Do you know what I learned?"

"That broth can lie," I said.

"That love is a job," she said. "That it's not a feeling you have, it's a thing you *do*, daily, in small controlled amounts, until the doing is what you are. Nobody ever taught me that. I thought I was learning to be a taster. I was learning to be this." She held up her half-faded hand, and turned it over, and looked at it not with horror but with something I can only call accounting. "Wren is in my contract. Wren was always in my contract. I just hadn't read that page yet."

I am a man who was imprisoned for insisting that true things were true. I have no argument against a woman like that. I only had a map, and a coast, and a knife in my boot, and the growing suspicion that the last page of her ledger was going to be very expensive.

* * *

We came down off the flats at dusk on the fourth day, and the sea was there, gray and enormous and smelling of everything the salt had denied us, and I have never been so happy to see water in my life, and I grew up in a flood plain.

Wren was solid. Wren was *singing*—a low, wordless Vhalenic thing, a walking song, a road song, the kind of song people make when a journey is almost over and they don't quite dare to hope. The child walked between us holding a hand of each of us, and the wind off the water was full of salt spray and gull-cry and the smell of kelp, and for one entire hour I let myself believe we had outrun it.

Then Sable stopped walking, and her grip went tight on my hand, and I followed her eyes to the beach below the bluff.

Fires. A camp. Horses picketed in the lee of a wrecked boat. And standing at the waterline, looking out at the horizon where the moon was beginning to rise—two turns short, fat, wrong, patient—was a man in a sorcerer's gray, and even at this distance, even with my half-blinded eyes, I knew the set of his shoulders.

"He's early," Sable said. Her voice was very calm, the taster's calm, the calm that means the opposite. "The door doesn't open for two more turns. Why would he be *early*?"

And then, from the beach, thin and clear across the dying light, Wren's song stopped—cut off mid-note, as if a hand had closed over the child's mouth from very far away—and Lord Corvane turned from the water and looked directly up the bluff at us, and smiled, and raised one hand in greeting, like a neighbor welcoming travelers home.

"He's not early," Osk said quietly. "He's been counting the same moon we have."
