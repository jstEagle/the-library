## 16. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel found us before we found it.

That's the only way I can describe it. Osk's map-line had run out an hour earlier—his finger tracing a coast that his own charts insisted was wrong, the ink fading where he touched it—and Wren had gone so pale she was nearly a rumor of a child, translucent at the edges like paper held too long against a window. And then the storm shoved us around a headland of black rock and there it was: a chapel standing in the shallows, sunk to its waist in the sea, its bell tower leaning like a drunk remembering an old argument.

"The tide took the village two hundred years ago," Osk said, shouting over the wind. "Or it didn't. My maps say there was never a village here at all."

"Your maps also say there's a door made of moonlight," I said.

"And you're carrying the key in a blanket." He shrugged, rain streaming off his half-blind eyes. "I've stopped apologizing for being right. It was taking too much time."

We waded in through the drowned doorway, water to our thighs, cold enough to make my teeth file a formal complaint. Inside, the chapel floor was stone above the waterline on one side—a raised chancel, dry as an old bone—where the altar had been. Someone had dragged driftwood into a rough pile near it years ago; someone else, decades later, had left a flint box in a niche, wax-sealed against the damp. Coastal folk, maybe, or smugglers, or just the kind of people who leave kindness in empty places because they can.

I got a fire going. It was a small, mean, salt-damp fire, and I have never been prouder of anything in my life.

Wren drank the broth I made from the last of our stores and slept almost immediately, curled against my side, her breathing shallow and quick. In the firelight I could count her ribs through her shift. I could count them through *me*, some nights, when I looked at her sideways. Every day she was less here. Every day the kingdom eating her from the inside grew a little more impatient.

Osk took first watch at the doorway, which mostly meant sitting with his back to a pillar and pretending not to listen while I checked Wren's pulse for the hundredth time.

It was near midnight when I heard the horse.

One horse. One set of footsteps in the flooded nave, deliberate, unhurried, splashing. I had a knife in my hand and Wren behind me before the sound resolved into a shape, and then the shape resolved into a face, and I confess I laughed—one short, ugly bark of a laugh—at the absurdity of it.

King Aldric Vane stood hip-deep in seawater in a drowned chapel, soaked to the skin, mud to the knees, holding a lantern like a man arriving very late to his own funeral.

"Before you stab me," he said, "I'd like to state for the record that this is the worst week of my reign, and my reign includes a plague year."

* * *

I didn't put the knife away. Let's be clear about that. But I let him climb onto the chancel, because he came alone—I looked, twice, at the black water and the blacker night beyond the broken doors, and there were no soldiers, no sorcerer, no second horse—and because Wren, half-asleep, made a small sound and reached for my sleeve, and I could not fight a war and hold a child at the same time. That is not a confession of weakness. It is a statement of arithmetic.

"You followed us across the salt flats," I said. "Alone."

"I told Corvane I was riding north to inspect the garrisons." He wrung out his cloak with the grim competence of a man who has camped in worse places, which surprised me more than his arrival did. Kings are supposed to be soft. Nobody tells you they're often just tired men with better tailoring. "He believed me. He always believes me. It's his one blind spot—he thinks I'm stupid, so he doesn't watch me as closely as he watches everyone else."

"He's watching *me* closely enough for both of you."

"Yes." Aldric sat down across the fire from me, and for a moment neither of us spoke, and the sea breathed against the walls like something enormous asleep. "You saved my life at the banquet."

"You owed me a boon. I collected it. We're square."

"We are not remotely square." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight did unflattering things to his face—not that it needed help; the man hadn't slept properly since coronation, if the court gossip I'd overheard while tasting his soup was accurate. "Sable Kavanagh. Or should I say Kavanagh of Vhalen?"

The knife shifted in my grip. "Careful."

"I've known since the vault," he said. "Since you asked for access to it. Do you know what Corvane said when you left? He said, *she asked for exactly what a Vhalenic heir would ask for.* And then he smiled, and I realized I had let a wolf into my archive because she'd pulled me out of a fire, and I didn't know which of those facts was supposed to matter more."

"And yet here you are. Alone. Unarmed, I assume."

"Sword's in the saddlebag. I'm not suicidal." He glanced at Wren, sleeping against my side, and something moved across his face that I couldn't name at the time. Later I would call it grief. At the time I mistook it for suspicion, and I answered suspicion with frost, and we wasted an hour that way—the two of us circling each other in a ruined church, trading barbs about Corvane and borders and whose fault the last three weeks had been, while the tide crept up the walls and the child beside me faded another shade closer to nothing.

What broke the circle was Osk.

He'd been dozing against his pillar, and he woke with one of his sudden cartographer's utterances, eyes still shut: "The bell tower's foundation is cracked. It'll come down within the decade. Shame. Good lines on it." Then he went back to sleep, having contributed nothing, as was his custom.

Aldric stared at him. Then at me. Then he laughed—actually laughed, tired and startled—and the sound cracked something open in the room, some seal I hadn't known was there.

"Is he always like that?"

"Worse. You should hear him defend his life's work. 'The map is correct; reality is sloppy.'" I hesitated. And then, because exhaustion makes honest people of us all, I added: "Why are you really here, Your Majesty? The garrison story is a lie. The vault visit was a lie. What's true?"

The laugh went out of him like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

He was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I started counting the drips of water from the rafters. When he finally spoke, he didn't look at me. He looked at the fire, the way people look at fires when the thing they need to say is easier to say to something that will burn it down afterward.

"My father died eleven months after his coronation," he said. "His father lasted eight months. His grandfather, fourteen. Before that—" he waved a hand, "—the records get strange. Dates that don't agree. Deaths listed twice. A king who supposedly reigned for thirty years and whose tomb says otherwise. I used to think it was bad record-keeping. Dynasties are full of bad record-keeping."

"And now?"

"Now I've read everything Corvane keeps locked in the tower under my own seal, because apparently I needed to become a conspirator in my own castle to learn my own family's history." His jaw tightened. "Every king of my line has dreamed of a door. From the night of coronation. A door of moonlight, standing alone, and something on the other side calling to them. Calling by *name*. Not their names, Sable. Their tasters' names."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark water, something sighed—or the sea did; I chose to believe it was the sea.

"They didn't die of illness," Aldric said. "They didn't die of poison, though the histories love that story—it's tidy, it gives the assassins a motive. They died in their beds, healthy, smiling, saying the same word over and over until their hearts stopped. Corvane has the word written down. He showed me once, thinking I wouldn't understand it." His voice dropped. "It's Vhalenic. It means *come home.*"

My scars ached. All of them at once, the old network of small poisons mapped across my tongue and throat and gut, and beneath the ache, faint as a bell heard through a wall, I felt something answer. Something patient. Something that had been waiting two hundred years and could wait a little longer, but not indefinitely.

"The kings weren't murdered," Aldric said. "They were *recalled*. Vhalen calls its tasters home, and the kings die of hearing it, because a king who hears his taster called is a king who knows his food is already dead. That's the curse of my line, if it's a curse at all. It might just be an echo. An invoice, coming due." He finally looked at me. "And now the heir of Vhalen is sitting across my fire with the last spell of that kingdom asleep against her ribs, heading for a door that my every ancestor dreamed of dying."

"So you came to stop us."

"I came to find out if I should." He spread his hands. "Corvane wants the door opened. Did you know that? He thinks I don't know. He's spent twenty years positioning himself for it—the returned things in his cells, his research, his little museum of horrors. He believes there's power on the other side of that door, and he intends to carry it back over the threshold in both hands." Aldric's mouth twisted. "I let him believe I share his fear of Vhalen. I don't. I fear something else entirely."

"Which is?"

"That he's right that there's power there. And that it doesn't want him." The king's voice was very quiet now. "My ancestors heard a door calling and died of longing, Sable. Not terror. *Longing.* What does it mean, that the most guarded men in the realm died homesick for a country that erased itself? What's on the other side of that door that makes dying men smile?" He shook his head. "Corvane fears losing. I fear *winning*. I fear opening that door and finding out that everything my line built—the crown, the court, the whole grinding machinery of Ashenmoor—is the thing that shouldn't exist. That we're the usurpers of a country that removed itself from history rather than be ruled, and history has simply been waiting, politely, for us to notice."

The honesty of it stunned me. Here was the man whose riders had chased me out of the vale, whose sorcerer had hunted me across half a kingdom, whose archive I'd broken into under cover of a boon—and he was sitting in seawater telling me he was afraid his throne was a theft.

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "You don't know me. You know what I am. By your own law, by Corvane's counsel, I'm the threat."

"By Corvane's counsel, everyone is the threat. It saves him the trouble of discernment." Aldric rubbed his face. "I'm telling you because you saved my life without knowing what it would cost you, and because you asked for a boon and spent it on *books*, and because I watched you feed that child broth like feeding her was the only law in the world." He nodded at Wren. "Tasters die for other people. It's the whole profession. Corvane would never understand that—kindness looks like weakness to him, so he assumes yours is a performance. I've eaten from your hand, Sable. I know it isn't."

I looked down at Wren. Her breathing had steadied in her sleep, and for once the translucence at her edges seemed to have paused, as if even the dying magic inside her wanted to hear how this conversation ended.

"She's not a weapon," I said. "Whatever Corvane told you. She's a message. And messages expire."

"What does the message say?"

"I don't know yet. I think—" I stopped. I had not said the next part aloud to anyone, not even to myself in the dark hours on the flats. "I think it says goodbye. I think Vhalen sent its last spell out into the world wearing a child's body because a kingdom that chose to erase itself still deserved a funeral, and no one was left to mourn it. Except us. Except the people it touched. My grandmother carried the spell out. My mother carried the silence. I'm carrying the child. Three generations of tasters, swallowing one last bitter thing."

Aldric was silent. The fire burned low. Out past the broken doors, the tide had begun its slow retreat, uncovering the drowned village street by street, stones emerging from the water like questions finally answered.

"If I let you go," he said at last, "to this door—if I stand aside and Corvane moves against you, I lose my sorcerer and possibly my throne. If I stop you, I break faith with the only person in twenty years who's shown me a kindness I didn't have to purchase." He exhaled. "I'm not good at choosing. I've had everything chosen for me since birth. Crown, duty, fear—all of it inherited, none of it asked for." A ghost of a smile. "You'd know something about that."

"A little," I admitted.

"So here is what I choose, badly and late and probably wrongly." He straightened, and for a moment, wet and exhausted and mud-splattered in a drowned church, he looked briefly like a king after all—not the crown, but the spine under it. "Corvane rides for the Door of Thirteenth Moons within two days. He thinks I don't know the location. I do; I've known for years; knowing things is the only power I have left. He'll force the threshold early—he won't wait for the thirteenth turn, patience isn't in him—and when he does, the boundary will tear, and everything Vhalen buried will come pouring out, and he'll try to catch it in his hands like a man catching the sea."

"And you?"

"I'm going to be there." He met my eyes. "Not to take anything. Not to stop anyone. To witness. Somebody from the winning side of this erasure ought to stand at that door and see what we did—or what was done—and remember it. Two hundred years of forgetting ends with somebody choosing to look." He paused, and the wry note crept back in, armor going on again piece by piece. "Also, you'll need help, and Osk appears to be mostly decorative."

From his pillar, without opening his eyes, Osk said: "I heard that."

"Good. Fetch the king a blanket. He's shivering."

"He's a king. Kings don't shiver."

"This one does," Aldric said. "Frequently. Mostly at night. You get used to it."

Something loosened in my chest then—not trust, not yet, trust takes longer than a night and a fire—but the beginning of it, the thawed ground where trust might eventually put down roots. An enemy is just an ally who hasn't been frightened in front of you yet. I'd learned that in the taster's hall, watching nobles bare their throats over shared soup.

I passed him the blanket. He took it like a man accepting a treaty.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When we reach the door—whatever happens, whatever comes through—you keep Corvane off Wren. Whatever she is, however this ends, she walks out of it a child. Not a vessel, not a prize. A child."

Aldric looked at Wren for a long moment, and the grief crossed his face again, unmistakable this time.

"On my crown," he said quietly, "which may be worth less than either of us hopes."

* * *

We slept in shifts after that, the four of us—king, taster, madman, message—in a chapel the sea had swallowed, and I dreamed of nothing at all, which after weeks of Vhalenic dreams felt like mercy.

I woke before dawn to gray light and gray water and Osk standing at the broken doors, staring east with his ruined eyes, his whole body gone rigid.

"Sable," he said. "The moon."

I came to the doorway. The sky was clearing in the east, the storm spent, and hanging low over the flat silver sea was the moon—thin, new, wrong. Counting the turns in my head the way Osk had taught me, the way I'd counted every night since the flats, I felt the arithmetic land in my stomach like a swallowed coal.

Two turns left.

And far down the coast road, small in the dawn haze, dust was rising. A lot of it. Moving fast.

"Corvane," Aldric said behind me, and his voice had gone very quiet, the way voices do on battlefields. "He's not waiting for the solstice."

"No," I said, and picked Wren up, and she weighed almost nothing at all. "Then we ride today. We ride now. Before he tears the sky open and lets everything out."

Behind us, in the drowned chapel, the cracked bell in its leaning tower stirred in the wind—and rang, once, though there was no rope and no hand and no reason, a single deep note rolling out over the water like a summons, or a warning, or a farewell.

All three of us pretended not to hear it.

None of us succeeded.
