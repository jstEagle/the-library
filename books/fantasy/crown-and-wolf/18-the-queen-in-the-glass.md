## 18. The Queen in the Glass

Here is what no one tells you about stepping through a door made of moonlight: it does not feel like crossing a threshold. It feels like being swallowed politely.

One moment I was standing on the salt flats with the wind screaming around me, Osk's blind eyes fixed on a map-line only he could see, Wren's small hand dissolving in mine like sugar in warm water. The next, there was stillness. The kind of stillness I had only ever known inside the vale, where magic couldn't follow—but this was stillness with a pulse underneath it, like pressing your ear to a sleeping animal's ribs.

I was inside Vhalen.

Or what remained of it, which was to say: a country remembered so hard by itself that it had forgotten how to die.

The sky above me was glass. Not metaphorically—I looked up and saw clouds moving beneath my feet reflected somewhere overhead, a whole weather system folded over like a book closing on its own page. Towers rose around me in the distance, slender and pale, some of them whole, some of them half-dissolved into mist at their tops as if the memory of them had simply run out before the building did. Streets of white stone stretched away in perfect silence. No birds. No wind. No smell except one—rosemary and ash, which is apparently what an entire dead kingdom smells like from the inside.

And in front of me, maybe twenty paces off, stood a woman made of mirror-light.

She had been waiting. You could tell, somehow, the way you can tell a table has been set for you specifically—the napkin folded just so. Her dress was old-fashioned even by the standards of the portraits I'd seen in Aldric's vault, high-collared and silver-threaded, and her hair was dark, dark, dark, shot through with a streak of white at the temple that I recognized because I found my first one last spring and spent a week sulking about it.

Her face was my face.

Not exactly. Older. Worn differently, worn by a different life. But the same jaw. The same mouth that went flat when it was thinking. The same eyes that everyone in the vale had always said were too dark for the family she'd married into—which they had said, apparently without ever asking themselves why a food-taster's daughter had such strange coloring.

"Kavanagh," the Queen in the Glass said, and her voice was the sound a bell makes long after it stops ringing. "Little taster. Come in out of the storm."

"I haven't agreed to come in," I said, because when the impossible addresses you by name, pettiness is the only handhold left. "For all I know this is a trap."

"Everything is a trap," she said pleasantly. "The question is always who set it, and whether they meant it kindly." She gestured, and a path unrolled between us through the glass-street cobbles, smooth and clear. "Mine was set kindly. I promise you that much. Whether it is *kind*—" her smile tilted, "—is not mine to decide anymore."

Behind me, faintly, impossibly, I could hear the door. Not see it—it was somewhere behind and also nowhere at all, the way a sound is behind you—but I could feel its edges, half-torn, leaking storm. Corvane would be coming. He had torn his way toward this moment for years, maybe decades, and he would not stop because a taster with sore feet got here first.

I walked the path. Of course I walked the path. I have never once in my life declined a suspect dish; why would I start declining now?

* * *

She led me through streets that remembered themselves as we moved. I watched a fountain assemble drop by drop out of nothing, water rising rather than falling, filling upward until it overflowed in a proper cascade. I watched a market square flicker with the ghosts of awnings and voices, there and gone, like a fire dying down. And everywhere, threaded through everything, I felt the same thing I'd felt since the night Wren knocked on my door: that ache in my scars. My poison scars, the ones I'd earned sip by sip across twenty years of swallowing death for a king I'd never met.

They weren't aching here. They were *singing*. Quietly. Like tuning forks struck once, long ago, still holding the note.

"You feel it," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"My blood," I said. "It recognizes the place."

"Your blood is the place," she said. "Or nearly. Kavanagh, sit."

There was a bench. There hadn't been a bench. There was a bench now, carved white stone, warm as sun-warmed despite the lightless glass sky, and I sat because my legs had opinions of their own and they were all in favor.

Wren was gone—or not gone, but elsewhere, held somewhere in this place the way a word is held in a closed mouth. I could feel the child the way you can feel a lit candle in a dark room without looking. Safe, for the moment. Dimming.

"Tell me the terms," I said. "Whatever this is, whatever you're about to offer—I know there's an offer. Everything in this kingdom speaks in offers. My grandmother did. The spell-child does. Even the poison does: *drink me and live longer than you should.* So say it plainly, Your Majesty, because plain speech is the one luxury I've never been able to afford."

Maren laughed. It was a real laugh, or the echo of one, and it cracked her mirror-light face just slightly—a seam of humanity showing through the shine.

"Aldric's vault told you something," she said. "I can hear it in your voice. Good. Then I don't have to explain the oath."

"The tasters' oath," I said. "Sworn to die for our queen."

"Sworn to *taste* for our queen," she corrected gently. "Death was only the price. The work was this: someone must stand between the crown and every harm the world can brew, and swallow it first, so the crown never has to know what poison tastes like. That is what my Kavanaghs did for six generations. That is what your grandmother did, in the end, when Vhalen itself became the dish." She turned her mirror hands over, and in their surface I saw reflections that weren't ours—the tower streets, the ghost-market, and beneath it all, far down, a slow red light like a heart beating under floorboards. "And when we chose erasure over conquest—we could not taste our own ending ourselves. A kingdom cannot lift the cup to its own lips. Someone had to carry it for us. Someone had to hold the whole of Vhalen in a single living body and walk it out through the world, past every map, past every memory, until it could be finished properly or restored wholly. That someone was a child. The last spell we had strength to cast."

"Wren."

"Wren," she agreed, and something in her voice bent around the name with terrible tenderness. "A message running out of time. Every turn of this impossible moon takes more of her. When the thirteenth completes, either Vhalen dies in her—and she dies with it, unwritten, unnamed, a letter burned unread—or the door opens fully and Vhalen lives again."

"And lives again how?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since the vault. Since I read my grandmother's handwriting describing the shape of the choice she'd refused to make for herself.

"The crown restores," Maren said. "You take it, and every street re-solidifies, every soul caught in the weave returns, the towers stand, the fountains fall the right way, the child is healed and named and home. Vhalen whole. Vhalen eternal." She paused, and the pause was the honest part, the part she couldn't make beautiful. "And you keep it. Forever. The keeper of a country that exists outside time needs a keeper who does too. That is what the Kavanagh line was bred and sworn and loved for, little taster. We were always meant to be the ones who stood in the doorway. Eternally. Tasting eternity's risks so it needn't taste its own."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you may still finish it. Drink the ending yourself, as I said the oath demands, and give us a true death, and give the child her life back." Her mirror-eyes did not blink. They couldn't. "But you should understand what refusal costs, because I will not cheat you and I will not charm you. Restoration gives you everything and takes your forever. Ending gives the child everything and costs you—" she considered, "—more than you have. But less than forever."

"That's a terrible sales pitch."

"I'm not selling," said the Queen in the Glass. "I'm tasting. Two hundred years, Kavanagh, I have stood in this glass waiting to learn what my line would choose, and I find I cannot know until you do. So choose. But first—" and here her voice changed, dropped its ceremony entirely, became almost young, almost desperate, "—walk with me a moment more. Indulge a dead queen. I want to show you what you'd be keeping alive."

* * *

She showed me a schoolroom. Ghost children at ghost desks, laughing at a joke two hundred years dead, and one small boy in the back with ink on his nose who looked up—directly at me—and smiled, and I felt it in my chest like a hook set behind the sternum.

She showed me a kitchen. Of course she showed me a kitchen. A fat cook singing off-key, a scullery girl stealing plums, and at the tasting table by the wall, a woman with dark eyes and a silver streak, lifting a spoon of suspect stew, sipping, waiting, surviving. My grandmother's mother, maybe. Some root of my root. She caught sight of the queens standing invisibly at the door—or sensed us, the way her descendants sense arsenic—and raised her spoon in a small salute.

"She knew," I said, my throat tight.

"They always knew," Maren said. "That was the cruelty and the gift of it. To taste for someone, truly taste, you must be willing to die for them. And to be willing, you must love them first. So every Kavanagh loved the crown they served, and the crown—" her voice caught on the glass of her, "—the crown loved them back, badly, distantly, the way kings love anything: too late."

We stood in the ghost-kitchen while the scullery girl ate her plum and the taster swallowed her sip and the cook sang, and I understood, finally, the shape of the thing being offered to me. It wasn't power. Power was the wrapping paper. The offer was this: *keep them. Keep all of them. The ink-nosed boy, the plum thief, the singing cook. Keep them forever, and stand in their doorway forever, and never again be a woman who gets to leave.*

And God help me, some traitorous part of me wanted it. I had spent twenty years poisoning myself in small doses for a stranger's king. Here, at last, was a king's table where the love ran both directions—where the crown knew my name, had always known my name, had crossed two centuries of oblivion to call me *little taster* like an endearment and not an insult. Belonging, offered in the shape of love. Which is, I think, the most dangerous shape there is. Poison works best in honey. Every taster knows that.

"You're crying," Maren observed.

"I'm immune to most things," I said. "Grief isn't one of them."

"No," she said softly. "It never was. That was always the family failing. We taste everything."

Somewhere behind us, the half-open door shrieked—a sound like tearing silk the size of the sky—and the glass streets shivered, and I knew without turning that Corvane had reached the threshold. That Osk was holding the map-line with his ruined eyes. That Aldric, if he'd come, was watching his worst dream unfold in the storm.

And I thought of Wren. Dimming somewhere in the weave of this place, carrying a whole country in her small body the way I had carried poison in mine—sip by sip, turn by turn, because someone had to, because love had chosen her for it before she could speak.

They had given her the whole cup. An entire kingdom's survival, poured down the throat of a child.

Every taster instinct I owned rose up in revolt at once. Because there are doses, and there are bodies, and there are weights, and no oath—no matter how lovingly written—justifies making a seven-year-old drink what was meant for a queen.

"Your Majesty," I said. "I have an answer."

Maren turned to me, and for a moment her mirror-face was only a face, only a tired woman who had waited two hundred years in glass for her family to arrive.

"Not yet," I said. "First I need to do what Kavanaghs do best."

"Which is?"

"Taste what's actually in the dish," I said, "before anyone drinks."

Down through the glass streets, toward the red heartbeat under the floorboards of the world, toward the torn and screaming door, I started walking—and behind me, for the first time in two hundred years, Vhalen followed.
