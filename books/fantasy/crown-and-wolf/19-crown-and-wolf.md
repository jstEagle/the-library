## 19. Crown and Wolf

The door stood open, and the world was leaking out of it.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the one discipline I never lost, even with the sky tearing like wet paper above me. Not "pouring." Leaking. A kingdom two centuries dead, seeping through a crack in the winter air the way broth seeps through a cracked bowl—slowly at first, then all at once, everywhere, unstoppable.

Ghosts walked the snow. Not the thin grey shivers the chapel priests sold indulgences against, but whole people: women with baskets of glass fruit, children chasing a dog made of lamplight, a man in a scholar's robe reading a book that had already burned, his eyes moving over blank pages with perfect comprehension. They did not look at us. We were the interruption. We were the ones who didn't belong.

"Osk," I said. "The line."

"I have it." His voice came out through his teeth. He knelt in the snow with his map spread across his knees, one hand pressed flat to the paper, and his eyes—what was left of them—had gone the white of old milk. He'd been paying in installments since the threshold tore, and the bill was coming due. "The line holds. Barely. Don't make me hold it and listen to you talk at the same time."

"Then I'll be quiet."

"You've never once been quiet in your life, Kavanagh."

He had a point. I shut up anyway, because there are exactly three things I trust: my tongue, my scars, and Osk's maps, and two of the three were currently bleeding.

The Door of Thirteenth Moons was not a door. I'd expected—what, oak? Stone? Something you could knock on, something with a handle, something that respected the basic courtesies of architecture. Instead it was a seam. A vertical absence in the air above the drowned chapel's altar, tall as a cathedral and thin as a knife, and through it I could see Vhalen: towers of glass catching a sun that had set two hundred years ago, gardens where the flowers were made of remembered music, streets washed in the amber light of a country's last afternoon, looping forever.

And in the middle of it, on a dais of white stone, a crown.

* * *

Corvane reached it first.

I should have known he would. The man had spent the whole chase being inevitable—the way winter is inevitable, the way poison is inevitable if you leave it sitting in the soup long enough. He'd come over the salt flats with forty soldiers and a look on his face that I'd last seen on a man eating the last peach of the season: the look of someone about to take something and enjoy the taking.

"Stay back," he said, without turning. He didn't need to raise his voice. The threshold amplified him, carried him, made his words land inside my skull like coins dropped down a well. "This is kingdom business, taster. You've done your part. You brought the key."

"She has a name," I said.

"Everything has a name. Names are how we file things." He stepped through the seam, and the world flinched. Where he crossed, the snow blackened. The ghosts nearest the door turned their faces away, the way you turn from a smell.

Wren made a sound I'd never heard from her before. Not Vhalenic. Not a word at all. A small, cracked note, like a glass about to go, and every ghost in the field stopped mid-step and shuddered.

I pulled her behind me. She was lighter than she'd been a week ago. Lighter than she'd been yesterday. Holding her hand was like holding a handful of smoke that had decided, out of stubbornness, to keep the shape of a child.

"Corvane!" Aldric's voice, from the ridge. The king had come the rest of the way on foot, his fine coat ruined, his crown—he'd actually worn his crown, vanity or armor, I never learned which—crooked on his head. "Corvane, step away from it. That's an order."

Corvane laughed. I'll give him this: he laughed like a man who has already won and is being generous about the timing. "Your Majesty. You came. I confess I hoped you would. It's fitting, isn't it? Every king of your line dreamed of this door. Every king died a year after coronation because Vhalen called and you couldn't answer. Well." He spread his hands. "I'm answering for you. You're welcome. History will be kind, if I leave any history to write it."

He reached for the crown.

* * *

Here is what I know about the guardian, and I know it the way you know a thing you've swallowed: completely, and too late to spit it back.

The wolf had been there the whole time. I understand that now. It was the seam itself, or the seam was its mouth—there's no honest way to separate the two. Vhalen's last spell had not only made a child out of a kingdom's memory. It had made a gatekeeper out of a kingdom's teeth. Every story Ilsen ever half-told, every glyph Osk mapped, every dream Wren woke from crying—the wolf was in all of them, at the edges, patient as winter.

It came through when Corvane's fingers touched the crown.

I will not describe it well, because no one could. It was the size of the idea of a wolf, which is larger than any wolf, and it was made of the same substance as the ghosts—memory, grief, the amber light of a last afternoon—but where the ghosts were gentle, the wolf was *jurisprudence*. It was law. It was the oldest law in Vhalen, the one law a kingdom of tasters and oath-keepers had carved into the foundation of everything: *nothing crosses unwelcome.*

Corvane screamed. I'd like to tell you I felt sorry for him. I felt something, but it wasn't sorry.

The wolf did not eat him. That's the horror of it. It *mauled the boundary*—tore at the seam between worlds with its teeth, and every bite took something from both sides. Snow became glass became snow. A soldier's shadow peeled off him and walked into Vhalen, and the soldier fell over, shadowless, screaming in a voice that came out flat as paper. The ghosts nearest the tear flickered, thinned, went out like candles in a draft. The wolf was not choosing sides. The wolf was *enforcing*, and enforcement, at the border of a dying country, looks a great deal like destruction.

"Osk!" I screamed. "How long?"

"Minutes!" His hand was shaking on the map. Blood was running from his nose, his ears, his eyes—paying, always paying. "Maybe less! Kavanagh, whatever you're going to do—"

I was already running.

* * *

Let me tell you about the oath, since I'm apparently the last person alive qualified to.

The taster's oath of the Vale of Ashenmoor is four sentences. I took it at nine years old, kneeling on a cold kitchen floor, with a woman named Mistress Hale holding a cup of something bitter to my lips. *I will taste what the king cannot risk. I will keep what the king cannot know. I will carry what the king cannot bear. And when the last dish comes, I will not refuse it.* We said it every morning, we tasters, over breakfast, like grace. I'd said it ten thousand times. I'd said it so often it had worn smooth as a river stone and meant about as much.

Or so I believed, right up until the moment I understood that I'd been rehearsing for this my entire life, and the rehearsal had been in the wrong key.

The crown sat on its dais of white stone, and it was beautiful, and it was a trap, and I knew it was a trap because Queen Maren had told me so—or her echo had, or the memory of her had, in the amber half-light inside the threshold, an hour or a lifetime ago. *Take it,* she'd said, with my face and my grandmother's face and a face older than both, *and be our keeper. Keep us. Forever.* And I had looked at forever and seen a kitchen with no door in it, and I had said no, and the saying of it had not killed me, which surprised us both.

But refusing the crown wasn't the same as ending this. The crown was the offer. The kingdom was the wound. And the wound was open, and bleeding, and a child made of its last magic was fading on the snow behind me like breath off glass.

Corvane lay where the wolf had thrown him, alive—I could see the rise and fall of him, furious and broken and not finished, because men like Corvane are never finished, they're only between courses. The wolf stood over the threshold, jaws wet with the stuff of two worlds, and it looked at me.

It knew me. That's the thing I'll carry out of this, if I carry anything. It looked at me the way Mistress Hale used to look at me over the tasting cup: measuring, weighing, asking the only question that had ever mattered in my trade.

*Will you take what's offered, or will you refuse it?*

I knelt.

The snow went through my skirts. The ghosts had stopped. Even the wolf stilled. Somewhere behind me Aldric was shouting my name—Sable, not Kavanagh, he'd finally learned which one to use—and Osk was shouting something about minutes, and Wren was making that small cracked-glass sound, and I knelt on the border of a dead kingdom and did the only thing I have ever been truly, perfectly, trained to do.

I took the oath inverted.

*I will taste what the king cannot risk*—and there was no king, there was only a kingdom, and it had been dying for two hundred years, and no one had been willing to sit at its table and take the first bite. *I will keep what the king cannot know*—and what Vhalen could not know, what it had been too afraid to know, was that it was allowed to end. That erasure had been its own choice, made in terror, and choices made in terror deserve better than to be carried forever by a child. *I will carry what the king cannot bear.* Two centuries of unbearable, and every Kavanagh before me had smuggled out pieces of it, grandmother to mother to daughter, a spell in a child's body, a taster's blood in a taster's veins, the whole lineage one long held breath.

*And when the last dish comes—*

The wolf lowered its head. Its breath smelled of snowfall and the inside of a locked room and, underneath, faintly, unbearably, of my grandmother's kitchen, of bread, of a life I'd invented for a woman I'd never met.

*—I will not refuse it.*

I opened my mouth, and the kingdom poured in.

* * *

I have tasted nightshade and felt my heart argue with itself. I have tasted arsenic and watched the world go white at the edges, politely, like snowfall. I have tasted things in Aldric's court that I still don't have names for, things that whispered to me from the bottom of the cup for a week afterward. My tongue has been a graveyard of small deaths, and I have buried every one of them and walked away.

Vhalen's ending was not like that.

Vhalen's ending was everything at once. It was the taste of the last bread baked in the last oven on the last morning, and the taste of the fear that came after, and the taste of the vote—the whole kingdom, standing in the glass towers, choosing to unmake itself rather than be unmade, and the taste of *regret*, because they'd regretted it from the first second, and regret ferments, and two hundred years of it is a vintage no throat was built for. It was every unfinished letter. Every child's shoe. Every song that stopped mid-phrase because the singer had stopped existing. It was the taste of a door closing from the inside, and it went down my throat like a swallowed sun, and I did not refuse it.

I heard myself make a sound. It was not a scream. It was the sound a taster makes when the dish is finished and the king still lives: acknowledgment. Receipt. *Noted, and survived, and no one else need know.*

The wolf closed its jaws an inch from my face, and exhaled, and stepped back, and the seam between the worlds began—gently, the way a hand closes a book—to draw shut.

Behind me, Wren stopped making the cracked-glass sound.

I turned. I was on my hands and knees in the snow and my whole body was a struck bell, but I turned, and I saw the child standing upright for the first time in days, solid, opaque, *present*, her eyes wide and wet and fixed on me with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone watching a person swallow something terrible on their behalf and not knowing whether that makes them saved or damned.

"You don't have to carry it," I rasped. My voice tasted of glass towers. "That's the whole point. That was always the whole point."

The seam was closing. Through the narrowing gap I could see the amber light of the last afternoon, and the queen with my face, standing on her dais beside the crown no one had taken, and she was—there is no other word—she was *thanking* me. The ghosts were turning toward the light. The wolf sat down at the threshold like a dog at a master's door, and waited, and its waiting was the gentlest thing I saw that night.

"Kavanagh." Osk crawled to me through the snow, blind, bloody, grinning like a man who'd won a bet with God. "Kavanagh, the line's holding, the door's closing on its own, you mad, magnificent—" He found my hand and squeezed it hard enough to crack knuckles. "You did it. You absolute— you *did* it."

"Mostly," I said. "I've still got the aftertaste."

And then, because the world has never once let me finish a sentence in peace, the snow behind us exploded.

Corvane was up. Corvane was *running*—burned, broken, one arm hanging wrong, his fine coat in ribbons, his face a mask of such pure, incandescent want that for one absurd moment I pitied him—and he was running not away from the closing door but *toward* it, toward the last sliver of amber light, toward the crown, screaming words in a language that made the snow flinch.

The wolf rose.

The seam narrowed to a ribbon, to a wire, to a thread of light with a man's outstretched fingers reaching through it—

And closed.

* * *

The silence afterward was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

No ghosts. No amber light. No wolf—though the snow where it had sat held the print of enormous paws, and I suspect, on certain solstice nights, it holds them still. Just the ordinary winter wind, the ordinary dark, the ordinary stars, and four people kneeling in the snow on the coast of a country that had finally, at long last, been allowed to die.

Aldric reached me first. He took off his crooked crown—actually took it off, held it in both hands like something embarrassing—and looked at me with an expression I'd spent a year learning to read across a banquet table and had never once seen on him before.

It was grief. Clean, unarmored grief, for a kingdom he'd been taught to fear, for a man he'd raised and been betrayed by, for the taster who'd just done the thing no king in his line had ever dared.

"Sable," he said. "What did it cost?"

I meant to say *nothing*. It's what you say. It's what a taster says; it's practically the job description. But my mouth had other ideas, and what came out, tasting of two hundred years of regret, was:

"Ask me tomorrow. If I wake up."

And somewhere behind me, small and clear and solid as a bell, Wren began to laugh.
