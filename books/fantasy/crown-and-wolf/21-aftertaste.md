## 21. Aftertaste

The king of Ashenmoor made me an offer in a room with no doors.

That's not poetry. The Door of Thirteenth Moons had closed behind us three days prior, and the chamber he chose for our conversation was one of the old council rooms in the deep keep, the kind built before anyone thought to put hinges on their paranoia. One entrance, warded, guarded. He wanted me to know that whatever happened next, nothing could interrupt it. Or perhaps nothing could leave it. With kings, hospitality and hostage-taking are often the same gesture wearing different gloves.

He looked terrible, Aldric. Better than he had at the threshold—everyone looked better than they had at the threshold—but still like a man who had slept badly for a year and had only just learned why. There was a new looseness in his shoulders. Fear, when it finally dies, leaves a body in much the same state as a fever breaking.

"You could stay," he said. He didn't dress it up. I'll give him that much; he'd learned from watching me taste his food for six years that I have no patience for sauces over bad meat. "A place at court. Honored. Protected." He paused, and then, because he was honest even when honesty cost him: "Perhaps more than protected, if you ever wanted it. My court would not object. Half of them watched you save my life and the other half are afraid of you, which in my experience amounts to the same thing at a banquet."

I let the silence sit between us like a dish neither of us wanted to taste first.

"Your Majesty—"

"Aldric."

"Aldric." His name felt strange in my mouth now. For years I'd swallowed it professionally, along with everything else meant for him. "Do you remember what you told me once, about your father? About how every king of your line dreams of a door?"

His jaw tightened. "I remember."

"And do you dream of it still?"

"No." He said it quietly. "For the first time since I was crowned, I sleep without dreaming at all. It is the most frightening peace I have ever known." He laughed, short and unamused. "You gave me that. Do you understand that? Vhalen called its tasters home for two hundred years and every heir who heard the call died of it. You went into the kingdom itself, and instead of answering the call, you *finished* it. You did what none of us could. You ended a haunting two centuries old with—" He gestured vaguely at me, as if I were a dish whose recipe he couldn't guess. "With whatever that was."

"It was an oath," I said. "Inverted."

"Then invert another one," he said, leaning forward. "Swear yourself here. Swear yourself to a living kingdom, Sable. I am asking you not as a king collecting debts but as a man who has spent his whole reign being lied to by everyone paid to tell him the truth. You are the only person alive who has never once flinched from what things *are*. I want that at my table. Gods know I want it at my side."

And there it was. Not just gratitude. Loneliness, dressed in ermine. I knew that costume intimately—I'd worn a version of it myself for thirty years, the food-taster alone at her little table in the vale, swallowing harm so no one else had to, telling herself it was duty when it was mostly habit.

It would have been easy to say yes. That was the trap of it, the way all gilded things are traps: the offer was genuinely good. A place of honor. Protection. A man who had earned, through terror and honesty both, some claim on my loyalty. Lark would have tutors, physicians, warmth. Osk would have his maps restored to the royal archive, his name cleared in ink that mattered.

All of it true. All of it gold. And all of it a cage with very comfortable bars, which are the only kind that hold people like me.

"I tasted for you for six years," I said.

"I know."

"Do you know why I was good at it? Not the immunity—the vale raised that in me like a scar raises skin. I mean why I never missed. Why nothing slipped past me onto your plate."

He considered. "Because you assumed everything was poison until proven otherwise."

"Because I assumed *I* was poison," I said. "Everything that came near me, I tested against myself first. Harm me before it harms anyone else—that was the whole discipline. And it worked, Aldric, it worked beautifully, and it made a life so small I could hold it in one hand." I turned my hand over, palm up, empty. "I'm not going to spend the second half of it learning a larger version of the same trick. A court is a table, Your Majesty. Everything on it is a dish, and everyone seated at it is either tasting or being served. I've done my years at the table."

"The door is shut," he said. "Vhalen is finished. There's nothing left to fear."

"There never was anything left to fear," I said, "and you feared it beautifully for years. So did I. Fear doesn't need a door. It builds its own." I stood, because sitting felt too much like accepting. "Ask me for something else. Anything else. Ask me to come back every solstice and taste your feast—that I'll give freely, and gladly, and it will cost you nothing because there will be nothing in it but food. But don't ask me to live inside walls again. Not even yours. Especially yours."

For a long moment he looked at me the way Corvane must have looked at the guardian wolf: knowing exactly what he wanted and exactly why he couldn't reach for it.

Then he laughed. It transformed him entirely—ten years fell off him and landed somewhere on the floor.

"A food-taster," he said, "refusing a crown-adjacent life because she finds courts indigestible. If I put that in a chronicle, no archivist would believe it."

"Don't put it in a chronicle at all," I said. "Put down that the king was saved by his taster, and that the taster asked as her boon a cartographer's pardon and passage home. Let them wonder about the rest."

"They'll wonder anyway."

"Good," I said. "Wondering is healthier than knowing. I learned that from a dead language."

* * *

Osk took the news of his pardon better than he took most things, which is to say he cried briefly, blamed the light, and then demanded ink.

We were in the guest quarters the palace had given us—he'd been given actual quarters, with a bed, which he treated as a personal insult—and he sat at the window working through the last of the sight the threshold had taken from him. It hadn't blinded him entirely. It had done something worse, something more cartographic: it had burned the map-lines out of his vision. He saw the world fine, he said, but he would never see a boundary again. Rivers, coastlines, the edge where one country stops and another starts—all of it had gone gray and formless, traded away at the Door of Thirteenth Moons so that Sable Kavanagh and a fading child could cross a line drawn in moonlight.

"You should be furious with me," I told him, watching him hold a page an inch from his face.

"I was," he said cheerfully. "For about a day. Then I remembered that I spent eleven years in prison for drawing maps of places that don't exist, and now those places definitely exist—or rather, existed definitively, which is better than existing ambiguously—and my maps are *true*, and I have a royal pardon saying so." He tapped the document on the table, weighted under an inkpot. "Do you know what that's worth to a man? Every cartographer in Ashenmoor will hate me. I intend to be insufferable about it for decades."

"Osk."

"Sable."

"Come with me."

He set down the page. For once he didn't fill the silence with talk, which from Osk was roughly equivalent to a eulogy.

"To the vale," I said. "There's nothing there. You know that better than anyone—you mapped its absence. No magic. No wonders. Snow, goats, a valley where the world goes quiet. I'm offering you gray coastlines and a village of two hundred people who think a cartographer is someone who draws pictures of roads."

"I know exactly what you're offering," he said. "I mapped it. Well—" he smiled, wry and tired, "—I mapped everything *around* it, which is nearly the same thing. The Vale of Ashenmoor. The blank space. The place where my lines always stopped." He looked out the window at the capital sprawling below, all its boundaries bright and legible to everyone but him. "Sable, I have spent my entire life believing in places no one else could see. You're inviting me to live in one. It's the only honest invitation I've ever received."

"That's a yes?"

"That's a yes with conditions. I get the good chair by the fire. And when the child asks me to draw her a map of somewhere imaginary, I draw it properly, with a compass rose, because imaginary places deserve rigor."

"Lark," I said. "She's called Lark now."

"Lark," he repeated, tasting it. "Good name. Small bird. Flies far. Yes, I'll come. Someone has to teach the girl that a map is a promise you make about the shape of the world, and clearly you're going to be busy teaching her everything else."

* * *

Lark was in the palace gardens, which she had annexed within a day of arriving and defended with the fierce territorial instinct of any six-year-old sovereign. She was sitting on the grass teaching a very confused noblewoman's daughter a song in High Vhalenic—a language that now existed nowhere else on earth except in the mouth of a child and, faintly, in mine.

She heard my footsteps and turned. Solid. Whole. Warm-skinned and mortal and hungry, always hungry, the way children are when they've recently stopped being spells. She'd gained weight at the palace. Color. An appetite that terrified the kitchen staff and delighted me beyond all reason.

Every morning I woke and checked her hands for translucence. Every morning they were just hands, jam-sticky and real.

"Were you talking to the king?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Did he cry?"

"He laughed."

Her eyes went wide with genuine alarm. "Is he dying?"

"Children are merciless," I said, sitting down beside her in the grass. "No, little bird. He's not dying. He offered us a castle."

"We have a house."

"We have a *hut*."

"With a good chair by the fire," she said solemnly, parroting Osk, "and a goat named after a mistake." The goat was named Corvane. I had lost the argument about this and had stopped fighting it; there was something cleansing about a child calling that name across a snowy yard to summon a bad-tempered animal to dinner.

"Do you want a castle?" I asked her.

Lark considered this with the gravity of someone deciding the fate of kingdoms, which, technically speaking, she was the last living piece of.

"It has too many rooms," she decided. "And nobody sings in the halls. I listened." She leaned against my shoulder. "The lady who taught me songs—Mother Ilsen, in the stories you tell—did she sing in halls?"

"She sang in a chapel," I said. "A small one. With bad heating."

"Then that's where the good singing lives." She yawned, enormous and unbothered, the yawn of a child who had once carried a dead nation inside her and now carried mostly bread crusts for the ducks. "Can we go home tomorrow?"

Home. The word dropped into my chest and settled there, warm and heavy, like the first swallow of a meal you trust completely.

"Yes," I said. "Tomorrow."

* * *

We left at dawn, three travelers and no escort, because Aldric had offered one and I had refused that too. He'd stood on the steps of the keep to see us off, which kings do not do, and which caused three separate minor diplomatic incidents among the assembled courtiers.

"At least take this," he said, pressing a small parcel into my hands. Wrapped in plain cloth. Heavy.

I opened it on the road, an hour out. Inside was a knife—not a ceremonial blade but a working one, the kind a cook uses, the kind a taster uses. The steel was Ashenmoor-forged, plain and honest. On the handle, small enough that only someone looking for it would find it, was engraved a single word in High Vhalenic.

*Lark.*

Not the girl's name. The other lark—the bird, the plain brown singer of hedgerows. He'd had it translated. Which meant somewhere in the royal archives, a scholar had spent part of the night finding a dead word for a common bird for a king who wanted to say *thank you* in a language only two people alive could read.

Osk peered at it, approved of the engraving's depth and spacing, and pronounced it "adequate craftsmanship." Lark asked if she could have a knife too and was told, in the unified voice of both adults present, absolutely not.

The road north took nine days. Magic thinned around us as we traveled, the way sound thins before snowfall, and by the time we climbed the pass above the vale I could feel the silence coming back like a tide. Wren—no. Lark. She was Lark now, wholly, the spell-name shed like a winter coat. Lark grew quieter as we descended, not frightened-quiet but listening-quiet, the way you go quiet entering a cathedral.

At the boundary line—the invisible seam where magic died two hundred years ago and never noticed—she stopped walking.

"It's like a held breath," she said.

"It is," I said. "Step through anyway. Nothing in there can hurt you. Nothing in there can do *anything* to you. That's rather the point."

She stepped through, and I watched her feel it: the great mute hush of the vale closing over her, the place where the last spell of a dead kingdom simply could not follow. Her face did something complicated. Wonder, and loss, and relief, all arguing.

Then she sneezed, because she was a child standing in snow, and the moment broke, and we walked home.

* * *

The hut was exactly as we'd left it, which felt like a miracle until I remembered that no one had wanted to steal from a poison-taster's house. The fire was cold. The good chair by the fire was, in fact, good. Osk claimed it within four minutes of arrival, established his maps on every available surface, and declared the vale "cartographically fascinating precisely because it refuses to be mapped," which is the sort of thing a man says when he has made peace with seeing no boundaries for the rest of his life.

I lit the fire. Green flames licked up for half a heartbeat—old habit, old echo, the memory of magic in wood—and then settled into honest orange, because we were in the vale now, and the vale keeps no wonders. Just warmth. Just light. Just the ordinary chemistry of burning, which suddenly seemed like plenty.

Lark stood in the middle of the room, turning slowly, taking inventory of a life with almost nothing in it.

"It's small," she said.

"Yes."

"And quiet."

"Very."

She thought about it. Outside, the wind moved through the pines with the vast indifference of a world that had never contained a single spell, and the snow came down soft and steady, filling in our tracks from the pass, erasing the evidence that anyone had ever come from anywhere.

"I like it," she announced. "Nobody here knows I used to be important."

I laughed until I had to sit down. Because that was it, exactly, the whole inheritance boiled down to a child's wisdom: *nobody here knows I used to be important.* Freedom, described accurately at last.

* * *

Winter settled in the way it does in the vale—thoroughly, without drama. We made routines. Osk drew maps of places that don't exist for Lark, complete with compass roses, and mailed careful, brilliant studies of real coastlines to the capital, where cartographers hated him exactly as much as predicted. Lark learned letters, and goat-milking, and the fine art of losing gracefully at cards, which she refused to master. I cooked.

I cooked the way I'd always cooked, but differently. That's the strange mercy no one warns you about: a skill built for survival doesn't die when the survival ends. It just changes employment. I tasted everything still—out of habit, out of craft—but for the first time in my life, nothing needed testing. The broth went to the table unpoisoned and untasted-for-danger, seasoned only with salt and thyme and the shocking luxury of carelessness.

Some nights I caught myself checking the spoon twice. Old scars raise skin; old disciplines raise ghosts. But the ghosts got quieter each month. The numbness in my tongue, the immunity I'd built bite by bite across thirty years, faded slowly, the way a callus softens when the work stops. I could taste bitter things again. Sweet things. I wept, absurdly, the first time a plum surprised me.

On solstice night, Lark sang.

She sang in High Vhalenic, the last speaker of a last language, and she sang not the mourning songs or the gate-songs or the queen's laments, but a nonsense rhyme about a hedgehog who loses his hat. Osk kept time badly with his spoon. The fire burned orange and ordinary. Outside, the vale lay silent under the stars, keeping its held breath, holding nothing, guarding nothing, just being a valley full of snow and sleep.

And far away—at the coast, at the cliffs where a door of moonlight had stood and shut—I knew there was nothing. No shadow, no seam, no ache in my scars. The thirteenth moon had turned and passed like all the honest twelve before it.

But I'll tell you what I saw that night, walking back from checking on the goat, breath steaming, stars wheeling overhead in their ancient, magicless order.

Out past the fence line, where the snow lay smooth and unbroken, there was a patch of darkness shaped like a doorway. Just shadow, just starlight failing to fall quite right, just the kind of thing a tired mind makes out of winter.

I stood and looked at it for a long time.

It did not open. It was not going to open. It was only the memory of a door, the way a scar is the memory of a wound—the body remembering without hurting.

"Goodnight," I said to it, politely, in a language with one living speaker left.

The shadow stayed shut. The snow kept falling. And I went inside, to the fire, to the noise and the warmth and the small immortal ordinary evening—

where Lark was demanding a second song,

and Osk was losing at cards on purpose,

and the soup needed tasting by no one but me.
