## 1. The Valley That Refuses Magic

The valley had no name that anyone agreed upon, which suited its inhabitants fine. Maps showed it as a wrinkle in the mountains, a fold of green tucked between two ridgelines too steep for carts and too poor for kings. Travelers who stumbled down into it — and few did — often remarked, in the vague way travelers remark on things they cannot quite name, that something was *missing* here. Not a thing you could point to. A pressure, perhaps. A hum.

The people of the valley knew what was missing. Magic could not enter.

It wasn't a ward or a wall or any working anyone had ever laid down. The oldest residents, the ones who remembered other lives in other places, said it simply *was* so: a spell unraveled at the ridgeline like thread pulled from cloth; a charm went cold in the palm; a curse died with a sound like a sigh. The valley refused magic the way some soil refuses wheat — not out of malice, but nature. And because of that refusal, the exiles came. The hedge-witch whose coven had turned on her. The soldier whose war-gift had made him a weapon others wanted to own. The woman who could speak to rivers and had said one thing too many to one river too important. They came down the goat paths with their talents dying behind them at the pass, and they found a place where no one's gift made them valuable, and therefore no one's gift made them hunted.

They found, in short, a place to be ordinary.

Wendell Dunmore had come down into the valley nineteen years ago with nothing but a satchel of carving knives, a coat lined with bones that clinked when they walked, and a grief so fresh it still bled. They had been twenty-two years old and running from a city where their mother's body lay unburied because no one would carve her resting-plate without payment Wendell did not have. In the valley, someone had given them a roof, and then a shed, and then a shop, and somewhere in those years the grief had settled into something quieter — not gone, never gone, but worn smooth, like a stone carried so long in the pocket it becomes part of the hand.

Now Wendell was forty-one, and a bone-carver, and content.

Mostly content.

They rose before dawn, as always, in the small stone house attached to the workshop, and built the fire, and put water on for barley tea, and stood at the window while the light came up gray over the eastern ridge. The valley in morning was beautiful in a way that never announced itself. Mist pooled in the low fields where Maren's goats grazed. Smoke rose straight from a dozen chimneys because there was never wind enough to trouble it. Somewhere downhill, Odell's inn door banged open — Wendell could hear it even here, could hear everything here, because the valley was so quiet it seemed to lean in and listen along with you.

The quiet was the first thing you noticed about the trade, if you were foolish enough to ask about it. Everyone else in the world, according to the exiles, lived inside a constant faint murmur — the ambient noise of magic, of workings great and small, of spirits brushing against the living. Here there was none of that. Here the silence was total, except—

Except for the bones.

Wendell took their tea to the workshop, a long room with a south-facing window and a workbench worn into two shallow dips where their forearms rested. Along the walls hung the stock-in-trade: ribs and femurs and antler, deer mostly, and the occasional cow from Odell's slaughter, bleached white in lye baths and dried on racks. On the bench lay the current commission — a flute, half-finished, for the Harrow girl who was leaving in spring to marry a shepherd over the western pass. Her mother wanted her to have a piece of the valley to carry with her. Everyone understood what she meant. Nobody said it aloud. You did not say aloud, in this valley, the true purpose of a carved bone.

Because here was the secret that kept Wendell fed and kept Wendell strange and kept Wendell, on certain long evenings, company:

The dead spoke through carved bone.

Not everywhere. Not in every bone. But when Wendell carved — when the knife moved through the material and the shape emerged, spiral by spiral, line by line — sometimes the silence of the valley developed a seam. And through that seam came whispering.

It had started, as far as Wendell could reconstruct it, with their mother's plate. She had been a carver too, back in the city, and had taught them the knife before the alphabet. Wendell had carved her resting-plate themselves in the end, alone, unpaid, in a rented room, weeping, and when they set their palm flat on the finished bone they had heard her voice — thin, unmistakable, amused: *You always did press too hard, love.* The city had magic in it, of course; the city murmured day and night. Wendell had assumed it was grief. Grief makes voices; everyone knows that.

But then they'd come to the valley, where there was no magic, no murmur, nothing — and carved a button, idly, the second week — and heard an old man's chuckle, dry as chalk, saying *left-handed, that stitch* — a tailor, apparently, dead fifty years, whose bones had been ground into fertilizer and whose dust had gotten into a cow and thence into a knucklebone on Wendell's bench. He'd talked for six days, complaining amiably about modern waistcoats, and then faded, and Wendell had sat in the workshop shaking, laughing, crying, understanding three things at once.

First: it was real. Second: it only worked through carved bone — plain bone was silent; the carving opened the door somehow, though neither Wendell nor the dead could say why. And third: it only happened here, in the valley, where the absence of all other voices left these faint ones audible, like stars visible only far from any city's glow.

The dead who whispered through Wendell's carvings were not the dead of the valley's graveyard, exactly. They were older, more scattered, stranger — fragments of whoever had lived and died wherever the bone had been bone. A deer's leg-bone might hold a hunter's last thought, or nothing at all, or — once, memorably — a song in no language Wendell knew, four notes repeating, patient as rain. The dead rarely explained themselves. They rarely stayed long. They came like drafts under a door, said a thing or two, and were gone, and Wendell had learned not to chase them.

What they had also learned, over nineteen years, was how to make things worth hearing.

* * *

The flute took shape under the knife through the grey morning. Deer femur, seasoned two years, hollowed with a hand-drill Wendell's mother had made and Wendell's hands had worn smooth. The design was the valley's own — spirals wound around the shaft like climbing vines, each spiral ending in a small stacked figure, a person standing on a person standing on a person, five high, because the Harrow family was five and would be five even after the daughter left. Wendell had invented that motif themselves, years ago, and now half the valley asked for it: the stacked figures, the little tower of the living.

"Careful with the third hole," said a voice like dry leaves. "She bites her lower lip when she plays. Always has."

Wendell didn't startle anymore; nineteen years will cure you. "Good morning, Master Ellery," they said to the empty workshop. The old schoolmaster had died in the city two generations back — his bones had traveled as cargo, as meal, as finally a marrow-spoon, and his spoon had ended up in Odell's kitchen scrap pail, and Odell, who knew exactly what he was doing, saved Wendell the large bones every slaughter-day wrapped in brown paper with no comment whatsoever.

"She'll do fine over west," Ellery's whisper continued, from everywhere and nowhere, the way the dead spoke — not from the bone exactly, but through it, the way wind is not in the flute. "Better than fine. She used to cheat off my best student, you know. Cleverly, though. I respect clever cheating. It takes twice the wit of honest study."

"Is that why you're here? To gossip about the Harrow girl?"

"Why else does anyone linger?" A pause, and then, softer, in the tone the dead got when they were being careful with the living: "Why else does anyone stay anywhere?"

Wendell let that sit, and kept carving, and did not answer it, because they knew what the old man was actually asking. The dead who came through Wendell's bench had watched them for years — drifting in and out with whatever bones passed across it — and the perceptive ones, Ellery chief among them, had assembled a picture of Wendell Dunmore the way you assemble a face from shadow: accurately enough to hurt.

Content, guarded, lonely. Three words, and only one of them a lie, depending on the day.

The valley was good to Wendell. That was the truth underneath everything, the bedrock. Here they were somebody — not grand, but fixed, known, needed. Every household owned something Wendell had carved. Buttons and combs, spoons and needles, whistles for children and grave-plates for the honored dead, and once, for old Tam Voss, a whole hand, carved from his own finger-bones after death, articulated with sinew and pegs, because Tam had sworn he'd be buried holding his wife's hand and his own hand had rotted with him before her. The valley had stood around that graveside, forty exiles in a cold drizzle, watching two carved hands clasped over a double grave, and nobody had called it morbid, because in this valley everyone understood that the body was just the last coat you wore, and Wendell Dunmore was simply very, very good with coats.

And the whispers — the whispers meant Wendell was never entirely alone. There was always a voice, eventually. Some fragment riding in on the latest delivery from Odell's yard, ready to complain or reminisce or sing. Nineteen years of it. Enough company, surely.

Surely.

Wendell set down the knife at midday and ate bread and hard cheese on the step, watching the mist burn off Maren's fields. Down the lane, the village proper stirred: the smithy hammering, the tanner's racks, children shouting at a hoop. Forty-one souls, give or take, plus a dog count that fluctuated. Small enough that everyone's business was common property and large enough to pretend otherwise. Maren Oduya ran it, in the loose way valleys are run — by opinion, memory, and the strategic deployment of disapproval. She was seventy-three and had once spoken to rivers, and now spoke to goats, and claimed the goats were better conversationalists.

"You're brooding," said Ellery's whisper, from the windowsill, where the half-finished flute lay in the sun.

"I'm eating."

"Eating and brooding. I had a student who could do both at once. Failed his examinations spectacularly." A rustle of dead attention. "Solstice is coming."

"I know when the solstice is."

"Do you? Time goes strangely in this valley. Nothing enters and nothing leaves, including the years. You've been telling yourself you'll go over the pass 'next spring' for nineteen springs, Wendell Dunmore. See the ocean again. Visit your mother's grave. Next spring." The old ghost's voice gentled further, which was somehow worse. "I only mean to say — the valley keeps you, but keeping and hiding are cousins, and families quarrel."

"I'm not hiding. I'm *here*. There's a difference."

"There is," Ellery agreed pleasantly. "It's just smaller than you think."

Wendell finished the cheese, went inside, and shut the window, which did nothing at all, since the dead did not use windows.

* * *

Afternoon brought customers, which was its own kind of conversation, and easier.

Old Sefa came for a comb, her third, because she kept snapping them — "not my fault, your teeth are too fine, Wendell, make them coarser" — and stayed an hour to talk about her granddaughter and the price of indigo and whether the new couple at the mill were happy or merely polite, a question the entire valley had been chewing since autumn. Then the Tanner boys, wanting a whistle apiece, having broken their previous two, and Wendell made them recite the rule before handing anything over: *never whistle it at night, never whistle it near the standing stones, never whistle it when you're angry.* The boys recited it with the bored fluency of catechism and ran off jangling.

The rule existed because of the whispers. Children's whistles caught fragments too — usually nothing, usually just air — but sometimes, at night, when the valley's silence deepened until it rang, a whistle would catch a voice, and a child would come running to Wendell's door at midnight saying the whistle *said my grandmother's name*, and what was Wendell supposed to do with that except sit the child down, take the whistle, listen, confirm, and walk them home through the dark explaining, gently, for the hundredth time, that the dead were not ghosts and not demons and mostly not dangerous, they were just — leftover. Echoes in the material. People don't fully leave, Wendell would say. Not all at once. Some of us take longer than others.

The valley had absorbed this doctrine with remarkable ease. Exiles are a credulous population about comfort; they'd fled a world full of magic and had found in Wendell's workshop a magic-shaped hole with something kind living in it. Maren had pronounced judgment in Wendell's first year, arms crossed outside the shed while Wendell trembled, waiting to be driven out: "So the bones talk. Good. Someone in this valley ought to have a talent, even a borrowed one." And that had been that. Wendell belonged.

By dusk the light went amber and long, and Wendell cleaned the knives, oiled the drill, and covered the flute with linen against the damp. The evening ritual, unchanged in nineteen years: bank the fire, bar the door, stand a moment in the middle of the quiet room listening to the particular quality of the valley's silence, which was not empty the way a room is empty but full the way a held breath is full.

On the shelf above the bench, in the place of honor, sat a single object: a small carved plate, no bigger than a handspan, spiral-wound, the first thing Wendell had ever carved in the valley. It held no voice — Wendell had never heard a word from it in nineteen years, which was itself strange, because every other piece they'd ever cut had eventually whispered something. This one stayed silent, utterly, absolutely, as if it were not open but *closed*, as if the door in it faced the wrong way.

Wendell's mother's second resting-plate. Carved from a chip of her coffin-nail box, the only wood she'd ever owned that mattered. Wendell had made it the first week in the valley, homesick past bearing, hoping to hear her again — and heard nothing, ever, and had never been able to decide whether that meant she was truly gone, or somewhere the valley's silence couldn't reach, or — and this was the thought Wendell did not permit often — whether she was *refusing*, the way the valley refused, keeping herself folded away behind some door Wendell couldn't find the seam of.

"Still talking to the shelf?" Ellery's whisper drifted up from the scrap bin, wry.

"Goodnight, Master Ellery."

"Goodnight, Wendell. Mind the third hole."

Wendell blew out the lamp.

* * *

Sleep came late, as it often did. Wendell lay in the dark upstairs and listened to the valley breathe, and did the accounting they did most nights, honestly, because lying to yourself in the dark wastes the dark.

Content: yes. The valley had saved their life, and gave them work that mattered, and neighbors who knew their tea, and a trade no one else alive could practice. Guarded: also yes, and unapologetically. Nineteen years ago Wendell had learned what the wide world did to people who were useful — it used them. Here, nothing entered, and so nothing could take. The deliberate smallness of this life was not cowardice. It was architecture. Wendell had built it stone by stone, and it held.

Lonely: well.

There was the ache. Wendell turned onto their side and looked at the dark wall and let it be true for the permitted minute. Lonely the way a well is lonely — surrounded by water, giving water, useful beyond measure, and yet never once touched by it. Because here was the thing about hearing the dead: it was company, but it was not companionship. The dead could not stay. The dead could not change. The dead could not, in nineteen years, answer the only question Wendell had never managed to stop carrying, the question that lived under the ribs like a swallowed stone: *is this all? Is this the whole shape of my life — a warm shop, a silent valley, voices that leave?*

Everyone in the valley was an exile from something. Most of them had stopped looking back. Wendell had stopped looking back too, outwardly, thoroughly, convincingly.

But tonight — Wendell couldn't have said why, except that Ellery had mentioned the solstice, and solstice always thinned something in the walls of things — tonight the wind coming down off the pass sounded different. Not louder. The valley didn't allow loud. Just *determined*, like a traveler walking all night rather than stopping, like something that had crossed a very great distance and was not going to be turned aside by a mere absence of magic.

Far off, at the top of the ridge, thunder muttered — early for the season, wrong for the clear sky — and Wendell felt it through the floorboards, through the bones of the house, through the small dense bones of their own ears.

In the workshop below, on the shelf, in the dark, the silent plate sat silent as always.

But in the scrap bin, in the half-finished flute, in every piece of carved bone in the building, the dead — all of them at once, the chatty and the mute, the fragments and the songs — went still.

Not quiet. Quiet was their natural state between words.

*Still.* The way a forest goes still when something is moving through it that the animals recognize.

Wendell lay in the dark and listened to nineteen years of company hold its breath, and did not know yet that by tomorrow's dusk there would be a knock at the door, and a storm behind it, and a child standing in the rain holding a whistle Wendell had never made — and that the silence in the workshop tonight was the sound of the dead, for the first time in Wendell's life, being afraid.
