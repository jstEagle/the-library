## 3. What Cyrus Carves

The storm broke sometime before dawn, and by morning the valley wore its wet gray like a coat it could not take off. Water ran in bright threads down the slate roof of my workshop and dripped from the eaves into the trough I had placed there years ago for exactly that purpose. The dead did not care for rain. Their whispers came thin on wet days, muffled, as though they too pulled blankets over their heads. For twenty years I had found this comforting. That morning, with a mute child asleep under my roof and a bone whistle I did not remember carving sitting on my bench like an accusation, I found the silence unbearable.

I worked anyway. A carver works; that is the whole of the cure for most things.

The child — Cyrus, I had decided to call them, because the name had arrived in my head fully formed the moment I saw them, the way names sometimes do when the dead are feeling generous — slept curled in the chair by my stove, still wearing the soaked wool I had wrung out twice in the night. They were perhaps ten years old, small for it, with the kind of thinness that comes not from poverty but from long fear. They had eaten three bowls of barley soup without once looking at me, watching the door between spoonfuls as if the door owed them money or meant to collect some.

They had not spoken. Of course they had not spoken. There was something wrong with their throat, or their tongue, or whatever deeper place words come from — I had seen them try, in the first terrible minutes, mouth opening around nothing, hands flying up to cover the failure as if they'd been struck. Then the whistling had stopped, the dead had gone silent all at once like birds before a landslide, and neither of us had done much of anything since except survive the night.

I split a piece of birch and set it in the vise. My knives were laid out in their proper order: roughing, detail, hook, the little crooked one I used for eyes. Bone waited in the brine barrel for the afternoon. Ordinary work. Sacred ordinary work, if you wanted to call it that, which I didn't, mostly.

Behind me, the child woke without sound. That was the first strange thing I noticed that day — later there would be so many more that this one nearly got lost — but at the time it stopped my knife mid-stroke. Everyone wakes with a noise. A breath, a shift, a small animal complaint against consciousness. This child surfaced out of sleep the way a fish rises through dark water: no ripple at all. One moment sleeping. The next, wide-eyed, watching me carve.

* * *

They began carving before breakfast.

Not wood — well, yes, wood, but not the way anyone carves. I have spent my life among people who make marks on things: letters on invoices, tally-scratches on barn doors, the crude hearts and hammers boys cut into fence rails. What Cyrus did was none of these. They took the birch offcuts from my scrap basket — I let them, curious despite myself — and a nail I had left bent in a corner, and they began to draw.

Spirals. That was the heart of it, spirals everywhere, but spirals with intention, spirals that tightened toward a center or loosened toward an edge, each one different, each one saying something. Around the spirals they stacked figures — small vertical shapes, like people made of lines, some standing alone, some leaning together, some crossed out with a single decisive stroke. They worked fast, faster than seemed possible for fingers that small, and they worked with the desperate fluency of someone speaking their mother tongue to a stranger in a foreign country, hoping speed would bridge the gap where vocabulary failed.

I stood at my bench pretending to work on a flute blank and watched them in the reflection off the polished horn plate I keep for checking angles.

They finished a piece of birch about the size of my palm and held it out to me, arm extended, face carefully empty. Offering. Not pleading — offering. There is a difference, and the difference is pride, and even half-drowned children have it.

I took it. The spiral at the center wound inward through seven turns — I counted — and ended at a small circle, deeply cut. Figures stood along the outer edge: four upright, two leaning, one crossed out. Below the crossed-out figure, a shape like a house, or a box, or a grave. Rain-lines fell across the whole of it, diagonal strokes in ranks, unmistakable.

"Storm," I said aloud, stupidly, testing. "You were out in the storm."

No reaction. Not to the word itself — but when I tapped the crossed-out figure, then pointed at the box-shape beneath it, the child went very still, and their eyes came up to mine with a weight that had nothing childish in it at all. Confirmation. Or grief. Possibly both.

I turned the scrap over. On the back, smaller, almost hidden: a single figure standing apart from the others, beside a shape I eventually understood to be a door.

Me. Last night. Opening the door.

I set the carving down with more care than the act deserved. My hands were steady — they always are, that's the trade — but something behind my sternum had begun, quietly, to knock.

* * *

By midday I had learned the grammar, or the edge of it.

Cyrus carved the way water finds cracks. Every surface became text: the tabletop, the bench legs, the end grain of the log pile visible through the window (they pressed their face to the glass and carved in the fog of their own breath, which told me how long they had been doing this, how deep the habit ran). Spirals were — time? Distance? Journeys? I couldn't be sure. The stacked figures were people, or kinds of people, or maybe roles: the crossed stroke meant ended, gone, dead. I tested this gently, laying my hand flat over the figure with the box beneath it, and Cyrus watched me do it and did not look away, and did not cry, which frightened me more than tears would have. Children who can't afford to cry have been crying for a long time already, somewhere I couldn't see.

I talked to fill the space, because that is what I do when I am afraid, though I would have denied it under torture.

"You'll want to know the rules of the house," I said, splitting kindling while they watched from the chair. "There aren't many. You eat what's cooked. You put the tools back where you found them — especially the sharp ones, especially the hook knife, I've had that since I was younger than you. And you don't go into the back room."

They tilted their head. The question needed no carving.

"The back room is where the bones wait," I said. "Before they're carved. Some people find it upsetting."

It was a lie of scale, not substance. People found the bones upsetting, true enough — travelers who saw them through the window sometimes crossed the street rather than pass my shop again. But the reason I kept the room shut was older and stranger, and I had not said it aloud in twenty years, and I was not going to start now, not with a child whose arrival had coincided with the loudest silence I had ever heard.

Here is the thing about my valley, the thing I have never written down and never will, the thing the exiles who live here came here to escape having known: magic does not enter it. It isn't warded, exactly. It isn't defended. The valley simply refuses, the way certain ground refuses certain crops. Spells gutter here. Curses arrive at the ridgeline and forget what they were angry about. The gifted — and our village is largely built of the gifted, runaways and cast-outs and self-exiled oddities, which is why we pretend so hard to be boring — the gifted wake up here feeling, for the first time in years, quiet.

Except me. Except the bone-carver, who hears the dead whisper through whatever he carves.

I had explained it to myself a hundred ways over twenty years. Perhaps bone remembers differently than flesh. Perhaps the valley's refusal has a seam in it, and I am the seam. Perhaps the dead simply speak softly enough to slip past any door, even one nailed shut against them. I had settled, long ago, into the comfort of not knowing. The whispers were quiet, patient, fragmentary — old neighbors commenting on weather, on craft, on the quality of the glue. They had never asked anything of me. They had never warned me of anything. They were company, and company was the one thing I had thought I didn't need until I had it, and I had built my whole deliberate smallness on top of that foundation like a house on a hillside, never asking what held the hill up.

At noon, Cyrus ate bread and cheese standing up, still carving. At half past noon, they stopped carving, went rigid, and stared at the half-finished flute lying on my bench.

* * *

I want to describe that flute honestly, because everything that happened after depends on it, and honesty is the only tool I've ever owned that doesn't dull.

I had started it nine days earlier. Elderberry wood — no, that's not right, elderberry is for whistles, flutes want something denser — plum wood, then, cured two winters, bored with six holes and a seventh unfinished, the bore reamed smooth, the exterior shaped but unpolished, sitting on my bench in the state every instrument of mine passes through: the state of being listened to. Because that is when the dead speak most clearly. Finished pieces hold their voices the way bottles hold messages. Half-made pieces are open doors, and through those doors the dead lean in and murmur, and I carve their murmurs into the walls of the instrument so that when it is played, the player carries the dead's small talk with them into the world.

It had been a flute for a shepherd's daughter up-valley. Nothing in it. Sheep, weather, a joke about her brother. The usual.

When Cyrus stared at it, the flute began to whisper.

Not the usual murmur. I knew the usual murmur the way you know your own heartbeat — dozens of overlapping voices, soft as silt, saying nothing that mattered. This was different. This was one voice, or many voices speaking as one, and it came up through the unfinished seventh hole like smoke through a crack, and it said:

*Run.*

I dropped the kindling. I am not a man who drops things; steadiness is my trade and my armor. I dropped the kindling and stood in the middle of my workshop listening to a plum-wood flute tell me a word I had never heard the dead say in twenty years.

They said names sometimes. Weather. Reminiscence. Once, memorably, a complaint about my glue. Never urgency. Never fear. The dead, in my experience, are past fear the way the mountains are past weather — it happens below them somewhere, and does not concern them.

*Run,* said the flute. *Run. Run.*

And Cyrus, who could not speak, who had been mute since before they reached my door — Cyrus opened their mouth and made a sound. Not a word. A note. A single low tone, round and old, that matched the flute's whisper the way a key matches a lock, and every loose bone in my workshop answered.

You have to understand what that means. Bones answer me, not each other. The carved pieces hum faintly when the dead speak through them, and only them, and only because of me. But when that child made that one note, the raw blanks in the back room — behind the closed door, the ones waiting for the knife — knocked against each other on their shelf like teeth chattering. The finished flutes on their pegs shivered. The grave-goods in the crate bound for up-valley rattled in their straw. Twenty years of careful, quiet, deliberate smallness, and in the space of one held note, every dead thing I owned sat up and paid attention.

The note stopped. Cyrus closed their mouth. The workshop settled, creaking, back into its ordinary silences, and outside the rain went on falling on a valley that refused all magic, including, apparently, whatever this was.

Cyrus picked up a fresh scrap of birch and the bent nail and carved, quickly, five figures in a row. Four upright, one crossed out. Above them, a spiral winding tight, tighter than any they had cut yet, ending in a point instead of a circle. Then they looked at me and pointed, with terrible clarity, first at the figures, then south — toward the pass, toward the world beyond the ridgeline — then at themselves.

Then, after a pause, at me.

"No," I said.

They hadn't asked anything yet. I said it anyway, because I knew what was coming the way animals know storms, and I hated myself a little for the speed of it.

"I don't leave the valley." My voice came out flat, final, a door closing. "Nobody here leaves. That's rather the point of here."

Cyrus looked at me for a long moment. Then they set down the nail, walked to my bench, and picked up the half-finished flute with both hands, carefully, the way you handle something living. They held it out to me.

*Take it,* the gesture said. *Listen again.*

"Don't—" I started, and stopped, because the flute was already whispering, and the whisper was no longer just *run.*

It said other words now. Few of them. Fragments, the way the dead always spoke, but larger fragments than usual, urgent, tumbling over each other: *— god — sleeping — the choir is broken — the last voice — solstice —*

Solstice. Forty days off. I counted without wanting to; arithmetic is another thing that steadies me.

And underneath the fragments, woven through them, persistent as a stitch through cloth, the same single syllable, over and over, patient now instead of panicked, the way you repeat a thing to a child or a fool:

*Run. Run. Run.*

I took the flute from Cyrus's hands. My fingers found the unfinished seventh hole and covered it, an instinctive kindness, closing the door. The whisper dimmed but did not stop. Nothing was going to stop it now; I understood that. Something had been set in motion the moment this child crossed my threshold with a whistle I didn't remember carving, and the dead — my quiet, patient, twenty-year companions — had finally, after all those years of small talk, asked something of me.

Cyrus watched me across the workshop, rain-light silvering their face, and carved one more figure into the bench-top where I couldn't miss it: a single upright person, beside a door, holding something small.

Then they pointed at the crossed-out figure in their earlier carving. The one above the box. The one beneath the rain.

Whoever that had been — monk, parent, whoever had pressed a bone whistle into small palms and turned to face whatever came next — they had been given the same choice I was looking at now. Someone had stayed behind so that this child could run.

I am not a brave man. I want that on the record, carved somewhere permanent. I am a bone-carver who built a small life inside a valley that refuses the world, and I liked my small life, and every plank of it was laid down deliberately, defensively, over the ache of things I had lost or never had.

But I stood there in my workshop holding a half-finished flute full of frightened ghosts, and I felt the first crack run through all of it — the deliberate smallness, the guarded quiet, twenty years of comfortable silence — and I heard, with perfect clarity, the sound a foundation makes when it decides to stop holding.

Outside, far up the pass road, a dog began to bark. Not at the rain.

At a rider.
