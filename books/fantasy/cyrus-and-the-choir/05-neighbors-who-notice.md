## 5. Neighbors Who Notice

Maren Ostwick had buried three husbands, outlived two kings, and been exiled from one country whose name she refused to speak aloud, and she had learned long ago that in a valley full of people running from something, the first sign of trouble was never a sound. It was a silence. A door that stayed shut. A chair at the market table left empty.

So when Tamsin Reed came up the hill on washday with her arms full of linens she had no intention of washing and her mouth full of everything else, Maren knew before a word was said that the trouble had already arrived. She just didn't yet know its shape.

"They say it's a child," Tamsin said, setting the basket down on Maren's porch rail without being invited, which was how everyone in this valley treated Maren's porch, because Maren had never once managed to make them afraid of it. "Down at Wendell's place. Been there four days now. Petra's boy swears he saw it through the window — small thing, dark hair, standing at the bone-carver's workbench like it belonged there."

"Children belong at workbenches," Maren said, shelling peas into a bowl that sat between them like a shared alibi. "That's where children have always belonged. The ones worth anything."

"Not like this." Tamsin lowered her voice as though the wind might carry it somewhere it shouldn't go. "Petra says it doesn't talk. Not won't — *doesn't*. And Wendell's been to market twice since it came, buying food for two, and you know Wendell. You could count the times Wendell's bought food for anyone but themself on one hand with fingers left over."

Maren kept her eyes on the peas. The pods split under her thumbs with small green sounds, and she let the rhythm of it hold her face still. But inside, some old instinct — the same one that had gotten her family out of Vessany with their necks intact, the one that had made her the person exiles brought their troubles to whether she wanted them or not — turned over like a dog hearing a distant bell.

"A mute child," she said.

"That's what they say."

"And it went to Wendell's door."

"That's what they say."

Maren looked up at the ridge, where Dunmore's chimney put out its thin thread of smoke into a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Four days. A silent child at the door of the only person in the valley who spoke to the dead.

"Well," she said. "Peas won't shell themselves."

* * *

By evening the whole settlement had an opinion, which meant nobody had any information.

They gathered in the long room of the old tithe barn, because that was what they did — every exile who'd washed up in the valley over the last thirty years, drawn together by the simple fact that nowhere else would have them. Hessa the dyer, whose hands were permanently blue to the wrist. Old Corvin, who'd been a soldier before he'd been a coward before he'd been a beekeeper, in that order, and who told the story differently depending on the drink. The Aldous twins, who'd fled a debt rather than a regime and were therefore considered slightly suspect by the rest of them, debt being a crime the exiles understood all too well. Petra and her endless boys. A dozen more. They called themselves neighbors, though half of them had come from places that had never heard of each other, and they called it a village, though it wasn't, quite, and they called their gatherings council, though no one had ever voted on anything except where to put the new fence.

It was a village the way a quilt is a blanket — patched, mismatched, held together by the stubbornness of the hands that made it.

"The Ashen Order takes children," Corvin announced, with the confidence of a man who had read about the Ashen Order in a pamphlet and never met one. "Everyone knows that. Burned the monastery at Callowmere to its foundation stones last spring. Monks, novices, the lot. If there's a child loose from that business—"

"It's not from Callowmere," Hessa said. "Callomere's six hundred miles east. What's a child doing walking six hundred miles alone?"

"Maybe it didn't walk alone."

"Maybe somebody walked it here," Petra said darkly, "and left it. Like you'd leave a sick calf at a neighbor's gate."

"Nobody leaves a calf at *Wendell's* gate," one of the Aldous twins said. "No offense to Wendell. But if you wanted a child cared for, wouldn't you take it to Maren?"

Every head turned to Maren, who was mending a net by the fire and wished very much that she were anywhere else, including several of the places she had been exiled from.

"I haven't seen the child," she said. "I don't intend to gossip about a child I haven't seen, as though it were weather. If Wendell wants us to know something, Wendell will tell us, or won't, and either way it's Wendell's to tell." She bit her thread. "What I want to know is why none of you are asking the question that matters."

"What question?" asked the other twin.

"Who told the child this valley existed?"

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has said the true thing out loud. Because that was the arrangement. That was the covenant, unwritten and unspoken and absolute: the valley took you in if you found it, and once you were in it, you did not describe it to outsiders. No letters with the valley's name. No directions given at the pass. The road in was deliberately bad and deliberately unmarked, and the folk who lived along it had a habit of misremembering distances for strangers. For thirty years the trick had worked. Magic couldn't cross the valley's rim — that was its own mystery, older than any of them, the reason half the exiles had chosen it — but neither, mostly, did the world's attention.

And now a mute child had appeared at the door of the bone-carver, from nowhere, knowing exactly where to knock.

"You think someone sent it," Hessa said slowly.

"I think children don't find valleys by accident." Maren folded the net. "And I think the world has started asking after us again, because Tam's cousin drove sheep up near the pass last week, and there was a traveler on the road. Gray cloak. Riding, not walking. Asked Tam's cousin how far it was to the low country, and then asked him—" she paused, because she had promised herself she would say it plainly even if she said nothing else plainly, "—asked him whether there were many settlements this side of the mountain. Whether folk here kept to themselves."

Corvin snorted. "Half the world keeps to themselves. That's a shepherd's question, not a spy's."

"Maybe." Maren set the net aside. "Tam's cousin said the man had a way of looking past you while he talked to you. Like you were furniture between him and the view." She let that sit. "He also said the man's horse was too good for his clothes. And that when he rode off toward the pass, he went slow, the way a man goes when he's counting fences."

Nobody laughed at that one. In the firelight Maren watched a dozen faces do the arithmetic she had already done — the child arriving, the stranger circling — and arrive, one after another, at the sum she had reached on washday morning.

"So what do we do?" Petra asked. "Send someone down to Wendell's? Warn them?"

"Wendell knows how to mind their own house," Maren said. "Wendell's minded it longer than most of you have been alive." But even as she said it, she thought of Wendell at market these past weeks — thinner, distracted, answering questions a beat late — and she amended silently, *and that's exactly why I'll go myself.*

* * *

The path down to Dunmore's workshop wound through birch scrub and old field walls, and Maren walked it slowly, partly because her knees had opinions and partly because she wanted time to decide what she was going to say.

She had known Wendell Dunmore for nineteen years, since the day a hollow-eyed young carver had appeared at the tithe barn door with a satchel of tools and a story so carefully empty that Maren had recognized the craftsmanship instantly. She'd fed them stew. She'd shown them the abandoned smithy up the slope, dry-roofed, near water, far from everyone. She hadn't asked a single question, and Wendell hadn't offered a single answer, and between those two mercies a friendship had grown — the quiet kind, built of borrowed tools and returned plates and the occasional evening when Wendell would sit on Maren's porch and say almost nothing, comfortably, for hours.

In nineteen years, Wendell had never once taken in a guest.

Maren knocked at the workshop door and waited. From inside came the small familiar sounds — the scrape of a blade, the whisper of bone dust — and then footsteps, and the door opened on Wendell Dunmore looking, Maren thought, like a woman who had slept badly for four nights and would happily sleep badly for forty more rather than discuss why.

"Maren." A pause, recalibrating. "Is something wrong with the fence line?"

"Fence line's fine. Can I come in, or does your new lodger make a policy of it?"

Something flickered across Wendell's face — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. As if they'd been waiting for the valley to notice and had hoped to be granted another week of invisibility.

Inside, the workshop smelled of oil and marrow-dust and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the stove. And there, at the far end of the bench, sat the child.

Small. Seven, maybe eight years old. Dark-haired, thin in the way of things that had gone hungry recently and for a long time before that. It — she, he, Maren couldn't tell and suspected the distinction mattered less than it seemed — was carving. A scrap of pine, a short knife, and total absorption; the child didn't look up when the door opened, didn't look up when Maren crossed the room, didn't look up until Wendell said, softly, "Cyrus. This is Maren. She's a friend."

Then the child looked up, and Maren felt the look go through her like a cold draft under a door.

Not because the eyes were strange. They weren't — brown, wary, ordinary. It was the *weight* behind them. Maren had seen that weight twice in her life: once in a priestess dying of a plague she'd spent her life fighting, and once in her grandmother, in the last winter, sitting by a window watching for someone who was never coming home. It was the look of a person carrying something enormous and having decided, already, that no one else could be told about it.

"She can't hear you wondering," Wendell said quietly. Maren realized her face had been doing its thinking out loud, as it always did. "And it's not catching. Sit down. There's tea if you don't mind it weak."

Maren sat. The tea was weak. She drank it anyway, watching the child carve over the rim of her cup. Spirals. Stacked figures, small and precise, row upon row, like a ledger written in a script she didn't know. The knife moved with a sureness that made her chest tighten — no child held a blade that way unless a blade had been the steadiest thing in its life for a long time.

"She talks with those," Wendell said. "Carvings. It's — we're working it out."

"Does she talk any other way?"

"No."

There was a silence with a great deal packed into it. Maren set down her cup.

"I'll come to it, then, since we're both pretending I came about tea." She kept her voice level and her eyes on Wendell's. "Tam's cousin was up by the pass road eight days back. Met a traveler. Gray cloak, good horse, questions dressed as small talk. How far to the low country. Whether folk this side of the mountain keep to themselves." She watched Wendell's hands go still around the teapot. "Rode off counting fences. And four days before that, a silent child turns up at your door, from nowhere, knowing to knock here. I don't believe in coincidence, Wendell. I've been exiled by better-armed coincidences than this."

Wendell didn't answer at once. They looked at the child — Cyrus — and something passed between them, wordless, the way meaning passes between people who have spent days learning each other's silences. Then Wendell set down the pot.

"How bad?" they asked simply. Which was the thing Maren loved about them, underneath everything: no protest, no performance. Just the question underneath the question.

"One rider. Asking, not searching. Yet." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on the bench, an old habit, aligning nothing. "But riders ask before searchers search, and searchers search before soldiers come, and I've seen this ladder climbed before. So. I'm not asking you to tell me what the child is carrying. I'm telling you that whatever it is, someone else may be carrying a map to it. And I'm asking you — as the closest thing this valley has to a head that thinks — whether we should start thinking about what happens if that rider comes down the pass instead of over it."

For the first time, Cyrus spoke. Not with voice — Maren had already accepted that the voice was a locked room — but with the knife. The child turned the pine scrap over and carved three quick strokes, then held it up: a spiral, tight-coiled, and beside it a figure lying flat, and beside that a figure standing with arms raised.

Wendell went pale in a way Maren had never seen.

"What's it say?" Maren asked.

"It says—" Wendell stopped. Started again. Their voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "The dead told us to run. Two nights ago. Through the flute on my bench — one word. *Run.*" They looked at Maren with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life, though she wouldn't understand all of it until much later. "Cyrus just carved that the running hasn't started yet. That there's still time. That time is the whole question."

Outside, the wind moved through the birches with a sound like many people whispering in a large room, and Maren — who had lived sixty-eight years without once believing the valley's ghosts were anything but Wendell's loneliness given craft — felt the hairs rise on her arms and sternly told them to stop.

"Then here's what I'll do," she said, standing, because sitting felt suddenly like a luxury. "I'll put Corvin on the pass road — he's useless in a fight and excellent at seeming harmless, and men in gray cloaks don't look twice at old shepherds. I'll have word sent to every house on the north slope: travelers get answered slowly and wrongly. And I'll tell the rest of them—" she paused, choosing, "—that there's sickness down this way and best give the workshop its distance. Nothing empties a valley road faster than fear of contagion."

"That's a lie that protects her," Wendell said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I've told worse lies for worse reasons." Maren pulled her shawl straight and looked at the child one more time. Cyrus was watching her — had been watching her throughout, she realized, reading her the way the child read everything, in carved lines and stacked figures. And as Maren reached the door, the knife moved again, quick, and Wendell translated in a voice gone rough:

"She says: thank you for lying well. She says the last people who lied for her burned for it."

The wind pressed against the shutter. Somewhere far up the valley, toward the pass, a bird went up out of the scrub all at once, a whole flock startled skyward by something none of them could see.

Maren stood in the doorway a moment longer than she needed to.

"Keep the shutters closed after dark," she said. "Both of you. And Wendell—" she hesitated, then said the thing she'd actually climbed down the hill to say, the thing she'd wrap in fence-talk and tea-talk and never say plain, because plain things can be repeated by enemies, "—if it comes to it, you know the tithe barn holds forty people badly and one child well. Whatever else happens. That door opens both ways."

She climbed back up through the birch scrub as the light went copper, and at the top of the path she stopped and looked north, toward the pass, where the road bent out of sight behind the shoulder of the mountain.

Far away — so far she might have invented it — there was a smudge of dust on that road. Moving. Slowly. The way a man rides when he is counting fences.

Maren stood watching until the light failed, memorizing the direction and the hour. Then she turned and walked, faster than her knees liked, toward the tithe barn, where the lamps would be lit and the exiles would be arguing, and began — for the second time in her long life — to prepare her people for a war she intended to lie about beautifully.
