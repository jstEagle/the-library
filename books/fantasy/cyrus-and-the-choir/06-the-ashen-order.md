## 6. The Ashen Order

The fire had been burning for six days when Inquisitor Serle arrived at the monastery of Saint Vellum-on-the-Sea, and it was still burning.

That was the first wrong thing. Wood burns out. Thatch burns faster. Even stone, in a fire hot enough, will spit and crack and settle into gray ruin within a day or two. But the monastery had burned for six days without consuming itself, a low orange glow pulsing beneath the wreckage like embers trapped under a banked hearth, and when Serle dismounted at the edge of the scorched ground and stood looking at it, he understood that what burned here was not wood or thatch or any ordinary fuel at all.

It was song. Or it had been. The fire was eating the last of it.

"Keep back," he said to his riders, though none of them had moved forward. They were good men—careful men—and they knew better than to walk on ground where something holy had died badly. Serle walked on it himself because that was his work, and because someone had to, and because he had learned long ago that fear deferred was fear doubled. He crossed the ash field with his boots sinking softly into gray powder fine as flour, and the heat came up through the soles of his feet like a fever, and the smell—

The smell was not flesh. That was the second wrong thing. A monastery held forty-one souls according to the Order's census; Brother-Captain Hask's report said all forty-one had been inside when the fire was set. But there was no sweet-rot stink of burned bodies here, no fat-grease reek of a charnel pit. What there was instead was something almost pleasant: a scent like candle smoke and old hymnals, like incense burned down to nothing, like a room where people had sung for a very long time and then stopped.

Serle had been an Inquisitor of the Ashen Order for nineteen years. He had put down heretic cells in three provinces, broken a bone-witch's circle in the fens south of Corvane, and read more confessions than he could number. He knew every smell a fire could make. This one was new, and new things were his profession.

He found the first body near the collapsed bell tower—or rather, he found its outline. Ash held the shape of a man kneeling, arms folded across the chest, head bowed as if in prayer. When Serle crouched and touched the edge of the shape, the whole figure slid apart into powder, and beneath it there was nothing. No bone. No teeth. Nothing that would not burn, which meant nothing that had ever truly lived, which meant—

Which meant the monks of Saint Vellum had done something to themselves before the end. Something that unmade the body down to its last mineral truth. Serle filed this away, the way he filed everything, in the cold ledger of his mind, and kept walking.

* * *

The vessel had escaped through the sea gate two hours before the fire reached the cloister. That much the Order's watchers had confirmed: a small figure, wrapped in a novice's cloak too big for it, slipping down the cliff stair while the brothers above sang the building alight around themselves. The watchers had been given one instruction—to observe, not to engage—and they had obeyed it, because disobedience in the Ashen Order was punished by fire and obedience was rewarded with more orders. By the time anyone thought to send riders after the child, the tide road was empty and the child was a rumor moving inland.

A rumor with a name, now. Serle turned the word over in his mind as he picked through the ruins: *Cyrus*. It had come from a fisherman at the village below the cliffs, who had seen the child once, carried up from the sea in a coracle years ago, and remembered the odd name because his own dead son had borne it. The fisherman had wept while telling them. Serle had noted the weeping in his ledger too. Everything went in the ledger. Grief, fear, love—the whole wet machinery of the human heart ran on rails that a careful man could map, and once mapped, could be ridden to any destination. That was the first lesson of the Order. Fire was only ever the last resort of men who had failed at listening.

He found the chapter house by its foundation walls, and in the chapter house he found the scriptorium, and in the scriptorium he found what he had ridden four days through coastal rain to find: the archive, half-burned, mostly intact.

The monks had tried to destroy it. He could see the haste in it—scorch marks across the tops of the shelf-rows, a great heap of parchment reduced to curling black lace in the center of the floor—but they had run out of time, or nerve, or both. And so perhaps three hundred volumes remained, their edges charred, their spines swollen with damp, sitting in the dark like patient animals.

Serle did not touch them yet. First he stood in the doorway and let himself feel the shape of the room. That was another lesson of the Order, the one they taught second: before you take a thing, understand why it was kept. An archive was an argument against forgetting, and men built arguments against forgetting only about the things that frightened them most.

What frightened the monks of Saint Vellum, Serle already knew. The Order had files going back a hundred years. A god, sleeping. A choir, singing it down. A binding renewed each century by a living voice—a vessel—and a doctrine, old and half-shamefaced, that the god must never wake, because grief given godhood does not wake gently. The Order's founders had looked at that arrangement and asked the question that ambitious men always ask of locks: *who holds the key?* And then they had spent a hundred years trying to turn it.

They had failed. Every renewal required consent—that much the old texts made clear, in language the Order's translators had argued over for generations—and consent could not be forced. Children raised in the choir's tradition grew up willing, and so the bindings held, century after century, and the Order's founders died old and furious, and their successors inherited the fury and lost the patience, until somewhere in the last twenty years the patience had gone entirely and men like Brother-Captain Hask had begun to speak openly of *acceleration*.

Hask had set this fire. Hask had ordered the monastery burned with its forty-one singers inside, on the theory—written in his own hand, in a report Serle had read twice—that a binding renewed by force would hold just as well as one renewed by choice, if the vessel were young enough and frightened enough. Take the anchor alive. Break it. Sing through the screaming.

Serle had signed his concurrence at the bottom of the report, because signing was his duty, and duty was the architecture he had built his life inside. But he had signed it the way a man signs a ledger entry: noting, in the small cold corner of himself where he kept the things he did not say aloud, that Hask's theory rested on an assumption no one had tested.

No one had ever tried to make the god wake angry.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon, cataloguing. His riders camped outside the burn line and did not ask him questions, because he paid them well and frightened them better, and by dusk he had the archive crated and a working inventory in his ledger. Most of it was liturgy—centuries of hymns transcribed and re-transcribed, the same songs copied by generation after generation of careful hands, and Serle skimmed these with professional interest, marking the variations. The hymns changed over the years. Slowly, the way a river changes course, but they changed. Someone had been editing the Choir's music, decade by decade, and Serle wanted very much to know who, and why, and whether the edits had been corrections or erasures.

It was in the last crate, wedged behind a case of musical notation, that he found the whistle.

It was a flute, properly—a bone pipe, six finger-holes and a thumb, carved from the femur of some animal he could not identify. It was old. The surface had gone the color of weak tea and it was worn smooth at the mouthpiece where lips had pressed it ten thousand times. There was carving along its length: spirals, and stacked figures like little standing stones, and marks that were neither letters nor pictures but something older than either. Serle recognized the style from the Order's oldest plates. The language before kingdoms. The tongue the Choir had sung in since before there were words for anything else.

Someone had hidden it behind the notation cases deliberately, and recently—the dust told him that, the clean shadow where it had lain. A monk had tucked this away in the last minutes, meaning to come back for it, and had burned instead.

Serle sat down on a fallen rafter and turned the flute in his hands and felt nothing.

That should have been the end of it. A relic, a curiosity, a piece of evidence to add to the inventory: *one bone pipe, provenance unknown, decorative carving.* He began to write exactly that. Then he stopped, because his pen had paused of its own accord, hovering over the page, and he realized he was waiting.

For what? For sound? The thing was a whistle. Whistles made sound. And this whistle belonged to a tradition whose entire mechanism was resonance—the Choir sang, the bones carried song, the sleeping god heard music even in dreams, that was the whole architecture of the binding. If any object in the world might hold an echo, might carry a residue, might whisper with the voices of the dead who had played it—

Serle was not a superstitious man. He wanted that recorded plainly, even here, in the privacy of his own mind: he believed in what could be measured, catalogued, predicted, and controlled, and everything else he believed in the way a mason believes in mortar—as a substance useful for holding other things together. The Order's talk of souls and vessels and divine grief was, to Serle, a set of operational terms. Useful fictions. The god might be real or metaphor; the binding worked either way; therefore the binding was real, and the rest was commentary.

But he was also an honest man, in the narrow way that honest men are honest, and so when he raised the flute to his lips and blew, and heard nothing—not silence, but *nothing*, a nothing with weight and texture, a hole in the air—he wrote down what happened next with complete accuracy.

Nothing happened next.

He blew again, harder, and the holes gave their little breathy notes, thin and tuneless in the ruined scriptorium, and the sound fell into the ash outside and did not echo. Not dampened. Not muffled. *Unanswered.* As if he had spoken a sentence into a room and the room had declined to exist.

And Serle sat there in the dying light with a dead monk's flute in his hands, feeling the hair rise slowly on his forearms, and understood that he was afraid.

Not of ghosts. He catalogued the fear the way he catalogued everything, and what he found at the bottom of it was worse than ghosts. He was afraid because the flute had been made to do something—to carry something, to mean something, to be part of a machine of meaning older than his order, older than his faith, older than the kingdoms his order served—and he had put his mouth on it and blown and gotten back air. Air, and nothing. As if the instrument had weighed him and found him empty. As if the whole vast apparatus of the Choir had looked at Inquisitor Serle of the Ashen Order and registered him as furniture.

Nineteen years he had done this work. He had stood in burning buildings and broken circles and rooms where men wept blood, and he had never once flinched, because he had always known exactly what he was looking at: other men, frightened men, men whose fears he could map and ride. This was the first time the thing in his hands had refused to be mapped. The first time he had touched the edge of something and felt it decline to know him.

He put the flute carefully into its own padded box, separate from the crates. He sealed it. He wrote on the lid, in his precise clerk's hand: *Do not open except in my presence.*

Then he went outside, where his riders had lit a cookfire, and ate his ration, and listened to the monastery burn behind him for a seventh day, and thought.

* * *

The problem, he decided, was Hask.

Not the theory—the theory might even be correct. Force might suffice. Fear might sing as sweetly as faith, if you got the pitch right. Serle had no quarrel with testing a hypothesis. What he quarreled with was the sloppiness. Hask had burned the archive's keepers before reading the archive. Hask had let the vessel slip through the sea gate because his watchers were observing instead of acting. Hask treated the operation as a hammer treats a lock: as a thing to be struck until it opened, with no curiosity about what the lock was *for*.

And now the vessel was loose in the world, carrying whatever it carried—the voice, the consent, the living hinge of a hundred-year machine—and every hour it stayed free was an hour it might find shelter, or allies, or understanding. Hask had dispatched riders along the coast roads. Riders were hammers. What the situation required was an ear.

"I'll need four men," Serle said to his lieutenant, a scarred ex-soldier named Dree who had followed him for eleven years and trusted him the way soldiers trust officers who never waste them. "Riders, but quiet ones. And I want word sent ahead to the garrison at the pass towns—gray cloaks only, no colors. I'm looking for a child traveling alone or near-alone, mute, likely heading inland." He paused. "Likely heading toward high country."

Dree frowned. "Toward what, sir?"

"The mountains." Serle looked east, where the coastal fog was giving way to the first blue suggestion of ranges still weeks distant, ranges the maps marked with few names and fewer roads. Somewhere in that direction lay the resting place the old texts called the Hollow Spire, and somewhere short of it lay a hundred valleys and market towns where a hunted child might hide among strangers, and somewhere among those strangers—

He thought of the flute. Of the nothing it had given him. Of the fact that somewhere in the world there were people to whom it would not give nothing—people who could blow across that old bone and raise a chorus out of the air, and who would know, the moment they heard a child hum, exactly what had escaped the fire at Saint Vellum-on-the-Sea.

There was a valley, the old survey records said, where magic did not enter. The Order had filed the report a lifetime ago and forgotten it, because a place where nothing worked was a place with nothing to control, and the Order did not waste ink on such places. But a vessel fleeing a fire does not think like an inquisitor. A vessel thinks like prey. And prey goes to ground where the hunters' tools fail.

"Pack the crates at dawn," Serle said. "We ride east."

Dree hesitated. "The captain's orders were to return with the archive."

"The captain's orders," Serle said mildly, "were to determine whether anything survived the fire worth recovering. I am determining that." He looked back once at the monastery, glowing on its cliff like a coal cupped in a stone hand, still burning, still singing its last note down into the sea. "Something survived, Dree. Something is still moving. And I intend to be the one who finds it—before someone with less patience does."

He slept that night in his tent with the sealed box beside his bedroll, and if he dreamed, he did not record it. But twice before morning he woke, certain he had heard music far off across the water—thin, many-voiced, patient—and twice he lay listening in the dark for a sound he knew could not cross the distance, from a building that had no singers left, and both times the silence that answered him was not empty.

It was waiting. And Serle, who had spent nineteen years teaching frightened men that everything could be catalogued, lay awake until dawn adding a final entry to his private ledger:

*Some things answer only to the willing. Find the willing. Do not let Hask reach them first.*
