## 7. The Dead Explain Nothing

The rain had stopped by midnight, but the valley kept dripping—a long, slow percussion off the eaves, off the gutted gourd trellis, off the stone lip of the well. Wendell sat at the workbench with a lamp turned low and every carved bone they owned laid out on the leather apron in rows, like a surgeon preparing for an operation no one had survived yet.

Cyrus sat across from them, knees drawn up on the stool, chin resting on them, watching.

Wendell had tried to send the child to bed twice. Both times Cyrus had simply looked at them—those hollow eyes that were not empty but *full*, full of something with too much weight for so small a face—and had not moved. So Wendell gave up, poured a cup of barley tea, pushed it across the bench, and got to work.

"Right," they said aloud, mostly to themselves. "Let's see what you lot have to say for yourselves."

The dead did not speak through living bone. That was the first lesson of Wendell's trade, learned at nine years old with a splinter of their grandmother's femur clutched in a bleeding fist and nothing in their ears but the rain. Bone had to be shaped—hollowed, thinned, opened like a throat—before anything could pour through it. A flute, a whistle, a bead drilled through its center, a button worn smooth by a thumb. The carving was not decoration. It was a door.

Wendell picked up the half-finished flute first—the one that had whispered *run* three nights ago, when the child stood dripping in the doorway. They held it to their lips but did not blow. Blowing was for music. For questions, you only listened.

"What do you know about the boy?"

Silence. Not the ordinary silence of the dead, which was more like the silence of people asleep in another room—present, warm, ignorable. This was a held breath. Dozens of held breaths.

"Don't," Wendell said quietly, "do this to me tonight."

A whisper came up through the flute like water finding a crack in a wall. It was old Tam, who had died in the year of the fever and whose voice always sounded as though he were talking around a pipe he'd refused to give up even in death.

*Ask it proper.*

"I don't know what proper is," Wendell snapped. "You've never once told me the rules. Forty years I've listened to you complain about your wives and argue about fence posts, and now there's something in my house that makes you all go quiet as churchmice, and nobody will say why?"

The whispers stirred. Wendell felt them the way one feels wind move across the fine hairs of an arm—not heard so much as registered. Across the bench, Cyrus set down the untouched tea and leaned forward, elbows on knees.

Then, one by one, in fragments, the dead began to answer.

*God,* said Tam.

*There was a god,* agreed Hesta, who had been a midwife alive and was a gossip dead, which Wendell had always suspected was the same profession. *Before names. Before kingdoms put their names on things.*

*Asleep,* said a third voice, fainter, someone from deeper down in the archive of the dead whom Wendell had never managed to identify. *It must sleep.*

Wendell's hands went still on the flute. "What god? Whose god? The valley keeps out magic—I've lived my whole life on that fact like a man lives on bread. What does a sleeping god have to do with us?"

*Not yours,* said Tam. *Never yours. Ours.* And then, correcting himself with the pedantic slowness of the very old: *Theirs. The singers'.*

"The singers." Wendell looked at Cyrus. The child had not blinked in a long time. "You're the singer?"

Cyrus shook their head slowly—no—and then, after a moment, tilted it side to side. Neither yes nor no. Or perhaps both. Then the child slid off the stool, went to the scrap basket by the wall, and came back with a piece of pine and one of the short nails Wendell used for hanging hinges. And began to carve.

Wendell had watched this before—had watched it every day since the storm, with the helpless fascination of a man watching a bird build something impossible out of twigs—but never with this much intent, because now they knew the shapes meant something. The nail moved fast, sure, no hesitation, no sketching first. Spirals. Stacked figures. A long vertical line struck through with three crossbars, like a ladder lying on its side.

When Cyrus finished, they turned the board around and pointed at the central figure: a small upright mark, alone, surrounded by dozens of smaller marks arranged in rings around it, like ripples around a stone dropped in water.

"That's you," Wendell said. "And those are—" They stopped. Looked at the flute. Looked at the rows of bone laid out on the apron, each one holding its little tenant. "Those are like me. Like what I hear. Singers. Many of them."

Cyrus nodded once. Then pointed at the rings of figures again, and dragged one finger across them, left to right, and made a sound—not a word, just a soft exhalation, the ghost of a gesture toward something gone—and then folded their smallest finger down, one by one, until only a single finger remained standing.

Wendell felt cold walk up their spine on small feet.

"They're gone," they said. "The rest of them. All the others like you. And whatever they were doing together—it needs doing still, and you're the last one who can do it."

Cyrus's face did something complicated then, the way a pond does when a stone goes in: a ripple of everything, quickly smoothed over. The child looked down at their hands.

From the flute, very softly, Hesta whispered: *The poor lamb.*

And then, all around the workshop—from the flute, from the three whistles on their pegs, from the prayer beads in the drawer, from the carved buttons in the coat hanging by the door—the dead spoke all at once, a dozen voices braided into one low murmur, and for the first time in Wendell's life they all said the same thing:

*A binding. A binding. A binding.*

* * *

Dawn found them still at the bench, the lamp burned to a coin of oil, the fire fed twice. Wendell had filled six pages of their ledger with notes—prices owed, orders promised, grain tallies all shoved ruthlessly to the back pages—and now stared at what the front pages had become.

A mess. That was what it had become. The dead explained nothing; they *leaked*. Getting a whole sentence out of them was like getting a whole bucket of water from a river with your two hands.

They knew these facts, or believed they did:

There was a god. Old past old, older than the language Cyrus carved, older than the grave-rubbings in Wendell's archive that predated every kingdom whose coins had ever passed through this valley. The god was asleep. It had been put to sleep—not defeated, not slain, Wendell pressed on this point three separate times and got the same answer in three separate voices—*sung* to sleep. By many voices. Together.

And the song wore out.

That was the part Wendell kept circling back to, reading it over and over in the lamplight, written in their own cramped hand: *the song wears out. renewed each hundredth year. by a living voice. a willing voice.*

That last phrase had come only once, from the deep unidentified voice, the far-down one, and it had come with a weight behind it that Wendell did not understand yet and already dreaded.

"A willing voice," Wendell muttered. "What does that mean? Willing to do what? Sing? Anyone can sing. Maren sings. Badly, but she sings."

The dead said nothing. The dead, Wendell was learning, were excellent at saying nothing when the question mattered.

Cyrus, meanwhile, had not slept either. The child sat wrapped in the blanket Wendell had insisted on, working steadily through Wendell's entire scrap supply, carving with a focus that looked less like industry than like prayer. Little boards accumulated at their elbow. Wendell glanced at them between questions and saw, with a creeping unease, that Cyrus was carving the same scene again and again with small variations: the ringed figure, the stone-in-water, the long line struck through. Practicing. Rehearsing. As though trying to get the telling exactly right before committing to it.

"You know more than you can say," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. "I don't mean the muteness. I mean—you understand all this. Whatever this is." They gestured at the pages, the bones, the child. "You understood it before you ever walked into this valley. Someone taught you."

Cyrus's nail paused mid-spiral.

"Monks," Wendell guessed. "Coastal accent on your boots' stitching—salt-cured leather, I've repaired enough fishermen's gear to know it. There's a monastery on the west coast, the gray one on the sea-cliffs. You walked a long way, child. From somewhere like that."

Cyrus looked at them for a long moment. Then set down the nail, took the newest board, and carved four quick marks beneath the ringed figure: a building shape, a flame, a scattering of running marks, and then—one figure standing still amid the scattered ones, hands cupped around something small held to its mouth.

Wendell read it the way they had learned to read all of Cyrus's carvings, slowly, letting the pictures assemble into sense. A building. Fire. People fleeing. One person staying, holding—

Their eyes went to the pegs by the door. To the bone whistle Cyrus had carried into the valley, the one Wendell did not remember making, the one that had arrived in the child's fist like a key arriving in the wrong lock.

"Somebody gave you that," Wendell said quietly. "While the place burned. Somebody stayed behind so you could run."

Cyrus did not nod. But their hand closed briefly, involuntarily, around the whistle where it hung at their throat, and that was answer enough, and worse than an answer, and Wendell had to look away for a moment and pretend great interest in the state of the fire.

Outside, the sky was going the color of dishwater. Chickens would need feeding. Maren would come by mid-morning with her basket of early apples and her talent for noticing everything, and Wendell would have to decide how much truth a woman like Maren could be trusted with—which was, historically, all of it, delivered loudly, followed by action.

But that was later. Right now there was one more question, and Wendell had been circling it all night like a moth around a lamp it did not trust.

* * *

They asked it at last with the morning gray coming through the window, holding the half-finished flute in both hands the way one holds a bird—firmly enough to keep it, gently enough not to crush it.

"The Order," Wendell said. "Or whoever's hunting the child. The ones the dead are afraid of." The whispers rippled at that word—*afraid*—a rustle of denial, or maybe protest at being named so plainly. Wendell talked over them. "I want to know what happens if they win. If the god wakes up. Nobody will tell me. Tam, you old coward, I can hear you pretending to be elsewhere. Tell me what a waking god does."

For a long time: rain-drip, fire-crackle, Cyrus's breathing.

Then the deep voice, the far-down one, the one that made Wendell think of cellars and roots, spoke directly into the marrow of their skull, and it did not whisper this time.

*It mourns.*

"What?"

*It is not wrath. It was never wrath. It woke once, long ago, and all it did—all it has ever done—is grieve. But its grief is a flood, child. It mourned a village and the village drowned in its own tears. It mourns, and everything under its mourning drowns.* A pause, like a breath drawn through wet cloth. *The Choir sang it to sleep out of mercy. Its, and ours.*

Wendell sat back. The bench creaked.

"And the renewal," they said. Their mouth was dry. "Every hundred years. A living voice. A willing voice." They turned the flute over. The unfinished holes looked like a face mid-scream. "How much longer does the current binding hold?"

The dead went quiet again—that held-breath quiet, the one Wendell was beginning to hate with their whole heart.

"How long?" they demanded, louder, and the sound cracked in the small room, and Cyrus flinched, and Wendell hated themselves for it and pressed on anyway because the alternative was drowning politely. "Answer me. Weeks? Years? Am I supposed to just—what, wait for the sky to start weeping? What am I supposed to *do*, if it comes to that? What did the others do, the ones in the rings, when their hundred years came due? Did they sing? Did they *go* somewhere? Is there somewhere to *go*?"

*Solstice,* said Tam, at last, small as a mouse under floorboards. *The turning. Soon. So soon, carver. So soon.*

"Soon," Wendell repeated flatly. "Define soon. Solstice is—" they did the arithmetic automatically, the way they did grain counts, and the number landed in their chest like a dropped stone. "Forty days. Forty days from tomorrow."

Across the bench, Cyrus had gone very still. Stillness, Wendell was learning, was the child's loudest voice. And this stillness was different from all the others—sharper, strung tight as wire. Cyrus was staring at the flute in Wendell's hands, and their lips had parted slightly, and one hand had risen halfway toward it as though pulled on a string before stopping, hovering, falling back.

Understanding more than they could say. Understanding, maybe, everything.

Wendell looked down at the six pages of fragments in the ledger. A god sung to sleep out of mercy. A song wearing thin. A child who was the last of a choir, hunted by people who wanted the flood let loose. Forty days. And a phrase that kept surfacing in the deep voice's cadence like a body refusing to stay sunk: *a willing voice.*

"Well," Wendell said, to the room, to the dead, to the child, to nobody. Their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Then we'd better finish this flute."

Cyrus blinked. And something moved behind the hollow eyes—not hope exactly, hope was too large a word for a face that had learned not to afford it, but its first cousin, its poor relation, come calling at dawn.

Wendell reached for the carving knife, and outside, far up the pass where the road bent out of sight of the valley, something glinted once in the early light—metal, moving—and was gone.
