## 8. Solstice in Forty Days

Cyrus counted the days the only way that made sense: by cutting them.

A notch for each morning, cut into the underside of the workbench where Wendell wouldn't see it until it was too late to pretend otherwise. Forty notches needed. One already made, the wood still pale and raw at the wound's edge. Cyrus ran a thumb over it and felt the way the grain resisted, then gave—the same feeling as a word leaving the throat, if words still lived there.

The bench was oak, old, scarred with decades of other people's mistakes. Good wood told you everything about itself if you pressed your palm flat against it. This bench had been patient its whole life. It would hold forty marks without complaint.

*Thirty-nine after this one.*

Cyrus made the second notch and tried not to think of it as a door closing. Doors closed all the time. Doors were nothing. But the body kept its own ledger, and the ledger said: *the light is lengthening in the wrong way, the air has a thinness coming, the solstice is a sound you can feel approaching through the floor like a wagon on a far road.*

Forty days.

The dead in the workshop knew it too. They had gone strange since the night the child arrived—no longer the low murmuring Wendell heard through his half-finished flutes and spoon-handles and memorial beads, but a kind of *attention*, the sensation of many faces turned toward one small point in a dark room. When Cyrus picked up a blank of bone, the attention sharpened until their teeth ached.

They wanted Cyrus to speak. They had been wanting it for years. Every monk who died still knowing the old shapes, every sister whose tongue the fire took before her courage did—all of them crowded now against the thin wall between living bone and dead, pressing their last syllables against it like palms against glass.

Cyrus could not give them what they wanted. So they gave them this instead:

Cyrus took up a scrap of antler, soft-grained, forgiving, and carved.

* * *

The first shape was always the spiral. Not because it was easy—because it was true. The spiral meant *turning, returning, the wheel of the year and the wheel of the song*. It was the first sign Brother Ilex had ever taught them, scratched in candle soot on a cellar flagstone while the other monks sang vespers overhead and the whole monastery hummed like a struck bell.

*Why does it turn?* Cyrus had asked, in those days, with quick fingers against Ilex's sleeve.

*Because the god dreams in circles,* Ilex had written back, soot-black letters on slate, *and the song walks the circle with him, around and around, so he never reaches the end of his own grief. We are not keeping him prisoner, little one. We are keeping him company.*

Cyrus carved the spiral into the antler scrap. Then the stacked figures above it—three lines of them, tall-tall-short, the sign-cluster that meant *the Choir assembled, voices joined, the long mercy sung.* Then the smaller cluster beneath, the one with the sharp descending stroke, the one that made even the dead flinch when they felt it take shape under a blade:

*the binding thins.*

The workshop went very quiet. Even the wind outside seemed to lean away from the shutters.

Cyrus set down the knife. Pressed both hands flat against the antler, the way you'd warm a bird. *I know,* the pressure said. *I know. I know.*

There were no signs left to carve for what came after the thinning. That was the terrible economy of the old language—it had words for ten thousand kinds of sorrow but none for the end of the world, because the people who made it had built the prevention into the grammar itself. You did not write *the god wakes*. You wrote *the renewal*, and you trusted the singing.

The monks had trusted the singing.

Cyrus sat very still in Wendell's workshop, among the sweet dust of sawn bone and the smell of linseed oil, and let the memory come, because fighting it never worked. It always came anyway. Better to open the door and let it pass through than to have it break the window.

* * *

Fire remembers itself in the body longer than almost anything else.

Not the heat—that faded early, strangely, swallowed by the bigger shock. What stayed was the *light*. Firelight on stone walls, on the upturned faces of brothers and sisters who should have been asleep, on the great painted eye of the chapel ceiling beginning to blister and run so that the god's painted gaze seemed to weep molten blue. The sound, too: singing layered over roaring, and the horrible discovery that from a distance they were nearly the same sound, that flame and voice shared a family resemblance no one warned children about.

Cyrus had been six. Maybe seven. The counting of years had stopped mattering in the cellar.

They remembered Brother Anselm's hands first, always first—big hands, cracked at the knuckles, smelling of tallow and garden soil—pressing something small and smooth into Cyrus's palms and then folding the small cold fingers over it, one by one, with a care that was worse than shouting. Shouting would have meant panic. This care meant he already knew.

*Keep it against your skin. If they come, do not sing, do not sign, do not make any sound at all. Be stone, little one. Stone and silence are the strongest prayer we have.*

The whistle was bone. Carved in the old shapes, so fine the spirals were nearly too small to see, the whole thing no longer than a thumb. It had been warm from his hands. Later Cyrus would understand why that mattered—bone carried the last touch given to it, held it like amber holds an insect—but in the moment there was only the weight of it, light as a promise and heavy as a name.

Then the singing.

That was the part the fire could never burn out. Twenty-three voices in the nave, singing the descent—the deep verse of the renewal, the one that walked the dreamer down through his own mourning to the quiet place beneath—and Cyrus, curled in the dark of the cellar with a dead man's gift against their breastbone, had felt every note land in their own ribs like feet crossing a bridge. The Choir sang *through* the living singers; that was the whole secret of it, the thing the Ashen Order would never grasp no matter how many monasteries they burned. The song was not stored in books or bones or bloodlines. It was stored in *agreement*. In twenty-three throats choosing the same mercy at the same moment.

Twenty-three became nineteen mid-verse. The sound did not stumble. That was somehow the worst part—that the song absorbed its own dying the way a river absorbs stones, and went on.

Fourteen. Eleven. The floor above glowed at the seams.

Nine voices, then six, and Cyrus felt each loss as a hand lifted off the bridge-rail, and pressed the whistle harder against their chest, and understood—with the terrible early wisdom of children raised in old places—that the bridge was getting narrower, and that someday it would be one plank wide, and that the one plank was currently folded small in a cellar holding its breath.

Three voices.

One.

Then the roof came down on the last of it, and the singing became memory, and memory became a whistle against a racing heart, and Cyrus learned what it was to be the last of something.

It was not being alone. Being alone was ordinary; the valley was full of people who had fled or lost or buried their way to solitude. Being the last was different. Being the last meant that somewhere behind your ribs, a library was burning slowly, and every book in it existed in exactly one copy, and the fire was patient, and the fire was you.

* * *

Cyrus came back to the workshop the way surfacing feels—too fast, lungs first, hands gripping the bench. Dusk had thickened the window to pewter. Somewhere in the house, Wendell was moving through his evening ritual: the kettle, the latch, the small deliberate sounds of a person keeping a life tidy by force of habit.

The antler scrap lay under Cyrus's palms. The carved signs caught the last light—spiral, assembly, thinning—and beneath them, without any memory of deciding to carve it, a new line had appeared:

*twenty-three minus twenty-two.*

Cyrus stared at it. The dead pressed close, and for once their attention wasn't hungry. It was the silence of people standing around a grave they all dug together.

*I didn't mean to write that,* Cyrus's stillness said.

*You wrote what's true,* the stillness answered. *We checked. From our side.*

Cyrus covered the antler with a cloth, the way you'd cover a mirror in a house of mourning, and went to the window instead. Below the hill, the valley lay in its blue evening hush—the crooked rooftops, Maren's goat already complaining at her fence, the far dark line of the pass where the world with magic in it began. Forty days from tonight, the sun would stand at its lowest ebb, and the wheel would turn, and the song would need to be sung again by living voices in the right place, in the old agreement.

And the agreement was a child who could not make a sound, sitting in a bone-carver's workshop at the bottom of the one valley in the world where no magic could reach them—or reach the god, or reach the song, or do anything at all except keep everything exactly, perfectly, fatally still.

The valley was safe. The valley was also a held breath, and held breaths have a limit.

Cyrus pressed their forehead to the cool glass and made themselves count, honestly, the way Ilex had taught them to count: without flinching at the sum.

What did they have? A whistle they had never dared blow—not once, not in nine years, because the first note would be a flare, would be a bell tolling *here, here, the last anchor here* across every listening mile. A language in their fingers that maybe three other living people could read, and all three might be dead. A bone-carver downstairs who heard the dead but had never, as far as Cyrus could tell, been asked to believe anything larger than tomorrow's weather. And forty days.

Thirty-eight, now. The two notches under the bench sat in Cyrus's mind like splinters.

What did the Order have? Riders. Fire. A century of patience and a doctrine that said grief was a door and crowbars were holy. They had taken the other names on the list alive—that much the dead had managed to say, in fragments, through Wendell's unfinished flute—and Cyrus had turned that fact over so many times its edges had gone smooth and terrible. Alive. Kept alive, the way you keep kindling dry. Because a forced voice was useless for waking and worse for sleeping, and the Order must know it by now, must have spent years learning it in failed rituals and scorched chambers, which meant—

Which meant the last name on the list was worth more alive than any of the crossed-out ones had ever been. Which meant the riders would not make the mistake of arrows. Which meant every day in this beautiful null valley was a day bought with the assumption that no one was looking.

Someone was looking. Cyrus had felt it for weeks now, a prickle between the shoulder blades that had nothing to do with the dead—a living, riding, map-unfolding attention, somewhere beyond the pass, drawing closer to the truth the way water finds a seam in stone.

Cyrus breathed fog onto the window glass. Drew, with one fingertip, in the clearing mist: a spiral. Small. Turning. Returning.

Then wiped it away with the whole flat hand, fast, before it could mean anything.

Downstairs, Wendell set the kettle down with the particular clink that meant tea, and solitude, and the belief that tomorrow would resemble today. Cyrus listened to those sounds the way you listen to a clock in a room where something is about to happen, and made a decision with no words in it anywhere—only the image of two notches under a bench, and thirty-eight empty spaces waiting for a blade.

Tomorrow, or soon, someone would have to pick up the antler scrap and show Wendell Dunmore the sign-cluster for *the binding thins*, and then the harder one, the compound phrase that took four linked figures and meant, as nearly as the old language ever came to urgency:

*we leave, or it wakes.*

Cyrus's hands were steady. That surprised them. Hands that had learned to speak when the throat refused had also learned, apparently, not to shake.

Outside, far off past the pass, thunder muttered—or hooves, or both—and the dead in the workshop all leaned, at once, toward the north wall, the way wheat leans ahead of weather.

Thirty-eight days. Cyrus went down to tea.
