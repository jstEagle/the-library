## 9. Leaving the Valley

I packed for three days and prepared for thirty.

That is a bone-carver's habit—measure twice, cut once, then measure again because the bone has opinions of its own. I laid everything out on the workbench in the gray hour before dawn: hard bread wrapped in waxed cloth, a skin of water, dried apples from Maren's orchard, the good carving knife and the ugly spare one I kept for rough stock. A wool coat that had belonged to my mother. And the bones.

The bones were the problem. There were too many to bring and too many to leave. Forty years of whispers lived in that workshop—flutes half-finished on their pegs, a cradle charm for a baby who'd grown up and gone over the pass years ago, the little carved hares I made for children who had never once heard them speak. Every piece held someone. If I left them behind, did they stay? Did they sleep? Or did they simply go quiet, the way the valley itself was quiet—not dead, just refusing?

I chose eleven pieces. It felt like choosing which mourners to invite to a funeral. The old femur flute went first, without question; it was the one who had whispered *run*, and whatever it knew, I wanted near me. Then the rib-bone that sang lullabies when I worked late. Then three vertebrae strung on cord, an ankle-bone polished smooth as a river stone, and five others whose voices I could not name but could not abandon either. They went into a leather roll against my spine, where they sat like a cold hand pressed between my shoulder blades.

Cyrus watched me pack from the corner by the stove, the way the child watched everything: absolutely still, absolutely present, eyes moving with the patience of something that had learned that watching was safer than being seen. The whistle hung at their throat—the little spiral-carved whistle I supposedly didn't remember making, though I had stopped lying to myself about that. Somewhere in those lost hours of the night the child had arrived, I *had* made it. My hands remembered what my mind refused to. That thought still woke me at odd moments with my heart hammering.

"Nearly done," I said aloud. Talking to Cyrus had become easy in the way that talking to the dead was easy—one-sided, trusting, oddly intimate. "We'll make the pass by midday if the ford's not swollen."

Cyrus crossed the room and touched the empty pegs where the flutes usually hung. Then looked at me. Then touched the pegs again.

"They stay," I said. "I can't carry them all."

The child's face did something complicated—grief and understanding braided together—and then did the thing that undid me every time: reached up and patted the air above the nearest peg, gently, twice, the way you'd soothe a horse. As if the empty workshop were a living thing that needed comforting.

Maybe it was. Maybe I was.

* * *

Maren met us at the well, which meant she'd been waiting there since before dawn, because Maren never waited anywhere she hadn't first arranged to look like she wasn't waiting.

She was seventy-three years old and built like the doorframe of a church that had survived two fires, and she wore her authority the way other people wore shawls—loosely, over everything, taken off for no one. She carried a basket. Of course she carried a basket. The woman had once sent me off to a funeral with a basket.

"You're going," she said. Not a question. News traveled through the valley faster than water through gravel, and by now every soul in Harrow's Bend knew the bone-carver and the mute child were leaving, and every one of them had an opinion about it that they would deliver to Maren instead of to me, because delivering things to Maren was simply how weather worked here.

"I'm going."

"The Order's real, then." She said it flatly, watching my face. "Not travelers' tales. Not the fever-dreams of folk who've been in the valley so long they've forgotten what fear tastes like."

"It's real. The child ran from it. The dead—" I stopped, because saying *the dead are terrified* out loud, in daylight, at the well, was a threshold of its own. Maren deserved better than a softened version. "The dead told us to run. The dead have never told anyone anything but their names and small kindnesses in forty years. So yes. It's real."

Maren set down the basket and put both her hands on my shoulders. Her grip was strong as a fence post driven deep. Up close her eyes were pale as winter light and wetter than she'd ever admit.

"Wendell Dunmore," she said. "Your mother pulled me out of the flooded quarry when we were girls. Your mother fed half this valley the winter the blight took the barley. You think I don't know what your family does? You think any of us don't?" She shook me, once, lightly, the way you'd shake a jar to settle its contents. "You've spent forty years thinking you were alone out here with your ghosts. Foolish, stubborn man. This whole valley is people who came here to hide from something, holding each other up in the dark. We just agreed not to say it aloud."

I had no answer to that. I looked at the well, at the rope worn smooth by generations of hands, at the mountains standing around our small green secret like patient guardians.

"There's bread in the basket," Maren said, releasing me. "And a flask. And if you tell anyone I gave you the flask, I'll deny it until my grave and haunt you after." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, just enough. She turned to Cyrus and crouched, which cost her—I saw her knees object—and took the child's small hands in her big ones. "You keep him alive," she said to Cyrus. "He'll try to be noble. Nobility kills more fools in this world than swords do. You bite him if he gets noble."

Cyrus nodded solemnly, and I swear the child almost smiled.

Then Maren stood, and kissed my forehead like I was six years old, and said, "Go on, then. Before I decide to tie you to the well."

* * *

Half the village came to the eastern edge anyway. They lined the path like ragged honor guards—Tomas the smith with his burn-scarred arms folded, the Widow Pell and her three ferocious daughters, old Hollis who hadn't left his porch in nine years and had apparently walked a mile to stand in the cold and say nothing. Nobody spoke much. Hollis pressed something into my hand as I passed: a carved wooden bird, crude, his own work. "For luck," he rasped. "Never believed in it. Seemed a poor time to start doubting."

I understood, looking back at them from the top of the rise, why none of them had tried to stop me. Exiles all of them. They knew exactly what it cost to leave a place that kept you safe, and they knew that some debts come due regardless, and the only dignity available is in paying them standing up.

Cyrus walked beside me, small hand hooked in my coat pocket rather than my fingers—I'd learned the child's terms of contact early, and respected them—and we crested the ridge together as the sun cleared the peaks.

The boundary of the valley is nothing. That's the truth no one believes until they cross it. No wall, no ward-stone, no shimmering veil. Just a fold in the land, a place where the stream bends oddly and the grass grows a finger-length taller on one side of an invisible line, and if you didn't know, you'd walk across it a hundred times without noticing. I had been born inside it. In forty years I had never once stepped out. Why would I? Everything I loved was within it.

I stopped at the line with my boots on the tall-grass side and looked back one last time—at the smoke rising from chimneys, at Maren's distant figure still planted by the well like a stake, at the workshop roof where my unfinished flutes hung silent on their pegs.

Then I took Cyrus's hand—they permitted it, today, gripping hard—and stepped across.

* * *

The world broke open.

There is no gentler way to say it. One moment I was walking through ordinary morning—the gray-green of the slopes, the smell of pine and cold stone—and the next, magic poured into me like a river into a cracked bowl, and I learned in a single terrible instant what the valley had been sparing me my entire life.

Color first. The world detonated into color. The pines weren't green, they were a hundred greens, each needle edged in gold where the sun struck, the shadows beneath them purple as bruises. The sky was so blue it hurt, blue like a wound, blue like a shout. Every stone on the path gleamed with veins of light I had no names for. I staggered. My eyes streamed. I had spent my life in a charcoal sketch and no one had told me the world was painted.

Then sound. Oh, God, the sound.

Inside the valley, the dead whispered. That was the bargain of the place, the mercy of it—a whisper through carved bone, faint as breath, deniable. Outside—

They *roared*.

Every bone in my pack woke at once. Eleven voices became fifty became hundreds, a chorus swelling up through my spine and into my skull, and it wasn't only my eleven. The land itself was full of them—the dead under the road, the dead in the hills, the dead in the ruined tower we passed an hour later, the dead everywhere, because the dead are everywhere outside the valley, they saturate the world like water saturates a sponge, and without the valley's nullity pressing them flat I could hear *all of them*. Names, fragments, pleas, songs, warnings, griefs, overlapping, interweaving, a tide of voices with no shore. Some were ancient. Some spoke in languages that predated kingdoms, the same spiraling cadences I'd seen in Cyrus's carvings. One voice, very close, very clear, said my name—"Wendell"—in my mother's tone, and I went to my knees in the mud of the road.

I heard myself screaming. I'm told I screamed. What I remember is pressure, vast and total, a cathedral collapsing inward, every soul I'd ever failed to listen to speaking at once, and my hands clawing at the leather roll against my spine trying to tear it away, trying to get it *out*, get them *out*—

Small hands caught my face.

Cyrus knelt in front of me in the churned mud, gripping my jaw with both hands, forcing my eyes to focus. And the child did something I will remember until I die: hummed.

One note. Low, steady, round as a river stone. The same note, over and over, patient as water wearing down rock. And the roar began to organize around it. The hundred voices found the pitch. Found each other. The chaos resolved into harmony—not silence, never again silence, but *music*, a chord sustained by a hundred throats that no longer had throats, anchored by one small living hum from a child who could not speak but could sing, because of course they could. Silence and muteness were never the same thing. I had known this child three weeks and only understood it now, on my knees in the mud with the whole weight of the unquiet world pouring through my skull.

The chord settled. The pain ebbed from unbearable to merely enormous, like a storm dropping to a steady rain. I breathed. I got a knee under myself. Cyrus kept humming, watching me with those old, old eyes, checking my pulse with two fingers on my wrist like a physician, and slowly, gradually, let the note fade.

The voices remained. But quiet now. Layered. Like living in a city after a lifetime in the country—you never stop hearing it, but you learn to walk through it.

"I hear them," I croaked. My throat was raw. "All of them. All the time?"

Cyrus nodded. Then pointed at me, and at their own ear, and tilted their head: *and you.*

"How have you borne it?"

The child considered the question with complete seriousness, the way they considered everything. Then they touched their chest, over the whistle, and made a small gesture: *carried properly, it becomes music.* Then, with brutal honesty, pointed at the ground, at the mud, at my kneeling shape: *sometimes you fall down.*

A laugh tore out of me, ragged and half-hysterical. Yes. Sometimes you fall down. Sometimes the truth of the world is so loud it puts you in the dirt, and the only choice is whether you get up knowing what you know.

I got up. My legs shook. The colors still burned—the world outside the valley was a place of unbearable vividness, and I understood now with a dull, sinking clarity why the exiles had founded their refuge where they had, why they'd chosen the nullity, the grays, the whisper-quiet. Not cowardice. Mercy. Survival. The invisible life was the only bearable one, and I had traded it in a single step across a line in the grass.

The cost of the small world, paid all at once, in full, with interest.

* * *

We walked. It was all there was to do.

By afternoon I had learned to narrow my attention, the way you learn to sleep in a noisy inn—letting the chorus wash past while I listened for the few voices I knew. My eleven traveled with me, tucked against my spine, murmuring. The old femur flute spoke most often. Its words were still fragmentary, still maddening, but louder now, clearer, as if the valley had been muffling it all along.

*"South,"* it whispered, as the sun leaned west. *"Follow the old road south. The spire. The singing place. He must reach the singing place."*

"He," I muttered. "You could use her name. It's Cyrus."

The flute was quiet a moment, in the way of the dead deciding how much truth they can afford.

*"Names have weight,"* it said finally. *"Out here, everything has weight. But—Cyrus. Yes. Cyrus must reach the Spire before the long night. And Wendell—"*

It paused so long I stopped walking.

*"Wendell, they are behind us already. Three riders on the north road. Gray cloaks. They stopped at the ford and one of them bent down and touched the mud where you fell, and he smiled."*

I turned north. Far off, at the edge of seeing, dust moved on a road that should have been empty.

Beside me, Cyrus had gone rigid, one small hand closed white-knuckled around the bone whistle at their throat.

The dead, all around us, in a hundred buried voices, began very softly to sing—a low warning drone that rose out of the earth itself, and I took the child's hand, and we started to run.
