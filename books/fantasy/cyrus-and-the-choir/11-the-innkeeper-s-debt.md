## 11. The Innkeeper's Debt

The rain came in off the moor an hour before the riders did, and Odell Brask knew trouble was on it the way he knew a bad keg—by the weight of the air and the way his dogs refused to leave the kitchen.

He'd been pulling shutters closed against the storm when the first of them came up the coach road at a trot that no honest traveler used in weather like this. Honest travelers hurried or they crawled; they didn't ride with the patient, unhurried confidence of men who had somewhere to be and no fear of being late for it. There were six of them, gray-cloaked, mud-spattered to the hip, and they sat their horses like the horses were furniture.

Odell's hands went still on the shutter latch.

He knew the mark before he could make out faces. He'd hoped never to see it again, and hope, as his mother used to say, is just grief wearing its good coat. Stitched into the left breast of each gray cloak: a circle bisected by a vertical line, the whole of it worked in thread so pale it looked like scar tissue. The sign of the Ashen Order. Odell had seen it last thirty years ago, on the chest of a man who stood in his mother's taproom and asked questions about a woman named Vess Dunmore, and Odell had been young enough then to think the questions were harmless and old enough now to know better.

He finished closing the shutter anyway. Slowly. A man who moved too fast told everyone what he knew.

* * *

By the time the riders dismounted under the eave, Odell had arranged himself behind the bar with a cloth in his hand and a face on him like wet slate—open enough to seem simple, closed enough to invite nothing. His dogs had retreated under the stairs, which was worse than growling. Dogs always knew.

"Six," said the leader, peeling back his hood. He was younger than Odell expected. That was somehow the worst part. Men who did this work into their fifties got a certain look, a settled look, but this one still had the hunger on him, the shine of a man who believed what he'd been sold. "Rooms if you have them. Stabling. Food."

"I've rooms." Odell counted them onto the bar with his eyes, not his finger. "Stabling's cramped in this weather, but I'll manage."

"You'll manage," the young man repeated, without inflection, as though testing whether the words held anything worth taking. He set coins down—too many, deliberately—and Odell understood the shape of the transaction being offered. Not payment. Purchase. "We're riding for the pass road. We're looking for travelers. A carver and a child."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the roof like a thousand small fingers.

"Carver and a child," Odell said. He wiped the bar where no spill was. "This time of year? Road's been empty since the frost. I get wool merchants in autumn and fools in summer, and neither one's fool enough for the moor in a storm."

"They may not be traveling together openly." The young man's eyes were pale, the color of watered milk, and they didn't blink often enough. "A man, forty-odd, plain-faced. A child, ten or eleven, dark-haired, doesn't speak. If they come through—"

"Doesn't speak," Odell said. "Mute?"

"That's the report."

"Huh." Odell scratched his jaw, letting the pause stretch until it looked like memory working rather than invention. "There was a mute tinker came through two springs back. Deaf too, or near enough. Sold my wife a kettle she didn't need." He shook his head at the ancient grievance. "No child, though. And nobody's come through tonight but you."

Something flickered across the young man's face—not doubt exactly, but the habit of doubt, the reflex of a man trained to find the seam in every wall. "You're certain."

"Certainty's for priests." Odell spread his hands. "I'm certain about my own taproom, and it's empty, and my stable's empty, and if a carver and a mute child walked in here tonight I'd remember it tomorrow and the day after, because nothing happens on this road, friend, and when something does, we talk about it for a season."

It was a good lie. It was built on truth the way all good lies are—the empty road, the garrulous valley folk, the tinker who really had sold his dead wife a terrible kettle—and the young man weighed it, and Odell watched him weigh it, and beneath the bar where the cloth hid his hands, Odell's knuckles were white around the rag.

Because the truth was that Wendell Dunmore and the child had come through four hours ago.

* * *

They'd arrived soaked through, half-frozen, the carver carrying the boy on his back the last mile with the grim economy of a man who would not say how long he'd been doing it. Odell had known Wendell on sight—not from the face, which had gone lean and gray since Odell last saw it, but from the hands. Bone-carver's hands, scarred fine as lacework, and the way they curled protectively even in exhaustion, the way a mason's curl around a chisel.

"Wendell Dunmore," Odell had said, and Wendell had flinched at the name like a man hearing it read off a warrant.

"You know me?"

"My mother kept this house before me. Before that, her mother. We've poured drink for every soul that ever crossed the moor going north." Odell had taken the boy's weight off Wendell's shoulders without asking, and the child had been light in a way that frightened him, light the way things are light when they've been running too long. "Vess Dunmore's son. You have her chin."

Wendell had stared at him then, really stared, and whatever he saw in Odell's face had made him sag against the doorframe. "You knew her."

"Everybody knew your mother. She pulled my brother out of the flooded ford below the mill when we were boys. Held his head above the water till the men got ropes to us." Odell had steered them toward the fire, talking because stopping would have meant feeling, and there wasn't time to feel. "She died before I took the house. I heard it was quiet. I heard the valley was kind to her."

"It was," Wendell had said. "It is."

And the boy—Cyrus, Wendell had called him, or hadn't called him; Odell couldn't recall the name spoken aloud, only that Wendell had touched the child's shoulder and the child had leaned into it with the practiced trust of someone who has learned exactly one person in the world is safe—the boy had stood dripping by the hearth and looked at Odell with eyes far too old for the face they lived in, and had not spoken, and Odell had understood without being told that the silence was not emptiness. It was a locked room. He'd spent his life around people; he knew the difference between a room with nothing in it and a room with the door barred.

He'd fed them stew and put them in the corner room, the one with the window over the woodshed roof, and he'd told Wendell, quietly, at the foot of the stairs: "If anyone comes asking, you weren't here. My house forgets faces. Always has. Bad for business, remembering."

Wendell had gripped his forearm, bone-carver's grip, precise and hard. "Odell. If they come—gray cloaks, a mark like a split circle—you give them nothing. Not a crumb. Not a direction. Do you understand? They burned a monastery full of children to get to this one."

"I understand," Odell had said.

And now they were here, six of them, drinking his ale and drying their cloaks at his fire, and the young one with the milk-pale eyes was watching Odell across the taproom with the patience of a man who has learned that most people lie badly and briefly.

* * *

The interrogation, when it came properly, came over supper, which Odell thought showed a certain sophistication. The young man—he gave his name as Serle, Inquisitor Serle, and the title sat on him like a coat cut for a bigger man—waited until Odell brought bread and the rabbit pie, and then asked his questions in the pauses, casually, between mouthfuls, so that each answer seemed to cost nothing.

"The valley south of here. The one the maps mark with a blank."

"Null Hollow, some call it." Odell refilled his cup. "Nothing lives there but exiles and stubbornness."

"What kind of exiles?"

"The kind everywhere breeds. Debtors. Heretics. A widow who poisoned a husband who deserved it. Folk the world spat out, who found the one place the world wouldn't follow." He shrugged. "Magic doesn't work there. Never has. So the clever ones and the cursed ones go where cleverness can't be turned against them."

"And a bone-carver could live there. Quietly. For years."

"A bone-carver could live anywhere quietly. It's the trade." Odell smiled with his mouth. "You want to know if your carver's from Null Hollow. I'd say the odds favor it. Half the valley works bone—it's what the land gives, besides turnips. But I couldn't tell you names. Valley folk don't come down much, and when they do, they don't talk. That's rather the point of them."

Serle turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the ring of moisture it left. "There are stories about the valley. That the dead speak there."

"There are stories about everything." Odell gathered plates. "Most of them started with a drunk shepherd."

"Inquisitor." One of the other riders, older, grayer, spoke from the corner table where the six of them had clustered. "The horses need seeing to, and the storm's turning. We won't ride tonight regardless."

"We won't," Serle agreed, not looking away from Odell. "But we ride at first light, and we take the pass road north." He let that sit. Then, mildly, terribly: "Which means anyone traveling north will be ahead of us on the same road, in the same weather, with nowhere to shelter between here and the ford except the ruin at Miller's Bend. You'd know the ford, I expect. Locals always do."

"Floods twice a year," Odell said. "Crosses easy enough in low water. In this—" he nodded at the window, where the rain had redoubled, hammering like a fist on a door "—it crosses when it pleases and drowns whoever argues."

"Mm." Serle rose, and for a moment his shadow fell across the table and across Odell entirely, and the milk-pale eyes were very still. "My order pays well for accurate memories, innkeeper. And it remembers inaccurate ones longer. Sleep well."

Odell slept badly, which surprised him. He'd expected to sleep like a stone after a lie that size, the deep dreamless sleep of a man who has done the right thing and paid for it. Instead he lay listening to the rain and thinking about his mother, who had died owing nothing to anyone, and about Vess Dunmore, whom he'd met exactly three times and remembered completely.

* * *

The first time, Odell was nine. Vess had come down from the valley to buy salt and lamp oil, a tall woman with her son on her hip—a baby then, red-faced and furious at the world—and she'd bargained like a woman who knew the price of everything because she'd paid for most of it. His mother had liked her instantly. Two women of the same iron temper, they'd talked for an hour while the baby chewed on a carved bone whistle, and when Vess left, his mother had said: *That one chose the hardest life available to her and made it look easy. Remember that, Odell. Some people aren't running from anything. They're running toward the place they can stand.*

The second time, Odell was nineteen, and Vess came alone, and bought nothing, and sat in the empty taproom with a cup of small beer and asked him strange questions. Whether travelers passed often. Whether soldiers came this way. Whether he'd ever seen men in gray. She'd paid for the beer with a carved button, bone, a spiral pattern finer than anything Odell had ever seen, and he'd kept it in his pocket for years until he lost it, and he still sometimes dreamed of the spiral.

The third time was the last. She'd been older, grayer, moving carefully, and she'd bought rope and a hatchet and asked him to keep something for her. A letter, sealed. *For my son, if he ever comes down the mountain. If he doesn't, burn it unread.* Wendell had never come, in her lifetime. Odell had never burned it. It was upstairs in a box with his mother's papers, and he had read it once, years ago, alone, drunk, ashamed of reading it, and unable to regret it.

*I have chosen a small life,* the letter said, in a hand like wire. *I chose it for reasons I will not write down, because paper travels. If my son reads this, tell him: the small life was not a retreat. It was a wall, and I was the wall, and I do not regret one stone of it. Tell him also that walls exist to protect something, and if the day comes when protecting the thing means leaving the wall, then leaving is not betrayal. It is the wall doing its work at last.*

Odell had not understood the letter for twenty years.

He understood it now, lying awake above a taproom full of gray cloaks, listening to the rain and to the creak of the boy's floorboards overhead—no, the boy was silent, it was the wind in the shutters—and understanding, finally, that his mother had not merely kept an inn. She had kept faith. With a woman she'd met three times, over a sealed letter and a bone button, because some debts don't come due in coin.

His mother had fed Vess Dunmore's son soup and given him a dry bed and asked nothing. Now the bill had arrived, thirty years later, presented by a man with a split-circle stitched on his chest, and Odell Brask intended to pay it in full.

* * *

At first light the storm had broken into a thin, mean drizzle, and the riders saddled in the yard while Odell fried eggs and said nothing and heard everything. They talked freely around him, the way armed men do, having decided long ago that innkeepers were furniture. He learned that they'd lost the trail at the river city—that a magistrate's clerk had sold them a page of erased records and a shrug. He learned that Serle had sent a rider back west with orders Odell couldn't hear. He learned that they expected to pick up the trail at the ford, because the ford was the only crossing, and the only road past it ran through open country where a man with a child could be seen for miles.

"Miller's Bend," Serle said, mounting. He looked down at Odell from the saddle, and the morning light made his eyes almost colorless. "If your memory improves, innkeeper—if you recall a face, a direction, anything—there's a garrison at Crossford two days east. Ask for the Order's post. There will be money in it."

"I'll keep it in mind," Odell said.

Serle held his gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he turned his horse, and the six of them went down the coach road north at that same patient trot, unhurried, inevitable, and Odell stood in his doorway watching them shrink into the drizzle until the dogs crept out from under the stairs and pressed against his legs, trembling.

He waited until they were gone. Then he went inside, took the stairs two at a time, and hammered on the corner room's door.

Wendell opened it already dressed, already packed, the boy behind him with the bone whistle clutched in both hands, and Odell saw that neither of them had slept either.

"How many?" Wendell asked.

"Six. Gray cloaks. Your monastery-burners, sure as sunrise." Odell pushed past them to the window over the woodshed roof. Through the drizzle, the coach road was a gray ribbon going north. "They're riding for the pass road. They figure you're ahead of them, and they figure the ford at Miller's Bend is where they catch you, because it's the only crossing and the country past it is bare as a plate."

"Then we don't take the ford."

"You don't take the ford, you lose two days on the high moor with a child in this season, and the high moor has its own ways of killing people." Odell turned from the window. He'd been awake half the night assembling this, and now that it was assembled it came out of him fast and flat, the way the important things always did. "But there's a drover's path leaves the coach road a mile north, behind Torlan's standing stone. It cuts down to the ford a half-mile upstream of where the road meets the water, through alder scrub thick enough to hide a herd, let alone two travelers. My father drove cattle over it forty years. The Order's maps won't show it. Their local maps never do."

Wendell was staring at him. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because your mother pulled my brother out of a flooded ford below the mill, and I've owed her thirty years, and I pay my debts." Odell heard his own voice crack slightly on the last word and hated it and went on anyway. "And because there's a letter upstairs I was meant to give you, and I'm giving you the easier half of it instead, because the letter can wait and you can't."

Downstairs, faintly, the dogs began to growl at nothing—or at something far off, down the coach road, where the drizzle hung like a curtain.

Odell went to the pantry and began filling a sack: bread, cheese, the smoked ham he'd been saving, a flask of the good brandy. Behind him he heard Wendell say, quietly, to the boy, words Odell couldn't make out, and the boy answer in the soft click of bone against bone.

"Listen," Odell said, shouldering the sack. "Ride fast, cross high, and don't stop until the land changes. Because if those men reach the ford and find fresh tracks they missed—" he handed Wendell the bag, and met his eyes, and made himself say the rest plainly, "—they'll come back here, and they'll ask why my memory improved so conveniently, and I'll be lying to them again with whatever's left of my face. So make it count, carver. Make it count for both of us."

Wendell took the sack. Took Odell's hand in both of his, bone-carver's grip, and held it a moment too long for words to improve on.

Then they were down the stairs and out the back and into the alder scrub, two figures swallowed by the gray morning, and Odell Brask stood alone in his kitchen with his dogs and his mother's letter waiting upstairs, and listened to the coach road, and waited for the sound of hooves returning.
