## 12. First Blood

The ford at Marrow Creek was the kind of place Wendell would have stopped to sketch, in another life. The water came down off the high fells in three braided channels, shallow enough to walk, loud enough to cover conversation. Willows leaned over the banks like old women eavesdropping. The stones beneath the current were pale and round as knuckles.

Wendell crouched at the edge and let Cyrus drink first, cupping water in both hands because they had lost the second waterskin two days back and Wendell had not yet found a way to say *I'm sorry* that didn't sound like an apology for everything else too—the leaving, the road, the fear that traveled between them like a third companion who never slept.

Cyrus drank, then looked up, and pointed downstream.

Wendell followed the small finger and saw it: hoofprints in the soft clay of the far bank. Four horses, maybe five, shod, recent enough that water still wept up from the prints like the ground itself was bleeding.

"They crossed," Wendell said quietly. "Or they're waiting on the other side."

Cyrus's face did something complicated—fear and fury pressed together into a single flat expression that no child should know how to make. The child reached into the satchel, pulled out a scrap of birch bark and the stub of knife Wendell had sharpened for them, and carved fast, the strokes deep and angry.

*They follow. They do not search.*

"I know." Wendell had known since dawn, when the dead in the pack had started murmuring—not words yet, but the restless shifting of voices before a storm. The bones knew things before Wendell's eyes did. It had been true in the valley and it was truer out here, where every mile of road carried its own congregation of the unburied: plague pits, gallows fields, soldiers left where they fell in wars whose names had worn smooth. The dead were everywhere outside the valley. That was the terrible joke of it. The valley's silence had never been emptiness. It had been mercy.

Now the dead murmured, and Cyrus carved, and the ford waited.

"We could go around," Wendell said, though even as the words left their mouth they heard how thin they sounded. Around meant the high pass, two days lost, and the solstice was not a thing that waited politely. "We could—"

Cyrus shook their head once. Then the child did something new: took Wendell's hand, turned it palm-up, and traced a line across it with one finger. A river. Then tapped the center of the palm. *Here.* Then drew the line again, and again, insistent.

"You want to cross here."

A nod.

"Why?"

Cyrus hesitated. Then, slowly, deliberately, carved into the birch bark and held it up so the wind couldn't take it:

*Water remembers. Water hides.*

Wendell stared at the words. Somewhere in the pack, a half-finished flute hummed against the other bones—a low, approving note, like a teacher hearing a correct answer.

"All right," Wendell said. "Here, then."

* * *

They were halfway across the middle channel when the riders came.

Wendell heard them before seeing them—the dead went silent all at once, the way a room goes silent when the wrong person walks in—and then the willows on the southern bank shivered and four gray-cloaked figures emerged from the green shade like things the trees had been keeping.

"Stop there!" The lead rider's voice carried flat across the water. He wore no insignia, but he didn't need to. The Ashen Order dressed like penitents and moved like tax collectors, and everyone on this road knew what both of those meant. "In the name of the Radiant Vigil, stop where you stand."

Wendell stood in knee-deep water with a child beside them and a pack of whispering bones on their back, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost holy, that running was over.

"Travelers," Wendell called back, pitching their voice into the mild register they used for customers haggling over combs. "Just crossing to the north road. We're bone-carvers. Festival goods. Nothing worth your trouble."

The lead rider dismounted. He was young—younger than Wendell had expected, with the soft jaw of someone who had never missed a meal—and he walked his horse down to the waterline while the other three spread along the bank with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times.

"The Order doesn't trouble travelers." He smiled. It was a well-made smile, practiced in front of something. "We're looking for a missing child. Fled from a monastery fire, poor thing. Traumatized. Might not speak." His eyes found Cyrus and stayed there, and the smile didn't move but something behind it did, some door opening onto a furnace. "Might be standing right in front of me."

Cyrus's hand found Wendell's sleeve and gripped.

Wendell's mind did what it always did under pressure: it went small. It retreated to the workshop, to the smell of linseed oil and the rasp of the burin, to the thousand hours of learning exactly how much force a material could take before it broke. Bone split along its grain. Wood along its fibers. People, Wendell had always believed, were not materials. People could not be worked.

The young rider waded into the shallows toward them, hand dropping casually to his sword hilt, and behind him one of the others laughed at something, and the sound bounced off the water and came back wrong, and Wendell thought: *He is going to take the child. He is going to take the child and I am going to let him, because I have spent forty years making myself too small to matter, and smallness does not stop swords.*

The flute in the pack sang one clear, urgent note.

Later, Wendell would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. There was the rider, waist-deep now, reaching. There was Cyrus, stepping back, water dragging at the small legs. There was the carving knife—the good one, yew-handled, honed that morning on a roadside stone because a dull blade was an insult to the work—and there was Wendell's arm moving with a speed that belonged to someone else, someone who had been practicing for this moment their whole life without knowing it, all those years of precise, economical cuts, of knowing exactly where the grain gave way.

The knife went in below the rider's jaw, angled up, the way you'd enter a knot to split it clean.

It was not clean.

The young man made a sound Wendell had never heard a living body make—a wet, astonished click—and his hands came up and found Wendell's shoulders, not pushing, just holding, the way you'd hold a dance partner, and then the water took his weight and the red bloomed out around them both, spreading through the braided channels in slow ribbons that the current pulled apart and carried downstream.

Wendell let go of the knife. The knife stayed.

"No," Wendell said. To whom, they couldn't have said. "No, no—"

"Wendell." Cyrus's voice was silence; Cyrus's hands were not. Small fingers closed around Wendell's wrist and pulled, hard, and the child's eyes were wide and wet and absolutely certain, and the certainty was the only solid thing in the world. The other riders were shouting now, splashing into the channel, and the spell of horror broke the way ice breaks—all at once, everywhere.

Wendell ran.

They ran through water that grabbed at their knees, up the northern bank, into the willows, Cyrus's hand locked in theirs, the pack slamming against their spine with every stride and the dead inside it screaming—not words, not warnings, just a raw harmonic shriek like a struck bell that wouldn't stop. Behind them a crossbow snapped and a bolt buried itself in a willow trunk with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Another. Branches whipped at their faces. The world narrowed to green shadow and pounding blood and the grip of a child's hand.

Then downhill, unexpectedly, the land folding away into a dry streambed, and Cyrus hauling them left, then right, then flat down into a hollow beneath a fallen alder, and Wendell pressed their mouth into the black leaf-mold and breathed as quietly as a corpse while hooves thundered past close enough to feel in the ground.

The riders shouted to each other for a long time. Their voices got further away, then circled, then faded toward the north road, arguing about which way prey would run.

The forest settled. Somewhere a wood pigeon called, idiotic and calm.

Wendell lay in the dirt and discovered that they were shaking so violently their teeth chattered, and that Cyrus had wrapped both arms around their arm and was holding on as if Wendell were the child, as if Wendell were the one who needed anchoring, and perhaps that was true, and then Wendell rolled sideways and was sick into the leaves, twice, three times, until nothing was left but burning.

* * *

They walked until dusk put the woods to bed, following Cyrus's inexplicable sense of direction through ravines and deer paths, putting miles between themselves and the ford. When they finally stopped, in the ruin of a shepherd's cot with one wall still standing, Wendell sat down heavily against the stone and could not get up again.

The knife was still in their belt. They hadn't cleaned it. They looked at their hands in the last gray light and saw the rider's hands on their shoulders, holding, holding, the way you'd hold a dance partner, and their stomach heaved again though there was nothing left in it.

"I killed him," Wendell said aloud. The words sounded absurd in the open air, too large for the small life that had produced them. "I killed a man."

Cyrus, crouched by a tiny fire—they'd built it shielded in a hollow of stone, barely bigger than a cupped flame—looked up. The child set down the kindling, came over, and sat close, shoulder against Wendell's shoulder, the way Maren's grandchildren leaned against her at the winter fires.

"He would have taken you," Wendell said. "I keep telling myself—he was going to take you, and I—" Their voice cracked and they hated it. "I've never hurt anything in my life. Not on purpose. I carve the dead so they can speak kindly. That was the whole—that was the entire point of me. And now there's a man at the bottom of a creek with my knife in his throat, and somewhere there's a mother, or there isn't, or he deserved it, or he didn't, and none of it—" Wendell pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "None of it changes what I did. That's the part no one tells you. You can have the best reason in the world and it doesn't make the thing clean."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Then the child took Wendell's right hand—the killing hand—turned it over, and studied the palm in the firelight with the grave attention of a craftsman.

Then Cyrus fetched the birch bark and carved, slowly, carefully, and held it up:

*I watched monks burn. I could not move. I could not help. You moved.*

Wendell read it. Read it again.

"That's not absolution," Wendell said hoarsely.

Cyrus shook their head. Carved again:

*No. It is thanks.*

And that, somehow, was worse and better than absolution, and Wendell pulled the child against their side and held on and let the tears come, ugly and graceless, while the little fire burned down to embers and the dead in the pack murmured among themselves in voices too low to parse.

Later—much later, when Cyrus had finally gone rigid with sleep against Wendell's ribs—Wendell took out the knife and cleaned it by ember-light, working the cloth into the joint of handle and blade with the same care they'd give any tool, because tools deserved care even when they'd done terrible work, especially then. The steel was fine steel. It had not asked to be used this way.

"It won't be the last time," Wendell said to the blade, quietly, testing the truth of it in their mouth. "If they're still hunting us, it won't be the last time."

The dead stirred in the pack. One voice rose above the murmur—an old voice, dry as autumn reeds, a voice Wendell recognized from a flute sold years ago to a shepherd's widow—and it spoke clearly for the first time since the ford:

*"The one you dropped carries paper. Paper keeps. Go back for the paper."*

Wendell went very still.

"What paper?"

But the dead had already sunk back into their chorus, leaving only the word hanging in the dark hut like smoke: *paper, paper, paper*—and Wendell looked north, toward the distant black line of the creek, and understood that the man they had killed was not finished speaking to them yet.

The dead, Wendell thought grimly, sheathing the knife, never were.
