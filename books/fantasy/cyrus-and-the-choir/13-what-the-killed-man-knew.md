## 13. What the Killed Man Knew

The river did not want the blood.

Wendell knelt at the ford's edge and watched it try—the current pulling thin red threads downstream, unraveling them, refusing to hold what they'd made here an hour past dawn. The water was cold enough to ache. They let it ache. It seemed only right that something should.

Cyrus sat a little way off on a flat stone, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, watching Wendell with those hollow dark eyes that had learned, in the weeks since the valley, to watch everything. Not afraid of Wendell. That was the worst part. A child who had watched monks burn alive, who had run from coast to mountain with a whistle clutched in one fist, had looked at a bone-carver kneeling sick over a dead man and felt—what? Recognition? Pity?

*It was him or us,* the dead whispered through the flute in Wendell's coat pocket, patient as ever. *You know this. We know you know.*

"I know," Wendell said aloud, because there was no one left to lie to.

The rider lay where he'd fallen, half in the shallows, his gray cloak spread around him like spilled ash. His horse had bolted upstream; the other two riders' horses were long gone as well, which meant the Order would know within a day that their patrol had stopped answering. Wendell had not been able to make themselves care about that yet. There would be time for fear later. There always was.

First there was this: a man with a face. Young, under the dirt. A scar through one eyebrow, old and white. Boots mended twice at the heel with mismatched thread, the kind of repair a poor man does himself by firelight. He had chewed the nails of his left hand down to the quick.

Wendell had killed him with a carving knife—a tool made for shaping whistles and grave-gifts—and the man had looked surprised, more than anything, before the ford took his weight and the water took the rest.

*I'm sorry,* Wendell told him silently. It was inadequate. Every apology in the world was inadequate, and people said them anyway, because the alternative was silence, and silence was how gods went mad.

Cyrus slid off the stone and came to stand beside them. Small hand hovering near Wendell's shoulder—not touching, never quite touching, though the distance between hover and touch had shrunk over the miles until it was nearly nothing. Cyrus pointed at the body, then at Wendell, then pressed both fists briefly against their own chest.

*I know,* Wendell signed back, using the three words Cyrus had taught them on the road. *I carry it.* The sign for *carry* was a cupping of the hands, like holding water, like holding a bird. Like holding a whistle.

They searched the body together, because it had to be done and because Cyrus refused to be sent away from anything anymore. Wendell understood. The child had been sent away from everything once already, and look what it had cost.

The pockets gave up the small archaeology of a life: a tin of greasy salve, a stub of black wax pencil, a wooden saint's medallion worn smooth—some god of the lowlands, its face rubbed featureless by a thumb that had worried it for years. A strip of dried meat. Three coppers and a silver bit. A folded paper, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with cord.

And a bone whistle.

Wendell's hands went still above it.

It was Order-made—they could see that at a glance, the workmanship precise and soulless, turned on a lathe rather than carved by hand—but it was bone, and bone was bone, and the dead lived in bone the way light lived in glass. Wendell picked it up carefully, the way one picks up a snake one hopes is harmless.

Nothing. No whisper. No hum. Just dead matter, drilled and polished, colder than the river water.

"It doesn't work," Wendell murmured, and then heard how strange that sounded, and heard too the memory of Inquisitor Serle's name in Maren's warnings—*he burns bones and feels nothing*—and understood, with a slow roll of nausea, that this was the point. That somewhere south of here, men in gray cloaks carried instruments designed to do exactly nothing, and that the nothing frightened them, and that they had built an entire order inside that fear.

Cyrus reached out and touched the whistle's mouthpiece with one fingertip. Then shook their head slowly, and made the sign Wendell had learned only two nights ago: fingers closing, one by one, into a fist. *Silence. Forced.*

Not a broken instrument. A gagged one.

"Put it in the pack," Wendell said. "We may need to show someone."

* * *

They walked a mile upstream before letting the horses drink, and another mile before making camp in a fold of hills where a fallen oak made a windbreak and the road was hidden behind two ridges of gorse. Wendell built no fire. Cyrus built a very small one anyway, shielded in a ring of stones, and when Wendell raised an eyebrow, the child pointed at the paper still wrapped in oilcloth, then at the fading light, then at their own eyes.

*We need to read it,* the signs said. *I want to see.*

So they sat shoulder to shoulder in the blue dusk with a fire small enough to hide in a cupped palm, and Wendell untied the cord, and unrolled the oilcloth, and found inside not one page but several, clipped together with a brass fastener gone green with age.

The top page was orders, written in a clerk's tight hand:

*Patrol the north ford road between the marker-stones and the shepherd's cut. Detain all travelers bearing instruments of resonance. Report any sign of the vessel to the relay at Tollhouse Bridge. The vessel is small. The vessel travels with one adult, unknown. The vessel cannot speak. Do not harm the vessel. The vessel must arrive whole.*

"The vessel," Wendell read aloud, and hated the word in their mouth. Not a child. Not Cyrus. A *vessel*, the way a jug is a jug, the way a lamp is a lamp—something whose entire worth was what could be poured out of it.

Cyrus had gone very still beside them. Wendell felt the stillness the way one feels a held breath in a dark room.

Then the child took the pages gently from Wendell's hands, turned to the second sheet, and began to read.

That was the thing Wendell kept forgetting, and kept being struck dumb by: Cyrus could read. Not the valley script, not the trade-tongue glyphs Wendell used for marking prices on crates—those the child had learned in weeks, haltingly, with Wendell sounding out letters by firelight. But the old language, the spiral-and-stack language that predated every kingdom, every archive, every grave-rubbing in Wendell's collection—that Cyrus read the way other children read their own names.

And these pages were written in it.

Not entirely. The clerk's hand annotated the margins in the trade-tongue, but the body of the document—the older parts, copied and recopied until the ink of the oldest lines had been traced over so many times it stood up from the page in ridges—was Choir-script. Cyrus's finger moved along the spirals, and Wendell watched the child's face change as they read, watchfulness giving way to something rawer.

"What does it say?" Wendell asked quietly. "Tell me all of it. Even the parts that frighten you. *Especially* those."

Cyrus was quiet a long moment. Then they set the pages flat on Wendell's knee, weighted with a stone, and began to draw in the dirt with a stick, translating as they went—old symbols becoming crude pictures becoming, in the margins, the trade-letters Wendell could puzzle out.

*The Choir sleeps beneath the Hollow Spire,* the first passage said—or said roughly; Cyrus's translation wobbled, and Wendell caught fragments like fish slipping through a net. *The Spire is hollow as a bell. The god lies beneath it, wound in song. Once in each hundred years the binding thins, and a voice must go down into the deep chamber and sing the god back under. If no voice comes—*

Cyrus stopped drawing. Their stick hovered over the dirt.

"If no voice comes," Wendell prompted, "then what?"

The child drew it instead of signing it: a spiral, unwinding. Coming apart. And beneath the spiral, a figure with arms flung wide—not sleeping. Waking.

*The god wakes,* the margin note confirmed, in the clerk's tidy hand, as if some Order scribe had once needed the horror spelled out plainly. *The god wakes, and it mourns, and its mourning burns.*

"Mourns," Wendell said. "It *mourns*?"

But Cyrus was already turning to the third page, and Wendell saw the child's shoulders change—saw them draw in, the way a body draws in around a wound—and knew, before reading a single symbol, that this page was worse.

It was a list.

The heading was in the clerk's hand: *ANCHORS — REGISTER OF LIVING VOICES.* Beneath it, a column of names, most struck through with a single decisive line, the ink of the strikethroughs varying in age—some faded brown, some fresh enough to shine. Wendell counted without wanting to. Fourteen names crossed out. Some had dates beside them. Some had single words: *dead. taken. failed. spent.*

*Spent.* Wendell's eye snagged on that word and could not get free of it. Spent, like a coin. Like fuel.

And at the bottom of the column, uncrossed, unmarked, the ink crisp and recent compared to the rest:

*Cyrus.*

No family name. No date of taking. Just the name, alone at the end of a list of the dead and the disappeared, waiting its turn.

Beside Wendell, the child did not cry. Wendell had learned that about Cyrus early—grief came out of this child sideways, through the hands, through carvings, through long motionless stares at things that weren't there. Now the child simply put one finger on their own name, and then lifted the finger and looked at it, as if expecting the ink to have transferred, as if being written in an enemy's ledger might leave a mark on the skin.

"They've been hunting your people for a hundred years," Wendell said. It wasn't a question. The dates on the strikethroughs spanned four generations. "One by one. Voice by voice. And now you're the last name on the page."

Cyrus nodded. Then, slowly, deliberately, they picked up the stick again and wrote in the dirt—not Choir-script, not the trade-tongue, but a picture plain enough for anyone: fourteen small figures, crossed out. One figure standing. Above the standing figure, a circle of smaller figures—monks, singing, from the memory Wendell had glimpsed in the child's carvings—all of them crossed out too.

*Everyone who could have done this is gone,* the picture said. *There is only me.*

"There is only you," Wendell agreed, "and me, and a knife I wish I'd never had to use, and—" they touched the flute in their coat, and the dead stirred against their ribs like birds settling, "—and everyone else we're carrying. Which is not nothing, Cyrus. I need you to hear that. It is not nothing."

The child looked at them. In the firelight their eyes were very old and very young at once, the way eyes get when they have seen too much too soon and had no one to give it to.

Then Cyrus did something they had never done before. They leaned, just slightly, until their shoulder rested against Wendell's arm. Barely a touch. A feather's weight. But Wendell felt it all the way down, the way one feels the first drop of rain after a drought.

They sat like that while the fire burned down to coals, and the pages lay on Wendell's knee, and the last name on the list gleamed faintly in the ember-light like a mark that had been waiting for them all along.

* * *

Sleep did not come, so Wendell worked.

This was the old habit, the valley habit: when the mind would not quiet, put the hands to something. Back home they would have carved—scrap wood into spoons, buttons, small animals for the village children. Here, with the pack open and the dead man's papers spread by coal-light, the work that offered itself was different.

They studied the remaining pages.

Most were logistics—the dry skeleton of a hunt stretched across months. Relay points. Names of informants, some marked with sums paid. A sketch of a wagon convoy's formation. A report, dated six weeks past, describing the burning of a coastal monastery in language so bloodless it might have been an inventory: *structure cleared, materials recovered, assets dispersed.* Wendell knew what *assets dispersed* meant. Somewhere in these pages, in a clerk's indifferent hand, was the sentence that described children being carried away in wagons.

But one page was different. Older. The vellum was soft as cloth, its edges rounded by centuries of handling, and the writing on it was not the clerk's hand at all but something formal and beautiful, each letter drawn as if by someone who believed the act of writing was itself a prayer. Wendell could not read it. But Cyrus, waking briefly in the night, had touched it and signed one word before drifting off again:

*Song.*

The renewal-song, perhaps. Or instructions for it. Or—Wendell's stomach tightened—the words a voice was meant to say at the moment of spending. They folded it with exaggerated care and put it in the inner pocket, against their chest, where the flute also rode. Let the dead and the living documents keep each other company.

Near dawn, Wendell found the last thing.

It was tucked inside the brass fastener's fold, a scrap half the size of a palm, easy to miss—easy, Wendell thought grimly, for a dying man to miss too, if he'd ever been meant to surrender it. A charcoal rubbing, the kind Wendell themselves had made a thousand times in the valley's burial caves, laid over carved stone and stroked with soft cloth until the image rose ghost-gray from the page.

But this stone had not been in any valley cave. The rubbing showed a map.

Or the beginning of one—a coastline, a river fork, and rising from the river's bend a shape Wendell recognized instantly because they had drawn it themselves, years ago, from a pilgrim's drunken description: a mountain like a finger pointing at the sky, its peak broken off flat, as if the gods had snapped it like a chalk stick.

Beneath the drawing, in the clerk's utilitarian hand, someone had added a label:

*HOLLOW SPIRE — APPROACH BY THE RIVER ROAD. THE CITY OF SCALES CONTROLS THE CROSSING. BRIBE ACCORDINGLY.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost an afterthought, in handwriting cramped with haste or fear:

*The anchors are taken to the Spire, not away from it. Ask why.*

Wendell sat with that a long time, watching the coals pulse.

*Ask why.* Someone in the Order had doubts. Someone had written a question they were not supposed to write, and hidden it in a patrolman's papers, and trusted—to whom? To chance? To whoever found it? The handwriting was hurried but steady. Not a coward's hand. A careful person's hand, doing a careless-seeming thing on purpose.

*The anchors are taken to the Spire, not away from it.*

Which meant the children—the voices, the spent and the unspent—were being kept. Held somewhere beneath that broken mountain, like tuning forks in a case, like candles in a cellar, waiting for the day the Order decided to light them all at once.

Cyrus slept on, curled against the fallen oak, one hand closed loosely around the bone whistle that had started everything. Watching the child breathe, Wendell felt the grief move through them—not the sick, shallow grief of the ford, not the self-loathing that had bent them over the river—but something with structure to it now, something with edges and a handle. Grief shaped into a tool. The dead man's boots had been mended twice with mismatched thread; the dead man had been somebody's boy once; and none of it, none of it, would be allowed to mean nothing.

"You see it too," Wendell whispered to the flute, to the fourteen crossed-out names, to Maren and the valley and the mother whose debts were still being paid by strangers downriver. "It's not a rescue anymore. It never was. It's a door, and we're going to walk through it, and we're going to bring everyone on that list home or know the reason."

The dead answered—not in words, for once, but in a single low tone that rose through the flute and through Wendell's chest and out into the graying air, a sound like the first note of something long withheld.

Across the dead fire, Cyrus opened their eyes.

They didn't ask what the sound was. They had grown up inside that sound. Instead the child sat up, pointed east—where the hills fell away toward the river, and the river ran down to the crossing, and the crossing belonged to a city where justice was bought by the pound—and then held up the charcoal rubbing of the Hollow Spire, and tapped it twice.

*There,* the gesture said. *Now. While they think we're running.*

Wendell began, quietly, to break camp.

The solstice was coming. And for the first time since the valley's edge, they knew exactly where they were going—and exactly what was waiting there, both the salvation and the trap, at the bottom of a mountain hollowed like a bell.

They saddled the horses in the dark before dawn, and turned east, toward the Scales.
