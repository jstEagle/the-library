## 14. The City of Scales

The river announced the city before the city appeared.

Cyrus felt it first in the soles of their feet, a low thrumming through the ferry planks, as if the water itself had learned to speak and never stopped. Then came the smell: tar and smoke and fish gone soft in the sun, and under it all something sweeter and stranger, spices Cyrus had no names for, sold by people whose languages rose and fell like birdsong on the docks.

They stood at the ferry rail beside Wendell, who gripped the worn strap of the bone-pack the way Cyrus gripped the whistle—always, everywhere, even in sleep. The morning fog was burning off the river in long gray ribbons, and through them the city assembled itself piece by piece: first the masts, a forest of them, hundreds, thousands; then the rooftops, tiled in colors that made Cyrus think of the monastery's painted eggs at springtide, before the springtide when everything burned; then the walls, and the towers, and at last the great bronze scales that crowned the central spire, wide as wagon wheels, turning slowly in the wind.

*The City of Scales*, Wendell had said on the road, the way a person says *beware of dogs*. *Everything there is weighed. Everything there is for sale. Including the thing they weigh you with.*

Cyrus had carved a question into the rail's soft wood with their thumbnail: *scales weigh what?*

Wendell had looked at the carving a long time. *Coin,* they said finally. *Coin, mostly. And when the coin runs out—people.*

Now the ferry bumped the dock and Cyrus's stomach bumped against their ribs, and the city took them.

* * *

It was too much. That was the first truth, and Cyrus held it the way they held everything, silently, pressing it flat.

In the monastery there had been bells and chanting, but the sounds had been *shaped*, had risen and settled like breathing. The city made no shapes. It made only noise—a grinding, layered roar of wheels on stone and gulls screaming over fish guts and a hundred voices all selling, arguing, singing, cursing, and none of them listening to any of the others. Cyrus pressed close to Wendell's side and put their hands over their ears and then took them away again, because covering their ears meant not hearing Wendell either, and Wendell's voice had become, in six weeks of road, the sound that meant *safe*.

*Stay where I can see you,* Wendell said, low, for perhaps the hundredth time since the ford. Since the blood. Since the night Wendell had knelt at the river's edge being sick and Cyrus had stood behind them not knowing how to carve *it was me or him* because Wendell already knew, and knowing hadn't helped.

Cyrus stayed where Wendell could see them.

The streets near the docks were canyons of gray stone, slick with river damp. Everything here had been built twice, Cyrus realized—built, and then built over—because the lower walls were older, darker, swallowed to their windows by the newer stone around them, like a city that kept burying its own dead and building houses on the graves. Cyrus thought of the Choir, of the sleeping thing under the mountain, of all the world's buried things, and wondered if the city heard them too, down in its foundations. Wondered if the city had ever once been quiet enough to listen.

They passed a courthouse on the second morning. Cyrus knew it was a courthouse because of the scales carved over its doors, twin to the ones on the spire, and because of the line of people waiting outside them in the drizzle—people with the particular gray patience of those who have been waiting a long time and will wait longer. A crier stood on the steps reading names.

*Petitioner Olla Vesk, versus the estate of Harn Vesk, deceased—heard at the fourth bell, chamber nine—*

An old woman in the line was feeding crumbs to pigeons off a cloth in her lap. Her hands shook. Her shoes had been mended so many times they were more thread than leather. When the crier reached a name she didn't rise, and Cyrus understood with a small cold certainty that her name was not on the list, that her name had been *removed* from the list, and that she would come back tomorrow anyway, because the scales were the only thing in the city that pretended to care whether she existed.

Cyrus stopped and looked at her until Wendell's hand closed gently on their shoulder and moved them along.

That night, in the corner of a stable they could barely afford, Cyrus carved into a scrap of lath: *the scales are broken.* Then, after a while, because it was truer: *the scales are sold.*

Wendell read it by candle stub and didn't argue. That was the worst part, Cyrus thought. Wendell didn't argue.

* * *

The magistrate's name was Corvess, and his chambers smelled of ink and lilies, which Cyrus would remember for the rest of their life as the smell of the first lie they ever watched Wendell tell.

They had found his name from a ferry clerk for the price of a smile and a copper, and his chambers were on the third floor of the Hall of Records, behind a door of black oak with a brass plate so polished it threw back their faces in little wobbles. Wendell knocked. A voice like dry paper said *enter.*

Corvess was small and neat and old, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of pond water, and he did not look up from his ledger for a full minute after they entered. Cyrus counted the minute. It was a power, the not-looking. Cyrus understood power that way—understood it better than speech, maybe. Power was making people wait.

*Travelers,* Wendell said. *Two of us. We came up the river road from the south, from the fords.*

*Many do.* Corvess turned a page.

*There was trouble at a ford. Some days back. Riders. There may be—reports. Descriptions.*

Now Corvess looked up. Pond-water eyes, and in them something quick and hungry, a fish rising. *Trouble,* he said. *What kind of trouble?*

*The kind that gets written down,* Wendell said.

The silence that followed had weight. Cyrus stood very still and watched Wendell's hands, which hung at their sides in the posture of a person deciding something, and Cyrus knew what was being decided, and their chest went tight and hot.

*Records,* Corvess said at last, delicately, *are expensive to maintain. Ink. Vellum. The clerks' time. And errors do occur, from time to time. A report misfiled. A description entered under the wrong date, the wrong road, the wrong—* he smiled, thin as a knife laid flat— *wrong everything. Such errors can be corrected. For those who can pay for the correction.*

*How much?* Wendell said.

*For a small error? Twenty crowns.*

Cyrus didn't know what twenty crowns was. But they knew Wendell's face, and they watched it do something it had never done before—watched it go smooth and empty, like the surface of the pond in those eyes, while behind it something raged. Wendell counted out the coins from the belt-purse, the purse that held everything the valley had sent them off with, Maren's coins and the innkeeper Odell's coins and the coins from the sale of the good carving knife, and Cyrus watched each one land on the black oak desk with a small bright click, and hated the sound, and hated the room, and hated the lilies most of all, their sweetness lying over the ink-smell like a cloth over a face.

*And the riders,* Corvess said, sweeping the coins into a drawer without counting them, which meant he had known the price before he named it, which meant everything was known before it was said. *The dead one at the ford. There will be a coroner's inquiry. Those are harder to—correct.*

*How much harder?*

*Forty more.*

The second purse. The emergency purse. Cyrus watched Wendell's hand hesitate over it—one heartbeat, two—and then pay, and the clicking of the coins sounded different this time, duller, like the sound of a door closing.

*The ford is a long way from here,* Corvess said, making a small note in his ledger with a flourish, *and the report, when it arrives, will describe an accident. A horse slipping on wet stone. A traveler, unnamed, passing through. Nothing to do with two people who were never on this road at all.* He looked up. *Where were you, in fact?*

*East,* Wendell said. *Toward the hill country. We're going east.*

*East,* Corvess repeated, writing it, and Cyrus understood suddenly that they had just paid forty crowns to be written into the record as a lie, and that the lie was now the truth, and that this was how the world worked outside the valley, outside the monastery, everywhere: the scales did not weigh what was true. They weighed what was *paid for*, and truth was just the thing left over in the pan when the coin ran out.

In the corridor afterward, Wendell walked fast, too fast, and Cyrus had to trot to keep up, and at the top of the stairs Wendell stopped and put one hand flat against the stone wall and stood there breathing, and Cyrus saw that the hand was shaking.

*I hate it,* Wendell said, not turning around. Their voice was low and rough, a voice talking to itself as much as to Cyrus. *I hate that it works. I hate that he'll take our coin and sleep fine tonight and the old woman in the rain will wait another year, because she can't—* The hand curled into a fist against the stone. *In the valley, if a man wrongs you, you say it to his face and the whole valley hears it and that's the law. Here the law is a door you buy the key to, and behind the door is another door, and behind that one—*

Wendell stopped. Turned. Looked at Cyrus with pond-colored exhaustion and something underneath it that Cyrus had learned to recognize as love, fierce and unschooled and slightly ashamed of itself.

*You carve it,* Wendell said. *You tell me if I've got it wrong.*

Cyrus took out the lath and the knife and carved, slowly, carefully, in the old language first and then, beneath it, in the trade-tongue Wendell had been learning letter by letter on the road, so there could be no mistake:

*you didn't buy justice. you bought us a road. it's not the same thing. it's enough.*

Wendell read it twice. Then Wendell laughed—a short, cracked sound, half grief—and put a hand on Cyrus's head the way the monks used to, before, in the before-time, and said, *Enough. Yes. All right.*

* * *

They stayed four days in the City of Scales, because the road east was closed by floodwaters and the ferry south was watched—Wendell saw the gray cloaks on the docks the second morning and steered them both into a fish market without breaking stride, and Cyrus spent an hour among the gutting tables learning the shapes of fish and pretending their heart wasn't hammering.

On the third day, Wendell sold the flute.

Not the bone-whistle—never the whistle, that stayed in Cyrus's fist, that was not for sale, and Corvess's clerks and every gray cloak in the city could have burned the whole city down before Cyrus let it go. But the river flute, the one Wendell had carved on the road from alder wood and river-clay, the one that had made Cyrus laugh the first time, a terrible honking laugh, at a campfire in week two—the flute went to a instrument-seller's stall for eleven crowns, and Wendell walked away from the stall without looking back, and Cyrus looked back enough for both of them.

On the fourth day the floodwaters dropped and they went east through the east gate, past the tollhouse where a bored guard waved them through because their papers—Corvess's papers, the beautiful purchased lie—said they were going east and had been going east all along.

Cyrus looked back once from the top of the bridge. The bronze scales on the spire turned slowly in the wind, weighing nothing, weighing everything, and beneath them the city roared on, and somewhere in it the old woman waited in the rain for a name to be read.

Cyrus carved nothing. There was no lath long enough.

* * *

Two days east of the city, at a crossroads shrine so old its god's name had worn off the stone, they met the boy.

He came out of the hedgerow like something the hedge had grown—a scrap of a person, all elbows and dirty blond hair, moving with the quick sideways economy of someone who has been chased by a great many things. He froze when he saw them. Wendell's hand went to the carving knife. The boy's eyes—quick, brown, absurdly young—went from Wendell's face to the knife to the bone-pack to Cyrus, and stopped on Cyrus, and stayed there.

Because Cyrus had, without thinking, without deciding to, raised one hand and made a shape in the air. An old shape. A Choir shape. The sign for *small one, no fear* that Brother Ilex used to make across the refectory when Cyrus woke screaming from the fire-dreams, and that no one outside the monastery had any reason to know.

The boy stared at the shape the way a starving man stares at bread.

*You're him,* the boy said. His voice cracked on the second word. *The one they're hunting. The vessel. I've heard them talk about you in the taverns—the gray cloaks drink at the Tollhouse, they talk too much when they drink—I heard them say a child, a mute child, carves in the old tongue—* He swallowed. *I can get you anything. Papers, horses, the convoy routes—I know the roads better than the roads know themselves. I'm fast and nobody ever remembers my face. My name's Bramble.*

*Why would you help us?* Wendell said, and the knife was still half-drawn, and Cyrus understood why—understood the arithmetic of the road now, the cost of every kindness, the way the scales tipped everywhere and always.

Bramble's eyes never left Cyrus. He looked, Cyrus thought, like someone who had been alone so long he'd forgotten it was a condition and not the weather.

*Because,* he said, *you did the hand-shape. And I haven't seen anybody do a kind thing in this whole rotten country since—* He stopped. Started over. *Because I want to be where you're going. Whatever it is. Wherever it is. I want to be where people do the hand-shape.*

Cyrus looked at Wendell. Wendell looked at Cyrus. The wind moved through the hedgerow, and somewhere far off, down under the mountains, under the whole sleeping weight of the world, something Cyrus could not hear but had never once stopped feeling turned over in its long dream, and the dead in Wendell's pack stirred against each other like birds settling on a branch before a storm.

Cyrus took out the lath and carved one word and held it up:

*stay.*

Bramble's face did something complicated and young and broke open like dawn, and Wendell sighed the sigh of a person whose life has just gotten larger and more dangerous, and the three of them turned east together, toward the hill country, toward the Hollow Spire, toward everything.
