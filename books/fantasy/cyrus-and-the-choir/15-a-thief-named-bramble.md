## 15. A Thief Named Bramble

There were three rules to working the Scales, and I'd learned every one of them the way you learn anything worth knowing: by bleeding.

One: never lift from anyone with calluses on their sword hand. Two: never work a crowd twice in the same week. Three — and this was the big one, the one carved into my skull in letters I could still feel — never, *never* touch a pilgrim's pack.

Pilgrims carried relics. Relics carried curses, and worse than curses, they carried priests who came looking for them.

So of course that morning, flat broke, three days behind on my debt to Vetch the ferryman, and hungry enough to consider eating my own boot leather, I broke rule three.

The mark came down Rope-Walk Row just past the fish stalls, moving slow, the way strangers do when they're reading the city instead of walking through it. Tall figure, gray travel coat gone brown at the hems, a long bundle wrapped in oilcloth slung across their back like a lute case, and a heavy satchel riding their hip. A child walked beside them — small, quiet, dark-eyed, holding the stranger's sleeve with two fingers, the way you hold something you're not sure will still be there when you look again.

Stranger and child. Soft marks. Easy marks.

I told myself all of that while my hands were already doing the work, because my hands have always been smarter than my mouth, which is saying something, because my mouth is a legend in four districts.

I drifted alongside them in the fish-stall crush, let a cart full of eel barrels cut between us, slipped through the gap, and had two fingers on the satchel's buckle before the world went wrong.

* * *

Here's what went wrong, in order.

First: the buckle wasn't a buckle. It was bone, carved into a little coiled shape, and the moment my skin touched it the whole thing made a sound. Not out loud. Inside. Like a bell ringing underwater, right behind my teeth.

Second: the stranger turned around faster than any person I've ever met, including a juggler named Fitch who could catch knives thrown at his blind side, and caught my wrist. Not hard. Just *there*, like a door closing.

Third: the child looked up at me, and I stopped being able to breathe for a second, on account of the child's eyes being the color of deep river water and about as friendly, and because the child raised one hand — deliberately, slowly — and pointed one finger at me, then curled the finger shut, like closing a book.

I don't know why that scared me. It should not have scared me. But my whole body decided otherwise, and my knees nearly voted to sit down right there in the fish guts.

"Thief," said the stranger. Calm voice. Flat accent, valley-flat, the kind that comes from places where nothing happens for a hundred years at a stretch.

"Alleged thief," I said, because rule four, which I invented myself, is *always contest the charge*. "Show me the warrant."

"There's no warrant."

"Then we're in negotiations." I tried to pull my wrist back. The grip didn't move. Not crushing — just absolute, like the grip of a statue that had decided to be polite about it. "Fine. Negotiations are going badly. What do you want? Coin? I have — " I patted my pockets with my free hand and produced, after genuine searching, half a dried plum and a button. "The button's worth something. It's horn. Horn buttons are fashionable this season."

The stranger looked at the button. Then at me. And here's the part I still think about: the stranger's eyes weren't angry. They weren't even annoyed. They were *sad*, the way you look at weather coming in over a mountain, and they said, quietly, almost to themselves:

"You're starving."

"I'm — no. I'm fine. I'm flush. This is my flush outfit."

The child tugged the stranger's sleeve. Pointed at my stomach. My stomach, traitor that it is, chose that exact moment to make a noise like a dying gull.

Silence. Fish-stall noise around us, eel carts and hawkers, but silence in our little triangle of shame.

"Oh, come *on*," I said.

The stranger sighed — a long, put-upon sigh, the sigh of someone whose week had already been very long — and did the last thing I expected, which was to open the satchel, take out a heel of bread and a wedge of cheese, and press both into my hands.

"Eat," they said. "And then you're going to tell me who runs this district, because you clearly know it better than anyone alive."

I want it on record that I considered running. I want it on record that I weighed my options like a professional. And I also want it on record that the bread was still warm, somehow, warm like it had been baked that morning by somebody's grandmother, and I ate the whole thing standing there with cheese grease on my chin while two strangers watched me like I was an exhibit.

My name's Bramble. Remember it. Everyone does eventually, usually while cursing.

* * *

They were looking for the Hollow Spire road, which is how I knew they were either pilgrims or lunatics, because nobody goes to the Spire. Nobody *wants* to go to the Spire. The Spire is a mountain with a hole in it, up past the Graywater ford, three weeks' bad road through country where the Order's riders stop travelers and ask questions you don't want to answer.

"The Spire," I repeated, mouth full. "Sure. Lovely time of year. Bring a picnic. Maybe a coffin."

"You know the way?"

"I know *a* way. Whether it's the way you want depends on whether you'd rather be robbed by bandits or drowned by ferrymen. I can arrange either, honestly, rates negotiable."

The stranger — Wendell, the child called them, though not with their voice; the kid pointed at their own chest and drew a little symbol in the dust, and Wendell read it like a letter — Wendell studied me with that same sad-weather look. Behind us the fish market roared. Somewhere a bell tolled the hour, and I noticed, without wanting to notice, that the bone buckle on the satchel hummed along with it, one beat off, like a second voice.

"What's in the bag?" I asked. Rule five: always know what you're stealing before you steal it. I was behind schedule.

"Bones," Wendell said. "Carved ones. Mine."

"You carve bones."

"It's a trade."

"It's a *creepy* trade."

Wendell shrugged, and the shrug had whole mountains in it, and I decided — recklessly, stupidly, the way I decide everything — that I liked this person enormously.

The child, meanwhile, had crouched down by a puddle and was doing something with a stick. Drawing. I wandered over, because curiosity has killed more cats than any disease, and looked.

Spirals. Stacked figures, little stick-people piled like cordwood, and above them a shape I couldn't parse — a circle with lines radiating off it, except the lines bent, all of them, bending away from the center like water around a stone.

"That your family?" I asked. "The stacked ones?"

The child didn't look up. Drew one more figure apart from the rest, small, with a line through its middle. Then looked at me. Held my eyes. And I understood, all at once and horribly, that the line-through-the-middle figure was me — that the kid had drawn *me*, marked, separate, and that the drawing meant something I couldn't read yet but would, later, and wouldn't enjoy.

"Cheerful little artist you've got," I said to Wendell, backing up a step.

"Cyrus doesn't speak," Wendell said. "Speaks another way. You'll learn it or you won't."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you'll keep misunderstanding each other. Most people manage it their whole lives."

I laughed. It came out too loud. The child — Cyrus — was still watching me, and I'll swear on whatever you like, on my mother's grave, on Vetch's ferry, on the Scales themselves: when Cyrus finally looked away, the bone buckle sang again, soft, and this time it sounded *pleased*.

I have been chased by dogs, dipped in the canal twice, and once spent a night locked in a grain silo with a rival thief named Pock-Mark Ludd. None of it unsettled me like that buckle sounding pleased.

"So," I said, clapping my hands together because my hands needed a job. "The Spire road. Three weeks, bad country, Order checkpoints at the Graywater and the tollhouse at Marrow Ford. You'll need a guide who knows which ditches to sleep in and which innkeepers can be bought and which ones will sell you. Lucky for you, I'm available. Unlucky for you, my rate is steep."

"We have no money," Wendell said.

"Then my rate is bread. Regular bread. And first pick of the sleeping ditch."

Cyrus made a sound. Small, dry, quick — I'd swear it was a laugh with the volume taken out. The kid drew in the dust again, fast: a stick-figure with wild hair (flattering), standing next to a bigger figure, and between them, three of those spirals.

Wendell looked at the drawing and went very still.

"What?" I demanded. "What's it say?"

"It says," Wendell said slowly, "that you come with us."

"I *just said* that."

"No. It says you come with us." They met my eyes. "That's the word for it. There isn't a separate word for guide. Or hireling. It's the same sign as — "

"As what?"

"*Kin,*" Wendell said, and looked faintly embarrassed about it, and Cyrus looked entirely unembarrassed, and I stood there in the fish market with cheese on my chin and a hole in my chest I hadn't known about until something fell into it.

"Well," I said. "That's a translation error, obviously. Your dead language is rusty. I accept anyway."

* * *

We left the city by the Weeper's Gate at dusk, because Vetch's boys watch the Ferry Gate and Vetch's boys have long memories and shorter tempers. I led them down the smuggler's stair behind the tanneries — hold your nose, I warned them, and Wendell held theirs with dignity while Cyrus breathed the tannery stink in like a flower — and out onto the north road under a sky going bruise-colored over the hills.

Three things happened on that first night out, and I set them down here because they matter, though I didn't know it yet.

First: Wendell cooked. Out of nowhere, a fire the size of a cupped hand, a pot, some roots Cyrus dug up with the efficiency of someone who'd been hungry before, and a stew so good I briefly forgave the world everything. I asked where they learned it. Wendell said, "A valley where nothing changes," and would say nothing else, and I filed it away, because I am a professional, and professionals file things.

Second: Cyrus taught Wendell signs. I watched them do it by firelight — the kid shaping little drawings in the air with two fingers, Wendell answering, slow, halting, like a man learning to waltz at fifty — and I noticed that whenever Cyrus used a certain spiral sign, the bundle of bones against Wendell's pack would tick. Gently. Like knuckles rapping a table. Like applause, maybe. Or a heartbeat.

Third: I couldn't sleep. So I sat up watching the road, because that's rule six — *first watch is always yours if you want to be trusted, and trust is cheaper than armor* — and somewhere past midnight I heard it.

Singing. Far off. Under the wind, under the crickets, a thread of voices with no bodies attached, rising and falling in a pattern too patient to be human and too sad to be anything else.

Cyrus was awake too, sitting up in the bedroll, listening with their whole face tilted toward the dark. And when I looked at the kid, really looked, I saw that they were mouthing along. Following it. Knowing it.

Like a moth knows a lamp.

I lay back down and stared at the stars and told myself: *Bramble, old friend, you have signed on to something with teeth in it.* And then I told myself: *Yes. Good. About time.*

* * *

Two days out, at the crossroads shrine where the north road splits toward Graywater, we found the first sign of trouble, and it found us wearing ash-gray cloaks.

Riders. Four of them, watering horses at the shrine well, their cloaks the color of old smoke, and even from the tree line I could see the mark stitched at their shoulders — a circle with a vertical bar, the Ashen Order's brand, the one they burn into heretics and, rumor said, into worse things.

We froze in the brush. Wendell pulled Cyrus close, and I felt rather than saw the change in the big carver — the mildness dropping away like a mask slipping, something older and harder underneath.

One rider was talking to the shrine keeper, an old woman who sold candle stubs and luck-charms. I read lips pretty well; it's a survival skill. What he said, I passed along in the smallest whisper I own:

"They're asking about a traveler and a child. Heading north. He's showing her a paper."

The old woman shook her head. Shrugged. The rider pressed, and she pressed back with the magnificent, weaponized deafness of old women everywhere, and eventually the riders mounted up and took the east fork, away from us, and we stood in the brush breathing until our lungs remembered their jobs.

Cyrus hadn't moved the entire time. Hadn't trembled. Had just watched the riders with that deep-water stare, and when they were gone, the child crouched and drew in the dirt, quick and certain:

*Ash. Fire. Song. Run.*

Then, beneath it, after a pause, after looking up at Wendell and then — unexpectedly, terribly — at *me*:

*But not yet.*

"Not yet," Wendell echoed softly. And then the carver looked at me, at the gutter-rat who'd picked their pocket two days ago, and said the words that finished me off for good:

"Can you get us past the Graywater checkpoint unseen?"

I grinned. It felt good to grin. It felt like putting on clothes that fit.

"Friend," I said, "I can get you past the Graywater checkpoint *dressed as the checkpoint.*"

Above us, in Wendell's pack, the bones began — very quietly, very patiently — to sing.
