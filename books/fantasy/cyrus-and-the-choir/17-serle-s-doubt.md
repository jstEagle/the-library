## 17. Serle's Doubt

The inn at Harrow's Ford smelled of tallow and wet wool, and Inquisitor Serle had been sitting in its common room for three hours without touching the ale the innkeeper kept refilling unasked.

He did not drink on duty. He did not eat in front of witnesses. He did not remove his gloves. These were not rules of the Order—there were no written rules against any of them—but they were disciplines, and disciplines were what separated an instrument of the Ashen Order from a man with a sword and opinions. Serle had spent nineteen years becoming an instrument. He resented, mildly and without letting it show, how often people assumed instruments could be bought with ale.

"Another one for you, m'lord?" Odell asked, hovering at the table's edge with the pitcher clutched like a shield.

"I told you I'm finished."

"Course you are. Course." The man poured anyway, sloshing foam over the rim, his hands not steady. "On the house. Can't have the Order thinking Harrow's Ford is inhospitable."

Serle looked up at him then, slowly, the way he'd learned to look—the look that made grown men remember debts they'd forgotten and sins they'd buried. "Your hospitality is noted. Your memory is what interests me."

"M-memory?"

"The travelers who passed through. Four nights ago. A tall carver, gray at the temples, hands like a stonemason's. And a child."

Odell's face performed a small, catastrophic theater of innocence. His eyes went to the ceiling as if the answer might be written there, then to the floor, then briefly—and this was the tell, the one Serle had waited all evening for—to the staircase that led up to the family rooms before snapping back to neutral.

"No travelers," Odell said. "Quiet week. Roads are bad since the rains."

"Since the rains," Serle repeated. He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the scarred wood. "You're lying badly, innkeeper. I want you to understand that I know it, and that I am allowing it. That allowance has a price, and the price goes up every time you spend it."

Behind the bar, half-hidden by a hanging bundle of dried herbs, a girl of perhaps fourteen went very still. She had been washing cups all evening with the mechanical diligence of someone trying to be furniture, but at the word *child* her scrubbing hand had stopped, and now she stood frozen with a wet cup in both hands, watching her father lie to a man in ash-gray wool.

Serle pretended not to see her. This was also a discipline: the pretense of blindness, held just long enough for the watched to forget they were being watched.

* * *

Her name, he learned from the parish roll he'd requisitioned from the village priest—a nervous man who sweated through his vestments and would have confessed to arson if Serle had asked—was Tamsin Odell. Only child. Mother dead eight years, fever. She was upstairs when the riders came at dusk, and she came down when summoned, moving with a strange deliberate calm, as though she had rehearsed this walk in front of a mirror.

She sat where he indicated, across the table from him, spine straight, hands folded. Her father stood behind her chair with his jaw working soundlessly.

"You may go," Serle said to him.

"She's a child—"

"She is fourteen, which makes her old enough to be questioned under Order statute, and young enough that her answers will be honest ones. Children haven't yet learned to lie well. You've demonstrated tonight that you never learned at all." He let that sit. "Go stand by your bar. If you interrupt, I will assume you have something to hide, and I will begin looking for it in your cellar, your ledgers, and your late wife's correspondence. Do you understand?"

Odell understood. He went to his bar and gripped its edge with both hands and stared at nothing.

Tamsin Odell looked at Serle across the candle flame with eyes the color of river water, and said nothing at all.

"You know why I'm here," Serle said.

"Do I, m'lord?"

That was better than her father's performance. Not denial—just a question, flat and careful, giving away nothing. Serle adjusted his assessment upward. The girl was not frightened. Or rather, she was frightened—he could see it in the pulse at her throat, quick as a bird's—but she was frightened *in order*, fear arranged and rationed, spent only where it would be believed. Someone had taught her that. Or she had taught herself, living under a roof with a father who lied this badly and needed covering.

"A vessel escaped us on the coast," Serle said. "Small. Young. Travels with a guardian. They came through the ford four nights ago and slept under this roof. I want to know which road they took when they left."

"And if I don't know—"

"You know."

"How would you know what I know, m'lord?" It was not insolence. Her voice didn't rise at all. It was a genuine question, almost scholarly, and it caught him off guard in a way he found distasteful. "You've been here one day. You've never seen me before. You're asking me to tell you true things about strangers I may or may not have fed, based on your certainty, which I can't check."

Serle studied her. Candlelight moved on her face. Somewhere behind the wall, the fire popped in the hearth, and rain resumed against the shutters, soft as sifted flour.

"You argue like a priest's daughter," he said.

"I argue like an innkeeper's daughter. Priests get believed. We get doubted professionally."

Despite himself—despite nineteen years of discipline—something at the corner of Serle's mouth threatened movement. He killed it. "The child travels with a bone whistle. Carved, old, no maker's mark. Have you seen it?"

"I've seen a lot of whistles. Inn full of drovers and pilgrims, there's always a whistle."

"This one, when played, does something to the air. To the ears. Did anyone complain of ringing? Of hearing voices? Of dreams?"

And there—there it was. Not in her face, which stayed smooth as pond ice, but in her hands. Her folded fingers pressed white against each other for the space of one heartbeat, two, and then eased.

*Yes,* said her hands. *We heard something.*

"You're lying again," Serle said quietly. "But more slowly this time. I appreciate the effort."

"I'm not lying."

"Your hands are."

"My hands are cold. It's a damp season." But she unfolded them deliberately, laid them flat on the table, palms down, as if to pin them still. "Ask me what you actually want to ask me, m'lord. All these little questions—you're circling. My da circles too, when he doesn't want to say a thing straight. It's very tiring to watch."

For a moment Serle simply looked at her, and the room seemed to contract around the candle between them, the shadows leaning in.

"What do you think I want to ask?"

"I think you want to ask whether the little mute child scared us." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes did not leave his. "Whether we heard singing in the night with nobody singing. Whether my da went pale when they left and wouldn't say why. Whether—" and here, for the first time, her composure flickered, a hairline crack running through the pond ice, "—whether I dreamed of a burning building even though I've never seen one burn."

The rain filled the silence. Serle became aware, distantly, of Odell at the bar, rigid, listening.

"Yes," Serle said. "Those are the questions."

"Then yes. To all of them." Tamsin swallowed. "They stayed one night. The carver paid silver, overpaid, wouldn't take change. The child didn't speak—not mute-mute, I don't think, because they talked to each other somehow, with their hands and their faces, whole conversations, faster than talking. The child carved little figures on the tabletop with a fingernail. Spirals. Stacked little people. My da thought it was mischief and nearly scolded them, and then—" She stopped.

"Then."

"Then the fire went out." Her voice dropped. "Not died down. Went *out*. All at once, like a breath blown across a candle, except it was the whole hearth, banked coals and all, and the room went cold enough to see our own breath. And in the dark, for maybe ten heartbeats, everyone in the common room heard—" She faltered, and Serle watched her search for the word and reject several. "Singing isn't right. It was more like... remembering. Like the sound a thing makes when it remembers being sung to. Then the fire came back and the child was looking straight at me across the room, and I have never—" She pressed her lips together. "I have never in my life felt so *seen*, m'lord. And I've been seen plenty. Girls in inns get seen constantly. This was different. It was like being counted."

"Counted," Serle repeated.

"Like a coin in a purse. Like something that belongs to a sum." She looked down at her flattened hands. "I didn't sleep after. And in the morning they were gone before light, and my da burned the table."

"Burned it."

"Scraped it first. The spirals. Scraped them into the fire, every shaving, and stood there while it burned, and crossed himself twice, which he hasn't done since my mother died." She raised her eyes. "So. Which road did they take? Is that what you wanted? Because I'll tell you truly, m'lord: I don't know. But I'll tell you something else truly, and you can do what you like with it."

"Say it."

"They weren't running *from* you." Her brow furrowed, and for a moment she looked much younger, and much older, both at once. "I've seen people run from soldiers. They flinch and scatter and count their spoons after. These two walked through my da's common room like people carrying something heavy toward somewhere. Like pallbearers. You don't hunt pallbearers, m'lord. You get out of their way, or you help."

* * *

He dismissed her at last, and she went up the stairs without hurrying, and Odell followed her with his eyes like a man watching his whole life climb those steps and disappear.

Serle remained at the table long after the serving girl had snuffed most of the candles. He sat with his gloved hands laced and his thoughts arranged in their usual columns, and found that the columns would not stay arranged.

The doctrine was simple. The Order had carried it for a hundred years, worn smooth as a rosary bead from handling: the Sleeper beneath the world was power, and power hoarded by death was theft. The god had been bound by cowards—by spirits who feared what waking it would mean—and the world had limped ever since, magic thinned to trickles, miracles rationed, the strong made to kneel to the weak merely because the weak were many. Wake the god, and the balance righted itself. Wake the god, and the Order's long vigil would be rewarded. Wake the god, and—

And what?

He turned the girl's words over. *Like something that belongs to a sum.* He thought of the bone whistle locked now in the lead-lined case in his saddlebag—the second one, taken from a smuggler at the pass, its twin already burned in his brazier at the monastery's edge. He had burned the first expecting *something*. Doctrine said the vessels of the Choir carried residue; doctrine said fire purified and revealed; doctrine said an Inquisitor of the third rank would feel the release like a struck bell.

He had felt nothing. Smoke, and a smell like a butcher's shop in summer, and silence. And the nothing had frightened him more than any presence could have, because doctrine did not account for nothing. Doctrine always accounted. Doctrine was a ledger, and ledgers balanced, and his had begun, in recent weeks, to show entries he could not explain.

The vessel—a child, a *mute child*—had emptied a hearth with a breath and made a roomful of strangers feel counted. That was power. That was exactly the kind of power the Order existed to seize and turn to purpose. So why, Serle asked himself, staring into the guttering candle, did the girl's account read less like a weapon being tested and more like a blessing passing through?

*Pallbearers,* she had said.

He took out his journal—black oilcloth, unmarked—and wrote by the last inch of candle:

*Harrow's Ford. Vessel confirmed, four nights prior. Witness: female child, fourteen, credible. Reports acoustic anomaly, thermal anomaly, emotional residue described as "being counted." No hostility observed. Guardian described as protective, deliberate, paying over odds. Route unknown. Recommend pursuit via north road, secondary via river path.*

He stopped. The nib hovered. Then, below the report, in smaller script, he added the line he knew he should not add, the line that would cost him if any superior read the journal, the line that was the first visible crack in nineteen years of instrument-perfect service:

*Query for the Archive: when the binding was laid, did the Choir sing against the god's will, or with its consent? The distinction may matter. The texts are unclear. The texts may have been made unclear.*

He blotted it, closed the book, and sat in the dark listening to the rain.

Through the wall, faintly, he heard the innkeeper's daughter moving above—floorboards, a chair, the small sounds of someone keeping watch over a sleeping father. And beneath that, or beneath his own thoughts, or nowhere at all, he thought he heard something else: a low sustained tone, like a finger run around the rim of a glass, coming from no direction, lasting three heartbeats, gone.

His hand went to the lead case at his belt. Inside, the confiscated whistle lay cold and silent.

It had not been silent, he realized, when he touched the case. For those same three heartbeats, it had been warm.

Serle rose, woke his riders, and gave the order he had been avoiding all evening: they would ride at first light—not north, as his report recommended, but east, along the river path, toward the ruins of the coastal monastery. Toward the ashes. Because the girl had dreamed of a burning building she'd never seen, and Serle had learned long ago that when the impossible spoke, it was best to go and stand where it had spoken from.

He did not write down the real reason. The real reason he kept where he kept all the dangerous things now: in the growing ledger of entries that did not balance.

*If the god wakes grieving,* he wrote finally, and then struck it through, hard, until the paper tore.

Outside, the rain went on falling on Harrow's Ford, patient as a choir counting rests.
