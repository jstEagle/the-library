## 18. The Monastery's Ashes

The sea smelled wrong before Cyrus saw the monastery.

It was the same salt wind that had come through the shutters every morning of their remembered life, the same gulls screaming their three stupid syllables over the cliffs, but underneath it now lay something scorched and sweet, like meat left too long on a hearthstone, and Cyrus's legs stopped working at the edge of the road as if the smell itself had put out a hand.

Wendell stopped a few paces ahead and turned back. The bone-carver did not say *you don't have to*. Wendell had learned that much about Cyrus in the weeks since the valley: that pity made them smaller, that patience made them taller, that silence shared was worth more than any comfort spoken aloud. Instead Wendell simply stood there in the wind with the pack of whispering bones on one shoulder and waited, the way you wait for weather to decide.

Bramble, behind them both, did not wait for anything. "That's it? That's the great fortress of song?" The thief shaded their eyes and squinted at the ruin below. "Looks like someone dropped a cathedral off a cliff and then set fire to the pieces."

Someone had done nearly exactly that.

* * *

Cyrus had seen the monastery burning from the inside of a dream, or what they had always assumed was a dream—the memory arrived that way even awake, heat pressing against the eyelids, Brother Anselm's voice cracking on the high note while smoke poured under the refectory door like a black tide coming in. They had thought for a long time that the fire had eaten everything. That was what fire did. That was what fire was *for*, when men brought it to a house of singing.

But standing now on the cliff path with the ruin spread out below—a hip of broken tower, a spine of collapsed cloister, the chapel's ribs still standing against the sky like something that had died kneeling—Cyrus understood that ash remembers. Ash held shapes. And beneath the ash, apparently, the ground could hold a person.

They knew this because of what the bones were saying.

Since the night by the inn's fire when Cyrus had hummed and every carved thing in Wendell's pack had rung like struck glass, the dead had not been quiet again. They traveled with them now, a low tide of murmurs in the flute and the whistle and the little spiraled amulet Wendell wore at the throat, voices layered over voices, and most of the time Cyrus could not pick one thread from another. But here, on this road, above these cliffs, the murmur had changed. It had become directional. It pulled.

*Down,* said the bones, in the wordless way bones said things, the way cold said *north*. *Down, down, under.*

* * *

They went the last mile single file along the goat track, Bramble first because Bramble insisted on being first anywhere dangerous, Wendell last so no one could come up behind. Cyrus walked in the middle with the bone whistle pressed to their lips—not blowing, just holding it there, letting the old smoothness of it rest against their teeth the way a child holds a thumb. The monks had given it to them in a cellar with fire overhead, a hand closing small fingers around small bone: *when you cannot speak, let this speak. When you are afraid, remember the Choir is not.* They could still feel the shape of that hand. They had spent years trying to forget its warmth and failing.

The gatehouse was gone. The gate posts remained, charred stumps, and tied to one of them—this detail Cyrus's eye snagged on and could not leave, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth—hung a strip of gray cloth, rain-rotted, order-marked. The Ashen Order had come here and stood here and tied their rag here like hunters nailing a pelt to a tree.

Bramble touched the cloth, rubbed it between finger and thumb, and spat. "Months old," they said. "Maybe more. Nobody's been back."

*Somebody has,* the bones murmured. *Under. Under.*

The courtyard had gone to weeds and gull droppings. Cyrus walked it the way you walk the floor of a house you grew up in after it has been robbed: knowing where everything should be, finding nothing where anything should be. There the well where Brother Fennic had dared them to look down (they never had; they had been afraid of their own echo). There the bench where Sister Maren—no, not Maren, that name belonged to another life now, to a gray-eyed woman on a mountain who carried a hoe like a spear—there the bench where Sister Odile had taught letters, tracing them in spilled grain. There the doorway of the dormitory where twenty children used to breathe in the dark in unison, twenty small chests rising and falling, and the sound of it, the monks said, was the Choir practicing.

Twenty chests. Cyrus counted the stones where the beds had been, out of an old habit of counting, out of the newer habit of counting what was lost, and got to eleven before their vision blurred and they stopped.

Wendell crouched beside them without a word and set a hand flat on the ground between them, palm down, the way Wendell listened to graves. A long moment passed. Wind moved the weeds. Somewhere in the fallen tower, loose masonry ticked and settled, ticking like cooling iron, like a clock counting nothing.

Then Wendell looked up sharply. Looked at the ground. Looked at Cyrus. Tapped the earth twice with two fingers, then pointed—down.

*I know,* Cyrus carved quickly into the dirt with one finger. *It says down. It has been saying down since the road.*

"It isn't only the bones," Wendell said quietly, and their voice had that careful flatness they used when they were frightened and refusing to perform it. "The ground is warm here. In one place. Feel."

Cyrus felt. Between two flagstones near the ruined well, where the weeds grew thickest and greenest out of season, the stone held a faint stubborn warmth, like a body recently risen from a bed. And threaded through the warmth, so faint Cyrus might have imagined it—but did not think they were imagining it, had not imagined anything since the night of the humming—came a rhythm. Slow. Patient. Deliberate.

A knock. Pause. Three knocks. Pause. One knock.

Then, after a breath long enough to hold doubt in: the pattern again.

* * *

The cellar door had been hidden under the grain room's collapse, which was perhaps why whoever hid it had chosen it, and why whoever burned the place had missed it. It took all three of them and half an hour of hauling broken beams, Bramble swearing creatively in two languages, before the hatch came free with a groan and a belch of cold air that smelled of earth, of tallow, of long patience.

And of a person. Living. Unwashed. Alive.

Cyrus went down first. Of course they went down first; Wendell grabbed at their shoulder and missed, and Bramble said something unrepeatable about children and death wishes, and Cyrus was already on the ladder, already descending into the dark on legs that shook and did not care.

The cellar was a low brick vault stacked with empty shelves, a broken churn, jars of preserves gone gray with age—and in the far corner, wrapped in every blanket and vestment the cellar had contained, a shape sat propped against the wall with a hammer in its hand.

The hammer rose. The knock had been going on for months, Cyrus realized. This person had been knocking on the underside of the world for months, keeping time, keeping faith, keeping sane, sending the only message a buried mute man could send: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

Light from the hatch reached him in a pale wedge. He flinched from it, raised a forearm, and then—as the light found his face, hollowed and bearded and crusted with the whole geography of his imprisonment—his arm dropped.

He stared at Cyrus.

Cyrus stared back.

Brother Ilex. Old, gentle Ilex, who had taught them to tune the great bells by feeling the air change against the skin of your cheek, who had once spent an entire winter building a wooden bird that flapped its wings when you hummed at it, because he believed every silence deserved a machine that answered it. He was thinner than memory, grayer, one ear crusted with a healed wound. But it was him. It was him, alive, in the dark, with a hammer in his fist and tears cutting clean lines through the grime of his face.

He made a sound. Not a word—he was Ilex, and Ilex had been born with his voice locked behind his teeth just as Cyrus had been born with theirs—but a sound, a cracked keening, half laugh and half sob, and he dropped the hammer and opened his arms.

Cyrus crossed the cellar in four steps and collided with him, and the bones in Wendell's pack upstairs began, faintly, audibly, to sing.

* * *

Later—they built a fire in the courtyard, because Ilex pointed at the sky and mimed terror of the cellar, of ever being under stone again, and none of them could blame him—they fed him broth from Wendell's stores and watched him eat with the terrible efficiency of the starving, and then Wendell laid out the tools of communication on a flat stone: charcoal stubs, a knife, a piece of seasoned birch bark sanded smooth.

Ilex took the charcoal. His hands trembled so badly the first strokes shattered, and he closed his eyes and breathed, the way the brothers had taught Cyrus to breathe, four counts in, seven counts out, until the shaking slowed. Then he wrote.

His script was beautiful even now. Monks' hands remembered.

**HOW MANY CHILDREN OUT. HOW MANY ANCHORS FREE.**

The question landed like cold water. Cyrus took the charcoal when Ilex offered it and wrote: **One. Me.**

Ilex read it. Read it again. His face did something complicated—grief moving through relief moving through grief, weather crossing a field—and then he wrote fast, urgent, pressing hard enough to snap the charcoal twice.

**NOT TRUE. LISTEN. THE ORDER TOOK THE OTHERS ALIVE.**

Bramble leaned in. "Took them—" the thief began, and stopped, working it through, and Cyrus watched the same arithmetic happen on Bramble's face that was happening in their own chest: anchors taken alive meant anchors still breathing, meant the list in the dead rider's papers—the list Wendell had found at the ford, names crossed out one by one, *Cyrus* the last name unstruck—meant the crossings-out were not deaths.

Or not all of them.

Wendell had gone very still. "The papers," Wendell said slowly. "We assumed crossed-out meant dead. Serle's riders burn what they can't carry." The bone-carver's jaw worked. "But if they wanted vessels alive—"

Ilex was writing again, jabbing the charcoal now, his neat script deforming with force:

**THEY WANT THE CHOIR BROKEN FROM INSIDE. A SLEEPING GOD WAKES IF ITS ANCHORS DIE. BUT IT RISES WHOLE ONLY IF THEY ARE MADE TO SING AGAINST THEIR WILL. THAT IS WHAT THE ORDER BELIEVES. THEY HAVE BEEN TRYING FOR A CENTURY. THEY KEEP FAILING. SO NOW THEY COLLECT.**

Cyrus read it three times. Around them the evening settled, gulls going quiet, the fire popping, and somewhere under everything the dead in the pack murmured agreement in their layered voices, a congregation assenting to a reading of scripture.

*Hope with teeth,* Wendell had called it once, on the road, talking about something else entirely. Here it was. Hope with teeth: the other anchors lived. The other anchors were prisoners. The difference between those two sentences was the width of a knife blade, and the Order held the handle.

Cyrus took the birch bark. Their hand was steadier than it had been in weeks. Something in them had hardened overnight at the ford, when blood was washed from a carving knife, and it hardened further now.

**WHERE DID THEY TAKE THEM.**

Ilex looked at the question a long moment. Then he drew.

He was a teacher; he drew the way he taught, simply and exactly. A coastline. A river mouth. A road running inland through hills drawn as patient humps. And at the end of the road, a shape Cyrus recognized from the dead rider's page, from the papers at the ford, from nightmares they hadn't known were memories:

A spire. Hollow at the crown. Drawn with a circle inside it, and inside the circle, a spiral.

**THE HOLLOW SPIRE,** Ilex wrote beneath it. **THE CHOIR'S FIRST HOME. THE ORDER HAS THE ANCHORS THERE. AND THE SOLSTICE—**

He stopped. His charcoal hovered. His face, firelit, aged ten years in a breath.

**THE SOLSTICE IS NOT WHEN WE ALWAYS TAUGHT.** He underlined it twice. **BROTHER ANSELM KEPT THE OLD CALENDAR AND HE WAS WRONG. WRONG BY FIVE WEEKS. THE TRUE SOLSTICE IS ALMOST UPON US. DAYS. THREE. PERHAPS FOUR.**

The fire cracked. A log shifted, collapsing inward, throwing sparks up toward a sky going purple at the edges.

Three days.

Not forty. All the road behind them, every mile bought with bribes and blood and Bramble's thievery and Odell's lie and a farmer's grave and a magistrate's corruption—all of it, and the deadline had been running out under their feet the entire time like water under ice.

Bramble said a word in their street tongue that needed no translation.

Wendell stood up. The bone-carver looked east, toward hills none of them could see in the failing light, toward a hollow spire full of prisoners and a god's sleep wearing thin, and when Wendell spoke, their voice was the voice Cyrus had heard only twice before: once at the ford, once at the ridge. Quiet, level, and absolutely without mercy in it.

"Then we stop sleeping tonight," Wendell said. "All of us. We ride for the Spire, and we do not stop."

Ilex caught Cyrus's sleeve. Turned the birch bark over. Wrote one line more, small, in the corner, where only Cyrus would look:

**YOU CAME BACK. THEN IT IS NOT TOO LATE. NOTHING IS TOO LATE WHILE ONE VOICE STILL CHOOSES.**

Cyrus closed their hand around the words until the charcoal dust bit into their palm, and looked east into the gathering dark, and began, silently, to count.
