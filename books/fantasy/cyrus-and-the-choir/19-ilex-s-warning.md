## 19. Ilex's Warning

The cellar still smelled of smoke.

Not the fresh smoke of a burning—eight years had scrubbed most of that from the monastery stones—but the old smoke, the kind that lives in mortar and memory, that rises again whenever rain finds a crack in the roof. Brother Ilex had lived in it so long he no longer noticed it, the way a man stops noticing his own heartbeat. He noticed it now because the child was sitting in it, cross-legged on the floor where Ilex had slept for nearly a decade, and the child's presence made everything sharp again: the damp stone, the tallow stub guttering in its dish, the smell of his own unwashed robe.

Cyrus had found him the way one finds a stone in a shoe—suddenly, painfully, undeniably. One moment the cellar had been Ilex's whole world, its shelves of salvaged books and jars of lamp oil and the narrow cot beneath the last intact arch; the next, there were footsteps overhead that were not rats, and then Wendell Dunmore's weathered face at the broken stair, and then Cyrus, small and silent and staring at him with eyes like wells.

Ilex could not speak. Had not spoken since the night the Ashen Order came to Saint Vessa's-on-the-Sea with torches and hammers and a list of names. He had been in the scriptorium when they breached the gate. He had heard the singing stop. He had pressed himself into the cold crypt below the chapel while fire walked across the ceiling above him, and somewhere in that long black night something in his throat had closed like a door, and it had never opened again. The healers called it nothing. The brothers who survived—and there were only three, scattered to the winds like ash—called it what it was: the voice dies with the song.

So he wrote. He had always written; it was his office, his vow, his joy. Copyist, annotator, keeper of marginalia. And now, with charcoal stubs and the backs of shelf-boards and, when he was lucky, real paper hoarded like a miser's gold, he wrote everything he could not say.

He wrote now, slowly, because his hands shook and because some things deserved care:

*You know what this is.* He held up the whistle—the twin of the one Cyrus clutched, the one Ilex had kept hidden in a hollowed psalter for eight years, through searches and hunger and the long gray years of hiding in the ruins of the place that had made him. *Brother Anselm carved it. He carved seven.*

Cyrus touched their own whistle, hanging at their throat, and nodded once.

*I was there when he carved them,* Ilex wrote. *I ground the bone. You were too young to remember. You cried when he took the femur from the reliquary. Do you remember?*

The child's face did something complicated—grief and recognition tangled together—and shook their head. Then, after a moment, nodded. Then pressed both hands flat against their chest as if holding something in.

Wendell stood by the stair, arms crossed, watching them both with the wariness of a man who has spent his life among whispers and has just been handed two of them at once. "Ask it," Wendell said. "Whatever you've been keeping behind your teeth all these years, monk. Ask it now, while we're alive to hear the answer."

Ilex looked at the charcoal. At the board. At the two faces waiting.

Then he wrote the thing he had sworn, in the ashes of his order, never to write unless the moment demanded it absolutely.

*The binding does not need a voice. It needs a willing one.*

* * *

Wendell read it twice. Ilex watched the words land—not all at once, but in stages, like stones dropped into deep water. First confusion. Then a frown. Then something colder moving behind the carver's eyes, the look of a person rearranging everything they believed about a room they thought they knew.

"That can't be right," Wendell said. "The dead told us—a living voice renews the binding each century. A voice. They were very clear."

Ilex wiped the board with his sleeve and wrote again, pressing harder:

*A voice, yes. But not any voice. Not a taken one. Not a forced one. The Choir sang the god to sleep out of mercy. Mercy cannot be compelled. That is the whole secret, carver. That is why the Order has failed for a hundred years.*

"The Order has succeeded," Wendell said flatly. "They burned your monastery. They've killed—"

*They have burned buildings,* Ilex wrote. *They have killed keepers. They have collected anchors like a miser collects coins. And every century, when the binding frays, they have brought their captives to the Spire and made them sing—or tried to—and every century, the god has only stirred and suffered and sunk back into worse dreams. Because a song sung under threat is not mercy. It is cruelty wearing mercy's clothes, and the god knows the difference. It has always known the difference.*

He paused, charcoal hovering, and then added, smaller:

*You cannot force someone to forgive you. You cannot force someone to mourn with you. The god is grief, carver. Grief given godhood. And grief is the one door in the world that only opens from the inside.*

The tallow flame bent in a draft from the broken stair. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the ruined cloister, through the ribs of the burned refectory, and made a low moaning note that none of them remarked on and all of them heard.

Cyrus had gone very still. Ilex knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a person listening to something far away—or something very close, inside the bones.

"What do you mean, they've tried?" Wendell asked. "Tried to make anchors sing?"

Ilex hesitated. But the moment had demanded absolute truth, and half-truths were how the Order had won everything so far.

*There was another anchor before you,* he wrote, looking at Cyrus. *A woman named Sefa. Taken from a fishing village in the north, forty years ago. I have read the Order's own records—I copied them, before I understood what I was copying. They kept her in a cell beneath their chapter house for eleven years. They starved her. They threatened her. In the end they dragged her to the Spire on the solstice and put her before the altar and told her they would burn a village full of children unless she sang.*

His hand stopped. The charcoal trembled against the board.

*She sang,* he wrote. *Every note perfect. Every note a lie. And the binding did not renew. The god woke halfway—only halfway, thank every mercy—and screamed, and the scream killed three hundred of the Order's own faithful, and Sefa died too, and the god sank back down into a dream darker than the one before. And the Grand Inquisitor of that age wrote in the margin of the report, in his own hand, these words:* 'It must be made to want.' *He thought the wanting was the god's. He never understood it was hers.*

Silence filled the cellar like water filling a lung.

"They've known," Wendell said at last, very quietly. "For a hundred years, they've known it doesn't work, and they've kept doing it anyway."

*Because the alternative is unthinkable to them,* Ilex wrote. *If forcing fails, then the binding depends on something they cannot seize, cannot buy, cannot burn. It depends on a free soul choosing to give itself. And men who build their power on seizure would rather break the world than admit such a thing exists.*

* * *

Cyrus moved.

It was a small movement—just the child reaching for the charcoal—but Ilex felt it the way one feels a change in weather. Cyrus took a fresh board, and did not write in the old language, the spirals and stacked figures that predated kingdoms. Cyrus wrote in plain trade-tongue letters, clumsy, unpracticed, learned no one knew where:

*the monks sang because they wanted to*

Ilex's throat ached around the silence in it. *Yes,* he wrote back.

*brother anselm sang even when the order came. i remember now. he put me in the crypt and he went up and he sang while they burned it. they could not make him stop. they could not make him afraid enough.*

*No,* Ilex wrote. *They could not. That is why the monastery lasted nine hundred years. Not our walls. Our willingness. Every century, when the time came, someone chose. Anselm chose, and his teacher chose before him, and back and back to the first voices. The Order thinks the song is a lock and the singer is a key. They are wrong. The song is a door, and the singer is the one who opens it—from the inside, freely, or not at all.*

He looked at the child a long moment. Then he wrote the rest, because the rest needed saying and there was no one else alive who could say it:

*Cyrus. Listen to me. The Order will try to make you sing. They will hurt you, and frighten you, and they may hurt others in front of you to bend you. Understand what I am telling you: if you sing under that compulsion, it will fail, and they will learn nothing except that they must try harder, and the god will suffer for it. Your silence is not weakness. Your silence is the strongest thing the Order fears. As long as you do not choose to sing, they have nothing. Nothing.*

Cyrus stared at the words. Then looked up at Ilex, and there was a question in the well-deep eyes that the child could not carve fast enough, and Ilex answered it before it was asked, writing steadily, not letting himself think about what the words meant for himself:

*And yes. If the day comes when you must sing—if the choice comes to you, freely, at the Spire, with the binding fraying and the god stirring—then it will work. A willing voice can renew the binding alone. That is what the walls say. That is what Anselm believed. One voice, wholly given, is worth more than a thousand compelled ones.*

*But hear the cost too. The old texts are clear. The voice that renews the binding joins the Choir. Wholly. The singer does not walk back up the mountain.*

* * *

Wendell made a sound—small, involuntary, quickly swallowed. Ilex did not look at the carver. Some truths were easier to say to a child than to an adult who had already begun, visibly, terribly, to do arithmetic.

Instead Ilex watched Cyrus.

The child read the last lines twice. Three times. A finger traced under *joins the Choir*, slow, the way one traces a scar. And then Cyrus did the thing that Ilex would remember for the rest of his life, however many years the Order left him: the child set down the charcoal, took the bone whistle from around their neck, and held it out—not to Wendell, not to the dark, but to Ilex, in both palms, the way one returns something borrowed.

And shook their head.

Not refusal. Ilex understood that, in the space of a breath. Not *no, I won't*. Something else. The head-shake said: *not yet. This isn't the day. This isn't the choice. When my choice comes, it will be mine, and I will make it myself, and no one—not the Order, not the prophecy, not the kind monk in the cellar—will make it for me.*

"Child," Wendell said roughly, "you don't have to—"

Cyrus picked the charcoal back up and wrote, fast, the letters jagged:

*i already chose once. in the fire. brother anselm put the whistle in my hands and i chose to live. that was my choice. it kept me silent eight years. when the next choice comes i will make it the same way. myself.*

The tallow guttered. In the draft from the stair, the flame leaned toward the child like a listener.

Ilex sat back on his cot and felt eight years of silence settle over him—not as a wound tonight, but as a vow. He had thought his muteness was the fire's last cruelty. Now he saw it might be the fire's last gift: a keeper who could not be made to speak, guarding the one truth that mattered, until the one who needed it came down the broken stair.

He reached for the board one more time. There was one thing left, and it could not wait, because the road outside the ruin was not empty—he had seen the dust of riders on the pass two days ago, and the Order did not send riders to places without reason.

*One more thing, and then you must go, all of you, tonight. The calendar. Anselm's tables say the solstice falls forty days from the burning of Saint Vessa's. We counted from the wrong fire. The Order counts from theirs. Their records name the true date—and by their reckoning, the binding frays in three days.*

He turned the board toward them and let them read it, and watched the color leave Wendell Dunmore's face, and understood that the quiet life in the null valley, the deliberate smallness, the forty days of road they thought they had—all of it had just burned away like the roof of the scriptorium, and what remained was the same thing that had always remained, in cellars and in ruins and in the hearts of the unwilling-to-be-broken:

Three days. One child. A choice that no army on earth could make for them.

Above them, faint through the broken arch, the wind in the cloister changed pitch—and for a moment, just a moment, it sounded less like wind and more like breathing.
