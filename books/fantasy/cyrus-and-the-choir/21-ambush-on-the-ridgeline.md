## 21. Ambush on the Ridgeline

The ridge had a name on no map that mattered. The drovers called it the Shepherd's Spine—a long gray backbone of stone running east toward the Hollow Spire road, bare of trees, bare of cover, bare of anything except wind and the low whistling grass that grew in the cracks like the mountain's own thin hair.

Wendell hated it before they ever set foot on it.

Not because of the exposure, though that was reason enough. Not because of the wind, which came off the high snowfields with a knife's edge to it and made every step a negotiation. Wendell hated it because of what the bones said.

They had learned, over the weeks since leaving the valley, to read the dead the way sailors read weather. A murmur meant unease. A whisper meant danger near. But for two days now—ever since they'd passed the burned waystation with its roof caved in like a skull—the bones had not whispered at all.

They had been screaming.

Not aloud. Never aloud, except through carved bone, and Wendell carried enough carved bone to make a graveyard jealous: the flute against their sternum, three finished whistles in the left coat pocket, the half-carved raven in the right, the bundle of grave-gifts wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the pack. All of it singing at once now, all of it shrieking in that voiceless way the dead had, so that Wendell's teeth ached with it and their thoughts came out jagged.

"They've been saying the same thing since dawn," Wendell said, for the hundredth time. "Same word. Over and over."

Cyrus walked beside them, small hands jammed in the pockets of the coat Wendell had bought them in the City of Scales, too big in the shoulders, sleeves rolled four times. The child looked up. *What word.*

Wendell didn't want to say it. Saying it gave it weight, and weight was the last thing they needed on an exposed ridge with a drop on one side and open sky on the other.

"*Below,*" Wendell said anyway. "They keep saying *below*. Below, below, below. Like a bell that won't stop ringing."

Bramble, ranging ahead as always—thief's habit, walking where the walking was easiest and the exits were nearest—turned and walked backward a few steps, grinning into the wind. Their hair stood out in all directions like a startled animal. "Below is the ground, bone-man. The ground is famously where the ground is."

"Not the ground." Wendell touched the flute through their coat. "The dead don't mean directions the way we do. When they say below, they mean—" They searched for it. "Underneath things. Underneath the surface of things. Underneath *us*."

Ilex, bringing up the rear with his slow monk's stride and his silence that matched Cyrus's like two halves of a broken bowl, signed something quick. Cyrus watched, then translated in carvings later—but just then the child simply stopped walking and pointed.

Down the slope behind them, back along the way they'd come, a hawk was climbing the air in a slow spiral. Except hawks didn't fly in weather like this, and hawks didn't hold a spiral that perfect, that patient, that *deliberate*, circling the exact point where four travelers crawled along a spine of stone like ants on a wire.

"That's been following us since the ford," Wendell said quietly.

"I know," Bramble said, and the grin was gone. "I've been counting its circles. It doesn't hunt. It *reports*."

The wind gusted. The grass hissed. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the bones screamed *below, below, below*—

—and then, all at once, they stopped.

The silence hit Wendell harder than any scream ever had. Two months of constant murmuring, a lifetime of whispers, and now nothing—not quiet, but *absence*, a hole in the world shaped exactly like every voice they'd ever known. Wendell staggered, actually staggered, one hand going out to the cold rock.

"Wendell?" Cyrus's eyes were wide.

"They're gone," Wendell breathed. "All of them. Every—" And then they understood, and the understanding arrived like ice water poured down the spine. "No. Not gone. *Listening.*"

The dead weren't silent because they'd fled.

They were silent because something had arrived that demanded every scrap of their attention.

* * *

"Off the ridge," Wendell said. "Now. Down the north face, all of us, right now—"

Bramble was already moving—they'd learned, somewhere between the tollhouse and the forgiveness, that when the bone-carver's voice went flat like that, arguing cost more than obedience. Cyrus grabbed Ilex's sleeve. They made it perhaps forty paces toward the slope's break-line, where the stone stepped down into scree and the scree into scrub, before the world ended politely, from above.

The first bolt took the rock beside Wendell's head and burst it into shrapnel. The second struck Ilex in the shoulder and spun him around, and he went down without a sound, which was somehow worse than screaming, because Ilex had spent his whole life being silent about pain and this was only more of the same.

"DOWN!" Wendell was already dragging Cyrus behind a shelf of granite, folding the child under their body the way they'd once folded kindling under an arm. Crossbow bolts sang overhead, three, five, eight of them, a rain of them, far too many for a patrol. This wasn't riders who'd stumbled onto them.

This was a trap that had been waiting, patient as the hawk, for them to walk the only walkable line across the Shepherd's Spine.

"Bramble!" Wendell shouted into the wind.

"HERE!" From somewhere to the left, behind a rib of stone. "Six—I count six on the south rocks, and there'll be more below, they always put more below—"

*Below.* The word went through Wendell like a bolt of its own.

And then the riders came up over the southern crest, and there was no more counting to do, because it didn't matter. Gray cloaks. Ash-gray, ash-trimmed, moving with the unhurried discipline of men who had done this a hundred times and never once doubted the outcome. And at their center, riding a black horse up a slope no sensible horse would climb, sat Inquisitor Serle, his pale eyes fixed on them across two hundred feet of open stone with the calm of a man reading a ledger he had already balanced.

He didn't shout. He didn't need to. His riders spread out along the crest in a line, crossbows leveled, and Serle simply raised one gloved hand, palm out, and the whole ridge held its breath.

"Cyrus," Wendell whispered. The child was pressed against them, shaking, small fingers digging into Wendell's coat. "Listen to me. Whatever happens—"

But even as they said it, Wendell knew how it sounded. *Whatever happens.* As if there were versions of this that ended well. As if six crossbows and an inquisitor on an open ridge left room for whatever.

Cyrus looked up at them. And in the child's eyes Wendell saw something that broke their heart clean through: not terror. *Apology.* As if being hunted were the child's fault. As if existing were a debt Cyrus had been trying to repay their whole short life.

"No," Wendell said fiercely, gripping the small shoulders. "You look at me. You are not a burden. You are not a curse. You are the best thing that ever knocked on my door, and I will carve that on my own gravestone if it comes to that—"

Serle's hand dropped.

The world became noise.

* * *

Later, Wendell would try to assemble the battle from its pieces and find that the pieces refused to fit. Memory did that with horror—it kept only fragments, bright and wrong, like bone shards after a bad break.

A fragment: Bramble rising from cover with a stolen crossbow and firing wildly, gloriously, uselessly, screaming curses in three languages, two of which Wendell was fairly sure they'd invented.

A fragment: Ilex, wounded, dragging himself upright and stepping—*stepping*, deliberately, into the open, arms wide, placing his mute body between the crossbows and the children, and the terrible arithmetic of what that gesture meant.

A fragment: Wendell's own hands, doing the only thing they knew how to do, pulling the finished whistles from their pocket and blowing all three at once, a sound like nothing human, and watching two horses scream and rear and throw their riders as the dead poured through the carved channels and into the open air—

Because that was the thing about the valley's edge, about the wide world beyond it: magic lived out here, and the dead could *act*. For two months Wendell had felt them swelling day by day, voices growing louder as the null valley's silence fell away behind them. Now Wendell opened every door they had and let the graveyard out.

The whistle-song hit the riders like a wave. Men clutched their ears. One dropped his crossbow and began weeping without knowing why. The dead did not kill—dead things rarely did—but they *flooded*, they filled the ridge with grief and memory and the weight of every name ever carved, and for ten full seconds the Ashen Order forgot how to be an order.

Ten seconds was almost enough.

It should have been enough.

In those ten seconds, Wendell got Cyrus to the break-line. Got the child over the first lip of scree. Saw the scrub below, the gully, the possible escape threading downhill like a promise. Shouted for Bramble, saw the thief vaulting down the rocks behind them, alive, swearing, magnificent.

And then turned back for Ilex.

That was the mistake. That was the moment Wendell would carry forever, the pivot on which everything afterward turned: they turned back, because you did not leave people behind, because Maren had taught them that, because the whole architecture of their life was built on the refusal to leave anyone behind—

—and Serle had counted on exactly that.

The Inquisitor hadn't ridden at the travelers. He'd ridden at a flanking angle, patient, letting the whistle-song break his men while he himself held back from the flood like a man wading at the edge of cold water. And when Wendell turned back up the slope, Serle was already there, already past the dead's reach—or armored against it, or simply too hollow to fill—and his gauntleted hand closed on Cyrus's collar as the child scrambled back up the scree after Wendell, after Ilex, after family.

"No—" Wendell's voice cracked apart on the word.

Serle looked at Wendell across fifteen feet of broken stone. Up close, his face was not cruel. That was the worst of it. It was the face of a man doing sums, and the sums had come out, and he believed in the sums.

"The vessel comes with me," Serle said. His voice was quiet, pitched under the wind, meant for Wendell alone. "The rest of you were never the errand."

"You'll have to take the child over my corpse."

"Yes," Serle said, without heat, without malice, with the terrible mildness of absolute certainty. "If it comes to that. I'd rather it didn't. I've seen enough burning."

He whistled—one sharp note—and the line of gray cloaks advanced down the slope, crossbows reloaded, the dead's flood breaking around them now as they closed ranks shoulder to shoulder, faith armoring them where steel couldn't.

Ilex moved. Wounded, bleeding, silent Ilex planted himself in Serle's path with his arms spread and his ruined shoulder held high, and for one heartbeat the Inquisitor actually hesitated—

—and a crossbow bolt took Ilex through the chest from twenty feet, and the old monk folded down into the scree like a coat slipping off a hook, and Wendell screamed a word they didn't know they knew, a word in the language older than kingdoms, a word the dead had taught them without their noticing, and every loose bone in their coat answered it.

The flute shrieked. The whistles shrieked. The grave-gifts in the pack, the half-carved raven, the beads and tokens and offerings of two months' road—all of it woke at once, and the ridge filled with the dead's true voice at last, not grief now but *rage*, a chorus of the murdered and the mourned rising out of the stone itself, and two more riders went off their horses, and Serle flinched for the first time, actually flinched—

—and Cyrus, tearing free half-turned in his grip, looked back at Wendell with those enormous eyes and did the last thing Wendell expected.

The child smiled. Small, fierce, certain. Then raised one small hand and carved a shape in the air—their language, the old language, drawn with a finger against the wind—and Wendell, who had learned three words and then eleven and then thirty, read it as clearly as if it were cut in oak:

*Go. Live. Tell.*

Then Serle wrenched the child up onto his horse, wheeled, and rode north along the spine with four riders falling in around him, and the remaining two stayed to finish the work, and a crossbow bolt caught Wendell high in the side, and the world tilted, and the stones came up fast and hard.

* * *

Pain arrived late, the way it does at the end of terrible days, polite as a creditor.

Wendell lay on the scree with the sky wheeling overhead, gray clouds shredding on the peaks, and took inventory the way they took inventory of bone: what remained, what was broken, what could still be worked. Ribs—cracked, maybe, on the left. Side—wet, warm, bad. Legs—working, barely. Hands—hands were fine. Hands were always fine. Hands were the one part of themselves Wendell had never doubted.

"Bramble." It came out as a croak. "Bramble—"

"Here. Here, I'm here, don't—stop moving, you idiot, stop—" Bramble's face swam into view, streaked with dust and something else, blood not their own, eyes huge and wet and furious. "Ilex is—I couldn't—he wouldn't let me—" The thief's voice shattered. Bramble, who joked through crossfire and grinned at inquisitors, sat down hard in the scree and put their face in their hands and shook.

Wendell reached out and found Ilex instead. The old monk was still warm. Still silent. Even at the end, silent—his mouth slightly open as if the words had finally, finally gotten free and gone on ahead. Wendell closed his eyes with two fingers, the way you'd close a book you weren't finished with but had to put down.

The dead, when Wendell listened, were keening. Not words anymore. Just grief, vast and formless, pouring through the flute against their sternum like water through a broken weir. Somewhere in it, Wendell thought, was Ilex already—already among them, already learning the shape of his new country. *Wait for us,* Wendell told him silently. *We're coming. We're coming, and we're bringing her home.*

Her. Cyrus. Gone. Taken north along the spine while Wendell bled into the mountain.

"We have to move," Wendell said, and tried to sit up, and the world went white and sideways, and Bramble caught them.

"You can't even—look at yourself—"

"There's no time." Wendell gripped the thief's wrist with both hands, and found, to their surprise, that the grip was strong. Grief did that. Grief was a fuel like any other. "They'll ride for the Spire road. Solstice is close—Ilex said—Bramble, listen to me. Listen." Wendell pulled the thief's face close, forehead nearly touching forehead, and spoke the plainest truth they had. "Everyone I love is either dead on this hillside or tied to an Ashen Order saddle. I am getting up. You can help me or watch me fall down, but I am getting up."

Bramble stared at them. Then laughed—a broken, sobbing, utterly Bramble sound—and hauled Wendell's arm across their shoulders.

"North," Bramble said. "The Spire road. And Wendell?"

"What."

"When we catch them—and we will catch them—I get five minutes with the one who shot Ilex."

"No," Wendell said, as the two of them limped together down the north face, away from the body they couldn't carry and toward the war they couldn't avoid. Behind them, the dead of the ridge followed in their own fashion, a cold wind at their backs, keening still. "You get as long as you need."

Far ahead, small against the gray sky, the hawk was climbing again—circling, patient, reporting—and below it, on the distant northern road, a column of gray cloaks moved with a captive slung across a black horse, carrying the last voice of the Choir toward the place where the god slept.

Wendell put one foot in front of the other, and bled, and followed.
