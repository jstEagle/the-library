## 22. The Valley Calls Me Home

The first thing I understood was that the sky was wrong.

It was the wrong color for a ridgeline at any hour I knew — not the iron gray of storm, not the honeyed rot of dusk, but a pale washed nothing, like cloth boiled too many times. It moved above me in slow rags. Trees went by. No — the trees were still and I was moving, which was worse, because my body had decided things without consulting me.

Then the pain arrived, all at once, like a debt collector kicking in a door.

I screamed. I know this because my throat tore doing it, and because somewhere above me a voice said, "He's awake," and another voice said, "Good. Awake men fight the fever better," and neither voice sounded particularly convinced.

My side. The spear — no, it hadn't been a spear, it had been a boar-spear with a crossguard, the kind you use on something that will keep coming at you, and Serle's rider had used it on me the way you'd use it on an animal, because to him that's what I was: a thing standing between the Order and its vessel. I remembered the sound it made going in. I remembered being surprised, genuinely surprised, that a body could be opened so easily, after everything else I'd watched bodies survive.

"Cyrus." My mouth shaped the name before my mind caught up. "Where — the child —"

"Hush now." A woman's face leaned over me, weathered brown, eyes like wet river stones. A stranger. Not one of ours. Behind her shoulder moved two more figures, men, one young with a farmer's shoulders, one old and bent as a shepherd's crook. None of them I knew. "Don't spend your breath on names yet."

"They took him." The words came out in pieces, each one costing. "On the ridge. They took —"

"We saw the riders." The old man's voice, dry as seed husks. "Four of them, gray cloaks, heading east with a wagon. We stayed off the road."

East. Toward the low country. Toward whatever road led where they were taking him, and every mile of it was a mile I was lying in the dirt watching the wrong-colored sky.

"You're bleeding through," the woman said, matter-of-fact, and pressed something hard against my side, and the world went white and then mercifully absent.

* * *

Fever is a country with poor roads. You travel it in fragments.

A fire, small and shielded, burning behind stones so no one down-valley would see it. The woman — Halda, they called her, though whether that was her name or her trade I never learned — cutting away my coat with a knife that had clearly been used for far worse than cloth. The young man, Pell, holding my head while she poured something into me that tasted like pine tar and regret.

And underneath it all, threaded through every fragment like wire through meat: the screaming.

Not mine. Mine had burned out hours ago. The dead. Every loose bone in my ruined coat, every carved charm, every sliver of grave-bone I'd ever carried against my skin — they were all of them shrieking, and not in words anymore. Words were what they gave me when there was time. This was before language, the raw thing underneath, grief and rage and terror all packed into one frequency, and it did not stop, and I could not answer them, and I understood dimly, through the fever, that they were screaming *his name*. Or trying to. Cyrus had no bones among us to speak through. So they screamed around his absence, the way wind screams around an open door.

"Can't you quiet them?" Pell asked once, hushed, staring at my pack where the flutes lay rattling softly against each other with no hand near them.

"No," Halda said for me, because my mouth wouldn't work. "And if he could, I'd worry about him less."

That should have frightened them, I thought, from very far away. Strangers carrying a bleeding man whose belongings sing. But they kept walking.

* * *

I surfaced properly on the second night, in the lee of a rock overhang, with rain ticking on the stone above and the fire down to coals. The pain had settled from a blade into a hot coal sewn under my ribs. I had been stitched — rough work, waxed thread, but honest — and bandaged, and someone had propped my pack beside my head like a pillow.

The bones were still screaming. Quieter now. Hoarse, if bones can be hoarse. They had worn themselves down to a low continuous moan, like a house settling in cold weather, and when I put my palm flat against the flute case I felt it in my teeth.

"I know," I whispered. "I know. I'm going back for him."

They didn't believe me. Why would they. I had said similar things to them across twenty years of small conversations and never once meant to act. The dead of my little world had made their peace with my cowardice generations ago. It was practically a covenant between us: you stay small, we'll stay close.

But something must have come through anyway, some change in how I held the case, because the moan shifted. Shaped itself. And out of the half-finished flute — the one I'd been carving the night Cyrus knocked, the one that had first said *run* — came a word.

*"East."*

"I can't walk east." I laughed, and it turned into a cough that felt like being unstitched. "I can barely breathe westward."

*"East."*

"I heard you. East." I closed my eyes. "Give me until morning."

Halda crouched at the edge of the firelight, watching me talk to a box of bones with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it on Maren's face twenty years ago when I first told her about the whispering: the particular stillness of someone deciding whether to run. She didn't run.

"The child," she said. "The one the riders took. He yours?"

"No." And then, because it was true and because fever makes liars of everyone eventually, "Yes. Both."

She nodded slowly, as if that answered something. "There's a place," she said. "Over the pass. Where my mother sent me when the Order burned our village. A valley where their witchery doesn't reach. Where their *anything* doesn't reach. People go there to disappear." She looked at me a long moment. "You've been there. Haven't you. That's why the cloaked ones' charms went dead when they passed us on the road yesterday. I felt it. Like a draft from a shut door."

I said nothing. Twenty years of saying nothing is a hard habit to break even with your ribs opened.

"If you're going after them," Halda said, "you'll die on the road. You know that. Whatever's in your box won't stop a spear twice." She fed a stick to the coals. "But there's a place partway, a hollow off the shepherd track, where a wounded man could be tended. And beyond that—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide to say the rest, "—beyond that, if you truly mean to follow, you'll want to pass through the null valley itself. Rest there. Come back to yourself. Nothing hunts inside it. Even the Order loses your scent at the border. Everyone does."

And the dead went silent.

All at once, completely, the way a room goes silent when someone important enters. Every bone in my pack stopped mid-moan. The flute's half-formed word died unspoken. And into that ringing absence rose a feeling I had never received from them in thirty years of listening, because I had never needed it:

Refusal.

Not anger. Not fear. Something older and sadder. It came up through my palm like cold water, wordless and unmistakable, the feeling of a door closing gently in front of you by hands that love you.

"What," I said. "What is it."

Nothing. The most complete nothing they had ever given me. In the null valley they would have no voice at all — I knew that, I had always known it, it was the whole architecture of my life, the silence I had chosen like a coat. But this was different. This was them, here, now, able to speak, choosing silence. Choosing to say *no* without words because the words themselves would cost too much.

"Don't take him back there," Halda said quietly. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at my pack, at the flutes rattling faintly, restlessly, against each other. "Whatever they just said — don't do it."

"They didn't say anything. That's rather the point of them."

"Mm." She stirred the coals. "My mother said the valley takes a price from those who leave it. Not a punishment. Just — interest. On all those years of borrowed quiet." Her eyes found mine. "How long were you there?"

"Twenty-two years."

She winced, actual physical wincing, and turned away to tend the fire, and would say no more about it.

* * *

We traveled three more days. Or the fever traveled three more days and I rode along in its pocket.

Pell carried me across the ford at Miller's Run with my arm over his shoulders, grunting apologies every time the jolt tore a sound out of me. Halda changed the dressing each dawn and each dusk and never commented on what she found beneath, which was either professionalism or pity. The old man — Odo, I finally learned — walked ahead and behind and around, a nervous dog of a man, checking backtrail, and twice he came back at a run to report dust on the eastern road that turned out to be carters, then sheep.

The dead spoke in single words now, rationed like water on a desert crossing. *"East."* *"Wagon."* Once, terribly, *"Alone"* — and I chose, deliberately, feverishly, to hear it as *you are not alone* rather than *he is alone*, because I could only carry one of those meanings and stay upright.

And always, whenever our wandering line bent toward the pass — toward home, toward the valley — the silence came down again. That loving, terrible refusal. They would not guide me there. They would not help me there. And I began, in the lucid cracks between fevers, to understand why, and the understanding sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

The valley had kept me safe by keeping me deaf. That was the bargain I'd struck without knowing I was striking it: no magic could enter, and so no magic could reach me, and so the dead who lived in my carvings were cut off from everything except me — a single thread, one listener, one small room. Safe. Contained. *Small.* I had built my whole life on the comfort of deliberate smallness, and I had told myself it was wisdom, and perhaps some of it was.

But Cyrus was out there in the wide loud magical world, in a prison wagon, and every hour I spent inside the null was an hour the dead could not help me find him. The refuge was also a cage. It had always been a cage. I had simply been comfortable in it, the way a bird is comfortable in a cage it has decorated.

"He's waking again," Pell said somewhere above me.

"I'm awake," I said. "Put me down. I can walk."

"You can't walk."

"I can walk *badly*, which is still walking." And I made him put me down, and I walked badly, ten steps, twenty, before the ground tilted and Halda caught my other arm, and we went on that way, three strangers holding up a stranger, along the shepherd track toward the pass.

Because here is what I decided in those lucid cracks, with the stone in my chest and the dead moaning their rationed words: I would go back. Through the valley, yes — because Halda was right, because I needed tending or I'd die on the road and a dead rescuer rescues no one. I would let them carry me to the border. I would let the null take the pain, dull it, hold me while I healed. I would rest in the silence like a man resting in a locked room.

And then I would walk out again. Over the same border I had crossed only weeks ago, where magic had flooded into me like light into a man leaving a cellar — colors sharpening, the dead swelling from whispers to a roar, my knees buckling under thirty years of muffled world arriving all at once. I remembered the cost of it. I would pay it again. Twice, if the road demanded.

Because the alternative was to lie in my beautiful silent valley, safe and deaf and whole, growing old on the comfort of deliberate smallness, while a mute child who trusted me was carried east in a cage of his own.

The dead, when I explained this to them — feverish, muttering, one palm on the flute case under a blanket of stars — went quiet again.

But it was a different quiet.

It was the quiet of people waiting to see if you mean it.

* * *

The border of the valley announced itself the way it always had: nothing. A fold in the hills, a stream that ran a little colder, a sense of pressure lifting off the ears like surfacing from a dive. Halda stopped at the stream and would not go further.

"This is where my mother's map ended," she said. "Beyond that, I've got no business. And neither," she added, glancing meaningfully at my side, "have you, really."

"Thank you." I meant it more than I could shape. "For the stitching. For the walking. For not asking better questions."

"Ask him instead." She nodded at my pack. "He seems to have answers."

Odo shook my hand with both of his, solemn as a priest. Pell hesitated, then dug in his own bag and pressed something into my palm: a stub of apple, wrinkled but sound. Road food. The oldest kindness there is.

"For the child," he said. "If you find him."

I held it the whole way down into the valley, cradled against the wound, absurdly careful, like a second heart.

The pain began to dull at the border, exactly as promised — the null pressing down soft and total, magic's absence laying itself over the injury like snow over a rutted road. By the time I reached the workshop door I was almost comfortable. Almost whole-feeling. The fever banked itself to embers. I lit the lamp. I lay down on my own cot in my own small room, in the deepest quiet in the world.

And the silence came down on me like a lid.

No whispers. No moans. Not even the refusal — refusal required a voice, and here they had none. Thirty years of companionship, and in my own home I could not hear so much as a breath of them. The flutes lay in their case like sticks. The grave-bone charms were gravel. Somewhere out there, past the fold in the hills, past the border I had crossed and recrossed, the dead were screaming my name and Cyrus's, and I lay in the perfect safety of the null valley and heard nothing at all.

No way to listen. No way to learn. No way to know if the wagon still rolled east, if the boy still breathed, if the solstice — that word the bones had gasped at me in another life, weeks ago, *solstice* — was rushing toward us or already past.

This was the cage. Not the borders. Not the nullity. The silence. All those years I had called it peace.

I lay there while the lamp burned down, one hand curled around Pell's apple, and I stared at the ceiling of the workshop where I had been content, guarded, and lonely for twenty-two years, and I waited for the fever to break, because the moment it broke, I was leaving.

Sometime before dawn, boots crunched on the frost outside my door. Many boots. And a voice I knew — rough, furious, terrified, beloved — calling my name across the dark yard.

Maren. Coming down the mountain.
