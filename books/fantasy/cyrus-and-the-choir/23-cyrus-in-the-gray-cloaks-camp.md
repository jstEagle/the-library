## 23. Cyrus in the Gray Cloaks' Camp

The wagon smelled of pitch and old fear.

Cyrus knew that smell. It lived in the cellar under the monastery, in the seam of Brother Ilex's coat, in the space between their own ribs where breath used to go before it learned to stop. Fear had a smell like tar and wet wool, and this wagon was soaked in it—not the fear of the men riding outside with swords at their hips, but an older fear, soaked into the boards themselves, from all the other children who had lain where Cyrus lay now.

That was a comfort, in its terrible way. Other children. Plural.

The wagon lurched over a stone, and Cyrus's shoulder struck the wall. They did not cry out. Crying out was a thing voices did, and Cyrus's voice had been locked away for so long behind the wall of silence that they sometimes wondered if it still existed—if somewhere inside them there was still a small door, or if the monks had burned that too, along with everything else at the monastery.

*Brother Ilex is alive.* They held that thought the way they held the bone whistle at night, curled fingers around it, hidden. *He got into the cellar. He is hiding. He will find a way.*

And Wendell—

No. They could not think about Wendell without the wall of silence cracking, and cracks let things through. The image of the bone-carver falling on the ridgeline, coat flapping, the loose bones in the pockets singing a scream that no living ear should have heard—the image came anyway, through the crack, and Cyrus pressed their face against the rough plank floor and breathed until it went away.

Breathing was something they could do. Carving was something they could do.

Their wrists were bound with cord, not iron—the Order feared iron around their vessels, Ilex had once said, though he'd never said why—and the cord was tight but not cruel. Their captors wanted them whole. That was the one mercy in all of this, and it was not mercy at all; it was inventory.

Cyrus rolled onto their stomach, dragged their bound hands under their body until they lay in front of them like two pale fish, and examined the floor of the wagon by the gray light leaking through the slats.

Pine planks. Old ones, silvered with age, soft-grained. Soft enough for fingernails.

Good.

* * *

The first thing Cyrus carved was small, almost nothing: a spiral, three turns, the opening turned inward toward the center of the wagon. It was a greeting in the old language, the language before kingdoms. *I see you,* it said. *Whoever you are, whatever you are, I see you.*

Then they waited.

The wagon swayed. Outside, hooves, the creak of harness, a man's voice complaining about the cold. Somewhere ahead, another axle groaning—the convoy was long, six wagons at least, maybe more. Cyrus had counted the shadows of them through the slats when they'd been loaded in at dusk, and then the dark had closed like a book.

Nothing answered the spiral.

Cyrus had not really expected it to. The dead did not live everywhere. The valley had taught them that—a place where magic could not enter, where even the whispering bones of Wendell's trade fell quiet as river stones. And the Ashen Order traveled light, traveled clean, burned what they could not carry. If there had ever been dead men in this convoy's road-dust, the Order's priests would have sung them out of the air like smoke from a room.

But Cyrus kept carving anyway, because carving was breathing, and breathing was refusing.

They worked along the seam between two planks, where the grain ran soft. *My name is Cyrus,* they cut, letter by letter, in the old script, each stroke shallow enough to be mistaken for wear, deep enough to be read by anyone who knew how. *I am the last anchor. I am going to the Hollow Spire. If you can hear this—*

A hand slammed down on the wagon roof above them, and Cyrus flinched so hard they bit their lip.

Not for them. Muffled shouting outside—a wheel, a stuck wheel, cursing, the crack of a whip on someone else's mule. The wagon rocked, righted itself, rolled on.

Cyrus lay still, heart hammering, and finished the sentence anyway.

*If you can hear this, you are not alone either.*

* * *

They stopped for the night in a hollow below a ridgeline, and the Order made camp with the efficiency of people who had done terrible things in many places and expected to do them again tomorrow.

Through the gap beneath the wagon's tailgate, Cyrus watched the fires bloom. Gray cloaks moving between them. Horses hobbled. A cook-fire smell of barley and fat that made their stomach cramp with want—they had eaten half a heel of bread since dawn, and growing bodies did not negotiate.

Two guards sat at the tailgate. Young. One of them kept flexing his sword hand like it hurt him. The other picked at the mud drying on his boots and hummed something tuneless under his breath.

A third man limped past the wagon on his way to the latrine trench, favoring his left leg, and Cyrus saw the way his boot dragged, and the way no one offered to help him, and the way he did not ask.

*Fallen,* Cyrus thought suddenly. *He walks like a fallen thing.*

It was a strange thought, shapeless, more feeling than idea. But the valley had taught Cyrus things about feelings—that they were often messages, that Wendell called them *the body's own whispers*, and that you ignored a whisper at your peril. This man walked like something already broken, something the war inside the Order had already chewed and spat out, still walking only because walking was the habit of the living.

And broken things had gaps in them. Cracks.

Cracks let things through.

* * *

In the dark, when the fires burned low and the guards changed, Cyrus went back to work.

They carved for hours, nails splitting, fingertips raw, working by touch more than sight. They filled the floor of the wagon with the old script in long unbroken lines, spirals nested inside spirals, the grammar of a language that had never needed to shout because it had always been sung:

*I am Cyrus. I was kept at the monastery on the coast. There were others. Where are the others. What is my name worth to you. Nothing. My name is worth nothing to you. But your names—your dead friends, your brothers who didn't come home from the last burning, the boy in the second wagon who cries at night when he thinks no one hears—I can hear them. I hear everyone the Order has spent. Ask your dead what the god's waking sounds like. Ask them. They know. They were there the last time the Choir sang.*

It was not entirely true. Cyrus did not yet know how much the dead knew. But Wendell had taught them something else, without meaning to, in the workshop among the hanging flutes and the shavings that smelled of cedar: *a story told plainly is a key; a story told well is a crowbar.* Cyrus had very few tools. They intended to use the ones they had.

Near dawn, exhausted, they added one line more—the truest one, the one that had been sitting under all the others like a stone under moss:

*I am not afraid of you. I am afraid for you.*

Then they slept, face down on their own words, like a child sleeping on a letter never sent.

* * *

They woke to shouting.

For one white instant Cyrus thought: *the ridge, Wendell, the exiles*—but no. The shouting was ordinary. A horse had thrown a shoe. Farriers shouted, curses flew, the column stalled in a marshy stretch where the road gave up being a road and became a suggestion.

And in the stall, in the waiting, a soldier wandered.

Cyrus heard him before they saw him: boots circling the wagon, slow, idle, the walk of a man killing time. Then the boots stopped. At the tailgate. Directly above the gap where the gray light fell across Cyrus's bound hands.

"Oi," the man said softly, to no one. "What's this?"

Cyrus went absolutely still. Prey-still. Heart-slow. The kind of stillness the monks had drilled into them without ever meaning to, the stillness of children who learned early that being noticed was the same as being taken.

The man crouched at the tailgate. Cyrus saw him upside down: weathered face, maybe forty, a beard going gray at the chin, eyes that had the flat shine of a pond in winter. An old scar split one eyebrow. His gaze moved across the floor of the wagon—across the spirals, across the lines of script—and Cyrus watched his expression do something complicated. First the boredom of a soldier seeing scratches. Then a flicker of recognition, quickly buried. Then—this was the part that mattered, this was the part Cyrus would remember for the rest of their life—fear.

Not fear *of* Cyrus. Fear the way a man is afraid in a church.

"You," the soldier said, barely a breath. "The little vessel. You wrote this."

Cyrus did not move. Did not nod. Words were dangerous; words could be reported. Instead they lifted one finger—one finger only, slow as honey—and pointed, deliberately, at the final line. The truest one.

*I am not afraid of you. I am afraid for you.*

The soldier read it. Cyrus watched his lips move faintly, sounding out shapes he clearly knew but had never seen written, the way an old song comes back when someone else hums the first bar. His winter-pond eyes went bright and awful.

"My brother," he said, so quietly it was hardly sound at all. "Died at Vell's Crossing. Three years back. The Order said—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked over his shoulder at the camp, at the gray cloaks moving between the fires, at the priests' black tent with its ash-white sigil.

He looked back at Cyrus. And Cyrus, very slowly, laid their palm flat against the floor of the wagon—over the words, over the spirals, over the greeting that had waited three days for an answer—and pressed, the way you press your hand to a grave.

The soldier's breath went out of him all at once, like a man struck.

"He used to sing," he whispered. "Off-key. Drove our mother mad." His hand rose halfway toward the tailgate, toward Cyrus, and stopped—stopped as if the air itself had thickened—and in that moment Cyrus felt it happen, felt it the way you feel a change in wind before the trees tell you: the space under the wagon, the gray morning air, the whole stalled camp around them, went *heavy*.

Heavy with attention.

Something was listening. Many somethings. Not here, perhaps—not in this clean-burned convoy—but near. In the marsh ground they'd crossed, where old wars had been fought. In the ridgeline behind them. In the soldier himself, who carried his grief the way some men carry knives, close and sharpened, and grief was a door, and doors opened both ways.

The soldier stood abruptly, as if scalded. He glanced up and down the camp lane. No one watching. He crouched again, faster now, urgent.

"There's a woman," he said. "Sergeant Halloway, supply train, third wagon. Her whole squad died at the ford last spring—all of them, all twelve, and the Order called it a routing error. She keeps their tags." He tapped his own chest, where dog-tags hung. "She talks to them at night. Thinks nobody knows." His jaw worked. "You want ears in this camp, little ghost? She's got twelve of them already."

Then he was gone, walking fast, not looking back—but not walking away either. Walking *toward* the third wagon.

Cyrus lay in the gray light, heart slamming, and understood that something enormous had just happened, something with a momentum that would not stop now whether they willed it or not.

They had spent their whole life being a secret. Kept in cellars, kept in monasteries, kept behind walls of silence, passed from hiding place to hiding place like contraband holiness. The Order called them a *vessel*. The monks had called them a *trust*. Even Wendell, gentle Wendell, had first seen them as something to be protected, carried, delivered—precious, passive, a package with legs.

But a signal fire is none of those things.

A signal fire does not hide. A signal fire stands on a height where everyone can see it, and its whole purpose is to be noticed, and everything it touches catches.

* * *

By midday, the convoy rolled again, and the third wagon's sergeant had found a reason to walk the length of the column twice.

Cyrus could not see her. But they could feel her, the way they'd learned to feel the dead through Wendell's carvings—a pressure, a warmth, a wrongness-that-was-rightness. When she passed the prison wagon the first time, her stride hitched, just slightly, the hitch of a woman whose twelve tags had all gone cold at once and then, impossibly, *tugged*. The second time she slowed further. By evening camp, she was standing at the tailgate in the guise of inspecting the prisoner, arms crossed, face hard as saddle leather, while the gray-bearded soldier lingered nearby whittling a stick that needed no whittling.

"Say your piece, ghost," Sergeant Halloway muttered, too low for any living listener. "Twelve of 'em are crowded round me right now saying there's words on a floor. So say your piece."

So Cyrus did.

They carved through that night and the next, faster now, hands bleeding freely, no longer hiding the marks—hiding was over, hiding was finished, the fire was lit and lit things spread. They wrote of the binding and the breaking of it. Of the god that was not a devil but a grief, and of the Choir that had sung it to sleep out of mercy, and of the renewal that required a willing voice, and of what the Ashen Order's hundred-year failure meant: that force had never once worked, and never would, and every soldier the Order spent was spent for nothing, spent *worse* than nothing. They wrote the truth the way Brother Ilex had taught them to write it: plainly, so it could be a key.

And the dead carried it.

Cyrus felt it happening like weather. Word passing tag to tag, grave to grave, memory to memory—twelve dead at a ford, one dead at Vell's Crossing, a boy who cried at night in the second wagon and whose grandmother's ghost apparently had opinions. The living soldiers began to look at the priests' black tent a beat too long. Began to speak more quietly. Began, in ones and twos, to drift past the prison wagon on invented errands, reading the floor through the tailgate gap, touching the planks the way you touch a wall in a house where someone you loved used to live.

On the fourth morning, the gray-bearded soldier crouched at the tailgate one more time. His face had changed. The winter ponds had thawed into something moving, something running underneath the ice.

"Message came back," he breathed. "Down the line. From—" he laughed, a cracked, disbelieving sound, "—from places I don't want to name. Your carver friend. The one they left on the ridge." His eyes were shining and frightened and fierce, all three. "*Alive.* Strangers pulled them off the rock, and there's others with them now—armed, angry folk from some hidden valley, following the blood trail like hounds. The dead along this road are talking about nothing else. They're coming, little ghost. Whoever they are—whatever they are—"

He glanced over his shoulder at the black tent, then leaned closer, and his whisper was the finest thing Cyrus had heard in four days of splinters and silence:

"They're coming *after* you. And old Halloway's twelve have decided that when they get here, this camp is going to be looking the wrong way."

Cyrus pressed their bleeding palm flat against the floorboards—against the spirals, against the words, against the fire they had started with nothing but fingernails and refusal—and for the first time since the ridgeline, since Wendell fell, since the wall of silence had cracked and nearly broken them with it—

—for the first time, Cyrus smiled.
