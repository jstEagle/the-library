## 24. Maren Comes Down the Mountain

Maren Osgood had buried three husbands, outlived two wars, and been exiled from a duchy whose name she refused to say aloud in the valley's hearing, so it took a great deal to frighten her. But when Tam Wicke came running down from the pass with his cap in one fist and blood on the other, she felt something old and cold settle into her chest, like a stone dropped down a well.

"She's hurt," Tam said. "Or somebody is. There's blood on the rocks past the standing stones—wiped at, but not wiped well. And boot prints. Too many."

"How many?"

"Six? Seven? Going east, toward Hollowford road." He swallowed. "And one set going nowhere. Drag marks."

The valley had gone quiet around them—the particular quiet of people listening through their doors. Maren had lived among exiles long enough to know that silence. It was the sound of everyone deciding at once whether to be brave.

"Get the cart," she said. "Get Halvord, get the twins, get anyone who can walk and swing something. Tell them to bring tools."

Tam blinked. "Tools?"

"We're farmers, Tam. It's what we have." She was already moving toward her own house, where under the floorboards beneath the loom there lay a scythe-blade that had never once cut wheat.

* * *

They left at first light, eleven of them, which was more than Maren expected and fewer than she'd hoped for. The valley kept its nullity like a held breath; magic could not cross the ridge-line, which was why every soul in it had come here—to be free of things that whispered and burned and followed you through dreams. They were cowards and fugitives and broken people, all of them, and Maren loved every stubborn one of them the way you love a scar: because it meant you'd survived something.

Wendell Dunmore had been the first exile she'd ever met, back when Maren herself arrived with nothing but a burned name and a bad leg. Wendell had carved her a walking stick that first winter, no payment asked, and had said only, "The wood likes you. I can tell." It wasn't until years later she learned what the bones said about her too—that they'd told Wendell her husband hadn't run off, as she'd always claimed, but had died in the crossing, and that Wendell had carried that knowledge alone rather than hand her a grief she wasn't ready for. She'd raged at them when she found out. She'd also sat with them until dawn drinking plum brandy, and neither of them ever spoke of it again.

That was family, in the valley. Not the saying of soft things. The carrying of heavy ones.

Now she stood at the valley's edge where the null thinned, looking at the standing stones and the smear of dried blood on the granite beyond, and she understood that whatever waited on the other side of that line would ask her to be someone other than a farmer.

"Maren," said Halvord behind her, a big slow man with a threshing flail over his shoulder like a war-hammer he didn't believe in. "You're sure about this. The rule is nobody leaves. That's the whole—it's how we stay safe."

"The rule kept us safe when we were hiding from our own histories." Maren touched the blood with two fingers. It was old, days old, browned into the stone. "This isn't ours. This is Wendell's. And if you think I'm letting that bone-carver bleed out on some road because leaving made us nervous, then you can turn around and go home and explain to the village why the bread's gone flat and the beer won't brew right, because half our luck walks on Wendell Dunmore's two feet whether any of us wants to admit it or not."

Nobody turned around. The twins, Pell and Sorrel, exchanged the look they thought adults couldn't see, the one that meant *she's right and it's terrible.*

"All right," Halvord sighed. "But I want it noted I complained."

"Noted," said six voices.

Maren stepped across the line.

She had braced for pain, for revelation, for the flood that travelers' tales promised when you crossed out of a dead zone into living magic. What came instead was color. The world sharpened the way a blade does against a whetstone—the pines went green enough to ache, the sky deepened, and somewhere far off she heard something like singing, or like wind through a bottle, or like a hundred voices trying to remember a song. Her bad leg stopped hurting for the first time in nine years. She nearly wept, and hated it.

"So that's what we've been missing," Sorrel said faintly.

"Don't get used to it," Maren said. "Come on. Blood doesn't wait."

* * *

They tracked the way farmers track anything—patiently, by what was bent and broken and fed upon. The drag marks became a proper trail after the second mile: someone walking wounded, favoring the left side, stopping often. At a stream crossing they found a strip of cloth caught in the rocks, gray wool, good weave, torn not cut. Maren knew the coat. She'd mended its elbow twice, complaining the whole time about Wendell's refusal to buy new wool.

"Wendell's alive," she said, and heard her own voice shake, and hated that too.

Past the stream the trail met the Hollowford road, and the road met trouble. A burned waystation, still smoking at the edges, its door hacked open. Inside, a merchant lay dead amid his scattered goods—not robbed, just killed, and killed badly, the wounds wrong somehow, too many, done in fear rather than purpose. Pell knelt by him and came up gray-faced.

"This wasn't soldiers, Mam. Soldiers kill clean. Whoever did this was scared of him."

Maren looked at the man's hands. They were scorched, blistered, as though he'd been holding something that burned. On his forefinger, a ring with an ash-gray seal pressed into wax still stuck to his palm—a tower, cracked, with flames rising from it.

"I know that mark," said Tam quietly. Everyone looked at him. Tam, who never said anything. "My brother rode courier on the coast roads, before. Before me. He wrote once about riders in gray cloaks asking questions at every toll. Ashen Order. He said you didn't answer their questions fast enough and your barn got struck by lightning on a clear night."

"Charming," said Halvord.

"They're who Wendell's running from," Maren said. It wasn't a guess. Nine days ago a child had appeared at Wendell's door in a storm, mute and hollow-eyed, and within a fortnight Wendell—who hadn't crossed the valley line in twenty years—had packed carved bones and dried plums and walked out into the world like a man walking to his own funeral, with that same child's hand in theirs. Maren had watched from her window and told herself it was right, it was necessary, the valley couldn't shelter them from whatever this was. She'd told herself that for nine days. Now there was blood on the road and a burned man with an ash-sealed ring, and she was done telling herself things.

"We follow the road east," she said. "Fast."

* * *

They found the ford at dusk, and they found the aftermath of something terrible.

There had been a fight here—recent, hours old. Blood darkened the shallows in ropes and ribbons. Two horses grazed loose on the far bank, one limping. A body floated downstream, snagged against a willow, wearing gray. And on the near bank, half-buried in the mud where someone had crawled, they found a carving knife with a snapped tip, and beside it, pressed deep into the soft earth, the unmistakable print of a small child's bare foot, and larger prints going away north along the river, hurried, uneven—one set dragging.

Sorrel fished the body from the willow while the others watched the road. Gray cloak, ash-seal brooch, young face slack with surprise even in death. In his saddlebag: hard bread, a coil of rope, and papers. Maren read the papers by the last light, and her hands went cold.

It was a list. Names, perhaps thirty of them, in a precise clerkish hand, most struck through with single lines like accounts settled. At the bottom, unstruck: *CYRRUS — vessel intact — priority absolute.* Above it, instructions she read three times without wanting to: *the vessel must reach the Spire alive; renewal requires willingness; extract willingness by any means.* Below that, a note in different ink, newer, shakier: *the woman at the inn lied. Find her daughter.*

"Maren," Halvord said gently. "Your face."

"They took the child," Maren said. "Wendell fought—Wendell *killed*, look at this, look at the ford—and they took the child anyway, and now they're hurting innkeepers' daughters to find their way." She folded the papers into her shirt, against her skin, like a coal she intended to carry somewhere useful. "We're past following a blood trail, friends. Now we hunt."

Pell laughed once, high and nervous. "Hunt? With flails?"

"With whatever's in our hands." Maren stood, joints cracking, leg aching now that the valley's mercy was miles behind them, and looked north along the river at the dark coming down. Somewhere up there was Wendell, bleeding, alone, believing—as that stubborn, self-sacrificing fool absolutely believed—that they had left everyone behind to keep them safe. "Here's what that bone-carver never understood," she said to the eleven faces watching her, farmer faces, exile faces, faces that had each fled something and sworn never again. "You don't get to decide what your family will risk for you. You only get to decide whether you'll be worth it. And Wendell has been worth it since the day they carved my walking stick."

She picked up the flail Halvord offered her.

"North," she said. "And Tam—when we catch up to them, and we will, someone find out how a person talks to the dead through a piece of bone. Because I have a few things to say to Wendell Dunmore, and I'll be needing a medium."

They were still laughing at that—thin, frightened, grateful laughter—when the first of them noticed the lights on the northern ridge. Not campfires. Lanterns in formation, moving in ranks, a dozen, two dozen, strung along the skyline like a gray necklace of embers.

The convoy. Heading for the mountains, heading for whatever the Spire was, with a stolen child inside it.

"Well," said Halvord, tightening his grip on the threshing flail. "At least we won't have to chase them far."

"No," Maren agreed, and began, deliberately, furiously, to climb. "Only uphill."
