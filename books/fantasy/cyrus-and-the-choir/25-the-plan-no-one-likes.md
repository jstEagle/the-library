## 25. The Plan No One Likes

The farmhouse they had borrowed—no one asked whose it was; the valley exiles had a talent for borrowing things that belonged to the dead—smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool. Rain came down outside in long gray sheets, drumming the thatch, filling the silence between voices with something almost like mercy.

Wendell stood at the table with their hands flat on the wood, the way they did before beginning a difficult carving. Around them, crowded into the lamplight like figures too large for a shrine, sat the strangest congregation the valley had ever produced: Maren, arms crossed, her knuckles still scabbed from the road; Bramble, dripping, grinning, and pretending not to be afraid; three exiles Wendell had known all their life as farmers and fence-menders and now, apparently, as soldiers without uniforms; Cyrus, small and silent at the far end, drawing spirals in the spilled flour with one careful finger.

And Brother Ilex, mute as Cyrus, writing on slate because his voice had burned out of him years ago in a cellar full of smoke.

Ilex turned the slate toward the table. His handwriting was a monk's handwriting—precise, unhurried, maddeningly calm:

*How many days do you think we have?*

"Forty," Wendell said. "Cyrus's memory was clear. The monks sang the count every morning. Forty days to solstice. We've traveled—" they did the arithmetic they'd done a hundred times since the ford, since the blood, since the page torn from Serle's saddlebag— "we've spent eleven. Twenty-nine remain. It's tight but it holds."

Ilex wiped the slate slowly. He looked, for a moment, very old—not old in years but old in the way that people who have watched institutions lie get old. He wrote:

*That is wrong.*

The rain filled the pause. Bramble stopped chewing.

"The monastery kept the count," Wendell said. "Forty days. It was carved above the chapel door. I saw it myself, in the ashes."

Ilex wrote: *You saw what the brothers were told. The calendar we followed was set by the founder of our order, four hundred years ago. He miscounted the lunar drift. Every generation knew the true date and let the error stand, because a longer vigil felt safer than a shorter one.* He paused, chalk hovering, then added: *Solstice is in three days.*

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Nobody moved.

"That can't be," said Tam, one of Maren's neighbors—a broad man who had once been fined for brawling at the harvest fair and had never lived it down. "Three days? The Spire's eight days' walk if the weather's kind, and the weather hasn't been kind since the Flood."

"Then we're already dead," said his sister Odile, who had a way of stating things plainly that made them worse. "We just don't know it yet."

Maren uncrossed her arms. "No." Her voice carried the particular weight it carried in the valley, the voice that ended arguments about drainage ditches and marriage matches alike. "No, we're not dead, because dead people don't plan. Wendell. The convoy. How fast?"

Wendell closed their eyes and let the map assemble itself behind their lids—the one drawn from the killed rider's papers, redrawn by candlelight on the back of an old grain ledger. "They travel heavy. Prison wagon, mounted escort, supply carts. Twelve miles a day, maybe fourteen if the Inquisitor pushes them. They'll take the Hollow Spire road because it's the only road—the cartway over the ridge floods out this time of year, and the Order doesn't strike camp in the rain if they can avoid it."

"They'll reach the Spire road junction in two days," Maren said. "A day ahead of us, walking."

"So we don't walk."

Everyone looked at Bramble, who had raised one finger and immediately seemed to regret it.

"Go on," Maren said, in a tone that promised no mercy for cleverness wasted on jokes.

Bramble swallowed. "There are horses. Not good ones. The miller at Ferrow Cross keeps draft horses and he owes half the valley money and hates the tax riders more than he loves any of them. Six horses, maybe seven if we flatter him right. That puts us at the junction a half-day *ahead* of the convoy instead of a day behind." A beat. "Assuming nobody falls off, which, speaking personally—"

"You'll ride behind me and hold my belt," Odile said, and there was no humor in it at all.

* * *

They argued for two hours. It was, Wendell thought later, the most alive they had ever felt—this room full of frightened, stubborn people tearing a plan apart the way you test a blade, bending it until something snaps or until you trust it.

The plan, when it finally stopped bleeding and held its shape, was simple enough to carve on a spoon:

Ride through the night. Reach the junction where the Spire road narrowed between rockfall and river—a place called the Tollhouse Cut, where twelve men could hold off forty. Maren and the exiles would take the high ground with every tool that could be made into a weapon and every grudge that had ever been sharpened on a whetstone. Their job was not to win. Their job was to be loud, to be many, and to make the escort believe the whole valley had come down the mountain after them.

Wendell and Cyrus would slip through the cut in the chaos, take Cyrus to the Spire. Bramble—who volunteered before anyone could ask, who volunteered the way other people bleed—would go ahead alone, unarmed, wearing a stolen Order seal and a face built for lying, and find some way to slow the rear guard or, failing that, learn exactly where in the column the prison wagon rode.

"And if they catch you?" Odile asked Bramble.

"Then I tell them a very sad story about how I stole the seal off a drunk sergeant in Ferrow Cross, and I'm so sorry, and I've always wanted to serve, actually, and isn't it funny how life works out." Bramble shrugged. "I'm very likable under pressure."

"You're a liar under pressure," Tam said.

"Those are the same thing, mostly."

Wendell watched Cyrus during all of it. The child had stopped tracing spirals in the flour. Now they sat with both hands pressed flat against the tabletop, the way Wendell themselves had been standing, and Wendell understood suddenly that it wasn't mimicry—it was listening. Feeling, maybe. The way you put a palm against a wall to feel footsteps in another room.

When the plan was settled, Wendell crossed to the child and knelt. "You don't have to agree," they said quietly. "If you want to run—north, anywhere—we run. This was never your burden to carry for our sake."

Cyrus looked at them with those hollow eyes that had grown less hollow, week by week, mile by mile. Then the child reached into the collar of their shirt and drew out the bone whistle—the first carving, the one Wendell didn't remember making—and pressed it into Wendell's hand, closing their fingers around it.

Through the bone, faint as breath on winter glass, the dead spoke. Not words. A chord. A single, sustained chord, like a hundred throats holding one note, and beneath it, unmistakably: *forward.*

"They want to go," Wendell said, and their voice cracked on the second word. They cleared their throat. "Well. Then we go."

* * *

It was past midnight when the house finally quieted. Exiles slept in corners and on benches, boots on, weapons near. Maren sat up on watch, sharpening a billhook with long, patient strokes.

Wendell found Ilex by the cold hearth, feeding splinters of wood into a little fire he'd coaxed from the last dry timber in the house. The monk looked up, and for a moment his eyes went to Wendell's hands—the carver's hands, nicked and scarred and steady—and something passed over his face like grief remembering itself.

He wrote on the slate: *You understand what the binding requires.*

"I know what the page said. 'Renewed each century by a living voice.'" Wendell sat down across from him. "Say the rest."

Ilex hesitated. Chalk dust drifted as his hand hovered. Then he wrote: *Not any voice. The monks tried substitutes for generations. Condemned prisoners. Willing pilgrims who didn't understand. Even recorded song, once, in my grandmother's time—wax cylinders, the first machines. Nothing held. The god knows the difference between a voice given and a voice taken.* He turned the slate. *It must choose itself. And it does not survive the choosing.*

Wendell had known. Had known since the Spire page, since the word *anchor*, since the dead first whispered *run* and meant *run toward*. Knowing and hearing it written in a calm monk's hand were different animals. Their chest felt carved out, scooped like green wood.

"Three days," they said. "You waited three days to tell us the calendar was wrong. Why not say it at the farmhouse door?"

Ilex met their gaze steadily. He erased the slate and wrote slowly, each letter deliberate:

*Because forty days gave you hope, and hope walks faster than truth. I needed you moving before you understood the distance. Forgive me. Monks are cowards with good posture.*

Despite everything—despite the carved-out chest and the rain and the sleeping exiles and the child upstairs breathing slow—Wendell laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, and Ilex's mouth twitched, the ghost of a smile on a man who had lost his voice and most of his world to fire.

"One more thing," Wendell said. "The man I killed at the ford. He had papers. Orders. There was a line in them I couldn't read—some phrase the Order uses. *The vessel sings at the renewal.* What does it mean?"

Ilex stared at the fire for a long time. When he finally wrote, his hand had gone less steady.

*It means the Order believes the binding needs only Cyrus. They mean to force the song from the child—at the altar, under threat, with blades at the throat. It will not work. It has never worked. But it will hurt the child terribly, and it will wake the god halfway, and a god half-woken is worse than a god woken entire.* He underlined the last sentence twice. *Whatever happens at that altar, Wendell Dunmore: the song cannot be taken. Only given.*

Wendell looked down at the whistle in their palm. The chord was still there, patient as stone, and threaded through it now—faint, familiar, warm—they could have sworn they heard something new. Not the valley dead. Not the Choir. Something closer. Something that sounded almost like a voice they hadn't met yet, waiting politely on the other side of a door they would open in three days.

They closed their fist around it.

"Then we give," they said. "God help us all. We give."

Outside, the rain thinned at last. Through the breaking clouds, low over the eastern ridge, a pale sliver of moon rose—the same moon Ilex had blamed for four hundred years of lies. Three days to full dark. Three days to the Spire.

And on the road below the farmhouse, faint but unmistakable in the wet night air, came the sound none of them wanted to hear: hoofbeats. Many. Moving fast. Not toward the Spire road—

Toward them.
