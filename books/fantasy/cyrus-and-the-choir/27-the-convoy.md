## 27. The Convoy

### Wendell

The Hollow Spire road ran along the shoulder of a ravine like a scar that hadn't decided whether to heal. Below it, water moved black between stones; above it, pine forest climbed until the dark swallowed it. Wendell lay flat against a granite outcrop and watched the prison wagon come.

Two horses in front. Six riders—four flanking, one ahead, one behind. The wagon itself was iron-banded oak, its windows slatted, and chained to its rear a lantern burned with the gray-green flame of consecrated oil. The Ashen Order did not trust darkness. Wendell had learned that much about them on the road, mostly from graves.

Bramble crouched at their left, coiled like something spring-loaded. "Tollhouse bend," they whispered for the fourth time. "Wheels slow to walking pace. Driver's got a whip he doesn't use because the horses are scared of the ravine edge. That's our moment."

"And Ilex?"

"Front wheel axle. He goes under it like he's drunk, they stop to deal with him, you cut Cyrus out." Bramble's grin didn't reach their eyes. "Simple as theft."

Nothing about this was simple. Wendell's hand rested on the open whistle at their collar—the bone flute Cyrus had made, cracked down one side since the ridgeline, since the night Serle had taken the child and left Wendell bleeding among the gorse. Through the crack, even now, the dead murmured. Not words. A tuning. Like an orchestra before the conductor arrives, every ghost in Wendell's coat finding its note.

Ilex had insisted on being the fallen monk. *They will know my face,* he had written, in his careful, silent hand. *And they will not expect a dead man to be walking.* Maren was three days buried, her grave still raw at the Spire's mouth behind them, and grief sat in Wendell's chest like a stone they'd chosen to carry rather than set down. There would be time to grieve properly later. Or there wouldn't. Either way, Cyrus came home tonight.

The wagon reached the bend. The wheels slowed. Somewhere below in the ravine, Ilex stumbled out onto the road ahead of the horses, ragged, ash-streaked, arms wide—a drowned brother returned from a burning monastery, a miracle too convenient for any sane man to believe.

But faith makes sane men stupid. The lead rider reined up. Lanterns swung. Voices rose, sharp with wonder, and the flank riders closed toward the front, toward Ilex, toward the light.

"Now," Bramble breathed.

Wendell went down the scree like water, low and fast, carving knife in hand—not to fight, never to fight if they could help it, but to cut. The wagon's rear door was padlocked; the hasp was not. Two strokes of the knife, a wrench of the blade as lever, and iron shrieked loose.

Through the slats: a small face, hollow-eyed, smudged gray. Cyrus pressed both palms flat against the wood from inside.

"I've got you," Wendell whispered, and meant it the way some people meant prayers.

* * *

### Cyrus

Inside the wagon there had been no day and no night, only the sway and the smell of the Order's oil and the sound of the chains of the others—Brother Anselm, Sister Vaelle, old Brother Corvin, all of them anchors like Cyrus, all of them crossed-off names made flesh. They had been kept alive because the binding refused them. Because unwilling voices sang nothing. The Order had not yet understood what Ilex had understood, what the walls of the Spire said plainly to anyone who could read them: *the god wakes only to a song given freely.* So the prisoners lived, and despaired, and prayed for exactly this.

Cyrus heard the knife before anyone outside could have. Bone sings to bone; the whistle in Wendell's collar hummed through the wagon walls, and every ghost riding with the convoy turned its attention like a head. The dead soldiers who marched for the Ashen Order—men who'd died believing or died doubting, it made no difference now—they gathered at the rear door of their own accord. Not pushing. Waiting. Holding the darkness open.

When the door tore free and Wendell's face appeared, gray-lit, sweat-slick, alive, Cyrus almost wept. Instead they climbed. Hands found hands. Cold air poured in like forgiveness.

Then Cyrus looked back.

The others were chained to the floor rings. Anselm shook his head—slowly, deliberately—and touched his own throat, then pointed down the road, toward the Spire. *Go.* Vaelle mouthed something the glass slats blurred. Corvin simply closed his eyes and began, silently, to sing.

There was no time. Cyrus knew it the way animals know weather. Every heartbeat spent unchaining one anchor was a heartbeat the riders spent noticing the empty rear of the wagon.

Cyrus jumped down. And then—because silence is also a language—turned back once more and carved, fast, into the soft pine of the doorframe where the lock had been: a spiral, broken; three stacked figures; the sign that meant *wait, keep singing, we come back.*

Anselm saw it. Nodded. The dead soldiers between the wagon and the treeline arranged themselves, invisible and patient, so that the first shouts of alarm would come too late and from too far away.

Cyrus ran. Wendell ran beside them. The ravine took them in.

* * *

### Wendell

For forty heartbeats, it worked.

They were in the trees, Cyrus's hand locked in Wendell's, Bramble already whistling the retreat signal low through their teeth, when the miracle on the road ahead curdled.

Wendell never saw what went wrong—Ilex always swore afterward, in writing, that nothing had gone wrong, that this was simply how covering an escape was done—but the lead rider stopped wondering at miracles and started counting. Counted the flankers. Counted the wagon. Looked back.

The shout went up like a struck match.

"Halt! HALT—" Boots, torches, the horrible ringing scrape of six swords drawn at once. And Ilex, standing in the middle of the road with his arms still open, did not run.

*Ilex!* Wendell turned. Bramble grabbed their sleeve. Cyrus pulled, small and ferocious, with the strength of someone who had watched a monastery burn.

He stood in the torchlight like a man greeting old friends. His mouth moved—he had been mute eleven years, but his mouth moved anyway—and whatever he said, the riders heard it. Wendell heard it too, faintly, through the cracked whistle at their collar, carried by the dead who stood in the road beside him: *You are late. The god has been waiting a hundred years, and you bring fire instead of song.*

The first rider struck him down. Then, because fear is crueler than anger, they struck him again.

Wendell would have gone back. Would have died there in the road, uselessly, gloriously. But Cyrus's grip was iron, and Bramble's arm was around their waist, and the dead themselves pushed—Wendell felt dozens of incorporeal hands flat against their spine, shoving them uphill into the dark, and the message in the pressure was unmistakable: *Not you. Not tonight. He chose.*

So Wendell ran, and hated every step of it, and the last thing they saw of Brother Ilex was torchlight on his upturned face, which wore—impossibly, unbearably—something close to peace.

* * *

### Cyrus

They ran until the torches were stars behind them, then farther, following Bramble through game trails only a thief would know, until the pines opened onto a shelf of rock above the ravine and Bramble called the halt with two fingers raised.

Cyrus's lungs burned. Their legs shook. They sat down hard on cold stone and put their face in their hands and let the shaking have them.

A memory, unwanted: Brother Ilex in the monastery kitchen, teaching Cyrus to knead bread without ever speaking, guiding small hands over dough with his own. Ilex pressing the bone whistle into their palms the night of the fire, folding their fingers shut around it, and the look in his eyes that had said everything a voice could not: *Live. Carry it. Forgive us for what we're making you carry.*

Gone. Struck down in a road by men carrying fire, and he had walked into that road knowing.

Cyrus picked up a fallen stick and carved into the dirt, fast, jagged: the sign for *dead*, and beside it the sign for *brother*, and then, because the language was older than kingdoms and honest in ways living tongues had forgotten, the sign for *willing*. He went willing. He gave himself the way the binding asks.

Wendell knelt and read it by moonlight. Their jaw tightened. Then they did something Cyrus did not expect: they took Cyrus's hand and pressed it flat against the cracked whistle at their collar.

Through the fracture in the bone, faint but unmistakable, came a voice. New. Calm. Singing a single sustained note that fit into the chorus of the dead like a keystone into an arch.

Ilex was in the Choir now. Not lost. Translated.

Cyrus cried then, finally, quietly, while the mountains held them and the dead held the note.

* * *

### Wendell

The triumph lasted exactly as long as it took Wendell to remember that Serle was still alive.

"We should move off this shelf," Bramble said, scanning the dark. "If they track—"

"They won't track far tonight." Wendell's voice came out flat and strange. "They'll count their losses and fortify the wagon. Reinforcements are a day behind—that's what the killed man's papers said." A pause. "But Serle will remember. Serle remembers everything."

As if summoned by the name, the whistle at Wendell's collar grew suddenly, violently loud.

It happened like this: the cracked flute, swinging free as Wendell shifted to stand, caught the moonlight and opened. All the way. Every fracture in the bone spread a hair's width wider, and the dead poured through the gap—not just the valley dead anymore, not just the monks, but the soldiers dead at the ford, the exiles' ghosts, Maren's voice rising beneath all of them like a root under a floor—and the sound that came out was not a whisper.

It was a chord. It rolled off the rock shelf, down the ravine, across the black water, vast and layered and grieving, a hundred voices singing one note of a song whose remaining verses lived in the walls of the Spire behind them.

Cyrus clapped both hands over their ears. Bramble swore. And far below, back along the road, torches froze.

One torch detached from the rest. Came on, alone, at a walk.

Serle.

Wendell knew his silhouette now—the straight shoulders, the unhurried stride of a man who had stopped fearing ambush because he had seen worse things than ambush. He stopped at the base of the shelf, twenty feet below, and looked up, and the moon was kind enough to show Wendell his face: young for an inquisitor, seamed with old burn-scars, eyes that held no hatred at all. That was the worst part. Serle had never hated them. He was hunting them the way a surgeon hunts disease—with conviction, and terrible care.

His gaze found Wendell's face and stayed there. Memorizing it. And then his gaze dropped, slowly, to the whistle at Wendell's collar—to the source of the chord still hanging in the air like smoke—and Wendell watched understanding arrive in him the way frost crosses a window.

"You," Serle said. Not loudly. The ravine carried it perfectly. "At the ridgeline. You're the carver." A breath. "That sound. What was—what *are* you carrying?"

The dead answered for Wendell. The chord shifted, deepened, and through it—threaded clear as a bell through mud—came a voice Serle must have known, must have buried or burned or ordered into the ground himself. A soldier's voice. Dead these two years. It spoke one word:

*Listen.*

Serle's hand went to his sword hilt and stopped there. He stood very still in the moonlight, a man listening to the arithmetic of his own soul, and Wendell saw the exact moment when the sum frightened him.

"The god," Serle said quietly. "When it wakes. What happens to—" He cut himself off. Shook his head once, sharply, like a man refusing his own reflection. "No. You're thieves and heretics and you stole property of the Order." But his voice frayed on *property*, and they both heard it.

"Come with us," Wendell said. The words surprised them coming out. "To the Spire. Read the walls yourself. Your scholars translated half the language already—you have the rubbings. Come and read what your Order has been trying to wake."

For one suspended moment, Wendell believed it might work. Serle's hand actually left his sword.

Then, downstream, the main body of riders sounded the alarm horn—one long blast, calling the day-behind reinforcements forward—and the spell broke. Duty snapped back over Serle's face like a shutter. Whatever war was happening inside him, he would fight it privately, in the margins, the way careful men did.

"Run, carver," he said. "I won't call it in tonight. I want to see those walls myself before anyone burns anything." He turned away, then paused, not looking back. "The monk in the road. He said the god waits to be mourned, not silenced. If that's written on the Spire—" his fists tightened, "—then God forgive us all, and the Order most of all."

He walked back down the road, alone, torchless, toward the sound of his own order's horn.

* * *

### Cyrus

They descended the far side of the mountain through the small hours, Bramble leading, Wendell's hand steady on Cyrus's shoulder, the dead humming low and constant through the cracked whistle like a hearthfire that refused to go out.

At the treeline above the pilgrims' path, they stopped to breathe, and Cyrus looked back one last time.

Below and behind them, the Hollow Spire stood against the stars—an impossible absence in the mountainside, a hollow shaped like a held breath. Dawn was still hours off. The solstice was two days away now, maybe less; Ilex's calendar correction had stolen thirty-seven days from everyone's sleep.

Cyrus took out their knife and a scrap of birch bark and carved, by feel more than sight: a spiral, whole this time, unbroken. Two figures side by side. The sign for *song*. The sign for *together*.

Wendell read it in the starlight and nodded once.

Somewhere below, a horn blew again—closer now, and answered by another, and another, the Order's reinforcements converging on the Spire road like wolves answering wolves. Two days. Maybe less.

Cyrus put the carving in Wendell's pocket, took Wendell's hand, and started down toward the Spire's mouth, where Maren's fresh grave waited, and the walls full of waiting words, and whatever the morning would bring.

The dead sang softly all the way down.
