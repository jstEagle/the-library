## 29. The Hollow Spire

Cyrus smelled the mountain before they saw it.

That was the first strange thing. For all the miles since the ford—since the blood in the water and the wagon and the shouting and Brother Ilex going down beneath the wheels with his hands raised, always his hands raised, even at the end—the world had smelled of road dust and horse sweat and the iron tang that Cyrus had learned meant people were about to do violence. But now, as the light went long and gold behind them, a new smell came down the wind like water over stone: deep cold air, the breath of somewhere vast and underground, carrying a faint sweetness like beeswax and old resin.

The dead felt it too.

They had been quiet since the convoy—quiet the way a house is quiet after a funeral, everyone moving carefully around the shape of what was missing—but now they stirred through every bone in Wendell's pack, every carved bead on Cyrus's cord, rising like birds before a storm. Not words yet. Not even whispers. A hum. A tuning. As if something far ahead had cleared its throat, and everything that had ever died was leaning forward to listen.

Wendell stopped on the trail and put a hand flat against the nearest rock, the way they did when they wanted to feel what the dead were feeling without asking aloud. Their face did the thing it always did when the voices came: jaw tightening, eyes going distant and slightly unfocused, mouth pressed into a line that could have been pain or could have been prayer.

"They're singing," Wendell said. "Not whispering. Singing. I've never—" They stopped. Started again. "It's coming from there."

They pointed up the valley ahead, where the mountains folded together into a dark throat of stone, and at the bottom of that throat, black against the bruised purple of the evening sky, stood a spire.

Cyrus had seen mountains their whole short life. Had crossed two ranges on foot with monks who sang to keep their courage up, had slept in the shadow of peaks that scraped the belly of the sky. But this was not like those. This rose out of the earth with a wrongness that the eye kept trying to correct—a column of stone so smooth, so deliberate, that it looked less grown than *built*, though no builder alive could have raised it. It was hollow—that was the smell, that was the hum, the whole mountain was a bell—and the wind moving across its mouth made a sound so low Cyrus felt it in their back teeth, in the small bones of their ears, in the cage of their ribs where their heart knocked like a fist on a door.

Bramble, riding behind on the mule they'd named Turnip, pulled up beside Wendell and squinted at it.

"That's it? That's the big scary resting place?" Bramble said. "Looks like a finger pointing at heaven. Rude, if you ask me." But their voice came out smaller than usual, and their hands, usually busy picking locks or pockets or scabs, were very still on the reins. Even Bramble could hear it. Even Bramble's bones knew.

Cyrus slid off their own mule and stood in the middle of the trail and let the sound come into them.

*Homecoming,* said a word in their head, in the old language, the language of spirals and stacked figures, the language that lived under their tongue like a stone they had carried so long they had forgotten it was not part of their body. The word for homecoming in that tongue was not a word about arriving. It was a word about *remembering*—a place remembering you, your name coming back to it after long absence. The carvings showed it as a spiral returning to its own center.

Cyrus had never been here. Cyrus knew that absolutely, the way they knew their own heartbeat. And yet the mountain's low note passed through them and something in them answered, some chord struck in a body they did not know they had, and they understood, with a certainty that should have frightened them and instead made them want to weep, that this place had been waiting. That the Choir had sung itself hoarse for centuries calling one name down the generations, and the name had finally walked up the trail on two small legs with dust in its shoes.

*You are late,* said the mountain, gently.

Cyrus signed it before they remembered no one would understand: both hands drawing the spiral inward, closing. Then they carved it instead, quick, into the bark of a wind-twisted pine at the trailside—one of the wood scraps they always carried, though this time the tree itself offered its skin. The spiral returning to center.

Wendell saw. Wendell, who had spent a season learning three words of the old language and had learned them the way Wendell learned everything—stubbornly, thoroughly, with notes scratched on leather cuffs—went pale in the dying light.

"What does it say?" they asked. "What did you carve?"

Cyrus took Wendell's hand—Wendell flinched, then didn't—and pressed their own palm flat against the back of it, and hummed the note the mountain was humming, and watched Wendell's face as the dead in the pack rose up all at once and *answered*, a hundred voices finding the harmony, and Wendell sat down hard on a rock because their knees stopped being interested in standing.

"It says," Wendell managed, when they could speak, "it says welcome home."

* * *

They climbed as the last light failed, because none of them suggested stopping, and no one would have been able to sleep within earshot of that hum anyway. The trail narrowed to a ledge above a gorge full of rushing darkness, and the stars came out hard and cold and close, and the spire grew until it was not a thing they looked at but a thing they were inside the shadow of. Bramble talked the whole way, because Bramble always talked when they were afraid—about the mule, about the innkeeper Odell and his terrible stew, about the magistrate in the city of scales whose bribe money Wendell had counted out coin by agonizing coin—but the talk thinned and thinned until it was just breath, and then just the crunch of boots, and then just the mountain's voice, which was not silence at all but the deepest kind of listening.

The entrance was not a door. It was a wound—or rather, it had been a door once, a great carved mouth in the base of the spire, and someone had tried to close it. Cyrus saw the evidence in the last grey light: rubble shoved against the threshold, the shattered teeth of a portcullis rusting in the stone, scorch marks fanning up the rock face where fire had been set against whatever the Order feared might come out. But stone remembers, and stone had shrugged. The gap stood open, tall enough for five men abreast, breathing its cold sweet air into the night.

Wendell lit a lamp. Its little flame leaned toward the opening as if pulled on a string.

"Dead ahead," Wendell murmured, and then laughed, a shaky, un-Wendell laugh. "Sorry. Bad habit. They're all making jokes. Dead people are apparently very funny when they're excited."

Bramble peered into the dark. "How far down does it go?"

"Down," said Wendell simply. "All the way. It's a chimney, Bramble. A chimney for sound. Everything we say in there is going to be heard by everything that's ever been."

"Great," said Bramble. "Love that. No pressure."

Cyrus stepped through first.

They did not decide to. Their feet decided, and Cyrus, who had spent years learning that other people decided things for them—monks deciding to hide them, soldiers deciding to burn them, a dying brother pressing a whistle into their palms and mouthing *run*—let them. The threshold stone was worn smooth in two parallel paths, exactly boot-width apart, polished by the feet of ten thousand singers walking down to sing. Cyrus's feet fit the paths.

Inside, the lamp-light fell upon walls of carving.

Floor to ceiling. Wall to wall. Up beyond the reach of the light into darkness, down beneath the drifted sand of centuries, every surface of the hollow mountain covered in spirals and stacked figures and running lines of script that flowed like water, dense as rain on a window. It climbed the walls in registers, in bands, the way a story climbs a page. And it was *legible*. Every mark of it. Cyrus stood three steps inside the greatest library of their people's memory and read the first line the way other children read the alphabet off a kitchen beam.

*In the beginning of the sorrow, there was a god who loved the world, and the world forgot it loved back.*

Cyrus made a sound. Small, involuntary, half a gasp, half the ghost of a word—and the mountain took the sound and carried it up and up into the dark overhead, and the dead took it and harmonized it, and the note came back changed, richer, *theirs*, and Cyrus stood trembling in the doorway of the only room that had ever spoken their language fluently, and wept without noise, the way they did everything.

Wendell knelt beside them. Didn't touch. Just knelt, lamp held high, and waited, because Wendell had learned that much at least: that Cyrus's silences were not empty rooms but crowded ones.

"I can't read any of it," Wendell said quietly. "But they can. They're reading it to each other. All of them. Like—like travelers comparing notes on a road they all walked at different times." A pause. "Cyrus. What does it say?"

Cyrus wiped their face on their sleeve, which was filthy, which made it worse, which almost made them laugh. Then they crouched, took out a knife and the flat piece of seasoned birch they had carried since the monastery, and began to carve.

They carved fast, faster than Wendell had ever seen, chips flying, because the story was pouring into them faster than their hands could hold it, and they had to give it away or drown in it.

* * *

By lamplight, in a chamber built to make every scratch of a knife audible to the honored dead, Cyrus told the story of the walls.

They carved it in pieces, holding each scrap up for Wendell and Bramble to see, and while Wendell studied the pictures, the dead translated through the bones in the pack, their voices overlapping, correcting, grieving:

The god had not been born evil. The god had been born *first*—a spirit older than kingdoms, older than the old language, who had loved the world with the whole of an enormous heart, and had given and given and given: rain, spring, mercy, the green push of things through dirt. And the world had taken, and taken, and forgotten to look up. And the forgetting hurt the god the way a wound hurts, except the god was too large to bleed, so the hurt became weather. The hurt became famine. The hurt became a grief the size of a mountain range lying down on the world, and people called it wrath, and prayed against it, and tried to kill it, and every spear thrown at grief only taught it more reasons to grieve.

So the singers had come. That was the second register of the wall: figures with their arms linked, mouths open, a spiral rising from each of them and joining into one great chord above the god's bowed head. Not warriors. Never warriors. The Choir had been chosen from among the grieving themselves—people who had lost what the god had lost, who could sing mourning *with* it instead of *against* it. They had sung it down into sleep the way you sing a child down through a fever: not to silence the crying but to hold it, to keep it company, to promise *you are not forgotten; rest anyway.*

And the sleep was mercy. That was written plainest of all, in letters larger than the rest, and the dead said it loudest: *the god dreams of a world that loves it back, and in the dream, it is true.* To wake it was to take the dream away and hand it the real world again—all the taking, all the forgetting, all the spears—and no one, waking, could survive receiving that much grief at once. The Choir had not imprisoned a monster. The Choir had tucked a wounded thing into bed and posted a watch over its dreams for four thousand years.

The binding needed renewing each century because grief is patient and songs wear out, like rope, like shoes, like bodies. And the renewal needed a living voice—not because the dead lacked power, but because the god had to hear that the living world remembered it. A dead voice was an echo. Only a living voice could be a promise.

There had been many anchors once. The walls recorded their names, hundreds of them, spirals within spirals. Some had served and gone home to plant orchards. Some had served until the serving used them up. One register near the bottom was different—newer cuts, cruder tools—and Cyrus read it twice before understanding, and then had to put the knife down and press both hands over their mouth.

It was a warning. Written by the last generation of anchors, in haste, in fear:

*They will come who want the god awake. They will call it justice, or order, or dawn. Do not hate them. They are grieving too, badly, and grief handled badly becomes a weapon. But do not let them wake it. And know this above all: the song cannot be forced. A stolen voice sings nothing. If they bring an anchor unwilling, the binding will fail and the waking will fail and the god will rise drowning in despair, and despair that size has only one shape. Consent is the hinge of everything. Guard the willing heart.*

Brother Ilex had known. Ilex, mute as Cyrus, writing on slate in a cellar while the world burned above him: *it needs a willing voice.* He hadn't guessed it. He'd read these same walls, or another copy, another teaching. And he had died on the wheels of a prison wagon rather than tell Serle how the song worked—because a man who knows consent is the hinge will die before he teaches you to break hinges.

Cyrus held the last carving up with a steady hand, and Wendell read it, and read it again, and Cyrus watched the meaning move through Wendell's face like weather—understanding, horror, and then something else, something dawning and terrible and resolute, a look Cyrus had never seen on them before and would spend the rest of their life recognizing.

"Willing," Wendell repeated softly. "A voice that chooses." They looked up at the walls, at the thousands of linked singers, at the great bowed figure of the dreaming god, and their hand drifted, without seeming to notice it was doing it, to the collar of their coat—to the flute that hung there, the one they'd been whittling since the valley, the one they said was practice and everyone knew was a letter to the future.

"Well," Wendell said. "Now we know what the song costs. And now we know why they've been failing for a hundred years." A grim almost-smile. "You can't rob a choir. You can only join one."

Above them, faint as frost forming, the dead began to hum the first line of the renewal song—teaching it, offering it up the walls like water—and Cyrus tipped their head back and listened with their whole body, memorizing, and the mountain memorized them back.

* * *

They made camp just inside the mouth of the Spire, where the dead were thick enough to stand watch and the wind couldn't find them. Bramble cooked; the food tasted of nothing, but nobody complained. Cyrus sat with their back against the threshold stone, in the exact spot where ten thousand singers had paused to draw one last ordinary breath before descending, and ran their fingers over the oldest carvings at knee height, the ones worn nearly smooth by touching hands, and felt less afraid than they had since the fire.

This was the strangeness they could not explain and did not need to: they had come home to a place they had never seen. Somewhere in the unbroken chain of anchors—teacher to student, whistle to whistle, palm to palm—the knowledge of this room had lived in Cyrus's bones before Cyrus was born. The monks hadn't invented the songs they sang. They were the last echo of an echo of an echo, and here at the bottom of all the echoes was the original voice, still warm.

Cyrus carved one more thing before sleep, a small piece, held close to the chest: a figure small and standing, a spiral above its head, and beside it the sign that in the old language meant *found*—not lost-and-recovered, but found-as-in-located, as-in-*at last*.

Then the mountain spoke.

Not through the dead. Not through bone. Through the stone itself, through the soles of Cyrus's feet and the seat of their bones, a vibration so deep it was less a sound than a change in what solid things were: a single enormous note rolling up from the depths, from the sleeping thing far below whose dream had begun, tonight, to fray.

The dead went silent—all of them, every ghost in the mountain, mid-hum—as if hushed by a parent's raised hand.

Wendell was on their feet, lamp swinging. "What was that? What *was* that?"

Cyrus pressed their palm flat to the floor and listened with their skin, and what they felt made their blood go cold and clear: the binding, fraying, yes—but fraying *faster than it should*. Faster than a century. Something below was pulling at the song from underneath, worrying it like a dog worries a rope—

And from far away, down the dark throat of the pass they had climbed that day, thin on the wind but unmistakable, came a sound Cyrus knew better than any lullaby: the creak of many wagons, and the ring of iron shod on stone, and voices singing—not the Choir's music, but a marching hymn, harsh and glad.

Torches. Many torches, rounding the shoulder of the mountain, hours earlier than any plan had allowed.

The Order had come early.
