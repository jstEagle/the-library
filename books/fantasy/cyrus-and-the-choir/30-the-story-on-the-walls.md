## 30. The Story on the Walls

The Spire breathed.

That was the only word for it. We had descended for the better part of a day—down stairways cut into the living rock of the mountain, down past ledges where pale fungus gave off a light like drowned moons, down until the air itself seemed to hum against my teeth—and now we stood in a chamber so vast that the torchlight could not find its ceiling. The walls rose around us in a perfect curve, and every inch of them, from the floor where we stood to whatever height the darkness swallowed, was carved.

Not decorated. Carved. Cut deep, in spirals and stacked figures and long lines of that language older than any kingdom, the language Cyrus had been speaking with a knife since the night they arrived at my door mute and hollow-eyed in the rain.

And the chamber breathed. A slow, tidal sound moved through the stone, in and out, in and out, so faint I felt it in my sternum before I heard it with my ears. Like a sleeper's chest. Like something enormous turning over in a dream it could not wake from.

Cyrus stood at the base of the wall with their torch raised, and I watched their face do something I had never seen it do.

They wept without sound. Tears ran freely down their cheeks, and their mouth stayed closed, and their free hand pressed flat against the carved stone as if greeting a wall of their own house after a long exile. Their shoulders shook. They did not make a single noise.

"They can read it," Bramble said quietly behind me. The thief had gone uncharacteristically still since we entered, their usual restless fingers curled into their sleeves. "All of it. They're reading it."

"They're *home*," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended.

Maren was dead two days and I could not yet think her name without the wound opening, so I did the thing I had always done in the valley: I made myself small, and I put my hands to work. I set my pack down. I took out my tools. And I turned to the wall beside me, where the carvings began at a height I could reach, and I waited for Cyrus to teach me what I had spent my whole life refusing to learn—that the world had never been the size of my valley, that the dead had never been only my dead, that the small deliberate life I had built had been a single candle burning in a cathedral I had never once looked up to see.

Cyrus wiped their face with their sleeve. Then they touched the first figure—a tall shape, human in outline but wrong in proportion, its head bowed, its hands open and empty—and looked at me, and pointed at the figure, and pointed at their own chest.

Then they began to carve.

They worked on a flat piece of slate Bramble had pried from a fallen marker near the stair, quick and sure, the way they always carved when the words mattered. I had learned to read some of it by now—the three words they'd taught me on the road, the spirals that meant *listen*, the stacked figures that meant *people, together*. But this was different. This was Cyrus translating a scripture, line by line, and I watched the slate fill and my chest tighten with every figure.

*This is the god,* the carving said. *Not a name. A name would make it smaller than it is.*

*It was born the way gods are born. Out of what everyone felt at once and no one could carry alone.*

I looked at the wall. The tall bowed figure stood alone in the first panel, and around it, smaller figures reached toward it and fell away, reached and fell away, a rhythm of reaching carved in stone.

"It's grief," I said slowly. "The god is—"

Cyrus nodded, urgent, and carved faster.

*It is grief. Not a god of grief. Grief itself, grown so large it became a god. When the first kingdoms burned each other, when the plagues took the cities, when the old forests fell—everyone's mourning went somewhere. Mourning does not vanish. It gathered. It grew a mind, because everything that gathers long enough grows a mind. And it loved the world. That was the worst part. It loved everything it mourned, and its love was a weight that pressed on the living, and the living could not breathe under it, and they called that weight a curse.*

I stood very still. The chamber breathed around us, in and out.

"They called it evil," I said. "The Order. The old stories. The Ashen Order says it's a god of ruin that must be woken to—what was Serle's word—*renewed judgment*? They think waking it is a cleansing."

Cyrus shook their head hard, and pointed at the wall, and carved again with strokes that bit deep enough to chip the slate.

*Lies are comfortable. That is why they last. The truth was written here where no one would come to argue with it.*

*The god never destroyed anything. It only mourned, and its mourning was too heavy, and the world mistook weight for wrath.*

I thought of the dream I had touched, three days ago, when the binding had frayed and my mind had brushed against the sleeping thing beneath us. I had not told the others what I felt there. I had not had words for it. Endless gray, endless sorrow, and beneath it a love so vast and so helpless it had curdled into despair—the mourning of something that remembered every field that had ever been harvested, every dog that had ever died at its master's door, every child, every city, every song, and could do nothing, nothing, nothing but grieve them all at once, forever.

I had felt, for one terrible heartbeat, that it was not a monster at all.

It was a mother at a grave with no end of graves.

"It's not evil," I said aloud, and my voice cracked in the huge breathing dark. "It never was. It's just—" I pressed the heel of my hand against my eyes. "It's grief with nowhere to go. It's all of us, everything we couldn't carry. It's *us*, Cyrus. It's what people become when they love too much and the world keeps taking."

Cyrus took my wrist, gently, and turned me back to the wall, and pointed to the second panel, and I understood: *keep reading. There is more. The truth is not finished.*

* * *

The second panel showed the tall bowed figure no longer alone. Around it now stood a ring of smaller figures, and from their open mouths, carved as thin lines of sound, waves radiated outward and wrapped the great grieving shape like bandages.

"The Choir," I said.

Cyrus carved, quick and certain:

*The Choir was not an army. There was no war. Remember that. Whatever the Order tells you, whatever the old songs say about binding and chains and victory—there was no victory. You cannot defeat grief. You can only sing to it.*

*The first singers were mourners themselves. Women and men and others who had lost everything and found that their loss, shared, made a sound. They discovered that the god could sleep—not because it was forced, but because when they sang, it could finally rest. It had been holding every grief in the world awake and alone. The Choir's song was the first time anything had offered to hold it with it.*

*They did not chain it. They cradled it.*

I looked at the carved waves wrapping the bowed figure, and I saw it now—not a binding, not ropes, not the prison the Order's doctrine described. The lines were gentle. They were the lines of a blanket drawn up over a sleeper's shoulders. They were arms.

"Mercy," I whispered. "They sang it to sleep out of mercy. Not victory. Not war. Mercy."

Cyrus pressed their hand over their heart, then laid that hand flat on the stone. *Yes.* Their eyes were shining again, but their jaw was set, and there was something fierce in their small face now, something that had teeth. They carved on.

*The Order will tell you the Choir was a cage. The Order will tell you the god is a sword that must be drawn. Ask them: who told you a grieving thing is a dangerous thing? Who benefits from a world that is ashamed of mourning?*

*The god asleep is a truth the powerful cannot use. A god of grief that only wanted company—what army can be built on that? What tithe? What fear? So they made it a monster. It is easier to wake a monster and fight it than to sit with a sorrow and share it.*

Bramble had come closer without my noticing. They stood at my elbow, torch tilted, reading Cyrus's slate with their brows drawn together. "My mother used to say the Sleeping God was the reason bad things happened," they said. "Plague, flood, the bad harvest the year I was born. Everyone said it. You didn't curse the weather, you cursed the god for dreaming it." They laughed, but it was a thin sound. "We built a whole religion out of blaming something for being sad."

"We build those every generation," I said. "It's easier than building the other thing."

"Which is?"

"Company."

The word hung in the breathing dark, and the chamber seemed to take it in, the tidal sound swelling faintly, and I felt the dead in my pack stir—not the urgent whispers of the road, not the screams of the ridge, but a low, attentive quiet, like a congregation leaning forward.

* * *

We worked through the panels as the fungus-light dimmed and our torches burned low. Cyrus translated. I carved the translation into my memory the way I carved names into grave-goods—carefully, permanently, because some things must survive the person who learns them.

The third panel showed the centuries. The Choir singing in shifts, generation after generation, the ring of mourners changing faces but never thinning. And then a fourth panel, and here Cyrus's hands slowed, and their breath went shallow, and I knew we had reached the part that concerned us. The part that had cost Brother Ilex his life to tell us. The part written in Maren's blood on the mountainside.

*The song has a rule, and the rule is not cruelty. The rule is honesty.*

*A binding sung by force does not hold. The god knows the difference between a voice that is made to sing and a voice that chooses. It has felt both. In the early centuries, kingdoms tried to bind it with captives—prisoners made to sing until they died, their voices wrung out like wet cloth. The binding frayed within a year, every time. The god does not accept stolen songs. It will not be soothed by a lie.*

*Every renewal requires a voice that chooses. A singer who knows the cost and sings anyway. This is not a cruelty of the Choir. It is a cruelty of truth: you cannot comfort a grief with something half-given. It knows. It has always known.*

I read it twice. Three times. My hands had gone cold, and not from the mountain's deep chill.

"That's what Ilex wrote," I said. "The binding doesn't need any voice. It needs a willing one. That's why the Order has failed for a hundred years—they took the other anchors alive, they tried to *force* the song out of them, and the god wouldn't take it." I looked at Cyrus. "They couldn't wake it, because you can't wake a sleeping thing with a stolen lullaby sung backward. And they can't renew the binding either, because they don't understand the first thing about it. They've been trying to break a lock for a century without knowing it was never a lock."

Cyrus's hand found mine. Small, cold, certain. They did not carve anything for a long moment. Then they set the slate down and carved one figure, slowly, deliberately, into the dust at our feet:

A single open hand. The sign they had taught me on the road. The word for *I choose*.

"Don't," I said, and my voice came out harder than I meant it to. "Cyrus. Don't. Not yet. We don't know—"

But I did know. I had known since the ridge, since I woke bleeding with the dead screaming through every loose bone in my coat. I had known in the fever, carried back toward the valley that could no longer protect me, that the song needed a second voice. The walls said it now in their ancient patient script: the renewal is a duet. One voice to lead, one voice to answer. A living anchor to begin, and a willing voice to complete.

Cyrus looked up at me, and their eyes were dry now, and steady, and old—old the way the walls were old, old the way the language was old. They pointed at the fourth panel. At the final figures carved there: two singers, side by side, their sound-lines braided together into a single cord that wrapped the sleeping god like a mother's arms.

They pointed at the second singer.

They pointed at me.

"No," I said again, but the word had no floor under it. The chamber breathed. The dead in my pack were silent—not absent, I realized, but *listening*, the way they had listened the night Cyrus first knocked, the night the whistle first sang. Waiting to hear what I would say next. Waiting, perhaps, the way the dead always wait: without judgment, without hurry, knowing that the living always arrive at the truth eventually, and that the truth is usually the thing we were avoiding.

I looked at the wall. At the tall bowed figure wrapped in sound. At the ring of mourners across a thousand centuries, each one a name no kingdom recorded, each one a person who had stood where I stood and read what I read and understood what I understood.

They had not been heroes. I could see that in the carving. The figures were not drawn tall or crowned or armed. They were drawn tired. Drawn ordinary. Drawn with the slight stoop of people who had carried things a long way. They had been bone-carvers and shepherds and innkeepers and thieves, I would have wagered my tools on it, ordinary people who loved an ordinary life and were asked, by the plain arithmetic of the world, to give it.

And they had sung anyway. Not because they were brave. Because they had read the truth, and the truth was heavier than the cost, and they could not put it down and walk away. That was the whole secret of the Choir, I thought. It was never an order of the chosen. It was a line of people who refused to live inside a comfortable lie.

I had spent forty years in a valley that refused magic, carving bones for the dead, telling myself the smallness was peace. It had been peace. It had also been a lie—the lie that a life can be small on purpose, that you can opt out, that the world's grief is someone else's weight. The valley had been a candle flame cupped in two hands, and I had called the cupping safety, and it had always, always been fear.

"Cyrus," I said. My voice was steady now. That surprised me. "Teach me the rest. Teach me every word on these walls before the solstice. If I'm going to sing, I want to know the whole song. Not the Order's version. Not the comfortable one. The true one."

Cyrus nodded once. Then they lifted the slate, and began again, and we worked.

* * *

It was Bramble who noticed, near what we guessed was midnight by the slow pulse of the fungus-light, that the chamber's breathing had changed.

"Listen," they said. "Listen to it. It's faster."

They were right. The tidal sound had quickened—not alarmed, not yet, but quickened, the way a sleeper's breath changes when the dream turns. And the dead in my pack had begun, very faintly, to harmonize with it, a low chord I felt in my back teeth, and Cyrus had gone rigid, their head cocked, their whole small body a listening instrument.

Then they grabbed my sleeve and carved, fast, the strokes jagged:

*The song is fraying faster here. Faster than the monastery's count. The calendar was wrong—Ilex said it, we didn't believe him. It is not three days to the solstice. It is less. Maybe one. Maybe tomorrow.*

And then, as if the stone itself wished to confirm it, a sound came drifting down the stairway we had climbed all day—a sound thin and far but unmistakable, carried on the Spire's strange resonance so that every footfall rang like a bell:

Boots. Many boots. Descending.

Bramble snuffed their torch with their bare hand, hissing at the burn. In the fungus-dark, Cyrus's eyes found mine, wide and certain, and the walls rose around us floor to unseen ceiling, every inch of them telling the truth no one had wanted, and somewhere above us the Ashen Order was coming down to wake a god that had never been a monster, and the song had maybe one day left, and the second voice it needed was standing in the dark with carving knives in his belt and forty years of smallness in his chest.

I picked up my pack. The dead inside it began, very softly, to sing.
