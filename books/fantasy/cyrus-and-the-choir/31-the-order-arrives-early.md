## 31. The Order Arrives Early

Bramble had stolen a great many things in their life—bread, knives, a silver hairpin off the ear of a woman who turned out to be a magistrate's wife, which was how Bramble had learned that some thefts cost more than they paid—but they had never before tried to steal time. That was, Bramble realized somewhere between the third watch and the fourth, exactly what this whole miserable night was: an attempt to steal an hour from the Ashen Order and hand it to two people descending into the dark belly of a singing mountain.

It wasn't working.

The first warning came as a sound Bramble almost didn't recognize, because it was too ordinary. A pebble, dislodged high on the Spire's flank, ticking down the switchback road like a knuckle rapped politely on a door. Bramble had spent nine years listening for footsteps that weren't theirs, and no trained soldier ever made a noise that small on purpose. Only tired men made noises that small. Tired men marching through the last hours of the night because some commander three days' ride behind them had sent word: *take the Spire at dawn, take it before the delay can matter.*

"They're early," Bramble breathed, and hated how their voice cracked on it.

The sentry post was a shelf of rock above the Spire's mouth, where the mountain's hollow throat began—a black archway tall enough to swallow a chapel whole, its edges carved so densely with spirals and stacked figures that the stone looked less like stone than like frozen rain. Below it, the valley exiles were bedded down among the boulders with their absurd arsenal: scythes, hay-forks, wood-axes, one actual sword belonging to a man named Corvin who had been a soldier before he'd been a fugitive, back when his name had been something else. Maren stood at the center of them like a fence post driven deep, arms folded, watching the dark the way she watched weather.

Bramble slid down the scree toward her, loose-jointed and silent, the way they'd learned to move through cities where being heard meant being caught or killed. Maren heard them anyway. She always did.

"How many?" she said, without turning.

"Can't count 'em yet. But the pebbles are talking." Bramble crouched beside her. "More than twenty. Could be fifty. They're not trying to be quiet, Maren. They're just *tired*."

Maren nodded slowly, and Bramble saw her doing arithmetic with faces—the same arithmetic she'd done in the valley every market day, deciding who could spare what. "Then we have until the sky goes gray," she said. "Wake Ilex's fire-watch. Get the wounded up the north shelf where they can see but not be seen. And get word down to Wendell."

Bramble flinched. "Down *there*? You want me to go down there?"

"I want someone to go down there who can move quiet and lie fast. That's you, thief."

"It's Bramble," they said automatically, and then, because the joke landed flat even on their own ears, "Right. Yes. Going."

* * *

Inside, the Spire was a throat, and the throat was full of song.

Bramble had felt it from outside since the evening they'd arrived—a hum in the teeth, a tremor underfoot, the sense of standing inside a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing after a hundred years. But descending past the first galleries, the hum became music. The walls rose on either side carved floor to ceiling in Cyrus's language, ten thousand spirals, and the air moving through them made each groove sing a different note, low and layered, a chord so vast you couldn't hear its edges. Every footstep added to it. Every breath. It was the most beautiful thing Bramble had ever been terrified of.

Wendell found them at the second landing, lantern raised, face hollowed by two sleepless nights and the wound along their ribs that the valley healers had stitched with thread pulled from Bramble's own coat hem, which Wendell had apologized for twice.

"The vanguard," Bramble said. "Dawn, maybe sooner. Fifty, maybe more. Serle's not with them—I'd bet my other boot on it. These are the ones who didn't wait for him."

Something moved behind Wendell's eyes—not fear exactly. Arithmetic again. Everyone in this story was doing arithmetic with faces. "Cyrus is already at the lower gallery," Wendell said. "Reading the walls. I won't drag them out; dragging them out would only prove the Order right about what we're doing here." They looked past Bramble, up the throat of the Spire, toward the distant gray promise of dawn. "Tell Maren we need whatever time she can buy. An hour. Half an hour. Ten minutes. Whatever she gives us, we'll spend it."

"And if she runs out?"

Wendell's hand went to the flute at their belt—the bone whistle, pale as milk, that had started all of this. "Then she runs out knowing why."

Bramble climbed back up through the singing dark and thought, not for the first time, that Wendell Dunmore had the calmest voice of any person Bramble had ever watched walk toward their own death, and that this was somehow worse than screaming.

* * *

Dawn came up bruise-colored over the eastern ridge, and with it came the Order.

They came in column, gray cloaks bleached nearly white by the early light, spears slanted, boots striking the switchback road in unison—and the Spire *took* the sound of them. That was the first horror of it. Each bootfall echoed up the hollow mountain and came back changed: forty men marching became four hundred, became a heartbeat, became a drum played by something enormous and patient underground. Bramble watched the front ranks falter as they heard themselves multiplied, saw a young soldier look down at his own feet as though they'd betrayed him.

"Steady!" called the vanguard commander, a broad man with a voice like a slammed door. "It's echo! Nothing but echo!"

He was wrong, and Bramble, flattened behind a boulder thirty feet from him, knew he was wrong. It wasn't echo. Echo repeats what you give it. This took what you gave it and gave it *back larger*, gave it harmony, gave it meaning. When the commander shouted his order to advance, the shout rolled into the archway and returned as a chord—his own voice braided with voices he hadn't spoken, deep ones, old ones, so many of them stacked that the single syllable bloomed into something like hymn. Several soldiers crossed themselves. One dropped his spear.

And beneath it all, faint but rising, the dead were beginning to answer.

Bramble had carried bones for weeks now—loose vertebrae and finger-bones in their coat lining, souvenirs of a life they'd never expected to lead—and lately those bones had begun to whisper at odd moments, murmuring directions, warnings, once a complaint about Bramble's cooking. Now, as the vanguard pressed toward the Spire's mouth, every whisper in Bramble's coat swelled to a voice, and every voice joined the chord rolling out of the mountain, and the effect was that of standing inside an organ while someone played it with armies.

"Beautiful, isn't it," said Corvin beside them, drawing his one true sword.

"No," said Bramble honestly. "Yes. Both. Shut up."

The exiles let the first rank pass the outer boulders. That was Maren's plan, such as it was—not victory, never victory, only *time*. Let them come into the kill-ground, then fall on the column's joints the way water falls on a wagon axle: not to break it, only to slow it. Maren herself stood on the highest rock with a hay-fork gripped like a halberd, and when she gave the cry, the Spire caught that too, and her voice came back off the walls as a chorus, dozens of Marens, hundreds, a whole congregation of stubborn old women shouting as one—

—and the battle began.

Bramble had seen violence before. City violence: quick, squalid, over in three breaths with someone bleeding in an alley. This was nothing like that. This was violence conducted inside an instrument. Every clash of steel rang up the hollow galleries and returned harmonized, so that the fight sounded orchestrated, sounded *intended*. A soldier screamed as Corvin's blade found him and his scream came back as five voices screaming in perfect fifths, terrible and gorgeous, and Bramble watched two of his comrades hesitate—not from mercy, but from awe, from the animal recognition that they were standing inside something holy and desecrating it with every step.

A scythe took a man's legs out from under him and his cry of pain rolled down into the depths, down toward wherever Wendell and Cyrus were, and came back up changed again—softened, mournful, sung. The dead were taking the Order's agony and making music of it, and Bramble could not decide if that was mercy or mockery, and suspected it was neither, and suspected it was simply what the Choir had always done: taken everything, every voice, living and dead, willing and unwilling, and woven it into the song that kept the sleeping god asleep.

*Even your screams serve,* the mountain seemed to say. *Especially your screams.*

"Fall back to the second line!" Maren's voice, raw now, hoarse from shouting against her own echoes. The exiles gave ground grudgingly, in the manner of people defending not territory but *people*—every yard bought so that somewhere below, a mute child could keep reading the walls.

Bramble fought the way they did everything: badly, sideways, and by cheating. They'd taken a short blade off a dead rider at the ford and mostly used it to threaten rather than cut, darting between boulders, tripping one gray cloak into another, shouting false orders in a pitch-perfect imitation of the vanguard commander—"Left flank, wheel left, they're coming round the ridge!"—and the Spire faithfully amplified the lie into a hundred lies, and half a squad actually wheeled left, into empty rock, buying thirty seconds nobody had planned on.

The commander figured it out eventually. He figured it out by watching Bramble's mouth.

"You," he roared, and the mountain made it a cathedral of *you*, and he came on like a door falling, sword high, and Bramble did the only sensible thing, which was to run, and the only stupid thing, which was to run uphill instead of down, and might have died there on the rocks in full view of everyone if a hay-fork hadn't come out of nowhere and hooked the man's ankle out from under him.

Maren stood over him as he scrambled up, breathing hard, fork leveled. Around them the battle had gone strangely still at its edges, both sides catching breath, the chord overhead sustaining itself without them.

"Go home," Maren told the commander, and her voice, small and cracked as it was, got picked up and carried and multiplied until it seemed to come from the mountain itself. "*Go home.*"

For one strange suspended moment, Bramble thought he might. He looked at the carved archway, at the light of dawn pouring into it, at his own soldiers' upturned faces lit with something that was not zealotry anymore—it had been washed off them by the music, scrubbed away like ash in rain—and his sword arm sagged.

Then the horns sounded from the switchback below. More of them. A second column, fresh, running, and behind it banners: not the vanguard's plain gray but the white-and-black of the Order's inner circle, and at its head a palanquin borne on eight shoulders.

Reinforcements. Not Serle's delayed company—someone had ignored his delay entirely. Someone very high up had decided the Spire would be taken today, whatever Serle had seen, whatever Serle feared, whatever Serle had tried to buy with a wasted day.

The commander's spine straightened. His sword came back up. And Maren, watching the new banners climb the road, did the arithmetic one final time, and Bramble saw the sum land in her face, and hated it, and understood it completely.

"Maren," Bramble said. "Don't—"

"Get below," she said quietly. "Both of you. Tell Wendell the door stays shut as long as it can. Tell them—" She paused, and for the first time since Bramble had known her, the old woman smiled, quick and crooked as a knife. "Tell them the valley sends its regards."

She turned to the exiles, to the farmers and deserters and debtors and thieves who had followed her down off their mountain with farming tools and old grudges, and she raised the hay-fork, and the Spire gathered its breath around her.

"Hold the mouth!" she cried, and the mountain sang it back in a hundred voices, and the second column crested the ridge into the dawn, and Bramble ran for the dark of the archway as the world behind them came apart into terrible, beautiful noise.
