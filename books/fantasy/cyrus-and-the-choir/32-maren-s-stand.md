## 32. Maren's Stand

Maren had buried three husbands, two children, and one entire life before she came to the valley, and she had learned this much from all of it: grief does not make you strong. Grief makes you stubborn. Strength is a gift some people are given. Stubbornness is a choice you make every morning when you open your eyes and decide that today, again, you will not lie down.

She opened her eyes on the morning of the battle and made the choice one more time.

The Hollow Spire stood against the graying sky like a finger raised in accusation, its peak lost in cloud, its base swallowed by a ring of black pines. The road to it climbed through a narrow defile between two shoulders of rock—a throat of stone, Wendell had called it, and Wendell was right about most things, though they'd deny it. At the mouth of that throat, where the mountain narrowed to a passage barely six carts wide, the valley exiles made their stand.

There were nineteen of them. Maren counted twice, as if the number might improve with attention. It did not.

Nineteen farmers, a cooper, two disgraced soldiers, a woman who had once poisoned a magistrate and fled rather than hang for it, a boy of sixteen named Tally who had lied about his age so convincingly that even his mother had stopped arguing. They held scythes with straightened blades. They held wood-axes and mattocks and the two swords the ex-soldiers had kept oiled through years of disuse, wrapped in sacking beneath floorboards, waiting for nothing in particular. They held old grudges, which Maren privately suspected were sharper than any of the blades.

And they held the line because Wendell Dunmore—who could not fight, who went gray at the sight of blood, whose hands were made for carving doves and flutes and small mercies—had gone down into the dark beneath the mountain with a mute child, to sing the world back to sleep.

"Somebody has to hold the door," Maren had said, and nobody had laughed, and nobody had argued, and that was how she knew they understood.

* * *

Dawn came up the defile like water filling a bowl—slow, then all at once.

Maren stood at the center of the line, her walking stick planted before her feet, her good knife—the one she'd used for gutting fish for forty years—in her right hand. She had put her hair up. It seemed important. If today was going to be the last day anyone saw her face, she wanted them to see it properly, not hanging loose and wild like some madwoman from a ballad.

Tally stood to her left, breathing too fast, gripping his father's billhook. To her right, old Hesta the poisoner had arranged herself with the calm of someone who had already died once, metaphorically speaking, and found it survivable.

"They'll come up the road," Hesta said. "They always come up the road. Soldiers are like water. They take the easiest path and pretend it was their idea."

"And we're the stone," Maren said.

"We're the stone," Hesta agreed. "Stones don't win. Stones just make the water go around, and by then the thing the water wanted is done."

"That's all I'm asking for," Maren said. "Done."

The first sound of the Order was not trumpets or drums. It was boots. Hundreds of boots, striking stone in unison, a rhythm like a heart beating inside the mountain. The sound rolled up the defile ahead of them, and Maren felt it in the soles of her feet, in her back teeth, in the scar tissue over her ribs where a husband's fist had landed thirty years ago.

Then they came around the bend, and the dawn showed them whole: gray cloaks, gray steel, gray faces beneath gray hoods, rank upon rank upon rank, filling the road from wall to wall like floodwater. At their head rode a figure on a white horse—even the horse looked gray in that light—and behind him, banners bearing the ash-white circle that meant, in whatever language the Order spoke, *the world must burn clean*.

"Hesta," Maren said quietly.

"Yes."

"How many?"

"More than us."

"Good," Maren said. "I'd hate for it to be embarrassing."

Beside her, Tally made a small sound that might have been laughter or might have been something else. Maren reached over without looking and gripped the back of his neck, the way she'd gripped her son's neck a hundred years ago, or five, time being what it was.

"Listen to me," she said. "When it starts, you don't be brave. Bravery is for people with nothing to lose. You be *stubborn*. You plant your feet and you make them pay for every hand's width of this road, and when it gets too bad, you run—not away, but down. Down past the tree line, around to the eastern shelf, and you find another way to stand. Do you hear me? The door only needs to hold long enough. After that, holding it is just dying with extra steps."

"You'll run too?" Tally said, and his voice cracked on it, and Maren loved him so fiercely for the cracking that for a moment she couldn't speak.

"I'm sixty-eight years old, boy," she said. "I've been running downhill my whole life. Today I run uphill."

* * *

The Order's commander rode forward alone, close enough that Maren could see the stitching on his hood, fine and precise, the work of someone who believed that even terror should be well-made.

"In the name of the Ashen Order," he called, and his voice carried unnaturally well, pitched by training or by something less natural, "stand aside. Your quarrel is not with you. We seek only what descends into the Spire."

"Then you've come a long way for disappointment," Maren called back.

The commander tilted his head. "Old woman. Go home."

"I am home," Maren said. "You're the one standing in my doorway."

Something moved through the ranks of the Order—not laughter, nothing so human, but a kind of collective settling, like a wave deciding to break. The commander raised his hand.

And the dead rose.

Not the Order's dead—they hadn't any yet—but the valley's. Maren felt it before she understood it: a pressure change, a cold that came up from the ground instead of down from the sky. All along the line of exiles, the air thickened, shimmered, took on shapes. The valley had been collecting ghosts for generations—every exile who had climbed the pass to escape a hanging, a burning, a debt, a shame; every one who had died in its quiet houses and quiet beds believing the world outside would never want them back. The valley refused magic, but it had never refused the dead. Perhaps, Maren thought, with the strange clarity of the last minutes, that was the same thing. Perhaps the dead were simply magic that had learned patience.

They rose out of the stones of the defile like frost forming in reverse. Maren saw the ghost of a man she'd shared a roof with for nine bitter years, standing at her left shoulder with his fists still clenched around an anger he'd never spent. She saw others—faces she knew from the graveyard on the hill, faces she knew only from stories, faces older than stories. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Every exile the valley had ever sheltered, risen now because the valley itself, the patient null thing, had finally been asked for help—or perhaps because Wendell, down in the dark, had asked.

The Order's ranks faltered. Horses screamed. And Maren heard, threading through the panic, a sound she would carry with her wherever she went next: singing. Faint, vast, coming from everywhere and nowhere—from the bones of the mountain itself. The Choir, awake below, answering the dead above.

"NOW," Maren roared, in the voice she'd used to call pigs home and sons to dinner and neighbors to witness, and the exiles surged forward into the gap between the living and the dead, and the battle began.

* * *

It was not a battle like the songs describe. There was no space for glory in that throat of stone. It was shoving and screaming and the wet shock of steel finding flesh, and it was over patches of ground measured in boot-lengths. The exiles fought the way they had lived: stubbornly, badly, ferociously, together. Hesta's knives found throats with the economy of long practice. The two ex-soldiers anchored the center and died there, back to back, taking eleven of the Order with them. Tally swung his billhook with his eyes shut half the time and somehow stayed alive, and Maren stayed near him without admitting she was staying near him, killing two men who came for him and telling herself afterward it had been tactical.

The ghosts did the rest. They could not grip, could not cut—but they could *be*, sudden and terrible, filling a soldier's vision with his own mother's face, with the faces of everyone he had burned, and men dropped their weapons and ran, or froze, or fell to their knees weeping, and the exiles finished what the dead started.

For an hour—it could not have been more than an hour, though Maren lived three lifetimes in it—they held. The Order pressed and pressed and the throat of stone stayed shut, and somewhere far below, faint through the rock under Maren's feet, she felt a humming begin. A song starting. Cyrus's song. Wendell's.

"It's working," Tally gasped beside her, bleeding from the ear, grinning like a fool. "Maren, it's working—"

That was when the Order stopped trying to walk through the door and started trying to burn it down.

They had saved their fire for exactly this, Maren realized—the disciplined core, the ones who hadn't broken, drawing back to loose arrows wrapped in pitch and prayer. The first volley came arcing over the press of bodies, trailing flame, and where the fire struck, the ghosts recoiled with a sound like a chord snapping. Fire didn't kill the dead. But it blinded them, scattered them, thinned them like smoke in wind, and under cover of the burning, the Order's reserve came on in a solid wedge, fresh troops against exhausted farmers.

Maren took stock in the space between two heartbeats. Nineteen had become seven. Seven, against a wedge. The song below was growing stronger; she could feel it climbing through her boots, and it needed minutes, only minutes—

"Tally," she said. "Eastern shelf. Now. That's not a request."

"But—"

"*Now.*"

He ran. She watched him go with the corner of her eye and turned back to the wedge with her fish-knife in her hand and her hair still neatly pinned, and she planted her feet in the center of the road like a stone that had decided, this morning and every morning, not to lie down.

The first soldier she killed, she killed cleanly. The second cost her a gash along her forearm. The third was the one who got her—a spearman, quick and professional, driving the point in under her ribs while she was busy with his friend. She looked down at it with something like annoyance. Of all the ways. Of all the stupid, ordinary ways.

But she was Maren Vane, née nothing, daughter of no one, founder of nothing, keeper of a valley that refused miracles, and she did not fall forward. She grabbed the spear shaft with both hands and pulled it deeper, pulling the man off balance, and drove her fish-knife up under his chin, and they went down together onto the trampled snow, and she lay there with the spear in her and the sky going strange and silver above her, and she thought: *long enough.* Whatever happened next, it had been long enough.

Around her, the battle dimmed. But the dead did not dim. The dead gathered.

They came to her out of the stones and the smoke and the long history of the valley—every exile, every ghost, the whole stubborn congregation of the refused—and they knelt, or the ghost equivalent of kneeling, which was a kind of bowing-down of light. And Maren, dying on the road to the Hollow Spire, felt them take hold of her voice. Not her breath; her breath was failing. Her *voice*. The thing that had called pigs and sons and neighbors and councils and, this morning, nineteen scared people to stand in a doorway. They lifted it out of her the way you lift a coal from a dying fire, cupped in careful hands.

And they carried it down.

She felt it go—felt her own voice descend through the mountain like water finding a crack, through stone and root and hollow dark, past the fighting and the fire, down and down and down to where a bone-carver and a mute child stood before a sleeping god. She could not follow it. But she knew where it was going. She knew it the way you know a letter has arrived, the way a valley knows its own echo.

Her last thought was not of her husbands or her children or her long, hard, chosen life. Her last thought was of Wendell Dunmore, who had never once in all their years in the valley asked her for anything, hearing her voice arrive in the deep of the mountain like a neighbor knocking at dusk:

*Hold the door,* she sent after it, with the last of everything she had. *We held yours.*

* * *

Far above, the Order's wedge broke against the ghosts' final surge and spilled back down the defile, leaderless, the commander's banner fallen in the snow. Tally, weeping on the eastern shelf, saw none of it. He saw only the mountain—and heard, rising up through its roots, a new voice join the song below. An old woman's voice, rough and clear and utterly unafraid, singing words no living throat taught it, in harmony with a carver and a child and a choir ten thousand years asleep.

The god stirred in its dreaming. And the door, though its keeper had fallen, held.
