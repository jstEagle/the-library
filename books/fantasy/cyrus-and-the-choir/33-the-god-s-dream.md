## 33. The God's Dream

The stairs went down farther than the mountain was tall.

That was not possible—Wendell had stood at the Spire's mouth and looked up its broken crown against the dawn sky—but the walls told them otherwise as they descended, spiraling inward through stone that hummed like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Cyrus walked ahead with a lamp, small feet sure on steps worn into shallow crescents by ten thousand years of bare feet. Behind them came Bramble, uncharacteristically silent, one hand on the wall as though the stone might be a friend in the dark.

And behind Bramble came the dead.

Wendell carried them all now—not just the bones in their pack but every voice the Spire had gathered on their approach, pouring itself into them like water finding a low place. The dead of the monastery. The dead of the ford, including the man whose blood still ghosted beneath Wendell's fingernails no matter how they scrubbed. The dead of the valley, faint and far, holding Maren's voice among them like an ember cupped in many hands. They were not whispering anymore. Whispering had been the valley's gift, the hush it wrapped around everything so that even ghosts spoke softly. Here there was no need for softness. Here the dead were *listening*.

"Carver." It was old Hesta's voice, or something wearing her cadence—the miller woman who'd taught Wendell to read the grain of bone thirty years ago. "The walls are thinning. Can you feel it? The song is worn through."

"I feel it," Wendell said, and their voice came out smaller than they wanted.

They had felt it for two days now, since the ambush on the ridgeline, since Serle's riders took Cyrus and left Wendell bleeding in the heather. Even before that—since the first night at the inn, when Cyrus hummed three notes and every bone in Wendell's pack had sung back. The binding was fraying the way a rope frays: invisibly, then suddenly, then all at once. And beneath them, under miles of resonating stone, something vast turned over in its sleep and murmured.

Cyrus stopped on the landing below. Turned. Held up the lamp, and in its light their face was grave and ancient and nine years old, all three at once. They pointed down, then pressed both hands flat over their own heart, then made the gesture Wendell had learned on the road—the spiral closing inward, the sign for *coming home* or *ending*, depending on which dead tongue you trusted.

"We're close," Bramble translated unnecessarily, because translating was how they kept their nerve. "I mean. Obviously we're close. You don't build stairs this long unless you're going somewhere terrible."

"Somewhere holy," Wendell corrected quietly.

"Same thing, mostly," Bramble said. "Terrible things are always holy. That's how you know."

* * *

The last door was not a door.

It was a membrane of sound—a curtain of air so saturated with held music that walking through it felt like wading into deep water. Wendell felt it part around them, felt the pressure of a thousand centuries of sustained note slide across their skin, and then they were through, standing on a ledge above the hollow heart of the mountain.

The chamber was a throat, or a womb, or a bell cast from living rock. Its walls rose out of lamplight into darkness, and every visible inch of them was carved—the language older than kingdoms, the language Cyrus spoke with their hands, covering the stone in rivers and terraces of script. The carvings caught the lamp's glow and seemed to move, shadows shifting in the grooves like fish under ice. At the chamber's center, suspended by nothing Wendell could name, hung a single point of pale light. Not fire. Not starlight. Something older than either.

And beneath it, coiled in the dark below the ledge where no eye could follow, the god slept.

Wendell could not see it. That was the horror and the mercy both. But they could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth, the way you feel a great animal breathing in a stable at night—vast lungs drawing slow, slow breath, each inhalation lasting minutes, the whole chamber swelling and settling around it. The pale light pulsed with that breath. So did the carved walls. So, Wendell realized with a lurch, did their own heartbeat, dragged out of rhythm until it matched the sleeping thing below, two hearts keeping time across the gulf.

"The binding's here." Bramble crouched at the ledge's edge, peering down, and their bravado had gone thin as paper. "The song. It's the song, isn't it? The whole mountain's a—" They waved a hand helplessly. "A bowl. A bowl that keeps the note going."

"A choir loft," Wendell said.

Below the ledge, half-sunk into the floor, lay the altar: a basin of black stone shaped like an open mouth, ringed by seven pillars each carved with a figure—seven figures, Wendell saw, seven singers frozen mid-song, their faces worn smooth by time or by touch. Six of the figures were intact. The seventh had crumbled to a stump, and from the stump, hairline cracks ran outward across the floor, spreading like frost, like veins, like the branches of a tree that had begun to die.

Cyrus knelt at the seventh pillar and laid their palm against the broken figure, and Wendell watched the child's shoulders shake once, twice, and go still.

"They knew them," Wendell said softly. "The last anchor before them. Whoever sang here last."

Hesta's voice stirred somewhere near Wendell's collarbone, in the flute of river-bone they'd carried since the valley. *"We all knew them. We have always known them, carver. That is the cruelty of the Choir. Every singer knows the ones who sang before. None of them ever get to rest beside them."*

The dead had never said so much at once. The Spire was loosening them, Wendell thought—or the fraying binding was. Either way, the voices were rising like floodwater, and Wendell gripped the pack straps and let them rise, because there was nothing else to do, and because some stubborn bone-deep instinct said that whatever happened next, they would want witnesses.

Then the god dreamed, and Wendell fell into it.

* * *

There was no warning. One breath the world was stone and lamplight; the next it was—

Rain.

Rain falling on a coastline that no longer existed, on cliffs green with forests that had been cut for timber four thousand years ago, and Wendell was seeing it through eyes the size of weather. Below the cliffs, a village of round houses, smoke rising from cooking fires, children running down to the shallows to scream with delight at a stranded whale. The whale was dying. The children didn't know. Their joy was enormous and innocent and it broke something open in the vast watching mind, and the mind reached down—

—and the rain stopped. The sea stilled. The whale breathed.

The children screamed louder, with terror now instead of delight, and the god flinched, and the whale died anyway, because grief cannot heal what it touches, only hold it.

Wendell gasped and was themselves again, on the ledge, knees hitting stone. Bramble had them by the shoulders. "Hey. Hey! Carver. What happened, what did you see?"

But the dream wasn't done with them. It came again, gentler now, like a hand testing whether the visitor could bear it:

A city on a river delta, white-walled, loud with market noise. The god walked through it—no, the god *was* it, the god was the ground under the paving stones and the water in the cisterns and the wind off the harbor, and the people lived their lives on top of it the way lichen lives on rock, unaware, ungrateful, alive. A woman gave birth in a house the god was holding up, and the god bent close to hear the first cry, hungry for it, starving for it, and the newborn's cry rang through the god like a bell through bronze and it was the most beautiful sound in ten thousand years and the mother never knew. No one ever knew. That was the whole wound. That was the entire wound: *no one ever knew.*

*"Stop,"* Wendell whispered, and the dream stopped, and they understood suddenly why the Order called it a monster, because a hunger that large looks like hunger for anything, for everything, for dominion. But it wasn't hunger for power.

It was hunger to be *witnessed*.

"It mourns," Wendell said. Their voice cracked in the middle of the word. They wiped their face with a sleeve and found it already wet. "Bramble, it's mourning. It has been mourning since before our languages were born. It loved this world—it *held* it up, it heard every birth, every death, it kept the rain kind and the whales from beaching when it could—and then the Choir sang it down, and the world forgot it was ever there, and it's been dreaming alone in the dark ever since. Ten thousand years of remembering things no one else remembers."

"That's—" Bramble swallowed. "That's not what the Order says. The Order says it's wrath. Sleeping wrath. Fire under the mountain waiting to—"

"I know what they say." Wendell looked down into the dark where the vast lungs drew their slow breath. "I've spent my whole life listening to the dead, and I'm telling you what I heard just now. It doesn't want to burn the world. It wants the world to say its name. And those aren't the same thing—except they will be. Except they will be, if it wakes up into a world that forgot it, because grief that size with nowhere to go becomes rage, and rage that size becomes fire, and then everyone who ever told the story wrong will be right."

The truth of it settled over them with terrible weight. This was why the Ashen Order wanted it awake—they would tell their recruits it was a war-god, a judge, a cleansing flame, order rising from chaos. Some of them might even believe it. But Wendell had seen the dreams now, and the dreams were of rain on green cliffs and a newborn's cry, and the god waking would not bring judgment. It would bring a wounded animal the size of the sky opening its eyes in a room full of strangers and asking, in the only voice it had, *do you remember me?*—and when no one answered, because no one would, because the forgetting had become the shape of the world—

"Fire," Wendell said. "Not because it's evil. Because it's heartbroken. The Order thinks they're unleashing a weapon. They're actually going to break a heart so large that the breaking ends everything." They laughed, and it came out ragged. "And the Choir—the original Choir—they weren't conquerors either. Look at the walls. Look at what Cyrus reads there. They didn't bind it out of fear. They bound it out of *mercy*. They put it to sleep the way you sing a grieving friend to sleep after a funeral. So it could stop feeling it. So it could rest."

"And now the song's ending," Bramble said quietly, "and it's starting to wake up, and it's going to feel all of it again. All at once. Everything it was dreaming to escape."

"Yes."

"Gods." Bramble sat back on their heels. "No wonder they needed a willing voice. You can't force someone to be compassionate. You can't chain mercy into place. It has to—" They stopped, looking at Wendell sideways. "It has to be given."

Wendell didn't answer. Down at the altar, Cyrus still knelt at the broken pillar, and the lamplight moved on the carved walls, and the god breathed.

* * *

The fraying came while they rested, eating hard bread and dried apples at the chamber's edge like travelers at any roadside, because the body insists on its small hungers even at the end of the world.

It began as a change in the hum. For two days the mountain had held its note, worn but steady, the way an old singer holds a pitch through a long phrase. Now the note shivered. Now it bent. Wendell felt it in their molars, in the plates of their skull, in the bones of their hands—and then they felt it everywhere at once, because the binding let go of another strand and the god's dreaming poured through the gap like light through a torn curtain.

This time Wendell didn't fight it. They closed their eyes and stepped in.

Frost on a window, melting. A dog dead in spring, buried under an apple tree, and the god had attended the burial unseen, had felt the small grief of the family as they shoveled, had treasured it—treasured even grief, even small human grief, because it was proof the humans felt things, proof the world it loved was still tender enough to hurt. A wedding. A ship lost at sea with forty souls, and the god had held the drowning sailors' last thoughts the way a miser holds coins, unable to save them, refusing to let them vanish unwitnessed. Wars. Famines. Ten thousand years of watching everything it loved hurt itself and each other and never once being able to explain, never once being able to knock on a door and say *I am here, I have always been here, I held your grandmother's grandmother when she died alone and she was not alone.*

*"It's not a monster,"* Wendell said aloud, inside the dream or outside it, they couldn't tell. *"It never was. It's the loneliest thing that ever lived."*

And the god heard them.

The vast attention turned—there was no other word for it—and fixed on the small bright speck that was Wendell Dunmore, bone-carver, exile, murderer-of-one-man, and Wendell felt themselves examined the way they examined a fresh-killed femur: turned in the light, weighed, tested for what could be made of them. Then, with a delicacy that undid them completely, the dream changed one more time.

It showed Wendell the valley.

Not as it was—as it had been watched. Every dusk storm that had rattled the workshop shutters, the god had felt the rattle and wondered who sheltered inside. Every winter Wendell had sat alone carving, the god had known there was warmth in that one small house in the magicless valley, one flame of aliveness burning down in the dark, and had listened to it the way a starving man listens to a feast through a wall. The null valley—the one place in the world where the god's awareness thinned to almost nothing—and even there, even at the very edge of its reach, it had strained toward Wendell like a plant toward a window.

*You,* the dream said, without words. *You were never forgotten by me.*

Wendell opened their eyes. Tears ran freely down their face; they didn't bother with them. Bramble was staring. Cyrus was standing, lamp raised, and on the child's face was an expression Wendell had never seen there before—hope stretched so thin it trembled, hope that dared nothing, hope holding itself perfectly still the way you hold still when a wild bird lands on your hand.

"Cyrus," Wendell said. Their voice came out wrecked and steady at once. "It knows you're here. It's been waiting for you. Not the way the Order waits—like the Order waits to take something. Like—" They searched for it and found it, and the finding hurt worse than anything on the road here. "Like the dead wait in the bones. Patiently. Hoping someone will pick them up."

Cyrus crossed the chamber floor to the altar, small feet sure on the ancient stone, and stood at the mouth-shaped basin, and looked down into the dark where the god breathed, and lifted their hands.

And began to carve the air—no, to *sign* it, the language older than kingdoms flowing through their fingers faster than Wendell had ever seen, and the walls answered, every carved line on every surface beginning to glow with a faint gold pulse that beat in time with the god's slow heart, and the humming note climbed and clarified and became, unmistakably, the first syllable of a song.

But the song stalled. Hung. The gold light flickered like a guttering lamp, and Cyrus's hands faltered, and Wendell saw the problem written plain in the child's straining face: one voice. One voice was not enough. It had never been enough—that was the whole architecture of the thing, seven pillars, seven singers, a chord and not a note—and the seventh pillar lay broken, and the binding was a rope of one strand now, and one strand does not hold.

The god's breath hitched. The whole chamber hitched with it. Somewhere overhead, muffled by a mile of stone, came the first distant sounds of the Order's vanguard arriving at the Spire's mouth—shouts, the ring of weapons, and beneath them Maren's exiles raising their farming-tools-and-grudges battle cry, buying seconds with their lives.

Seconds. Wendell counted what they had and found it almost none.

The song needed a second voice. The walls said so; the dead in Wendell's pack said so, all of them at once now, urgent and clear: *a willing voice, freely given, the hinge of everything.* And Wendell stood at the edge of the altar with the knowledge settling into them the way cold settles into wet cloth—from the outside in, slowly, then all together—and understood exactly what *willing* meant, what it cost, what it would leave behind. Their hands. Their workshop. The smell of bone dust and beeswax. Cyrus learning to whistle on the road, laughing without sound. The valley. The morning light on the bench where they'd worked for thirty years, content in their deliberate smallness, aching underneath.

All of it, traded for a song.

Above them, the battle at the Spire's mouth grew louder. Below them, the god turned its ruined, hopeful, endless attention upward, waiting.

Wendell took off their coat, folded it, and set it on the stone—carefully, the way they set down a finished carving—so that whoever came after would find it, and the flute in the pocket, and know.

"Cyrus," they said. "Teach me the first line."
