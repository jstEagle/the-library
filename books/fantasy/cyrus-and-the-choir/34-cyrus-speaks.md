## 34. Cyrus Speaks

The deep chamber does not echo. That is the first thing Cyrus understands about it, standing at the edge of the last stair with the torchlight trembling on walls that rise beyond sight.

Sound goes in and never comes back out.

Every word spoken since they descended has fallen into the stone like a coin into a well — Wendell's voice, Bramble's curse when they stubbed their toe on the final step, the wet rattle of Maren's name that no one has dared speak aloud since the mountain's mouth. All of it swallowed. The chamber drinks sound the way the valley once refused magic: absolutely, without malice, because it was made to.

Made to hold one song, Cyrus thinks. And nothing else.

They walk forward alone. This was not decided; it simply happened, the way water happens downhill. The others stopped where the floor began to slope, where the carvings thickened until the walls became a single unbroken sentence wrapping the world, and only Cyrus kept walking, because only Cyrus can read what comes next.

The altar is not what the monks taught them to expect. There is no blood-groove, no iron, no ring for chains. It is a basin of black stone worn smooth as a riverbed, shallow as a cupped hand, and around its rim runs the oldest carving Cyrus has ever seen — older than the monastery's founding stones, older than the grave-rubbings in Wendell's archive, older, perhaps, than the idea of kingdoms. A circle of small figures, each with a spiral where a mouth should be.

A hundred voices. Maybe a thousand. All of them singing into the dark together.

Cyrus lays both palms flat against the rim.

*Hello,* they think, the way they have thought hello ten thousand times in ten thousand silent rooms. *I'm here. I came.*

And the chamber answers.

Not with sound. With pressure — a slow tide rising through the soles of their feet, up through the bones of their legs, settling behind the sternum like a second heart learning to beat. The dead in Wendell's pack, the flute and the whistle and the half-finished things, all of them left above with Bramble, but the chamber does not need them. The chamber *is* them. Every carved figure on every wall is a vessel, and every vessel holds something patient and vast and grieving, and all of it turns toward Cyrus now like flowers toward an impossible sun.

*I know,* Cyrus answers. *I know you've been waiting. I'm sorry it took so long. I'm sorry about the others.*

Names rise in them — Brother Anselm who taught them letters before the letters stopped mattering, Sister Vey who braided their hair, the anchors whose names were crossed off a list found in a dead man's coat, one by one by one by one, until only a single line remained. Cyrus had learned to read grief as ink. Now they learn it as weight.

Behind them, Wendell's footsteps on the slope. Careful. Unhurried. The bone-carver stops a few paces back and says nothing, which is its own kind of speech — Wendell has learned some of Cyrus's language after all, the language of standing near without demanding.

Cyrus looks down into the basin. It is empty, and it is not empty. Far below the smooth black stone, far beneath the mountain's hollowed heart, something sleeps. Cyrus has felt it since the first stair: a sorrow so old and so large that the mind keeps trying to file it as weather. A god that was never worshipped into being. A god that *grew*, the way rot grows in a house no one visits — out of mourning, out of being forgotten, out of a war so ancient even the walls record it only as a scar.

The Choir sang it to sleep. Not to cage it. To comfort it. To give it a dream better than waking, because waking meant remembering everyone it had lost.

And now the Ashen Order stands at the mouth of this mountain with torches and oil and certainty, and their commander wants the grieving thing awake, and the binding frays a little more with every year no living voice renews it, and the solstice is *today*, and Cyrus —

Cyrus opens their mouth.

Nothing comes.

It has been nine years. They know this the way they know the number of stairs, the way they know the exact weight of a bone whistle in a small palm. Nine years since the fire took the monastery, since a monk's burned hands pressed a whistle into theirs and pushed them out a cellar door into the smoke, since the last sound they ever made was a scream that no one living heard over the roar of burning rafters. After that, the silence came down like snowfall — soft, total, burying everything. They had tried, over the years, in ditches and wagons and stolen rooms. They had opened their mouth and pushed, and what came out was nothing, or a rasp like wind through a broken shutter, and each failure built another layer of snow.

The chamber waits. The whole mountain waits. Somewhere above, men are lighting torches.

Cyrus grips the rim of the altar until the black stone bites their palms.

*You don't need a beautiful voice,* the dead seem to say, patient as sediment. *You never did. You need a willing one.*

Willing.

Brother Ilex died three days ago covering their escape from the convoy, and his last written words are still folded against Cyrus's chest: *The binding doesn't take a voice. It takes a choice.* Cyrus has turned those words over so many times the paper has gone soft as cloth. And they understand now, standing in the drinking dark, what Ilex could never quite finish writing: the Order has spent a century trying to force this god awake, dragging anchors across kingdoms, crossing names off lists, and failing every single time — because you cannot force a song. You cannot torture a lullaby. The hinge of the world is consent, and consent cannot be stolen, only given.

So Cyrus gives.

They do not decide it the way decisions are usually made, with weighing and dread. It is simpler than that. It is like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders have forgotten its shape. *Yes,* they offer into the dark. *I'll sing. I'm afraid. I'll sing anyway. That's what willing means — I learned it from a bone-carver who hates fighting and fights anyway, and a thief who lies badly and comes back anyway, and an old woman with a farming hoe who stood in a gate anyway.*

The tide inside the chamber stills.

Then, gently — so gently, the way Wendell handles a half-carved flute — something rises to meet them. Not force. Accompaniment. A thousand dead throats clearing themselves, a thousand dead lungs drawing a breath they will never need again, arranging themselves around one small living voice the way a choir arranges itself around its weakest singer, because the weakest singer is the one carrying the melody.

Cyrus breathes in.

And screams.

That is how it begins — there is no gentle way past nine years of snow. The first sound tears out of them ragged and raw and terrible, a sound like a door ripped off its hinges, like a child's throat remembered all at once, and it hurts, it hurts like swallowing broken glass, and Cyrus does not stop. They lean into the scream and let it bend, let it break apart, let the pieces fall into shapes, and the shapes are notes, cracked and ugly and *alive*, and the moment the first true note lands in the black basin —

The mountain sings back.

It comes up through the floor like a struck bell the size of a country. The walls ignite — not with fire but with resonance, every carved figure flaring faintly silver-blue, every spiral-mouth opening into light, and the harmony that pours out of them is nothing like the whispers in Wendell's workshop. This is the Choir at full voice, the sound that put a god to sleep when the world was young, and it fills the chamber so completely that Cyrus feels it pressing on their eardrums like deep water, feels it in their teeth, in the soft plates of their skull, in the space behind their eyes where memory lives.

Their scream becomes a note. The note becomes a phrase. The phrase becomes —

The renewal. The song the monks sang once a century in a coastal monastery, the song Cyrus heard through a nursery wall before they could walk. It was never written down. It lives in the body, passed from anchor to anchor the way a heartbeat is passed from mother to child, and Cyrus thought it drowned in the fire with everyone who taught it.

But it didn't. It waited. It is pouring out of them now, syllable after syllable in the language before kingdoms, and Cyrus does not understand all of it and does not need to. The words mean things like *rest* and *enough* and *you are not forgotten, we forgot ourselves instead, forgive us, sleep, beloved, sleep.*

Far below, the god stirs.

Cyrus feels it happen — feels it the way you feel a great ship shift at dock, the whole structure of the mountain flexing around a movement too large to see. The sorrow in the deep rolls over. Something that has slept through the rise and fall of empires opens one dreaming eye, and its attention falls on the small figure at the altar like a shaft of moonlight falling into a well, and Cyrus should be terrified, and is, and keeps singing.

Because the sorrow is not angry.

That is the thing the Order will never understand, standing up there with their oil and their scripture. The god wakes into mourning, not malice. Its first act upon stirring is not wrath. It is *listening*. Cyrus can feel it listening — this vast wounded thing drinking in the sound of a living voice saying its name in the old tongue for the first time in a hundred years, and if Cyrus's voice falters, Cyrus knows, with a certainty that sits in the marrow, that the listening will curdle. Grief interrupted becomes rage. Grief honored becomes rest.

*Sleep, beloved,* sings the Choir through a thousand stone mouths. *Sleep, beloved.*

*Cyrus!* Wendell's voice, cracking across the chamber — swallowed instantly by the stone, but Cyrus sees it, sees the bone-carver stumbling down the slope with their hand pressed to their chest, face gray in the blue light. Behind Wendell, at the head of the stairs, Bramble is shouting something, pointing upward, and even without sound Cyrus reads it plainly:

Smoke. Torchlight. Boots on the upper stair.

The Order is coming down.

Cyrus does not stop singing. That is the terrible arithmetic of this moment and they understand it completely: the song cannot pause, cannot falter, cannot wait for swords. The god is halfway between sleep and waking, balanced on a knife-edge of attention, and the song is the only hand holding it steady. If Cyrus stops — to run, to fight, to breathe — the god wakes into a chamber full of screaming men with fire, and grief interrupted becomes rage, and the rage of a mourning god will make the Order's century of cruelty look like a rehearsal.

So the smallest person in the mountain keeps singing.

They watch Wendell reach the altar's edge and stop, sword drawn and useless, placing themselves between Cyrus and the stairs — a bone-carver who has killed exactly one man and been sick afterward, standing guard with a blade they hate. Cyrus loves them so much in that moment that the note wavers, and the god shifts uneasily below, and Cyrus wrenches the melody back with tears streaming down their face and blood on their lips where the unused throat has torn itself raw.

The first soldiers appear at the bottom of the stair.

Cyrus sees them through the blue haze of the singing walls — gray cloaks, drawn steel, faces lit from below like masks. And behind them, descending with the unhurried calm of a man entering his own cathedral, comes the commander. Tall. Iron-gray. His eyes find the altar and fix on Cyrus with an expression that is not hatred. It is worse. It is *triumph*.

He raises one gauntleted hand.

"Fire," he says, and the chamber swallows the word, but fire needs no permission. Torches arc down the stairwell trailing orange tails, landing among the carved figures at the base of the wall, and old oil — the Order came prepared, the Order always comes prepared — catches with a soft, greedy *whump*.

Flame climbs the wall of sleeping voices.

Cyrus feels every burn. That is the horror no one warned them of: the carvings are vessels, and the vessels are connected, and as fire eats through a spiral-mouthed figure, that voice drops out of the harmony like a plucked string — one, then three, then seven, the chord thinning, fraying, the god below shifting harder now, restless, the sorrow beginning to curdle at its edges into something with teeth. The song is a bridge, and the Order is burning the bridge while they stand on it.

Wendell is shouting — at the soldiers, at Bramble, at the universe — and the soldiers are advancing, and Bramble appears out of nowhere with a stolen sword held in entirely the wrong grip, and none of it matters, none of it can matter, because the equation has not changed: *keep singing or everything ends.*

But Cyrus's voice is tearing apart.

Nine years of silence were not a wall they climbed over; they were a wall they broke through, and the passage is bleeding. Each note costs more than the last. The high phrases have already gone; Cyrus has dropped an octave and then another, rasping the melody out through a throat that feels packed with ash and glass, and the god needs the *whole* song — the walls say so, the walls are the score, and the final verses require two voices, the walls have always said two, the binding is a duet, it was always a duet —

*Ilex knew,* Cyrus realizes, through the roaring and the smoke and the blood-warm taste running down their chin. *Ilex knew, and he died before he could finish the sentence.*

One voice starts the renewal. One voice completes it. The Choir was a thousand singers once, but the binding itself, the hinge, the choice — it takes two who choose. A voice to carry the melody. A voice to hold the harmony. Living and — and what? What does the second voice have to be?

Cyrus looks at Wendell.

Wendell is looking back, and the bone-carver's face is doing something terrible and quiet, the way it did at the ford before the carving knife came out, the way it did on the ridge before the riders came — understanding arriving ahead of fear. Cyrus watches them glance from the altar, to the burning walls, to the thinning chord, to the score spiraling around the basin's rim where the final verses show two figures side by side, mouths open, spirals aligned.

Wendell's hand drifts to the flute at their belt. The one they carved in a valley where magic cannot enter, from a bone that whispers.

"No," Cyrus tries to say, and what comes out is a shredded whisper swallowed whole by the stone, and the god stirs again, and the fire reaches the third tier of carvings, and Wendell steps toward the altar with a face like someone reading the last page of a book they wrote themselves.

Below the floor, vast and grieving, the god begins to wake.
