## 35. The Voice That Chooses

The song had been going for the length of three breaths when Wendell understood what it wanted.

Not from Cyrus's raw, cracked voice—though that voice was a thing to hear, scraped thin as winter ice, a sound made by a throat that had sealed itself shut for years and was being torn open again by necessity. Not from the god either, though its stirring filled the chamber like water filling a bell: a vast slow pressure against the walls of the world, grief so old it had forgotten it was grief, mistaking itself for the shape of everything.

Wendell understood it from the bones.

They had brought every carved piece they owned down into the deep chamber—the flute Cyrus had carried out of the fire, the half-finished flute that had first whispered *run*, the whistles and beads and buttons of their trade, the little spiraled tokens Cyrus had pressed into their palm on the road like coins in a language only two people alive could read. All of them lay now in a crescent at Wendell's feet, and all of them were singing.

Not the dead's usual whispering. Not the mutter of voices through carved channels, one at a time, patient as rain. This was harmony. Hundreds of voices, thousands, braided into a single sustained tone that lived in the marrow of Wendell's own skeleton, that made their teeth hum and the fluid of their eyes tremble. The Choir. The real Choir, the whole of it, risen up through the Spire's hollow throat because Cyrus's voice had called them home the way a shepherd's whistle calls sheep across a dark hillside.

And beneath the harmony, threaded through it like a darker strand in rope, came the words.

*Two voices,* the dead said. *The binding is a door with two hands upon it. One hand opens. One holds it shut.*

Cyrus stood at the altar—a slab of pale stone worn smooth by centuries of song, rising from the chamber floor like a tooth—and sang. Their eyes were closed. Tears cut clean lines through the ash on their face, and they did not stop singing to wipe them away. Above them, the walls of the Hollow Spire rose in a spiral of carved figures, floor to unseen ceiling, ten thousand years of the story written in the language before kingdoms: the god who had been a mourner, the Choir who had sung it to sleep out of mercy, and the long procession of renewals, century after century, each marked by a pair of figures side by side, mouths open, joined by a single unbroken line.

Wendell had seen those paired figures on the walls an hour ago and understood them only dimly. Now, with the harmony shaking the dust from the ceiling far above, they understood completely.

*One voice begins,* said the dead. *One voice completes. The beginning can be given. The completion must be chosen.*

They looked at their own hands. A bone-carver's hands, scarred and nicked, knuckles swollen from thirty years of work done well and small. Hands that had never held a sword until a ford, never held much of anything except tools and quiet.

The cost was not hidden from them. That was the terrible courtesy of it. The dead did not soften; they had never once softened, not in all the years Wendell had listened. They told it plainly, each voice taking up a fragment, passing it along the way villagers passed a heavy load hand to hand:

*The song spends a life.*

*A willing life. Given, not taken—it cannot be taken, it has been tried, oh how it has been tried—*

*Given wholly. Voice, breath, body, name.*

*You will not die as the dying die. You will pass over the way we passed. And you will remain.*

*Here. In this place. Bound to the binding.*

*Forever is a word we know the weight of, carver. We carry it. So would you.*

Wendell thought of the valley then, which surprised them. Not of death, not of light or gods or the great wheel of the story turning above their head—but of the workshop in the morning, the smell of fresh-cut bone and linseed oil, the particular gray of the light through the east window that meant the day would be cold and good for carving. The kettle on the iron hook. The second cup they'd bought last autumn without letting themselves think why, and washed every week since, unused.

Small things. The deliberate smallness they had built their whole life inside, like a bird building a nest one twig at a time, and calling the nest a world.

They had left it. That was the thing they kept circling back to, the splinter in the mind. They had crossed the valley's edge and the magic had flooded in and nearly broken them, and they had learned in one white-hot moment that the silence they'd treasured had also been a wall, and the safety had also been a cage, and the smallness had also been a kind of hiding. Maren had known it. Maren had looked at them across her kitchen table the night before they left and said, *You've been keeping yourself like a tool in a drawer, Wendell Dunmore. Tools are for using.* And then she had come down the mountain with farming tools and old grudges to hold the Spire's mouth while a child sang.

Maren who might be dead right now, above them, in the noise and firelight. Bramble, who had run back through Order lines with a stolen seal and a grin like a knife. Odell in his inn. Ilex, who *was* dead, who had died covering the escape at the convoy, mute to the last, his warning written in a steady hand: *it needs a willing voice.*

Everyone Wendell loved was spending themselves. Had spent themselves. Were spending themselves right now, somewhere overhead, in the dark.

And here stood Wendell, alive, whole, holding the one thing the song required—and hesitating over the price.

Shame arrived first. Then, strangely, something gentler underneath it, almost like relief. Because the hesitation wasn't cowardice. Wendell examined it honestly, the way they'd examine a flawed piece of material before deciding whether to carve it anyway. It wasn't fear of dying. They had been sick with fear at the ford and they knew that animal taste; this was not it. It was grief. Grief ahead of time. Grief for the mornings they wouldn't have, the second cup that would stay empty forever, the flute they'd meant to finish, the look on Cyrus's face—

Cyrus's face.

Wendell looked up.

The child was singing with their whole small body, feet braced, fists tight at their sides, pouring out a voice they had locked away behind silence since the night the monastery burned. Every note cost them. Wendell could see it costing them, see the effort in the tendons of their neck, the trembling of their jaw. They had watched this child learn to whistle on the road, clumsy and furious at their own mouth, laughing—for the first time, laughing—in the inn's yellow lamplight. They had taught Cyrus three carved words and Cyrus had taught them five, and somewhere between the sixth and seventh mile of some gray morning it had stopped being a rescue and become a family, the found and fragile kind, the kind you don't get to choose the terms of.

And Cyrus could not do this alone. That was written on the walls in a language older than kingdoms and repeated now in the bones at Wendell's feet: the renewal failed with one voice. It had always been two. The Order had dragged anchors across a hundred years and forced songs out of terrified throats and the god had never woken and the binding had never renewed, because terror opens nothing. Only willingness opens. Only choice.

If Wendell stepped back—if they let Cyrus sing alone, if they ran for the valley and its merciful nullity and hid behind Maren's ghost and Odell's debt and Bramble's quick fingers—then Cyrus would stand at this altar until the Order's fire came down, and the binding would fray, and the god would wake mourning into a world that had already forgotten how to mourn with it, and everything everyone had spent would have been spent for nothing.

There was no version where Wendell kept their life and kept the truth. They saw that now, plainly, the way you see the grain in bone once you've turned it to the light. Every other path was a carving of a lie.

*Carver,* said the dead, gently, hundreds of voices gentle at once, which was somehow worse than menace. *You may say no. That is what makes it worth anything. That is all that has ever made it worth anything.*

"I know," Wendell said aloud.

Their voice sounded very small in the chamber. Small, and human, and theirs. For a moment they simply stood in it, in the sound of their own living voice echoing off ten thousand years of carved stone, and let themselves be afraid, properly, fully, the way you let yourself feel the full weight of a thing before lifting it.

Then they picked up the unfinished flute.

It fit their hands the way it always had, the way their own work fit them, thumb finding the third bore without looking. Half-finished. They had meant, on the road, in the inns, in the stolen hours by fires, to complete it—there had been a design, a spiral ending in an open mouth, a figure singing. They would never carve it now. Someone else would have to. Someone else would have to do all the small unfinished things, and that was the true shape of the sacrifice, Wendell realized—not the dying, but the leaving-behind. The unfinished flute. The second cup. Cyrus growing taller, year by year, in a valley Wendell would know only as a voice in bone.

They would take it. They would take all of it. Because the alternative was a world where Cyrus's sacrifice at the ford and Ilex's at the convoy and Maren's at the Spire's mouth meant nothing, and Wendell Dunmore had never in their life made a beautiful thing mean nothing.

"Cyrus."

The child didn't stop singing—they couldn't, Wendell understood that now, the song was holding the fraying edge together and stopping was death—but their eyes opened, and found Wendell across the chamber, and Wendell raised the flute so they could see it.

Then Wendell did the thing they did best. They spoke in carvings.

With the knife from their belt—the same knife, gods forgive them, that had killed a man at the ford—they cut quick strokes into the flute's pale body, there at the altar's foot, working fast but clean, the way they worked when the work mattered. Three words. The first ones Cyrus had ever taught them, back on the road, scratched into a scrap of alder beside a dying fire:

*I stay. You live. Home.*

Cyrus read it. Cyrus's song stuttered—one note, half a heartbeat, a crack in the ice—and their eyes went wide and wet and furious all at once, and they shook their head, hard, still singing, mouthing around the melody: *no, no, no.*

"Yes," Wendell said quietly. "I'm sorry. Yes."

Cyrus's voice climbed. Not louder—desperate, pleading, bending the ancient melody into shapes it was never meant to hold, and the Choir shivered around them, discordant, grieving, because the dead too had hoped. Wendell knelt and took the child's free hand in both of theirs, the way you hold the hand of someone crossing ice.

"Listen to me." Their voice was steadier than they had any right to expect. "I spent thirty years making myself small enough that nothing could reach me. And it worked, and it was warm, and it was the loneliest thing I ever did. Then a mute child knocked on my door in a storm and the dead went silent, and I have been more alive these forty days than in all the years before." They smiled, and found to their surprise that it was a real smile. "You didn't take my quiet away, Cyrus. You gave my loudness back. Let me spend it where it counts."

Tears fell from the child's chin onto the altar stone. The song wavered toward breaking.

"And—" Wendell's throat tightened, and they pushed through it. "And I'll be here. That's the secret nobody tells you about ghosts. I'll be here. In every bone anyone ever carves in that valley. You'll knock on my door your whole life, Cyrus, and I will always, always answer. Now sing me the line. The true line. The one that needs two."

Above them, faintly, muffled by miles of hollow stone, came the sounds of battle—the Order's horns, the clash at the Spire's mouth, Maren's exiles holding the door with scythes and fury. Time was bleeding out. Somewhere in that noise Serle was choosing, or failing to choose; somewhere the god turned in its sleep like a sleeper near dawn.

Wendell lifted the flute to their lips.

The unfinished flute. Of course it was the unfinished flute. It would be finished by what happened next, finished in the only way that matter allowed, the final bore cut not by any knife but by a voice passing through it and not coming back.

They breathed in. The chamber held its breath with them—Cyrus, the Choir, the ten thousand carved generations on the walls, the sleeping god beneath the floor whose dream had begun, at last, to lighten.

*I choose this,* Wendell thought, deliberately, offering the thought outward like a held-out palm. *Nobody takes it. Nobody forces it. I give it.*

The dead answered as one, a sound like dawn coming up over water:

*Then begin, carver. Sing with the child.*

Wendell put their lips to the bone and gave the song their voice.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the last mercy and the last astonishment. It felt like the valley's edge, the moment of crossing—everything sharpening, colors igniting, the world suddenly, overwhelmingly *real*—except that this time there was no falling, no collapse, only expansion. Wendell's voice poured into the flute and the flute poured it into the chamber and the chamber caught it and multiplied it, and Cyrus's cracked human note met it halfway and wrapped around it, and the two sounds braided together into a single line the way the paired figures on the walls were joined by a single unbroken line.

*Two hands upon the door,* sang the dead, triumphant and sorrowful at once. *One opens. One holds it shut.*

Wendell felt the cost arrive, and it was not like drowning and not like burning. It was like setting down a heavy pack after a long road—the sudden strangeness of lightness, the disbelief that the weight was gone. Their body grew distant. Their knees stayed locked, standing, but the standing became something happening to a coat they were wearing rather than a self they were inhabiting. The chamber brightened. No—their sight was widening, pulling back, and they could see the whole spiral of the Spire at once, and beyond it the mountain, and beyond it the valley lying green and null and beloved in the evening light, and the tiny scatter of torches at the Spire's mouth where their family fought and bled, and past even that, the whole sleeping curve of the world.

And beneath it all, the god.

Turning toward the song. Turning, for the first time in an age, not away from the sound that had silenced it but *toward* it—because this sound was not a lid placed over its grief. This sound was grief, shared. Two voices, one living and one newly dead, mourning *with* it instead of *against* it, and the god's endless solitary sorrow cracked down the middle like river ice in spring, and through the crack came something none of the carvings had named, because none of the carvers had dared hope:

Comfort.

The binding closed. Wendell felt it close, felt themselves become part of the closing—voice, breath, name, poured into the hinge of the world, holding the door shut on a grief that could drown nations, holding it not with force but with company.

Somewhere very far away, Cyrus was still singing, alone now, tears streaming, and the song needed only the one voice to sustain what two had sealed. Somewhere very far away, men screamed and steel rang and Serle of the Ashen Order made his choice. Wendell perceived all of it the way the living perceive a village from a mountaintop: small, vivid, dear, and no longer theirs to touch.

The last thing they felt with living senses was the flute, still warm at lips that were becoming memory.

The first thing they felt as something new was the valley, answering, across all the distance, like a struck bell.

And in the chamber below, Cyrus sang on into the settling dark—until the floor of the Hollow Spire began, very softly, to glow, and every carved figure on its ten thousand walls opened their carved mouths at once, and the god spoke its first word in a thousand years.

It said: *Thank you.*

And then it said something else—something that made Cyrus's song falter, made the dead cry out in a thousand tongues, and sent Cyrus running for the stairs with the flute clutched to their chest, because the god had spoken a warning, and the warning was a name, and the name was *fire*.
