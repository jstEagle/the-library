## 38. The New Ghost of the Valley

The valley does not want them back.

Cyrus feels it the moment they cross the invisible line at the pass—the way the world goes quiet in a manner that is not silence but subtraction. On the road behind them, birdsong had been layered and bright; here it flattens to a single note. The colors of the pines dull by one shade, as if someone has drawn a gray cloth over everything and left the shapes showing through.

Bramble feels it too. They stop walking mid-stride, one boot still raised, and their face does something complicated—relief and grief wrestling for the same ground.

"So this is it," Bramble says. "The place where nothing works." They say it lightly, the way they say everything, but their hand drifts to the stolen Order seal at their belt, then away again, as if even touching it here feels like blasphemy. "No wonder you all stayed. It's like being wrapped in wool."

Cyrus does not answer. Cyrus has not answered anyone aloud since the Spire, since the last note of the duet left their throat raw and ringing and empty in a way that was somehow full. The voice is there—they have used it, sparingly, twice on the road home, once to tell Bramble which fork to take and once, at night, to hum a fragment of the renewal song so the exiles could sleep. But words feel like coins spent from a purse that should never be opened carelessly. The carvings are still truer. The carvings have always been truer.

They carry Wendell's flute.

It rides against Cyrus's chest in a sling of linen Maren's daughter wove before she died holding the Spire's mouth, and Cyrus can feel its weight the way other people feel a heartbeat. Bone, pale as milk, carved with spirals that are also stacked figures that are also, when the light hits them right, a name. Wendell had made it in the first week, back when Cyrus was still a stranger at the door and the dead had gone urgently silent, and Wendell had never once played it. *For emergencies,* Wendell had said, tucking it into the pack. *Or for after.*

*After* had seemed, then, like a country far away.

The exiles walk ahead and behind them in a loose, tired column—nine of them now, where there had been eleven at the Spire. Old Hesta with her farming scythe cleaned but never resheathed. The twins, Pellam and Rook, who had held the eastern wall and come out of the resonance chamber with their hair turned white at the temples though neither would admit why. Tam, who is fourteen and had thrown his first stone at a living man and still flinches at the memory of the sound it made. They do not speak much either. Grief has made the whole column mute, and Cyrus walks among them feeling, for the first time since the burning monastery, less alone in that silence rather than more.

Maren is not among them. Maren is buried at the Spire's mouth under a cairn of river stones, and every exile had added one, and Cyrus had carved a single spiral onto theirs—a word in the old language that means *the voice that stays*. The dead of the valley had risen to carry her down into the deep chamber, the story says, and Cyrus believes it, because Cyrus heard her. In the last moments before the god settled into true rest, beneath Wendell's dying note and Cyrus's raw living one, there had been another voice, older and rougher, singing slightly flat, the way a woman sings who learned songs from work and not from choirs.

Maren had joined the Choir. Somewhere in that vast sleeping harmony, she was still telling Wendell what to do.

Cyrus finds that thought does not hurt the way they expected. It hurts the way a bone hurts when it is setting.

* * *

The village looks smaller than Cyrus remembers, which is strange, because Cyrus only ever saw it from inside Wendell's workshop, through one window, in one season. Now they come down the cart track past the terraced gardens and the smokehouse and the long low house where Odell's cousin keeps bees, and everything is miniature, a model of itself, the way places become when you have stood inside a mountain hollowed by gods.

The exiles peel away to their own doors one by one. Hesta clasps Cyrus's shoulder—briefly, hard, saying nothing, which is the correct amount—and goes. The twins go. Tam hesitates, looks at the flute-sling on Cyrus's chest, opens his mouth, closes it, and runs the last stretch to his mother's house like a boy again instead of a soldier.

Only Bramble remains, hands jammed in pockets, rocking on their heels in the middle of the empty lane.

"Well," Bramble says. "This is the part where I ask if you want company, and you carve something at me, and I pretend to understand, and we both know I understood perfectly."

Cyrus takes out the knife. Takes out a scrap of birch from the bundle at their hip. Carves two figures side by side, then a third small one above them, then a spiral binding all three.

Bramble squints. "That's us. And—" They stop. Their swagger drops off their face like a dropped cloak. "That's Wendell."

Cyrus nods. *Coming with us. All three of us.*

Bramble clears their throat roughly, twice, which is what they do instead of crying. "Right," they say. "Lead on, then."

* * *

The workshop door is unlocked.

Of course it is. Wendell had left in a hurry, forty days becoming three, leaving the half-finished flute on the bench and the grave-rubbings pinned to the wall and the kettle—Cyrus sees it immediately, and something in their chest folds—still sitting on the cold hearth, waiting for water that never came. Dust lies over everything in a fine, patient gray, undisturbed, because no one in the valley enters another person's workshop uninvited. Not even now. Especially not now.

Cyrus stands in the doorway a long time.

Bramble waits behind them without fidgeting, which for Bramble is a form of heroism.

Then Cyrus steps inside, and the smell hits them—oils and bone dust and wood shavings and the particular mineral coolness of the archive room where the grave-rubbings sleep in their drawers—and it is Wendell's smell, whole and entire, and Cyrus's knees nearly go. They catch themselves on the doorframe. Breathe. The flute presses warm against their chest, warmer than body heat has any right to make it.

They cross to the bench. The half-finished flute is still there, clamped in the vise, Wendell's knife resting beside it with a curl of shavings frozen mid-fall. Cyrus touches the shavings. They crumble slightly, aged, but they were made by those hands, those careful scarred hands that taught Cyrus to whistle on the road of small kindnesses, and Cyrus holds the little curl of bone-dust-colored wood in their palm like an egg.

On the wall hang the rubbings. Cyrus reads them now—all of them, easily, the way they could read the walls of the Hollow Spire floor to ceiling. *Here lies Aldric, who kept bees.* *Here lies Sefa, who forgave everyone except the river.* *Here lies a child, unnamed, beloved.* The old language, older than kingdoms, scratched by people who never knew they were writing it—carved into grave markers by bone-carvers who thought they were making pretty patterns, while the dead whispered through the grain and only one carver in a hundred years ever listened.

Wendell had listened. Wendell had spent a whole life listening, thinking it was a small thing, a private thing, a deliberate smallness—and it had been the hinge of the world.

Cyrus picks up the finished flute from their chest-sling. Turns it in the light. The spirals catch the gray window-glow.

"Go on, then," Bramble says softly from the doorway. "I'll be right here."

Cyrus lifts the flute to their lips.

They do not blow a melody. That isn't how it works—they learned that at the Spire, learned it in their bones, which is the only place worth learning anything. They breathe into it the way Brother Ilex taught the novices to breathe before song: low, slow, willing. *It doesn't need any voice,* Ilex wrote, in the cellar under the ashes. *It needs a willing one.* The breath goes down the bore of the bone and through the carved chambers and out into the null air of the valley, where magic cannot enter—

—but the dead were never magic. That is the thing Wendell knew and the valley never understood. The dead are older than magic. The dead are what magic was built on top of, the way the old language is what kingdoms were built on top of. Magic stops at the pass. The dead do not stop anywhere.

The flute sounds—one clear note, thin and sweet, hanging in the dusty air.

And the workshop answers.

It comes up through the floor first, through the foundations, through the mountain itself: a rustle, a stirring, the sound of a great many people turning over in their sleep and opening their eyes. Cyrus feels it in their teeth. Then the half-finished flute in the vise hums, faintly, harmonizing. Then the drawer of grave-rubbings rattles once, gently, like a knock. Then every unfinished piece of bone on every shelf begins to sound—not notes exactly, but voices, the dead of the valley pressing themselves through the material they loved best in life, the medium they had always used to speak.

And through Wendell's flute, clear as a bell, patient as a long afternoon:

*"Well. You took your time."*

Cyrus makes a sound that is not a word. Their hand flies to their mouth.

The voice chuckles—dry, wry, unhurried, unmistakably Wendell, the exact tone Wendell had used when Cyrus burned the first batch of glue, when Cyrus fell asleep in the shavings pile, when Cyrus asked, in carvings, whether the dead got lonely. *"Don't look like that. You'd think after everything, you'd have learned I don't frighten."*

Cyrus's hands are shaking. They set the flute down on the bench, pick it up again, set it down. Finally they grab the knife and a scrap of wood and carve, fast and clumsy, the question that has been sitting in their chest since the Spire, since the last note, since they watched the light go out of the person who carried them out of the world:

*Does it hurt?*

A pause. Through the flute, Wendell sighs the sigh of someone settling into a comfortable chair after a very long journey.

*"You know, I asked the dead that exact question, my first year here. Forty years of asking, off and on. And do you know what they told me?"* The voice softens. *"They told me it's like the valley. Everything loud falls away, and what's left is what mattered. And what mattered, little one, was mostly you, these last months. So no. It doesn't hurt. It's the opposite of hurt. It's—"*

The voice pauses, searching, and Cyrus realizes with a jolt that Wendell is doing what Wendell always did: reaching for the precise word, refusing the easy one.

*"It's home. It turns out I was practicing for it my whole life. All that quiet. All that deliberate smallness."* A dry laugh. *"Turns out I was just getting comfortable."*

Cyrus sits down on the stool—Wendell's stool, worn to the shape of Wendell—and puts their head in their hands and cries, silently, the way they cried for years, except that now the crying has a different flavor entirely. This is not mourning alone. This is mourning that is also reunion, grief braided through with relief, tears that taste of arrival. Wendell is here. Wendell is in the bones and the dust and the flute and the half-finished work on the bench, and Wendell is not gone, Wendell is only elsewhere, in the country of the deliberate small, and the border between that country and this one turns out to be a flute's width wide.

Bramble has crept inside at some point and stands by the wall of rubbings, sniffling ostentatiously and pretending to read. *"Is that the thief?"* Wendell's voice asks, amused. *"Tell them the dead remember the inn. All of it. The soup especially."*

"I KNEW the soup was bad," Bramble says thickly, and then laughs and cries at the same time, which for Bramble is practically a religious experience.

* * *

They stay until dark.

Cyrus sits at the bench and tells Wendell everything, in carvings, holding each one up to the flute the way you hold a page to a candle. The convoy. The rescue that went half-wrong. Ilex, who died covering their escape, and whose name Cyrus carves with a spiral around it, *the voice that chose*. Serle, who turned his sword on his own commander, who knelt in the resonance chamber afterward with ash on his face and said, in a voice like a man reading his own death warrant, *I heard it too. I will testify.* The Order fracturing into factions like ice on a thawing river. The cairn at the Spire's mouth. Maren's voice, flat and true, in the last harmony.

At that, the flute is silent a long moment. When Wendell speaks again, the wryness has thinned to something bare.

*"Flat,"* Wendell says. *"She sang flat her whole life. Told me once it was because the valley wouldn't let music be pretty, and she wasn't going to give it the satisfaction."* A pause. *"Tell her—I mean, when you're next at the cairn. Tell her the dead sing in tune. She'll be furious. She'll love it."*

*I'll tell her,* Cyrus carves. Then, after a moment, the harder question, the one that has been waiting all day like a stone in the shoe:

*What do I do now?*

The flute hums, considering. Outside, the valley has gone fully dark, and the stars over the rim of the world are the flattened, wool-wrapped stars of the null place, and somewhere a nightjar makes its single note.

*"Now,"* Wendell says at last, and the voice has shifted—gentler, but with an edge of command that Cyrus recognizes, the voice of the woman who decided to leave the valley, who killed a man at a ford, who stepped forward at the altar when the song needed a second voice. *"Now you open the archive drawers. All of them. Every rubbing, every grave-marker sketch, every fragment I copied and didn't understand. Because here's the thing I figured out at the end, little one, the thing the walls of that Spire were shouting and I was too deaf with fear to hear: the Choir almost ended because it depended on one voice. One anchor. One child in a monastery. The Order didn't break the binding. We did—we let it get thin enough to break."*

The flute's note rises, steadies, becomes almost a chord.

*"So you teach them. The children. The language before kingdoms, the spirals, the stacked figures. Start with Tam—he's got good hands, I've seen him mend a net. Make it ordinary. Make it as common as carving a spoon. Let there be a hundred anchors, a thousand, so that no Ashen Order, no grieving god, no solstice ever again comes down to one small throat in one small room."* A breath through the bone, almost a laugh. *"You'll be terrible at it at first. You'll carve instructions nobody understands and lose your temper and throw your knife across the workshop. I did all of it. There's a dent in the third shelf from 1189. Check if you don't believe me."*

Cyrus checks. There is a dent in the third shelf.

They laugh—soundlessly, shoulders shaking—and then they take out the knife and a fresh piece of birch, and they begin, right there in the dust and the lamplight, to carve the first lesson. Two figures side by side. A spiral binding them. *We speak together.*

Through the flute, soft as shavings falling, Wendell hums along.

Outside, far up the lane, a light kindles in Maren's old house, where Hesta has already begun sorting the elder's papers, and down in the village the twins are lighting lamps, and the valley that refuses magic holds its new ghost and its old ones close in the wool-gray dark, and somewhere beyond the pass, in the world where colors burn sharp, the Hollow Spire stands hollow and singing, waiting for the hundred voices that are coming, season by season, child by child, to fill it.

Cyrus blows one last note into the flute—goodnight, not goodbye—and sets it gently beside the half-finished work on the bench, where it belongs.

Tomorrow, the lessons begin.
