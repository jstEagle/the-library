## 40. Coda: The Choir Sings On

Bramble ran the inn the way they'd once run rooftops in the City of Scales—fast, loud, and three steps ahead of disaster. The sign above the door was a carved fish that Cyrus had made in their first year back, and beneath it, in letters Bramble had painted themselves and then repainted every spring because the paint kept peeling, it read: THE WHISTLE & FLAME.

"Odell's ghost would want you to know," Bramble liked to tell travelers, leaning on the bar with both elbows, "that I paid off his debts, fixed his roof, and married nobody's daughter against her will. So really I've improved the place." The travelers usually laughed. The regulars knew better. They knew Bramble counted Odell's daughter Hessa as family now, that the girl who had once been a hostage of Serle's riders sat at the corner table most evenings doing sums for the inn's books, and that Bramble never once raised their voice at her.

The valley had changed in seven years. It could not have stayed the same even if it wanted to; the world had learned where it was.

It had started small. Exiles came down from other null places—there were more of them than anyone had guessed, little pockets of silence scattered across the kingdoms like holes punched in cloth—and they came because word had spread of a valley where no magic entered but something better did. Where the dead spoke kindly through bone. Where a mute carver taught children an alphabet of spirals older than any kingdom, and no one called it witchcraft, because the Ashen Order was gone, broken into confessions and cartloads of records, and no one else had the appetite for fear.

Bramble kept a room upstairs always made up—the blue room, they called it, though the quilt was green—for the nights when old exiles arrived half-frozen off the pass and needed somewhere warm before they could even say their names.

On the evening of the solstice, Bramble stood at the inn's window with a mug of nothing stronger than cider and watched the whole valley walk uphill together.

* * *

Serle had not gone back to any city. He had tried, the first winter, and lasted eleven days in the capital before the archives' smell of hot wax sent him shaking into the street—he had heard too many men burn things that smelled like that. So he had come back to the Hollow Spire, hat in hand, and asked Maren's successor—a stone-faced shepherd named Odo who had loved Maren in the graceless way shepherds love difficult dogs—if he could tend it.

"Tend it," Odo had said. "Like a grave."

"Like a garden," Serle had said. "I don't know which yet."

Seven years later he knew. It was a garden.

Every morning he climbed the Spire's outer stair with a broom and a lantern and swept the wind-scoured steps, and every morning he passed the two graves at its mouth. Maren's stone read only WHAT SHE HELD, SHE HELD WELL, which she would have found sentimental and which everyone agreed was exactly right anyway. Beside it lay the stone Wendell had never needed, because Wendell was not there—but the exiles had insisted on marking it regardless, and so it said, in the old language and the new: TEACHER. LISTEN AND YOU WILL HEAR.

Inside the Spire, Serle kept the walls clean of moss and the resonance chamber free of rubble, and he learned the language the way a man learns a country by living in it rather than reading about it—slowly, wrongly at first, then all at once. He could read the story on the walls now. All of it. Even the parts about the Order, which were not kind, and even the parts about the god, which were not what he had been taught, and which he had spent one entire winter copying onto paper so that no future order could claim ignorance.

Because that was the thing Serle understood better than anyone left alive: ignorance was the fuel. The Ashen Order had never been an army of monsters. It had been an army of tired people promised order and given a torch instead, marching into fire because someone told them it was dawn.

So he copied. And he taught. Once a month, children from the valley school climbed the Spire with Cyrus and sat in the great humming chamber while Serle showed them the wall where the binding was written, and Cyrus explained—not the fear of it, never the fear—the mercy.

On solstice morning, Serle swept the chamber last. The walls sang faintly under his broom, the way they always did when the year turned, a sound like a held breath. He put his palm flat against the carving and stood there a long moment, an old man with white in his beard and a scar he had never explained to anyone, listening.

"Tonight," he told the walls. "They're all coming tonight."

* * *

Cyrus had a voice now. That still startled people who met them for the first time—this quiet-handed carver who spoke in class and market and kitchen, whose voice was rough and low and used sparingly, like a tool kept sharp for the work only it could do. Cyrus talked the way some people sang: not often, but completely.

They were thirty now, or thereabouts; Brother Ilex had once worked out the years on his fingers before the ford, and Cyrus had never bothered to correct the total. They lived in the old workshop—Wendell's workshop—which the exiles had refused to let fall to ruin and which Cyrus had refused to let become a shrine. The bone dust still hung sweet in the rafters. The tools still hung in their rows, oiled and waiting. On the workbench, under glass, lay a flute.

Cyrus did not play it often. But every solstice, before the climb, they took it out and held it, and if the light was right and the day was kind, the dead spoke first.

*You'll be late,* said the flute, in a voice dry and patient and wry, a voice like a man amused to find himself still fussing after seven years of being dead. *You're always late. The child will beat you up the mountain, and she's six.*

"She's carrying the heavy lantern," Cyrus said aloud, smiling. "She's allowed to go slow."

*Nobody allowed me to go slow. I carried an entire fugitive child over a mountain ridge with nothing but a coat full of complaining bones.*

"You complain louder now than you ever did alive."

*Death has improved my confidence.* A pause, the particular pause Wendell had always made before saying the thing underneath the joke. *The god is restless this year. You felt it in the spring carvings.*

"I know."

*It isn't waking. Don't make that face. It's dreaming harder. There's a difference, and you of all people know there's a difference.* Another pause, longer. *Bring the children close to the altar tonight. All of them. The binding likes new voices best.*

"The binding," Cyrus said, "is not supposed to have favorites."

*Neither am I, and yet here we are, favoring you.*

Cyrus laughed—it still felt like a small miracle, the laugh, seven years of solstices later—and wrapped the flute in wool and did not carry it to the Spire, because they never did. Wendell went where Wendell wished, and where Wendell wished, every solstice, was up.

* * *

The procession began at dusk, as it had every year since the first anniversary, and it was not a solemn thing, which would have surprised every kingdom that had ever imagined how such rites must look. Children raced the lanterns up the lower path. Old exiles rode in carts pulled by fat patient ponies, arguing about whether the harmony had gone sharp last year—Odo insisted it had; his wife insisted Odo's hearing had. Bramble brought a barrel of cider partway up and abandoned it at the switchback, guarded by a sign that read DRINK ME AND FACE MAREN'S GHOST, WHICH IS REAL, I'VE MET HER.

Hessa carried the ledger up in her satchel, because someone had to record the renewal each year, and she had decided the dead deserved accurate bookkeeping.

And at the mouth of the Spire, where the two graves stood, the procession slowed and grew quiet without being told. That was custom now: everyone touched Maren's stone in passing, even the smallest children, even the ones too young to remember her except as a story about a woman who came down a mountain with a hay fork against armed riders and did not lose.

Then they poured inside, filling the great resonance chamber floor to gallery, hundreds of voices shuffling and settling, and the walls took the sound of them and turned it soft, the way the chamber turned everything—every cough became a chord, every whisper a warmth.

Serle stood at the altar steps with his lantern lit. Cyrus stood beside him. And around the altar itself, in a wide ring, sat the children—two dozen of them now, the oldest nearly grown, the youngest asleep against her brother's shoulder—all of them fluent, all of them carriers, none of them alone.

"Read the wall," Cyrus said. Their voice filled the hollow mountain without effort; the Spire had always been kind to quiet voices. "All of it. Not just the song. The story."

And they read. In unison, in the old language, the words crawling along the walls in spirals of lamplight: how the god had grieved, and how its grief had grown until it could have drowned the world, and how the Choir had not defeated it but had sung to it, had mourned *with* it, had promised a sleeping thing that it was not forgotten. How the promise had to be renewed, century upon century, by voices that chose. How the god slept, and was not imprisoned but *kept*, the way you keep a promise.

Then the song.

It started low, as it always did—Cyrus's rough voice alone, holding the first line the way a hand holds the first stitch. Then the children caught it, and then the gallery, and then the whole chamber, living voices braided together, and beneath them, through every piece of carved bone in the valley—every pendant, every flute, every keepsake button and memorial bead that folk had worn up the mountain in their coats and hair—the dead rose to meet them.

That was the difference. That was the thing the old Choir had never had, and why it had thinned to one frightened child with a whistle: the old Choir had been a choir of the chosen few. This one was a valley. It was farmers and innkeepers and runaway thieves and reformed inquisitors and bookkeeping girls and shepherds' wives, and it was the dead who loved them, all singing one song because they had all decided—together, stubbornly, in defiance of every kingdom that had ever told them silence was safety—that some things were worth raising your voice for.

In the deep below the altar, the god turned in its sleep like a sleeper turning toward a window where morning stands.

And through the flute in the workshop down in the valley, through the bones sewn into Bramble's coat hem, through Maren's old hay fork hanging honored on the inn's wall, through every grave and keepsake and half-finished carving left warm on a bench, the dead of the valley sang too—and among them, clear and wry and patient, one voice that had crossed the valley's edge knowing the price and paid it laughing.

*You're going sharp, Odo,* Wendell observed, mid-verse. *You always go sharp. Your wife is right. She's always been right. Tell her I said so, and watch her face.*

Odo laughed in the middle of the harmony, and the chamber caught the laugh and made it music, and the song rolled on.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the cider barrel was empty and the children had been carried home asleep and the mountain had gone quiet again—Cyrus walked down alone by lantern-light and stopped at the workshop door, as they always did.

The flute lay where they had left it, wrapped in wool.

"Everyone's asking when the next renewal lesson starts," Cyrus said to the dark room. "Hessa wants to write it all down properly. A real archive. She says the dead deserve footnotes."

*A terrifying woman,* said the flute fondly. *Marry her into the family immediately.*

"Bramble already claims her."

*Bramble claims everyone. It's how Bramble loves.*

Cyrus unwrapped the flute and held it a moment in the cold, and neither of them spoke, because there were silences and silences, and this one had never once been empty.

"The god dreamed hard tonight," Cyrus said at last. "Deeper down than usual. I felt it turn toward the singing. Toward us." They hesitated, thumb moving over a spiral in the flute's body—one they hadn't carved, one that had appeared slowly over the years, growing under Wendell's ghost-hands like a plant toward light. "Wendell. What does it mean when a sleeping god starts dreaming in *our* direction?"

The workshop was quiet for a long moment. Outside, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, the Spire hummed faintly against the stars, holding its note the way it had held it for a thousand years.

Then Wendell's voice came, warm and dry and unhurried, from the bone in Cyrus's hands:

*It means,* said the valley's newest ghost, *we should start teaching the children a second song.*
