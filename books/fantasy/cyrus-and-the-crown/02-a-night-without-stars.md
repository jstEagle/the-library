## 2. A Night Without Stars

The trouble with auditing memories, Quentin had learned in her eleven years with the Mnemosyne Guild, was that you could not count them the way a merchant counted coins. Coins did not weep when you weighed them. Coins did not whisper.

She sat in the counting room beneath the Guildhall, three floors of stone and silence above the vaults, with a ledger open before her and a tray of vials arranged in rows like sleeping wasps. Each vial held a memory drawn from some client who had decided that living without it was cheaper than living with it. Entry four hundred and twelve: *a first kiss, harvested from a widower on Copperlane, sold twice, recalled once.* Entry four hundred and thirteen: *the face of a man's murderer, retained as evidence, decay at nine percent.* Entry four hundred and fourteen made her pause, as it always did: *a miracle witnessed at sea — a woman walking out of the waves and back into them, unburned by salt or storm.*

Miracles were the guild's rarest stock. They kept poorly. Wonder curdled into doubt when removed from the moment it belonged to, and doubt was nearly worthless on the open market.

Quentin dipped her quill, checked the seal wax against the ledger's description — amber-gold, threaded with a hair of silver, the mark of the deep vaults — and moved to the next row.

This was the work no one thanked her for and everyone depended upon: the annual audit, when every memory in the guild's keeping was matched to its entry, its provenance, its price. Other clerks handled the buying and selling, the soft-voiced negotiations with grieving families and guilty men. Quentin handled the truth of it. The arithmetic of what people could not bear to carry.

*Competence is a kind of armor,* she had written once, years ago, in a journal she had since burned. She wore hers well. Her columns were immaculate. Her seals were verified to the grain of wax. And if there was a hollow place behind all that precision — a space where other people kept their childhoods, their first loves, their mothers' voices — well. Hollow places held nothing that could be stolen.

Except one thing.

In the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs where a knife would sit if she were the sort of clerk who carried knives, rested a single vial of grey glass. It had no entry in any ledger. It had no price. It contained, according to the faded tag knotted around its neck in a dead woman's handwriting, only two words: *hers. sealed.*

Her own childhood. Taken from her before she was old enough to know it was being taken, purchased by the guild from someone whose name even Corvane claimed not to remember, and locked away so thoroughly that Quentin herself had needed six years and three forged requisitions simply to learn which shelf it slept on. She had never opened it. She told herself this was discipline. She told herself that a keeper of ledgers who broke her own rules was worth nothing.

She told herself many things. That was, in its way, the guild's oldest trade.

* * *

The audit was half done when the light changed.

It did not dim. Dimming was gradual, honest, a thing with a direction. This was different. The lamplight in the counting room stayed exactly where it was, exactly as bright — and yet the darkness beyond the windows deepened by a degree that Quentin's eyes registered before her mind agreed to notice. She looked up from entry five hundred and one, a soldier's memory of his brother's funeral, and frowned at the window.

The city of Vael Sarn spread below the Guildhall hill in its usual night arrangement: ten thousand lamps, the river carrying their reflections southward in broken gold, the smoke-haze that never quite lifted. But tonight the haze was wrong. It glowed faintly, uneasily, like the skin of something bruised, because behind it —

Behind it, there were no stars.

Quentin stood so quickly her chair went over backward. She did not hear it fall.

She knew the sky over Vael Sarn the way she knew her own ledgers. She had walked home under it a thousand nights, cataloguing constellations without meaning to, the way other people hummed songs. The Lantern. The Three Sisters. The long pale scar of the Riverstar that sailors steered by and farmers set their planting by and the border wards of the Nine Provinces were said to have been tuned to, back when tuning was still possible. The stars were the one inventory in the world that had never once been miscounted.

Now the sky was a black page with nothing written on it. Not clouded — she could see the bruise-glow of the city haze clearly enough for that. Not fogged. *Empty.* As though someone had taken an eraser to the whole upper hemisphere and left the ink of the world untouched below.

"Master Sterling?" The junior clerk, Pell, stood in the doorway with a stack of receipts he would never deliver. He was nineteen and afraid of her, which she had always considered efficient. Now his face had gone the color of the vial-wax. "Master Sterling, the observatory bell — they're ringing it wrong. It's not the hour bell. It's—"

The bell in question began, distantly, to ring in earnest. Not the measured hour-stroke but the rapid, arrhythmic clamor that Quentin had read about in guild histories and never heard: the *discordance*, rung only when the natural order itself required reporting.

And then, before she could answer Pell, before she could do anything but stand in the falling shadow of her own overturned chair, the second sound came.

It came from the north and west, from beyond the hills, from beyond the farmlands, from the border that most citizens of Vael Sarn thought of as a line on maps and a tax on imported wool. It came the way thunder comes from a storm over the horizon — except it was not thunder. Thunder rolls. This *rang*. A single vast note of breaking glass, so large and so distant that it should have been inaudible, should have been nothing, and yet it passed through the stone floor and up through the soles of Quentin's shoes and into her teeth, and every vial on every tray in the counting room sang back at it, a chorus of tiny high harmonics, hundreds of memories crying out in sympathy with whatever had just shattered forty leagues away.

The wards. The border wards of the Nine Provinces, raised by the Ward-Kings in the age when kings still did such things, maintained since by rites no living warden fully understood — the wards that had held for nine hundred years against everything from invasion to plague-wind to the slow gnawing of ordinary time.

They had sounded, Quentin thought, exactly like glass. Like a window struck by a stone thrown by a child who did not know what was on the other side.

Pell made a small sound. Quentin realized she had crossed the room and gripped his shoulder hard enough to hurt them both.

"The ledgers," she heard herself say, in the flat, precise voice she used for corrections. "Ring the vault bell. Wake the under-clerks. Nothing leaves the building."

"But the stars—"

"*Nothing leaves the building.*"

He fled. She remained at the window a moment longer, alone in the counting room among the singing vials, and looked north into a sky with no stars, and felt — not fear, not yet, fear required understanding — something older than fear. The feeling of a column refusing to sum. Of a number appearing in a ledger that had no entry, no provenance, no explanation. The world's arithmetic had developed an error, and errors, in her experience, were never small. They were either trivial or catastrophic, and nothing about a sound that shook the marrow of the earth was trivial.

She picked up her chair. She straightened her ledger. And then she went down to find Corvane, because dread, like debt, always traveled uphill toward the person who thought himself owed.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvane received the news standing at the orrery in his study, a great brass engine of rings and spheres that modeled the heavens and had, presumably, until an hour ago, been accurate.

He did not look surprised. That was the first thing Quentin noticed, and she noticed it the way she noticed everything — automatically, professionally, the observation filed before the feeling arrived. Corvane was seventy if he was a day, a spare man built like a folded letter, with white hair cut close and hands that were never still. He had taught her double-entry memory accounting when she was fifteen. He had promoted her over eleven senior clerks. He had never once asked about the grey vial, and she had never once thanked him for the silence, and between those two silences they had built something that functioned, in the guild's cold way, as trust.

Now he turned the orrery's crank with one finger, slowly, watching the little brass planets wheel through their little brass dark.

"You rang for me," Quentin said. "Before I came. How?"

"I didn't." He did not look up. "I rang for the night wardens, and you came with them, because you are the sort of person who comes. Sit down, Quentin."

She did not sit. "The wards are down. All of them, from the sound of it — the resonance reached us whole, which means the failure wasn't local. The stars—" Her voice caught on the word, which annoyed her. "The stars are gone. Every one. I counted."

At that, Corvane did look up, and something in his face made her wish he hadn't. Not shock. Recognition. The expression of a man reading an entry he had hoped never to audit.

"You counted," he repeated softly. "Of course you did." He let go of the orrery's crank. The brass heavens continued turning anyway, momentum carrying them through their false night. "Then you will understand why I am sealing the ledgers. All of them. Deep vault, shallow vault, the client indexes, the provenance rolls. Everything."

"Sealed how? Sealed against whom?"

"Against everyone. Including ourselves." He moved past her to the study door and shot the bolt, which he had not done in her presence in eleven years. When he turned back, the lamplight put his shadow long across the floor, reaching toward her like something with fingers. "What happened tonight is not a war, Quentin. Wars I could sell you a hundred memories of, and cheaply, because wars repeat. What happened tonight is a *clause coming due*. Something written into the world a very long time ago, by people who believed they would be forgiven for the terms."

"What terms? Whose forgiveness?"

Corvane studied her for a long moment. Outside, the discordance bell still clanged its broken rhythm, and beneath it now she could hear the city waking — doors, dogs, the rising murmur of ten thousand people stepping into their streets to look up at nothing.

"There is a reason," Corvane said, "that the Mnemosyne Guild has survived nine hundred years while kingdoms fell around it. We survive because we keep what others cannot afford to remember, and we sell it back at need. And there is a reason — one reason, one — that the guild has never, in all those centuries, audited the Founders' Shelf. Not the wardens. Not the Guildmasters. Not me." His stillness now was total; even his hands had stopped. "Tonight, for the first time, I am going to break that rule. And I find that I want my best auditor beside me when I do."

"The Founders' Shelf," Quentin repeated. The words meant nothing to her, which meant they had been kept from her deliberately, which meant they mattered. Eleven years in the guild had taught her that the things she did not know were never accidents. "That's below the third sub-vault. There's nothing below the third sub-vault. I've seen the plans."

"You've seen the plans we allow to exist." Corvane took a ring of black iron keys from around his neck — keys she had never seen him wear — and held them a moment, weighing them, or weighing himself. "The wards that fell tonight were not attacked, Quentin. I want you to understand that before anything else, because everything else follows from it. They were not broken. They were *unpaid*. There was a price written into their founding, a price paid on a schedule, and tonight the account ran dry."

"Paid with what?"

The old man looked at her then with an expression she had never seen him wear — grief, perhaps, or guilt, or the particular exhaustion of a merchant who has just discovered the true cost of goods he sold long ago.

"Come downstairs," he said, "and I will show you what we were paid with. And then, I think, you will want to ask me about the vial you keep in your coat. Yes," he added, before she could speak, watching her hand move to her ribs without her permission, "I have known about the vial since the day you hid it there. I have known what it holds far longer than you have. Come downstairs, child. The stars went out for a reason, and I am beginning to suspect the reason is wearing your face."

Above them, unheard through the bolted door, the last of the discordance bells fell silent — and in the new quiet, faint and far away across the starless city, the first screams began.
