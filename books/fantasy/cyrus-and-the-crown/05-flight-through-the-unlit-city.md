## 5. Flight Through the Unlit City

The vial weighed nothing and everything.

Quentin had carried it for eleven years, tucked against her ribs in a lead-lined pocket sewn into every coat she owned, and she had told herself so many lies about its weight that she had almost believed them. It was small. It was glass and cork and a memory that was not hers, not really. It was a ledger entry deferred.

Now, running down the servants' stair behind the guildhall with the vial burning cold against her breastbone and a ledger bound in grey calfskin under her arm, she finally understood what it had always weighed: everything she might have been.

Behind her, above her, the guildhall was waking into fury. She had heard Corvane's voice through the door of his study—low, reasonable, terrible—the way it always was when he spoke of balance. *Surrender the vial, Quentin. Surrender the name. The guild will shield you from what's coming.* As if shielding were something the guild did out of love rather than investment. As if she were an asset to be protected instead of an entry to be collected.

She had said no. She was still astonished at herself for saying no. Eleven years of ledgers, eleven years of watching people sell the best of themselves piece by piece to pay debts they could never otherwise cover, and she had learned every language of persuasion the guild spoke—except how to say no to Corvane himself.

But she had. And then she had run.

* * *

The stair let out into the tannery lane behind the guildhall, where the smell of lye and old hides usually covered the sound of footsteps. Tonight there was no need. The whole city was making noise.

Quentin stopped in the mouth of the alley and stared up, because everyone in Vhalsund was staring up, because there was nothing else to see.

No stars. Not clouds—she knew clouds, knew their weight and color and the way they moved against the moon. This was absence, clean and total, a black so complete it felt less like night than like a hole cut in the world. The moon was gone too. Somewhere beyond the eastern hills, the border wards had shattered—she had heard it with her own ears two hours ago, a sound like a cathedral bell breaking, carried forty miles on air that should not have carried it—and whatever the wards had held up, whatever ancient light they had been propping in the sky all these centuries, had come down with them.

People stood in doorways with candles they had forgotten to light. A woman knelt in the middle of Tanner's Row, praying aloud to constellations that no longer existed, naming them one by one like a mother calling children who would not answer. Two men argued over a cart of winter turnips, their voices climbing toward violence with nothing above them to feel ashamed beneath.

*Without stars,* Quentin thought, *people forget they are small. And when they forget they are small, they become dangerous.*

She pulled her hood lower and went north.

* * *

The Ledger of Impossible Things rode under her arm like a sleeping animal. She had taken it from the vault in the same breath as her decision—a theft committed in under four seconds by a woman who had audited those shelves for a decade and knew exactly which lock stuck, which shelf sagged, which entry was misfiled where no auditor would look for years. Ledger Nine. The early entries. The ones from before the guild learned discretion.

It contained the harvest records. Not the polite ones, not the sanitized summaries Corvane showed to clients and councilmen. The originals. Names. Dates. Quantities. *One childhood, complete, purchased from the foundling house at Ashfall for three silver and a warm meal.* She had read her own acquisition record a hundred times without letting herself understand it. Tonight she understood it. Tonight it was evidence.

Because the dying wardwright had gasped a word at her with his last breath—not her name, not the name she'd carried since the foundling house, but another one. An older one. And Ledger Nine would tell her whether he had been raving or revealing.

First she had to get out of Vhalsund alive.

* * *

The city changed character street by street as she moved away from the guild quarter. Near the guildhall, fear still wore the shape of order: watchmen with lanterns, merchants nailing shutters, priests ringing bells that answered nothing. Three streets further, the lanterns stopped. Five streets further, the bells stopped. By the time she reached the Weft—the tangled district south of the river where the guild's debtors lived because they could not afford to live anywhere else—the dark had teeth.

She heard the first scream near the chandlers' row. Not a scream of pain. A scream of recognition—the sound a person makes when they realize no one is coming. Then glass somewhere, and laughter, ugly and bright, and a barrel rolling, and somewhere a dog barking itself hoarse at a sky that gave it nothing back.

Quentin pressed herself into a doorway and made herself small, made herself quiet, made herself the thing she had trained herself to be for eleven years: unremarkable. A clerk. Nobody. A woman with a ledger and a secret, which was to say, the most dangerous kind of person in any city.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake and hated them for it.

*Fear is information,* Corvane used to say in the training rooms, back when he taught the junior clerks himself, back before the teaching became delegation and the delegation became distance. *Fear tells you what you value. Read it like an entry.*

Fine. She read it. She valued the vial. She valued the ledger. She valued—this surprised her, arriving like a stranger's thought—*knowing*. Whatever her childhood held, whatever reason her mother had for selling it, sealing it, burying it inside her own daughter's skull, Quentin wanted it back. Not comfort. Not safety. Truth. The wards had fallen because of her blood; the warlord was coming because of her face; the guild wanted her because of her name. Every road forward ran straight through the contents of that little glass tube, and she would open it if she had to crack it on a cobblestone.

Her hands steadied. Not because the fear left—it didn't—but because the fear had finally found its work.

* * *

"You're being followed."

The voice came from above and behind, low and amused, and Quentin spun with her hand already inside her coat for the little bone-handled knife she kept for opening stubborn crates. On the loading balcony of the derelict rope-walk overhead, a man sat on the rail with the ease of someone who had been born sitting on rails, one boot swinging over a thirty-foot drop into darkness.

He was broad through the shoulders and narrow everywhere else, dressed in the layered greys and browns of a man who had made a profession of not being looked at, and he swung down the scaffold ladder with a fluid economy that said *smuggler* louder than any tattoo or scar. He landed close enough to talk and far enough to dodge.

"Brannic," he said, when she didn't speak. "You're the Sterling woman. The Guildmaster's shadow." His eyes flicked to the lump of the ledger under her arm, then to her coat, then—briefly, knowingly—to her ribs. "And you're running."

"I don't know you."

"No. But you know *of* me. I'm in your books, Mistress Sterling. Entry four-forty-one, Ledger Twelve. Smuggling tariff, unpaid, three years standing." He smiled, and it was a good smile, crooked and unhurried, the smile of a man who had decided the night was interesting. "Corvane called in every marker he owns tonight. Every runner, every fence, every debtor. The whole city is looking for you. I was sent to find you first."

Quentin's knife was in her hand now, low against her thigh. "Then do your job."

"My job," Brannic said, "was to bring you back. My *debt*, however, is to the guild, and the guild—" he tipped his head back toward the starless sky, and for a moment the humor drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and wearier underneath, "—the guild just lost its collateral. You can't collect on a world that's ending, Mistress Sterling. So here's my counteroffer. I get you out of Vhalsund. In exchange, when this is over, my entry burns. Four-forty-one. Gone. Clean slate."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I shout, and the six men in the next street who are also looking for you come running, and we both find out how the night goes." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "I'd rather not. You strike me as someone who bites."

*Everyone pays,* Quentin thought. That was the first law of the guild, older than any ledger. Everyone pays, and everyone collects. Brannic was simply negotiating his price in public, which was more honesty than Corvane had ever shown her.

"The entry burns," she said. "And you tell me how to reach the Ashfall road without passing a single guild eye."

"Ashfall." Something flickered across his face—recognition, maybe, or memory. "That's where you're from. The foundling house." He studied her a moment longer, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly, as if confirming a figure in a column. "All right. Deal. Keep the knife out if it makes you feel better. Follow me, stay off the lantern streets, and when I say *down*, you're already down."

* * *

He moved through the Unlit City like water finding cracks.

He took her through the rope-walk's gutted interior, along a beam walkway that swayed with their steps, out over a tanner's roof where the hides hung drying like flayed ghosts in the dark. He took her down through a cooperage, between stacks of staves that smelled of oak and iron, and paused there once, hand raised, while somewhere ahead boots passed and voices argued about reward money—*the Guildmaster's paying in memories now, real ones, fresh ones, a year of summer for the girl alive*—until the voices faded and Brannic's hand dropped and they flowed on.

A year of summer. Quentin turned the phrase over as they crossed a courtyard where a fountain had run dry, its stone nymph streaked white with frost that should not have existed yet, not for weeks. Frost. Already. The cold from the broken border was moving faster than any army.

They came out at last onto the river quay below the Weft, where fishing boats knocked against pilings in water gone black as ink, and Brannic stopped so abruptly that Quentin nearly walked into his back.

Bounty boards. Someone had nailed them up in the last hour—rough planks, rougher paper, and on every paper a charcoal sketch that made Quentin's stomach drop through the cobbles.

Her own face. Older than she remembered being, harder, but hers—the set of the jaw, the arch of the brows, rendered by someone who had clearly studied her closely and recently. Beneath it, in block letters:

*THE HEIR OF THE WARD-KINGS LIVES. THE ASHEN THRONE PAYS IN BLOOD-PRICE.*

Below that, smaller, in a different hand, a guild addendum pasted on like a parasite: *ALSO SOUGHT BY THE MNEMOSYNE GUILD FOR LEDGER IRREGULARITIES. REWARD NEGOTIABLE.*

Two bounties. One face. The warlord wanted her dead for a war fought before her birth; the guild wanted her back for a theft committed an hour ago; and both of them had plastered her across the city in the space of a single starless night.

"They work fast," Brannic murmured. There was no amusement in him now. He was reading the board the way she read ledgers—fast, thorough, missing nothing. "Warlord's heralds must've ridden through the gates before the wards even finished falling. They knew. Whoever he is, whatever he is—he *knew* this was coming." He glanced sideways at her. "Did you know? Does the vial know?"

"I intend to find out." Her voice came out flat and steady, and she was distantly proud of it. "Ashfall road. Now. Before whoever sketched that face decides to earn double."

Brannic pushed off from the wall—and then stopped, head tilting, listening. Down the quay, lantern-light bobbed. Six lights, moving in formation. Retrievers. She knew their gait; she had signed their expense accounts for years.

"Down," Brannic breathed, and Quentin was already down, folding herself into the shadow between a mooring post and a stack of crab pots, the vial cold against her heart, the ledger clutched to her chest like a child.

The lanterns swept closer. Frost glittered on the boards an inch from her nose. She could hear the retrievers talking—Corvane's voice reproduced in their mouths, patient and reasonable: *she'll go for the records, they always go for the records, watch the roads east.*

East. Toward Ashfall. Toward the truth.

Good, Quentin thought, pressing herself flatter against the frozen wood, fear and resolve fusing in her chest into something new and hard and bright, something that felt almost like a plan. Let them watch the east road.

Let them watch it while she took it.

The lanterns passed. Brannic's hand closed on her shoulder, pulling her up, pulling her onward, into the dark that had swallowed the stars—and somewhere behind them, faint over the water, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall.
