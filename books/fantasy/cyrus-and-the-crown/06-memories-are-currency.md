## 6. Memories Are Currency

Brannic had sold pieces of himself before, but never in front of a witness.

He told himself that was the only reason his hands were unsteady as they came down the crooked stair into the Lamplighter's Row — not the memory he was about to feed into the scale, but the woman following him, the guild's own ledger-keeper, watching everything with those gray eyes that weighed and priced by habit. Quentin Sterling walked like someone who had never had to count what she was worth because someone else kept the books for her.

The starless dark pressed down on the city like a lid on a pot. No stars, no moon, no wards — just the oil-lamps of the underworld guttering in their brackets, throwing more shadow than light. Brannic knew every one of these shadows. He'd grown up in them, back when there were still stars to be homesick for.

"You said a fence," Quentin murmured behind him. "Down here?"

"Where else? The guild doesn't buy from people like me. Guild prices are for merchants and magistrates with something worth forgetting." He ducked under a sagging beam strung with drying fish-skins. "Down here we sell what we can't afford to keep."

"And what's that?"

"Everything," Brannic said. "Everything except what keeps you alive."

* * *

The fence worked out of a chandler's shop that smelled of tallow and old smoke, behind a door with three locks and no sign. Maren was her name, though half the Row called her Mother Candle, because she melted herself down for other people's comfort and everyone pretended not to notice.

She opened the third lock before Brannic finished knocking, which meant she'd been listening, which meant she already knew who he'd brought.

"Brannic Vael." Her voice was gravel soaked in honey. "Twice in one year. The last time you swore on your mother's grave you'd never sit at my table again."

"My mother's grave is empty, Maren. You know that. I sold the grief of it to a priest in Harrowgate."

"I remember." She smiled without warmth and stepped aside. "I bought it. Come in before the dark does."

The shop's interior was warmer than the street, lit by a single lamp turned low. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves stood vials — hundreds of them, thousands, each stoppered and labeled in Maren's cramped hand. Not the guild's crystal and silver; these were cheap glass, corked with wax, the kind of vessels a fishmonger might use for brine. First kisses. Last words. A child's laughter. A man's whole belief in the gods, sold off in three installments when his daughter needed a surgeon.

Quentin stopped just inside the door. Brannic watched her look at the shelves, and watched the ledger-keeper's face do something he hadn't seen it do yet: go pale.

"This is—" she began.

"This is the other ledger," Brannic said. "The one nobody audits."

Maren settled herself at a scarred table where a brass balance sat waiting, its pans polished bright from use. On one pan rested a small wooden case of blank vials. On the other, nothing yet. That was how it always started. Empty against empty, until somebody put their life on the scale.

"Sit," Maren said. "Both of you. The lady too. I don't do business with people standing over me like bailiffs."

"We're not here for me," Quentin said.

"Everyone who says that is here for themselves." Maren poured something dark from a clay jug into three cups. It wasn't wine. Brannic didn't ask. "You're here because the world went black two nights ago and your guildmaster sealed his doors, and now you need passage out of a city that's about to eat itself. And you've come to me because the boy knows I'm the only soul in this district with river-papers that aren't forged." She looked at Brannic over her cup. "Aren't they?"

"They're real," Brannic said. "That's the problem. Real papers have real names on them."

"Mm." Maren set the cup down. "So. Passage upriver, past the toll-cutters, through the ice-fog that's coming off the border like a tide. Two souls, one boat, and a pilot who'll want paying in something that won't freeze or burn. You know my rates, Brannic. What have you got?"

And there it was. The question he'd been turning over in his head since the wards shattered, since he'd looked up into a sky with nothing in it and felt something in his chest crack open like river ice.

He had money. Not enough. He had favors owed across half the underworld. Not the right ones. He had one thing Maren wanted, and they both knew it, and both of them were letting the silence stretch because naming it made it real.

"The summer house," Brannic said finally.

Maren's eyebrows rose a fraction. Even Quentin leaned forward.

"Go on."

"You know the one. My grandmother's place, the orchard, the year the plums came in heavy and she let me climb the tree by the well even though my mother said I'd break my neck. Whole afternoon. Sun through the leaves. She was singing some old song in the valley tongue, the one about the river taking back what it lends." His throat tightened. He pushed through it. "I was eight. It's the oldest thing I have that isn't pain."

Maren was quiet a moment. For the first time since he'd known her, she looked almost gentle. Almost.

"That's a good one," she said softly. "That's a very good one. You could live another forty years on that memory, boy. Old men in the Hill pay fortunes for exactly that kind of thing — warm afternoons, dead grandmothers, plum trees. They drink it like brandy."

"I know what it's worth."

"Do you?" She tapped the balance with one finger. "Because I'll tell you what I see. I see a man who's spent twenty years selling himself off piece by piece so he could stay free of the guild, and now he's selling the last warm thing he owns to ferry a guild clerk out of the city. That's not commerce, Brannic. That's a eulogy."

"It's passage," Brannic said. "Name your price for the rest."

* * *

They haggled. Of course they haggled; that was the liturgy of the Row, and skipping it would have offended them both. Maren wanted the memory plus his recollection of the guild's river routes — worthless to anyone else, gold to her, since the retrievers would soon be buying pilots' knowledge and she intended to be the one selling it. Brannic countered that the routes were worth a second favor: the pilot's fee waived, and a sealed letter carried to a name in Greyharbor if he ever sent one.

In the end they shook on memory, routes, and one letter, and Maren drew a fresh vial from the wooden case and set it on the table between them like a small glass altar.

"Now," she said, all business again, "you know how this goes. I don't take memories out of your head with a hook like the guild does — that's butcher's work, leaves holes. Mine's slower. You hold the vial. You think of it. All of it, start to finish, every sense, every sound, and you *give* it. Not lose it. Give it. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you spend the rest of your life reaching for a hole or reaching for a gift you chose to pass along." She slid the vial toward him. "Most men can't tell the difference. But most men are fools."

Brannic picked up the vial. It was lighter than it should have been, the way all empty things are heavier than they look.

Behind him, Quentin spoke for the first time in minutes. "Wait."

Maren's eyes flicked up. "The lady has an objection?"

"How much of him goes into that glass?" Quentin asked. "Not the memory. Him. When the guild extracts, there's residue — I've seen the ledgers. Fragment loss. Emotional bleed. The client remembers *that* something happened but can't feel it anymore. Is that what happens here?"

Brannic almost laughed. Here she was, the Mnemosyne Guild's own bookkeeper, asking the fence whether her trade was crueler than his employer's.

Maren answered without looking away from him. "The guild takes. I receive. When a man gives me a memory freely, it comes out clean, because shame doesn't cling to a gift. Take a memory out of a man against his will and you tear it — you get the facts but the feeling stays in him like a splinter, festering. That's why guild clients wake up hollow. That's why your vaults are full of screaming, if anyone ever bothered to listen to the glass." She turned the vial in her fingers. "What I do isn't kind, girl. But it's honest. He'll still know he loved his grandmother. He'll know it the way you know a story someone told you. Warm, and far away, and not his anymore."

"Not his anymore," Quentin repeated quietly.

"That's the price of everything down here," Maren said. "Up in your towers you trade in other people's lives. Down here we trade in our own. Which of us do you think sleeps better?"

Neither of them answered. Brannic suspected neither of them could.

* * *

He held the vial to his forehead, the old way, and closed his eyes.

The orchard came up in him like water rising in a well. Heat first — the particular heat of a late summer noon, baking the stones around the well, making the plum leaves hang heavy and still. Then smell: bruised fruit, dust, his grandmother's soap, lye and lavender. Then sound, the worst part, the part that nearly broke him open right there at Maren's table — her voice, cracked and off-key, singing the river song in the valley tongue while below him he heard the pump handle squeak and his own eight-year-old laugh tumble out of the branches.

*Come down before you fall, little crow.*

*I'm a wren, Gran. Wrens fly.*

*Wrens fall too. Come down and hold the basket.*

He had climbed higher. He always climbed higher. And she had let him, because she was the only person in his whole childhood who understood that some things mattered more than safety — that an afternoon in a plum tree, with the sun coming through the leaves in coins of green-gold, was worth the risk of a broken neck. That joy was worth the risk of joy.

*Give it,* Maren had said. So he gave it. He opened his hands around the vial the way you'd cup water from a stream, and he let it pour.

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible thing. It felt like exhaling after holding your breath for twenty years. The afternoon left him gently, leaf by leaf, sunbeam by sunbeam, until the vial in his hands glowed faintly amber and his grandmother's voice faded to a hum and then to silence, and Brannic Vael sat in a tallow-scented shop in the dark under a starless sky knowing only that somewhere, sometime, someone had sung to him in a plum tree, and that he had loved her, and that the love was now a story instead of a scar.

When he opened his eyes, they were wet. He didn't wipe them. Let the ledger-keeper see. Let her write it down.

"Well," Maren said quietly, stoppering the vial and holding it up to the lamp. Amber light swirled inside it like trapped honey. "There she is. Hello, little crow." She set it on the shelf behind her, between a sailor's last sight of his home port and a widow's memory of her wedding night, and wrote a label in her cramped hand. *B.V. — Orchard, age 8. Do not dilute.*

"Do not dilute," Brannic managed.

"Some buyers like them watered down. Stretch the stock." Maren's mouth twisted. "I don't. Whatever else I am, I don't cut the good years with filler. People sell me enough of themselves. The least I can do is keep them whole."

* * *

Outside, the dark had thickened. The lamps along the Row burned lower than they should have, as if the cold coming off the border was drinking the flame. Brannic walked fast, hands jammed in his coat, and Quentin matched his stride in silence for half a street before she couldn't stand it anymore. He'd expected as much. Ledger-keepers audited everything, including their own consciences.

"Say it," he said.

"You gave up your oldest happy memory. For me. For a woman you met yesterday, who works for the people who ruined your river routes and probably half the rest of your life."

"For passage," Brannic corrected. "For you, for the vial you're carrying, and for whatever it is you're going to do that made the Guildmaster seal his own doors. Don't flatter yourself, clerk. I'm not noble. I did the arithmetic. My grandmother's orchard against a chance to matter, once, before the winter eats us all." He kicked a bottle out of his path and heard it roll away into the dark. "Besides. Now you know."

"Know what?"

"What it costs." He stopped at the corner where the Row met the flooded market square, and gestured with his chin at the scene below. "Look. Really look. You audit numbers in a vault. This is the ledger."

The square was full of them — the poor of the Unlit City, huddled around braziers and peddler's lanterns, and moving among them the memory-brokers with their trays and their scales, doing brisk business in the dark. Brannic watched Quentin watch them: a young woman trading the memory of her wedding day for a sack of flour and a blanket; an old man selling his daughter's face, one year at a time, ten years gone already, keeping only the last one because it was the year she died and grief was cheaper to carry than love; a boy of maybe twelve offering up the taste of his mother's cooking, and the broker haggling him down to just the smell of it, bread only, none of the kitchen, none of the humming.

"They'll buy it back someday," Quentin said. Her voice was thin. "If they earn enough. That's what the guild—"

"The guild buys back at triple rate and calls it mercy." Brannic's voice came out flat and hard. "My father sold his wedding day when I was six. Worked thirty years to buy it back. Died two winters ago, still four hundred marks short. My mother visits his grave and can't remember why she loved him. She remembers *that* she did. Just not why." He turned to face Quentin fully, and saw the lamplight catch the vial beneath her coat, the one she thought he didn't know about, the one that hummed against her ribs like a second heart. "That's the trade you keep books for. That's the balance you audit. So don't stand there looking wounded, clerk. You knew. You've always known. You just never had to watch."

Quentin didn't answer. She stood very still, the way people do when something large shifts inside them and they're afraid any movement will spill it. Below them, the brokers moved through the crowd with their little brass scales, weighing out human lives in drams and pennyweights, and the dark above had no stars in it at all.

Then she reached into her coat and drew out the vial.

Brannic's breath caught. Up close, in the brazier-light, it was beautiful — crystal older than the guild, silver-capped, filled with something that wasn't quite liquid and wasn't quite light, swirling slow as smoke in winter air. It hummed. He could feel it in his teeth.

"One memory," Quentin said, staring down at the square, at the boy trading his mother's kitchen for a loaf of bread. "One freely given memory is going to matter more than all of this. I don't know how I know that. But I know it the way you know a load-bearing wall." She looked at him then, and her gray eyes had changed — the auditor's flatness cracking, something fiercer showing through underneath. "Your grandmother's song. The river taking back what it lends. What were the words, Brannic? Before it became a story instead of yours. Tell me what the words were."

He closed his eyes. Reached for the gift he'd chosen to pass along, the warm far-away shape of it.

"*The river takes back what it lends,*" he recited slowly, "*but the sea gives back the drowned.*"

Quentin went white as the frost creeping in from the border.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's the covenant verse. That's the first line of the Ward-Kings' oath — it's in the archive seals, it's everywhere in the old records, and I never knew where it came from. It's not scripture. It's a *lullaby*. Your grandmother was singing ward-song, Brannic. Valley folk-song. The words the wards were built on." Her hand closed tight around the crystal vial until the knuckles went white. "The magic was never locked in bloodlines. It was hiding in *songs*. In kitchens. In people like you — people the guild never thought worth auditing."

Somewhere across the dark square, a bell began to ring — not the hour, not the alarm, but the deep triple toll of the guild's own tower, the sound Corvane's retrievers made when they had a scent.

Brannic took Quentin's arm. "Whatever you just figured out," he said, "figure it out running."
