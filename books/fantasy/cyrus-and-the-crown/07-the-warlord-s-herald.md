## 7. The Warlord's Herald

The city had learned new prayers overnight.

Quentin heard them as she moved through the Saltmarket in the grey hour before dawn—fragments of petition pressed close and urgent between strangers' teeth, the way coin used to be passed. *Keep my mother's face for me. Keep the road to Harrow Fen. Keep my own name.* The cold that had come crawling inland from the unwarded border was still a rumor here, three days' walk from the ice, but rumor traveled faster than frost, and faster than either traveled fear.

She kept her hood up and her eyes down and her hand in her pocket, curled around the vial like a talisman. It had been four days since she fled the Guildhall with a ledger under her coat and Brannic Vael's rough laugh at her back, four days since Corvane's voice had followed her down the stairs like a closing door: *You cannot run from your own inheritance, girl. I only ever wanted to help you carry it.* Four days, and already the city had changed shape around her, gone soft at the edges, lawless in its marrow. The constables who should have walked the market with lanterns were absent—or worse, present but idle, standing in doorways with their hands folded, watching the crowd with the careful blankness of men deciding which master to serve.

The stars were still gone. That was the thing that made her stomach clench every time she glanced up through a gap in the awnings: not darkness exactly, but absence, a sky like a page torn out of a book. She had spent her whole life beneath those stars without once thanking them, and now their silence felt personal.

She had come to the Saltmarket for bread and information, in roughly that order. What she got instead was the herald.

* * *

She heard him before she saw him—a sound like a horn being played underwater, long and low and wrong, rolling over the rooftops from the direction of the old assize court. The market stilled the way water stills when something large passes beneath it. Then the crowd began to move, not away from the sound but toward it, because terror in a starving city is also spectacle, and people will queue to watch their own doom arrive if there's nothing else to do.

Quentin should have gone the other way. Every instinct she had honed in fifteen years among ledgers said: *when the predator announces itself, be elsewhere.* But the crowd carried her, a river of shoulders and breath, and by the time she understood where she was being taken she was standing in the shadow of a spice merchant's stall, forty feet from the herald, with no hope of retreat.

He did not look like war. That was the first obscenity of it. He looked like a clerk.

He stood on the courthouse steps in a coat of ash-grey wool, unarmored, unhelmed, a lean man of middle years with a scribe's stoop and ink-stained fingers, holding a scroll he plainly did not need—the words, when they came, came from memory, worn smooth as a stair by repetition. Beside him, two soldiers in lacquered black stood with the terrible patience of furniture. Between them, planted in the courthouse stone itself as if grown there, stood a board of pale wood, seven feet tall, and on the board—

On the board was a face.

Quentin knew the technique. She had seen memory-portraits before, in the Guild's vaults: memories rendered onto treated vellum by a mnemonic artist, faithful down to the light in the eyes, because a memory does not know how to flatter. Someone had sold this portrait. Someone who had looked at her—no, not at her. Someone who had *remembered* her, and remembered well enough to sell the remembering.

It was her face. Not as she saw it in mirrors now, hollowed by four sleepless days, but as she had been at the Guildhall: hood back, lamplight on her cheekbones, mouth set in the particular grim line she made when Corvane lectured her. The portrait was captioned in a fine, formal hand:

**QUENTIN STERLING. ALSO KNOWN BY OTHER NAMES. HEIR OF THE WARD-KINGS. REWARD: WHAT YOU DESIRE MOST.**

Below the words, where the Ashen sigil should have been, was a small stamped image of a hearth with no fire in it.

"Good people of Cindral Vale." The herald's voice was mild, almost kind, the voice of a man reading a eulogy he had written himself. It carried anyway; the square seemed built to carry it, or the cold was. "I bring you terms. Not demands—terms. The Warlord knows you are not his enemies. The Warlord remembers—" and here he smiled, thin as a paper cut, "—that remembering is what none of you can do anymore."

A murmur ran through the crowd. Quentin felt it pass through her like current.

"You have lived under wards you did not make, paid for with blood you never saw spilled," the herald continued. "For nine hundred years, the Ward-Kings fed your borders with harvested memory—the childhoods of a subject people, poured into stone so that kings could sleep safe. You have forgotten this because they made you forget. We have not forgotten. We are the ones they harvested." He unrolled his scroll anyway, a gesture for rhythm rather than need. "The wards are fallen. The winter has begun. It will not be turned back by bloodline—it was *made* by bloodline—and so the last of that line must answer for it."

He let the scroll roll closed. His ink-stained finger rose and indicated, gently, the portrait on the board.

"The heir lives. The heir hides among you. Perhaps she buys bread from you. Perhaps she sleeps in your cellar. Look at this face. Look well, and hold the looking—for the reward is this: whatever memory you most regret surrendering, the Warlord's mnemonic-priests will return it to you whole. Your first love. Your mother's voice. Your name, if you have sold even that." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And for the heir herself: she will be brought to the Ashen Court, and there she will answer for the old war. The old accounts will be settled."

Someone near Quentin whispered, "Flayed," and someone else shushed them, and the word went through the crowd anyway, mouth to mouth, quiet as lice: *flayed, they say he means to flay her, not the skin—the memory, layer by layer—*

Quentin did not move. Fifteen years of standing perfectly still while powerful people discussed dangerous things held her spine straight and her breathing slow. Inside, she was doing arithmetic. It was the only prayer she knew.

The portrait was good. That was the arithmetic that mattered, the sum that made her hands cold despite her gloves. It wasn't a likeness from life. It was a likeness from *memory*, which meant someone in the Guildhall had sold a recollection of her—recently, within days, after the fall. Which meant Corvane had not merely sent retrievers after her. He had opened the vaults and auctioned her, piece by piece, to anyone who could pay, and the Warlord could pay in the one currency a starving city craved more than bread.

Her identity was not a secret anymore. It was inventory. And it was moving fast.

* * *

The herald finished, bowed—a small, courteous bow, obscene in its humility—and descended the steps. The black soldiers took up the board between them and began to walk, unhurried, along the market's edge, and Quentin watched the crowd's attention follow the portrait the way iron filings follow a lodestone. By nightfall, she understood, there would be boards like it in every market and crossroads from the Saltmarket to the sea-gates. The Guild had taught the city that memory was money. Now the Warlord had taught it that memory was betrayal—that your neighbor's stolen childhood might buy his mother's voice back, and all it would cost him was a stranger's face and a pointing finger.

She slipped away sideways, through the gap between the spice stall and a tanner's cart, moving with the unhurried gait she'd learned from watching smugglers work: never flee, merely *go*. Her heart was a fist striking at the inside of her ribs. Her mind was already three moves ahead, because that was the only place it was safe.

*They'll post the name,* she thought. *Sterling. They'll say Sterling, and half the city knows the Sterling ledger-clerk, the Guild's quiet little auditor, the woman who counted other people's sins for a living. I've signed receipts in six markets. I've haggled over the price of a fishmonger's first kiss.*

She had spent her entire adult life being memorable in precisely the ways that could not be traced—not her deeds, which left records, but her *presence*: the dry voice, the ink stain always on the left middle finger, the habit of correcting prices under her breath. Habits were fingerprints. She was wearing her own fingerprints everywhere.

She cut up Cooper's Lane, then down again through the rag-and-bone yard, taking the route Brannic had drilled into her the first night—"never trust a straight road, straight roads are for processions and corpses"—and surfaced behind the drowned chapel where they had sheltered two nights past. He wasn't there yet. He was late, which meant either trouble or trade, and with Brannic the two were often the same transaction wearing different coats.

While she waited, she did the thing she had forbidden herself to do, and opened the coat, and looked at the vial.

It was small enough to hide in a closed fist. Plain glass, plain cork, sealed with wax stamped by a hand three hundred years dead. Inside, suspended in a suspension that was not quite liquid and not quite light, her childhood turned slowly—her mother's kitchen, the smell of the seal-wax her father worked, the sound of the wards humming through the floorboards of a house she could not picture except as an absence shaped like a home. The Guild had taken it from her when she was seven. She had audited ten thousand vials since and never once let hers touch the scales.

*The heir of the Ward-Kings.* The herald's voice again, mild as milk. *The old accounts will be settled.*

She had thought, until this morning, that she feared the Guild. Corvane's retrievers, the sealed ledgers, the long institutional patience of an organization that had buried empires in its cellars and called it archiving. That fear had a shape she understood. This was different. This was the discovery that the whole world outside the Guild's walls had been waiting for her too—that her face had a price tag in every language, and the price was other people's hearts, and the poor would sell her for less than the rich would, because the poor had less to lose and everything to want back.

She thought of the fence in Gull Street, the one Brannic had traded a happy memory to for passage—had shown her how it was done, the little brass reader, the fence's bored tenderness, the moment when Brannic came back from wherever memories go when they're extracted, blinking like a man surfacing, saying, "What? What did I just—" and then stopping, because he couldn't remember what he couldn't remember. She thought of how many people in that square this morning had sold something they wept over, and how many of them were walking home right now with the herald's offer ringing in their ears: *whatever memory you most regret surrendering, returned to you whole.*

All it costs is me.

"They'll find you by nightfall," said Brannic's voice behind her, and she spun, hand going to the knife at her hip, and found him leaning in the chapel's broken doorway with snow—or not-snow, the strange grey fallout of the starless sky—in his hair, and no smile anywhere on him.

"You're late," she said, because her voice needed something ordinary to do.

"I'm late because I took the long way, and I took the long way because there are men on the short way asking questions about a woman with ink on her fingers." He pushed off the doorframe and came inside, out of the falling grey, and in the dim light his face looked older than it had four days ago, or perhaps it had simply stopped pretending. "There are six boards up already. Six that I counted, and I count badly. By tomorrow there'll be sixty. By the day after, every soul in this city who's missing a memory will be walking around with your face folded in their pocket like a prayer."

"Then we leave tonight."

"We leave tonight," he agreed, "but not the way you're thinking. You're thinking south, over the marsh causeway. Everyone thinks south; the boards'll be thick as reeds on the causeway by morning. We go north."

"North is the border. North is the winter."

"North is the archive." He said it quietly, watching her face. "You told me about it. The place your ancestors kept their records—the forge-records, the harvest-records. If we're going to understand what's hunting you, we go where the hunting started." He crouched by the cold chapel hearth and began, with the economy of long habit, to lay a fire no larger than a cupped hand. "Besides. There's a smuggler's gate at Northgate that owes me. Owes the guild, really, but the guild's credit died with its stars, so it'll have to owe me."

Quentin stood a moment longer with the vial against her heart, listening to the grey fall softly on the chapel's broken roof, and understood that she was grieving. Not for the danger—danger she could price, danger had a column and a sum—but for the smaller death happening underneath it. All her life she had been nobody, and nobody had been a kind of armor. Now the world had named her, priced her, and nailed her name to wood in every square, and the armor was gone, and what remained underneath was a question she had been avoiding since the night the stars went out:

If the heir of the Ward-Kings was going to answer for the old war—if the whole machinery of two armies was turning toward her like a millstone—then who was she, actually? Quentin Sterling, the auditor, the woman who kept the ledgers of impossible things?

Or just the last entry in someone else's account, waiting to be settled?

"Quentin," Brannic said, and she realized he had said it twice. "Fire's lit. Road's waiting. Whatever storm you're having, have it walking."

She closed her coat over the vial. Outside, far off toward the assize court, a horn sounded again—underwater, endless, patient—and somewhere a crowd murmured, and somewhere a hand went up to point, and the hunt found its first legs.

They went north, into the dark where the stars should have been.
