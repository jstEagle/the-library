## 8. The Archive Beneath the Ice

**Quentin**

The cold came up through the stones like a voice from a grave.

Quentin felt it first in her boots, then in her teeth, then somewhere deeper—in the place where the vial rode against her sternum inside its wrapping of wool and oilcloth. Three days out of the city, three days of starless dark and roads that had forgotten they were roads, and now the mountain rose before them, black against the blacker sky, its summit sheathed in ice that caught no light because there was no light to catch.

"There," Brannic said, and pointed.

She saw it the way you see things in dreams: a seam in the mountainside, a doorway too regular to be natural, framed in stone that even the frost could not claim. The archive. Her ancestors' house of records. The place the dying wardwright's word had sent them—*Ashgrove*, he'd gasped, *the Ashgrove vault beneath the glacier*, and then his heart had quit like a lamp run dry.

"You're certain this is it," Brannic said. Not quite a question. He was checking her certainty against his own doubt, weighing one against the other the way he weighed everything—like a man who had learned that certainty was just another currency, and most people were spending counterfeit.

"The wardwright drew me a map in his own blood." She said it flatly, because the truth of it still sat strangely on her tongue. A man had died pressing a finger to his own opened vein so that a stranger would know where to walk. "And the door matches."

"Match what?"

"The sigil." She traced it without touching: a tree whose branches held up seven stars, carved deep into the lintel. The same tree that bloomed across every ledger cover in the Mnemosyne Guild's counting rooms. The guild had stolen its emblem from her family, or her family had lent it and never been repaid. She no longer knew which possibility disgusted her more.

Brannic built no fire at the threshold. Neither did she ask him to. Some doors you did not warm yourself outside of; some thresholds you crossed cold or not at all.

The seal on the door had been blood-locked once—she could feel the old craft humming under her palm, patient as a spider—but the wards' collapse had loosened something here too. When she pressed her hand flat and spoke the only name she had: *"Sterling,"* the lock considered it, found it wanting, and broke anyway, too starved for any bloodline to refuse a knock.

The door swung inward on air that had not moved in two hundred years.

It smelled of paper and pine resin and something older underneath—cold stone, yes, but also cold iron, the smell of the forge her blood remembered even if she did not.

"Ladies first," Brannic murmured, and when she glanced back at him his grin didn't reach his eyes. Nothing about Brannic reached all the way anywhere. That was how he survived.

They went down together into the mountain, and the mountain swallowed the sound of their footsteps whole.

* * *

**Brannic**

Brannic had robbed tombs before. It paid better than honest work and the dead complained less than the living. But he had never walked into a tomb that was still, in some stubborn fashion, *occupied*.

The stairs descended in long switchbacks, each landing marked with a lantern niche. In the third niche, impossibly, a flame still burned—a bead of blue fire no bigger than a thumbnail, feeding on nothing, shedding light like an apology. Wardlight. He'd seen it once, years ago, in a border fort, before the forts went dark.

"It shouldn't still be lit," Sterling said. Her voice echoed wrong down there; the stone ate the edges off her words. "Unless—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at the thought the way you watch someone step into freezing water: reluctantly, all at once. "Unless the wards are still drawing. Somewhere. Even broken."

"Drawing from what?"

"I don't know yet."

But her hand had gone to her chest, to the wrapped shape beneath her coat, and Brannic thought: *she knows more than she's saying.* Everyone did. That was the whole architecture of the world, far as he could tell—everyone carrying their little sealed vials, trading scraps, hoarding the rest.

He kept his hand near his knife and followed her down.

The archive opened around them at last like the inside of a bell: a vast circular chamber, ringed with shelves cut directly from the living rock, ascending in galleries toward a ceiling lost in darkness. And everywhere, *everywhere*, books. Ledgers. Folios bound in leather gone grey with age, scrolls packed in cedar tubes, loose pages weighted under river stones. Tens of thousands of them. Hundreds of thousands.

"By the drowned gods," he breathed. His voice came back to him flattened, reverent despite himself. "What is all this?"

"Records." Sterling stood very still in the middle of it, turning slowly, and Brannic saw her face do something complicated. "Every memory the Ward-Kings harvested. Every extraction. Catalogued. Filed." She laughed—one short, ugly note with no humor in it at all. "My family weren't kings, Brannic. They were *clerks*. They were clerks with a monopoly."

He understood ledgers. He'd spent half his life dodging men who carried them. So he understood, faster than she perhaps wanted him to, what a room full of harvest-ledgers meant.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

She didn't answer. She'd already found the nearest shelf and pulled a folio free, and whatever was written on its first page stopped her breath.

He left her to it. That was the arrangement between them—he handled doors, locks, exits, and the practical business of staying alive; she handled whatever it was that lived behind her eyes. He took the perimeter instead, counting gallery mouths, noting the secondary stairwell in the east wall, testing the draft that whispered down from somewhere above. Old habits. The debt he owed the guild was paid in full the night he'd gotten her over the city wall, but habits outlived debts the way scars outlived wounds.

That was when he noticed the ice.

Not the glacier above—the ice *inside*. Along the base of the lowest shelf-runs, where the floor met the rock, frost had crept inward in perfect, deliberate lines, like veins seen through skin. And in the frost, faint as breath on glass: shapes. Small ones. Handprints. Dozens of them, hundreds, pressed into the ice from below, as though the cold itself remembered everyone who had ever been brought down here and pressed against it.

Brannic decided, firmly, not to think about that.

"Sterling," he called instead. "How long are we staying?"

But she wasn't listening anymore. She was reading.

* * *

**Quentin**

*Extraction Record, 4th Year of the Reign of Queen Maren Ashgrove.*

*Subject: Tomas of the Fenmarch villages. Age eleven.*

*Yield: the memory of his mother's face. Quality: exceptional. The subject wept throughout the procedure and asked afterward whether he might be permitted to see her again. Request denied per standing protocol. Subject released to the eastern labor camps with standard severance (bread, two copper bits, a coat).*

Quentin set the folio down. Then picked it up again, because putting it down felt like agreeing it could stay closed forever, and someone owed Tomas of the Fenmarch the small dignity of being read.

Her hands were shaking. She noted that the way she noted everything now—clinically, from a slight distance, an auditor observing her own deterioration. The shaking was new. So was the cold in her fingertips that never quite warmed, and the way names sometimes slid away from her mid-sentence like fish through a net.

*Frost-eating*, the refugees had called it. The cold that came inland from the unwarded border and took recollection first, gnawing outward memories before it touched flesh. It was supposed to be a thing that happened to other people, farther north, closer to the breach.

She turned another page.

*Subject: unnamed girl, age six, presented by her father for assessment. Remarkable density of formative memory. Recommended for the Crown Reserve. Extraction scheduled upon the mother's completion of the ward-sealing rites.*

The page blurred. She blinked, and it was still there, the ink patient, the handwriting elegant—the same handwriting, she realized with a lurch, as the letters in her mother's hand that the guild had sold her fragments of, years ago, before she knew whose hand it was.

*The Crown Reserve.* She said the words aloud and the chamber repeated them back to her from a dozen galleries, mocking.

"What is it?" Brannic's voice, close. He'd come down from the perimeter without her noticing. He did that—moved quiet as spilled sand.

"They didn't power the wards with their own memories." Her voice sounded like someone else's. "I always assumed—I don't know what I assumed. That the royal line gave of itself. That it was a burden they bore." She looked up at him, and his face, usually so carefully arranged, had gone genuinely grim. "They bore nothing. Every generation, the reigning monarch fed the wards with *harvested* memory. Taken from subjects. From children. There's a reserve—a vault of stored memory, centuries deep—and the wards drink from it, and when it runs low, they refill it."

"From who?"

"From whoever was convenient." She gestured at the shelves, at the tens of thousands of folios, each one a person filed and numbered. "Farmers. Prisoners. Orphans bought at market rates. And once every generation or so, someone with a particularly rich inheritance—someone whose childhood was dense enough, potent enough, to matter." She heard herself continue, distantly, like a woman reading another woman's audit aloud. "Like a six-year-old girl whose mother had just finished sealing the northern wards. Whose bloodline made her memories worth more than a village's worth of ordinary lives."

Brannic said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had lost its smuggler's drawl entirely. "You."

"My mother sealed the wards. My memory was taken to fuel them. I've been telling myself it was a sacrifice—that my parents hid me from the warlord, protected me, paid some terrible price for love." She pressed her palm flat against the open folio, against Tomas of the Fenmarch's eleven-year-old grief. "But look at the accounting, Brannic. Look at the *entries*. Bread, two coppers, a coat. Standard severance. I wasn't hidden. I was *harvested*. And then—"

"And then what?"

She turned to the final page of the folio, the disposition record, the entry every guild clerk learned to write last:

*Subject released with standard erasure. Retained by the Crown for observation. Note: subject displays unusual resilience; recommend full removal of lineage context at earliest opportunity. Memory of the extraction itself transferred to secure storage. Vessel designation: ASH-1.*

Vessel designation. As if her childhood were a cask of wine.

"She couldn't do it," Quentin whispered. The understanding arrived all at once, whole, terrible. "My mother. She finished sealing the wards and then she couldn't hand her daughter's memory to the reserve. She stole it. Vessel ASH-1—she took it out of the vault and hid it inside her own child, wiped from the official record, and then she had my whole childhood taken from *me* so I'd never go looking for it. So no one could trace it back." Her laugh came out cracked. "She saved me from the harvest by making sure I'd never know what I'd lost. She buried the crime and the treasure in the same hole."

"That's not saving," Brannic said slowly. "That's—"

"Bookkeeping. Yes." She closed the folio. "Welcome to my family, Brannic. We've been in the memory trade for nine hundred years. The Mnemosyne Guild didn't invent the trade in impossible things." She looked up at the galleries rising into darkness, shelf after shelf after shelf, the frost-handprints gleaming pale along the floor like a congregation kneeling. "We *franchised* it."

Somewhere above them, in the dark beyond the ceiling, something shifted—a groan of stressed stone, or ice settling, or the mountain itself turning over in its sleep.

And beneath her coat, against her sternum, the vial pulsed once, faintly warm, like a heart remembering it had a body.

* * *

**Brannic**

Brannic had heard men talk themselves into despair before. It was a common condition among the ruined. But watching Sterling stand in the middle of her ancestors' crimes, cataloguing them with an accountant's precision while her own hands trembled—there was something worse than despair in it. There was *inheritance*.

"You can't fix this," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out anyway, rough. "Whatever you're thinking. Whatever plan is forming behind those eyes. You're one woman with a bottle of your own childhood and a head start on frostbite. The wards are down. The stars are out. The Ashen Warlord has half a million soldiers who think your family ate his people alive—which, from the looks of these shelves, they did. You can't un-eat them."

"No," she agreed.

"You can't pay back ten thousand harvests."

"No."

"So what exactly—"

"I'm going to read every ledger in this room." She said it simply, the way she said everything, and began climbing the stair to the second gallery. "Every entry. Every name. Because if the wards ran on stored memory, then the reserve still exists somewhere, and if the reserve exists, the wards can be refed. And if they can be refed—" she paused on the landing, silhouetted against the blue wardflame, "—then maybe they can be fed differently. I need to know how the plumbing works before I decide what flows through it."

He stared at her. "You want to rob your own family's vault and rewire the border of civilization."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounds insane every way I say it."

"Good." Something flickered across her face—not a smile, nothing so generous, but the ghost of where a smile might have lived before the world ended. "Then we understand each other."

He should have left. That was the sane arithmetic. The debt was paid, the roads were dying, and the woman he'd agreed to guide was planning to commit treason against a dynasty that had been dust for two centuries and a guild that very much was not. Every survival instinct he owned told him to walk back down the glacier, find a fence in the lowlands, sell the location of this archive to the Warlord's heralds, and buy himself a long life in a warm country.

Instead he found himself following her up the stairs, into the dark, toward the shelves of the harvested dead.

Because—and he examined this feeling with the suspicion it deserved—because she had said *every name*. And Brannic had grown up in the fen-country too, the same drowned marches as Tomas of the Fenmarch, and he had a suspicion, cold and growing, that if she read far enough, she would find his grandmother's village in one of these folios. Sold. Filed. Severance: bread, two coppers, a coat.

Someone ought to be reading those names.

He took down the nearest folio and began, unwillingly, to help.

Above them, the mountain groaned again, longer this time. And far away—too far to be real, too close to deny—they both heard it: a sound like a great bell struck underwater, rolling up through the stone from the direction of the border.

The second wardline, falling.

Quentin's hand went to the vial. "We have less time than I thought," she said.

Brannic blew out a breath that steamed in the wardlight. "Story of the year," he muttered, and climbed faster.
