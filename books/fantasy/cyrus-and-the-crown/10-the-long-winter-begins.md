## 10. The Long Winter Begins

The frost came inland the way rumor came to a city: slowly at first, in whispers at the edges, then all at once, everywhere, undeniable.

Quentin saw it first on the window of the wayhouse where they'd stopped three nights out from the archive—a fern of ice spreading across the glass like something growing rather than freezing. She had watched it for a long time, her breath fogging a small circle that the frost slowly reclaimed, and she had thought: *that's not weather.* Weather did not move with intention. Weather did not climb.

Brannic had gone down to argue with the waykeeper about horses. Quentin heard his voice through the floorboards—low, patient, wearing that particular smuggler's tone he used when he was being cheated and wanted the cheater to believe otherwise—and she was grateful for it. Grateful that someone else could do the talking while she stood at the glass and watched winter arrive ahead of schedule, months early, from a border that no longer had wards to hold it back.

The fields beyond the wayhouse were silvered. Not dusted—*silvered*, as if someone had poured molten light across the barley and let it set. The stalks glittered without warmth. There was no sun worth mentioning; there had been no sun worth mentioning since the stars went out, only a flat gray ceiling of cloud that pressed down on the world like a lid on a pot. Under that lid, everything was cooling by degrees, and the degrees were getting smaller, which meant the cold was getting closer together, which meant—

She made herself stop doing arithmetic. Numbers were how the guild had trained her mind to move, ledger-lines and balances, and right now the numbers were telling her things she didn't want to know.

* * *

They joined the road south two days later, and the road was full of what the frost had made.

Refugees, mostly. Families with carts, families without carts, families carrying their children because the children could not be trusted to walk—the cold, they said, took the young first, or perhaps the young simply wandered, because wandering was what the cold *made* you do. Quentin walked among them in her guild-gray cloak with its hood up, listening the way she had once listened across an audit table: patiently, attentively, cataloguing.

An old woman shared her fire on the second night, unasked, because there was no longer any reason to hoard warmth against strangers when the whole world was becoming one long stranger.

"They say it eats remembering," the woman said. She had a face like crumpled vellum and hands that kept working at a knot of yarn whether or not there was yarn there. "My sister went out to the well one morning in the white country, before we left. Came back and looked at me like I was a lodger. Asked me when I meant to go." The yarn-knotting stopped. Started again. "She remembered the well fine. Remembered the bucket, the rope, the crack on the third stone. Just couldn't fit me in anywhere. Like a house with a room bricked up."

"The cold took her memory?" Quentin asked carefully.

"Took the *warm* ones first," the old woman said. "That's how you can tell it's coming for you. You forget your wedding day before you forget your debts." She laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "Most folk round here have more debts than weddings, so they last a bit."

Around the fire, other refugees nodded, and some of them did not nod, and Quentin understood after a moment that the ones who did not nod were the ones who had already lost the memory of having been told. Their faces held a pleasant blank attention, the look of people hearing a fireside tale for the first time. One man, broad-shouldered, with a scar across his knuckles from some life he might still recall, leaned toward the old woman with genuine curiosity and said, "Is that true? It takes the warm ones first?"

And the old woman looked at him for a long moment, and then she reached over and took his hand, and told him the story again, from the beginning, kindly, the way you feed someone who has forgotten they are hungry.

Quentin sat very still and felt the frost on the inside of her ribs.

* * *

It began small, what happened to her. Small things. That was what made it terrible; the large things announce themselves, but the small things simply fail to arrive.

On the third day south she realized she could not remember the color of the Guildmaster's door. She had walked through that door every morning for nine years. Oak, she knew it was oak, she could produce the word—but the color would not come. Brown, presumably. All doors were brown. But once there had been a specific brown, a dark honeyed grain she had seen ten thousand times, and it was gone, and in its place was a smooth pale patch like the bricked-up room the old woman had described.

On the fourth day she forgot the name of the clerk who had trained her. She remembered his face perfectly—long jaw, ink-stained cuff, the way he tapped twice on a ledger line before entering a figure—but his name had been... it began with... it had been a name, a real name, a name she had spoken daily for years—

"Vessal," she said aloud, suddenly, standing in the middle of the road so abruptly that Brannic nearly walked into her.

"What?"

"His name was Vessal." Relief flooded her so hot and sudden she felt dizzy. "I thought I'd lost—I thought—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, where the vial hung beneath her shirt on its cord, cool as always. Too cool. It had always been cool, hadn't it? Hadn't it?

Brannic studied her. He had gotten good at studying her in the weeks since the flight from the guild, in the way of men who navigate by reading weather. "You're forgetting things."

"I'm tired."

"You're forgetting things," he said again, not cruelly, the way he'd say *it's going to rain*. "Started two days ago. You called me Tamir this morning."

"I did not."

"You called me Tamir and then you apologized to Tamir for keeping him waiting." He shifted the pack on his shoulder. "I've been Tamir since dawn."

Quentin opened her mouth to deny it and found, behind the denial, the actual memory surfacing: herself, shaking him awake in the gray half-light, saying *Tamir, we have to move,* and meaning it completely, meaning it with a whole history attached—a history in which a man named Tamir had apparently existed, slept beside her watch, earned her urgency—and then the history folding up like a fan, leaving only Brannic's bewildered face and her own rising confusion.

"Oh," she said.

"Yeah," said Brannic.

"It's the cold," she said. "Everyone's losing—it's in the air now, it's—" But even as she said it her hand had risen, without her permission, to close around the vial through her shirt. Cool glass. Cool as riverbed stone. Cool as it had always—

*Had it always been cool?*

She tried to remember the vial warm. She tried to remember holding it in the vault, in the dark of her own keeping, all those years—the one thing in the entire Mnemosyne Guild she had never sold, never traded, never entered in any ledger except as a blank line with her own private mark beside it. Surely she had held it. Surely her hand had known its weight, its temperature, its small stubborn presence against her palm during the worst nights. But the memories she reached for came back wrong, came back *frosted*, the vault dim and silvered, her own fingers white with cold in a recollection that should have been lit by lamplight, and she understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only alongside dread, that the frost had gotten into her past. Not her future. Her *past*. The cold was not approaching her memories. It was eating them from the far end backward, the oldest first, the warmest first, exactly as the old woman said.

And underneath that understanding, colder than either: *or it isn't the cold at all.*

*Or the vial is leaking.*

Her childhood was in that vial. Sealed, purchased back, hidden—her mother's face, her mother's hands on the ward-stones, whatever terrible thing had been done to her at seven years old that had required the removal of seven years. And if the seal was failing—if the contents were bleeding out into her the way ink bleeds through wet paper—then she wasn't being robbed. She was being *dosed*. Fed herself drop by drop, in an order she didn't choose, at a rate she didn't control.

"Quentin." Brannic's voice, careful. "Your eyes just did something."

"Did something."

"Went somewhere. Like a shutter closing." He glanced up at the flat gray sky, then at the ragged column of refugees around them, the carts, the children, the old woman with her endless yarn. He lowered his voice. "Half these people are walking away from places they can't remember anymore. You can see it. They keep checking their pockets. Keep counting their kids." His jaw worked. "If it's getting into you faster—"

"It's not the cold," she heard herself say. "Or not only. Brannic, the vial—when we get to the archive, I may have to open it."

He stared. In all the weeks since the guild, since the dying wardwright had gasped her true name into the dust, she had guarded that vial like a heart outside her body, and she had told Brannic exactly why: *the moment it opens, whoever I was comes back, and I don't know if there'll be room in me for who I am.* He had built their whole route around keeping it closed.

"You don't know what's in there," he said.

"No," Quentin agreed. "But I'm starting to learn what's coming *out*."

* * *

That night the frost reached the camp.

Not gradually. Not creeping along the ground the way it had crept across the barley. At moonset—or at what would have been moonset, under a sky that had offered no moon in weeks—the cold simply arrived among the tents and blankets like a tax collector, and the temperature dropped so fast that breath cracked audibly in the air, and the fire shrank to a sullen red eye and then to embers and then to a shape that had perhaps never quite been fire at all.

People woke screaming, but quietly, the way you scream when you've learned that loud noise draws the wrong kind of attention. Mothers stuffed straw into clothing. Men beat their arms across their chests and muttered names—not prayers, Quentin realized. *Names.* Their own names, repeated like counters on an abacus, like coins gripped in a fist. *Marek. Marek. Marek.* Holding onto themselves by enumeration.

Quentin sat with her back against a cartwheel and her knees drawn up and did the same, silently. *Quentin Sterling. Keeper of ledgers, third rank. Quentin Sterling.* It steadied her the way numbers always had.

Then, unbidden, unearned, a memory surfaced that was not hers to have.

A kitchen. Low ceiling, black beams, a smell of bread and tallow and cold stone. A woman's hands—strong, scarred across the knuckles in a pattern Quentin somehow recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in a warped mirror—kneading dough, and a child's voice asking a question, and the woman answering in a language that was almost common speech but older, accented, worn smooth like a river stone:

*"—and when the crown is worn, the crown wears back, little bird. That is the price of the forging. We pay it so no one else must. Say it back."*

*"We pay it so no one else must."*

*"Good girl. Now help me with the loaves."*

The memory ended. It lasted four seconds. It left Quentin shaking so hard her teeth chattered, though the cold could not be entirely blamed.

Her mother. That was her mother's voice. That was her mother's *hands*. That was a piece of her stolen childhood escaping the vial like heat through a cracked door—and it was warm, gods, it was warm, the bread-smell and the lamplight and the love in that worn-old accent, warmer than anything she had felt since the stars went out, and it was already fading, the edges frosting over even as she clawed at it, the black beams going silver, the dough turning white, the voice thinning into wind until only the refrain remained, floating:

*We pay it so no one else must.*

"Gone," she whispered. "It's gone. I had it and it's *gone*—"

Across the dead fire, Brannic sat up sharply. "What's gone? Quentin. What did you see?"

She couldn't answer. Her hand was around the vial again and now, finally, unmistakably, the glass was *cold*—colder than the night, colder than the frost, cold with a purpose, cold like a held breath—and through her shirt, against her palm, she felt it pulse. Once. Softly. Like something knocking.

Like something asking, politely, from the far side of seven sealed years, to be let out.

Far down the refugee column, a child began to cry, and then stopped, mid-wail, and Quentin heard the mother's voice rise in raw panic, calling a name over and over, a name the child had apparently, in one night, in one breath of white air, misplaced forever.

"We move at first light," Brannic said grimly, reaching for his boots. "Faster than first light. Whatever's chasing us—the guild, the warlord, the winter itself—we outrun it or we bury it. Because if that thing in your shirt knocks one more time, Quentin, I want us both somewhere warded when you decide whether to answer."

Quentin looked down at the small cold weight against her chest, pulsing again—twice now, patient, insistent, *hers*—and heard herself say, in a voice she barely recognized:

"It isn't asking whether to knock again, Brannic."

She lifted her eyes to him, and he went still, because whatever he saw in her face made him reach instinctively for the knife at his hip.

"It's starting to open on its own."
