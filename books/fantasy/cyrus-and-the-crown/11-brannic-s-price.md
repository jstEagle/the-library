## 11. Brannic's Price

The cold came down from the border like a debt collector.

Brannic felt it first in his teeth—a deep, mineral ache that had nothing to do with the season and everything to do with the frost creeping inland from the unwarded north. Three days out of the ruined archive, and the world was forgetting itself street by street. He'd watched an old woman in Harrow's Cross stand in the middle of the road, staring at her own hands as if she couldn't remember whose they were. He'd seen a dog howl at an empty doorway for an hour, waiting for a master it no longer recalled. The cold didn't kill you quick. It ate you slow, starting with the small things—the name of your first kiss, the smell of your mother's kitchen—until there was nothing left inside you but weather.

And Quentin walked ahead of him through it like a woman carrying a lit lantern in a flood.

She kept the vial against her sternum, wrapped in oilcloth and cord, tucked beneath her shirt where a pilgrim might wear a relic. She touched it constantly. Not clutching—checking. The way a gambler checks his last coin. Brannic had smuggled enough dangerous cargo in his years running the guild's dirty errands to know what that gesture meant. She wasn't guarding a thing.

She was guarding herself.

*Don't think about it,* he told himself, for the hundredth time since the archive. *You owe the guild one job. Get her where she needs to go, collect your clean slate, walk away.* That was the arithmetic. That was all it ever was with him—arithmetic. Weigh what you're carrying against what you owe, and never once look too long at either.

Except he'd started looking.

* * *

They made Saltmere by nightfall—or by what passed for nightfall now, the sky a flat grey lid pressing down on the town like a hand on a coffin lid. No stars. No moon worth the name. Just the sick amber glow of the harbor lamps, burning whale-oil because the light-wards along the quay had died with everything else.

Saltmere was a smuggler's town, which meant it was honest about being dishonest, which meant Brannic loved it the way other men loved their mothers. The taverns still poured. The fences still fenced. The only law anyone acknowledged was the law of the open palm—and even that had gone strange since the wards fell. Memory-fences had tripled their rates. Half the docksiders were walking around with gaps in their heads where the rent money used to be.

"The Gull's Tooth," Brannic said, nodding toward a squat stone building at the end of Rope Walk, its sign creaking on rusted hinges. "We can get a room, a boat, and passage south before the tide turns."

Quentin pulled her hood lower. "You have contacts here."

"I have *debts* here." He said it lightly. It wasn't light. Every favor he'd ever banked in Saltmere had been borrowed against the guild's patience, and the guild's patience had run out somewhere around the time Corvane's retrievers started asking after a woman with ash-grey eyes and a ledger under her arm.

He'd seen the notice nailed to the tollhouse outside town. Not ink this time—*blood*, or something that looked like it, painted in the Ashen Warlord's crude heraldry. THE HEIR OF THE WARD-KINGS LIVES. And beneath the warlord's mark, in smaller, neater script, the guild's own seal, pressed into wax like a brand into flesh.

Two hunters. One quarry. And Brannic standing squarely in between, holding the only map to where she was going.

* * *

The trouble started before they reached the tavern door.

Brannic smelled it first—tallow smoke and cold iron, the particular stink of guild-issue cloak wax. Then he saw them: three figures at the mouth of Rope Walk, standing too still for the crowd flowing around them. Retrievers moved wrong, always had. Like puppets who'd studied people from the inside. Grey cloaks, hoods back, faces bare and forgettable on purpose. The guild didn't waste glamour on its hounds. It just taught them not to be remembered.

"*Back door*," Brannic breathed, and caught Quentin's elbow, and turned them both down the alley beside the chandlery with the smooth, unhurried gait of a man who had done this a hundred times and intended to do it a hundred more. Don't run. Running is a confession written on the body.

But the alley had a man in it already.

He stepped out from behind a stack of salt-barrels, and Brannic knew him, and knowing him was worse than not. Vess. Junior retriever when Brannic had last seen him, six years ago, the night Brannic had traded away the only secret the guild ever feared he might sell. Now Vess wore the senior clasp at his throat, silver worked into the shape of an open book, and his eyes had the flat patience of a man reading a ledger line he'd known was coming.

"Brannic Hale." Vess's voice was mild as milk. "You've been hard to find. Or easy to find, depending on how you look at it. We knew you'd come to Saltmere eventually. A man runs to the ports he trusts."

"Vess." Brannic put himself half a step in front of Quentin without seeming to. Old habit. Old lie—he wasn't protecting her so much as positioning himself between the blade and whatever he could bargain with. "You're looking well. Guild feeding you?"

"The guild feeds everyone who balances." Vess's gaze slid past Brannic's shoulder, unhurried, and settled on Quentin. Something flickered in his face—not recognition. *Appetite.* "Quentin Sterling. Keeper of Ledgers. The Guildmaster asks after you warmly."

"He has a funny way of asking."

"He does." Vess smiled without warmth, the way ice smiles when it cracks. "Here is how tonight goes, and I'll say it once, because I respect you both and I dislike repeating myself. You will come with me. The ledger comes with you. The vial—" his eyes touched the place beneath Quentin's coat where the oilcloth bundle sat, and Brannic saw her whole body go rigid, "—the vial comes with us, and no one is hurt, and the Guildmaster will be generous, because the Guildmaster is always generous to assets that cooperate."

"And if we don't cooperate?"

Vess tilted his head, genuinely curious. "Then the two men behind you take your legs, and we carry what's left. I told you I disliked repeating myself. I didn't say I disliked the alternative."

Brannic heard them then—the soft double footfall at the alley mouth, the whisper of drawn steel kept low against a thigh. His heart was doing arithmetic again, fast and ugly. Two blades behind, one retriever ahead, a dead-end alley, a woman who couldn't fight and wouldn't run, and a bundle against her chest worth more than the town they stood in.

*You owe the guild one job,* said the old voice in his head. *Step aside. Hand her over. Your slate wipes clean, your debts dissolve, and you walk out of this winter with your skin.*

*And then you spend the rest of your life remembering the sound she makes when they take her.*

*If you can still remember anything at all, this far north, this close to the cold.*

"No," Brannic said.

Vess sighed. "I hoped you'd say yes. But I confess I budgeted for no."

* * *

It went fast and bad, the way these things always do. Brannic got a knife up in time to turn the first blade—not stop it, turn it, steel shrieking off steel—and drove his shoulder into the second man's chest and put him into the salt-barrels hard enough to crack two of them. White grains spilled across the cobbles like snow that had given up pretending. Quentin did something with her ledger—swung the heavy iron-bound book into Vess's reaching hand, and Vess hissed and staggered—but it was three against two in a dead end, and dead ends keep score.

The first man's boot caught Brannic behind the knee. The cobblestones came up and met his jaw, and the world rang like a dropped bell, and through the ringing he heard Quentin cry out—not pain, fury—and heard cloth tear and heard Vess say, calm as a clerk:

"Hold her. Carefully. The Guildmaster wants the vessel unbruised."

Brannic rolled onto his side, blood in his mouth, salt in his eyes, and understood with terrible clarity that this was the moment. This was the fork in the road that every smuggler knows is coming and every smuggler believes will somehow pass him by.

There was one card left in his hand. One thing in Saltmere that moved faster than a retriever, bought faster than the guild could threaten, and owed Brannic Hale nothing except the memory of a debt paid late.

Mother Coriander. The memory-fence on Anchor Row. The woman who bought and sold the intangible, who kept a vault of other people's yesterdays beneath her tea-house, and who had once told him, drunk on plum wine, that she would trade *anything* for a memory fresh from the Mnemosyne vaults themselves.

Not a memory *of* the vaults. A memory taken *from* one of its keepers.

From the woman the entire northern world was hunting.

"Wait," Brannic rasped, and spat red onto the cobbles, and raised one hand as though surrendering—which, in every way that mattered, he was. "Wait. There's a better trade than bruised goods and broken bones, and you know it, Vess. You know what Mother Coriander pays for provenance."

Vess paused. That flat patience again, but underneath it, interest. Retrievers were forbidden to trade privately—it was the third rule of their order, carved somewhere into whatever passed for their souls—but rules bent for men who wanted things. Brannic had spent thirty years learning what men wanted.

"You have nothing," Vess said. "You're bleeding on salt."

"I have *her*." Brannic forced himself upright, knees screaming, and hated every word even as he weighed each one like coin. "I've traveled with her for nine days. Slept within arm's reach of her. Watched her open the archive, read the Ward-Kings' records, talk in her sleep in a language that hasn't been spoken since the wards were young." He heard Quentin's breath catch behind him and made himself keep going, because stopping now meant dying stupid. "Memories of the heir don't exist anywhere else in the world, Vess. Not in the guild vaults. Not in the Warlord's trophy houses. Fresh. Unrecorded. Mine to sell."

The silence in the alley had texture. Salt dust hung in the lamplight. Behind him, he could feel Quentin staring at the back of his head with an intensity that might have scorched hair.

"You're offering to broker," Vess said slowly.

"I'm offering to make us all richer than the Guildmaster's wildest audit. Coriander pays in more than coin—she pays in silence, in boats, in doors that open and close without witnesses. You take your cut. I take my passage. The guild gets its asset back in one piece instead of four." He swallowed blood. "Everyone balances."

It was a beautiful lie. It was also, in its rotten way, true—because Brannic had no intention of selling Vess anything except time. Coriander's tea-house had a cellar with a second exit, and Coriander owed him, and once they were inside her walls, Vess and his knives could stand in the rain and knock like gentlemen.

That was the plan. That was the whole plan, assembled in four seconds out of blood loss and desperation.

He just hadn't counted on the price.

* * *

Mother Coriander received them in her counting-room above the tea-house, a low chamber lit by one lamp and lined floor to ceiling with little glass vials, thousands of them, glowing faintly with colors that had no names—memories in suspension, other people's lives shelved like preserves. Brannic had been in this room twice before and it had never frightened him. Tonight it looked like a mausoleum where the dead were still dreaming.

Coriander herself was small and silver-haired and sharp as a fishhook, and she looked at Quentin—at the torn coat, the iron-grey eyes, the bundle at her chest—and Brannic watched sixty years of trade instinct kindle in her face like a lamp catching oil.

"Oh," she breathed. "*Oh.* You're her."

"She's a client," Brannic said quickly.

"She's the *heir*." Coriander's fingers twitched toward her shelves the way a miser's twitch toward gold. "Do you know what I could—"

"Coriander." Brannic put every ounce of the old charm into it, the charm that had gotten him out of a hundred rooms like this one. "You want provenance. Fresh, unwitnessed, from inside the vault itself. I'm selling one memory. One. Of her. Taken clean, tonight, by your own hand, sealed under your own seal. In exchange: passage south for two, no questions, no whispers, and your cellar door stays shut behind us."

Vess stood by the door with his arms folded, watching. His two men waited below in the tea-house, drinking Coriander's worst wine and believing they had the evening handled. Everyone believed they had the evening handled.

Coriander circled Quentin slowly, the way appraisers circle. Quentin endured it with her chin up, and Brannic saw the exact moment she understood what he was doing—saw her glance at him, quick and unreadable, and give the smallest nod. *Buy us the room.* She thought it was part of the plan.

It was part of the plan.

It just wasn't the whole price.

"One memory," Coriander said. "My choice of extraction. Sealed to my key alone. And you'll tell me one true thing about the wards before you go—something no one else living knows."

"The extraction can't touch the vial or anything about the wards," Brannic shot back. "Those are poisoned goods. You know what the cold does to stored memories now. Take something clean."

Coriander's smile was thin as paper. "I know exactly what the cold does, boy. I know it eats recollection, and I know that memories touching the heir are the only currency in the north that's *rising*. Clean or poisoned, they'll buy cities soon." She turned to Quentin. "Well, keeper? Do you consent? Because I won't dip a ladle into an unwilling well. Even now. Even for this price."

Quentin looked at Brannic.

And Brannic looked at the floor, because he could not look at her.

*Say no,* he prayed, to gods he'd never believed in. *Say no, and I'll find another way, I swear it on the salt and the tide—*

"Yes," Quentin said quietly.

* * *

The extraction took less time than a confession.

Coriander brought out the apparatus—copper circlet, silver leads, a receiver-vial of black glass—and set it on Quentin's brow while Brannic stood in the corner feeling each passing minute like a lash. He told himself it was nothing. A scrap. A few minutes of travel, a face by firelight, nothing load-bearing. Memories weren't souls; taking one wasn't theft of the self. He'd watched clients walk out of this room lighter and laughing a hundred times.

But he knew which minutes mattered. He knew what the copper circlet would drink first, because Coriander was a professional and professionals skim the cream.

He knew she'd take the best of what he'd given Quentin to see.

When it was done, the black vial glowed faintly warm, and Quentin sat very still on the stool with her eyes closed, and Brannic watched her lips move—counting something, maybe. Checking her own inventory. Finding it short.

"What did you take?" Her voice was steady. That was the worst part. Steadier than his.

"A journey," Coriander said, cradling the vial like a newborn. "A cold road, a stubborn guide, a fire in a ruin full of dead kings' papers. And the moment at the fire when—" she smiled at some private sweetness in it, "—when you laughed. You have a rare laugh, keeper. Most people's are counterfeit."

The archive. The road. The fire. The laugh.

Everything. Everything but the vial and the wards, which had been the only fence he'd asked for, and the fence had held, and it didn't matter, it didn't matter at all, because Brannic stood there watching the black glass glow and understood that he had just sold the one person in the world who trusted him to the highest shelf in a vault full of strangers' yesterdays.

"Not a word of this reaches Vess," he said hoarsely.

"It won't." Coriander was already moving, all business, pulling ledgers, unlocking the back stair. "Your boat leaves on the midnight tide from the coal quay. Captain Ilsabet. Tell her 'plum wine,' and mind you tell her before she asks for coin, because she'll ask for coin." She paused at the stair door and looked back at Brannic, and for one moment the fishhook softened into something almost like pity. "You paid fair, boy. That's the tragedy of this trade. Everyone pays fair."

* * *

They went down through the cellar, out the hidden door, along the reeking black throat of Anchor Row with the harbor lamps smearing the fog. Quentin walked beside him and said nothing, and the silence had edges.

At the coal quay, in the shadow of the coal heaps, with the boat's lantern bobbing and Ilsabet's silhouette waiting at the rail, Quentin finally stopped.

"You gave her the fire," she said. "The archive road. My laugh."

"Quentin—"

"Did you give her the vial?" Flat. Cold. The question of an auditor, not a friend.

"No." He held her gaze and meant it with everything he had. "On my life. The fence held. The wards, the vial, the forge—none of it went into that glass. I'd sooner sell my own grave."

Something moved behind her eyes—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not ever, but the acknowledgment of a boundary respected. She nodded once, turned toward the gangplank—

—and stopped.

Because down the length of Anchor Row, unhurried, patient as arithmetic, came a lantern. And beside the lantern walked Vess, and in Vess's hand, held up to the light like a jeweler examining a stone, glowed a little vial of black glass.

Coriander had sold him a copy. Or Coriander had sold him *her* copy, keeping the original. Or Vess had simply followed them and bought the truth outright—because in Saltmere, on a night like this, with the world forgetting itself street by street, everything was for sale and everyone was buying.

Brannic heard himself make a sound that wasn't quite a word.

Vess stopped ten paces away and smiled his milk-mild smile, and raised the vial so its glow lit his forgettable face.

"Now then," he said pleasantly. "Let's see where the keeper of ledgers has been keeping herself."
