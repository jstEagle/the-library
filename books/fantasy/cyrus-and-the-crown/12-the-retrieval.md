## 12. The Retrieval

They took her at the edge of the salt flats, where the frost had begun to write its pale script across the ground and every footprint she left glowed faintly silver, a confession written in her own wake.

Quentin had known for two days that they were behind her. You did not keep the ledgers of the Mnemosyne Guild for nine years without learning how the guild hunted. Retrievers moved like weather—you could not see them coming, but you learned to read the pressure in the air, the way birds went quiet, the way Brannic stopped humming his tuneless smuggler's songs and started checking the knots on his gear twice instead of once.

"They're close," he'd said that morning, not looking at her, watching the horizon where the mountains should have been and were not—only a white nothing where the wards had failed to hold back the winter's dark. "Three of them. Maybe four."

"How do you know?"

"Because the crows are following *us*." He'd spat into the frost. "Crows follow retrievers the way gulls follow fishing boats. Waiting for scraps."

Now the scraps were her.

They came out of the ground, which was the worst part. Quentin had always imagined retrieval as a chase—boots and ropes and shouting—but these three rose from the frozen earth as if they had been buried there all along, waiting for the exact longitude of her despair. Frost sloughed off their grey cloaks. Their faces were smooth and forgettable in the way of people who had sold their own faces' memories to make themselves better at this work: you could look straight at them and an hour later be unable to swear you'd ever seen them.

The tallest one held up a hand, palm out, almost courteous.

"Quentin Sterling," it said. Not a question. "By the authority of the Ledger, you are recalled."

Brannic moved—she saw it happen, the shift of weight onto his back foot, his hand drifting toward the knife at his hip—and one of the other retrievers simply looked at him. That was all. A look. But Brannic went grey under his weathered brown, and his hand stilled, because everyone in the trade knew what retriever-craft could do with a look. They could take the memory of why you were fighting before you finished raising your fist. They could leave you standing there, knife half-drawn, wondering whose side you were on and why your arm was tired.

"Don't," Quentin said quietly. "Brannic. Don't. This is mine to pay."

"It isn't fair—"

"Nothing is fair. It's only *balanced*." She stepped forward, away from him, toward the tall retriever, and lifted her chin the way she used to when Guildmaster Corvane quizzed her over the valuation tables. "I'll come. But I have terms."

Something flickered across the retriever's smooth face—the ghost of amusement, perhaps, or the memory of amusement, secondhand and worn. "Recalled assets don't set terms."

"This asset does. Because if Corvane wanted me dead, I'd already be dead, and if he merely wanted the vial, you'd have taken it off my body by now. He wants me *brought in*. Which means I'm worth more breathing than bleeding. Which means—" she let the pause stretch, cold air burning in her lungs, "—I'm worth more than he thinks I know."

The third retriever, who had been circling to cut off any flight toward the flats, drifted closer. The tall one raised two fingers—a small gesture, but Quentin read it the way she read everything now, in the grammar of the guild. *Hold.*

"You have nothing," the tall one said. "You have a vial you can't open safely, a ledger you stole badly, and a name that's worth more flayed than free. What could you possibly have that the Guildmaster doesn't already own?"

Quentin smiled. It cost her something to smile; her lips were cracked from the cold and the wind off the unwarded border tasted of iron and old snow. But she smiled anyway, because she had spent nine years learning that the most dangerous thing in the Mnemosyne Guild was never a memory. It was a person who understood exactly what their memories were worth—and what everyone else's were worth too.

"I have an address," she said.

* * *

They bound her hands—not with rope but with a thin cord of woven hair that drank warmth and whispered against her skin, and she chose not to wonder whose hair, because she already knew the answer would be *someone's*, and someone had been paid for it, and that was simply how the world worked now. They walked her south through the frozen flats while the light died early, as it did every day now, the sun a bruised coin sinking through haze. Behind them, Brannic stood untouched among the crows, and she did not look back, because looking back was a luxury priced beyond her current means.

They traveled by night and by memory both. The retrievers did not carry maps. The tall one—who eventually gave her a name, Vess, though Quentin suspected it belonged to someone else entirely, some clerk or victim whose identity had been repurposed—simply closed her eyes at each crossroads and *remembered* the way forward. Routes bought from couriers. Landmarks purchased from farmers. Somewhere in Vess's skull was a perfect map of the northern provinces assembled out of a hundred strangers' sold recollections, and Quentin found it beautiful and obscene in equal measure, the way most things in her profession were.

On the second night, huddled in a ruined waystation while the frost crawled up the walls like ivy made of needles, Vess sat across from her and studied her with those forgettable eyes.

"The address," Vess said. "Corvane will want it verified before he honors anything."

"He can't verify it. That's the point." Quentin flexed her bound fingers, feeling the hair-cord bite. "No one alive knows where the Forge of Kings lies except the Ward-Kings' line, and the Ward-Kings' line consists of me and whatever's floating in my vial. My mother burned the maps. She burned the *memories* of the maps—sold them to the guild herself, decades ago, specifically so no one could find it. You think she didn't anticipate this? She was a Warder. Anticipation was the whole trade."

"And yet you know."

"I know because children remember what parents think they've erased." She said it flatly, but inside, the words rang against something raw. She remembered fragments still—the smell of hot stone, a lullaby sung in a language of measurements, her mother's hands stained with something darker than ink. Fragments the extraction had missed, lodged like splinters too small for the tongs. "The guild took my childhood, Vess. It didn't take it *cleanly*. Nothing in our ledgers is clean. There's residue. And residue points somewhere."

Vess was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind made a sound like a ledger's pages turning.

"You understand what happens if you're lying."

"I'm a guildswoman. I invented understanding that." Quentin leaned forward, letting the firelight catch her face. "Here's what you tell Corvane. Tell him the heir of the Ward-Kings offers him the one asset the guild has spent three hundred years failing to acquire. Not the vial—that's just the *key*. I'm offering him the door. The forge itself. The place where wards were first hammered out of living memory, where a man could rewrite the covenant entire. Think about what that's worth on the open market. Think about what the Ashen Warlord would pay. Think about what the southern colleges would pay. Think about what a Guildmaster with ambitions might—"

"Stop." For the first time, Vess's composure cracked, just a hairline. "You think I haven't considered selling you myself?"

"I'm counting on it," Quentin said softly. "That's why I'm telling *you* and not the other two."

* * *

The city, when they reached it, wore its darkness like a shroud pulled too tight.

Quentin had grown up—or grown *back*, after the extraction—in streets that glittered. Lamplighters, lantern-festivals, windows full of warm gold. Now the starless night pressed down and the city answered it with fear: shutters closed, braziers burning low, whole districts gone silent except for the sound of the cold itself, a vast crystalline ringing that lived in the stones. Refugees camped in the great square under tarps stiff with rime, and as the retrievers walked her through, she heard them murmuring. Not about the warlord. About forgetting.

"My daughter's face," an old woman was saying to no one, rhythmically, like a prayer worn down to the warp. "I had it yesterday. I had it yesterday. I had it—"

Quentin walked faster.

The guildhouse rose ahead, and even now, even darkened, it had presence: a black stone edifice shaped like a hand cupped around something precious, which was exactly what it was. The Mnemosyne Guild had bought its architecture the way it bought everything, from a architect who'd sold the memory of designing it and so could never build another. Every stone was unique. Every stone was a secret.

At the threshold, Vess paused.

"One more thing," Vess said. "He'll ask you why you came back. He'll ask whether you turned yourself in or whether we caught you. Choose your answer carefully. He buys stories the way other men buy wine—he can taste the vintage."

Quentin almost laughed. Almost.

"Tell him the truth," she said. "Tell him I negotiated. He'll believe that. In nine years, I never once told him a story he wanted to hear. It's the only reason he trusts me."

"That and the fact that you're the last person alive who can read his private cipher."

"That too."

They descended. Past the public vaults with their rows of labeled vials glowing like a pharmacy of souls; past the valuation chambers where clients once wept over prices; down stairs Quentin had never been permitted to climb, into the deep rock beneath the guildhouse, where the air grew warm and smelled of candlewax and old paper and, faintly, unbearably, of *home*—of woodsmoke and hot stone, the scent of her erased childhood leaking up from whatever lay below.

The inner vault door was iron faced with lead, and across it, in the guild's oldest cipher, a single inscription Quentin had memorized from a stolen page:

*All debts come home.*

Vess knocked—a pattern, three and two—and the door swung inward on warmth and lamplight and the figure of Guildmaster Corvane, seated at a desk far too small for his reputation, a single ledger open before him, a pen laid neatly across it like a sword returned to its scabbard.

He was older than she remembered. Everyone was, lately. His hair had gone the color of the frost outside, and there were new lines around his eyes, fine as ledger-rules. But his gaze was unchanged: patient, appraising, the gaze of a man who had spent a lifetime pricing the unsellable and had long ago stopped seeing people as anything but entries.

"Quentin." Her name in his mouth sounded like a figure being entered into a column. "Sit down. You look terrible."

"I've been walking through the end of the world. It's hard on the complexion."

"So I hear." He gestured, and Vess withdrew, closing the door with a click that sounded final as a stamp on wax. Alone, Corvane folded his hands. "Before we begin: I want you to know that none of this is personal. When you ran, I felt genuinely disappointed. I had plans for you. Succession plans."

"I know. That's why I ran."

"And now you've come back. Voluntarily, I'm told. Negotiated your own capture." His eyebrow rose a fraction. "Which means you believe you have leverage. Which interests me enormously, because I have spent weeks trying to determine what you could possibly possess that I don't already own. I own your childhood. I own your family history. I own, as of Tuesday, the loyalty of every smuggler on the northern coast—including, incidentally, a large man named Brannic, whose debt I called in the day you fled. Did he mention that? No? He's a decent sort. He'll feel awful about it later, when it's safe to."

The floor tilted under Quentin's heart, but her face, trained by nine years of telling widows what their husbands' memories fetched on the open market, did not move. *Later,* she thought. *Grieve later. Balance now.*

"I don't have leverage," she said. "I have *inventory*."

"Show me."

She leaned across his desk, close enough to see her reflection doubled in his eyes, and spoke the words she had carried since the archive beneath the ice, since her ancestors' records had shown her the truth of harvested generations and the forge where the first covenant was struck in blood and memory.

"The Ward-Kings didn't keep their secrets in vaults," she said. "They kept them in a forge built into the roots of the world, warded so thoroughly that even the kings themselves could only find it by inheritance. My mother sold the way there to this guild—piece by piece, over twenty years, disguised as worthless childhood trivia. A song. A game. A shape drawn in ash. You catalogued them as dross, Corvane. Low-value juvenile residue, batch seven-seven-four. I checked the ledgers before I ran. I *wrote* those entries—I just didn't know what I was writing." She let that land, watched his pupils tighten. "But I grew up in that house before you took it from me. And residue remembers. I can reassemble the way. Every step. To the Forge of Kings."

Silence. Somewhere above them, muffled by tons of stone, the cold rang against the city like a bell that would not stop.

Corvane unfolded his hands very slowly.

"Do you know," he said, "in thirty years of this trade, I have been offered crowns, curses, confessions, and one genuinely authentic miracle. And this is the first time I have ever wanted to applaud." He rose, buttoned his coat, and became abruptly the mentor she had once trusted with her life—an act she now recognized as the finest performance either of them had ever staged. "Come. The inner vault has a table where we can work. You will show me everything, entry by entry, and if the assembly holds—if your residue leads true—then perhaps we discuss what a finder's fee looks like for the woman who delivered the forge."

"And the vial?"

"Safe. Sealed. Untouched." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "For now."

He led her deeper, past shelves of vials older than the guild itself, past a case containing something that glowed faintly and made her teeth ache, down to a round chamber where a single lamp burned over a table scarred by centuries of work. On the wall hung the guild's founding charter, and beneath it, framed, the first ledger ever kept—open to its first entry, in faded brown ink:

*Item one: one memory, freely surrendered. Price: fair.*

Quentin sat, and spread her hands over the table as if warming them, and began—carefully, precisely, holding back the final piece the way a card player holds the ace—to speak her childhood aloud.

And beneath her voice, beneath the lamp, beneath the whole patient weight of the guild, she felt it: a faint, familiar warmth rising through the stone. Hot stone. Woodsmoke. Home.

The residue wasn't pointing the way to the forge.

It was calling her back.
