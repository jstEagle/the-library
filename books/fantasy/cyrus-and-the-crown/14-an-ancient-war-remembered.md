## 14. An Ancient War, Remembered

The vial did not open so much as *admit* her.

Quentin had expected a cork, a seal, some mechanical surrender. Instead, when she pressed the glass to her brow in the dark of Brannic's safehouse cellar, the memory simply recognized her—blood calling to blood—and poured itself into her like wine into an empty cup. There was no falling asleep, no dream's soft threshold. She was simply *elsewhere*, standing in a summer that had died a thousand years before her birth.

The light was wrong for a memory of winter. It was high summer, gold and heavy, and the air smelled of cut hay and woodsmoke and something sweeter beneath—blossoms from trees she had no name for. She stood on a hillside above a river valley dotted with white-walled villages, and the people moving through the fields below were brown-skinned and black-haired, singing as they worked, their language old enough that Quentin understood it only the way one understands a mother's heartbeat: not with the mind, but with something older.

*These are my fields,* said a voice inside her skull that was also outside it—a woman's voice, warm and weathered. *These are the fields your grandmother's grandmother sang to. Remember them.*

*I don't know you,* Quentin thought back, and felt the memory smile around her like sunlight.

*You will. That is what remembering is.*

---

The first vision broke like a wave and reformed as another. Time in the vial did not flow; it *piled*. A hundred years passed between one breath and the next, and Quentin stood in the same valley watching the same hills wear different faces.

She saw the first war begin—not with armies but with a census.

Scholars came up from the capital in gray robes, carrying instruments of brass and bone, and they measured things in the villages: the depth of a child's sleep, the vividness of a widow's grief, the weight of a shepherd's joy at lambing season. They wrote everything down in books whose covers drank the lamplight. They were polite. They were thorough. They paid well.

*They're measuring our memories,* whispered a boy in the second vision, crouched beside Quentin—beside the body she wore, a young woman of the valley with strong hands and a scarred knuckle. The boy was her brother in this life; Quentin knew it the way she knew her own name, or what had been her own name. *Da says the King wants them for something.*

*For what?* the mouth she wore asked. *You can't build a wall out of remembering.*

But you could. That was the horror the visions unfolded slowly, patiently, the way rot unfolds through timber. Quentin watched the first border wards rise across the maps in the scholars' books—watched the diagrams of them, great lattices of woven intent that needed fuel, and the fuel was not stone or fire or blood spilled once. The fuel was *lived experience*. Memory was the only substance dense enough to hold a shape against the raw chaos past the world's edge, and the dynasty that forged the first wards had discovered that other people's memories burned cleaner than one's own. A king could not be expected to forget his coronation. But a farmer's wedding? A fisherman's last sight of his son sailing out? Those could be drawn off like sap from a tapped tree, and the tree would live—dimmer, quieter, hollowed—but it would live.

At first.

* * *

The third decade of the harvesting brought the Gray Quiet to the valley.

Quentin walked through it wearing the body of a midwife named Sefa, and she felt what Sefa felt: the wrongness of a village where everyone smiled a beat too late. The harvesters' wagons came every season now, and the brass-and-bone instruments had grown larger, and the payment had grown smaller, because the guilds of the capital had learned what every lender learns—that those who need to sell will sell cheaper each time. Whole families sat in their doorways in the evening, unable to say why the fire looked beautiful to them. Children who had forgotten their grandparents' faces wept without knowing why. A man named Ollin hanged himself from his own barn beam and left a note that read only: *I know there was something I loved. I cannot find where I put it.*

Sefa buried him. Sefa remembered for all of them, hoarding her memories like grain against famine—and then the wagons came for her specifically, because the ledgers flagged her kind. *Resistant stock. High-density retention.* She was taken to the capital in a wagon with barred windows, and Quentin rode with her, feeling her heart hammering, feeling her hands pressed flat against the cold bars as the white walls of the palace rose ahead.

They called the place where they took her the Anvil Hall. It was beautiful. That was the worst part—the beauty of it, vaulted ceilings inlaid with constellations, and at its center the First Forge, a structure of black metal that hummed like a struck bell, and around it, chained to chairs of silver, the fuel.

Not prisoners. *Stock.* That was the word the wardwrights used. Stock. People whose memories were drained in slow rotations, year after year, their minds kept just intact enough to generate new memories worth taking. Sefa lasted eleven years. In her final vision, she was an old woman who could not remember her own children but who could recite, unprompted, the exact emotional texture of four hundred strangers' happiest days—because she had carried them to the Forge herself, load after load, and the loads never left her entirely. They layered.

*We are the wall,* she thought, in her last lucid hour, staring at the humming black metal. *Gods forgive us. We are the wall, and no one told us.*

* * *

Then the valley burned.

Quentin had braced for this—of course she had, she was a ledger-keeper, she knew how stories of old wars ended—but nothing braced her for the specific shape of it. Not conquest. *Reckoning.* The descendants of the harvested, generations deep in grief they couldn't name, finally found someone who could name it for them: a warleader from the eastern marches, another people the crown had tapped, whose shamans had preserved the stolen years in song. He marched under a banner of ash because he promised the harvesters would become ash, and the valleys rose with him, whole villages arming themselves with scythes and fury and the one thing the crown had accidentally left them—their anger, which the instruments had never figured out how to draw off, because no one wanted to buy it.

Quentin fought in these visions. That was the part she hadn't expected—to feel a spear in her hands, to hear men screaming words she suddenly understood, to see the Anvil Hall breached and the wardwrights dragged from behind their beautiful constellation ceilings. And she saw what the rebels did with the Forge when they took it: they could not destroy it. It was warded too well by the very power it consumed. So they shattered what they could, salted what they could, and left, believing it dead.

It was not dead. Nothing built on that much stolen living ever dies cleanly. And when the last loyal wardwright fled east with the Forge's heart-stone hidden in a cart of seed grain—when the old wards began their long centuries of flickering starvation—the crown's remnant made the choice that Quentin's bloodline would carry forward like a curse:

They rebuilt. Quieter. Smaller. And this time they did not tap strangers.

They tapped themselves.

* * *

Quentin surfaced from the vial gasping, drenched in sweat, Brannic's rough hands on her shoulders, the cellar candle guttering. But the vial was not finished. It had shown her the war; now it showed her the wound.

Because there was a final memory, the oldest one, pressed at the bottom of the glass like sediment—and it was not from the valley at all. It was from the enemy's side.

A shaman's tent, generations after the burning. An old man of the eastern marches holding a carved memory-bone, teaching children the songs of preservation. And Quentin, seeing through the shaman's eyes, heard what he taught them:

*The wall-people forgot what they took. That is their sickness. We must never have their sickness. Every child of ours will learn the names of the harvested. Every generation will remember the Anvil Hall. And if the wall falls someday—if their stolen kingdom freezes and starves—we will not celebrate. We will grieve for them, because grief is the tax of memory, and we have always paid our taxes.*

One of the children looked up—a girl with ash-grey eyes—and asked, *And if they come to us begging to rebuild the wall?*

The old shaman was silent a long time. Then: *Then we ask them one question. Who will feed it this time? If they answer "the poor," we burn them again. If they answer "everyone," then perhaps the world has finally learned to share its warmth.*

* * *

Quentin opened her eyes in the cellar. The candle had burned down to a stub. Brannic crouched over her, his face grey with worry, and behind him the trapdoor rattled—footsteps above, urgent ones, and Brannic's smuggler instincts already reading them.

"Quentin. You were under for six hours. You were saying names. Hundreds of names."

"I was paying taxes," she said, and her voice came out cracked and strange.

Above them, fists hammered the street door, and a voice called down through the floorboards—a voice Quentin knew from the bounty boards, from the herald's proclamation, from the warlord's own envoy who had come offering alliance: smooth, patient, ash-grey eyes.

*"Keeper of the ledger,"* the voice said. *"I know what you saw in there tonight. Come up and tell me—did the old shaman's question reach you? Because I've been asking it my whole life."*

Brannic's knife was out. Quentin, still trembling with a thousand borrowed deaths, found she was already reaching for the trapdoor latch.

*Who will feed the wall?* The question hung in the frozen dark like the first starless snow.

And she realized, with terrible clarity, that her answer would decide whether the long winter ended—or swallowed everything.
