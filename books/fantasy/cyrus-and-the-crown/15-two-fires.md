## 15. Two Fires

The envoy came at dusk, which was no longer dusk but merely the hour when the dark grew patient.

Quentin saw him first as a shape against the frost-white ruins of the archive's outer colonnade—a tall figure wrapped in grey ash-colored wool, walking unhurried through snow that should have swallowed his boots to the knee but instead seemed to part for him, as if the cold recognized its own. Behind him, at a respectful distance, came two soldiers of the Ashen host, their spears tipped not with iron but with something darker that drank what little light remained.

Brannic's hand found her elbow. "We can still run."

"Where?" She said it without bitterness, only arithmetic. The archive sat on a shelf of rock above a valley that had been, three weeks ago, a road. Now it was a river of refugees frozen mid-flight in places—she tried not to look at the ones who had stopped moving, the ones the cold had found and opened like letters. "There's nowhere left that isn't either his or Corvane's. At least this way I choose the ground."

"You always were a ledger-keeper," Brannic muttered, but he moved to stand at her shoulder anyway, one hand resting on the knife at his belt with the easy lie of a man who had never needed to draw it fast.

The envoy stopped ten paces away and bowed. Not deeply. The bow of one sovereign power acknowledging another, which was either flattery or a joke, and Quentin could not yet tell which.

"Lady Sterling," he said. "Or shall I say—" and here he smiled, thin and courteous as a paper cut, "—*Vessarin*?"

The name went through her like a splinter finding the grain. She had worn it now for only days, learned it from a dying wardwright's last breath, and already it felt less like a name than a wound that other people knew how to touch.

"You have me at a disadvantage," she said.

"Deliberately." He drew back his hood. He was younger than she had expected, with the grey-streaked temples of a man who had sold years of his life the way the poor sold memories—one season at a time—and eyes the color of banked coals. "I am Sarel of the Ash Marches. Herald, envoy, and formerly, in a life I no longer entirely remember, a schoolteacher." He said the last without self-pity, the way a man reports the weather. "You are wondering why the Warlord sends words instead of spears."

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"Because the Warlord has read the same records you have." Sarel's coal-dark eyes flicked toward the archive entrance behind her, where the frozen shelves of her ancestors' confession waited. "He knows you found the harvest ledgers. He knows you know what your bloodline did to mine."

The wind moved through the colonnade, and somewhere in the ruined archive a shelf groaned under ice. Quentin kept her voice level. "Then he knows I'm not my ancestors."

"He knows no such thing. He knows only that the last Vessarin lives, and that every Vessarin for nine generations stood at an anvil while his people were brought to it in chains of their own remembering." Sarel's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it never rose. It simply stated, the way she had once stated figures in a ledger, and the figures had been worse for their calm. "He offers you the chance to be the first who doesn't."

* * *

They talked in the archive's reading room, because Quentin refused to negotiate standing in the snow like a supplicant, and because some stubborn guild-trained part of her insisted that any conversation involving the fate of the world deserved a table.

The reading room had been beautiful once. She could see it in the bones of the place: the long oak table, the high windows set with colored glass that had depicted, she was almost certain, the Ward-Kings themselves—nine crowned figures whose faces the frost had eaten down to featureless suggestion. Her family, rendered anonymous by cold. She found she did not mind.

Brannic stood by the door. Sarel's soldiers remained outside. The vial hung at Quentin's throat, and she was aware of it the way one is aware of a heartbeat—constant, intimate, faintly alarming.

"Tell me the offer," she said. "Plainly. I've spent my life reading contracts written to deceive. I'll know if you do the same."

"Good." Sarel folded his hands on the table. "Then hear it plainly. Let the winter fall."

She had expected many things. Not that. "You're asking me to do nothing."

"I'm asking you to do the hardest thing a Vessarin has ever done, which is *nothing*. The wards are dead. The stars are dark. The cold is coming inland and it is eating recollection as it comes—you've felt it, I think. Little losses. A word you can't find. A face that won't resolve." His eyes were kind, and that was worse than cruelty. "The old wards could be restored. You know how. Your blood, your memories, poured into the forge the way your mother poured hers, the way her mother did, back and back to the first theft. One more generation of Vessarins fed to the fire so that everyone else can keep their warm, forgetful, comfortable lives."

"And the Warlord's alternative is that everyone loses everything."

"The Warlord's alternative is that everyone loses *equally*." For the first time something moved behind the coal-dark eyes—not anger, exactly. Grief that had been kept so long it had fossilized into doctrine. "You read the ledgers, Lady Vessarin. Tell me what you read."

She could have refused. She could have said *that's not why we're here*. But she had read them, every page, by candlelight while Brannic slept, and the reading had changed the architecture of her sleep as well.

"The first war," she said quietly. "It wasn't a war over territory. It was a war over *who would burn*. The founders of my line didn't invent the wards. They inherited a dying magic and had to choose what would feed it. And they chose—" Her voice caught, and she hated the catch, hated that this man could hear it. "They chose a people who had no king to defend them. Your people. They called it conscription of memory. Harvest-tithes. They took whole generations. First loves. Mother-tongues. Names. They took children's entire childhoods and burned them in the forge so the border would hold and the harvest-fields of the heartland would stay green."

"And no one in the heartland ever asked what the wards cost," Sarel said, "because the wards were warm, and the people who paid were far away and conveniently voiceless. Nine generations. Do you know what my people are now? We are a nation of amputees. We remember our grandparents as weather. We have songs whose meanings were sold before we were born. When the Warlord—when *Ashkan*, his name is Ashkan, he had a name before the ash took it—when he was a boy, he could not remember his mother's face, because his mother's mother had sold her memories of *her* mother to pay a tithe, and the loss had propagated down the line like a crack in glass." He unfolded his hands. "So. The offer. Let the winter fall. Do not rebuild the wards. Do not restore the old covenant. In ten years the cold will pass—it always passes, it is not malice, it is only the world remembering how to be unguarded—and what survives will survive into a world where no bloodline is ever harvested again. No anvil. No tithes. No children sold by weight of memory. A hard world, but a *just* one. The first just world in nine generations."

The silence after was very long. Outside, the wind worried at the colonnade like a dog at a bone.

"And the refugees in the valley," Quentin said at last. "The ones the cold has already opened. The ones it will open in the ten years while the world 'remembers how to be unguarded.' The children who will lose their names to the frost before they're old enough to know they had one."

"Yes," Sarel said. "Them."

"That's your answer? *Yes, them?*"

"My answer is that every covenant has its *them*, Lady Vessarin. Yours had mine for nine generations. The heartland's *them* lived beyond the wards and froze in the dark and were called savages for their trouble. The guild's *them* are the poor who sell their weddings by the pound so the rich can feel young again." He leaned forward, and now the banked-coal eyes held actual fire, banked no longer. "I am not asking you to love this offer. I am asking you to notice that it is the same offer your ancestors made, with the arithmetic reversed. And to notice that when the arithmetic was theirs, they called it necessity. And when it is ours, you call it atrocity. Both words are true. That is what I have come to say."

* * *

He left her alone after that, at her request, and she sat in the ruined reading room with her ancestors' frost-eaten faces above her and did not move for a long time.

Brannic brought her tea at some point—actual tea, hoarded, brewed on a smuggler's spirit-stove, tasting of smoke and someone else's better days. He set it down without a word and stood watching the broken window, where the starless dark pressed like a face against glass.

"He's right," she said finally.

"He's right the way a landslide is right," Brannic said. "Gravity's not wrong either. Doesn't mean I want to stand under it."

"He's offering me a just world, Brannic. No more harvests. No more vials. No more guilds trading in other people's first kisses and deathbed confessions. Everything I've spent a month learning to hate—ended, permanently, if I simply have the courage to let the winter do its work."

"Everything *he's* spent a lifetime learning to hate," Brannic corrected. "There's a difference, and it's the width of a valley full of frozen refugees." He turned to look at her. In the candlelight his face was all angles and honesty, the face of a man who had sold pieces of himself his whole life and knew exactly what each one had cost. "Quentin. I've watched you for a month. You audit everything. It's what you *are*. So audit this. That man's grief is real. His cause is real. And his answer is to let ten years of winter eat the names of children who never sold a single memory in their lives, because their suffering is *convenient* to the arithmetic. Sound familiar?"

She looked down at the tea. Her hands were shaking, and she let them.

"It sounds like a ledger," she said. "It sounds like every balanced ledger I ever kept, where the columns added up beautifully and somewhere in the footnotes there were people."

"There it is." Brannic's voice was gentle. "Now audit the other column."

But the other column was the problem, wasn't it? Because the other column was her mother at the anvil, which she had not yet seen—only sensed, a locked room in the vial she still had not opened—and before that, nine generations of Vessarins pouring themselves into the fire, and the wards had *held*, hadn't they? The border had held for nine generations. Children in the heartland had grown up warm and whole and never knowing. The wards had been built on atrocity, and they had *worked*, and the world they protected had been real, and the people in it had been real, and every one of them was now in the path of a cold that ate recollection.

Two fires, she thought. The forge-fire and the winter. One consumed the few to warm the many. The other would consume everyone to free the few. And between them, her, the last heir, the last entry in a ledger nine generations deep, being asked by both sides to sign.

"I need to see the forge," she said.

Brannic went still. "The Ward-Kings' forge. Sarel will never—"

"Sarel doesn't need to know. Not yet." She stood, and the vial swung at her throat, and for the first time it did not feel like a weight. It felt like a key she hadn't yet found the door for. "Because here's what neither of them has considered, Brannic. Sarel wants the wards dead because they run on stolen blood. Corvane wants them restored because a restored ward is a commodity. And the Warlord wants me flayed because my name is on the anvil. None of them has asked whether the anvil *must* run on what it has always run on. They've all read the same records I have, and they all stopped reading at the part that confirmed what they already believed."

"You think there's another way to feed a ward."

"I think I'm the last heir of the family that *built* them," Quentin said, "and I have not yet opened the one document that matters. My mother sealed these wards with her own hands, Brannic. And then she had my childhood taken from me and hidden in a guild vault. Everyone assumes she did it to protect me from the harvest." She touched the vial, and its glass was warm, warmer than the room, warmer than it had any right to be. "But my mother was a Vessarin. And Vessarins do not do only one thing at a time. I think she left me more than a name. I think she left me instructions."

Outside, in the dark, one of Sarel's soldiers coughed—a small, human sound—and Quentin realized with a lurch that she had made a decision without noticing herself make it, the way the cold made decisions, the way ledgers balanced, silently and then all at once.

She would hear Sarel out. She would let him believe she was tempted, because she *was* tempted, honestly and terribly, and because a herald who thought he was winning told you everything. And in the morning she would ask him—casually, as one sovereign power to another—exactly where the Warlord believed the old forge lay.

Because there were two fires in the world now, the winter's and the forge's, and Quentin Sterling, last heir of the Ward-Kings, keeper of impossible ledgers, had just decided she intended to light a third.

She only had to reach the anvil before either army did.
