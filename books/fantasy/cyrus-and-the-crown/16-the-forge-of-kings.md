## 16. The Forge of Kings

### Quentin

The mountain had a name once. Every mountain did, in the old ledgers—names were the first things guilds learned to price—but the maps Brannic had stolen from a dead smuggler called it only *the Old Man*, and the name it had borne when kings still walked its slopes had been sold so long ago that even Quentin's vial-blood could not remember it.

She felt that absence like a tooth missing. Three days out from the frozen archive, and the long winter was no longer something happening at the border. It was in the air they breathed, a fine glittering dust that settled on the shoulders and whispered. She had caught herself twice now reaching for memories that came back wrong: her mother's face with a stranger's eyes, the smell of the guild's counting room where there should have been bread.

*The cold eats recollection.* The refugees had said it as superstition. It was not superstition. It was the wards dying, and everything the wards had held up—names, faces, summers—starting to fall.

"Stop looking at the sky," Brannic said beside her. "It's not going to apologize."

"I'm not looking at the sky. I'm looking at what isn't in it."

"That's worse."

They climbed through a pass where the pines stood silvered and rigid, frost-furred, like an audience petrified mid-ovation. Behind them, days away but closing, the Warlord's army moved along the low roads—a darkness that smoked without fire. Ahead, somewhere past the shoulder of the Old Man, the retrievers' lantern-line snaked up the western approach. Corvane had not bothered to be subtle. Corvane never bothered; subtlety was for people who hadn't bought enough of the world to stop needing it.

And somewhere above them both, at the roof of the ruined world, the Forge of Kings waited under the ice with its mouth open.

Quentin adjusted the strap across her chest. The vial rode against her sternum, warm as a second heartbeat, and she hated how much comfort she took from it—the way you take comfort from a knife you've carried so long you forget it was made for someone else's throat.

Her mother had sealed the wards with this. Her mother had harvested, and sealed, and harvested again, and then one night had taken her own daughter's childhood and corked it in glass rather than feed it to the border. Love or theft. Probably both. Quentin was learning that most terrible things were both.

"You're doing the ledger face," Brannic said.

"What's the ledger face?"

"The one where you're adding up what everything costs." He kicked through a drift of frost that shattered like sugar-glass. "You do it before you tell someone bad news. I've watched you do it four times since the archive."

"Have you." She kept her voice flat. "Counting my habits now?"

"It's what I'm good for," he said, and there was something under the joke, some sediment, that she would think about later, at the worst possible moment.

The defector walked ten paces behind them, the way she always did—not far enough to seem like desertion, not close enough to seem like friendship. Sera Vane. Third blade of Corvane's retrievers, until the night in the vault when she had looked at Quentin standing over the open vial and made a choice that was going to follow her for the rest of her life, however long the winter let that be.

"Pass narrows ahead," Sera called. "If the retrievers know the mountain, they'll cut us at the saddle."

"They don't know the mountain," Quentin said.

"No," Sera agreed. "But they bought the map off the same dead smuggler we did."

* * *

### Brannic

Brannic had stolen many things in his life and been caught stealing exactly three of them: a loaf, a kiss, and a memory.

The loaf had cost him a beating. The kiss had cost him a marriage. And the memory—the memory was costing him everything, every hour of this climb, because he could feel the hole where it used to be the way your tongue finds a missing tooth, worrying at the smooth absence of it.

He remembered selling it. That was the cruelty the fences never told you about. You remembered the *selling*. You remembered the fence's back room smelling of tallow and old paper, the brass instrument like a corkscrew for the soul, the specific weight of coins in your hand afterward—heavy, always heavier than you'd sworn the thing was worth. What you didn't remember was the thing itself. You just knew the shape of where it had been.

He knew it involved Quentin. The fence had been clear enough about that, gloating, counting the coin into his palm while retrievers waited in the alley: *a memory concerning the woman. Something recent. Something tender, if you have anything tender.*

He'd had one thing tender.

So now he climbed the Old Man with a hole in his chest shaped like her, walking beside her, and she trusted him, and the retrievers following them up the western approach were following a trail drawn partly from what he'd sold. He'd pieced that together two nights ago, listening to Sera interrogate a shepherd: *the trackers moved like they already knew the route.* Not the map. The map was common. The route—their route, the deviations, the shrine where Quentin had wept, the hollow tree where she'd hidden the ledger—only one person alive knew all of that besides Quentin herself.

Him. Half of him. The half he'd kept.

And the other half was in a fence's vault in the Unlit City, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting to be read aloud to whoever paid.

"There," Sera said, dropping to a crouch among the silvered pines. "Saddle."

Brannic looked. Lanterns, six of them, moving fast and orderly up the snow of the saddle like beads on a string, cutting the line between them and the summit cave. Retrievers didn't hurry and didn't tire; Corvane paid for both qualities and got them.

"We go around," Quentin said. "The eastern face—"

"Is a cliff," Sera said.

"Then we go through them."

They both looked at her. She had the ledger face on. Of course she did.

"Six," Sera said. "Third blade, fourth blade, and I don't know the rest. Ossory carries a hooked net. They will not kill you, Quentin. Corvane wants you breathing. The rest of us are negotiable."

"I know," Quentin said. "That's why I'm going first. They need me more than I need my fingers."

"That's not arithmetic," Brannic heard himself say. "That's just spending."

She smiled at him then—quick, crooked, the smile he had half a memory of and would sell the other half of his soul to keep—and it hurt so much he almost told her right there, on the freezing saddle, with lantern-light coming.

Almost.

He should have.

* * *

### Sera

Sera Vane had served the Mnemosyne Guild for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned exactly one doctrine worth keeping: *everyone pays.*

It was carved over the door of the training house. Recruits thought it meant the clients. Sella had understood by her second year that it meant everyone, always, including the hand that held the knife—including especially that hand.

So when she slipped down through the pines ahead of the others, quiet as a debt coming due, she did not tell herself she was doing anything noble. She was paying. Eleven years of wages, of purpose, of belonging to something older and larger than herself—all of it purchased at a price she was only now being asked to settle, on a mountainside, against former colleagues who would hook her with the net and take her apart to learn what she knew.

Ossory knelt at the saddle's crest, checking the net's barbs. Beyond him, four lanterns, one scout turned away toward the drop.

Sera walked out of the trees with her hands empty and her blade sheathed, because the first move mattered, and she wanted them confused for exactly three heartbeats.

"Vane," Ossory said. He didn't stand. That was the insult. "Corvane offers amnesty for returned assets. Even now."

"He offered you that?"

"He offered me double rate and a clean slate." Ossory's eyes went past her, to the treeline, counting shadows. "He offered me you, unlisted."

"And you believed him."

"I believe in the ledger," Ossory said, rising at last, slow, the net uncoiling from his fist like something alive. "The guild keeps balance. Without the guild, everything anyone ever suffered is just—waste. Noise. At least we make it *mean* something. At least we file it."

"She's not an asset, Ossory."

"Nobody's not an asset." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way you'd explain weather to a child. "That's the whole teaching. Come home, Sera."

Behind him, the scout turned back. Two more lanterns swung toward the saddle.

Sera drew her blade.

"Not everyone," she said. "I've decided. I'm allowed. I resigned."

The net came for her, and she paid.

* * *

### Quentin

They came through the trees at the sound of steel and found Sera backed against a stone outcrop, blood on her sleeve—hers, mostly—and Ossory's net snagged on the rock above her head, and two retrievers down, and two more closing from the flank with the unhurried competence of people who had done this a hundred times and been paid well for it.

Quentin did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat and hated the answer.

She stepped into the open. "Ossory."

Everything stopped. That was the power Corvane had accidentally given her: the whole guild wanted her breathing, wanted her soft, wanted her *delivered*, and none of them quite dared be the one who broke the merchandise. Six trained killers, and a woman with frostbitten fingers and a glass vial could walk into their midst and freeze them like the weather had frozen the pines.

"Ma'am," Ossory said. Even now, the courtesy. Even now, the market manners.

"Your employer means to sell me," Quentin said. "Not shield me. Sell me—to the Warlord if the price holds, to the refugees if they'd pool their copper, to anyone. I've seen his private ledger. I read it in the vault, third shelf, under the false bottom, because I keep ledgers too and I know where people hide the real ones."

A flicker in Ossory's eyes. Doubt, or the recognition of doubt.

"The wards are failing," she went on, stepping closer, voice carrying in the dead cold air. "Everything the guild has ever bought and buried—every crime, every miracle, every first love—it only has value inside a world that remembers. Let the winter win, and Corvane's vault becomes a cellar full of pretty glass. There is no trade without memory. He knows. Ask yourself why he's climbing this mountain instead of guarding his own vault."

"Words," Ossory said. But his net had lowered a degree.

"Sera Vane bled for a resignation you'd have denied her anyway. I'm offering you a better one: walk down the mountain, and when they ask, tell them the heir of the Ward-Kings took your memory of the whole encounter." She held up one hand, and let a thread of the vial's warmth show through her skin, faint gold under frost-pale flesh. "I can nearly do that now, Ossory. By spring, I'll be able to do it properly. Do you want to bet your recollection of this conversation that I'm lying?"

The wind came down the glacier then, and it was not empty. It had voices in it, thin and hungry, the sound the cold made when it ate a memory—she had learned to hear it, God help her, she was learning all the wrong things.

Ossory looked at Sera's blood on the snow. Looked at the lanterns of the Warlord's vanguard, days out but visible now at night, a smolder on the southern horizon. Looked at Quentin Sterling, last heir of the dynasty that had built the world's walls out of other people's lives.

"Amnesty," he said hoarsely. "If the forge works. If the world doesn't end. Amnesty, and you burn my contract."

"If the forge works," Quentin said, "there won't be any contracts. That's rather the point."

* * *

### Brannic

The summit cave opened out of the ice like a throat, and the Forge of Kings lay at the bottom of it.

Brannic had imagined anvils. Hammers. Smoke. What he found was a cathedral turned upside down—a vast chamber descending into the mountain's roots, ringed with galleries carved with names in an alphabet that predated the alphabet, and at the center, sunk in a shaft of frozen blue light that came from nowhere, the anvil itself: a single block of black metal, star-flecked, older than the ice around it, humming.

Humming. In a world gone silent, it hummed.

Quentin went down the stairs like a pilgrim, like a daughter coming home to a house that had eaten her family, and Brannic followed, and Sera followed limping, and at the bottom of the stairs Brannic stopped, because he could not take another step while carrying what he was carrying.

"Quentin."

She turned. The forge-light—whatever it was, whatever impossible lamp fed it—moved across her face.

"I have to tell you something before you touch that anvil, and if I wait until after, I'll never tell you, because after, you'll owe me, and I can't have you owing me."

The cold breathed down the stairwell behind them. Below, the anvil hummed its one patient note.

"In the Unlit City," Brannic said. "The night the retrievers found us at the fence's. You asked why they came to that alley, of all alleys. You asked, and I changed the subject, and you let me, because you were tired and bleeding and you trusted me." His voice was failing him. Good. Let it fail. "They came because I sold them the way in. Not the map. Me. I sold a memory—I didn't know what else to give, they had us cornered, and it bought us the hour we needed to run, and I told myself that made it all right, that at least it bought something, that at least—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "It was a memory of you. One of mine. About you. And I don't remember it anymore, which means it's sitting in some fence's vault or some retriever's head, being *used*, and I chose that. I chose it with my eyes open. Everyone pays. I taught you that phrase. I want you to know I learned it from the paying side."

Silence, except the anvil, humming.

Quentin looked at him for a long moment. Then she drew her belt knife.

Brannic did not step back. He had decided, somewhere on the stairs, that he wouldn't, and he discovered in the moment that deciding a thing and meaning it were different countries, and his boots were planted in the second one whether he liked it or not.

She crossed to him. She took his hand—his knife hand, the smuggler's hand, scarred and steady—and she turned it palm-up, and she set the flat of her own blade against his palm, hard enough to bite, hard enough that he'd carry the line of it for weeks.

"Swear it," she said quietly. "On the hand that sold me. Whatever's left of me that's worth keeping—what you kept back, the half you didn't sell—you swear you'll spend the rest of your life guarding it better than you guarded the first part."

"I swear," Brannic said. The blade's edge stung; he welcomed it; everyone pays, and this payment felt like the first honest coin he'd minted in years.

She lowered the knife. And then—worse than the knife, infinitely worse—she smiled at him, tired and crooked and entirely without pity.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I sold things too. Whole ledgers of people. I just did it behind a desk, so it never looked like betrayal." She turned back toward the anvil, and the blue light rose along her like water rising. "We'll call ourselves even at the forge, and start fresh accounts after. If there is an after."

There was a scrape of boot-heels at the top of the stairs.

Sera stood there, one arm hanging wrong, her face gray with pain and something worse than pain, staring down into the chamber with the expression of a woman reading the last page of a ledger.

"They're here," she said. "Both of them. Retriever lanterns on the west ridge—and below, coming up the south face, torches. Hundreds." She swallowed. "The Warlord's found the forge. And riding at the front of the retrievers' column—" her voice cracked— "it's Corvane himself. He's brought the vault. He's brought *everything*."

Below them, in the roots of the mountain, the ancient anvil sang one low note—and answered itself, from somewhere beneath the ice, with a second.
