## 21. The Woman With No Name

The city had learned to live with light again, but Brannic had not.

He stood in the doorway of the Saltcellar, his old haunt, watching the morning sun come up over the rooftops of Vharen's Rest like a debt finally paid, and he could not stop checking the sky. Old habits. A man who had spent half a year under a starless dark did not simply stop looking up. The stars were back—had been back for three weeks now—and still every dawn felt like a trick, some ledger-entry error that the world would correct the moment he grew comfortable. He had grown comfortable exactly once since the wards ignited, on a night when he'd drunk too much plum wine and slept without dreaming, and he'd woken in a cold sweat convinced the dark had taken him while he wasn't paying attention.

So he watched the sky. And he watched the streets. Because there was someone else he was looking for too, and he was beginning to fear that what he'd kept back from the forge hadn't been enough to find her after all.

* * *

He had told no one about it. Not the defecting retriever, Sera, who now spent her days helping rebuild the border villages and asking careful questions about what else the guild had buried. Not the refugees who had fed their memories into the new covenant until the wards drank light instead of blood. Not even himself, if he could help it—he tried not to think of it as a thing he owned, because things got sold, and he had sworn off selling pieces of himself the way a reformed drunkard swears off drink: loudly, publicly, and with frequent relapses of temptation.

But on the night before they reached the Forge of Kings, cornered by Corvane's retrievers in a frozen gully with the warlord's outriders closing from the south, Brannic had done what Brannic always did. He had opened himself up and looked for something to pay with.

And he had found one memory he refused to touch.

It was small. That was the strange part. He had memories of Quentin that were worth far more at market—the moment she'd first smiled at him across the guild's counting room, sharp-eyed and suspicious; the night in the archive beneath the ice when she'd read her ancestors' crimes aloud by candlelight and her voice had only broken once. Any fence in the city would have given him passage to the far side of the world for those. Instead, cornered and desperate, he'd sold the retrievers a memory of a completely different woman entirely—a fishwife from his boyhood village, laughing on a dock, sun on the water—and kept back the smallest thing he had.

Quentin, asleep against a wall in an abandoned smokehouse, her head tipped back, frost on her eyelashes, breathing.

That was all. No revelation. No plot. Just a woman sleeping, alive, in a world going cold around her, and the stubborn fact of her breath fogging in the dark.

He had kept it back deliberately, though he couldn't have said why at the time. Now, standing in the Saltcellar's doorway three weeks into the thaw, he thought he understood. Every other memory of her was tied to who she had been—the heir, the last of the Ward-Kings, the woman whose name was worth kingdoms and whose blood was worth wars. But that one memory had nothing to do with lineage or legacy or any of the great machinery that had chewed through her life. It was just *her*. And when she had walked into the wards at the forge and poured herself into them—her name first, the name Ashgrove, the dynasty, the whole weight of it—he had understood with terrible clarity that everything the world remembered of Quentin Sterling would be gone. The guild's ledgers. The bounty boards. The stories people told.

Everyone would forget her name. Everyone except the man who had never sold the one memory that didn't need it.

* * *

The problem, he discovered, was that finding a woman with no name in a city of two hundred thousand souls was harder than smuggling contraband past a customs house.

He started where logic said she'd be. The guildhall itself had been seized by the city magistrates in the weeks after the forge—Corvane dead, his retrievers scattered or turned, the vaults cracked open and their contents returned to the streets in a great chaotic homecoming of stolen selves. Brannic had gone down to watch the returnings. Whole crowds gathered daily outside the guild's bronze doors as clerks read names from manifests and held up vials to the light, calling out descriptions: *a first kiss, salt-sweet, summer rain; a mother's face, singing; the smell of bread in a house that burned.* People wept in the square. People fainted. One old soldier received back the memory of his daughter's birth and sat down in the mud and laughed for an hour.

Brannic had watched all of it and asked each clerk, casually, whether anyone had come asking after a woman with no name. They always said no. Of course they said no. If she had come, she wouldn't have been able to ask for herself. That was the cruelty of it, the elegance of Corvane's old trade laid bare at last: you could lose your name and still walk and talk and eat and love, but you could not *find yourself*, because finding required knowing what you were looking for, and she had paid even that away.

She wouldn't know to look for him either. That was the thought that kept him up at night. She might pass him in the street and feel nothing but a vague warmth toward a stranger, the residue of whatever the wards had left unclaimed. He had heard the philosophers in the taverns arguing about it—whether a self could survive the loss of its own name, whether the woman who walked out of the forge was the same woman who walked in. Brannic didn't care about philosophy. He cared about the frost on her eyelashes, and whether anyone in this vast, brightening city was making sure she ate.

* * *

He found traces of her the way a tracker finds a deer—not by seeing, but by reading disturbance.

A baker on Cordwainer Street swore that a woman came every third morning, just after the ovens were lit, and bought a single roll with coins she counted out carefully, and always asked—this was the detail that stopped Brannic's heart—always asked what the baker would recommend, as though she had never eaten a roll before, as though taste itself were a country she was visiting for the first time. A lamplighter on the east wall mentioned a quiet woman who climbed the tower stairs some evenings to watch the stars come out, and who thanked him each time as if the lighting of lamps were a miracle performed personally for her benefit. A ferryman knew her. A flower-seller knew her. None of them knew her *name*, because she had none to give, and so they had all begun, independently, to call her by the same invented things—the Quiet Woman, the Star-Watcher, Miss Evening—and Brannic collected these fragments like a man piecing together a map from torn pages.

She was living. That was what the traces told him. She was living carefully, gently, day by day, learning the world as though newly born into it, and she was doing it alone.

On the seventeenth day of his search, he followed the trail to the terraced gardens above the River Quarter—a place the poor called the Widow's Walk, because the stone balustrade had once been carved with the faces of queens and the revolutionaries of two generations back had chiseled every royal feature into smooth blankness. He had heard the story from Sera: how the woman with no name liked to stand there among the faceless queens at dusk, looking north toward the border, toward the wards, toward the thing she had become.

He almost didn't approach. That was the truth of it, and he was honest enough with himself to admit it. What if she looked at him with a stranger's eyes? What if the warmth he carried was only his, a one-sided ember, and hers had gone into the wards along with everything else? There were worse fates than being forgotten by someone you loved. Being remembered by someone who had forgotten you—that was the knife with the curved edge.

But then he saw her turn from the balustrade, and the evening light caught her face, and though the name was gone from behind her eyes—though the dynasty and the bloodline and the whole crushing inheritance had been burned out of her like rot cut from a beam—there was something else there. Something that had never belonged to the Ward-Kings at all. A wryness. A watchfulness. The particular way she held her chin when she was deciding whether a person was worth trusting.

He had seen that look across a counting-room desk, in a guild full of impossible things, on the worst night of both their lives.

"Miss Evening," he said, because he had no right to anything older.

She regarded him with perfect politeness and no recognition whatsoever. "You have me confused with someone."

"No," Brannic said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I don't think I do."

* * *

They talked, or rather he talked, while the stars came out over the Widow's Walk one by one, each ignition a small silent miracle that neither of them remarked upon because remarking had become unnecessary. He told her nothing of who she had been. He had decided that on the stairs, climbing: he would not hand her back her past like a debt collector presenting terms. If she wanted the story of the heir of the Ward-Kings, the archives were open now, the histories rewritten, her face in every chronicle. She could find it whenever she wished. What he offered instead was smaller and, he suspected, harder to refuse.

"I owe you a confession," he said. "From before the forge. In the gully, when the retrievers had us—I bought our escape with a memory. That's what we do, people like me. We pay in ourselves." He turned the coin he always carried, an old guild token worn blank, between his fingers. "But I want you to know I kept one thing back. Deliberately. On purpose, with my whole heart, against all my instincts and my survival habits and thirty years of bad practice." He looked at her. "I kept you."

She frowned slightly, the expression of a woman trying to place a word in a language she almost spoke. "Kept me?"

"A memory of you. Just one. Small. You were asleep in a smokehouse, and there was frost on your lashes, and you were breathing, and I remember thinking—" He stopped. Started again. "I remember thinking that the world was ending, and it was still worth saving, because of that. Because of you breathing."

The silence stretched long. Below them the River Quarter lit its lamps, street by street, a constellation answering the one above.

"You're a stranger," she said at last, but her voice had changed, gone quieter, uncertain in a way that politeness could not account for. "I should tell you to leave. My instinct says—" She pressed a hand briefly to her sternum, as though feeling for something. "My instinct says I know you. Isn't that strange? There's no reason for it. No name comes with it. Just... a shape. Like a chair I've sat in before. Like a door my hand knows."

"The wards took your name," Brannic said softly. "They didn't take you. Turns out those are different things. Took me half a year and one very cold gully to learn it."

"And what do I call you?" she asked. "Since you seem to know me better than I do."

"Brannic," he said. "Smuggler. Debtor. Recently reformed, mostly." He hesitated, then held out the blank guild token, the one he'd carried since the vaults, the one stamped with nothing at all. "And here's a thought, since you're short a name and I'm short a use for this. Names aren't only given. Sometimes they're chosen. I hear there's a woman doing good business advising on the subject."

Something moved behind her eyes—not memory, which was gone, but its cousin, its ghost, the groove worn deep enough that water still found the channel. She took the token. Turned it in her fingers the way he'd turned his. And she smiled, and it was not the smile of a stranger, and it was not quite the smile of the woman he remembered either. It was newer than both.

"Cyrus," she said.

Not a question. Not a memory. A choice, arriving whole, as if it had been waiting in her all along for the world to make room for it.

Brannic felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched since the forge. "Cyrus," he repeated, testing it, keeping it. "Well then. Cyrus it is."

Above them the stars burned steady in their courses, fed now by ten thousand freely given memories, and somewhere in that vast bright lattice, he liked to think, was a woman's name, holding up the sky.

She slipped the blank token into her pocket, next to—she must have noticed his glance—a small leather-bound book, empty, its pages waiting.

"I've been meaning to start a ledger," she said. "There are things I've decided shouldn't be for sale. I thought I'd write them down, so the world remembers at least one person kept accounts honestly."

Brannic laughed—really laughed, for the first time since the stars went out. "Then you'll need a clerk," he said. "I hear the trade's dying out. Terrible hours, no benefits, occasional flaying."

"Terrible references, more likely," said Cyrus, the woman with no name, walking beside him out of the garden of faceless queens and into the lamplit streets of the city she had saved. "Come on. I'll buy you a roll. The baker recommends the ones with currants. Apparently I'm learning to have opinions."

And under the returned stars, two people who owed each other everything went down the hill together, toward breakfast, toward the first page, toward whatever came next.
