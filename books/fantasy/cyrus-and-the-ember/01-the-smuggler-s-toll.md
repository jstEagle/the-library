## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The snow began an hour before dawn and did not stop, which Lior Whitlock considered a personal insult.

They stood at the head of the caravan, hip-deep in the pass called the Needle's Eye, watching fat flakes come down with the lazy confidence of something that had never once been late for anything. Behind them stretched eleven mules, four sledges of contraband salt, and seven smugglers who trusted Lior's judgment for reasons that had never once survived daylight scrutiny.

"You're frowning," said Brammel, the eldest of them, hauling himself up through the drifts to stand at Lior's shoulder. He was sixty if he was a day, built like a rain barrel, and had once told Lior—drunk on plum brandy, weeping freely—that they were the finest thing this country had produced since the invention of cheese. Lior had not corrected him. It would have required a longer conversation than either of them wanted.

"I'm assessing," Lior said.

"You're frowning *assessingly*."

"The snow's wrong."

Brammel squinted up into the grey. "It looks like snow."

"It's falling too straight. There's no wind." Lior turned their face into it, letting flakes land on their eyelashes, melt there. "Wind comes off the eastern shoulder before a storm like this. It always has. I've walked this pass since before you could grow that magnificent beard, and the wind has never once failed to arrive and make my life miserable."

"And now it hasn't?"

"Now it hasn't."

Brammel considered this with the gravity of a man deciding whether to have a second breakfast. "Bad?"

"Could be nothing." Lior blew out a breath. "Could be someone's bent the weather around us. Could be wolves using the quiet to hunt."

"Wolves don't bend weather."

"No," Lior agreed. "Wolves are honest creatures. That's what makes them better company than most people."

* * *

The salt was the easy part. Salt was always the easy part—the Compact taxed it past reason, so every village from Thornfell's knees to its ankles bought it black-market, wrapped in oilcloth and buried under honest cargo like turnips nobody wanted. The hard part, the part Lior was actually paid for, was the knowing: which drift hid a crevasse, which shelf of rock would sing under a mule's hoof and bring half the mountainside down, which silences were empty and which were *occupied*.

Lior had been a swordspeaker once. Formally sworn, blade-blessed, one of the last cohort ever to take the oath before the kingdom of Vael finished crumbling into rumor. The magic of it was simple and terrible: you spoke a vow aloud, truly meant, and the words became light along your blade. *I will hold this bridge until dawn*, and your sword burned white until dawn came or you died trying. *I will guard these children home*, and no shadow could touch them while you stood. Oath-magic. Promise made flesh and fire.

The Compact had burned the last of the sword-schools eleven years ago. They called it historical correction. Lior called it Tuesday, mostly, and tried not to think about it at all otherwise.

Now they used what remained of the gift for wolves and weather, which felt roughly like using a cathedral bell as a soup ladle.

The wolves came at dusk, as wolves do, when the light goes blue and everything honest gets tired.

Lior heard them first—a wrongness in the silence behind the caravan, the particular quality of quiet that means something is listening back. They raised one hand, and the column stopped without a word, because after six years of running this route the smugglers knew that hand the way sailors know a dropped sail.

"How many?" Brammel whispered.

Lior closed their eyes. Listened with the old training, the part of them that had been sharpened in a school that no longer existed, by teachers whose names the Compact had scraped from every record and monument. "Seven. Maybe eight. Circle's forming off our left flank. They're hungry enough to try us, but not desperate yet."

"Yet being the important word."

"Yet being the important word." Lior drew their sword—not the oath-blade; that was long gone, broken and buried somewhere Lior visited only in dreams—but a good honest smuggler's steel with a chipped edge and a comfortable grip. They walked ten paces ahead of the lead sledge, planted their feet in the snow, and spoke.

Not loudly. That was the thing about swordspeech that the songs always got wrong. It wasn't volume. It was truth.

*"I stand between,"* Lior said, meaning every word of it, the way you had to, the way that cost something each time, *"and nothing crosses me tonight that I do not permit."*

Light woke along the blade like dawn trapped in metal. White-gold, steady, humming faintly, throwing their long shadow back across the mules so that it looked—Brammel swore afterward—for all the world like a giant standing guard over a train of ants hauling turnips.

The wolves came anyway, because hunger is also a kind of honesty. Lior saw the eyes first, green coins floating in the dark between the pines, and then the shapes themselves, lean and frost-rimed, moving low.

They did not charge. They never did, once the light was up. Wolves were smart enough to recognize a vow when they saw one burning. Instead they paced at the edge of the glow, close enough that Lior could hear breath steaming from their jaws, see the ribs under the winter coats, and Lior hated this part most of all—not the danger, which was simple, but the arithmetic. Seven wolves. One haunch of salt-pork in Brammel's pack that he thought Lior didn't know about.

"Throw it," Lior said over their shoulder.

"Throw wha—"

"The pork, Brammel. Left side, toward the rocks, gently."

A pause. A sigh with decades of practice behind it. Then the thump of wrapped meat in snow, and the green eyes flowed away toward it like water finding a channel, and the night went back to being merely cold instead of hungry.

"You knew about the pork," Brammel said, coming up beside them, wounded.

"I know about everything. It's my entire personality."

"That pork was for my birthday."

"Your birthday was two months ago and you ate it already."

"My *second* birthday. Some of us celebrate more than once a year. It's called hope."

Lior let the blade-light gutter down to embers, then out, and felt the small familiar ache where the vow released—like a muscle unclenching after holding too long. Every promise took something. Nobody warned you about that at the school. They taught you the glory parts: the burning blades, the kings you'd kneel to, the songs. They did not teach you that you'd spend your forties spending your soul in pennies, buying safe passage for turnip carts.

"Camp here?" Brammel asked.

"Not here. Twenty minutes further, there's a hollow below the split crag, wind-shadowed. We camp, we post watches, and tomorrow we're through the Eye and down to the waystation at Greyfall."

"The old Vaeli one? With the roof?"

"The roof is optional. The walls are load-bearing, which is more than can be said for most things these days."

* * *

The hollow was exactly where Lior's feet remembered it being, which was the only map Lior had ever fully trusted. They got fires lit small and shielded, mules rubbed down, sledges checked, salt accounted for. The smugglers ate in the firelight and told the kind of jokes that only work when everyone is cold and tired and glad to be both together rather than separately.

Dess, the youngest, seventeen and all elbows, scooted over next to Lior with the transparent casualness of youth approaching an elder for stories.

"Lior. Is it true you were at the Fall of Cairnwatch?"

"No."

"Is it true you *knew* the last king?"

"I knew a man who owed me money. The crown was incidental."

"Is it true—" Dess lowered their voice, reverent, "—that swordspeakers could burn a whole army with one word?"

Lior looked at the kid across the firelight. Seventeen years old, born after the fall, carrying a country's memory entirely secondhand, hungry for anything true. It was, Lior reflected, the oldest trick kingdoms ever played: dying spectacularly enough that the living spent generations grieving the show instead of noticing what was actually lost. What was lost was boring. Roads maintained. Granaries full. Judges who couldn't be bought with less than a fortune. Teachers. The school where Lior had learned that a vow was a weight you trained under, like stone, until your soul grew shoulders.

"No," Lior said. "One word, one vow, one cost. Nothing in this world burns free, Dess. Anyone who tells you different is selling something, usually to people who can least afford it."

"But it's real. The swordspeech. You just—you did it tonight. I saw it."

"It's real," Lior admitted.

"What's it feel like?"

Lior considered lying. Considered the usual deflections. Then looked again at that open, freezing, hopeful face and heard themselves say, quietly:

"Like making a promise you can't take back, and feeling it settle into your bones like a stone you'll carry forever. And also like—the moment right after you catch someone falling. When you know, for exactly one heartbeat, that your hands were enough." They poked the fire. "Both at once. Forever. That's why there were never very many of us."

Dess sat with that, which Lior appreciated. Kids who sat with things were rare. Kids who sat with things and then asked the *next* question were rarer still.

"Would you do it again?" Dess asked. "If the country asked? If Vael came back?"

"Vael isn't coming back, Dess. Countries are like teeth. Once they're gone, you can be sorry about it, but no amount of wishing grows a new one."

"But if—"

"Sleep," Lior said, not unkindly. "We cross the high shoulder tomorrow, and the mountain doesn't care what we believe. Only whether we're rested."

* * *

Lior took the last watch, because they always did, because sleep had been negotiating poorly with them for years and both parties had stopped pretending.

Snow had stopped. Stars in ragged patches overhead. The mules breathing steam. Somewhere below, a wolf howled once—not threat, just announcement, the way neighbors knock—and Lior found their hand resting on their sword hilt without having decided to put it there.

They thought about the oath. Not any specific vow—the oath itself, the deep one, the one taken at nineteen with a blade on each shoulder and a king's representative reading names off a scroll. *I swear by ember and iron, by hearth and harvest, by the blood of Vael and the bones of her hills.* Everyone had laughed, afterward, at the feast. *By the bones of her hills,* a fellow candidate had snorted. *As if the hills care.* And Lior had laughed too, and drunk too much, and gone to bed certain the world would always need them.

Then the world had decided otherwise. Plague in the lowlands, war in the east, and a Compact rising out of the ashes wearing ash-grey livery and calling itself order, burning records first and resisters second, because a people with no history have no argument for being anything other than what they're told to be. Vael hadn't fallen. It had been *edited*.

And the oaths? The oaths had simply—stayed. In Lior's chest. Like furniture left in an abandoned house. Still functional. Still binding, in the way that a scar binds skin. No king to release them. No court to dissolve them. Just Lior, walking salt through mountains, keeping vows to a country that no longer officially existed, and getting paid badly for it in coin that said nothing at all on its face, because even money had been reformed.

"Bones of her hills," Lior muttered to the stars. "You could have mentioned."

The stars, being stars, declined to comment.

It was nearly dawn when they heard the sound.

At first they took it for the wind finally arriving, fashionably late, making up for lost time with interest. But wind didn't come up the pass from the south. Wind didn't stumble. And whatever this was, it stumbled—it crashed through the frozen scrub below the hollow with the graceless urgency of something running on the last of itself, and Lior was on their feet with their blade drawn before the conscious part of them caught up.

A figure broke from the treeline. A man—or what had been a man—lurching uphill through the snow, dark-cloaked, leaving a red thread behind him on the white that even starlight couldn't hide. Behind him, far down the pass, faint but unmistakable: horn calls. Two notes, repeated. Compact signal-calls. Lior knew them the way they knew toothaches.

The man reached the edge of the firelight and fell, and Lior moved to catch him, and the whole night changed shape.

He was young. Mid-twenties, courier-lean, boots worn through, three arrows in him—one in the shoulder, one in the thigh, and one, Lior understood with a cold settling in the gut, in the belly, which meant there was nothing anyone could do, and the man knew it too, because his eyes went past Lior's sword, past the light, past everything, and fixed on Lior's face with terrible focus.

"Swordspeaker," he rasped. Blood on his teeth. "Gods. An actual—I'd given up—"

"Easy. You're among—"

He grabbed Lior's collar with a fist that had maybe a minute of strength left in it, and pressed something against their chest. A leather tube, wax-sealed, stamped with a sigil Lior recognized and had hoped never to see again: the crossed keys of the Vaeli Royal Cartographers.

"The Ember Road," the courier whispered. Each word cost him visibly, and he paid anyway. "The Door. Before winter. Before—they must not—" His grip tightened. His eyes searched Lior's, hunting for something. Finding it or not finding it, Lior would never know. "Sworn... you're still..."

And whatever else he meant to say went out of him like a candle pinched, and the pass was suddenly very quiet, and very cold, and Lior Whitlock knelt in the snow holding a dead man's map and feeling, for the first time in eleven years, the old oath stir in their chest like a sleeper turning over.

Down the pass, the horns sounded again. Closer now.

Behind them, Brammel's voice, hoarse with dread: "Lior? What in the name of every abandoned god is happening?"

Lior looked at the wax seal. Looked south, toward the horns. Frowned, assessingly.
