## 5. The Cartographer's Daughter

Sefa Idrin knew three things about the stranger who came into her shop on the last market day of autumn, and she learned all of them in the space of a single breath.

First: they were not from Marrowgate. Nobody in Marrowgate wore a sword like that — old, plain-hilted, worn at the hip the way other people wore their own hands, as if it had grown there and been apologized for ever since.

Second: they were looking for her specifically, because they'd walked past two other map-sellers, past the chandler's, past the pie stall whose smell could turn a saint's head, and come straight to the crooked little shop at the end of Tanner's Row with the hand-painted sign that read IDRIN & DAUGHTER, MAPS, CHARTS, AND CORRECTIONS.

Third — and this was the thing that made her set down her pen very carefully, so as not to smudge the coastline she'd been inking for an hour — they had a scar through one eyebrow and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they looked at, including her.

"Are you Idrin?" the stranger asked.

"I'm the daughter half," Sefa said. "The Idrin half is elsewhere." She kept her voice pleasant and her hands where they could be seen. Years of living in a border town taught you that much. "Can I help you find something? A chart of the lake country? Corrections to an older survey? I do very good corrections. People bring me maps drawn by people who clearly never saw the rivers they were drawing."

The stranger didn't smile. "I have a map," they said. "I need someone who can read it."

"Then you've come to the right shop, provided it isn't a Compact survey. I don't touch those."

"It isn't."

"Provided it isn't stolen."

A pause. A long one. The kind of pause that has a whole confession folded up inside it, pressed flat.

"That depends," the stranger said finally, "on who you think maps belong to."

Sefa put down her pen entirely now. "That," she said, "is either the most honest answer I've heard this year or the least, and I genuinely can't tell which. Close the door. There's a draft, and also I don't sell my father's secrets to the whole street."

* * *

Her father's secrets. She'd said it without thinking, and the moment it left her mouth she felt it land somewhere in her chest, heavy as a dropped weight.

Tammas Idrin had been gone eleven months. Not dead — she refused the word — *gone*, north along the old salt roads with his good satchel and his bad knee and a commission he wouldn't name, leaving behind a letter that said only: *Keep the shop. Keep the ledgers. If anyone comes asking after me, be duller than ditchwater.* And then, smaller, squeezed into the corner as if written after midnight: *And if anyone comes carrying something with my mark on it, Sefa — look at it before you let them burn it.*

She had thought about that sentence every day for eleven months. *Before you let them burn it.* As though there would be a moment when someone stood in front of her holding fire and her father's work, and she would have some say in which happened first.

Now here was a stranger with a sword and a guilty conscience standing in front of her, and under their arm, wrapped in oilcloth, something rectangular and stiff.

"You knew my father," she said. It wasn't a question. "You're the one he wrote about. Or you're the reason he wrote it."

"I never met him," the stranger said. "My name is Lior Whitlock."

The name went through the shop like cold air through a cracked pane. Sefa had grown up on stories of the swordspeakers — every Vaelish child had, back when Vaelish children were allowed stories — but those stories were twenty years old and buried under twenty years of ash-grey law, and hearing the name spoken aloud in a map shop in Marrowgate was like hearing a ghost clear its throat.

"Lior Whitlock," she repeated. "Who is supposed to be dead."

"Rumors of my death were greatly exaggerated by me," Lior said. "It was convenient."

"And now?"

"Now I have a map I can't read, and a courier died giving it to me, and the people who killed him will level this town to get it back. So I need to know what's on it, quickly, and then I need to decide whether to destroy it."

"*Before* you let them burn it," Sefa murmured, mostly to herself, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

She knew the hand before she knew the map.

That was the terrible part. Eleven months of grief, and grief had apparently been taking notes, because the instant the wax seal cracked — her father's mark, the little compass rose with the crooked east point, because Tammas Idrin had broken a compass in 914 and never forgiven cartography for it — her eyes went straight to the linework and her heart went straight through the floor.

The strokes were his. The confident downstrokes, the tiny hooks at the ends of contour lines like the mountains were trying to wave. The way rivers thickened toward their mouths as if gaining courage. The marginalia in his cramped, slanting script — *here the road lies, whatever the Compact says* — and, in the lower corner, a signature she had watched him write ten thousand times over her childhood drafting table:

*T. Idrin, Cartographer Royal (deposed), for whom it may still matter.*

"He drew this," she said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. Far away. "This is recent. Within the year. The ink oxidation alone—" She stopped, because Lior Whitlock was watching her with that arithmetic look, and she realized she was doing the thing she always did when she was frightened, which was lecture. "He's alive," she said. "Or he was. When he drew this."

Lior said nothing, which was its own answer, and Sefa hated them a little for it.

The map itself was unlike anything she'd ever held. Oh, it looked ordinary enough — mountain ranges, passes, a road running north through the Thornfell spine marked in red ochre — but when she tilted it toward the lamp, the lines *moved*. Not obviously. Not the vulgar shimmer of enchanters' novelties sold at fairs. Just a suggestion, at the edge of seeing, that the pass called the Kettle was slightly deeper than ink could say, and that somewhere past the glacier wall there was a seam in the world drawn in a color she had no name for.

"This is true cartography," she breathed. "Actual true cartography. I thought it was lost. I thought *he* was the last one who—" She caught herself again. "Do you know what you're holding? This doesn't just show roads. It shows roads that only exist for certain people. Sworn people. My father used to talk about it like a bedtime story. *The map knows your name, Sefa-poppet, if your name is worth knowing.*"

"My name appears to be worth knowing," Lior said grimly. "Against my will. That's rather the problem."

"Then you've read it."

"Some of it. Enough to know where it leads. Not enough to know how to get there alive." Lior's jaw tightened. "There are gaps. Deliberate ones. Your father locked parts of it."

"Of course he did." And despite everything — the fear, the grief, the stranger with the dead man's map — Sefa felt a fierce, sudden, ridiculous pride. *Locked parts of it.* Her father, the deposed royal cartographer, the man who once spent a week arguing with a duke about whether a bridge counted as infrastructure or opinion, had built a lock into a dying art's last masterpiece, and the lock was a language, and she was raised in that language the way other children were raised in lullabies.

She looked up. Lior was already reaching for the map.

"No," Sefa said.

Lior stopped.

"You want to take it and go and probably burn it somewhere dramatic, and I can see it in your face, you have a whole plan, and your plan does not include me." She stood, planting both palms on the drafting table. "So here's what happens instead. You sit down, you drink the tea I am going to make because you look like you haven't slept since the war, and we discuss terms."

"There are no terms. This isn't a negotiation—"

"Everything is a negotiation. My father taught me that too, usually while negotiating with me about bedtime." She held up fingers. "One: I read the map. All of it. Two: I come with you."

"No."

"Three: you'll notice I skipped asking permission, because I wasn't going to get it, so let's move to four, which is compensation—"

"Sefa." Lior's voice was quiet, and tired, and underneath the tiredness something old and sad. "The people hunting this map burned a waystation two days ago. They hanged a man who'd done nothing worse than sell salt. Whatever you're imagining this journey is, imagine it colder, hungrier, and shorter. I'm not dragging a mapmaker's daughter into that."

"My father is *in* that," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked, and she kept going anyway. "He walked north eleven months ago and every night since I have made up a story about where he is, and every morning the story gets worse. You are standing in my shop holding his handwriting and his blood — don't look surprised, I can see the stain on the corner, I've catalogued every stain he ever made, it's a professional habit — and you're telling me to stay home and be duller than ditchwater?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "He even predicted you. He *knew* someone would come. He wrote to me about it. Which means he knew it was dangerous, and he went anyway, which means whatever's at the end of this road mattered more to him than coming home, and I will go out of my mind if I don't know why."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Outside, Marrowgate clattered through its market day, oblivious.

"The courier," Lior said at last, slowly. "Did he say anything? Before he died?"

"'The Door before winter,'" Sefa said. "I assumed it was delirium."

"It wasn't." Lior rubbed their eyes. "Your father found the Last Door. Or the way to it. And he sent it south instead of using it himself, which means—" They stopped. Sefa watched them arrive at it, watched the arithmetic finish. "Which means he couldn't use it. Or wouldn't. Which means the Door costs something."

"Then we'll find out what," Sefa said, "together, because you cannot read his locks without me, and I cannot survive the road without you, and neither of us trusts the other, which frankly sounds like the healthiest possible foundation for a partnership."

For the first time, something moved across Lior Whitlock's face that might, in better light, among friendlier company, have been the ancestor of a smile.

"You argue like him," they said.

"Everyone says that." Sefa was already pulling her travel satchel from under the counter, already sweeping her best instruments into it — the good dividers, the brass alidade, the roll of waterproof vellum — moving fast before either of them could reconsider. "Terms. I keep the map at night. I make the copies. When this is over, if my father is alive, you help me find him, and if he's not—" Her hands stilled on the satchel buckle. "If he's not, you tell me exactly how, and you don't soften it, because I've had eleven months of softening and it's worse than the truth."

"Agreed," Lior said quietly.

"Agreed." She buckled the satchel. Then, because her father had also taught her that a good negotiator always secures one concession the other party hasn't noticed yet: "Also you're carrying the tent."

* * *

They left Marrowgate an hour before dusk, slipping out the north gate with the market crowds as cover, and Sefa spent the first mile learning what Lior Whitlock's weariness looked like up close: the constant half-turn, checking the road behind; the way they walked on the verge rather than the road proper, out of habit older than sense; the way their hand rested near the plain sword not gripping it, just *remembering* it.

"So," she said, to fill the silence and because silence made her think of empty drafting tables. "The oath. The swordspeech. Does it hurt?"

Lior glanced at her. "Why do you ask?"

"Because you flinched at the gate. There was a Compact notice board — the usual, rewards, prohibitions, a list of banned words — and you flinched. I watch people for a living. Lines and faces are the same problem."

"It didn't hurt," Lior said. "It *woke up*. Like a dog that's been asleep for years hearing its name. I've spent a long time teaching it to lie quiet, and since the courier died it keeps... offering. Every time I pass a boundary stone, every time I swear something trivial — 'I swear this tea is terrible,' 'I swear we'll make the ford by dark' — it leans in, like it wants to make them real." Their mouth twisted. "An oath-magic that answers a woman who swore years ago never to use it again. There's a joke in there. I haven't found it yet."

"Give it time. Jokes are like maps. The good ones reveal themselves slowly, and usually at the worst possible moment." Sefa adjusted her satchel strap and nodded back the way they'd come, where the smoke of Marrowgate's evening fires was rising straight and peaceful into the cold sky. "Besides, you might want it awake soon. My father locked that map for a reason, and in my experience, when Tammas Idrin locks a door, it's because something on the other side is worth guarding — or worth keeping *from* somebody."

Behind them, faint across the fields, a bell began to ring. Not the market bell. Not any bell Sefa knew.

Lior turned, and Sefa watched the arithmetic start all over again in their eyes — faster this time, and ending somewhere grim.

"That's the alarm bell," Lior said. "Marrowgate doesn't have an alarm bell."

"It got one," Sefa said, "about the time we started walking away from it."

Far off, thin against the darkening sky, a thread of smoke rose from the direction of Tanner's Row — from the crooked little shop at the end of it, with the hand-painted sign.

Lior took her arm. "Run."
