## 7. The Ember Road

The map had been singing to Sefa all morning.

Not aloud—she'd checked that twice, holding it to her ear like a seashell while the others watched her with varying degrees of concern. But there was a warmth in the wax-sealed paper, a faint pulse against her palms whenever she unrolled it, and when she traced her father's inked lines with one finger, a thread of ember-light kindled along the route like a fuse catching.

"It does that," Lior said, not looking up from the scree they were climbing. "It's been doing it since the shrine cave. I assumed you knew."

"I assumed you'd *tell* me."

"I assumed you'd ask." A pause. "You didn't. You've been talking to it for an hour."

Sefa rolled the map up with as much dignity as she could muster, which was not much, given that she was doing it while walking uphill in someone else's boots. The boots were Lior's spare pair, two sizes too large, stuffed at the toe with dried moss. They made her walk like a heron. She had decided not to care.

Behind them, Marrowgate's smoke still smudged the southern sky—a grey bruise on the horizon that none of them looked at directly, the way you don't press on a wound to check if it still hurts. The town was gone. The inquisitors had come for a map and found a cartographer's daughter instead, and Sefa had left behind everything she owned except her father's drafting compass, which rode in her breast pocket now, cold and familiar as a second heartbeat.

And the children. She had the children too, which was its own kind of weight.

They walked ahead of her on the narrow trail: Pell, who was maybe nine and hadn't spoken more than six words since Marrowgate burned, and Wren, who was seven and had apparently decided that silence was for other people. Wren had asked forty-one questions since dawn by Sefa's count, most recently: *"If the old kingdom is dead, who buried it? And did they cry at the funeral?"*

"Good questions," Lior had said gravely. "Ask me again when we're not being followed."

"We're not being followed," Sefa said now, scanning the canyon walls out of habit. Red sandstone rose on either side of them in layered cliffs, streaked with white mineral veins like old scars. The trail—her father's trail, she was nearly sure of it now—wound north through the canyon floor along a dry streambed, and the boundary stones stood beside it every half-mile like patient sentries.

That was the thing she couldn't stop thinking about. The stones.

They were waist-high pillars of grey granite, older than anything else in sight, carved with characters she half-recognized from her father's reference books—the old Vaelish script that the Compact had spent fifteen years scraping off official buildings, bridges, and, in one memorable case that Sefa's father had told her about over dinner, an entire cathedral. These stones had escaped the scraping because no one remembered they mattered. And yet.

When she passed the first one, she'd felt it: a hum, low and felt-more-than-heard, like a tuning fork pressed to bone. The second stone hummed too. So did the third. By the tenth she'd stopped pretending she wasn't pressing her palm flat against each one as she went by, and by the fifteenth she'd noticed something stranger.

They weren't all humming the same note.

"They're tuned," she said, catching up to Lior. "The boundary stones. Each one's pitched differently. If you walked this road and listened—" she hummed three ascending notes, "—it's a scale. It's a *song*, Lior. Someone built a song into a border."

Lior's expression did something complicated. Sefa had known them four days and had already catalogued a whole taxonomy of those complicated expressions: the ones where grief and wonder arrived at the same time and neither would yield the door. This was one of the big ones.

"The wardens sang the bounds," Lior said quietly. "Twice a year. Spring and harvest. Every swordspeaker in every garrison walked their stretch of border and spoke the road awake." Their hand brushed the nearest stone almost absently, the way you'd touch a doorframe of a childhood home. "I thought the Compact had silenced all of them."

"You thought wrong. Or they missed some." Sefa glanced back down the trail, where Wren was currently hugging a boundary stone with both arms, ear pressed to the granite, reporting findings to Pell, who pretended not to be interested but had crept closer. "Or maybe you can't silence something people keep humming to."

Something flickered across Lior's face—too quick to name—and then they lengthened their stride, because Lior dealt with feelings the way other people dealt with loose rocks on a cliff path: quickly, and without looking down.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, which Sefa suspected was the only way Wren ever fell asleep. Pell lay awake watching the fire with the particular stillness of a child relearning that fire could also be warm.

Lior took first watch. Sefa unrolled the map across her knees by firelight and let herself look at her father's handwriting properly for the first time since Marrowgate.

She knew this hand better than her own. The confident verticals. The little hooked serifs he added to his numerals because, he claimed, plain numbers were "morally lazy." The way he always drew north arrows as tiny flames, because "north is just the direction you go when everything else has burned down, and you should honor that." She used to laugh at that. Now she read it and her throat closed.

*Papa, where are you?*

He'd gone north eight months ago. Commissioned, he'd said—a private client, good pay, secrecy required, and her father had been poor and flattered in equal measure, which was a dangerous combination in a man whose entire profession was knowing where things were. He'd written twice from the road. Then once. Then nothing.

And now Compact inquisitors had burned her town hunting a copy of a map in his style, and the original was glowing in her lap like a live coal, leading her north along roads that officially did not exist, toward a Door that a dying courier had whispered about with his last breath.

Either her father was alive at the end of this road, or he had died making sure the road existed. Sefa wasn't ready to know which. She suspected the map was going to make her know anyway.

"You're doing the thing," Lior said, without turning from their watch.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the map like it owes you money."

"My father drew north arrows as little flames," Sefa said. "Did you know that?"

A long pause. "Yes," Lior said. "I did." And then, softer: "He drew mine that way too. On the garrison surveys. Before you were born."

Sefa looked up sharply. Lior was still facing the dark, silhouette straight-backed against the stars, giving away nothing—which was, Sefa was learning, exactly how Lior gave away everything.

"You knew my father before I did."

"Everyone who ever held a Vaelish surveyor's compass knew Aldous Idrin's work. Half of us learned our borders from his maps." A pause. "He once argued with a warden-commander for three hours over a river that had moved. He won. They redrew the charter."

"That sounds like him."

"He brought honeycakes to the argument," Lior added. "For the loser. Which was the commander. It was, everyone agreed, the cruelest kindness anyone had ever committed in that garrison."

Sefa laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked partway through and became something else, and she sat with the map in her lap and let the fire blur until it was just light. Lior let her. That was another thing she was learning about them: they understood that sometimes the correct response to grief was to stand very still nearby.

* * *

Two days north, the canyon narrowed and the ember-trail on the map brightened, and so did the stones.

Sefa noticed it first as color: a faint orange glow in the heart of each pillar as dusk came down, deepening as true dark fell, until the boundary stones marked the road like lanterns set a hundred paces apart, burning low and steady in the black. Not firelight—she'd held her hand over one and felt no heat, only that hum, stronger now, resonant, as if years of silence were ending and the stones were remembering how.

Wren was ecstatic. Pell, cautiously, touched one and then, finding himself unburned and unarrested, touched several more with increasing boldness.

"Is it calling someone?" Wren asked, walking backward between two glowing pillars so as to miss nothing.

"It's remembering," Lior said. "There's a difference."

"How?"

"A call expects an answer. A memory doesn't need one. It just... insists on having happened."

Wren considered this with the full seriousness of a seven-year-old. "Like Mama humming in the kitchen even when nobody was listening."

Lior stopped walking for exactly one heartbeat. "Exactly like that," they said, and walked on, and Sefa filed that away next to everything else she was learning about the person who claimed, loudly and often, to have no country anymore.

They made camp in a wide place where a tributary canyon opened eastward, and it was there, over a supper of toasted barley and a truly criminal amount of stolen honey, that Sefa got drunk—not on wine, but on hope.

Because the map, laid flat on a flat rock, had begun to show things it hadn't shown before.

Roads. Faint threads of ember-light branching off the main line, running to places she'd never heard named: *Harrowfen. The Nine Sisters. Candlemere.* Villages too small for imperial atlases, towns the Compact's censuses recorded as "uninhabited"—and yet each thread of light pulsed gently, rhythmically, and when she bent close, she could swear the pulses weren't random.

"They're hearth-fires," she breathed. "Lior. Look. Each of these lights—it beats. Slow. Like—"

"Like breathing," Lior said, very quietly. "Like a house at night."

"Someone lives there. Someone's kept a fire going. All these years, all these places the empire says are empty—" Sefa's voice wobbled and she steadied it with fury, because fury was easier. "They lied. Or they couldn't see. Fifteen years of 'Vael is dissolved, Vael is a legal fiction,' and there are *kitchens*, there are people lighting lamps and raising children and probably arguing about rivers—"

"Probably," Lior agreed, with the ghost of a smile.

Sefa laughed, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, laughed again. For one reckless hour around the small fire, the world remade itself: Wren demanded stories of Candlemere and got one; Pell, unprompted, asked what Harrowfen was like and listened to the answer all the way through; and Sefa sat wrapped in her father's too-big map-light and let herself believe, fully and stupidly and wonderfully, that the road led somewhere warm. That her father had walked ahead of her the whole time, drawing little flame-arrows, leaving a trail a daughter could follow home.

She went to sleep smiling. She wanted to remember that she'd gone to sleep smiling.

* * *

Morning corrected her, as mornings do.

They reached the fork at midday: the main canyon bending northwest, a narrower gorge splitting northeast, and the ember-thread on the map splitting with them—one branch bright and steady toward the northwest, the other, toward the northeast, guttering. Flickering. The light ran along that line unevenly, stuttering like a candle in wind, and where the northeastern branch ended, the map showed nothing at all. Not blank paper. Nothing. As if the ink had been *taken* rather than never written.

Sefa stood over it for a long time.

"Lior," she said. "What happens to a hearth-fire when the house burns down?"

Lior came and looked. Said nothing. Which was answer enough.

"The guttering line goes toward Thornfell's high passes," Sefa said, and her voice came out level, which cost her everything she had. "Toward the Last Door. Toward wherever Papa was going. And it's dying, Lior. Whatever's at the end of that line, it's going out. It's been going out for months."

Eight months. The length of time since her father's last letter.

She knelt there in the dirt with the map spread under her hands and let herself do the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since Marrowgate: that maps don't gutter because their makers moved on. Maps gutter because their makers died, and the last light of what they knew was bleeding out of the paper. Her father's hand, his hooked numerals, his flame-arrow north—all of it fading on a road that only answered to sworn blood, drawn by a man who must have known, drawing it, exactly what it would cost.

"Papa," she whispered. "You absolute stubborn man."

"Hey." Wren appeared at her elbow, unexpectedly grave. "Sometimes fires go out and it's not anybody's fault. Sometimes it's just wind."

Sefa looked down at the child. Wren gazed back with the terrible clarity of the very young, who understand loss professionally and offer comfort without anesthesia.

"Sometimes," Sefa managed, "it is just wind."

"But sometimes somebody relights it," Wren said, and pointed at the northwest branch, the strong one, the one pulsing steady as breath. "That one's still lit. Somebody kept it going. So if yours went out—" a shrug, devastatingly casual, "—somebody else can hold it till you get there. That's what fires are *for*."

The canyon hummed around them, patient, insistent, remembering. Somewhere far south, a town she'd loved was ash. Somewhere far north, a door waited in the ice with her father's last secret behind it. And here in the middle, on a road the dead kingdom still sang, a seven-year-old handed her the only theology worth carrying.

Sefa folded the map along her father's creases, careful as closing a book over a pressed flower, and stood up.

"Northwest first," she said. "We follow the living line. We warn the hearths the Compact hasn't found yet—and we find out who's still keeping the fires." She met Lior's eyes. "Then we go find what killed the other one."

Lior studied her for a moment—measuring, Sefa realized, not whether she meant it, but how much of herself it had cost to say it. Apparently satisfied, they nodded once.

"Northwest," Lior said. And then, nodding past Sefa's shoulder toward the mouth of the gorge:

"But we may want to decide quickly. Because something's coming up the canyon—and it isn't humming."

Sefa turned. Far down the ember-lit road, where the boundary stones burned their low orange vigil, the light was going out. One stone. Then the next. Then the next—a darkness moving toward them up the canyon, steady as footsteps, swallowing the old song stone by stone.

And above it, thin and clear on the wind, came the sound of bells. Small, many, and wrong—like a chime rung inside a coffin.

Wren's hand found Sefa's. Pell stepped back against the humming stone behind him, as if it might protect him, as if it weren't already falling silent.

"Sefa," Lior said quietly, and their hand had gone to the broken-hilted blade at their side. "Get the children behind the stones. Now."
