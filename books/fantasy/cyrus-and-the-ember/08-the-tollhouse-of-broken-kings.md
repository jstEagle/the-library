## 8. The Tollhouse of Broken Kings

The tollhouse appeared out of the snow like a rumor someone had finally decided to make true.

It stood where the Ember Road narrowed between two shoulders of rock, a squat stone building with a roof of old legion slate and a sign that had been repainted so many times the original letters bled through like ghosts. Lior could just make them out beneath the fresh coat: *TOLLHOUSE OF THE NINTH GATE — PAY WHAT YOU OWE.*

"Charming," Sefa said, blowing on her gloved fingers. "Very welcoming. Very *pay us or freeze*."

"That's the idea," Lior said. "It's honest, at least. Most kingdoms dress it up in flags and coronations. This just says it plainly."

Behind them, Tam and Wren had stopped arguing about whose turn it was to carry the water skin — a dispute that had lasted, by Lior's count, three days and had outlived two blizzards and one wolf sighting. Wren, the smaller and more ruthless of the two, was winning, as usual, by the simple expedient of walking faster.

"Stay close," Lior said. "And whatever happens in there — don't tell anyone where we're going. Don't tell anyone where we've been. If someone asks your name, make one up."

"Can I be a duke?" Tam asked.

"You may be whatever you like as long as you're nobody."

"I'll be Duke Nobody," Tam said, satisfied, "of the Frozen Wastes."

"See, *that's* ambition," Sefa said. "I like this child."

Lior pushed open the door.

* * *

Warmth hit them first — woodsmoke and stew and the particular animal smell of too many people sleeping in too small a space. The common room was low-beamed and crowded, filled with perhaps thirty souls who all had the same look: the look of people who had once worn a uniform and had never quite stopped standing like it.

Lior counted the tells without meaning to. The way the woman stacking firewood kept her weight on her left foot — old cavalry habit, a horse gone from under her years ago. The man mending a net by the fire with soldier's hands, overhand and quick, though there was no river within fifty miles. The two by the window playing dice with border-guard rules Lior hadn't seen since Vael still existed on maps.

Deserters. Every last one of them. Or — Lior corrected themself, because the tollhouse folk would correct it harder — *leavers*. Men and women who had walked away from every army that ever served the old kingdom, and who had gathered here, at the crossroads of a road that officially did not exist, to charge travelers for soup and a roof and call it honest work.

Behind the counter stood a mountain of a man with a beard you could lose a child in — and Tam was already eyeing it speculatively, so Lior put a hand on the boy's shoulder before he got ideas.

"Travelers," the man rumbled. "Four of you. Stew's a copper, bed's two, silence is free but only because we've got plenty."

"We'll take all three," Lior said, setting down coins.

The man's eyes flicked over Lior — over the sword, over the way Lior stood, and something in his face shifted, the way a man's face shifts when he recognizes a uniform he used to wear. "Swordspeaker," he said quietly. Not a question.

The room didn't go silent, exactly. But it got *attentive*, the way a room of old soldiers gets attentive, all the pretend-sleeping and dice-throwing pausing just enough to listen.

"Ex-swordspeaker," Lior said. "Retired. Very retired. I'm just a guide with a sword and a bad attitude about winter."

"Uh-huh." The big man leaned on the counter. "Merrick. I held the eastern wall at Cael's Ford. Third Legion. Until the third legion stopped existing, and then I held a fishing boat, and then this." He gestured at the room, the stew, the thirty ex-soldiers pretending not to listen. "We get all kinds on this road. But we don't get many of *you*."

"What do you charge for not being interesting?" Lior asked.

"Nothing. It's rare enough I'd hate to tax it." Merrick ladled out four bowls without being asked. "Sit. Eat. You look like the road's been chewing on you."

The road had, in fact, been chewing on them since Marrowgate, and the stew was hot and thick with barley and somehow also with lamb, which Merrick refused to explain and which Lior chose not to investigate. Tam fell on his bowl like it owed him money. Wren ate with the careful deliberation of a child who had learned never to trust that food would keep coming, and Lior watched her, as always, and made a note to fill her bowl again before she asked.

Sefa had the map out under the table, tracing the road ahead with one finger, frowning.

"Problem?" Lior murmured.

"The route goes through here," she said. "But then it—" She turned the map slightly. "There's a mark. My father's mark for *sworn ground*. He used it for places that only open to oath-blood." Her frown deepened. "But it's crossed out. He crossed it out and wrote something in the margin, but it's in cipher. His work cipher. I know it, I just—" She rubbed her eyes. "I'm tired. I'll have it by morning."

"Sleep on ciphers," Lior said. "They're like wolves. They come to you when you stop chasing them."

"That's not true at all."

"It's very true. I once spent a week hunting a countersign I'd forgotten, and it came to me in a dream where I was being scolded by a very large goose."

Sefa stared at them. "A goose."

"A sergeant, actually, but the dream had opinions about which."

Despite herself, despite the burned town behind them and the ash-grey riders surely somewhere behind *that*, Sefa laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd forgotten the mechanism. Lior counted it a small victory and banked it with the others.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble often did, with a drunk man being honest.

He was a leaver from the river garrisons, gray at the temples, sitting close enough to the fire that Lior could smell the spirits on him from across the room. He'd been watching them eat with the unfocused intensity of the very drunk, and finally he stood, swayed, and pointed at Lior with the gravity of a man announcing an eclipse.

"You," he said. "You're one of the oath-sworn. One of the *real* ones. Before it all went bad."

"Retired," Lior said again.

"Retired!" The man laughed, and it wasn't a kind laugh. "You know who else was sworn? You know who else stood in the white cloaks and spoke the words and had the light come up out of the ground?" His voice cracked across the room. "*Him.* The Warden-General of the Compact. That's who. Veyran Ash. He was one of you."

The room went very still.

Lior kept their hands around the bowl. Kept their voice level. "You're drunk, Corvus," Merrick said, low. "Sit down."

"I'm drunk, but I'm not *lying*." The man — Corvus — jabbed his finger at Lior again. "I served under him. Before. When he was just Veyran, oath-sworn like the rest, blade-light off his sword like morning off a lake. I *saw* it. And then the kingdom fell, and he didn't fall with it. He *climbed*." He spat into the fire. "They say he still speaks. But the light comes up black now. Black as his banners. You tell me, swordspeaker. You tell me how a man's oath goes *black*."

Lior set down the bowl.

There was a sound in Lior's ears like a door closing very far away. Because Lior knew the name. Of course Lior knew the name. Veyran Ash had been two years ahead in the training halls at Cael's Ford, the one who could hold a ward-speech longer than anyone, the one the masters pointed to when they wanted to show what discipline could build. Lior had been eleven years old and had watched him demonstrate the First Vow and had thought, with the whole burning certainty of a child: *I want to be that.*

"You knew him," Corvus said, reading Lior's face the way old soldiers read everything. "You knew him."

"I knew of him," Lior said. "Everyone in the order knew of him."

"That's not what your face says."

"My face says I'm tired and the stew was good."

Merrick came around the counter and steered Corvus back to his seat with the gentle, immovable authority of a man who had done it a thousand times. "Leave it," he said. But his eyes, when they met Lior's, were not unkind and not comfortable. "Come outside a moment, guide. If you would."

* * *

The cold outside was the clean kind, the kind that hurt and then stopped mattering. Snow had stopped falling; the stars over the pass were hard and close. Merrick stood with his arms crossed, breath steaming, and for a while said nothing at all, which Lior respected.

"You knew him," Merrick said at last. "The General."

"Veyran Ash." The name came out of Lior like a splinter that had been in too long. "Yes. I trained under the same masters. He was — " Lior searched for the word and hated every candidate. "He was the best of us. That's not a figure of speech. He was genuinely, measurably the best. The oaths loved him. When he spoke a ward, the light would stand up straight like it was proud to be there."

"And now?"

"Now they say his light burns black, and he's burned nine towns to prove it."

Merrick nodded slowly. "Here's what I know, from travelers and leavers and the occasional Compact deserter we don't ask too many questions of. The Compact didn't beat the old order, swordspeaker. It *bought* it. Half the Warden-General's inner circle are ex-sworn. Oath-breakers, or worse — oath-*sellers*. Men who figured out that a vow is just power wearing a promise, and power can be pointed anywhere you like." He looked at Lior steadily. "The Compact didn't conquer Vael. It inherited it. Like a son inheriting a house and burning the furniture."

Lior said nothing. The stars said nothing. Somewhere down the valley, wind moved through the pass with a sound like a long exhalation.

"I renounced it," Lior said finally. "The oaths. The order. All of it. I told the magic it had the wrong person."

"And how'd that go?"

"It went fine for eleven years." Lior's breath fogged. "Lately it's been less fine."

"Because the General's out there doing what you could do," Merrick said. It wasn't cruel. It was just true, the way Merrick's statements all seemed to be, the way a wall is true. "Every town he burns, every oath he twists black, some part of the world is looking at the both of you and asking what the difference is. And you know what the difference is."

"Neutrality," Lior said.

"I was going to say *choice*. But sure. Neutrality." Merrick shrugged, a mountain range moving. "I deserted the Third Legion because they ordered me to burn a granary with the farmers locked in it. I've thought about that order every day since. And I'll tell you what I decided, out here, charging travelers for soup. The uniform was never the point. The vow was never the point. The *point* was the morning after, when you look at your hands and decide what they're for." He turned back toward the door. "Your hands look like they're deciding something. Come in when they're done. Stew's on all night."

He went inside. Lior stayed out a while longer, looking north up the pass, where the Ember Road climbed toward whatever Sefa's father had ciphered into the margin of his map.

*Veyran Ash.* Lior tried to fit the boy from the training halls — the straight-backed, serious boy whose ward-light stood up proud — into the shape of the Warden-General with his black banners and his ash-grey riders, and the two shapes would not meet. That was the horror of it, Lior thought. Not that a bad man had worn the white cloak. That the best of them had. That the oath had never once asked Veyran Ash what he wanted to *be* with it — and he had never once asked himself.

Corruption hadn't conquered the old order. It had simply applied for the position, with excellent references.

Lior's hand drifted to the sword hilt, and beneath the leather and steel, faint as a heartbeat under snow, something answered. Not a demand. Not yet. Just a recognition — the way one dog knows another down a long street.

*I know you,* the swordspeech seemed to say. *I know him, too. What will you do about it?*

"Ask me in the morning," Lior muttered, and went inside.

* * *

The common room had settled. Tam and Wren were asleep in a heap by the fire with Merrick's enormous spare cloak over them both, and Sefa sat at the corner table with the map spread out before her and a candle guttering, her lips moving through the cipher, her pencil scratching.

Lior sat down across from her. She didn't look up.

"Goose," she said. "You said the countersign came to you in a dream with a goose."

"Sergeant."

"You said goose first."

"I said what I said."

"Well." She turned the map around and pushed it toward Lior. In the margin, in her father's small, precise hand, decoded at last, were six words. Lior read them twice, and the cold from outside followed Lior in and sat down between them.

*The Door is sworn. Ask what it swears to.*

"That's not a route note," Lior said slowly.

"No," Sefa said. "That's a warning. He knew what the Door was. He knew what it would cost." She looked up, and in the candlelight her face was pale and set, the mapmaker's daughter all the way down. "And there's more. Under it. Smaller." She turned the map back, angled it to the light.

Three more words, pressed so hard into the vellum the pen had nearly torn through:

*He's still alive.*

Lior looked at her. "Who's still alive? Your father?"

"No." Sefa's voice was very quiet. "The cipher key. It's keyed to names, Lior. I checked it three times. It's not *my* father he's writing about." She swallowed. "It's the name in the seal on the back of the map. The one I couldn't read."

She slid the map fully around, and there, embossed in the broken wax, was the sigil Lior had been carrying against their chest for two hundred miles without truly seeing it: a sword crossed over a rising flame.

The mark of the order. The mark of the white cloaks.

The mark of a swordspeaker who had signed a map to the Last Door and then vanished — and whom every survivor of Cael's Ford had mourned, and whom the Compact's wanted-notices had never once named.

Lior's mouth went dry. "That seal," Lior said. "Whose seal is it?"

Sefa looked at them with something like pity.

"You tell me," she said. "You're the one who went to the funeral."
