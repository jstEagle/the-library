## 9. Sefa's Bargain

The trouble, Sefa Idrin would think later, began with her own handwriting.

She'd been copying the map. That was the whole of it. Three nights camped in the shadow of a ruined signal tower, she had sat by the fire with her father's original spread across her knees and a sheet of good vellum on a board, reproducing every line, every annotation, every little marginal note in his cramped, slanting hand. *The fords freeze unevenly. Trust the stone that hums. Do not trust the stone that does not.*

It was, she'd told herself, simple prudence. If the map was lost or stolen or soaked in a river, the journey died with it. A copy was insurance. Lior had grunted something noncommittal when she'd announced the plan, which Sefa had chosen to interpret as approval, because Lior's noncommittal grunts covered a remarkable range of opinions and she'd learned not to interrogate them too closely.

She had not considered — not once, not for a single moment — that a girl hunched over a map by firelight, copying it in her dead father's hand, might look exactly like a girl preparing to sell it.

Which was why, when the three riders in ash-grey came down out of the trees at dusk with crossbows levelled, her first thought was not *I am going to die* but *oh, no, this looks so bad.*

"Sefa!" Lior's voice, from the far side of the camp, sharp as a drawn wire. "Don't."

Don't what? Don't move? Don't speak? Don't be standing there holding a forged Compact-baiting treasure map like an offering? She froze with the vellum still in her hands, which in retrospect was the worst possible choice, because the lead rider swung down from his horse, crossed the firelight, and plucked it from her fingers with the easy confidence of a man collecting a debt.

"Well," he said, turning it toward the light. "Well, well."

* * *

They took her to their camp half a mile down the slope, and this was the part Sefa would remember with the most shame: they didn't even tie her hands.

They didn't need to. There were three of them and one of her, Lior was somewhere behind her — she'd heard no shout, no fight, which either meant Lior was circling for rescue or had made the coldly sensible decision that one life was not worth three crossbows — and the girl they'd left bound and gagged by the fire, Tam, was nine years old and crying through her nose in small, terrible snuffles that Sefa could still hear in her memory for the rest of her life.

The lead rider — a lieutenant by the piping on his collar, young, with the particular smoothness of a man who had never once been cold — sat across from her on a fallen log and unrolled her copy with the reverence of a priest handling relics.

"Cartographer's work," he said. "Idrin's hand. The Compact has wanted this man for two years." He looked up. "You're the daughter. Sefa Idrin. Reported traveling north with a swordspeaker."

"I'm a cook," Sefa said.

"You're holding his map."

"I'm holding *a* map. I draw maps. It's what I do. I draw them for anyone. I drew one last month for a man who wanted to find a hot spring, and I'll be honest with you, lieutenant, I made that one up entirely, and he found it anyway, which I think says something profound about—"

"Where is the original?"

And there it was. The question, sitting in the firelight between them like a stone she'd have to pick up with her bare hands.

Here is what Sefa knew, in the space of about four heartbeats, with a crossbowman watching her left side and another watching her right and a child crying behind her: she knew where the original was. Folded into the lining of her coat, against her ribs, where it had lived since Marrowgate burned. She knew that if she gave it up, the Ember Road died, the Last Door died, her father's last two years of work and his final, terrible walk into the high country died — all of it folded neatly into a lieutenant's saddlebag and carried away to be burned, because that was what the Compact did with roads and doors and countries.

And she knew — with a clarity that felt like falling — that they were going to kill her anyway. Not because she'd lied about the cook thing, which had been weak, but because she'd seen the lieutenant's face when he said *the swordspeaker.* He wasn't hunting a map. He was hunting a person, and the map was bait, and bait did not get released back into the wild.

So she did the only thing her father had ever taught her to do well.

"Kill me," she said, "and you'll never find it."

The lieutenant's smile flickered. Just at the edge. "That sounds like a confession."

"That sounds like arithmetic. You think my father taught me nothing? I've had this map since I was twelve. I know it the way you know your own teeth. The original is hidden somewhere you will never find in a hundred years, and the only person alive who can walk you to it is me." She let that sit. Then, because her father had always said the best lies are mostly true: "And I want things. I want my father's name cleared. I want passage out of this country. I want it in writing, with seals, because I've seen what Compact promises look like when they're not written down."

The lieutenant studied her for a long moment. Behind him, the crying by the fire had stopped, and Sefa found she could breathe again, a little.

"The original," he said. "Prove you can produce it."

"I don't carry it. Obviously. I carry a copy — the one you're holding, congratulations on your prize — so that if I'm taken, I lose nothing but ink." She nodded at the vellum in his hands. "That's real, by the way. Every line. Compare it to Compact records when you can. My father's work. You'll want to be careful with it; the edges are annotated with water sources."

The lieutenant looked down at the map. And Sefa watched him do the arithmetic she had counted on: a copy in the hand was worth a promise in the bush. He would report that he had recovered Idrin's map. He would report that the daughter was cooperating. And when his superiors discovered the copy lacked whatever secret made the original worth two years of hunting — and they would discover it, because Sefa had left exactly one thing off, the one thing that mattered, the little sigil at the road's end that her father had drawn in ink mixed with his own blood, the true cartography, the part that answered only to sworn blood — well. By then she intended to be a very long way north.

"Three days," the lieutenant said. "You'll take us to the original. If you're telling the truth, you live. If you're not—"

"If I'm not, you'll kill me, and I'll have died having wasted my last hour talking to a man whose coat doesn't fit him, so we'll both have lost something." She stood, slowly, hands visible. "Now. The child and the old man you left by the fire. They walk free, or there is no bargain, and you can explain to your Warden-General why you traded a cartographer for two corpses."

* * *

They did not walk free, quite. The lieutenant kept Tam and old Marta — Lior had doubled back for them, Sefa learned later, arriving at the campsite to find it empty and following the tracks with a face like a closing door — as "assurance of good conduct." But he let Sefa send word. A note, in her own hand, delivered by one of the crossbowmen under a white rag on a stick:

*They have me. They have the copy. I told them the original is hidden and I'm the only one who can find it. I have three days. Don't do anything brave. — S.*

She watched from the lieutenant's camp as the messenger picked his way up the slope, and she watched the moment the note was taken — a shape at the treeline, a hand, a pause that lasted three heartbeats and felt like three years. Then the shape withdrew, and she was marched back to the fire, and she did not sleep at all.

* * *

In the morning, she led them in a wide, beautiful circle.

This was not treachery, she told herself. This was *negotiation.* The original was hidden, wasn't it? Hidden in the lining of her coat, which was on her body, and she was walking, wasn't she? Technically, she was leading them directly to it. The fact that she was leading them to it by a route that added six miles, crossed the same stream twice, and included a forty-minute pause to examine a rock formation she described as "a possible landmark, I need to be sure" — well. Cartography was a science of precision, and precision took time.

The lieutenant grew less patient by the hour. By midday he had stopped dismounting to look at her landmarks. By early afternoon he had started saying things like "daughter of Idrin" in a tone that had lost all its reverence.

It was late afternoon, with the light going long and amber through the pines, that the world ended.

It ended quietly. One moment they were walking a game trail between two granite shoulders; the next, the crossbowman behind Sefa made a small surprised sound and sat down in the bracken with a feathered shaft in his shoulder, and Lior Whitlock stepped out of the shadows looking like winter itself had learned to hold a grudge.

What happened next took perhaps twenty seconds, and Sefa missed most of it, because she had thrown herself flat the instant she heard the first body hit the ground. When she looked up, one scout was unconscious in a heap, one was running — Lior didn't even chase him, just watched him go with something that might have been contempt — and the lieutenant was on his knees with a sword of pale blue light resting at the join of his jaw and neck, humming faintly, like a held breath.

"Please," the lieutenant said.

"You had a child tied up by your fire all night," Lior said. Their voice was very quiet, which Sefa had learned was worse than shouting. "Nine years old."

"She wasn't harmed—"

"No. She wasn't. Because you didn't need to harm her. That's the Compact way, isn't it? Everyone behaves, and no one gets hurt, and the country disappears one polite day at a time." The sword-light didn't waver. "Get up. Walk south. Don't stop until your boots forget the smell of these mountains."

The lieutenant got up. He walked. He did not look back, which Sefa thought showed a certain wisdom.

Then Lior turned around, and Sefa's stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because their face was not angry.

It was worse. It was *tired.*

* * *

They didn't speak until camp, hours later, in a hollow under a leaning pine with the horses — their two poor borrowed horses, plus the lieutenant's, which Lior had taken with the flat comment that "he can walk south faster without it" — picketed and dozing. Tam was asleep between Marta and the fire, finally, mercifully, done crying. Marta was pretending very hard to be asleep herself, in the way of old women who wanted very much to hear everything.

Sefa sat across the fire and waited for it.

She had rehearsed the confession forty times on the march. She had versions. There was the noble version, in which she explained she'd traded the copy to protect the group. There was the tactical version, in which she laid out exactly what the Compact now possessed and what they'd deduce from it. There was even, in her lowest moments, a version in which she got her apology in first, fast and loud, so that Lior's anger would arrive already blunted.

But when Lior finally looked up from the fire and said, simply, "Tell me," every version fell out of her head at once.

"I copied the map," she said. "Three nights ago. I didn't ask. I should have asked. I was going to tell you, I was — I was waiting for the right moment, which is a lie, there is no right moment, I was waiting until you couldn't be angry at me anymore, which is never." Her voice cracked. She kept going, because stopping felt like dying. "And the scouts saw me doing it, and they took me, and they had Tam, and they were going to kill me, and I told them the original was hidden and only I could find it. I gave them the copy to buy my life. I gave them my father's work. And I led them in a circle for a day and a half, and it worked, it *worked*, and I would do it again, and I hate that I'd do it again, and now you're looking at me like that and I can't—"

"Breathe," Lior said.

"I *am* breathing, this is what breathing looks like when you've ruined everything!"

"Sefa." Lior leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight moved on their face. "The copy. What did you leave off it?"

Sefa blinked. "What?"

"The blood-ink sigil. At the road's end. The part that only answers to sworn blood. Did you leave it off the copy?"

"I—" She had. Of course she had. She'd spent three nights copying her father's hand down to the ink-stains, and the one thing she had not copied, could not copy, was the sigil that required blood she did not have and a magic she did not believe in. "Yes. I left it off."

"Then you didn't give them the map." Lior sat back. "You gave them a very beautiful piece of paper with a road on it that ends in nothing. They'll spend a month wondering why it's worthless. They'll interrogate their own cartographers. They'll suspect each other." Something moved at the corner of Lior's mouth — not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "You didn't betray us, Sefa. You handed the Compact a forgery so good they'll trust it more than the truth. That's not a bargain. That's a *gift.*"

Sefa stared at them. "I lied to armed men. I lied to *you.* I led you on a chase across half a mountain because I was too much of a coward to tell you I'd made a copy, and then I got captured, and Tam cried all night because of me—"

"Tam cried all night because armed men tied up a child, which is not a thing you did." Lior's voice was steady. "And you lied to me, yes. And I have spent the last day and a half tracking you across a mountain in the dark, planning — I want to be honest with you, since we're being honest — planning to kill all three of them and burn their camp. That was the plan. That was the whole plan. No rescue, no negotiation. Three bodies and a fire."

Sefa's mouth went dry. "You'd have killed the lieutenant."

"I'd have killed all of them, and I'd have slept well." Lior fed a stick to the fire. "You want to know the difference between us? You lied to save your life and hated yourself for it. I was prepared to kill three men and the only thing I hated was how easy it would have been." They looked up, and their eyes in the firelight were old, so much older than the rest of their face. "You'd have done worse, you said. Sefa. I *have* done worse. I did it for years, quietly, for money, while the country that owed me everything forgot my name. The only thing you did wrong tonight was copy a map without asking, and apologize too much."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope, a night bird said something rude.

"So you're not angry," Sefa said carefully.

"Oh, I'm furious." Lior said it so flatly that Sefa almost laughed. "You're going to ask before you copy anything ever again. You're going to tell me when you're frightened instead of improvising alone against men with crossbows. And you're never, ever going to trade yourself for anything again, because I have known you for three weeks and I have already spent more worry on you than I spent on the last ten years of my life combined, and I resent it *deeply.*"

And there it was — the thing under the words, warm and unmistakable, and Sefa felt her eyes sting and had to look at the fire for a while.

"Three weeks," she managed. "You've known me three weeks and you're already lecturing like my father."

"Someone has to. He's not here."

"No," Sefa said quietly. "He's not." She pulled her knees up, and after a moment, so did Lior, and they sat like that, two people shaped the same by the fire, while Marta snored with tremendous artifice ten feet away.

"Next time," Lior said, "when you make a bargain — make it with me first."

"Next time I'll make it *with* you. That's what a bargain is. I'm told I'm good at them."

"You're good at lying to lieutenants. We'll work on the rest."

Sefa smiled into the fire. It felt strange on her face, after the day it had had. But it stayed.

* * *

She woke once in the grey hour before dawn, and Lior was sitting up, awake, watching the north.

Sefa followed their gaze. Far off, past the black teeth of the ridgeline, the sky over the high country had gone the wrong color — not the grey of coming snow but a bruised, yellowish green, like light through sick glass, and the wind that reached them carried a smell she'd never smelled before and knew instantly: old ice, and something beneath the ice, waking.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Lior was silent a long moment. When they answered, their voice had the sound in it that Sefa had learned to dread — the sound of someone reading a road they'd hoped never to walk.

"That's not weather," they said. "That's *made* weather. Someone's raising a storm over the passes." They reached over and nudged Marta's boot. "Get up, both of you. We need to find shelter before the sky comes down — and I know exactly one village mad enough to take us."
