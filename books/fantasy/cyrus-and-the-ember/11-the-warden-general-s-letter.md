## 11. The Warden-General's Letter

The blizzard broke on the third morning, and with it came the parley.

Lior knew before anyone told them. The swordspeech had been restless all night—not the hungry pull it had become since Marrowgate, but something subtler, a prickle along the old scar tissue of their vows, like a dog lifting its head at a sound too far off for human ears. They'd lain awake in the villagers' guest loft listening to the wind and thinking: *someone is coming who speaks my language.*

They were right. Of course they were. That was the whole trouble with swordspeech; it was never wrong about its own kind.

"Rider on the road," called Tam Vetch from below, in the tone of a man announcing weather rather than danger, which was very Tam Vetch. "One rider. Grey coat. No sword drawn."

"A parley banner?" Sefa asked, appearing at the loft ladder with her hair still full of straw from the mattress she'd complained about for two days.

"No banner," Lior said. "He won't need one. Nobody shoots a messenger in the high country. Not even us." They pulled their boots on. "Especially not us. We're famous for our hospitality. It's how we lure people into buying salt."

Sefa gave them the look—that flat, unamended look she'd perfected somewhere between her father's grave and the frozen ford—and said nothing, which was how she said most things.

* * *

The rider was young. That was the first thing Lior noticed, and it sat wrong in their chest like a swallowed stone. Ash-grey uniform, Compact-cut, but new wool, barely creased, the insignia at his collar still sharp-edged. A boy doing a man's errand, or a man dressed up as a boy so that no one would feel right about killing him. The Compact was good at that sort of thing. It had learned everything worth learning from the kingdoms it replaced.

He dismounted at the edge of the village green, where the snow lay trodden to grey ice by children who'd spent yesterday discovering that snowballs could be manufactured and deployed. He stood very straight beside his horse and waited, breath steaming, hands visible, the way couriers were trained to stand when carrying something that might get them shot.

Lior walked out alone. They'd insisted on that part. Sefa had argued—of course she'd argued—and lost, which she took with the grace of a cat being carried away from a birdcage.

"You're Whitlock," the rider said. His voice cracked once on the name, and he pretended it hadn't.

"I'm nobody's anything this week. Who sent you?"

"Warden-General Corvess." He drew a letter from inside his coat—a heavy envelope, sealed in black wax stamped with the Compact's sigil, the ash-tree with its roots cut off. Lior had always found that emblem honest, at least. An organization that admitted what it did to the past, right there on the letterhead.

Lior didn't take it. "Read it to me."

The boy blinked. "It's addressed—"

"It's addressed to a dead order. Read it aloud or ride back down the mountain and tell him the last swordspeaker is illiterate. He'll believe it. Everyone believes whatever's convenient about us."

Something flickered across the young man's face—fear, yes, but under it, briefly, a spark of something almost like relief. As if he'd hoped someone would give him permission to say these words out loud instead of just delivering them like a parcel of knives.

He broke the seal and read.

"'To the bearer, Lior Whitlock, sometime Oath-Sworn of the Ninth Company, greetings.'" His voice steadied as he went, finding its rhythm. "'You do not know me, though I have known your name for eleven years. I was at the mustering at Cael Ford when the oaths were released. I watched you refuse your discharge and walk north alone. I have often wondered if you knew then what you were walking away from.'"

"He's wrong there," Lior said mildly. "I knew exactly. That was the fun part."

The courier hesitated, glanced at the letter as if hoping it would tell him whether he was allowed to smile. It didn't. He read on.

"'I will not waste your time with threats. You have seen what we do to those who obstruct us, and you have drawn your own conclusions, some of them even accurate. Instead I offer you the truth, because you above all people will know what to do with it.

"'The Last Door exists. Your map leads to it. What lies beyond it, you cannot yet imagine—but I can, because I have read the same archives you were trained to guard, and I know what the Ember is. You may have guessed already. If you have not, I envy you, and I am sorry for what the knowing will cost you.

"'Here is what I ask, and here is why. The Door opens only to a sworn oath. Any oath. That is its nature and its flaw—it cannot distinguish between a vow of protection and a vow of conquest. It has waited three hundred years for someone to come to it with enough power and enough hunger to make the distinction irrelevant. When that happens—and it will happen, Whitlock, if not through me then through my successors, or yours—the Ember will go to whoever stands closest. And the person who holds the Ember holds every oath ever sworn on Vael's soil. Every hearth-vow. Every marriage vow. Every soldier's promise and every mother's. All of it, in one hand, bendable to one will.'

"'I do not want that power. I want it *closed.* Sealed, buried, made unreachable, as it should have been left. But sealing it requires an oath-sworn of sufficient strength, and there are so few of us left, and fewer every season, and I confess I no longer trust myself to stand before that Door and want nothing. Do you trust yourself? Truly? Because I have read your record, and I will tell you honestly: neither do I.'

"'So: surrender the map. Come south, freely, and I will give you what the old kingdom never did—a pardon, a rank, a place where your oath means law again instead of memory. In exchange you help me close the Door forever. Thousands live in the valleys below the glacier, Whitlock. Villages, towns, whole generations who have done nothing but be born in the wrong country. If the Door opens wrong—if it opens for the ambitious—they burn. Or worse than burn. You of all people know what a bound will does to a free mind.'

"'This is not a trick. I have no tricks left worth spending. I have only arithmetic, and arithmetic says the risk of leaving the Door findable is greater than the cost of closing it. Come and argue with my sums if you like. I would genuinely welcome the argument. It has been eleven years since anyone argued with me about anything.

"'Under the ash-tree, Corvess. Warden-General of the Compact. Formerly, and I say it with more grief than pride: Oath-Sworn of the Second Company.'"

* * *

The silence after lasted long enough for the horse to stamp twice and blow steam through its nose.

Lior looked at the letter in the boy's hands—at the neat Compact script, the careful phrasing, the places where a man had clearly gone back over his own words and softened them, sharpened them, tried again. It read like a letter written late at night by someone who kept standing up and pacing and sitting back down. It read like a letter written by someone telling the truth badly, or lying extremely well, or both.

That was the problem. That was the entire problem, folded up small and sealed in black wax.

"Thank you," Lior said, and meant it, which surprised them. "You read well. Did they teach you that, or did you come by it honestly?"

"Honestly," the boy said. Then, before his courage could reconsider: "He made me memorize it. In case you wouldn't let me read it. He said—" the boy's throat worked—"he said, 'Tell him I remember Cael Ford. Tell him I was the one who held the gate.' I don't know what that means."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. Eleven years ago fell on them like snow off a roof. Cael Ford. The last mustering. Nine hundred oath-sworn releasing their vows in a single hour, the light going out of nine hundred blades at once, and Lior walking north through the mud while behind them the kingdom finished dying with a sound like a long exhale. Someone had held the gate. Someone had stood in the cold and kept the door open for everyone else to leave, and Lior had never learned their face, because you didn't look back at Cael Ford. Looking back was how you stayed.

"Tell him," Lior said quietly, "the gate-keeper did his duty. And that I've thought about that day every winter since. He'll know whether I mean it kindly."

The boy nodded, mounted, and paused. "There's an answer expected. Within six days. He said—" and now the boy did flush, embarrassed on his general's behalf— "he said, 'Tell him I know better than to set terms. But the snow won't wait for either of us.'"

"He's right about that," Lior said. "Ride safe. There's wolves past the second marker. Say something rude about kings and they'll leave you alone; they're old court wolves, they still hold grudges."

And then the rider was gone down the white road, and Lior stood alone on the village green holding nothing at all, which was somehow heavier than holding the letter.

* * *

They read it properly that evening, by lamplight, in the loft. Sefa sat across from them with her arms crossed and her father's journal open on her knee, not reading it, just resting a hand on it the way you'd rest a hand on a shoulder.

"Well?" she said finally.

"Well what?"

"Well, are we going to pretend you haven't already read it four times? You've got the creases memorized. You keep touching the fold where he wrote 'thousands' like it might bite you."

Lior looked at the fire. The villagers had given them the good room, the one with the hearth that drew properly, and someone—Tam Vetch, almost certainly, who performed kindnesses the way other men performed card tricks, quickly and while pretending not to—had left a plate of honey-bread on the sill. The bread sat there judging Lior. Everything in this house judged them gently.

"The offer isn't a lie," Lior said. "That's what I keep coming back to. I've read enough Compact documents to smell the rot in them. This one doesn't stink. He wants the Door closed. He means it. Every word of it is true."

"Every word can be true and the whole thing still be a trap," Sefa said. "My father taught cartography. He used to say a map is true in every detail and still lead you off a cliff if it leaves out the river."

"And what river is this map missing?"

"Us." She leaned forward. "Think it through, Lior. He offers you pardon and rank. Rank means command. Command means oaths—you'd be swearing again, into *his* structure, under *his* seal. You'd walk into the Compact wearing their collar to close a door, and the moment that door closed, you'd be the last swordspeaker inside the machine that erased the rest. He doesn't have to lie to you. He just has to wait. People change after eleven years of rank and warm rooms. Ask him."

Lior turned the letter over. The black wax seal lay broken on the table, the ash-tree with its severed roots staring up at the rafters.

"That's not the part that keeps me up," Lior said. "The part that keeps me up is the arithmetic. He's right, Sefa. That's the filthiest thing about it. The Door opens to any oath. We know that now—we read your father's journal, we know what it costs, and we know it doesn't check references. Sooner or later someone comes to that glacier who wants what's behind it. Maybe not this year. Maybe not in my lifetime. But the Ember doesn't rot, and ambition certainly doesn't. Closing it saves thousands. Opening it risks everything. Those are the sums. I've run them myself, lying awake, and gotten the same answer he did."

"So what's stopping you?"

Lior was quiet a while.

"When I was twelve," they said, "my oath-mistress told me the difference between a wall and a door. She said walls are built by people who've decided the question. Doors are kept by people who haven't. A keeper has to stay awake. A keeper has to keep choosing. And it's the hardest work in the world, and everyone hates it, and half the time the keeper gets blamed for what comes through." They tapped the letter. "Corvess wants to build a wall. And I understand him. Gods, I understand him—I've wanted to build that wall every single day since Marrowgate. Since the ford. Since I carried those children out of the smoke and thought, *if I had real power, real law, none of this would have happened.*

"But the Ember isn't a weapon. It's every promise anyone ever kept. Every hearth-oath these people whisper in secret. Every song. If we seal it away in the dark because we're afraid of it, we're saying promises are too dangerous to leave in human hands—which means the Compact wins without firing another shot. It wins the argument. It gets the world it's been building: one where nothing is owed to anybody, because owing is outlawed."

Sefa studied them. "And if you open it?"

"If *anyone* opens it, it goes to whoever's standing closest. That's the trap in the floor of the trap." Lior laughed, short and without much humor. "He's offering me a choice between two doors and calling it mercy. Close it and betray every promise ever kept, or open it and bet everything on the quality of whoever arrives first."

"Or," Sefa said, "there's a third thing, and you haven't thought of it yet, because you're tired and the bread's gone stale."

Lior looked at her. "You have a third thing."

"I have a father's route notes and a bad feeling," Sefa said. "Which in my experience is the raw material of all third things." She closed the journal. "But first—six days, he said. What do we send back?"

Lior looked at the fire for a long time. Somewhere downstairs a child laughed, one of the ones they'd carried out of the burning, and the sound went through Lior like a splinter finding its old channel.

"We don't send anything," they said at last. "We go ourselves. Both answers are traps, so we bring the argument in person. He asked for one." They stood, knees cracking, thirty-eight years old with seventy years of mountains in them. "Get your pack ready, cartographer. We leave at first light, ahead of the storm he's so politely mentioned."

Sefa frowned. "How do you know there's a storm coming? The sky's clear."

Lior's scarred hand drifted, unconsciously, to the hilt of the blade at their side—the old blade, the oath-light sleeping in it like banked coals. It had been humming since morning. Low. Wrong.

"Because my sword's been singing dirges all day," Lior said, "and it only does that for one kind of weather."

Outside, far to the south, beyond the dark ridgelines, a glow was rising against the clouds that had nothing to do with sunrise.

Smoke. More than one village's worth.

Lior was moving before they'd decided to move, shouting for Tam Vetch, for boots, for horses—and beneath the shout, cold and clear, the thought arrived like a blade laid flat against the throat:

*He didn't send the letter to bargain. He sent it to hold me here while the example was made.*
