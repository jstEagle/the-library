## 12. The Village That Refused

The storm told them before anyone did.

Lior had spent enough winters in the Thornfells to know that snow had a grammar. Ordinary storms grumbled. Ordinary storms complained about the mountains the way travelers complained about inns—loudly, but without meaning anything. This one was different. This one came down the valley like a verdict.

They were a half-day out from Hearthvale when the wind changed, and Sefa stopped dead in the snow and said, "That's not weather."

"I know," Lior said.

The two orphans—Piet, nine years old and mostly elbows, and little Annet, who had not spoken more than ten words since Marrowgate burned—walked between them on the packed trail. Piet had made a game of counting the boundary stones they passed. He'd gotten to eleven before the wind took his count away.

"Twelve," he shouted anyway, into the gale, because he was nine and nine-year-olds do not surrender to anything, including arithmetic.

"That's the spirit," Sefa called back, hunched into her father's old coat with the map wrapped against her chest like a second heart. "Shout at it. Storms hate that."

It was a thin joke, and everyone knew it was a thin joke, and everyone laughed a little anyway, because that was how the four of them had survived the last week: by agreeing out loud that things were fine while quietly checking each other's hands for frostbite.

Hearthvale sat in a bowl of the mountains where the pass narrowed, a scatter of stone roofs and smoke that had taken them in three nights ago without a single question. Old Marta, the village headwoman, had fed them barley soup thick enough to stand a spoon in and asked only, "You'll be gone before the Compact comes looking?" And when Lior said yes, she'd nodded as if that were a fair price and gone back to her knitting.

They should have been gone already. The blizzard had kept them. Now Lior stood on the shoulder of the trail with the wind screaming past and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in cold, that the blizzard had not kept them at all. The Compact's weather-rites had penned them here like sheep, and somewhere behind that wall of white, soldiers were marching on a village of forty souls because forty souls had fed four fugitives soup.

"We have to go back," Sefa shouted over the wind.

"They'll have scouts on the road."

"Then we go around the road." She was already moving, climbing the slope, and there was something in the set of her shoulders that Lior recognized from their first meeting in Marrowgate—the cartographer's daughter refusing to accept that a place was unmappable. "There has to be a way down through the rocks. Your magic knows these mountains better than any of us."

Lior's hand went to the hilt of the broken sword they carried now, its stump of steel still faintly warm. Swordspeech wasn't magic you used. It was magic that used you—it turned spoken vows into light and edge, and it always, always asked for more than you meant to give. Lior had spent years starving it on small vows. *I will see this caravan safe. I will walk this pass tonight.* Small promises, small light.

Lior drew breath, tasted the storm, and spoke into the wind.

"I swear by the hearth-oaths of Vael: show me the path to those who sheltered me, though the sky itself stand against me."

The oath-light bloomed up the broken blade like dawn poured through a crack, blue-gold and furious, and the mountain answered. Snowflakes hung suspended for a heartbeat—and in that heartbeat Lior saw it: a seam in the rock face, a goat-track dropping steeply along the cliff, the old paths of Vael lighting themselves like veins of fire under the skin of the world.

"There," Lior said, and hated how much hope was in their own voice.

* * *

The track down was murder. It was also, Piet declared somewhere near the top, "the best thing that has ever happened to me," which was either genuine nine-year-old joy or terror wearing joy's coat. Annet clung to Lior's back, small arms locked around their neck, her chin hooked over their shoulder. She hadn't spoken since Marrowgate, but she pressed her forehead once, briefly, against the back of Lior's skull, and Lior understood it as fluently as any speech.

Sefa led with the ember-lit map held open against the wind. In true cartography, drawn in sworn blood, the roads glowed faintly gold where they still existed and went dark where they didn't. She navigated by the light of a dead kingdom, cursing steadily under her breath in a tone that would have made her father proud.

"Left!" she bellowed. "No, your other left!"

"You said left!"

"I said left meaning my left! We're facing opposite directions, Whitlock, keep up!"

They came around the last shoulder of the cliff as full dark fell, and saw the valley below.

Hearthvale was burning.

Even muffled by the storm, even at this distance, the sound reached them—the roar of flame fighting wet snow and winning, the thin thread of screaming underneath, the deep bell-note of something large falling. The Compact had come with fire-rites of their own, and the snow that should have saved the village hissed into steam instead. Against the orange glare, figures moved in ash-grey livery, unhurried, methodical. Making an example. Taking their time about it.

Annet's arms tightened around Lior's neck until the world narrowed to that grip.

"No," Sefa whispered. "No, no—we're too late, we're too late—"

"Nobody is too late while anyone is alive." Lior heard their own voice come out flat and strange, stripped of everything soft. Below, a Compact officer was herding a knot of villagers toward the well square. Counting them. Sorting them. Somewhere in that knot might be old Marta with her knitting needles still tucked in her sleeve. "Get the children behind the rocks. Both of you stay down. If I don't come back—"

"Don't finish that sentence," Sefa said.

"—then take the map north and don't look back."

"I said don't finish it."

But she was already pulling Piet behind the boulder, already wrapping her arms around him, and Piet was fighting her hold, shouting, "You can't leave Annet—you can't—" and Annet herself slid off Lior's back and stood in the snow looking up at them with eyes that had seen Marrowgate burn and refused, absolutely refused, to see it again.

She opened her mouth. For the first time since the fire, she spoke.

"Come back," she said.

Lior knelt so they were level. Up close, the oath-light still bleeding from the broken sword lit both their faces, hers small and smudged with soot, theirs no doubt looking exactly as frightened as they felt. Lior had made a great many vows in their life—to a king, to a caravan, to a country that had forgotten them. None had ever been harder than this smallest one.

"I swear it," Lior said, and the words came out in swordspeech whether they willed it or not, glowing faintly in the air between them, *I swear I will come back*, and the child watched the vow hang there like a firefly, and put out one finger, and touched it.

Then Lior stood, turned down the slope, and ran.

* * *

Later, Lior would not remember arriving. Memory gave them only pieces after that, the way a storm gives you lightning—one flash at a time, each one printed permanent.

A flash: the well square, heat like a physical blow, and the villagers in a line with their heads down while an officer with a voice like dry paper read charges from a scroll. *Harboring enemies of order. Aiding the movement of proscribed documents. Refusal, upon inquiry, to disclose.* Forty people charged with soup and silence.

A flash: old Marta lifting her head when the officer demanded who had sheltered the strangers, and saying, in a voice that carried clear across the burning square, "We all did. Write that down properly or don't write it at all."

A flash: Lior stepping out of the smoke with the broken sword bare and blazing, and every ash-grey helmet turning at once, and the officer's voice faltering mid-word because even the Compact's new men had grown up on stories of what a swordspeaker looked like when the oath finally woke all the way.

"Disperse the line," Lior said. "Now."

The officer recovered beautifully. They always did; cruelty is a kind of confidence. "The swordspeaker," they said. "The Warden-General will be pleased. Take them alive if you can. Dead is acceptable."

Twelve soldiers moved forward through the snow, and Lior learned, in the next ninety seconds, exactly how much grief weighs when you set it down and pick up a blade instead.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about swordspeech at full strength: it is not like fighting. It is like being fought *for*. Every vow Lior had ever spoken honestly rose up in the broken steel at once—the salt caravans guided safe through twenty winters, the smugglers' children carried across ice, the small mercies done quietly and never named. The blade drank all of it and wanted more, and Lior fed it the only thing left.

"I swear," Lior said, walking forward into twelve spears, "that these people will see morning."

The oath detonated.

Light went out from the broken sword in a ring, and where it passed, spears of compacted oath-force—hard light, harder than steel—rose from the snow and barred the soldiers' advance like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. Two men went down clutching faces scorched by the flare. The rest broke, because men break; they are trained to die for abstractions but oath-light is not abstract, it is every promise anyone ever kept to you made visible, and it is very hard to stab through that.

The officer alone stood their ground, and Lior would remember that too—a young face above the grey gorget, wide-eyed, saying, almost wonderingly, "It's real. The stories are real."

"The stories are real," Lior agreed, "and you are standing in one. Run."

The officer ran. Lior let them. There would be a reckoning with the Compact entire, soon, and it would not be a skirmish at a well—but this night, this hour, Lior had room in their hands for exactly one purpose, and it was not killing.

"Marta!" They turned to the line of villagers. "How many children?"

Marta's face was streaked with soot and blood and fury. "Eleven. In the root cellar under the longhouse—the soldiers haven't found it yet, but the roof's alight, and Tam's hip is ruined, he can't climb the ladder—"

"Show me."

* * *

The longhouse was a torch. Heat rolled off it in waves that made the falling snow hiss and vanish a body-length overhead. The cellar door was in the floor beneath the worst of it, and Tam, a grandfather of sixty with a shattered hip, lay beside it with an iron poker in his hands, holding the position of a man defending a gate.

"Took you long enough," he wheezed, when Lior dropped through the smoke beside him. "Heard you were coming back for us."

"You heard wrong. Open the door."

Below, eleven children looked up out of the dimness, ranging from Piet-and-Annet-sized down to a toddler blinking sleepily in a bundle of blankets. Smoke was threading down through the ceiling boards in lazy grey ropes. Not minutes. Heartbeats.

Lior stood at the mouth of the cellar and did the mathematics every oath-bound soul eventually must: eleven children, one broken sword, a storm outside, and a body that could carry perhaps two at a time through fire. The blade hummed against their palm, greedy and eager, whispering the old arithmetic of power—*swear bigger, burn brighter, spend yourself, we can do anything if you'd only stop rationing your soul*—

"No," Lior told it, out loud, and the children looked up at the madwoman arguing with a sword.

Instead Lior swore small. Eleven times.

"I swear Annet goes out whole." Light. The girl rose weightless on a ribbon of blue-gold and drifted up through the smoke-hole like a lantern returning to the sky, into Sefa's waiting arms beyond the square.

"I swear Piet goes out whole." Light. His voice going up through the flames, delighted, terrified, yelling "AGAIN, DO ME AGAIN—"

One by one. Eleven oaths, eleven small lights rising through the smoke, each one costing Lior something real—warmth, strength, years, maybe; swordspeech never sent an itemized bill. By the eighth, their knees were in the snow. By the tenth, the world had gone narrow and ringing. By the eleventh—a fat toddler who grabbed fistfuls of Lior's hair as she floated past and shrieked with what could only be described as *joy*—Lior was on their hands and knees in a burning yard, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the sheer stupid beautiful fact of it.

Tam dragged them upright with surprising strength. "Up," the old man grunted. "Vow-keepers don't get to lie down. Bad example to the children."

* * *

They went up the goat-track in the dark, forty-four souls strung along a seam of oath-light that Lior renewed step by step, each vow smaller than the last, hoarser. Sefa carried the toddler. Marta carried the count of her people aloud, names and numbers, a litany against panic. Piet walked beside Lior and narrated the mountain's features with the authority of someone who had lived there nearly a week.

"That's a cliff. That's another cliff. That one's got a dead tree. Probably haunted."

"Piet," Sefa said, "if it were haunted, it would be the least haunted thing we've met this month."

Behind and below, Hearthvale burned itself out against the snow, and no one looked back except Lior, once, at the ridge-top. The Compact's soldiers were picking through the ashes. Let them find soup pots and knitting and nothing else. Let them report that the village had refused, and that refusal had walked away uphill carrying its children on threads of light.

The example the Compact meant to make would become a different story by morning. Stories traveled faster than soldiers. That was the Compact's oldest fear and Lior's oldest trade.

Annet found Lior's hand in the dark and held it. She did not speak again. She didn't need to.

* * *

Dawn found them in a hollow below the tree-line, a fold of stone where the wind couldn't reach, and Lior finally allowed the exhaustion to arrive all at once, like a debt collector. They sat down hard against the rock and discovered their hands were shaking and did not try to stop them.

Sefa crouched in front of them. Her face was filthy, her hair was full of ash, and she was trying very hard to look angry and failing completely.

"You broke your rule," she said.

"I know."

"Ten years of neutrality. Ten years of 'keep your head down, Whitlock, carry the salt, mind your own grave.' You told me yourself the smart move was to never be worth anyone's notice."

"I know what I told you."

"And now the Warden-General has your name and your face and a dozen witnesses who saw the last swordspeaker in Vael burn a hole in the sky over a village of farmers." Sefa's voice cracked in the middle, and what came out of the crack wasn't anger at all. "So why does it feel like the first honest day you've spent since I met you?"

Lior looked at their shaking hands. At the broken sword across their knees, its light banked now to a low ember-glow, patient, satisfied. At Annet asleep against Marta's side with one fist still closed around a scrap of fading blue-gold light she'd caught like a firefly and refused to release. At Piet teaching the toddler to make snowballs with terrible technique.

"For years I thought the oath died with the king," Lior said slowly. "That I was just carrying the corpse around on my hip. But it turns out the oath was never his. It was mine. I just kept swearing it to things that didn't deserve it." They looked up. "No more neutrality, Sefa. No more bargains. Whatever's at the end of your father's map—I'm going to see it, and I'm going to decide for myself what Vael gets to be. And the Compact can send whatever they like down that road to stop me."

Somewhere above them, far up the pass, a horn sounded. Once. Long. Then again—from the east. Then, fainter, from the west. Three horns, converging, and under them a vibration in the ground that might have been thunder and might have been boots.

Sefa went pale. "They're boxing the pass."

"They think they are." Lior gripped the broken sword and stood, legs wobbling, resolve settling like load-bearing stone. "But there's a road through these mountains the Compact's maps don't have. A road my oath remembers, and theirs never will." They looked north, to where the peaks split around a black seam in the rock that the old songs called the Hollow Steps. "The Ninth Legion's dead still stand watch down there. And it's time somebody paid them a visit."

The horns sounded again, closer, and Lior smiled with no warmth in it at all, and led the living down into the mountain where the dead kept faith.
