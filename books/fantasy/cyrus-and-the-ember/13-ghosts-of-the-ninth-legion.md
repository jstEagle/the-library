## 13. Ghosts of the Ninth Legion

The Hollow Steps announced themselves long before they appeared.

Sefa felt it first as a wrongness in her teeth—a faint hum, like biting foil, that deepened as they climbed the switchbacks above the frozen river. Then the map began to do what it always did near the old roads: the ember-ink stirred beneath its waxed surface, glowing faintly through her father's careful linework, tracing a path where no path showed.

"It's waking up," she said, for perhaps the hundredth time on this journey, and for perhaps the hundredth time Lior grunted in reply.

Lior grunted the way other people wrote letters—economically, but with meaning if you knew how to read them. This particular grunt meant *yes, I know, stop telling me.* Sefa had become fluent in Lior's grunts over the past weeks. It was, she suspected, a skill that would serve her nowhere else in life.

Behind them, the children had stopped complaining about the cold, which worried her more than complaining would have. Tam walked with his arms wrapped around himself, jaw set in that stubborn way of his, while little Wren rode on Lior's pack like a barnacle, half-asleep, her mittened hands knotted in the straps.

"How much farther?" Tam asked, in the tone of a boy who had learned that the answer was always "farther" and asked anyway out of principle.

"The map says the entrance is past the split crag," Sefa said. "My father drew it with a little door symbol. And a note."

"What does the note say?"

Sefa looked at it again. Her father's handwriting, cramped and slanting, the letters of a man writing fast by bad light: *Do not wake them politely.*

"'Nothing helpful,'" she lied.

Lior glanced back at her with one eyebrow raised—the wordless equivalent of *liar*—but said nothing. That was another thing Sefa had learned: Lior noticed everything and commented on almost none of it. Living with them was like traveling with a very tired owl.

The split crag rose out of the snowfield an hour later, two towers of black stone leaning together like conspirators, and between them, exactly where the ember-trail ended, a stairway descended into the mountain. Not carved into it—descended *into* it, each step cut from rock so dark it seemed to drink the daylight. The steps were wide enough for six soldiers abreast and worn down the middle by centuries of boots.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Even Wren woke up and stared.

"Well," said Tam finally, "that's ominous."

"That's Vael," said Lior quietly. "Everything worth anything looked ominous from the outside."

They made camp at the mouth rather than descend in the failing light. Lior built the fire small and shielded, fed everyone salt-pork and the last of the barley, and then did the thing they did every night now: sat apart with the sword across their knees and went silent in a way that wasn't quite sleep.

Sefa watched them from across the flames. She'd been watching for weeks, cataloguing. The way Lior's lips moved sometimes, shaping words too soft to hear. The way their left hand trembled after they used the swordspeech, just the fingers, just for a minute. The way they never, ever spoke an oath casually—not even "I swear" when Tam dropped the stew, which he did nightly, and which deserved it.

She thought she understood the burden. She'd written three pages in her journal about understanding the burden.

She was wrong. She would learn that tomorrow.

* * *

The Hollow Steps swallowed them at dawn.

For the first hundred paces there was nothing but darkness and the echo of their own breathing, Lior's oath-light spilling from the blade's edge in a pale gold wash. The stairs went down and down, curving gently, and the hum in Sefa's teeth grew until she could feel it in her sternum, in the roots of her hair, like standing inside a struck bell.

Then the walls began to speak.

Not words—at first. Carvings. As the light touched them, ranks of figures emerged from the stone: soldiers, thousands of them, marching in relief up the walls and across the ceiling, spears and banners and faces rendered with such precision that Sefa could see the fear on some of them, the resolve on others, the terrible youth on nearly all.

"Oh," breathed Wren, pressing herself against Lior's leg. "Oh, look at them all."

"Ninth Legion," Lior said. Their voice had gone strange—flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "Last legion ever sworn. Three thousand swordspeakers who bound themselves to the kingdom's defense, body and breath and bone. When Vael fell, they were stationed here, holding the Hollow Steps against the northern incursion." A pause. "They never got the order to stand down."

"They're dead, though," Tam said. "Right? They're dead and this is just carvings. Right?"

Lior didn't answer, which was itself an answer, and Sefa's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

The stairs ended.

The cavern opened around them like the inside of a held breath—a vault of stone vast enough to swallow a cathedral whole, lit not by any flame but by veins of ember-gold running through the rock, pulsing slowly, like the heartbeat of something enormous sleeping under the mountain. Ranks of pillars marched away into the gloom.

And between the pillars stood the Ninth Legion.

Three thousand soldiers in armor gone green-black with age, standing at attention in perfect silence, spears upright, faces hidden behind helms of the same dark stone as the stairs. They did not breathe. They did not shift. Snowmelt had dripped onto some of them over the centuries and frozen into long glassy beards of ice, and still they stood, because they had been ordered to stand, and their oaths had never been released.

Sefa had seen wonders on this road. She had seen boundary stones humming with old magic and a tollhouse staffed by deserters from five armies who shared soup like brothers. She had thought she was getting used to wonder.

This was not wonder. This was grief given architecture.

Wren made a small sound and hid her face. Tam grabbed Sefa's hand without asking, gripping hard enough to hurt, and she didn't let go.

"They've been here," Sefa whispered, "the whole time? All these years?"

"Every year of them." Lior stepped forward, alone, into the aisle between the ranks. "Waiting for relief that never came. Waiting for someone to come and tell them they could rest." They stopped in the center of the cavern, in a circle of worn flagstones older than everything else, and Sefa saw their shoulders rise and fall once, twice—the breathing of someone gathering courage, which was somehow more frightening than all the standing dead.

"Lior?" she said. "What are you doing?"

"What I came here to do, eventually. What the map needed me for." They turned their head, and in the ember-light Sefa saw that their face was wet. "You have to understand. I learned the countersign when I was eight years old. My mother taught me. Every swordspeaker's child learned it, in case we ever came this way—in case the legion ever needed to hear a true voice again. I swore I'd never use it. I swore off all of it, the whole inheritance, the whole bloody beautiful machine of oaths and duty that ate my mother alive and spat out what you see before you." A ragged laugh. "And now here I am. Because the only alternative is walking past them. Leaving them standing here another hundred years, because *I* wanted a quiet life."

Tam, remarkably, said nothing. Even Wren peeked out from behind Sefa's coat.

Lior drew the sword.

The blade caught the ember-veins and blazed—and the cavern answered. Every vein of gold pulsed brighter. Every carved soldier on the walls seemed to lean forward. And among the standing ranks, three thousand helms turned, slowly, with the grinding patience of mountains, until three thousand empty visages faced the center of the room.

Sefa's heart stopped, started, stopped again.

Lior lifted the sword in salute—the old salute, blade vertical, left hand open at the heart—and spoke.

The countersign was not in any language Sefa knew. It was older than language, maybe; it sounded like the shape of promises, like the sound a vow makes in the moment before words. Lior's voice carried impossibly through the vault, layered over itself, one voice becoming many, and with each phrase the ember-veins flared higher and the standing dead shuddered—shuddered, all together, like trees in wind.

And then, from three thousand throats that contained no breath, came the reply.

It was not loud. It was worse than loud. It was a whisper, a single whispered word repeated in perfect unison, and Sefa felt it in her bones and behind her eyes: the legion answering its countersign after decades of silence, confirming itself, presenting itself, *still here, still sworn, still waiting for orders.*

Lior lowered the sword. Their voice, when it came, was very small in the enormous dark.

"Ninth Legion. Your watch is done. Your oath is fulfilled. You kept faith when there was nothing left to keep faith *to*—" their voice cracked, steadied— "and Vael owes you more than it ever paid anyone. Go home. Rest. You are relieved. You are *relieved*, do you hear me? By order of the last swordspeaker who remembers your names."

Silence.

Then the nearest soldier moved. One gauntleted hand rose—the motion slow as a glacier, graceful as a dancer—and pressed against the breastplate, over the heart. The old salute. And down the ranks it spread, hand after hand after hand, three thousand gestures rippling away into the dark like a wave crossing a dark sea, and the whisper came again, changed now, softer:

*Acknowledged.*

They began to fall.

Not collapse—*settle*. Each soldier sank down where they stood, armor folding, stone going soft as ash, and where each one had stood there remained a drift of grey dust and, glinting in it, a small point of ember-light that hung in the air for a heartbeat before rising, drifting upward, joining the veins in the ceiling until the whole vault blazed like the inside of a lantern.

It took a long time. Nobody moved during it. Wren cried quietly, and Tam cried less quietly, and Sefa found she couldn't cry at all, could only stand with her hand over her mouth watching a nation's oldest debt get paid in dust and light.

When it was done, Lior stood alone in the center of the empty circle, sword-point resting on the flagstones, both hands shaking now, shoulders heaving.

Sefa crossed the distance and put her arm around them, because there was nothing else in the world to do.

"You knew them," she said. "Somehow. You knew—"

"My mother served with their descendants' tradition. Trained in their forms. Swore on their pattern." Lior wiped their face with the heel of their hand, rough and unashamed. "I grew up on stories of the Ninth. Every swordspeaker child did. They were the best of us. And the best of us got left standing in a hole in the ground because the kingdom died mid-sentence." They looked around at the drifting motes of light. "That's the cost, Sefa. That's what you asked me once, on the Ember Road—you asked what the burden costs. It isn't the magic. The magic is easy. It's this. It's being the one who *remembers*, when remembering is the worst job in the world. Everyone else gets to forget. Somebody has to walk into the dark and say *you can rest now*, and mean it, and carry it out with them."

Sefa nodded, because her throat was closed.

From the drifts of ash, the points of light were sinking now, settling into the flagstones, and where they settled the stones began to glow softly gold—a path, leading onward, deeper into the mountain. Toward the Door.

"They're showing us the way," Tam said hoarsely.

"No," Lior said. "They're *escorting* us. There's a difference. It matters to soldiers." And despite everything, despite the tears still drying on their face, Lior smiled—small and crooked and real. "Come on, then. We've been given honors. Least we can do is march properly."

* * *

They followed the golden path for hours, down stairs and along galleries where the carvings continued—here the legion's founding, there its battles, and in one long hall, heartbreakingly, its homecoming: carved soldiers embracing carved families, carved bread on carved tables. Sefa walked it with her journal out, sketching by oath-light, because someone had to remember this too, and she was beginning to understand that remembering was not a burden you inherited.

It was a burden you chose. And choosing it was the only thing that made it bearable.

They camped in a side gallery where the ember-veins gave warmth enough, and Wren fell asleep mid-sentence for the first time in days, and Tam actually managed to keep the stew off the ground. Small victories. Sefa was learning to count those too.

Lior sat apart, as always, cleaning the blade. But tonight, when Sefa brought them tea, they didn't wave her off. They scooted over on the stone bench—an actual invitation—and she sat.

"I heard you," Lior said, after a while. "In the vault. You weren't crying. You were memorizing."

"Is that all right?"

"It's the whole point." They turned the blade so the light ran along it. "Oaths die when nobody writes them down. Kingdoms too. My mother told me that. I didn't believe her until today."

Sefa wrapped her hands around the tin cup. "My father believed it. That's why he made the map. Why he—" She stopped. The grief for him was a door she kept almost opening. "I'm going to find out what happened to him. At the Door, or past it, or in whatever grave the mountains gave him. I have to."

"I know," Lior said. "I've known since Marrowgate."

"And you let me come anyway. Into all this." She gestured at the glowing gallery, the sleeping children, the whole impossible road. "Knowing what it costs. What you just—what it *cost* you back there."

Lior was quiet for a moment. "Costs are cheaper shared," they said finally. "That's the other thing my mother told me. Also didn't believe it until recently."

Sefa smiled into her tea, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the ember-light pulsed slow and patient around them like a heartbeat that had waited thirty years and could wait a little longer.

It was Tam, on the last watch, who saw it.

He shook Sefa awake with a hand clamped over her mouth—which nearly earned him a broken nose—and pointed back the way they had come, down the long gallery.

Far below, at the edge of the oath-light's reach, a light was moving. Not gold. Not ember-warm.

A cold white lantern-light, bobbing with the rhythm of marching men. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, drifting up through the ancient stone: the ring of iron-shod boots on the Steps, and voices—many voices—speaking in the clipped cadence of the Ashen Compact.

"They found the entrance," Tam whispered. "There's dozens of them. Maybe more."

Lior was already standing, sword drawn, and the expression on their face was one Sefa had seen only once before—in the burning streets of Marrowgate.

"Then the legion bought us a head start," they said, "and we're going to use every hour of it. Wake Wren. Pack everything. We reach the Door before they reach us—or we don't reach it at all."
