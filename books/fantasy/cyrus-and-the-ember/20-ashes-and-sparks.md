## 20. Ashes and Sparks

The silence afterward was the loudest thing Lior had ever heard.

For three days the mountain had roared — the Compact's host breaking like a black wave against the glacier wall, the shattering of the Door splitting the sky from ridge to ridge, the thousand sparks of the Ember streaking north and south and east like a flock of burning birds finding their homes. And now: nothing. Snow falling in fat, unhurried flakes. The wind gone gentle, almost apologetic, as if it too were catching its breath.

Lior sat on a shelf of rock above the broken ice and tried to remember how to be a person. Their hands shook. Not from cold — though the cold was considerable, the kind that got into old wounds and made itself comfortable — but from emptiness. When the counter-oath had left them, it had taken something with it. Not the swordspeech; Lior could still feel that banked in their chest like coals under ash. Something else. Something they suspected they would only identify at three in the morning for the rest of their life.

Below, on the white slope where the Compact's army had knelt blinded and broken, the survivors were picking themselves up.

Sefa picked her way up the rocks to join them, wrapping her father's journal in oilcloth as she climbed, because she wrapped everything in oilcloth now — "habit," she'd said, "and also grief looks better in waterproofing" — and lowered herself onto the stone beside Lior with the groan of someone whose body had filed a formal complaint.

"They're not fighting," she said. "That's the strange part. I keep waiting for the second act. Armies don't just... stop."

"This one did."

"Why?"

Lior watched the sparks still drifting down from the heights, settling on the snow like pollen, like snowfall inside snowfall. Some of them were finding the soldiers. She saw one land on the shoulder of an ash-grey pikeman, who stared at it, then at his own hands, then sat down in the snow and wept without any apparent intention of stopping.

"Because you can't hold a sword when your hands remember why there used to be hands worth shaking," Lior said.

"That's terrible poetry."

"I'm exhausted. It's the best I have."

Sefa leaned her head briefly against their shoulder — the closest thing to affection she permitted herself before noon — and looked down the slope. "The Warden-General is just sitting there. In the middle of everything. Nobody's guarding him. Nobody's even looking at him."

"I noticed."

"He's been kneeling for an hour."

"I noticed that too."

"You're going down there, aren't you." It wasn't a question. Sefa had learned, over these long winter weeks, that most of what came out of Lior's mouth was unnecessary, since their face announced everything first, like a herald who couldn't keep a secret.

"I'm going down there," Lior agreed.

"Alone."

"Alone."

"With no weapon."

Lior patted the empty scabbard where their sword had been, before they'd broken it across a ridgeline rather than kill a brother-in-arms. The gesture was mostly habit; the blade had shattered two days ago and Lior kept reaching for it anyway, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

"He'd know if I brought one," they said. "And I'm tired of him seeing weapons when he looks at me."

* * *

Getting down the slope took longer than the decision. The snow had drifted deep against the rocks, and twice Lior went in to the hip, and once disappeared entirely, which drew a bark of laughter from somewhere among the scattered soldiers — the first laugh anyone had heard on this field, probably, and aimed at the person who'd just unmade an empire's army. There was a justice in that. Lior chose to enjoy it.

The field itself was strange country now. The Compact's banners lay where they'd fallen, ash-grey silk going greyer under the snow. Men and women moved among the wounded with the dazed courtesy of people after a house fire, handing each other water skins, binding each other's eyes where the Ember-light had seared them. No one gave orders. No one seemed to need any. That, more than anything, told Lior the Compact was finished. It had never been an army, exactly. It had been a set of instructions, and the instructions had burned.

The Warden-General knelt at the center of it all, in a circle of trampled snow that the soldiers unconsciously kept clear, the way water keeps clear of a stone.

He looked smaller than Lior remembered. On the ridge, two days ago, wreathed in black swordspeech, he had filled the sky. Now he was just Corvan Ashgrave — Lior let the old name surface, tasting it like an old coin — a broad man going soft at the edges, hair more salt than iron, wearing the scorched ruin of a uniform whose insignia meant nothing anymore. His hands rested open on his thighs. Empty. The stolen power was gone; Lior could see that the way you see a hearth is cold. Whatever he had drawn out of the Ember's dark twin, whatever he had fed himself for twenty years, it had left him when the Door broke, and what remained was kneeling in the snow getting quietly covered in white.

He heard Lior coming — of course he did; he'd taught them half of what they knew about listening — but he didn't turn around.

"Lior Whitlock," he said. His voice had lost its parade-ground bronze. What was left was just a voice, middle-aged and tired, with the northern burr of the border villages underneath, the accent they'd both worked so hard to drill out of themselves in the king's service. Hearing it was like hearing a door open into a room Lior had bricked shut.

"You're supposed to be dead," Lior said. "Or victorious. Those were the options on offer."

"The options changed." Corvan turned then, slow, careful, a man mindful of old injuries and older sins. He looked up at Lior from his knees and did not ask help standing, which Lior noted and respected. "You broke it. You understand that. You didn't take the Ember for yourself, or give it to them, or sell it to the highest bidder like any sane person would have. You broke it. Gave it away. To farmers."

"To everyone."

"Worse." He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn at the hinges. "Twenty years I planned for every kind of swordspeaker. The loyal one. The dead one. The ambitious one. I never planned for the stubborn one who'd rather lose a kingdom than win one."

"It wasn't losing," Lior said. "It was putting it somewhere it could be found again."

They stood there while the snow came down between them. Around the field, Lior was aware of soldiers drifting closer, not to fight — there was no fight left in anybody — but to watch, because this was the last unresolved thing in a day full of endings, and people cannot look away from an ending.

"So arrest me," Corvan said at last. "Execute me. I'll go quietly; my knees have opinions about standing anyway. You'd be justified. Half these people lost family to my inquisitors. Marrowgate alone—"

"Don't," Lior said, and something in their voice must have carried, because the nearest soldiers flinched. "I was in Marrowgate. I don't need the inventory."

"Then do it," Corvan said. "It's what the law would want. If there were a law. Which—" he gestured, a small, defeated motion that took in the banners, the mountain, the whole smoking ruin of the country they'd both served in different ways and broken in different ways, "—there isn't. Anymore. You saw to that."

"Yes," Lior said. "I did."

"And so here we are. Two oath-breakers in the snow, and nobody left with the authority to punish either of us." He laughed, short and awful. "Do you know how long I wanted exactly this? No crown. No law. Just strength and what you choose to do with it. I built an empire to arrive at this moment, and now I'm in it, and I find I don't know what it's for."

He looked up, and for the first time Lior saw what he'd actually come here to say, the thing under all the rest, the thing he'd been kneeling an hour in the snow to work up the courage to ask.

"What is an oath for, Lior?" His voice cracked on their name, on the intimacy of it, on twenty years since anyone had called him by anything warmer than Warden-General. "I swore oaths to Vael. They were supposed to make me solid. Instead I watched the kingdom rot and my oath held me to the rotting, and I decided next time I'd swear to power instead, because power doesn't die. And that oath held me too. Held me all the way down here." He spread his empty hands. "So which of us is wrong? Both? Neither? What are they *for*?"

Lior looked at him for a long moment.

And the honest answer was: they didn't know.

They thought of the oath to the dead king that had dragged them out of smuggling salt and into all of this. They thought of the Ninth Legion, standing watch in the dark long past any purpose anyone could name. They thought of the villagers keeping hearth-oaths in secret through twenty winters, and Sefa's father dying above the treeline for a map, and the children of the village that refused, carried through the storm on light that shouldn't have existed. Every one of those oaths had cost something. Some had cost everything. None of them had ever once done what Corvan clearly believed oaths should do, which is protect the one who swears them.

"They're not armor," Lior said slowly. "If that's what you were hoping. They don't keep you safe. They point you at what's worth being unsafe for."

"That's a mad way to build a life."

"Yes," Lior agreed. "It is. It's completely mad. Everyone who ever kept faith with anything has been at least a little mad, and I include myself with enthusiasm." They crouched down then, to his level, knees cracking in solidarity with his. "But here's the difference between us, Corvan, and it isn't talent, and it was never talent. When my oath pointed at something, I followed it even when it led off a cliff. When yours pointed at something, you asked whether it would make you stronger first. That's all. That's the entire war we fought. A disagreement about what to do when the oath is inconvenient."

Corvan was silent. The snow gathered on his shoulders. Somewhere behind Lior, a soldier began softly singing one of the old hearth-songs, the ones the Compact had banned, and was not stopped, and others joined, badly, in the way of people who only remember every third line.

"I have done things," Corvan said finally, very quietly, "that can't be mended. Marrowgate. The village. I could give you the reasons; they were good reasons, at the time, they always were. But you'd see through them, wouldn't you. You always did."

"Always," Lior said. "It was my single most annoying quality. Ask anyone."

A breath of laughter escaped him before he could stop it — startled, rusty, the laugh of a man discovering the machinery still worked — and Lior thought: *there*. There was the boy who'd shared a barracks with them, who'd cheated at cards and given the winnings to the stablehands, who'd sworn his first oath with tears in his eyes because he believed it so hard. Twenty years of ash on top of him, but underneath, somewhere, that boy had survived everything, including Corvan's best efforts.

"So what happens to me?" he asked.

Justice outside the law, Lior thought. That's the job now. Nobody applied for it. Nobody wants it.

Every law they'd ever known was ash. Every court, every code, every tidy mechanism for deciding what a man like Corvan Ashgrave deserved — burned with the Door, with the nation, with all of it. There was nothing requiring mercy here. Nothing forbidding vengeance either. For the first time in history, this man's fate rested entirely on the free choice of someone he had hunted, and Lior stood at that crossroads and felt its full weight, and understood at last why the Ember had scattered itself to kitchens instead of thrones. Because power held alone curdles. Because the only thing that keeps a choice honest is having no authority except your own conscience, and no excuse.

"I don't know," Lior said. "Honestly. I've spent two days thinking I'd kill you, and two days thinking I'd drag you to every village that has to live with what you did, and both of those felt like the wrong shape. So I'm going to do neither."

Corvan waited.

Lior opened their hand.

The spark had been riding in their palm since the Door broke — one stray ember of the thousand, small as a moth, warm as a kitchen. It sat there now, guttering gently, throwing gold light up between their faces.

"This is what's left of the Ember," Lior said. "One piece. It won't bind you. It won't strengthen you. It won't rewrite anything. All it does is remember — every oath ever honestly kept, every promise anyone ever made and held to when holding cost them. If you take it, you'll carry that. Forever. Every hearth-song. Every debt paid. Every kindness done when no law required it." They paused. "Including Marrowgate. It remembers Marrowgate too, Corvan. From the other side. You'll know what they knew."

"That's not mercy," Corvan whispered. "That's worse than the rope."

"It might be," Lior said. "It's also the only honest sentence I can pass, because I'm not wise enough to weigh what you owe, and I refuse to pretend otherwise. So I'm not sentencing you at all. I'm offering you the truth, in a form you can't argue with, and letting you walk around with it for the rest of your life. Take it or leave it. Leaving it means staying exactly what you are. Taking it means finding out what an oath is for — firsthand, every day, forever." They held out the spark. "Your call, Warden-General. First real choice you've had in twenty years."

For a long moment Corvan Ashgrave looked at the little flame in the outlaw's hand.

Then he reached out with both hands, the way you receive something holy, the way you received bread in the border villages during the famine years, cupped and careful — and took it.

The light sank into his palms and he made a sound like a man taking an arrow, and then, strangely, like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten he carried, and he bowed his head over his hands while the snow came down and the bad singing swelled, and Lior rose on aching legs and stepped back and gave him to his conscience, which was, they reflected, the harshest punishment available and the only one with any chance of doing anybody any good.

Sefa was waiting at the edge of the circle, arms crossed, eyebrows raised in her usual manner of someone auditing reality and finding discrepancies.

"Well?" she said, as they walked away together. "What's an oath for?"

"Ask him," Lior said, jerking a thumb over their shoulder. "He's got a lifetime and a study guide."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Lior agreed. "It's homework. Spring's coming, Sefa. There's a lot of it to go around."

Behind them, faintly, the singing found a verse everybody knew, and even the snow seemed to lean in to listen — and far to the south, along a pass they had not yet walked, a hundred small lights were already waiting in a hundred small windows, each one with questions of its own.
