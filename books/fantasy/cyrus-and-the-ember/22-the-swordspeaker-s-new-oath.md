## 22. The Swordspeaker's New Oath

The snow on Thornfell Pass had finally begun to speak.

That was how Lior thought of it, anyway—the long creak and settle of a mountain shrugging off winter, meltwater ticking somewhere beneath the crust like a clock that had kept faith through the coldest months anyone could remember. Spring came late to the high passes, and it did not arrive so much as negotiate: a patch of bare stone here, a stubborn crocus there, pushing up through snow that should have buried it twice over.

Lior stood where the caravan road bent around the shoulder of the mountain, exactly the spot where, a lifetime ago—or one winter, which amounted to the same thing—they had guided twelve sledges of contraband salt through a whiteout while wolves sang in the dark. The wolves were quieter now. Not gone; nothing in the mountains ever truly went away. But quieter, as if they too had heard what happened at the glacier and decided, in whatever way wolves decide things, to give the place some room.

"You're doing the thing," Sefa said behind them.

Lior didn't turn. "What thing."

"The standing-and-staring thing. The brooding-with-your-back-straight thing." Boots crunched closer, and then Sefa was beside them, wrapped in a coat that had been dyed three different colors by three different villages who'd each insisted theirs was the correct shade of welcome. "You've been standing there since dawn. I counted. I'm very good at counting. I had a father who drew maps for a living, you know."

"I'd heard something about that."

"He was insufferable about precision. So am I." She squinted at the pass, at the thin gold light coming down the eastern face. "Is this where it happened? Where the courier gave you the map?"

"A mile further down. He made it to the waystation before he died." Lior's breath fogged and drifted apart. "I used to think I'd burn that map. First night I had it, I sat in a shrine cave with my knife out and everything."

"What stopped you?"

Lior considered the question honestly, because Sefa deserved honest answers and because they had spent a whole winter learning to give them. "It was warm," they said. "The map. Drawn in true cartography—it answered blood and oath—and when I held it, it was warm, like a hand. Like someone saying *you're still mine*. And I realized nobody had said that to me in eleven years." A pause. "Also I'm a coward."

"You are many terrible things, Whitlock, but I've watched you walk into burning buildings and duel a Warden-General with half a sword. Cowardice isn't on the list."

"It's always on the list. It's just usually last."

Sefa laughed—that sharp bark of hers that startled marmots—and pulled something from inside her coat: a folded square of oilcloth. She held it out without ceremony, the way she did everything that mattered, as if it were an invoice.

"Your new map," she said. "Finished it last week. I wanted to wait for today."

Lior took it and unfolded it with the care owed to anything drawn by an Idrin hand.

It was the mountains—but not as any crown's cartographer had ever drawn them. No borders. No boundaries marching in imperial red across the stone. Instead, every village marked with a small flame: Hollowmere, where the widow Anselm fed travelers first and asked questions after. Greyfall, where the miller still sang the old harvest song with the king's name scratched out and her grandmother's substituted. Kestrel Ford, rebuilt, its children—who had been carried through a blizzard on oath-light by someone who claimed afterward it was nothing, anyone could have done it—running wild along the banks. A hundred small fires stitched up and down the range, connected not by roads but by faint lines of light, like embers passed hand to hand in the dark.

At the bottom corner, in her father's careful script—which she had copied so faithfully that Lior's chest hurt looking at it—was a legend:

*Here lies Vael. Population: everyone who kept the fire.*

"Sefa," Lior said, and found their voice had gone rough. "This is—"

"It's accurate," she said quickly, ears going pink. "That's all. Don't make it a moment. You know how I feel about moments."

"You love moments."

"I tolerate them under strict supervision."

* * *

They walked down together toward the gathering, because there was a gathering, because there had been gatherings all spring and this one had been planned for weeks whether Lior liked it or not. Which they did not. Which is why everyone had organized it anyway.

The orphans saw them first. Tam and Wren—the two who had come north with them from Marrowgate, who had watched a ford run red and a village burn and a Door shatter into a thousand pieces of morning—came skidding down the scree with the total disregard for self-preservation that only children and certain kinds of swordspeakers possessed.

"You're late!" Tam announced. He was nine now, or claimed to be; his actual age had been lost somewhere in the winter and he'd simply appointed himself one. "Wren bet me you wouldn't come at all. She said you'd stand on the ridge brooding until midsummer."

"I would never—" Lior began.

"You absolutely would," Wren said serenely. She was younger, smaller, and had learned from watching Sefa that the best defense was unshakeable confidence. "We won bread off it. Well. I won bread. Tam bet on you being late instead of absent, which was cowardly betting."

"Cowardly," Lior agreed gravely. "I'm surrounded by professionals."

The rest of the company was gathered at the flat shelf of stone below the bend in the road—a hundred people, maybe more, drawn up out of the valleys for the day. Villagers in their good coats. Deserters from the tollhouse, wearing mismatched uniforms they'd finally been permitted to stop apologizing for. Old Maren from Hollowmere, who had once hidden Lior from Compact patrols and now refused to let them leave her house without eating something, standing with her arms crossed as if daring the weather to misbehave. Even a handful of former Compact soldiers—not the ones who'd burned villages, never those, but the ones who had laid down ash-grey livery in the spring thaw and asked, awkwardly, if there was work for hands that knew how to build walls.

And at the edge of the crowd, apart from everyone, as he had been apart from everything since the glacier—

The Warden-General. Corren Vale, once. Once Lior's brother-in-arms, sworn on the same stones, taught the same countersigns. His stolen swordspeech had burned out of him entirely when the Ember scattered, leaving him just a man: grey at the temples, hollow-cheeked, carrying himself like someone still waiting to be told what he was for.

He nodded to Lior. Lior nodded back. It was, between the two of them, practically a sonnet.

"You're really going to do it?" Tam asked, tugging Lior's sleeve. "Swear again? After everything?"

"That's generally what one does at these events, yes."

"But oaths got your whole country destroyed."

"No," Lior said. They crouched down so they were eye level with him, which was getting harder every season; the boy grew like a weed in a hurry to be somewhere else. "Oaths didn't destroy Vael. Men who thought an oath was a thing you *took*—a possession, a lever, a chain for other people—that destroyed it. But an oath isn't a thing you take." They tapped the boy's chest, right over his heart. "It's a thing you keep. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire world."

Tam frowned at this, filing it away in whatever fierce little ledger he kept behind his eyes. "Wren says you'll cry."

"Wren," said Wren, "is a liar and a scholar."

* * *

Old Maren opened the proceedings, because Maren opened everything—if a barn needed raising, a funeral needed singing, or a Compact officer needed staring down, Maren was there first with her arms crossed—and because she had declared, in the tone of a woman announcing the sunrise, that no swordspeaker swore anything in her presence without a proper meal first.

So they ate. That was the part that undid Lior, later, when they tried to describe it to Sefa. Not the vow itself—that had been simple, almost quiet. The eating. A hundred people sitting on the stones of a smuggler's pass sharing barley bread and hard cheese and a stew Maren had been simmering since before dawn, passing bowls down the line the way the map showed embers passing light. Somebody had brought a fiddle. The fiddle player was bad. Nobody minded. Halfway through the meal the bad fiddle and a worse drum found each other and produced something that was not quite a song and not quite a rhythm but was unmistakably, irreducibly, *them*—all of them, the ragged remnant and living continuation of a country that officially no longer existed.

"They'll want you to say something," Sefa murmured, near the end of it.

"I know."

"You hate saying things."

"With a passion usually reserved for tax collectors."

"So don't say something. Do the other thing."

Lior looked at her. "Which is?"

She smiled, and for a moment she looked so much like her father—like the man whose grave they'd found above the treeline, whose journal had cost him everything and given them the truth—that Lior had to look away. "Whitlock. You're the last oath-bound swordspeaker in the world. In about ten minutes you're about to stop being that. Whatever comes next doesn't have a rulebook. Nobody's ever done it before." She shrugged. "Do the other thing."

* * *

The stones where the old kings of Vael had once bound their swordspeakers were still there—worn smooth, cracked by frost, humming faintly the way boundary stones hummed when someone who remembered them came near. Lior had knelt on them at eight years old and sworn to a crown, and the words had entered them like iron entering a forge, and they had spent twenty years trying to pretend the heat wasn't there.

Now they stood before the stones with no crown, no king, no kingdom—just a hundred ordinary people in the wind, holding empty bowls.

They drew their blade.

It was the same sword, or what remained of it—broken at the ford, broken again against the Warden-General's black-burning steel, reforged twice by a farrier in Kestrel Ford who charged nothing and accepted payment exclusively in stories. Oath-light ran along its edge even before Lior spoke, soft gold, patient, the way a dog waits by a door.

"I had a speech," Lior said, and their voice carried in the mountain air the way swordspeech always carried, touching each listener like a hand on the shoulder. "Sefa talked me out of it. She was right. Speeches are for people asking permission, and I'm not asking anyone's permission for what I swear next. Least of all yours."

Someone laughed. Maren shushed them, then laughed herself.

"I was bound once," Lior went on. "To a king. To a crown. To a country that drew lines on maps and called the lines sacred, and when the crown fell, my oath fell with it, and I told myself I was free." They shook their head slowly. "I wasn't free. I was just unemployed. An oath to power ends when power does. So I'm not swearing to power. Not to a king—there isn't one, and may the mountains keep it that way. Not to a country—countries are lines, and lines are what the Compact burned. Not even to Vael." A breath. "Because Vael was never the lines. We learned that in kitchens, in the blizzard, in every house that hid us and fed us and sang the old songs with the wrong names scratched out. Vael was a promise. And promises are made of people."

The blade brightened. The old stones began to hum louder, a sound like a held note finally allowed to resolve.

"So here it is. Freely given, freely kept, breakable by no one's death but mine, owned by no one but all of you: I swear to the people of these mountains. To the hearth-fires and the hands that tend them. To the widow who feeds travelers first. To the children of Kestrel Ford and every child after them. To the deserters and the doubters, the stubborn and the kind. While I draw breath, your fires do not go out alone. When you call, I answer—not as a servant answers a master, but as a neighbor answers a neighbor, which is the only rank I'll hold and the only one worth having. This I swear, not to a crown above me, but to a fire beside me. And when I die, let the oath pass to whoever picks it up freely, and let it be lighter in their hand than it was in mine, until someday it weighs nothing at all—because everyone keeps it, and no one has to carry it alone."

For a heartbeat, silence.

Then the sword answered.

Not the way it had ever answered before—not the cold disciplined flare of oath-magic answering a sworn superior, not the sullen ember-glow of the dead kingdom's lingering commands. It answered like dawn. Light poured up the blade and broke outward, gold and rose and white, arcing over the gathered crowd and catching in a thousand small reflections—in bowls, in eyes, in tears, in the frost-wet stones—and from those reflections it leapt again, spark to spark, the way it had leapt from the shattered Door across half the range. Every hearth within sight of the pass, every banked coal and morning fire, flared once in answer, miles apart, all at once, like a country inhaling.

And underneath the light, faint but unmistakable, came the sound the old boundary stones made when they were happy: a deep, humming chord, the mountains themselves keeping time.

"Oh," Wren said, in a very small voice. "He *is* crying."

"I am not crying," Lior said, crying. "It's oath-light. It gets in your eyes. It's documented."

* * *

Afterward—after Maren had force-fed them a second bowl, after the bad fiddle had struck up something almost triumphant, after Tam had solemnly informed them that he intended to pick up the oath someday and Lior had said *not for thirty years at minimum, we'll negotiate*—Lior climbed back up to the bend in the road one more time, alone, as the light went long and amber across the snow.

Sefa found them there, naturally. She always did.

"Regretting it yet?" she asked, leaning on the rocks beside them. "The whole 'owned by a hundred villagers' arrangement?"

"Terribly. Maren's already scheduled me to mend fences in Greyfall next week. Apparently swordspeakers are also carpenters now."

"You'll be insufferable at fence-mending. You'll give the fence a speech."

"Fences deserve speeches."

They stood together while the sun dropped toward the western peaks, painting the pass in colors no Compact cartographer would ever bother recording—colors that existed only here, for whoever happened to be looking, which was its own kind of map.

"Do you feel it?" Sefa asked quietly. "Different, I mean. Than before."

Lior considered. Before—an oath had felt like armor nailed onto them from outside, heavy and holy and suffocating, warmth that belonged to someone else. Now...

Now it felt like the map in their coat pocket. Warm. Like a hand. Like someone saying *you're still ours*.

"Yeah," they said. "Lighter."

Down in the valley, one by one, the hearth-fires of the mountains were being lit against the evening—not because anyone commanded it, not because any law required it, but because that was what people did, and always had, and always would. Small flames in small windows, stubborn as crocuses, patient as stone.

From the pass, Lior watched them kindle, and the sword at their side glowed softly in sympathy, and somewhere below, a child's voice rose in an old harvest song with all the wrong names cheerfully scratched out.

Winter had been long. But it was, at last and utterly, over.
