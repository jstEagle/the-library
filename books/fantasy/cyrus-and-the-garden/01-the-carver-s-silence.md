## 1. The Carver's Silence

The dead woman's name was Maren Ostley, and she had been dead for six days when her husband came knocking at Xanthe's door with a lump of riverbone wrapped in a flour sack.

Xanthe knew before they opened the door. They always knew. Grief had a smell, or perhaps a sound — a thin, keening frequency that lived just below the range of ordinary hearing, in the same register where the dead liked to whisper. Xanthe had spent thirty-one years learning to tell the difference between a customer at the door and a haunting, and this morning both had arrived together, which was, in Xanthe's long and careful experience, the worst combination.

"Carver Amberdale." Tam Ostley stood on the threshold with his hat in his hands, turning it the way men in Hollowmere turned things when they couldn't afford to wring their own necks. He was a miller's man, broad and flour-pale, with the particular hollowed look of someone who had spent six days being polite to a corpse. "I've brought the bone. For the grave-gift."

"I know why you've come, Master Ostley." Xanthe stepped back to let him in, and the autumn wind followed him across the threshold, carrying with it the smell of the millpond and, faintly, of something older and colder underneath. "Set it on the table. Mind the tools."

The workshop was small and warm and smelled of beeswax and bone dust, and it was the only room in Hollowmere where Xanthe felt entirely themselves, because it was the only room where the voices behaved. Carved things held what they held. Unfinished bone was quiet, waiting, patient as an unopened letter. Xanthe had arranged their life around that patience — the racks of blank blanks, the drawers of prepared stock, the little iron stove that hissed and popped and said nothing at all, which was Xanthe's favorite quality in any object.

Tam set the riverbone on the worktable and unwrapped the flour sack with the tenderness of a man undressing a child. The piece was good stock — dense, cream-colored, taken from the bed of the Mere where the old river had deposited its strange gifts for centuries. Xanthe turned it in the lamplight and felt nothing from it yet, which was right and proper. A grave-gift only woke when it took its shape, when the carver's knife found the form the dead had been waiting for.

"She liked wrens," Tam said. His voice cracked on the word and he pretended it hadn't. "She kept the feeder by the kitchen window. Winter and summer. She'd go out in the snow in her slippers to — " He stopped. He had clearly rehearsed a speech on the walk over and had now lost all of it. "Whatever you think is right. You're the carver. Everyone says you know what's right."

*Everyone says.* Xanthe had heard that phrase so many times it had worn smooth as sea glass. What everyone actually said, in the tavern and the market and the whispered conversations that stopped when Xanthe passed, was that the Amberdale carver's grave-gifts were uncannily true — that a gift carved for a fisherman always showed the fish he'd been proudest of, that a gift for a mother held her children's faces in the grain, that no one knew how Xanthe did it and no one asked, because in a border town you learned very quickly not to ask questions about the things that kept you alive.

"They say you can tell what a person loved," Tam said, "just from the bone."

"I listen to what the family tells me," Xanthe said, which was true, and also a lie, and also the only answer that had ever kept a customer's eyes from going strange and frightened. "Wrens. The millpond. Did she have a garden?"

"Beans. She was proud of her beans." Tam laughed, wet and sudden, and wiped his face with the back of his hand. "Gods, she'd be furious, being remembered for beans."

"Beans and wrens, then. And the pond." Xanthe hefted the bone. "Come back in three days. It'll be ready before the burial."

Tam thanked them twice, bowed once — an odd, formal little bow, the kind men gave Xanthe when they weren't sure whether they were dealing with a craftsman or a priest — and let himself out into the grey morning. Xanthe listened to his boots fade down the lane, and then stood for a long moment with the riverbone in their hands, feeling its weight, its cold patience, and the faintest stirring at its center, like a bird turning in an egg.

"Well," Xanthe said to the empty room. "Let's see what you were, Maren Ostley."

They clamped the bone to the bench, took up the finest of their knives — a blade of Thornfell steel, older than the town, older than the wars, older by Xanthe's guess than most of the graves in the churchyard — and made the first cut.

The first cut was always the door.

* * *

It came slowly, the way it always came: a thinning of the air between Xanthe and the bone, a softening, as though the workshop's walls had moved back and the lamplight had gone the color of water. The stove's hiss faded. The town's morning noises — carts, gulls, the millwheel's groan — receded behind a curtain of silence that was not silence at all but a held breath.

And then, faint as a voice through a closed door, Maren Ostley began to speak.

She did not speak in words at first. They never did. What came through the bone was the shape of a life: the smell of bean flowers in June, the particular cold of pond water on bare ankles, the small fierce joy of scattering seed while a wren scolded from the fencepost. Xanthe's knife followed it, finding the wren first — it was always there, near the surface, the thing the dead loved most — and the bird's body rose out of the riverbone with startling ease, wings folded, head cocked, its tail a single clean stroke of the blade.

Xanthe carved and listened and did not speak aloud, because the dead could not hear the living through the bone; the traffic went one way only, and Xanthe had long ago stopped trying to explain that to grieving families who wanted, more than anything, one word back.

Maren's life unspooled. A girl on the millpond ice. A young woman with flour on her forearms, laughing at a boy who would become Tam. The beans, year after year, a green litany. And underneath it all, growing louder as the knife went deeper, something else — a thread of wrongness, a dark note running through the music of her days like a hair caught in a hinge.

Xanthe had learned to read these undertones. Every life had them. Regrets, mostly. Small ones: a quarrel not mended, a kindness not returned, a letter never sent. Xanthe carved around them, gave them their small places in the grain, because a grave-gift that lied about a life was worse than no gift at all.

But Maren's undertone was not small.

It grew as the wren took shape — as the wings smoothed, as the eye emerged under the point of the knife — and it was not regret. It was fear. A fear with a shape to it, a fear with *direction*, and in the last hour of the carving, as the lamplight burned down and the stove needed feeding and Xanthe's hands ached in the good way that meant the work was nearly done, the fear gathered itself and became words.

The dead rarely managed words. It cost them something, Xanthe thought — it was like watching someone shout underwater, all that force and so little of it arriving. But Maren Ostley had been a stubborn woman; Xanthe could feel the stubbornness in the bone, in the set of the carved wren's shoulders, and she spent whatever it cost her, and the words came through cold and clear as pond water in January:

*They sold her soul.*

Xanthe's knife stopped.

The workshop was very quiet. The stove had burned low. Outside, the gulls were arguing over the midden heaps, and somewhere a cart with a loose axle squealed its way up the Hollowmere road, and none of it mattered at all, because the words were still hanging in the air between Xanthe and the bone, and they did not fade the way the dead's words usually faded. They *sat* there. They waited.

"They," Xanthe said softly, to the bone, to the wren, to the dead woman folded inside it. "Who is *they*, Maren? And who is *her*?"

The bone said nothing more. It never did. One message was what the dead could afford, most of them; one message, and then the long quiet. Xanthe turned the finished grave-gift in the lamplight — a wren perched on a bean-row, its wings half-lifted as if startled into flight, and worked into the base, so small you'd need to know to look, the shape of the millpond with its ice and its girl upon it. It was good work. It was true work. And it was the first grave-gift in thirty-one years that Xanthe wanted to throw into the stove.

*They sold her soul.*

Xanthe set the carving down on the bench, wiped the knife with great care, and did what Xanthe Amberdale had done with every dangerous thing that had ever come through the bone: they folded the warning up small, and they put it away.

* * *

The trick to surviving in Hollowmere — in any of the free cities, but especially in a border town like this one, close enough to Thornfell to smell the usurper's fires on the north wind — was to know exactly what you were and exactly what you were not.

Xanthe was a carver. That was the shape they showed the world: a solitary craftsperson with steady hands and fair prices, who kept to the workshop and the market and the low tavern bench in the corner where the light was bad. Xanthe was not a priest. Xanthe was not a hedge-witch, though old Sefa down the lane called them that with a wink. Xanthe was not a spy for the free cities' councils, though two of those councils had asked, in the polite way councils ask, and had been told, in the polite way Xanthe answered, that bone was bone and carving was carving and the Amberdale carver heard nothing but the sound of their own knife.

That last part was the most important lie Xanthe told, and they told it so often and so smoothly that sometimes, in the deep of night, they almost believed it themselves.

Because the truth was this: Xanthe heard the dead. Had heard them since they were nine years old and first took up a knife against a piece of gravebone in their master's workshop, and the bone had *spoken*, and their master — kind, patient, dead-these-twelve-years Aldous Vane — had gone white as his own shavings and taken the knife from Xanthe's hand and said, in a voice Xanthe had never heard from him before or since: *"Never tell anyone. Never. Not the temples, not the councils, not the ones who love you. Especially not the ones who love you."*

Xanthe had kept that rule for twenty-two years. It had kept them alive through the Thornfell wars, through the years when the usurper's priests had gone through the free cities like a scythe through barley, looking for exactly the kind of person Xanthe was, and finding them — or not finding them — because Xanthe had learned to be a stone in a stream, a shadow on a stair, a carver and nothing more.

The dead who spoke through Xanthe's carvings asked for small things, mostly. A name said aloud. A grave marked true. A message carried to a daughter in the next town over — and those Xanthe did carry, in the shapeless way of rumor, never signing their work, never letting the thread run back to their own hands. It was a narrow life. It was a careful life. It was, Xanthe told themselves most evenings, enough.

*They sold her soul.*

Xanthe fed the stove, banked it, and put the finished grave-gift in its cedar box, and the wren's carved eye caught the firelight and seemed, for one uncharitable moment, to look at Xanthe with an expression of profound disappointment.

"I don't involve myself," Xanthe told it. "That's not false modesty, that's arithmetic. I involve myself, I end up in a priest's cellar, and then who carves the grave-gifts? Who carries the names? You want me to be a hero, and heroes are dead carvers with unmarked graves."

The wren continued to be carved bone. But the words — *they sold her soul* — sat in the room like a guest who would not leave, and Xanthe, pulling on their coat for the walk home, took the words with them, folded small, tucked away, exactly where they put every dangerous thing.

It did not work. It had never really worked. But Xanthe had thirty-one years of practice pretending it did.

* * *

Xanthe's house stood at the edge of Hollowmere where the town gave up and the marsh began — a low stone cottage with a slate roof furred in moss, bought cheap because of the damp and kept because of the quiet. No neighbors close enough to hear anything. A garden gone wild with the season's end. A door with two locks, one of which Xanthe had installed themselves and told no one about.

The walk home took them along the millpond, because the lane along the water was the shortest way, and Xanthe told themselves the tightness in their chest was only the cold.

The pond lay grey and still under a sky the color of old tin. Reeds stood along the near bank with their heads gone to seed, and the mill's wheel was stopped for the evening, and the whole scene had the particular stillness of a place where something had recently been removed. Six days ago, they said, Maren Ostley had been found here — not drowned, the town said, nothing so simple; found at the water's edge with no wound and no explanation, dead as a dropped bird, and the priest had said her heart had failed and the town had believed him because the priest was the priest and the alternative was to think about it too hard.

Xanthe stopped at the pond's edge.

The dead did not speak to Xanthe in the open air. That was the rule of Xanthe's whole strange life: the dead spoke only through the carved, only through the shaped and finished thing, only through the door a carver's knife made. Open air was silent. Xanthe had walked past ten thousand places where the dead had died, and heard nothing, and been grateful for it every time.

But standing at the margin of Maren Ostley's pond, with the first stars pricking through the tin-colored sky, Xanthe heard — not a voice, not a word, nothing so generous — a *pressure*. A leaning. The particular sensation of being listened to by something that had no ears, the way one feels a stare on the back of the neck in an empty room.

"You're not here," Xanthe said quietly, to the pond, to the dark, to the stubborn dead woman who had spent her last coin on six words. "You're in the bone. I finished you. You're in the box on my bench, and tomorrow you go in the ground with Tam standing over you, and that's the end of it. That's the whole of it."

The pond said nothing. The pressure did not lift.

*They sold her soul.* Xanthe turned the words over the way they'd turned the riverbone in the lamplight, looking for the flaw in them, the misreading, the thing a tired carver at the end of a long day might have gotten wrong. *Her.* Not *mine*. Maren had not meant her own soul. Someone else's, then — someone Maren had known, or known of. And *sold* — not lost, not stolen, not wasted. Sold. A transaction. A price. A buyer.

Xanthe knew, in the place where they kept the things they refused to think about, that the usurper's priests in Thornfell had grown rich these last years, and that no one knew on what, exactly, and that the free cities' councils had stopped asking because the answers frightened them. Xanthe knew that the old gods had been silent for three hundred years and that the temples had filled the silence with their own commerce. Xanthe knew that a border town was full of smugglers' roads, and that smugglers carried more than salt and silk, and that some of the things that went north into Thornfell in locked wagons were never inventoried.

Xanthe knew all of this, and folded it away, and walked home in the dark, and locked both locks.

* * *

Sleep did not come easily, and when it came, it came thin.

Xanthe woke once in the small hours to the sound of the wind in the reeds, and lay listening to it, and told themselves it was only the wind in the reeds. They woke a second time to a knock — or what their sleeping mind insisted was a knock, three slow raps, patient as a carver's mallet — and rose with the knife they kept by the bed and found the cottage empty, the door barred, the window shutters still, the marsh outside silvered and utterly still under a rising moon.

They woke a third time and did not sleep again.

Dawn came up the color of uncarved bone. Xanthe rose, built the fire, put water on for tea, and stood at their kitchen window looking out at the marsh grass bending in the wind, and made the morning argument with themselves that they had been making, in one form or another, for twenty-two years.

*It's not your debt. You didn't sell anything. You carve what the dead give you; you don't answer for what they say. Tam Ostley gets his wren, the priest gets his fee, and Maren Ostley's six words go into the ground with her, and that is the end of it.*

The argument was airtight. It was always airtight. It had kept Xanthe alive through wars and purges and the long hungry years when a carver with Xanthe's gift could have been rich a dozen times over and dead a dozen times over too, and Xanthe had chosen poor and breathing, and had never once regretted it.

*They sold her soul.*

"Stop it," Xanthe said aloud, to the kitchen, to the wind, to the dead. "I finished your gift. I carved you true. Whatever else you wanted, I can't — "

And then they heard it.

Not from the marsh. Not from the wind. From inside — from the front room, from the shelf above the cold hearth where Xanthe kept the pieces that had never sold, the carvings too true for strangers' walls: a little bone boat, a bone hand holding an apple, a wren in flight that had never found a buyer.

The wren was singing.

It was not Maren's grave-gift — that lay boxed on the workshop bench across town. This was an older carving, years old, finished and silent for years, and it should have been silent now. Finished things went quiet. That was the rule. That was the whole architecture of Xanthe's careful life: carve, listen, deliver, and the door closes.

But from the shelf, thin and clear and unmistakable, the old bone wren was singing — and beneath the song, in a voice Xanthe now knew as well as their own, Maren Ostley was saying the only words she had left:

*They sold her soul. They sold her soul. They sold her soul.*

Xanthe stood in the grey light of their kitchen with the tea going cold in their hands, and understood, with the particular dread of a careful person watching their careful life come apart at a seam, that the dead woman was not going to stop.

And that somewhere — north, beneath the mountains of Thornfell, in whatever dark court bought what the usurper's priests were selling — something had heard her too.
