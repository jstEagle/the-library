## 2. Prayers Answered

The voice did not stop at Xanthe's door.

That was the first wrong thing. In thirty years of listening to the dead, Xanthe Amberdale had learned the architecture of silence — where it pooled, how it could be shut out with oak and iron and a good strong latch. The dead were polite in their way. They clung to what had held them: a ring of stones, a carved lintel, the grave-gift buried with their knuckles. They did not follow a living body through the streets of Hollowmere like a stray dog scenting blood.

But the old woman's voice came home with them anyway.

*They sold her soul.*

It waited while Xanthe banked the workshop fire. It sat patient as a customer while they ate cold barley porridge standing up, because sitting down meant stopping, and stopping meant listening, and listening was how you ended up involved. It was still there when Xanthe lay down on the narrow bed above the workbench, staring at the ceiling beams where they'd hung their first carvings — crude little animals, a fox with mismatched eyes — and it was still there when sleep finally took them, so that they dreamed in an old woman's rasp:

*They sold her soul. My girl's soul. Sold her down under the mountain, and no one said a word, and I am dying with it in my mouth.*

Xanthe woke before dawn with their hands aching. They looked at them in the grey light — a carver's hands, scarred across the knuckles, the left index finger shortened by a chisel slip when they were nine years old and their master still alive and laughing. Hands that had made a hundred grave-gifts this year alone. Hands that had heard every one of those hundred dead speak through the bone and wood and river-clay.

They had buried the warning once already, pressed down under work and willful deafness. But the dead woman's voice had teeth in it now, and grief, and grief was the one thing Xanthe had never learned to carve away.

*Fine,* they thought at the ceiling, at the voice, at whatever stubborn scrap of soul had decided to make a nuisance of itself. *Fine. One question. One answer. Then you go to your rest and leave me be.*

*Ask,* the voice said, and there was something almost amused in it, which was worse than the weeping.

Xanthe sat up. "Who sold her?"

A pause. Long enough that Xanthe thought the voice had gone, that the night had swallowed its own warning whole.

*Priests,* the old woman said at last. *Priests of the new temple, with their clean white collars and their scales for weighing souls like fish. Three winters past. She was sick, and they said they could save her if she gave what was hers beyond death. And she was afraid, and I let her be afraid, because I believed them too.* The voice cracked like river ice. *And then she died anyway, and they carried something away from her bedside in a little box, singing, and I have been dying ever since, slower than she did.*

Xanthe sat with that until the light came up properly over the rooftops of Hollowmere, over the smoke of the morning fires, over the shrine at the town's edge that everyone walked past and no one looked at anymore.

Then they got up, and put on their coat, and went out into the cold — not toward the temple district, not toward anyone who might be asked dangerous questions, but toward the shrine of Ashen-Who-Waits. Because if a god was going to wake up after three hundred years, Xanthe wanted to hear it happen from a safe distance, with their back against a wall.

Old habits. Old, useless habits, as it turned out.

* * *

The shrine of Ashen-Who-Waits stood in a fold of land below the eastern terraces, half-swallowed by blackthorn, its walls of dry-stacked fieldstone furred with moss the color of old verdigris. There had been a roof once, slate and timber; now there was only sky, and a floor of packed earth swept clean by someone — Xanthe had never learned who, though they suspected old Marta Tulliver, who came every dusk with a birch-twig broom and spoke to the empty air as if it answered.

It never had. Not in three centuries. That was the whole of the covenant with the forgotten gods: they had gone silent in the time of the great wars, gone quiet as drowned bells, and the free cities had learned to live without them. Prayers became habit, then courtesy, then superstition. The priests of the new temple called Ashen-Who-Waits a husk-god, a hollow name, good only for teaching children that all things fade.

Xanthe arrived mid-morning to find the shrine full.

Not crowded — Hollowmere wasn't large enough for crowds — but full in the way a room is full when everyone in it has stopped breathing. Marta Tulliver knelt at the broken altar stone, her broom laid aside, her hands folded so tightly the knuckles stood out like roots. Behind her, awkward and hungry-eyed, stood perhaps forty townsfolk: the miller with his flour-white forearms crossed; the ferryman's widow; two of the temple's own acolytes in their clean white collars, hovering at the edge with expressions caught between contempt and terror; children peeking around their mothers' skirts.

Everyone had heard the rumor by now. Everyone had come to see.

"Go home," Marta was saying, in her cracked, gentle voice. "There's nothing here but an old woman and an older stone."

"You prayed," said the miller. "Last night. Tam's boy heard you from the road. He says—" the miller's throat worked— "he says the air *answered* you."

"He's a boy. Boys hear owls and call them ghosts."

"Marta." The ferryman's widow stepped forward, and her voice was very soft. "My husband's been dead six years. If there's even a chance—"

"There's no chance," said one of the acolytes, stepping forward too, collar bright as a wound. "The Intercessor's doctrine is clear. The old gods are dead of their own unworthiness. What the boy heard was wind in the thorn. What you're doing, mistress—" he nodded at Marta, and his contempt slipped just enough to show the fear beneath— "is inviting sickness into the town. The last time folk gathered at a husk-shrine in Vell's Crossing, half of them took the sweating fever within the month."

"The last time folk gathered at a husk-shrine," Marta said, without turning around, "was before your grandmother's grandmother was born, boy, so you can keep your doctrine and your fever both."

Xanthe slid along the outer wall, found their place against the stone with their arms folded, and listened.

Not to the living. To everything else.

This was the skill no one knew about, the thing that had kept them alive through Thornfell's wars when smugglers and soldiers and priests all wanted the same roads: Xanthe could hear what the dead left behind, and more than that, they could hear the shape of a place. The shrine hummed low and constant, the way old things do — three centuries of prayers soaked into the fieldstone like tallow into a wick, none of them answered, all of them stored. Xanthe had always assumed the prayers simply sat there, going stale. Dead gods kept nothing; dead gods *were* nothing.

But this morning the hum was different. This morning the hum had a pulse.

Xanthe frowned and listened harder, letting the crowd noise fall away, letting the acolyte's droning warnings fall away, sinking down into the deep register of the stone the way a diver sinks past the sunlit water into the cold and the pressure and the truth.

Something down there was waking up. Slowly. The way a man wakes who has been buried and has decided, somewhere in the dark, that he is not finished.

*Xanthe Amberdale,* said the ground.

Xanthe's heart stopped, started again, and hammered.

No. No, that was — they'd misheard, or some dead thing in the wall had spoken, or —

*I know your hands,* said the ground, in a voice like ash settling in a cold grate. *I have watched them work for thirty years. You are the only one in this town who listens properly.*

"I don't listen," Xanthe breathed, barely moving their lips, eyes fixed on the middle distance where no one would think they were talking to anything at all. "I hear things. It's not the same. It's a curse, not a devotion."

*A distinction without a difference,* said Ashen-Who-Waits, and if a voice made of settling ash could sound dry, this one did. *Watch the woman. She has prayed to me every dusk for fifty years, and tonight I intend to answer, and when I do, this town will tear itself apart between hope and fear. You will want to run. Don't.*

"Why me?" Xanthe hissed. "Why does everyone want something from me? The dead woman last night, now you—"

*Because you are the messenger whether you consent to it or not,* the god said, with terrible mildness. *Bone remembers. Bone carries. You have spent your life making vessels for other voices, carver. It was never going to end any other way.*

And then the presence withdrew — not gone, Xanthe understood, but folding itself down, gathering, the way a man gathers himself before rising from a long sickbed — and Xanthe stood against the wall with sweat cooling at their temples and the whole day tilting sideways around them.

The miller was still arguing with the acolyte. The widow was crying quietly. Children stared at Marta Tulliver's bent back as if she were a saint, or a madwoman, or both.

Xanthe should have left. Every instinct honed across thirty years of neutrality screamed *leave, leave now, be elsewhere when the trouble starts.* They had survived the sack of Corven's Ford by being three miles away. They had survived the priest purges by carving angels for the men who ordered them. Leaving was the whole of their wisdom, the entire architecture of their life.

They stayed.

God help them — and apparently one of them was awake enough to try — they stayed.

* * *

Dusk came down violet and cold over Hollowmere, and the crowd at the shrine had grown instead of thinning. Word had traveled the way word travels in small places: silently, completely, arriving everywhere at once. Two hundred people now stood among the blackthorn and the mossy stones, breath steaming, and the acolytes had gone — fled, someone said, to fetch the temple's Intercessor himself, which meant that whatever happened next would happen in front of witnesses who mattered.

Marta Tulliver knelt at the altar as she had knelt every dusk for fifty years. Her broom lay beside her. Her hands were folded. She was praying, and Xanthe, wedged at the edge of the crowd with their hood up, could hear the words as clearly as if they'd been carved:

*Ashen-Who-Waits. I have nothing to ask for myself. I'm nearly done, and I've made my peace with done. But my granddaughter's soul was taken, sold to pay for medicines that didn't save her, and I cannot die knowing she's in a box in some priest's vault, or worse, down under the mountain in that court where they buy and sell the dead. If you can hear me — if anything of you is left to hear me — please. Please. Not for justice. Justice would be nice. Just tell me where she is. Let me know she isn't alone.*

Xanthe closed their eyes.

*So it was your girl,* they thought at the dark. *Your granddaughter. The one whose voice followed me home.*

*She followed the one person in Hollowmere who could carry the message,* said Ashen-Who-Waits, from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone and the soil and the blackthorn roots. *Now hush. This part requires precision. I have not spoken aloud in three hundred years, and I am out of practice, and there are two hundred frightened people watching.*

The temperature fell. Not gradually — all at once, the way a cellar door opens, and two hundred people felt it and went silent in a single collective intake of breath.

Frost bloomed across the broken altar stone. Across the moss. Across the packed earth in long feathering lines, spreading outward from where Marta knelt, tracing patterns no one had seen since the oldest stories: the waiting spiral, the sign of the god who sits at the door of endings and keeps account.

And then the air itself spoke.

It was not a booming voice. Xanthe had expected thunder, or at least the theatrical resonance the new temple's priests affected during their incantations. Instead it was quiet — quieter than speech, somehow louder than it — a voice like ash settling, like embers shifting in a grate hours after the fire seemed dead, and it came from everywhere: the stones, the frost, the blackthorn, the bones of the earth itself.

**"MARTA TULLIVER."**

Two hundred people made a sound like a single wounded animal. Several fell to their knees. The miller dropped his lantern, and it guttered and went out, and no one moved to relight it, because the frost-light off the altar was brighter than any flame.

**"FIFTY YEARS YOU HAVE SWEPT MY FLOOR AND SPOKEN TO MY SILENCE. I HAVE HEARD EVERY WORD. I COULD NOT ANSWER. THE WARS UNMADE US, AND WHAT WAS LEFT OF ME HAD NOT THE STRENGTH TO SPEAK. FORGIVE AN OLD GOD HIS LONG CONVALESCENCE."**

"Ashen," Marta whispered. She had not turned around. She knelt with her face wet and her hands still folded, and her voice was the voice of a woman setting down a weight carried fifty years. "You're real. You were always real."

**"AS REAL AS YOUR GRIEF, WHICH IS THE ONLY INCENSE I EVER WANTED."**

The crowd was weeping, praying, screaming, all at once. Somewhere behind Xanthe a man began to laugh hysterically and couldn't stop. The frost-spirals crawled further, touching boot-soles, and where they touched, people gasped and reported afterward — all of them, every one, in identical words — a feeling like being *acknowledged*. Like being seen by something vast and patient that bore them no malice whatsoever.

**"YOU ASKED AFTER YOUR GRANDDAUGHTER'S SOUL. I WILL ANSWER YOU TRULY, BECAUSE YOU DESERVE TRUTH AND THIS TOWN HAS BEEN FED LIES UNTIL THEY TASTE LIKE BREAD."**

The silence that fell was absolute. Even the hysterical laughter died.

**"HER SOUL WAS TAKEN BY PRIESTS OF THE NEW TEMPLE AND SOLD TO THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN, WHERE IT BURNS AS A LANTERN IN THE HALLS OF RENDERED LIGHT. SHE IS NOT ALONE. THERE ARE THOUSANDS. THEY ARE ALL AFRAID. AND THE GOD WHOSE CHILD WAS SOLD BEFORE THEM HAS COME TO COLLECT THE DEBT."**

Xanthe felt the words go through the crowd like a blade through wet canvas. *Thousands.* The widow sat down hard in the frost. The miller's lips moved without sound. Someone began reciting the Intercessor's catechism in a high cracked voice, as if the words could hold back what was coming, and the words dissolved into the frost like spit into a river.

**"THE DEBT WILL BE PAID. THE CHILD WILL BE FREED. BUT I AM OLD AND SLOW AND MADE MOSTLY OF PATIENCE, AND I CANNOT GO MYSELF. I REQUIRE HANDS."**

The frost-spirals converged. All of them, every feathering line across two hundred feet of earth, flowing together, streaming toward a single point in the crowd — toward the figure in the hooded coat pressed against the outer wall who had spent thirty years perfecting the art of being overlooked.

Toward Xanthe Amberdale.

**"XANTHE AMBERDALE."**

Every head turned. Two hundred faces, lit blue-white by the frost, found them in the space of a heartbeat, and Xanthe understood with perfect clarity what it felt like to be a rabbit between the hawk's shadow and the ground.

"No," Xanthe said aloud, to a god, in front of the entire town. "Absolutely not. Find someone else. Find someone *devout.*"

**"I DO NOT WANT DEVOTION. DEVOTION IS JUST FEAR KNEELING DOWN. I WANT SOMEONE WHO LISTENS TO THE DEAD WITHOUT FLINCHING AND LIES TO THEM NEVER."** A pause, and the ash-settling voice grew quieter, almost gentle, which was infinitely worse. **"MY CHILD-GODLING'S SOUL WAS STOLEN AND SOLD THREE GENERATIONS AGO BY THE SAME PRIESTS WHO BUY THE DEAD TODAY. I HAVE WAITED THREE HUNDRED YEARS TO SAY THAT SENTENCE OUT LOUD. YOU WILL CARRY IT WHERE IT NEEDS TO GO, CARVER. OR YOU WILL SPEND THE REST OF YOUR LIFE IN A SILENCE SO COMPLETE THAT EVEN THE DEAD CANNOT REACH YOU — AND WE BOTH KNOW, DON'T WE, WHAT THAT WOULD COST YOU."**

The threat was precise. That was the horrifying part. Not fire, not plague, not the sweating fever the acolyte had warned of — just silence. The loss of the voices. The loss of the only company Xanthe had never been able to refuse, the chorus they claimed to hate and could not imagine the world without.

Around them, the crowd was on its feet, pressing close, and the questions came from every side at once — *what did it say, what did it say to you, why you, what's happening* — and above it all rose Marta Tulliver's voice, cracked and fierce and suddenly young:

"It chose the carver. Look at it. Look at its hands. Of course it chose the carver."

Xanthe looked down at their own scarred, shortened fingers, trembling in the frost-light, and heard the god settle back into the earth like a man returning to a sickbed, satisfied, leaving behind one final whisper that no one else in Hollowmere could hear:

*A knight is coming. Disgraced, memory-broken, asking dangerous questions. He will need a guide into Thornfell, and you are the only one who knows the smuggler roads. When he asks — and he will ask tomorrow — say yes.*

*The debt begins.*

Far off to the south, past the terraces and the blackthorn and the sleeping town, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky — or something that sounded like thunder, if thunder had a voice, and names, and three hundred years of patience.

Xanthe pulled their hood lower, turned their back on two hundred staring neighbors, and walked home through the frost, listening to the dead begin, very softly, to sing.
