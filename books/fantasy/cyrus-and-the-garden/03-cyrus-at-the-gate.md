## 3. Cyrus at the Gate

The rain had followed him for nine days, and Cyrus had begun to suspect it was not weather but judgment.

It came down now in gray sheets over Hollowmere, hammering the shingled roofs, turning the town's single cobbled street into a river of muddy light. He stood at the gate with his hood drawn low and his sword wrapped in oilcloth across his back — not hidden, exactly. A knight who hid his blade was a knight with something to confess. But he had learned, in the long months since the temple cast him out, that there were places where an unwrapped sword asked questions he could not afford to answer.

Hollowmere was such a place.

He passed beneath the gate arch where two old wardens sat under an awning, playing dice on an upturned barrel. They looked at him the way border towns look at strangers: measuring his boots for the dust of far roads, his hands for the calluses of violence, his eyes for the particular hunger of men who want something.

"Business?" the elder warden asked, without looking up from his dice.

"Pilgrimage," Cyrus said.

That earned him both their attention. The younger one laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Pilgrimage. To what? There's nothing holy left between here and Thornfell but graves."

"That's what I'm told," Cyrus said. "I'm counting them."

He walked on before they could decide whether he was joking. Behind him he heard the younger warden mutter something about mad knights and bad omens, and the rain swallowed it.

* * *

He took a room at the Drowned Bell, because it was the only inn whose sign had not been riddled with arrow-slits during the last war, and because innkeepers talked. Innkeepers always talked. It was the one lesson of his former life that had proven useful in disgrace: every town had a heart, and the heart of most towns beat loudest behind a bar.

The common room was half-full and wholly silent when he entered. That silence had a texture he recognized — the particular hush of people who had something to hide and were waiting to see if he was the one sent to find it. He had stood in rooms like this before, wearing white and gold, and watched smugglers' faces drain as the temple's questions began. Now he wore no colors at all, and the silence regarded *him*.

He paid for a room, a meal, and information, in that order, sliding coins across the bar with the flat efficiency of a man who had done this in a hundred towns.

"I'm looking for a guide into Thornfell," he said quietly. "Someone who knows the passes. Someone who doesn't ask why."

The innkeeper — a broad woman with flour to her elbows and a scar through one eyebrow — wiped her hands slowly on her apron, which was how he knew she would make him wait. "Thornfell," she said. "That's usurper country now. Has been since the priests moved in. You want to die, there are cheaper ways. Rope's free by the well."

"I don't want to die. I want answers."

"They're the same thing, some days." She leaned closer, lowering her voice to match his. "There's been talk lately. Strange talk. Old Marta went to the shrine of Ashen-Who-Waits three nights past — you know the one, the forgotten god, the one nobody's prayed to since our grandmothers' grandmothers — and her prayer was *answered*. Out loud. In front of witnesses." Her eyes flicked toward the room, then back. "Half the town says it's a miracle. The other half says it's the end of the world coming up out of the ground like a corpse. And the third half—"

"There is no third half."

"There's always a third half," she said. "The third half says someone's going to come asking questions about it. And here you are."

Cyrus set another coin on the bar. "Who else has been asking?"

"A temple man came through last month. White cloak. Asked after anyone who'd heard voices, seen signs, dreamed true dreams. Took two names south with him." She paused. "Neither came back."

The cold that settled in Cyrus's chest was familiar. He had worn that cold himself once, delivering such orders. *Take them for questioning,* the preceptors said, and questioning in the temple's hands was a door that only opened outward for the dead.

"And guides?" he pressed. "Into Thornfell?"

"Every guide in Hollowmere has heard about the white cloak too." The innkeeper straightened, conversation clearly concluded. "You'll find none of them fool enough. Ask the bone-carver, if you like. They say Amberdale will go anywhere for coin. They also say Amberdale hears things. Dead things." She shrugged. "But they also say Amberdale trusts no one, so good luck with that."

* * *

He found the workshop on the second day, after the rain broke.

It sat at the edge of town where the houses gave up and the scrubland began — a low stone building with a yard full of antlers, ribs, and femurs drying on racks like strange laundry. The smell of it reached him first: dust and calcium and something faintly sweet, like old incense. Carved pieces hung in the window. Grave-gifts, he realized. Little animals, little hands, little doors. Things meant to be buried with the dead.

Through the open door he could see the carver at work, and he stopped, unaccountably, in the yard.

Xanthe Amberdale was smaller than he'd expected, and younger — or ageless, in the way of people whose faces had decided expression was a luxury. Their hands moved over a piece of carved bone with a steadiness that seemed almost liturgical. Cyrus had spent twelve years among temple artisans and had never seen hands so certain. They carved the way he had once prayed: completely.

"You've been standing there long enough to be rude," Amberdale said, without looking up. "Come in or leave. The goats don't care either way, but I do."

Inside, the workshop was dim and dense. Bones covered every surface — sorted, labeled, stacked in careful strata. Cyrus had the sudden, irrational sense of standing inside a library where every book was a person.

"I'm told you guide people into Thornfell."

"I'm told lots of things." The knife did not pause. "Most of them are lies, and the rest are exaggerations."

"My name is Cyrus. I was a knight of the Temple of the Nine—" He caught himself. "Of the temple. Before."

Amberdale's knife stopped. For the first time they looked up, and their eyes were pale gray, the color of ash, and entirely without warmth. "Before," they repeated. "And now?"

"Now I'm looking for something I lost."

"People usually mean money when they say that."

"It wasn't money."

They studied him for a long moment. Then they set down the knife, wiped their hands, and held one out — palm up, fingers spread, in a gesture Cyrus did not understand until he saw their gaze drop to his chest.

"The stone," they said. "Around your neck. Let me see it."

His hand closed over it before his mind agreed — the reflex of a man guarding a wound. The oath-stone hung against his sternum on a leather cord, as it had for years: a disc of white stone the size of a coin, cracked clean through the middle, its engraved sigils half-shattered. Every knight of the old order had carried one. His had broken the night of the massacre at Vaelmoor — the night he could not remember — and no smith, no mason, no temple artificer had ever explained how an oath-stone, cut to endure a lifetime of war, had snapped like an eggshell.

"No," he said.

"Yes," Amberdale said calmly. "You want me to walk you into a country that kills pilgrims. You want me to trust you with my life on roads I've spent fifteen years learning. And you won't show me the one thing about you that's screaming loud enough for even a deaf man to hear it." They tilted their head slightly, listening to something Cyrus could not hear. "Your stone is loud, knight. Whatever happened to you, it hasn't finished happening."

Every instinct Cyrus owned told him to walk out. Twelve years of discipline told him to lie. But lying had gotten him exiled, and truth had gotten him nothing, and he was tired of carrying a broken thing no one would look at.

He drew the cord over his head and laid the stone in Amberdale's palm.

The carver turned it over once, twice. Their thumb traced the crack, and their face — already so still — went stiller, the way water goes still before it freezes. When they spoke again, their voice had changed. Quieter. Careful. As though walking on ice over deep water.

"Where did this break?"

"I don't know. That's the problem. There was a night — a village called Vaelmoor. I woke on the road three days later with blood on my armor that wasn't mine and no memory between dusk and dawn. The temple said I did it. Forty-one dead. I said I didn't. They believed themselves." His jaw tightened. "They stripped my rank, burned my name from the rolls, and put me out the gate. The stone broke that same night. I know because I remember holding the halves and not understanding."

Amberdale was not listening to him anymore. They were listening to the stone. Their lips moved faintly, shaping words with no breath behind them, and Cyrus felt the hair rise on his arms — because whatever they heard, they heard it in a room where he heard nothing at all, and yet the silence itself seemed to lean in.

"What does it say?" he asked. "What do they say?"

Amberdale looked up. Something in their expression had shifted — not softness, exactly. Recognition. The look of a person who has found, in a stranger's pocket, a piece of their own map.

"It says you weren't lying," they said. "It says forty-one voices have been shouting the same sentence for years, and no one ever listened long enough to hear it." They closed their fist around the stone and held it out to him. "It says: *we saw who gave the order.*"

The words landed in Cyrus's chest like a dropped anvil. He took the stone back with hands that were not quite steady. "Whose order?"

"I don't know. The dead don't give names freely — they give them like coins, one at a time, and only to people they trust. And I don't trust anyone." Amberdale turned back to their carving, dismissal in every line of their body. "Which brings us to your real question. You want a guide. My answer is no. Find your own way into Thornfell. Die in your own ditches."

"I can pay."

"Everyone can pay. That's never been the problem."

"Then what is?"

Amberdale's knife resumed its work. Shavings curled away from the bone, pale as fingernails. When they finally answered, they did not look up.

"Because the moment I take you through that pass, I stop being neutral. Fifteen years I've survived every war, every purge, every priest with a list, by belonging to no one and owing no one. The dead tell me things, knight. Terrible, useful things. And I trade in them carefully, and I keep my hands clean, and I stay alive." The knife paused. "You're carrying a broken oath-stone and a story about a massacre the temple committed and blamed on you. If I help you, I'm helping someone the temple wants erased. And the temple, lately, has been asking my neighbors questions. Two names went south last month. Neither came back."

"I'm not asking you to fight for me. Just to walk."

"Everything is fighting, eventually. That's what the dead taught me." A pause. The knife stilled. "Go away, Cyrus-of-the-temple. Come back tomorrow if you must. I'll probably say no again. I always say no. It's how I'm still breathing."

* * *

He came back the next morning, and the morning after that.

On the third day, the yard gate was open and Amberdale was standing among the drying bones with their arms crossed, watching the road south as though expecting it to produce something unpleasant.

"The white cloak came back," they said, without greeting. "Last night. With four soldiers. They took the miller's son — the boy who fainted at the shrine when the god spoke. They put him in a wagon and drove south, and his mother ran after the wagon until she fell, and nobody stopped." Their voice was flat, but their knuckles were white around their own elbows. "This morning the innkeeper tells me a knight with no name has been asking after guides into Thornfell. Asking loudly. Asking everyone."

"I've been discreet—"

"You've been *present*. In a town this size, present is loud." Amberdale finally turned to face him. Their ash-gray eyes had a fever-light in them that hadn't been there before. "Here's what's going to happen. You're going to get us both killed, or arrested, or worse — and I've thought very hard about which of those I prefer, and I find I prefer none of them. So I'm going to say yes, and I'm going to hate myself for it, and you're going to swear me terms."

"Name them."

"One: we move tonight, before the white cloak's soldiers finish their wine and start asking my neighbors about me. Two: you follow my word on the road as you'd follow your god's — my road, my rules, my detours. Three." They stepped closer, and their voice dropped to something almost gentle, which was somehow more frightening than everything else they'd said. "Three: when we find whoever gave the order at Vaelmoor — and we will, because the dead won't let that stone rest until we do — you don't flinch. Whatever it costs. Whatever it *is*. You see it through."

Cyrus thought of forty-one voices shouting into a silence that had lasted years. He thought of a mother running after a wagon until she fell. He thought of his own name, burned from the rolls, and the empty space where his memory should have been.

"I swear it," he said. "By the stone and what's left of my name."

Amberdale held out their hand — the carver's hand, steady and bone-dusted — and Cyrus took it. Their grip was stronger than he expected, and colder, like clasping something that had just been kept in a cellar.

"We leave at moonset," they said, releasing him. "Bring bread, rope, and no hope. Hope weighs more than all of it, and there's nowhere to resupply."

They turned back to their racks of drying bone, and Cyrus walked out into the bright, washed morning feeling the broken stone warm against his chest — warmer than it had been in years, as though something inside it had heard the swearing of terms and approved.

Behind him, faintly, he thought he heard Amberdale speaking to the empty yard in a low voice — not to him, not to themselves.

To someone else.

And whatever they said, it sounded, for the first time since he'd known them, like fear.
