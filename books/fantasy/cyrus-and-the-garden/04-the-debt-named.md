## 4. The Debt Named

Xanthe did not sleep.

They tried. They banked the workshop fire until it was a red eye in the dark, they lay down on the narrow cot behind the curtain of dyed wool, they even performed the old ritual of pressing their palms flat against the floorboards — the way their mother had taught them, back when the only voices were the house's creaks and the wind's complaints — and waited for the night to pass over them like weather.

But the dead woman's words sat in their skull like a splinter of whalebone: *They sold her soul.*

And beneath that, quieter and far worse, the memory of what had happened at the shrine three days past. The old woman kneeling with her hands full of dried marigolds, praying to Ashen-Who-Waits for her grandson's fever to break — and the prayer *landing*. Not echoing off stone the way prayers always had for three hundred years, not falling into the silence that every priest had long since made peace with. Landing. Being *heard*. The whole town had felt it, a hush like the moment before lightning, and then the fever broke by morning, and Hollowmere had spent the days since torn between ringing bells and barring doors.

The town heard thunder. Xanthe alone had heard the word that came with it.

*A name.* Spoken in a voice like ash settling after a fire too large to bury. A name that was theirs.

So when the knocking started at midnight — not at the door, but from inside the workshop, from the shelf where the finished grave-gifts waited in their rows of linen-wrapped patience — Xanthe was already sitting up, already dressed, already resigned in the way one is resigned to a debt collector who has finally found the right address.

They lit no lamp. They didn't need one. The bone shelf was glowing.

* * *

It came from the grave-gift first — the small carved sparrow they'd finished two nights ago for the drowned ferryman's daughter, wings folded, head tucked, the kind of piece Xanthe made without thinking because grief needed somewhere to go and bone was willing to hold it. The sparrow's hollow eye sockets filled with light the color of coals under grey ash, and the light spread outward, hopping from piece to piece along the shelf: the mourning comb, the prayer beads, the little door-shaped amulet meant to be buried over a child's heart so the dead could find their way home. One by one they woke, each glowing faintly, each humming at a different pitch, until the whole workshop sounded like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Xanthe stood in the doorway with their arms crossed and said, to the room in general, "I'm not going anywhere."

The voices of the ordinary dead — the ones that lived in every carved thing, faint as breath on glass — went silent. All of them. Every relic Xanthe had ever touched, every whisper they'd carried for thirty years like stones in a sling, gone at once, and the silence was so total and so wrong that Xanthe's knees nearly buckled. It was like being set down suddenly weightless. Like having a limb amputated and forgetting, for one merciful second, that it had ever been there.

Then something else spoke through the bones instead.

It did not use their ears. That was the horror of it, and the strange mercy. The voice arrived directly behind the eyes, in the marrow, in the place where Xanthe's gift lived — as if someone had reached into the mechanism of their hearing and plucked the strings themselves.

**"You heard My name."**

"I heard *a* name," Xanthe said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Names are cheap. I carve them on things all day."

**"You are Xanthe Amberdale. Bone-carver. Listener. Neutral as a knife's edge, they say in the smuggler dens — useful to everyone, owned by no one."** The light in the sparrow's eyes deepened, reddened. **"That neutrality ends tonight."**

"See, that's where you're mistaken." Xanthe moved to the hearth and fed the fire a split log, mostly to have something to do with their hands. "I don't work for gods. I don't work for kings, priests, smuggler queens, or anyone else who thinks a title entitles them to my labor. I carve. I listen. I keep my mouth shut about what I hear. That's the whole arrangement, and it's kept me alive through three wars and two purges."

**"Your arrangement,"** said Ashen-Who-Waits, **"was possible because We were silent. Three centuries of silence, while Our priests were flayed and Our names scraped from stone and Our children scattered. You learned to survive in a world where the dead could not ask anything of you. That world is ending, carver. The old gods are waking, one by one, and We are waking *hungry*."**

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the street a dog began to bark, then thought better of it.

Xanthe turned around. "Then wake up hungry somewhere else. There are priests in Thornfell who still remember how to flay. Go bother them."

**"They sold My child."**

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Xanthe felt the splash of them all the way down.

For a moment they said nothing. Then, carefully: "Explain that."

**"Come closer. I will show you rather than tell you. Words are thin things between us; you will want the truth entire."**

Every instinct Xanthe possessed — thirty years of instincts, honed in border towns where being interesting got you killed — screamed at them to stay exactly where they were, to let the fire burn down, to wait out this night and every night after until whatever had woken in the world's foundations went back to sleep or found a louder prophet. But the silence where the dead voices should be was aching now, an absence with teeth, and Xanthe understood with cold clarity that this was the leash. Ashen had taken the voices away the way a hand closes over a caged bird, and would open again only when it chose.

They crossed the room. They picked up the sparrow.

The moment their skin met the carved bone, the vision took them.

* * *

They saw a garden.

Not any garden that existed anymore — the trees were silver-barked and taller than towers, and the fruit hanging from them glowed softly, each one holding a small warm light like a lantern with a soul inside. And moving among the trees was a child. Small, barefoot, laughing in a voice like water over pebbles, gathering fallen lights into the folds of a robe woven from smoke and morning. The godling. Ashen's child, tending the souls of the waiting dead the way a shepherd tends lambs, carrying each one gently toward — Xanthe couldn't see what. Something beyond the garden's edge. Rest, perhaps. Whatever rest had been, three hundred years ago, before the world forgot how to give it.

Then men came. Men in grey robes with their faces wrapped, carrying iron censers that smoked black, and the garden burned — not with flame but with *unmaking*, the silver trees crumbling to powder, the lights scattering like startled birds. The godling ran. The godling hid inside a dying oak and pressed itself small, and the robed men pried the tree apart, and what they took from its heart they sealed into a box of carved bone — a reliquary, fine-worked, latticed, beautiful in the way that cages are always beautiful —

— and Xanthe knew the carving style before the vision showed them the maker's mark. Knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The way you know your teacher's.

Their master's chisel had cut those lattice lines. Their master's thumb had worn that corner smooth.

The vision shifted. Grey-robed men crossing mountains, descending into the dark beneath Thornfell's roots, passing through gates of salt and iron into a court where lanterns burned with a light that made Xanthe's stomach turn even inside the dream — because they recognized that light, had seen it once years ago in a smuggler's strongbox, had assumed it was some alchemist's trick. Rendered souls. The court beneath the mountain paid for everything in souls, and the priests of the usurper had brought it currency.

The reliquary changed hands. Coins of pale metal changed hands. Somewhere in the dark, something vast and patient counted its new treasure.

And Ashen-Who-Waits — Xanthe saw it too, in the last flicker of the vision — Ashen knelt in the ashes of Its garden, a figure of grey and embers, and did not weep, because gods of waiting do not weep. It simply began, in the silence of three centuries, to gather itself. To wait more precisely. To put out feelers through every carved bone and forgotten shrine in the world, searching for one thing: someone who could hear, who could go where gods could not, who owed the dead enough to pay them back.

Someone like a bone-carver in Hollowmere who had spent thirty years listening and never once answering.

The vision released them. Xanthe came back to themselves standing in the middle of their workshop, the sparrow still clutched in their fist hard enough to leave marks, sweat running cold down their spine.

"No," they said.

**"You have not yet heard the price."**

"I don't need to. The answer's no. Find a priest. Find a knight. Find some fool who wants a quest — the free cities are full of them lately, all these broken temple men wandering around looking for meaning—"

**"The court beneath the mountain does not admit priests or knights. It admits debtors, smugglers, and those who know the roads. You are all three."** The voice softened, which was worse. **"And carver — understand what I hold. Your gift. The hearing. Every voice you have ever carried, every dead soul that has whispered its last words through your hands. I can return them to you, or I can keep them. Forever."**

"You'd make me deaf to them." Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "You'd cut out the only thing I am."

**"I would take back what was always Mine, lent to your line as a kindness. But hear Me — I do not wish to. I have watched you, listener. Thirty years you have carried the dead gently. You gave the ferryman's daughter a sparrow so her father would not grieve alone. You told old Marta where her husband's bones lay buried in the fen, though it cost you a beating from the men who'd hidden them. You are neutral because you care too much, not too little — I have known ten thousand souls and I know the difference."** A pause, like ash sifting. **"Retrieve My child's soul from the court beneath the mountain. Bring it to any shrine of Mine, anywhere in the world, and set it free. Do this, and you may keep the hearing all your remaining days, and I will owe *you* a boon besides. Refuse—"**

"—and I lose the voices anyway," Xanthe finished bitterly. "Either way I end up doing it. That's not a bargain, that's a mugging."

**"It is a debt,"** Ashen said. **"There is a difference, though I grant it is subtle. Your trade has always run on debts, carver. Grave-gifts are debts paid to the dead. Smuggling is debts paid to the living. You understand better than most that nothing given is ever truly free."**

Xanthe set the sparrow down on the workbench with exaggerated care, because the alternative was throwing it into the fire, and they suspected the fire wouldn't have it.

"And if I go down there and die? What happens to your child then?"

**"Then I will wait longer. I am Ashen-Who-Waits. Waiting is the whole of My nature."** The ember-light dimmed, almost gentle. **"But you will not die. You are too stubborn, too careful, and too well-connected in the world's shadows. Which is why I chose you, though it grieves Me to coerce a soul I respect. Coercion is the usurper's tool. I despise that I must borrow it."**

"That's very touching," Xanthe said. "Write it on my gravestone. 'Coerced respectfully.'"

Silence. The real kind, the living kind, except that now Xanthe could feel the dead voices hovering just outside their reach like birds around a shuttered window, waiting to see what their keeper decided.

They looked around the workshop. At the tools their master had left them — the chisels, the burnishing stones, the great vice scarred with forty years of work. At the half-finished grave-gift for the miller's wife. At the little door-shaped amulets, row upon row, made to guide the dead home. *Home,* they thought. Three hundred years, and the dead had been locked out of their homes, and everyone had gotten used to it, and called the getting-used-to-it *peace*.

And their master. Their master, whose hands had carved the cage. Whose confession — if he'd left one, if there was anything left of him to confess — was presumably down there in the dark with the thing he'd helped sell. Xanthe had spent twenty years believing the old man had died innocent, run out of Thornfell by the purges, another victim. The vision had taken that belief and broken it across its knee like green wood.

Anger, they discovered, was an excellent cure for fear. Not a permanent one. But it would get them to the mountain.

"Fine," Xanthe said aloud. "I'll go. I'll get your child back. But I set terms, god or no god."

**"Name them."**

"One: I do it my way. Smuggler roads, smuggler contacts, my timeline. No miracles, no signs, nothing that paints a target on me. Two: when it's done, the voices come back clean — all of them, everything I've ever heard, no gaps, no edits. Three—" they hesitated, then pushed through, "—three, you tell me what happened to Master Oren Vael. Wait." The name snagged. "Vael. Why does that name sit wrong in my mouth tonight?"

The ember-light in the bones flickered — and Xanthe would have sworn, later, that the god hesitated.

**"Oren Vael was My servant once,"** Ashen said at last, **"before he became the usurper's. He is not dead, carver. He is the High Priest of the court beneath the mountain. He built the soul-trade. And he knows your name, because he trained your hands."**

The workshop seemed to tilt. Xanthe gripped the edge of the bench. Twenty years of grief rearranged itself into something hotter and harder, and they let it, because the alternative was to fall down.

"So it's not a rescue mission," they said quietly. "It's a reckoning."

**"It is both. They usually are."** The light began to fade, the voices of the ordinary dead seeping back in one by one — the ferryman's daughter's sparrow sighing, the prayer beads murmuring, the whole shelf resuming its ancient quiet chorus. **"One more thing, listener. You will not go alone. A man is coming to Hollowmere within the week — a knight with a broken oath-stone and holes where his memory should be. He is hunting the waking gods, though he doesn't yet know why. Take him with you. The road beneath the mountain eats solitary travelers alive."**

"I don't travel with knights. Knights bring attention, and attention brings—"

**"Bring him, Xanthe Amberdale."** For the first time, the god's voice carried something that was not command. It sounded almost like pity. **"His stolen memories and your stolen godling come from the same thief. When you understand that, you will stop arguing with Me."**

The glow died. The workshop was only a workshop again — firelight, woodsmoke, the smell of bone dust and linseed oil. Outside, Hollowmere slept on, unaware that its quietest resident had just been conscripted into a war three centuries in the making.

Xanthe stood for a long time in the dark. Then they pulled their master's oldest chisel from its roll of leather, wrapped it, and packed a bag with the mechanical efficiency of someone whose hands needed work or they'd start screaming.

Three days' walk south, past the fen and the burned watchtower, lived Mother Quill — smuggler queen, occasional savior, and the only person alive who knew the salt gates beneath the mountain. Xanthe would need her favor. Xanthe would hate owing it.

And somewhere on the north road, a disgraced knight was walking toward Hollowmere with a broken oath-stone and no idea why.

"Damn you, Ashen," Xanthe said to the empty room — and the empty room, for the first time in three hundred years, answered.

**"Already done. Travel at dawn."**
