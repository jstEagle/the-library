## 5. Smuggler's Road

The pass above Hollowmere had three names, depending on who you asked. The smugglers called it the Shepherd's Throat. The soldiers of the usurper called it a supply route, when they thought of it at all, which was rarely. And the dead who had died along it — there were more of them every year — called it nothing at all, because Xanthe had asked, once, and the voices had only sighed like wind through a collapsed mine.

Xanthe climbed it at dawn with a temple knight one step behind, and hated every minute of it.

Not the climbing. The company.

"You're slowing down," Cyrus said. He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. He had the particular patience of a man who had been trained to wait — outside doors, outside city walls, outside the moment a blade was needed.

"I'm reading the road," Xanthe said, and it was even true. The scree below the third switchback had been disturbed — not by weather, not by goats. Someone had walked up the slope and walked back down, and recently enough that the frost hadn't healed the marks. "There's a shrine ahead. We'll stop there."

"I thought we weren't stopping until the ridge."

"We weren't." Xanthe kept walking. "Plans change. Roads change. The mountain changes, if it's in a mood."

Cyrus said nothing to that, but Xanthe felt his eyes on their back, weighing. Let him weigh. Every traveler who hired Xanthe Amberdale got the same treatment: three days of small lies, one deliberate detour, and a test dressed up as carelessness. It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. A person who lied well under pressure was either a professional — in which case they were useful — or an infiltrator, in which case it was better to find out on a mountainside than in a smuggler's den where the penalty for betrayal was paid in fingers.

The shrine was a hollow in the rock, older than any of the three names for the pass. Someone long ago had carved a niche into the stone, and travelers had filled it with offerings until the niche overflowed: candle stubs, a child's shoe, a bird's skull, a wooden charm so weathered its shape was more suggestion than object. The dead who had left them were long gone, scattered or dissolved, and the hollow was quiet in the way Xanthe liked best — the silence of things that had finished speaking.

Xanthe stopped, made a show of checking the wind, and said, "The route forks past here. Low road's faster but there's a tollhouse. We take the high road."

The high road was, in fact, slower by half a day and passed no tollhouse at all. The low road was the one Xanthe had used for eleven years. But Cyrus only nodded, adjusted the strap of his pack, and said, "Whatever you think's safest."

*Too easy.* Xanthe's fingers itched for a knife to carve, something to do with the disappointment. A man who agreed with everything was either a fool or a spy, and Cyrus was no fool. The broken oath-stone hung at his throat on a leather cord, and Xanthe could feel it from three paces away the way you feel a tooth that's about to ache — a wrongness in the air, a hum with no voice in it. Dead stone. Dead oath. Whatever had been written on it had been unwritten, and that was not a thing that happened by accident.

They climbed. The morning sharpened. Below them, Hollowmere shrank to a scatter of grey roofs and woodsmoke, and beyond it the lowlands lay brown and gold under the first frost, the free cities' patchwork of stubborn little fields. Somewhere down there, Xanthe knew, the usurper's armies were gathering the way stormwater gathers — invisibly, and then all at once.

"You were at Thornfell," Cyrus said, after an hour of silence. "During the war."

"I was everywhere during the war. Carving doesn't stop for politics."

"You guided smugglers through the siege lines. Mother Quill's people. I've heard the stories."

"Stories exaggerate."

"Did you ever carry anything for the temple?"

Xanthe stopped so abruptly that Cyrus nearly walked into them. They turned. Up close, the knight had the kind of face that had been handsome once and had then been lived in — a scar through one eyebrow, hollows under the eyes, a jaw that looked like it spent its nights clenching things. The oath-stone swung free of his collar, and up close its wrongness was almost loud.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I'm trying to remember what side I was on," Cyrus said. "And you're the first person in four hundred miles who might actually tell me the truth."

The honesty of it was disarming, which was exactly why Xanthe distrusted it. "I don't carry messages for temples. Temples write things down, and things written down get people killed."

"Then you'll have no reason to lie when I ask: did you ever hear of a knight named Ser Aldous Vane?"

The name meant nothing. Xanthe shook their head, and watched something go out of the man's face — not hope exactly, more the last ember of it, banked and covered over so it wouldn't show.

They went on. The high road narrowed to a ledge above a ravine where the snowmelt ran loud and white, and Xanthe began, quietly, without any change in their breathing, to count the ways this day could go wrong.

* * *

The ambush came at the place Xanthe had privately named the Bad Bend, because it was.

Later, Xanthe would try to reconstruct the sequence and fail. There had been no warning from the dead — that was the thing that would keep them up at night afterward. The pass was littered with the unquiet, and usually they muttered at Xanthe like old men complaining about weather. But the bend was silent, and the silence should have been the warning, and Xanthe had walked into it thinking about lunch.

The first crossbow bolt took the pack off Xanthe's shoulder and spun it into the ravine. The second missed because Xanthe was already moving, dropping off the ledge into the shadow of an overhang that every smuggler in Hollowmere knew and no map showed. Three men in the usurper's grey-and-black came around the bend at a run — outriders, scouts, the usurper's eyes pushed far ahead of his armies, exactly where they should not have been.

"Amberdale," the lead one said, and Xanthe's stomach dropped. They knew the name. Someone had sold it. "The guide. And the knight. Vael wants the knight alive."

Then Cyrus moved, and the world changed key.

Xanthe had seen soldiers fight. They had watched duels in the free cities, watched border skirmishes from ridgelines, watched the war chew through the young and the stupid for years. What Cyrus did in the next eleven seconds was none of those things.

He did not draw his sword — there was no room on the ledge for a sword. He stepped *into* the first man's thrust, turned his wrist inside the man's grip, and put him over the edge of the ravine with the unhurried economy of someone closing a door. The second man's blade came down and Cyrus wasn't there; he was inside the arc, and there was a sound like a branch snapping, and the man folded around Cyrus's elbow and went down and did not get up. The third man was smarter. He backed away, bolt trained on Cyrus's chest, and said something — Xanthe never learned what, because Cyrus took a throwing knife from his own belt, a knife Xanthe had never seen him carry, and put it through the man's hand before the crossbow could fire, and then through the man's throat before the man finished screaming.

Then it was over, and Cyrus stood in the middle of the ledge breathing hard, three bodies around him, and looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger.

"I didn't—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know that form. The wrist-turn, the — that's temple work. Close-quarters doctrine. I've never been taught it."

"You've never *remembered* being taught it," Xanthe said, before they could stop themselves.

Cyrus looked up. There was blood on his cheek that wasn't his, and his eyes had gone very wide and very still, the stillness of a man standing at the edge of something deep. "You said you don't believe anyone."

"I don't."

"Then believe your own eyes." He held out his hands, palms up, and they were shaking now — the shaking had waited until the killing was done, which was its own kind of discipline, its own kind of horror. "Something in me knows how to do this. Something in me has *done* this. A lot." His voice dropped. "How many men do you think I've killed, Xanthe? Because I count three, and it felt like counting to three after a long walk. Like my body was glad to be home."

The wind came up the ravine and moved the blood on the stones, and the dead of the pass — where were they? Xanthe reached out with that sense they had never been able to name, the hearing that was not hearing, and found the bend still silent, still swept clean, as though even the dead had stepped back to give the fighting room.

"The lead one said Vael wants you alive," Xanthe said. "Vael Corvane. High Priest of the Ascendant Flame. That name mean anything to you?"

Cyrus went white. Not the white of fear — the white of a door being forced open somewhere behind his eyes, and something on the other side pushing back.

"I've heard that name," he said slowly, "in my own voice. In dreams. Saying it the way you say a name when you've said it a thousand times." He looked down at the dead man at his feet, at the grey-and-black livery, at the flame sigil stitched in silver thread. "Xanthe. I think I didn't lose my memory. I think someone *took* it. And I think the taking was the kindest thing the temple ever did for me."

Xanthe crouched by the lead outrider — the one in the ravine was gone, but this one still breathed, barely, a wet whistle in the ruined throat. They had seen enough death to know there was nothing to be done, and they had learned long ago not to pretend otherwise. But they took the man's hand, because someone should, and leaned close.

The voice that came was thin and full of static, the way voices always were at the very end, when the dead were barely dead at all.

"*The pass at Redwater,*" the dying man whispered, or the dead man through him, or both. "*They're coming through Redwater. Tell the guide — tell the guide the mountain road is already bought.*"

Xanthe sat back. The cold of the stone came up through their knees and into their spine.

"What is it?" Cyrus asked. "What did it say?"

Xanthe looked up the pass, toward the high road they had chosen, the road that was supposed to be safe, the detour they had taken on a whim that now felt less like a whim and more like the first finger of a hand closing around them.

"It said our road is already bought," Xanthe said. "Someone sold us before we ever left Hollowmere."

And behind Cyrus, in the niche of the old shrine, the weathered wooden charm that had hung silent for a hundred years began, very softly, to hum.
