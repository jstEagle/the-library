## 6. What the Dead Remember

The storm found them an hour past the second ridge, and Xanthe found them shelter twenty minutes after that — a fissure in the limestone that breathed warm air from somewhere deep below and smelled, faintly but unmistakably, of old grief.

They knew the smell before they knew what it was. Every bone-carver did. It was the scent of a charnel cave: tallow and dust and the sweet-sour ghost of bodies long stripped to their quietest parts.

"Stop." Xanthe's hand went up, palm flat, and Cyrus stopped as if he'd walked into a wall of glass. He was learning. Three days on the smuggler's road and he had learned that when Xanthe raised a hand, it meant *the mountain is speaking*.

"What is it?" His voice was low. Snow clung to his shoulders and melted down the black of his ruined temple cloak, and he shivered without seeming to notice he was doing it.

"Cave." Xanthe tilted their head, listening past the wind. "Old one. Consecrated once, maybe. There were rites here."

"You can hear that?"

"I can hear everything that was ever said near a carved thing, knight. And there are carvings in there." They paused. "A lot of them."

Cyrus looked at the fissure, then at the sky, which had gone the color of dirty wool and was already shrugging its first snow down onto the pass. "Bodies?"

"Probably. Old ones. The dead don't trouble me." This was not entirely true, and they both knew it, and neither said so.

Xanthe went first, because Xanthe always went first into places where the dead might have opinions. The fissure widened after ten body-lengths into a chamber the size of a shrine hall, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor layered with niches cut into the living rock — hundreds of them, row upon row, each holding a folded skeleton or a bundle of bones wrapped in rotted linen gone the color of weak tea. Whoever had dug this place had done it with love, or at least with patience. The niches' edges bore tiny carved marks: a name-sigil, a day-sign, a small invocation for safe passage into whatever came next.

Someone had grieved here, carefully, over generations. And then everyone who remembered why had died, and the cave had been left to hold its dead alone.

Xanthe stood in the entrance while the wind moaned behind them like something being strangled, and listened.

It was quiet. That was the strange part. Charnel places usually murmured — a thousand half-voices caught in the grain of the offerings left with the bones, beads and tokens and little carved animals meant to keep the dead company on the road. But these voices had thinned to almost nothing, worn smooth by centuries, like river stones. What remained was less speech than weather. A pressure. A vast, patient *waiting*.

"Well?" Cyrus had crowded in behind them, dripping snowmelt onto stone that had been dry for three hundred years, and Xanthe had to bite down the reflexive irritation.

"It's safe. As much as anywhere is." They unslung their pack and began, without asking permission, to make camp — the small efficient economy of a person who had slept alone in bad places their whole adult life. Fire-ring of stacked femurs? No. Never femurs; the dead took it personally. They built the ring from loose scree instead, and Cyrus, watching, actually had the grace to look embarrassed about his own boots on consecrated ground.

*He has manners under all that iron*, Xanthe thought, and immediately distrusted the thought. That was how it started. First you noticed a man's manners. Then you noticed he shared his rations without being asked. Then you noticed he stood watch through the whole night so you could sleep, and told you he hadn't needed the sleep anyway, which was a lie so transparent it was almost charming. Then one day someone asked him where your loyalties lay, and you discovered you'd misplaced them somewhere between Hollowmere and the second ridge.

Xanthe had watched that happen to smugglers. To guides better than them. To Mother Quill, once, though she'd cut the loyalty out of herself with a knife before it could kill her, and look what it had cost her — a heart like a coin purse, everything weighed, everything priced.

Xanthe fed a twist of tinder into the scree ring and coaxed a flame from their flint, and did not look at Cyrus kneeling across the fire, working snow out of his hair.

* *

The storm settled in for the night the way storms do in the high passes — completely, vindictively, as if the sky held a grudge against anything warm. Wind screamed across the cave mouth and turned back on itself. Somewhere above, snow slithered off the rocks in soft collapses.

They ate in silence. Barley and dried mutton and a scrap of hard cheese Cyrus produced from some inner pocket with the solemnity of a man presenting relics. Afterward he sat sharpening his sword, the whetstone whispering in slow strokes, and Xanthe sat cross-legged by the fire with their carving knives laid out on a square of oiled leather and made themselves busy re-honing edges that didn't need it, because their hands wanted work and their mind wanted a reason not to think.

Across the fire, on Cyrus's pack, the broken oath-stone caught the light.

It was a fist-sized chunk of white marble, sheared clean through, and he wore it on a cord at his throat the way other men wore saints' medallions. Xanthe had seen it every day since the gate at Hollowmere, had heard it every day too — a thin, continuous keening like a wire singing in wind, the sound of a vow torn in half and never finished. Most people couldn't hear it. Cyrus couldn't hear it. But it filled Xanthe's skull from dawn to dusk, a needle of sound behind the eye, and three days of walking beside it had frayed their temper down to the quick.

Tonight, finally, curiosity outweighed annoyance. Or perhaps it was only that the cave's silence had made room for it. The dead here waited so patiently, so completely, that the stone's keening seemed suddenly rude by comparison — and things that were rude invited examination.

"That stone," Xanthe said. "Where did you get it?"

Cyrus's hands stilled on the whetstone. For a long moment he didn't answer, and the fire popped, and the wind clawed at the cave mouth.

"They gave it to me when I took my vows," he said at last. "Every knight of the Vigilant Order swears on one. It's split with the half you keep and the half that stays in the temple at Cael Serrat. When you die, they join them again and put the whole in your tomb. If you break faith—" His jaw worked. "If you break faith, they break the stone."

"Yours is broken."

"My stone is broken." He said it flatly, the way a man says *my leg is broken*, having lived with it long enough that the words have stopped bleeding. "I woke up four months ago in a ditch outside Vellin Cross with no memory of the previous two years and this around my neck. No trial I remember. No sentence. Just — gone. Everyone in the Order looks at me like I'm a ghost that owes them money." He turned the stone over in his fingers. "I've been trying to find out what I did ever since."

"And if you find out you did it?" Xanthe kept their voice neutral, a craftsman's voice, inquiring about materials. "Whatever it was. Whatever got your stone broken and your memory taken. What then?"

Cyrus looked at them across the fire, and there was something naked in his face that he probably thought he was hiding. "Then I'll carry it," he said. "But I want to know it's mine first. You can't repent honestly for a sin you can't remember committing. That's not penance, that's just punishment wearing penance's clothes."

*Xanthe has survived by never believing anyone.* The old litany rose automatically, the way a hand rises to shield the eyes. *Everyone lies. Everyone curates. Everyone shows you the version of themselves that serves them.* It had kept them alive through Thornfell's wars, through smuggler feuds and priest purges and the slow grinding years when neutrality was the only country that would have them.

And yet.

"Give me the stone," they said.

Cyrus blinked. "What?"

"You heard me." Xanthe held out their hand, palm up, steady. "You want to know what you did. I'm the only soul in Thornfell who can tell you. But understand what you're agreeing to, knight. When I read a thing, I hear everything it ever witnessed. Everything done in its presence, everything said, everything felt hard enough to soak into the grain. Stones are worse than bone. Bone forgets eventually. Stone remembers *everything*." They let that sit. "Some of what's in there may be things you'd rather stay forgotten. Some of it may be lies — stones record, they don't judge, and men lie to stones all the time. And some of it will be true. Are you ready for true?"

The firelight moved on his face. He was afraid — Xanthe could see it plainly now, the fear he'd been armoring over with soldier's posture since Hollowmere. Fear of himself. It was, Xanthe reflected, a fear they understood better than any other kind. They had spent thirty years afraid of what the dead might say next.

"If you're lying to me," Cyrus said quietly, "about what you can do—"

"I don't lie about my craft." Xanthe flexed their fingers. "It's the only honest thing about me. The stone."

He lifted the cord over his head. He hesitated, the broken marble cupped in both hands like a wounded bird, and Xanthe watched him take one breath, two, three — the breathing exercises of a man preparing for pain — and then set it in their palm.

It was cold. Colder than the cave, colder than the snow outside, cold the way graves are cold: a cold with intention behind it.

Xanthe closed their fingers over the broken faces of the stone and let go of the surface world.

* *

The keening swallowed them first — that wire-song rising to a shriek and then resolving, unbearably, into voices. The stone had been present at so much. A chapel with incense and boys' voices. A courtyard at dawn, oaths recited in unison, dozens of palms pressing against the stone's face one after another, each touch leaving an impression the way footprints leave impressions in wax: *I will hold the vigil. I will guard the low roads. I will not raise my sword against the innocent, nor stand aside while others do.* Cyrus's touch among them — younger, callused differently, trembling slightly with the enormity of it. Xanthe recognized the tremble. Everyone trembles at their own vows. It's the ones who don't that you should run from.

Then years, compressed. Marches. Watchfires. The stone riding against a heartbeat, learning its rhythm the way Xanthe's knives had learned theirs. Prayers muttered over it in the dark. Once — a woman's laugh, close, private, the sound of someone loved. Xanthe noted that and filed it away and did not linger, because ahead of them the visions were darkening, gathering like weather.

The village. They knew it was a village even before the stone showed it — knew it by the smell of woodsmoke and bread, by the feel of a well-worn road, by the particular texture of ordinary lives going about an ordinary evening. Dusk in autumn. Lamplight beginning in windows.

And soldiers. White-and-gold, the Vigilant Order's colors, moving through the streets in file.

*No,* Cyrus's memory said, even as the stone recorded it. *No, we're not posted here, we're a week's march from — whose order was this? Whose seal?*

The stone could not answer that. The stone only recorded. And so Xanthe saw what Cyrus had seen, from the height of his eyes, at the speed of his horror:

The knights — his brothers, his sworn brothers, men whose palms had touched this same stone — going house to house. Not searching. *Emptying.* Dragging families into the square. And in the square, a priest in vestments Xanthe did not recognize, gray and silver, and a brazier, and —

Xanthe flinched. Even secondhand, even filtered through marble, some things soaked through. The stone had been in Cyrus's clenched fist, pressed so hard the edges cut, and it had recorded the blood, and the screaming, and the smell — rendered fat, the smell of the courts beneath the mountains, the smell of souls being *rendered* —

"He tried to stop it." Xanthe's own voice, hoarse, far away. "He tried to stop them."

Because he had. The vision showed it: Cyrus drawing steel against his own brother-knights, shouting words the stone drank in whole — *"This is murder! In the name of the Vigilant, this is MURDER—"* — and being struck down from behind, and the last thing the stone recorded before darkness was the priest's voice, calm as bookkeeping: *"Take his stone to Cael Serrat. He broke his oath tonight. Let the records say so."*

Let the records say so. Not *he broke his oath*. Let the records *say* it. The distinction mattered, in the way such distinctions always matter to liars.

Darkness. Then fragments, scattered like dropped coins: a cell. Questions he couldn't answer because the answers had been taken out of him — Xanthe felt the wrongness of that, the surgical absence, memories excised not crudely but *cleanly*, by someone very skilled, leaving smooth walls where doors used to be. Hands binding the cord around his neck — his own hands? someone else's? — and a voice saying *walk east and keep walking and if you come back we will finish what the priest started.* And then the ditch outside Vellin Cross, rain, waking, and the terrible blank morning where a man discovers he is a stranger wearing his own name.

Xanthe surfaced gasping.

The fire had burned low. Cyrus was on his feet, staring at them, and his face was wet, and he didn't seem to know it. The stone lay in Xanthe's lap, silent now, spent, its keening quieted as if the telling had eased something in it.

"What did you see?" Cyrus whispered. "Tell me. All of it. Tell me exactly."

So Xanthe told him. The village. The square. The gray-vested priest and the brazier. The rendering-smell — and at that, Cyrus went rigid, because he'd been beneath the mountain court too, in the days Xanthe was negotiating with Mother Quill, and he knew that smell from the source. His own hand on his sword. His own voice breaking on the word *murder*. The blow from behind. The priest's ledger-calm sentence.

When Xanthe finished, the cave was very still. Even the wind seemed to be listening.

"They framed me." Cyrus's voice came out scraped raw. "There was no crime of mine. There was a massacre — *their* massacre — and I drew steel to stop it, and they wrote it down backwards." He laughed, a horrible sound with nothing in it. "Two years of my life. My vows, my brothers, my name. Erased. Rewritten. Because I was inconvenient."

"Yes."

"And the villagers—" He stopped. Started again. "Did anyone survive? Did the stone—"

"The stone doesn't know what happened after you fell." Xanthe turned the broken marble over in their fingers, feeling its exhausted quiet. "I'm sorry."

Cyrus sat down slowly, like a man whose legs had been quietly removed. He stared into the fire for a long time.

*Here it comes*, Xanthe thought. *Now he asks me to believe him. Now he wants me on his side, vouching, testifying, standing witness.* And the litany rose again, faithful as a heartbeat: *never believe anyone, never carry anyone's cause, the moment you carry a cause you become target and tool both.*

But that wasn't what happened.

What happened was that Cyrus looked up, and instead of pleading, he said, in a level, careful voice: "Don't believe me."

Xanthe blinked. "...What?"

"Don't." He leaned forward, firelight cutting his face into planes and shadow. "You've been waiting for three days to decide whether I'm worth trusting, and I'm telling you — don't trust me. Don't believe my word. I'm the least reliable witness to my own life in all of Thornfell; half of it's been cut out of me. Believe the stone. You said it yourself — stone records, stone doesn't judge, stone can't lie. You heard what it heard. So the question isn't whether you believe *me*." His eyes were bright and terrible. "The question is whether you believe what you heard with your own gift. Because either the dead and the stones of this world are liars too, Xanthe Amberdale, in which case nothing you have ever carved means anything — or somebody in white and gold slaughtered a village full of the innocent and hung it around my neck. Those are the only two choices on the table."

The words landed like a knife between ribs, and Xanthe sat very still with them.

Because it was true. That was the obscenity of it. Thirty years of neutrality, thirty years of *believe no one, owe no one, carve your grave-gifts and keep your mouth shut* — and it had all rested on a foundation Xanthe had never once examined: that hearing the dead meant something. That testimony mattered. That truth, spoken for the voiceless, was worth the danger of speaking it. Strip that away and Xanthe Amberdale was not neutral, was not wise, was simply a coward with a peculiar talent.

*I don't have to decide tonight*, they told themselves. *No one's making me choose in a hole in a mountain with a storm overhead.*

But even as they thought it, the cave answered — the way caves do, the way the dead do when they have waited three hundred years and finally sense someone listening. From the niches all around them, from the folded skeletons and the name-sigils and the little carved animals meant to keep lonely travelers company, came a sound Xanthe had not heard since childhood: not words, not yet, but attention. Hundreds of hollow sockets turning, in the manner of speaking, toward the firelight. Toward the two living people. Toward the story that had just been spoken aloud in a house of the dead.

And underneath the attention, faint as a pulse, one voice — older than the rest, worn nearly to nothing, a woman's voice by its timbre — repeating something over and over, patient as water wearing stone:

*They sold her soul. They sold her soul. They sold her soul.*

Xanthe's blood went cold. That voice. That exact voice, those exact words, from a grave-gift carved in Hollowmere a week ago, from a dead woman who had never met Cyrus, never seen a knight, never known the first thing about massacres or priests — and here she was, or her echo was, or something wearing her echo was, four ridges into Thornfell's high passes, inside a sealed charnel cave.

"How," Xanthe said aloud, before they could stop themselves, "is she here?"

Cyrus frowned. "Who's here?"

Xanthe didn't answer. Outside, the storm had begun, very gradually, to ease — and beneath the dying wind, from far down the southern slopes, came a new sound threading through the silence. Faint. Rhythmic. Human.

Hundreds of voices, murmuring, weeping, praying. The sound of a refugee column on the move in the dark.

And above it, carried impossibly clear on the freezing air, a single line of song in a language older than the usurper, older than the temple, older than the free cities themselves — a hymn to the gods returned.

The dead in the niches stirred, all together, like a field of wheat in wind.

"They're coming," Xanthe said, and reached for their pack.
