## 7. The Free Cities Burn Slow

The road down from the Thornfell passes was a wound in the world.

Cyrus had walked war roads before — or his body had, even if the memories of them belonged to some other man, some earlier version of himself that the temple had scraped out like marrow from a bone. His feet knew how to find the hard-packed center of a track. His shoulders knew to turn slightly into the wind so that it carried voices toward him rather than past him. His hands, at rest on his sword-belt, knew the weight of the blade the way a man knows the weight of his own arm.

But he had never seen anything like this.

They came over the last shoulder of the high pass at dawn, and the lowlands opened beneath them like a page torn from a book of horrors. The valley of the Sarrow ran wide and brown with early thaw, stitched with fields that should have been green with winter wheat and were instead black with the passage of armies. Smoke rose from a dozen places along the river — not the clean gray smoke of hearth-fires but the oily, clinging black of burning barns and granaries, of thatch and timber and everything else that could not run.

And running — the gods forgive me, Cyrus thought, they are all still running.

The road itself was choked with them. Refugees, hundreds upon hundreds of them, a slow river of humanity flowing south and west away from the advance, flowing in the same direction as the water, as if the whole valley had decided to drain itself of life. Carts heaped with children and chickens and grandmothers. Men carrying doors on their backs because doors were what stood between a family and the cold, and a man will carry a door forty miles if he must. Women with bundles tied to their chests, walking with the terrible mechanical steadiness of those who have stopped believing that stopping is allowed.

"They're coming from the fords," Xanthe said quietly beside him. The bone-carver's voice was flat, but Cyrus had learned in five days of travel to hear the things underneath the flatness — the way a listener hears the room behind a closed door. "The usurper's outriders burned the bridge at Millbrook three days ago. Anyone who couldn't swim the Sarrow went north along the bank." A pause. "Anyone who couldn't swim at all—"

Xanthe didn't finish it. Didn't need to.

Cyrus looked down at the valley and felt something shift inside him, some load-bearing wall of his self cracking under a weight it had been built to hold and was no longer sure it wanted to.

*This is your war,* said a voice in him that might have been memory and might have been conscience. *You wore the white-and-gold. You carried the temple's banner. Whatever they took out of your head, they cannot take out of the fact of it: you served the order that did this.*

He had told himself, since Hollowmere, that his quest was simple. Find the gods who had woken. Find the fragments of himself scattered through their returning attention like coins dropped in snow. Learn what he had done, pay whatever penance the truth demanded, and be done with it. It had felt like a private matter, almost — a debt between one man and his own missing years.

There was nothing private about this.

A column of refugees passed close below the track where they stood, close enough that Cyrus could see individual faces. An old man leading a mule loaded with looms — actual looms, dismantled and lashed together, because a weaver without his looms was not a weaver, and a man would rather die a refugee than live as nothing. A girl of perhaps eight carrying a cage with a single dove in it, holding it carefully against her chest as though the bird were the last thing in the world made of gentleness. A young woman whose belly was heavy with child, walking with one hand pressed to the small of her back and the other gripping the rope-harness of a cart, her face set in an expression Cyrus recognized with a jolt: it was the expression of someone doing arithmetic. Counting steps. Counting hours. Counting how much longer she could do this before the body simply refused.

He had seen that face before. He was certain of it. Somewhere in the five years the temple had carved out of him there was a road like this one, and faces like these, and he had looked at them then too — but how? As what?

As a knight of the old order, riding escort, keeping the column safe? Or as something worse?

"You've gone pale," Xanthe observed. They had stopped a few paces ahead, pack shifted, watching him with those colorless eyes that seemed always to be listening to something just beyond the air. "Or grayer than usual, which for you is saying something."

"I served them," Cyrus said. The words came out of him like teeth being pulled. "The usurper's order. The Temple of the Ninefold Crown — whatever it calls itself now. I don't remember serving. But I know I did. The oath-stone proves it. And this—" he gestured at the valley, at the smoke, at the endless shuffling river of the dispossessed, "—this is what my service built."

Xanthe considered him for a long moment. The wind off the pass moved their gray-streaked hair, and for once they did not look away first.

"Yesterday you asked me why I stay neutral," they said. "Why I carve for anyone who pays and trust no one. This is why." They nodded at the valley. "Every side in every war tells its soldiers that the other side are monsters. And every soldier believes it, right up until the day the war comes for *them*, and then they learn that monsters are just people with reasons. I keep my hands clean by keeping them empty." Their jaw tightened. "But the dead don't keep their hands clean, Cyrus. The dead tell me what was done to them, and lately—" a flicker of something crossed their face, some echo of the voices only they could hear, "—lately the dead have been *loud*. Louder than they've been in three hundred years. And most of what they're saying is names. Names of people in valleys like this one."

Cyrus looked back at the refugee column. The girl with the dove had fallen a few steps behind her family; she had stopped to let the bird drink from a puddle in the wheel-rut. She cupped water in her palm, patiently, again and again, while the column drew away from her, and Cyrus understood that she would do it until the dove was done, even if the outriders came over the hill while she knelt there. Some part of him that predated his amputation — some part the temple's knives had missed — rose up in his chest with a force that nearly buckled him.

*I know this girl,* that part of him insisted. *I have seen her before. Not her — someone like her. In a village. In a village that—*

The thought shattered before it could form, leaving only a ringing absence, the familiar sick vertigo of the erased. He put his hand against a boulder to steady himself and felt, through his glove, the faint warmth of the broken oath-stone in the pouch at his belt. It had been warming more often lately. Warming when he thought of the massacre at Vael's Crossing — the crime whose name he carried, the crime he could not remember committing.

"Come on," Xanthe said. "If we're going down among them, we go down now, while the light's good. Roads full of refugees attract wolves. Two-legged ones."

*

They descended into the human river and became part of it.

It was strange, Cyrus thought, how quickly a man could become indistinguishable. He had spent his adult life — whatever portion of it remained to him — marked. First as a knight in white-and-gold, visible from a thousand paces, a symbol men saluted or spat at. Then, after his disgrace, as a branded thing, an oath-breaker, recognizable by the scar of the brand even under his collar. Now he was simply a tall man with a sword walking beside a thin carver with a sledge of tools, and the refugees barely glanced at them. There were armed men aplenty in the column — deserters mostly, soldiers of the free cities' levies who had abandoned losing positions and now guarded their own families with the same swords they'd failed to hold the line with. One of them, a heavyset man with a crossbow slung across his back, fell into step beside Cyrus for half a mile and talked the entire time, needing no answer, needing only a listener.

"Burned us out of Harrow Ford," the man said. "Didn't even want the town. Just burned it. Priest came through two days ahead of the soldiers, riding a white mule, telling everyone to swear to the Ninefold Crown and they'd be spared. Old Marta Tull wouldn't swear. She'd buried three husbands in that churchyard and she wasn't going to leave them under a foreign god." He laughed, a horrible hollow sound. "So they burned the churchyard first. Made her watch. Then the town." He shook his head. "Swear or burn. That's the whole gospel of them now. Swear or burn."

"Where are the free cities' armies?" Cyrus asked.

The deserter gave him a look of exhausted contempt. "Where do you think? Behind walls. Saltmere's shut its gates and won't open them for us — says they can't feed refugees and garrison both. Corvane's vanguard doesn't need to beat our armies, friend. It just needs to keep them busy while the countryside empties. You can't defend cities with no one left alive around them to defend." He spat. "We're not fleeing the war. We *are* the war. We're what it does."

He wandered off toward his own cart then, having gotten whatever a talking man gets from a listening one, and Cyrus walked on in silence with the arithmetic of it turning over in him.

Vael Corvane. Even the name of the High Priest reached this far, riding ahead of the soldiers on a white mule, offering conversion at spear-point. Cyrus turned the name over in his mind and felt the oath-stone warm against his hip, and wondered — not for the first time — whether the warmth was recognition or warning.

Near midday they passed a shrine at a crossroads, one of the little wayside shrines that had dotted the free cities for centuries — a stone hood housing a carved figure worn featureless by weather, offerings of bread and ribbon and copper coins heaped in the alcove. Except this one had been defaced. Smashed, deliberately, with hammers or mace-heads; the figure's face obliterated, the offering-alcove fouled. And nailed above it, on a plank, a proclamation in red paint:

**THE OLD GODS ARE DEAD. THE NINEFOLD CROWN ALONE REIGNS. WORSHIP OF CORPSES IS PUNISHABLE BY FIRE.**

Beneath the shrine, someone had laid fresh offerings anyway. Bread. A ribbon. A handful of copper coins, arranged in a careful spiral. And scratched into the stone base, small and defiant, in a hand that shook:

*ashen waits*

Xanthe stopped and looked at it for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice was very quiet. "Three hundred years," they said. "For three hundred years nobody prayed to Ashen-Who-Waits, and the god slept, and nobody got hurt. Now the god's awake, and the priests are smashing shrines, and people are dying for scratching two words in stone." They looked up at Cyrus. "Do you understand what that means? The waking isn't a blessing. It's a battlefield opening. Every god that stirs is a front in a war the living didn't choose."

"And yet they leave offerings," Cyrus said, nodding at the bread, the ribbon, the spiral of coins. "Knowing it can get them burned."

"People have always prayed best when praying is dangerous," Xanthe said. "It's the safe prayers that mean nothing."

They walked on. But Cyrus looked back once, at the smashed shrine and the shaking handwriting, and felt the oath-stone pulse warm against his hip like a second heartbeat, and thought: *I was there when the first shrines fell. I know I was. Whose hands swung the hammers?*

Mine? Or the hands of the men I was ordered to stand beside while theirs did?

He honestly did not know which answer he feared more.

*

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined tithe-barn, sharing fire with perhaps thirty other travelers who had clustered together for safety. Nobody asked anyone's name. That was the custom of the road now: names were dangerous, names were how lists got made, and everyone had heard about the lists — the priest-census-takers who moved through the occupied territories recording who had sworn and who had not, whose names went into ledgers that decided which villages burned.

Cyrus sat apart, cleaning his sword by firelight, and watched the camp.

A mother was feeding her children from a pot of barley broth stretched with wild onions. A boy of twelve was sharpening a billhook on a whetstone with the grim concentration of a much older man. Two old women were mending a cart-cover by the light of a rush-candle, their needles flashing, their conversation conducted entirely in the shorthand of sixty shared years. Ordinary lives, ordinary tenderness, carried on in the shadow of annihilation with a stubbornness that made something ache in Cyrus's chest.

And he understood, sitting there with the whetstone-rasp of the boy's billhook in his ears, that his quest had changed shape.

He had begun it as a man hunting his own past — a private hunger, a need to know what had been taken from him, to be whole again or at least to understand the shape of his own brokenness. That hunger was still there. It would always be there; the temple had seen to that, carving out five years of him and leaving the wound dressed in silence.

But looking at the boy with the billhook, at the mother rationing broth, at the old women mending cloth by candlelight in a world that intended to burn the cloth and the candle and the hands that held them — looking at all of it, Cyrus felt the private hunger transmute into something larger and far more dangerous.

*Justice.*

Not the justice of courts. He had stood in a court already — the tribunal at the Bastion of the Ninefold Crown, five years ago, where men in white-and-gold had read charges against him for a massacre he could not remember and pronounced him disgraced, stripped, branded. He knew exactly what courtly justice was worth. It was a ledger kept by the victors, balanced in favor of whoever held the pen. If he ever learned the truth of Vael's Crossing, no court of the usurper would hear it. And the free cities' councils — Mother Quill's smuggler-princes, the merchant-lords of Saltmere — would weigh truth against trade goods and sell it to whichever side paid.

No. If justice was to be had, it would have to be the kind the road offered: the kind made by what a man did with his hands in front of witnesses who could not be bought. The kind the boy with the billhook would remember. The kind the old women would tell their grandchildren about, in rebuilt kitchens, in towns that had survived.

*Cyrus the Oath-Breaker,* they called him. Let them. An oath is only a promise made to an institution, and institutions lie. What mattered was the older law underneath — the one the temple had taught him before it taught him obedience, the one that had somehow survived the carving-out of his memory the way a seed survives a fire.

Protect the small. Stand in the gap. Do not look away.

He realized Xanthe had come to sit beside him — silently, the way the carver did everything, materializing out of the dark like something the night had decided to lend him. They handed him a strip of dried apple without comment and chewed one themselves, eyes on the fire.

"You've decided something," Xanthe said at last.

"How can you tell?"

"You stopped looking hunted. Hunted men look behind themselves. You've started looking forward." Xanthe turned the dried apple over in their fingers. "Whatever it is — think carefully. People who decide things near burning valleys tend to decide badly. Heroics are just fear wearing armor."

"It's not heroics." Cyrus turned his sword in the firelight, watching the oil sheen slide along the steel. "Five days ago I wanted my memories back because they were *mine*. Because a man has a right to his own life. That's still true. But it's not enough anymore." He nodded across the fire, at the boy and the billhook, at the mother and the broth. "Corvane's priests are making lists. Burning churchyards. Emptying valleys so the free cities fall like fruit with no branches left to hang from. Somebody gave those orders. Somebody designed this — the census, the shrines, the swear-or-burn. And I served that somebody. My hands may be clean of specific crimes or they may not; I don't know, and that not-knowing is its own punishment. But I'm done waiting for a tribunal to tell me who I was." He looked at Xanthe. "I'll know who I am by what I do next."

Xanthe was quiet for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, a child cried and was hushed.

"The mountain court keeps records," Xanthe said finally, reluctantly, as though each word cost a coin. "Of debts. Of sales. Of everything that's ever been traded beneath the mountain — including souls. If your memories were taken by the temple, and the temple deals with the court..." They shrugged, elaborately casual, unconvincingly. "Debts leave paper. Paper leaves traces. Traces can be read."

"You've heard something," Cyrus said. "One of the dead."

"I hear lots of things." Xanthe stood, brushing off their breeches, and for a moment their silhouette against the fire was all sharp angles and wary distance, the neutral carver, the man who trusted no one. "Get some sleep, knight. Tomorrow we reach the salt-road junction, and after that the roads stop being ours."

They withdrew to their bedroll. But Cyrus stayed up a while longer, watching the fire, feeling the oath-stone warm against his hip like a sleeping animal dreaming of home.

*Justice outside any court,* he thought. *Testimony that can't be bought. Witnesses who can't be silenced.*

He thought of the smashed shrine, and the shaking words scratched beneath it — *ashen waits* — and of the godling's soul sold beneath the mountain, and of a High Priest named Vael Corvane who rode a white mule ahead of the fires and offered men a choice between a foreign crown and a grave.

Tomorrow the salt-road. After that, the mountain.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, in ledgers of sold souls and bartered sins, Cyrus suspected, waited the truth of Vael's Crossing — and the beginning of everything he would have to do to make it right.

The fire burned low. Around him the refugees slept the thin sleep of the dispossessed, and far to the north, unseen, the fires of the advancing army lit the underside of the clouds like a second, crueler dawn.

Cyrus slept at last, and dreamed — for the first time in five years — of standing in a churchyard, sword drawn, shouting *no* at men in white-and-gold.

He did not know if it was memory.

He was beginning to hope it was prophecy.
