## 8. A Bargain in Salt

The salt-house at Grayharbor smelled of brine and old secrets, and beneath both — faint as a hairline crack in glaze — of rendered soul-light. Xanthe felt it the moment they crossed the threshold: a low hum in the bones of their skull, the way the dead always announced themselves before they spoke. Here it was not one voice but many, layered and thinned, like songs heard through a wall. The lanterns that lit the smuggler queen's hall burned on something that had once been alive, and every one of them whispered.

Xanthe had not come back to Grayharbor in nine years. They had sworn they never would.

*Sworn oaths are cheap,* said the dead woman from Hollowmere, whose last words still followed them down every road. *It's debts that have teeth.*

"Quiet," Xanthe murmured, and a dockhand loading salt-barrels glanced up as if Xanthe had spoken to him. Xanthe pulled their hood lower and kept walking.

Cyrus came behind them, half a step back and to the left — the position of a shield-arm, though Xanthe doubted he knew he was doing it. He had been quiet since the charnel cave, since the fragments Xanthe had read out of his broken oath-stone. Quiet in the way of a man carrying something heavy and trying not to let it clank.

"You've been here before," he said. Not a question.

"I was dying here before." Xanthe's boots found the familiar path between stacked barrels, past the tallow-marked doorposts where smugglers left signs only other smugglers could read. "Fever, after a job went wrong in the passes. I was nineteen and stupid enough to take a commission without asking who wanted whom dead. Mother Quill bought the physician, the bed, and the three weeks it took me to stop hallucinating. She didn't do it for kindness."

"What did she do it for?"

"She told me she was investing." Xanthe's mouth twisted. "I told her investments mature. We agreed to disagree for nine years."

The hall opened around them all at once — a cavernous space where the salt warehouse's upper floor had been cut away, beams hung with drying fish and hanging lamps, tables scattered among the barrels like islands. And at the far end, enthroned on a chair made from the stern of a wrecked ship, sat Mother Quill.

She had not changed. That was the unsettling thing. Time touched everyone Xanthe had ever known — bent backs, silvered hair, softened faces — but Mother Quill seemed carved from the same material as her reputation, and neither weathered. She was small, narrow as a heron, with iron-gray hair bound in a net of knotted cord and eyes the pale gray of a winter sea. Rings covered her fingers to the second knuckle. Each one, Xanthe happened to know, had been taken from someone who had failed to pay her.

"Well," she said, and her voice carried across the hall without effort, the way a blade carries into a body. "The bone-carver comes home. Sit down before you fall down, child. You look like something the tide refused."

Xanthe sat. Cyrus remained standing until Quill's chin tipped toward him — *you too, knight* — and then he sat as well, careful, watchful, his broken oath-stone hidden inside his coat but Xanthe could feel him aware of it the whole time, the way a man is aware of a wound.

"Wine?" Quill asked.

"No."

"You'll want it by the end. Everyone wants wine by the end of talking to me." She poured two cups anyway, pushed one toward each of them, and drank from her own. "Nine years. You send letters like a man paying taxes — rarely, grudgingly, and only when forced. And now you walk into my hall with a temple knight at your heel and a look on your face I've seen twice before in my life. Both times, the person wearing it came to ask me for something impossible."

"The gods are awake," Xanthe said.

The hall did not go silent — smugglers were too professional for that — but the conversations nearest them thinned, the way birdsong stops when a hawk crosses the sky. Quill did not move at all.

"So I've heard." Her voice dropped to something meant only for their table. "Prayers answered in Thornfell. A shrine at Hollowmere speaking with a voice like a landslide. My people are frightened, and my people are never frightened, which means I am frightened twice over. Tell me what you know, carver, and mind you tell it true. I can smell a shaped story the way you smell rot."

So Xanthe told it. Not all of it — never all of it; that was how you survived — but most. The grave-gift and the dead woman's warning. Ashen-Who-Waits stirring after three hundred years of silence. The name spoken into the prayer-smoke, theirs and no one else's. The debt named in bone and firelight: a godling's soul, sold down into the court beneath the mountain, and a carver conscripted to fetch it home. They gave Cyrus only a glance when they reached him — *he has his own reasons; ask him if you dare* — and Quill, mercifully or cruelly, did not ask.

When Xanthe finished, Mother Quill sat turning one of her rings, slowly, around and around her finger. A habit, Xanthe remembered suddenly, from nine years ago. She did it when she was calculating.

"The court beneath the mountain," she said at last. "You understand what you're saying. There are four ways under Thornfell that I know of, and I know more than any living person. Three of them will kill you before you see daylight again. The fourth goes through my hands."

"I know."

"And you came to me anyway." She smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. It was the smile of a woman looking at a ledger. "That's either loyalty or desperation, and in my experience the two feel identical from the inside."

"Quill." Xanthe leaned forward. Nine years ago they had lain in this woman's house burning with fever, and she had sat beside them through the worst nights, reading ledgers aloud in her flat harbor-voice because she claimed the sound kept delirium at bay. Whether that was true or whether it was simply the only tenderness she knew how to perform, Xanthe had never decided. "I'm not here to pretend we're family. You saved my life and I've repaid what I could — the routes I mapped, the marks I carved for your runners, the grave-gifts nobody asks questions about. We're square, or near enough. What I need now isn't charity. Name your price."

Something flickered across the old woman's face — quick, quickly buried. Disappointment? Or its twin, relief? With Mother Quill it was impossible to say, and Xanthe suspected she cultivated the impossibility deliberately.

"Direct," she said. "Good. I hate haggling with people who think we're friends. Friends expect discounts." She folded her ringed hands on the table. "Here is my price, carver, and hear all of it before you flinch. Passage under the mountain costs more than coin, because coin is common and my passage is not. It costs a favor. Unspecified. Uncapped. To be called when I choose, wherever I choose, and honored without argument, appeal, or delay."

The hall's whispering lanterns seemed to lean closer.

"That's not a price," Cyrus said quietly. "That's a leash."

"It's exactly a leash," Quill agreed pleasantly, "and I've found leashes hold better than promises. Promises break. Leashes just chafe." Her pale eyes stayed on Xanthe. "Well?"

Xanthe looked at their hands on the table. A carver's hands, scarred at the knuckles, stained faintly at the nails with bone-dust that no amount of washing ever fully lifted. These hands had survived Thornfell's wars by belonging to no one. No faction, no oath, no debt that couldn't be paid off and forgotten. That was the whole architecture of their life: stay unclaimed, and nothing could spend you.

And now an old god had claimed them anyway, and a dead woman's last words had hooked behind their ribs like a fisherman's barb — *they sold her soul* — and somewhere beneath a mountain a child-godling waited in a box, screaming silently into rendered light, and the only road to it ran through the hands of a woman who collected favors the way other collectors gathered skulls.

*You always knew it would come to this,* said the fever-dead, a smuggler boy Xanthe had carved a grave-gift for eleven years ago, who sometimes still lingered near salt-water places. *Nobody stays unclaimed forever. Better to choose your chains.*

"Shut up," Xanthe murmured, and Quill raised an eyebrow, and Xanthe shook their head. "Not you."

"I hear voices too," Quill said. "Mine mostly talk about money." She turned her ring again. Once. Twice. "Decide, carver. The tide won't wait and neither will I. There's a gate-crew leaving for the Underway in two days, and if you miss it, the next window is a month out, and I don't think your god waits a month. Gods in a mood are worse than creditors."

Xanthe thought about refusing. They actually considered it — weighed it honestly, the way they weighed everything. Walk away. Find another road, another way under, some desperate climb through the deep galleries where even smugglers feared to tread. Maybe die in the dark. Maybe not. At least they'd die unowned.

But silence would be worse. That was the truth Ashen had built its bargain on, and it was the truth of Xanthe's whole life, if they were honest — every grave-gift carved, every warning buried, every night spent listening to the dead beg for justice they'd never get. Silence had never once made anything better. It had only made things quieter.

"One favor," Xanthe said. "Unspecified. Uncapped. Called when you choose." They held out their hand. "But hear this, Quill, and mark it, because I'll only say it once. If the favor you call would put the godling's soul back in a cage — if you'd sell what I bring up out of that mountain — I will break this bargain with my last breath, and you know I'm good at breaking things quietly."

Mother Quill took the offered hand. Her grip was dry and strong and cold as the stones of a harbor wall.

"Oh, child," she said softly. "I'd never sell it back into a cage." She smiled again, and this time there was something almost gentle in it, which was somehow worse. "I'd sell it *out* of one. To the free cities' councils, who are already sending agents sniffing at my doors, asking after relics and miracles. Do you know what a living god's soul is worth to men who've spent three centuries learning to pray to nothing? More than this harbor. More than the war. Enough to buy the free cities a year of armies — or to buy one council a crown over the others." She released Xanthe's hand. "But that's a conversation for when you come back up. If you come back up."

The lanterns whispered. Somewhere in their layered thin voices, Xanthe thought they caught the dead woman from Hollowmere again, saying something that might have been *fool* or might have been *forgiven*, and could not tell which.

"There's one more thing," Quill said, rising from her wreck-stern chair, rings clicking. "Since you're spending my goodwill so freely tonight, spend a little more. The Underway's third gate is watched these days — Vael Corvane's priests have bought eyes in every port south of the pass. They're looking for a bone-carver and a big man with a knight's shoulders. My crew can get you past the eyes, but not past the question. So you'll travel as mine. Salt-cloaks, harbor-marks, my colors stitched at the collar." She plucked two rough gray cloaks from a barrel beside her chair and dropped them on the table like a sentence being passed. "Anyone asks, you're salt-runners out of Grayharbor, and I'll swear to it myself, loudly and with witnesses. That part's free."

"Why?" Cyrus asked. His first words since entering. His voice was careful, the way a man walks on river ice. "Nothing about you is free."

Mother Quill looked at him for a long moment, and Xanthe saw something pass over her face that they had never seen there before — not softness exactly, but its shadow. Memory, maybe. Of a feverish nineteen-year-old, or of someone older and deader than that.

"Because thirty years ago," she said, "a god sold *me* something, and I paid, and I've wondered ever since whether I got the better end of the trade. Consider this me finding out." She turned away, dismissing them both with the ease of long practice. "Two days. Rest, eat, keep your heads down and your stone out of sight, knight. My people will come for you at slack water."

They walked out through the barrels and the whispering light, and the salt air hit Xanthe's face like a slap — clean, cold, honest, smelling of nothing but the sea. Behind them the hall hummed with its stolen souls. Ahead, the harbor lay black under a moon the color of scraped bone.

Cyrus walked beside them in silence for a long while. Then: "You didn't hesitate."

"I hesitated plenty. You just didn't see it."

"You gave her everything. An open favor, from a woman like that. You spent nine years making sure no one owned you, and you handed her the deed in under an hour." He stopped at the harbor rail, looking out at the black water. "Why?"

Xanthe gripped the rail. Below, the tide moved in the dark, patient as everything else that intended to collect.

"Because she saved my life," they said finally, "and I've spent nine years telling myself that debt was paid. It wasn't. Debts like that don't close, they just wait. And I'd rather pay her on my feet than owe her on my knees." A breath. "Besides. The godling's soul was sold by a priest who turned faith into currency. If I walk into that court to drag it free, I'd rather not arrive owing my passage to another kind of ledger. Now I do. So." They shrugged, and it cost more than the shrug showed. "Old loyalties surface at the worst times. That's what makes them old loyalties."

Cyrus said nothing to that. But he stood close, shoulder nearly touching theirs, the way he'd stood in the ambush on the pass — shield-side — and after a while Xanthe stopped pretending not to notice.

They were halfway back to the lodging-house when Xanthe felt it: a warmth against their chest, sudden and wrong, like a coal tucked inside their shirt. They stopped. Frowned. Patted through their layers and drew out the thing they had carried wrapped in oilcloth since Hollowmere — not their own carving, not a grave-gift, but the shard of shrine-bone Ashen had pressed into their palm when it named its debt. A splinter of the god itself, inert for weeks, dumb as driftwood.

It was glowing. Faintly, fitfully, like a lamp behind fog — and warm, warm as a living hand.

And along its edge, where yesterday there had been only smooth pale grain, fine lines had surfaced overnight, delicate as spider-silk, deliberate as script.

Someone — something — had begun writing to them.

"Cyrus," Xanthe said quietly. "We have a problem. The god doesn't want to wait two days."

Down in the black harbor, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure in temple-gray stood watching them from the end of the pier — still as a post, faceless in the dark, and not moving, and not leaving.
