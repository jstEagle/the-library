## 9. Beneath the Mountain

The gate was not a door. That was Xanthe's first thought, standing before it in the dark of Mother Quill's tunnel, lantern held high enough to catch the edges of the thing. It was a mouth — a wound in the mountain's flank where the stone had been cut away and replaced with something older, something that predated the cut. Bone, but not animal bone. The arch rose twenty feet and more, and every inch of its surface was carved: spirals within spirals, faces within faces, a thousand small figures climbing over one another toward some summit that never arrived.

Xanthe had heard about the Threshold all their life. Smugglers spoke of it the way sailors spoke of sea-serpents — with bravado they didn't feel. But hearing about it and standing beneath it were different things entirely, because Xanthe could hear it.

Not one voice. Not ten. A chorus so dense it had stopped being voices at all and become weather — a pressure against the inside of their skull, like standing too close to a waterfall. The dead in that arch were not whispering. They were screaming, or singing, or both, and they had been doing it for so long that the sound had worn grooves into the world.

"You feel it too." Cyrus stood beside them, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched theirs. He had gone pale beneath the road-dirt. His hand rested on the broken oath-stone at his belt, and Xanthe noticed — not for the first time since the charnel cave — that he touched it the way a man touches an old scar. Without thinking. Without wanting to.

"It's loud," Xanthe said, which was true and insufficient.

"Is it always like this?"

"No one comes back to say." They lifted the lantern higher. Beyond the arch, the passage plunged downward at a slope no cart could manage, and the light simply gave up after thirty feet, swallowed by a darkness that seemed less empty than patient. "Mother Quill said the toll is taken at the threshold. Coin won't serve."

"What serves?"

"Something you'd rather keep." Xanthe turned the words over as they said them, tasting the old smuggler warning. "A memory. A name. A grudge. The court below deals in what people are made of."

Cyrus's jaw tightened. Of everyone in the free cities, he was perhaps the one person who could least afford to lose anything else from inside himself. Xanthe saw him arrive at the same arithmetic and hate it.

"Then we go through fast," he said, "and hold on to everything."

"That's not how it works." Xanthe crouched at the base of the arch, where the bone met the living rock, and set down their pack. From it they drew three small carvings — grave-gifts, unfinished, blank-faced figures of river-stone white antler. They had made them on the road without quite admitting why. "But there are courtesies. Even among the dead. Especially among the dead."

They pressed their palm flat against the lowest face in the arch. It was a child's face, or something shaped like one, its eyes closed, its mouth open mid-cry or mid-song. The chorus surged against Xanthe's skull, and for a moment they let it in — really in, the way they hadn't dared since Hollowmere — and caught a single thread from the tangle.

*Cold,* said the voice behind the child-face. *So long cold. Who remembers our names?*

"They do," Xanthe murmured aloud, and set the first carving in a niche worn smooth by centuries of other offerings. "I remember them. I'll carry them up when I go."

The chorus did not quiet. But it shifted — the way a crowd shifts when a speaker takes a platform — and the pressure eased just enough that Xanthe could breathe fully again. They set the second and third carvings beside the first, then stood, knees cracking, and looked at Cyrus.

"Whatever asks you for something down there," they said, "answer politely. And lie if you have to. But don't offer anything freely. The court takes gifts as contracts."

"And you? What will you offer?"

"Nothing." Xanthe picked up the lantern. "I've already been conscripted. There's nothing left of me they can buy."

They stepped through together.

* * *

The descent took longer than it should have. That was the second wrongness: the slope was steady, the footing good, and yet hours passed and the air grew neither warmer nor colder, and when Xanthe counted their paces out of habit the numbers refused to add to any distance they could trust. The tunnel breathed around them. Once, far behind, they heard the threshold-arch sigh shut — not slam, not grind, just a soft closing like a book — and neither of them suggested going back to check.

Then the darkness ahead turned grey, and the grey turned gold, and they came out onto a ledge above the city.

Cyrus stopped walking so abruptly that Xanthe nearly collided with his back.

Below them, filling a cavern so vast its ceiling vanished into haze, lay the court beneath the mountain. Terraces of black stone descended in concentric rings toward a central spire, and every surface was lit — but not by fire. Lanterns hung from iron brackets along every stair and bridge, thousands of them, and each burned with a light that moved. Not flickered. *Moved*, swirling gently inside the glass like milk stirred into tea, gold and white and occasionally, horribly, a color that had no name above ground.

"Soul-light," Xanthe said quietly. Their voice came out flatter than they intended. "Rendered souls. That's what burns in those."

"I know the word." Cyrus's hand had found his oath-stone again. "From the temple texts. They called it the Second Harvest. We were taught it was a heresy that died out."

"It didn't die. It moved somewhere with better ventilation."

They took the switchback stairs down into the city, and the closer they came, the more the details resolved, and the worse the details became. The streets were clean — immaculately, unnaturally clean — and crowded. Courtiers in layered silks the color of bruises moved between colonnades; merchants cried their wares in a market square where the stalls sold thread, salt, ink, and, on a raised dais at the square's center, other things entirely. Xanthe made themselves look. Vitrines of smoked glass, and behind the glass, shapes. Some were the size of a fist. Some were smaller. One vitrine bore a placard written in a fine clerical hand: *Fresh griefs, unspent. Best quality. Inquire within.*

And the dead walked openly here. That was the thing that finally made Xanthe's skin crawl in earnest — not the lanterns, not the market, but the servants. Pale figures in plain grey livery moved through the crowds carrying trays, sweeping steps, holding doors, and the living passed through them without a glance, the way one passes through weather. Some of the dead wore collars of worked silver. Some wore chains of light that ran from their throats back to the nearest lantern-bracket, thin as spider silk, and where the chain entered the lantern the soul-fire swirled faster.

"They're fuel," Cyrus said. His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "The ones in the chains. When the lanterns dim—"

"Yes."

He was staring at a dead woman kneeling on a terrace above, scrubbing stone that was already spotless, her chain running taut to a bracket overhead. Her face was peaceful. That was somehow the worst part. Whatever rendering did to them, it took the suffering out first, and left something serene and hollow that did its work forever.

"My order burned cities over rumors of this practice," Cyrus said. "Whole garrisons marched on rumor alone. And it was real. It was all real, under the mountain, while we policed the surface for lesser sins." He laughed once, short and bitter, without humor. "Maybe they erased my memory to spare me the shame of it."

"Or to use you," Xanthe said. "Shame makes obedient soldiers."

That landed. He flinched, just slightly, and said nothing, and they went on down.

The oath-stone began to warm outside a shrine.

Xanthe felt it before Cyrus did — felt it the way one feels a hearth across a room — because the dead near it reacted. They were crossing a colonnade lined with memorial niches, hundreds of them, each holding a carved token or a pinch of ash behind glass, when the dead servant tending the niches went still. She was an old man, actually, grey-livered and grey-faced, dusting the glass with a cloth that could not have improved anything. He stopped mid-motion. Turned his head. Looked directly at Cyrus with eyes like frosted glass, and then bowed — low, deliberate, the bow one gives a superior.

Cyrus froze. "What—"

"Don't stop walking," Xanthe breathed. "Don't speak to it."

But the old dead man straightened and spoke anyway, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. "*The stone remembers.*" His frosted eyes never left Cyrus's belt. "*It was forged in the deep gallery. It has come home.*"

Cyrus's hand closed over the oath-stone, and Xanthe saw him feel it then — the warmth, unmistakable now, radiating through his glove like a held coal. "It's temple-made," he said hoarsely. "It was blessed in the High Sanctum at Corveth. On the surface. I know because I—" He stopped. His face did something complicated. "I don't know how I know. But I know."

"*Forged in the deep gallery,*" the dead man repeated, with the patience of a thing that had said the same sentence for a hundred years, and returned to his dusting as though they had never existed.

They walked on. The stone grew warmer with every street. By the time they reached the terraces near the spire — where Mother Quill's letter of credit would open the guest-wing of a minor house, and from there, if the gods were kind and the smugglers honest, a route into the soul-market itself — the oath-stone was hot enough that Cyrus had wrapped it in a scarf, and the scarf was beginning to smell faintly of scorching.

At the guest-house door, while a living steward with ink-stained fingers examined Mother Quill's seal, Cyrus leaned close to Xanthe.

"Something down here knows it," he whispered. "Knows it better than I do. Better than the temple ever did."

"I noticed."

"If it recognizes the stone, it might recognize me. What I was. What happened." His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it with a cough. "I came down here for your god's errand. But I'm starting to think my answers are in the same place."

Xanthe looked at him — this big, battered, half-empty man who had fought like a legend in the pass and remembered none of it — and felt the old reflex rise, the one that said *trust no one, owe no one, survive*. And beneath it, quieter, the newer thing. The thread from the charnel cave. The fragments of massacre that pointed away from his guilt like signposts.

"The deep gallery," they said. "Wherever that is, we'll find it. After the reliquary."

"After," Cyrus agreed.

The steward cleared his throat and swung the door wide, bowing them into warmth and lamplight and the smell of rendered souls. "Welcome to the court beneath the mountain," he said pleasantly. "Your rooms are prepared. Supper is at the ninth bell. And sirs — a word of advice, freely given, as house custom requires." He smiled with genuine kindness. "Whatever dreams come to you tonight, do not answer them aloud. Things listen here that take answering as invitation."

Above the steward's shoulder, down the lamplit corridor, a dead servant in grey livery stood motionless, watching Cyrus's wrapped belt with frosted eyes.

And deep beneath the city, in a gallery no map admitted to, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to remember the shape of a stone it had forged three hundred years ago — and the name of the knight it had sworn it to.
