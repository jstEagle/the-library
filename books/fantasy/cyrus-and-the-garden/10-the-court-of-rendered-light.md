## 10. The Court of Rendered Light

The market opened its mouths at dusk.

That was how Xanthe thought of it, standing at the edge of the great cavern while Mother Quill's escort melted back into the tunnel behind them — that the whole court beneath the mountain was a mouth, and the market its throat, and everything that passed down it would be swallowed in good time.

Lanterns hung from the vaulted dark on chains of black iron, and they did not burn oil or wick. They burned *light itself*, drawn slow and steady out of what was sealed inside their glass bellies — rendered souls, refined to a pale gold shimmer, each one dimming by fractions over decades until nothing remained but a smudge of grey ash and a lantern that no longer glowed. Xanthe had heard about such things all their life, in the whispers of smugglers who'd gone deep enough to come back changed. They had never believed the half of it.

They believed it now. Every lantern sang.

Not with voices — not exactly. The dead spoke to Xanthe through carved things, through worked bone and stone and wood, through anything shaped by a human hand for the purpose of holding memory. Lantern glass was not carved. But the souls inside were still *souls*, and souls made sound whether Xanthe wanted them to or not. It came to them as a pressure behind the eyes, a low continuous keening like wind through a field of hollow reeds. Hundreds of lanterns. Thousands. The cavern stretched farther than Hollowmere's entire valley, tiered terraces descending into gloom, stalls of bone and lacquer crowding every level, and above it all that choir of diminishing light.

Xanthe pressed two fingers hard against their temple and breathed through their nose until the keening settled into something bearable.

"You look as though you're about to be sick," Cyrus murmured at their shoulder. He had traded his knight's surcoat for a smuggler's drab wool in the last waystation, but he could not trade his posture; he stood like a man built to be seen, and in this place that would get him killed twice over.

"I'm fine." A lie. "Stay close. Don't touch anything. Don't meet anyone's eyes longer than a breath. And if someone asks you your name, give them one that isn't yours."

"And what do I do if they ask what we're buying?"

Xanthe started down the first terrace stair. "We're browsing."

* * *

The soul-market had no smell of fish or fruit or smoke. Its smells were stranger: cold stone, tallow, something sweetish and wrong like fruit left too long in a sealed room. The stalls sold grief in every denomination. Vials of last breaths, stoppered and waxed, priced by the virtue of the breather. Rings of finger-bone that let a widow dream of her husband once a season, no more — the sellers were scrupulous about dosage, because a customer who overdrew her dead would come back hollow and buy nothing ever again. Shelves of small carved animals, each one holding a child's short life, sold to grandparents who wished to hear laughter in the house again.

And everywhere, the currency. Not coin. The dead themselves walked the aisles as tokens of account — bound spirits wearing bodies of stitched shadow, chained at the wrist to money-changers' tables, weighed and valued and lent against. A farmer's ghost might fetch a winter's grain. A soldier's, trained and angry, three times that. Xante watched a woman pay for a vial of her mother's voice with six years of her grandfather's shade, and watched the money-changer file the ghost away under the counter like a ledger book, and felt something in their chest go cold and hard as river ice.

*This is what the world is,* said the dead in the shelves around them, softly, without malice, which was somehow worse. *This is what hands do.*

"Xanthe." Cyrus's voice, very low. "There's a stall selling oath-stones."

They turned. In an alcove off the second terrace, behind a curtain of grey silk, a dealer displayed broken vows the way other merchants displayed jewels: on black velvet, pinned with silver wire. Sword-oaths. Marriage oaths. An entire tray of temple oath-stones, cracked clean through, each one silent to everyone in the world except — Xanthe realized with a lurch — potentially to them.

Cyrus's hand went to his chest, where his own broken stone hung wrapped in linen beneath his shirt. His face had gone the colour of the lantern-light.

"Don't," Xanthe said quietly. "Whatever you're thinking. Not here. If you sell it, it's gone, and whatever's left inside it goes with it."

"It's worthless. It's broken. It's been broken since—" He stopped. Since before he could remember, his eyes finished for him.

"Broken isn't empty." Xanthe took his elbow and drew him away from the alcove, feeling through their palm the tremor he was trying to hide. "I've told you. When we're out of here, when there's fire and quiet, I'll read it properly. Not in a market where every third soul is owned by someone who'd pay well to know what a temple knight forgot."

He let himself be led. That was new. Three weeks ago Cyrus Amberdale's opposite number would have dug his heels in at a word like *properly* and demanded answers at knifepoint if need be. Somewhere between the ambush in the passes and the charnel cave, he had begun to trust them — and Xanthe, whose entire survival had been built on trusting no one, found they did not know what to do with that weight except carry it carefully and resent it a little.

* * *

They found the reliquary district at the bottom of everything, past a gate of femurs lashed together with iron, where the light ran older and yellower and the keening changed key.

Mother Quill had given them the name in salt and smoke: *Ask for the Vault of Quiet Things. Tell the keeper Quill remembers the interest.* Even so, getting through required an hour of bartering Xanthe would remember with distaste for years — a carved grave-gift from their own kit, offered as a token of craft; a promise witnessed by a notary-ghost with inkwell eyes; and finally the surrender of the bone needle Xanthe had used for twenty years, the first tool their master had ever put in their hand. The keeper weighed the needle in his palm, looked at Xanthe with sudden, unpleasant attention, and said, "You were taught by the Amberdale line," and let them in without further charge.

Which should have been the warning. Xanthe knew that later, walking out. At the time they were too busy keeping their feet under them in the Vault of Quiet Things.

It was smaller than the market above, and colder, and utterly silent — which for Xanthe meant it roared. Here were kept the great containers, the reliquaries that held not fragments and echoes but whole souls, whole lives, sealed in vessels of carved bone worth more than the free cities' annual grain harvest. Each vessel sat alone in a niche lit by a single rendered-soul lamp, and each one *screamed*, silently, in the register only Xanthe could hear. A queen who had been entombed standing up. A general. A poet whose patron had preferred owning her to reading her.

And in the central aisle, on a plinth of its own, apart from the others as though even the keepers sensed it was different — a reliquary the size of a bread loaf, carved from a single piece of some pale wood that was not wood at all but ancient whalebone, yellowed with centuries.

The moment Xanthe came within ten paces of it, the screaming stopped.

What replaced it stopped their heart.

A child was singing. Wordless, tuneless, the way children sing when they think no one is listening — a little wandering melody that doubled back on itself, picked up a phrase, dropped it, laughed somewhere in the middle of a note. The godling. Ashen-Who-Waits's stolen child. Three hundred years in a box, and still singing to itself, still finding ways to play.

Xanthe stood in the aisle with tears running down their face before they noticed they were crying.

"Remarkable piece," said the keeper, materializing at their shoulder with the softness of long practice. "Pre-Conquest work. The provenance is disputed — the free cities claim it was looted from a shrine, the mountain claims it was lawfully purchased. Both are lies, of course. Everything old enough is built on lies." He tilted his head. "You're hearing it sing. I can always tell. The ones who can't hear stand there looking clever. You look like a man at his mother's grave."

"What is the price?" Xanthe managed.

"For that? There is no price. It is not for sale. It is held in trust." The keeper smiled with too many teeth. "In trust for parties who will make use of it when the time is right. You may look. Looking is free."

So Xanthe looked. And because they were a carver, and because their hands understood other hands across any distance of years, they stepped closer than courtesy allowed and bent over the reliquary's surface, and read the carving.

It was magnificent. Whorls of waiting figures — the iconography of Ashen's cult, the seated patient shapes, the long horizontals that meant vigil — cut into the whalebone with a control that made Xanthe's fingers ache with envy. Border-work of sleeping moths. A lid carved as a closed eye. Whoever had made this had loved the godling. That was the horror of it; the love was in every stroke. No one cuts tenderness into a prison unless they hate what they've been made to do.

And then the light shifted as Xanthe leaned, catching the lower band of the carving where the maker had signed the work — as makers did, as Xanthe signed their own pieces, a small mark tucked into the whorl of a moth's wing —

— and the mark was a sparrow holding a needle in its beak, standing on a crescent moon.

Xanthe knew that mark the way they knew their own pulse.

Master Aldous Amberdale had cut that sign into eleven thousand pieces of bone and wood and horn over sixty years. Had taught Xanthe to cut it, laughing, saying *always hide your name where only another carver would think to look, child — the world punishes honest signatures*. Had died eight winters ago in a fever-hut in Hollowmere with Xanthe holding his hand, poor, beloved, stubborn, honest Aldous, who had refused three offers from rich patrons because he said a carver who served gold would soon serve whoever counted it.

His mark was on the cage of a stolen god.

"No," Xanthe said aloud. The keeper raised his eyebrows. "No. This isn't his work. Someone copied his mark. Counterfeit. It happens—"

"The piece predates your birth by a century, carver." The keeper's tone had cooled by degrees. "The signature is original to the cutting. I have handled this object for forty years. I know forgery the way you know your own teeth."

*A century.* The arithmetic turned over slowly, horribly, in Xanthe's mind. The godling was taken three hundred years ago, at the same hour the old gods went silent — that was the story Ashen had told them through the shrine-bone, the debt named in the dark. But reliquaries could be recarved. Reused. Resold. A vessel that old might have carried many souls before this one. Perhaps Master Aldous had carved it for some other purpose entirely, some innocent commission, and never known what it would finally hold—

But even as they reached for that mercy, their own training took it away. Carvers didn't reuse reliquaries. The whole craft rested on it: a vessel was cut *for* its occupant, tuned to one soul the way a lock was cut to one key. Master Aldous had drilled it into them before they could write their own name. *You will never carve a vessel blind, Xanthe. Never carve for a soul you haven't listened to. A misfit vessel grinds a spirit down to nothing in a decade, and that is a sin no penance pays.*

He had carved this vessel for this soul. He had listened to the godling sing. And then he had sealed it shut.

*They sold her soul.* The dead woman in Hollowmere, in the grave-gift, the first warning Xanthe had buried and refused. *They sold her soul.* Xanthe had assumed *they* meant the usurper's priests, the mountain court, the usual monsters. It had never once occurred to them that the sentence could have a carver's hands in the middle of it.

Their hands. Their inheritance. Every skill Xanthe owned had been handed down from the man who built this cage.

The keening of the market above seemed suddenly very far away, and very loud.

"Carver." The keeper's voice came from a distance. "You've gone grey. If you're going to be ill, do it in the aisle, not on my plinth."

"I'm fine." Their own voice sounded like someone else's. "How long has it been held here?"

"Sealed and catalogued eighty-one years ago. Sold to us by—" the keeper consulted a ledger that appeared from nowhere, "—a broker operating out of Saltmarch. Records list him as an agent of the Corvane estate."

Vael. Even here, the priest's name coiled through everything, patient as root.

Cyrus appeared at Xanthe's side — Xanthe hadn't heard him approach, or perhaps the singing had swallowed everything — and put a steadying hand between their shoulders. He didn't ask what was wrong. He simply read their face the way he had learned to in three weeks of bad roads, and moved half a step closer to the keeper, planting himself like a wall.

"We're done looking," Cyrus said.

"Yes," Xanthe whispered. "We're done."

They walked out of the Vault of Quiet Things on legs that belonged to someone else. Behind them the godling went on singing to itself in the dark, small and unbroken, still playing, still hoping — and Xanthe understood now why the god Ashen had chosen *them*, of all the carvers in all the world, to collect this debt.

Not because they were skilled.

Because the debt ran through their own bloodline, and the gods, whatever else they were, kept immaculate accounts.

At the mouth of the femur-gate, the keeper's notary-ghost drifted close and murmured, in a voice like wet paper:

"The priest knows you've come, bone-hearer. Vael Corvane sends his regards. He requests an audience — tonight, if it pleases you. He says he has information about your master that you will want to hear."

Xanthe closed their eyes.

Of course he did. The man sold memories and bought silence; he would already know exactly which wound to press.

"Tell him," Xanthe said, opening their eyes onto the lantern-dark, "we'll come."
