## 11. Audience with the Usurper's Priest

The summons came at the hour when even the lanterns seemed to sleep.

Cyrus had been counting them again — the lights of the court beneath the mountain, each one a small white flame that burned without oil or wick, steady as a held breath. He had learned what fed them on his second night below. A servant with eyes like smoked glass had told him plainly, the way one tells a guest which door leads to the privy: *rendered souls, ser knight. The bright ones burn longest.* Then she had bowed and gone, and Cyrus had stood in the corridor a long while, watching a lamp burn and thinking: *someone is in there.*

Now the same servant stood in the doorway of the guest cell they had given him — comfortable, gilded, inescapable — and said, "The Lord Corvane will see you."

Not High Priest. Not His Radiance. *Lord Corvane*, as though the title were a courtesy extended to a man rather than an office owed to a god's thief.

Xanthe was two corridors away, asleep or pretending to be, their bone-carved tokens sewn into the lining of their coat. They had argued about this audience for three days — Xanthe wanting to refuse it entirely, Cyrus knowing that refusal below the mountain was simply a slower form of being taken. In the end they had agreed to go separately, so that whatever Vael Corvane learned from one of them could not be used to unravel the other. It was the first plan they had made together that felt like trust, and Cyrus hated how little he trusted it himself.

He followed the servant down stairs that spiraled inward, deeper than the guest levels, past galleries where bone reliquaries stood ranked like soldiers at attention. His oath-stone hung against his chest, and it was warm — warmer than it had been in the smuggler's road, warmer than in the charnel cave where Xanthe had read it with shaking hands. Down here it was almost hot, a coal banked behind his sternum, and every few steps it gave a faint pulse, like something knocking from inside.

*Something down here recognizes it,* Xanthe had said. Cyrus was beginning to suspect the something recognized *him*.

The doors at the bottom were black wood banded in pale metal — bone-iron, he thought, though he did not know how he knew the name of it. The servant knocked once, softly, and the doors opened on a room that stole the breath from him.

It was a chapel. That was the horror of it — not a throne room, not a counting-house of souls, but a chapel, beautiful the way only chapels know how to be. Vaulted ceiling ribbed like the inside of a shell. A floor of dark stone polished to mirror-depth. And light everywhere, soft and sourceless, the same white as the lanterns above, except here it moved — drifting in slow currents through the air like snow falling upward, like dust motes dancing to music no one else could hear.

Cyrus knew what the light was. He understood, standing on the threshold, that he was breathing it.

At the far end, before an altar of uncarved stone, a man knelt in prayer.

Vael Corvane did not look up. He let the silence stretch, and Cyrus recognized the technique because he had been trained in it himself — by whom, he could not remember, but his body remembered, settling into parade rest without his permission, weight even, hands loose, eyes forward. Let the visitor wait. Let them fill the silence with their own guilt. He had done it a hundred times; he could feel the hundred times sitting in his muscles like sediment.

So instead of waiting, he looked around the chapel, and he read it the way Xanthe read carved bone.

There were names here. Not written — *incised*, cut fine as spider silk into the black floor stones, thousands upon thousands of them, radiating outward from the altar in concentric rings. He crouched, ignoring protocol, and touched one. The stone was warm under his fingertip, and the warmth pulsed in time with his own oath-stone.

*These aren't memorials,* he realized. *They're ledgers.*

"You read quickly," said Vael Corvane. "That's good. I remember that about you."

Cyrus rose and turned. The High Priest had come to his feet without sound — a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-haired, robed in white so deep it drank the drifting light. His face was kind. That was the second horror. He had expected cruelty, and instead he saw the face of a grandfather at a harvest feast, lined and gentle, eyes the color of weak tea.

"I don't know you," Cyrus said.

"No." Vael smiled, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "That was the point of everything, Ser Cyrus. Come. Sit. You've been walking my halls for three days wondering why no one has thrown you out. You deserve an answer."

There were two chairs before the altar, plain wooden things, absurdly domestic amid all that incised darkness. Vael sat in one and gestured to the other, and Cyrus — because refusing would tell the man more than sitting would — sat.

"You know my name," Cyrus said.

"I gave you your name." Vael folded his hands. "Well. I signed the document that erased the old one. Close enough, after twenty years. Do you know what an erasure costs? Temple resources, temple will, temple *mercy*. We do not do it lightly. An erasure means a man's whole record is struck — his vows, his crimes, his commendations, the faces of everyone who loved him. It means starting the poor wretch over from nothing, with a new name and a hole where his history was." He tilted his head. "You've lived in that hole for two decades, Cyrus. Tell me. What does it feel like?"

"Like a tooth," Cyrus heard himself say. "Missing. You keep finding the gap with your tongue."

Something flickered across the old man's face — not quite pain. Recognition. "Yes," Vael said quietly. "I imagine it does." He leaned back. "You want to know why. Every disgraced knight wants to know why. You've spent years chasing rumors of it — the massacre at Saint Verren's ford, the burning of the hospice, the children. You've been told you did it. You've half believed it yourself, because a man can live with being a monster more easily than living with being a blank page. Am I close?"

Cyrus's hands had gone still in his lap. Very still. "Say what you brought me here to say."

"The truth, then, since you ask so bluntly." Vael's voice dropped, went confessional, warm as a hearth. "You didn't do it, Cyrus. You *saw* it. You were there at the ford when the temple's own blade-work opened those gates, and you were too young and too honest to hold your tongue about what you witnessed. So we took the memory out and put the crime in its place, and we sent you away to live out your life as a cautionary tale with a missing tooth." He spread his hands, almost apologetic. "That is the truth of you. All of it. Yours for the asking."

The chapel was very quiet. The drifting light moved between them like slow fish.

"Why," Cyrus said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Underneath the levelness something was screaming, and he made himself listen to it without obeying it. "Why show me this now?"

"Because you're here, and because I am not a wasteful man." Vael rose and walked to the altar, trailing his fingers along its uncarved surface. "Twenty years ago you were a problem to be solved. Today you are an opportunity, and I have learned — believe me, I have learned, at great cost — that opportunities should never be buried alive." He turned. "The woman. Amberdale. The bone-listener. She carries something out of my gallery within the week unless someone stops her, and she will stop for no argument, no threat, no bribe. She hears the dead, they say. The dead adore her." His tea-colored eyes settled on Cyrus, mild and patient. "But the dead cannot pay a man's debts. I can."

"There it is," Cyrus said.

"There it is," Vael agreed pleasantly. "Bring me word of where she hides the reliquary — or better, bring me the reliquary itself — and I will give you back everything. Every name. Every face. Your mother's voice. Your first vow. The taste of the bread you ate the morning you were knighted. And the truth entire, unedited: what happened at the ford, who ordered it, and why a young knight with honest eyes was chosen to carry the blame." He stepped closer, and his kindness did not waver. "You have spent your whole adult life starving, Cyrus. I am offering you a meal. All I ask is that you hand me the plate she's carrying."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk out of here with your tooth-gap and your blank pages, and in six days the court takes the reliquary back anyway, and Amberdale dies badly, and you spend another twenty years wondering." Vael shrugged, a small, tired motion. "I lose nothing. That is the advantage of being what I am."

Cyrus looked at his hands. They were steady. He noted that with a strange detachment, the way he'd noted his body remembering parade rest — evidence of training, evidence of a man he had been, a man whose whole shape was locked behind the erasure like furniture in a flooded house.

He tried to think of Xanthe and found, uncomfortably, that he could. That was the trap's flaw, or perhaps its honesty: Vael was not asking him to betray a stranger. Three weeks ago Xanthe Amberdale had been a smuggler's guide with sharp eyes and sharper silences. But he had watched them bargain passage from dead legionnaires with nothing but their own voice. He had watched them sit all night in a charnel cave reading his shame off a broken stone, and when the evidence said *he was framed*, they had not comforted him — they had said, flatly, *evidence lies. People lie easier. Don't make me believe you yet.* And then they had kept watch over his sleep anyway.

They had never once asked him to prove himself innocent. Only to prove himself *here*.

And Vael Corvane was offering him the one thing in the world he wanted, priced in exactly that.

"You're good at this," Cyrus said at last. "The kindness. Was that in the original design, or did the office teach you?"

Something moved behind the tea-colored eyes — the first true thing Cyrus had seen in the man's face. "The office," Vael said. "The kindness was mine. That's rather the tragedy of both of us, isn't it?" He returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands, patient as stone. "You needn't answer now. I'll give you until the third bell tomorrow. The servant will find you." He paused, and added, almost gently: "Ask her, while you decide, what her name was before she came below. Ask any of them. Then ask yourself what a man is worth, who can be bought with the promise of his own self."

Cyrus stood. His legs carried him to the doors on the old drilled certainty, and it was only at the threshold that he stopped.

"One question," he said, not turning around. "The light in this room. The lamps upstairs. Whose souls are burning?"

Behind him, a long silence. When Vael answered, the warmth had gone out of his voice, and what remained was worse — administrative, tidy, the voice of a ledger.

"Debts, mostly. Criminals, heretics, the condemned. No one you'd have known." A pause. "No one you'd have *remembered*."

Cyrus walked out. The doors closed behind him without a sound.

* * *

He did not go back to his cell. His feet took him up, and up, through the guest corridors, past the ranked reliquaries, until he stood in the long gallery where the soul-lanterns burned in their hundreds along the vaulted ceiling, and he stopped beneath them and looked up and counted, because counting was the only prayer he knew.

Three hundred. Four hundred. Each one steady. Each one someone.

His oath-stone was burning against his chest now, hot enough that he fished it out and held it in his palm — the broken half-stone, the crack running through the old order's sigil, and beneath his fingers, for the first time since Thornfell, he felt it *move*. A tremor. A pulse answering the pulse of the incised floor far below, the ledger of names, the chapel full of drifting light.

And threaded through the heat, faint as a voice through a wall, came something that was not memory and not imagination: a fragment of cadence, a rhythm of speech, the ghost-shape of words he had once said aloud with his hand on that stone. He could not recover the words. But he could feel their *weight* — the weight of a vow taken in daylight, in front of witnesses, by a young man who believed the temple was the world's best hope.

*They made me swear to protect people from exactly this,* he thought. *And then they made me the thing people needed protecting from. Or they made me the witness. One of the two. And either way—*

Either way, Vael Corvane had just shown him the price list, and the item for sale was himself.

"Cyrus."

He turned. Xanthe stood at the mouth of the gallery, wrapped in their coat, bone-tokens glinting faintly at their collar where the lining had shifted. Their face in the lantern-light was unreadable — but their eyes went from his open hand, to the hot stone in it, to his face, and narrowed.

"You met him," Xanthe said. Not a question.

"He knows what happened at the ford," Cyrus said. "All of it. He says I didn't do it. He says he can give it back — every year, every name, my mother's voice." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He's not lying, Xanthe. That's the worst part. I know a lie when I'm sold one, and this isn't one. It's real. It's all real, and it's mine, and he's holding it out to me like bread."

Xanthe came closer, slowly, the way one approaches a spooked animal. "What's the price?"

Cyrus looked up at the lanterns. Four hundred small white flames, burning on rendered souls, and somewhere among the ranks of them — he felt it suddenly, with sick certainty — were faces from a ford he could not remember, a hospice he could not mourn, a crime with his name signed to it in a hand he had never held.

"He wants the reliquary," Cyrus said. "And he wants me to buy my past with your future." He closed his fist around the burning stone. "I told him I'd answer by third bell."

Xanthe was quiet a moment. Then, dry as bone dust: "And what did you tell him *now*?"

Cyrus opened his hand and looked at the oath-stone, pulsing in the lantern-light like a small stubborn heart.

"I told him the truth," he said. "I just haven't figured out yet whether it was the truth he wanted to hear — or the truth that gets us both killed by morning. Because Xanthe — the stone's waking up. And whatever it remembers—" he lifted his head toward the deep places of the mountain, where the ledger of names lay incised in the dark, "—it remembers *him*."

Far below them, faint through miles of stone, a bell began to toll. Not the third bell. Something older. And every lantern in the gallery flickered at once, as if the souls inside had all turned their heads.
