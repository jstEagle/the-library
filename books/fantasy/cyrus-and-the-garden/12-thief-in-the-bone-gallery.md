## 12. Thief in the Bone Gallery

The Bone Gallery did not sleep so much as it listened.

Xanthe had known that from the moment they crossed its threshold — a corridor of rib-arched vaulting that ran the length of a fallen god's femur, walls paneled in femurs and scapulae and skulls arranged in patterns too deliberate to be decoration. Every court beneath the mountain displayed its wealth in bone; this gallery was the jewel case. And like any jewel case, it had locks.

The trouble with locks made of the dead was that Xanthe could hear them.

They moved along the eastern wall in the shadow of a rendered-soul lantern, its light the color of drowning, and kept their breathing shallow and their thoughts shallower. Cyrus walked three paces behind, his boots wrapped in wool against the marble, his broken oath-stone cupped in one palm like a coal he was afraid to drop. It had been warm since they passed the threshold. Warmer now, Xanthe thought, than stone had any right to be.

*There,* signed the smuggler's hand-code they'd taught him on the road: *third alcove.*

The reliquary sat where Mother Quill's maps said it would sit — though Quill's maps were worth exactly what you paid for them and no more, which tonight was everything. A case of black glass on a plinth of vertebrae, and inside it, faint as a candle behind fog, a light that was not light at all. The godling. Ashen-Who-Waits's stolen child, sold away by priests who priced souls like spice.

Xanthe could hear it. That was the horror of it. Not the dead woman's voice, not the whisper of grave-gifts — but something younger, thinner, a sound like a child humming to itself in an empty house because no one had told it yet that it was alone.

*Don't listen,* Xanthe told themselves. *Take it and go.*

Their fingers closed around the glass.

The gallery woke all at once.

It was not sound. It was the absence of sound — the constant low murmur of the dead that Xanthe lived inside like a fish lives inside water suddenly going still, every voice in the walls stopping mid-word, the way a forest stops when the predator enters. Then the lanterns guttered, all of them, down to embers of drowned blue, and the bones began to move.

Skulls turned on their shelves. Ribs flexed like the gills of some vast submerged thing. In the alcoves flanking the reliquary, figures assembled themselves out of the paneling — guardians stitched together from a dozen donors each, tall as doorframes, their joints clicking into place with the patience of things that had waited centuries and could wait centuries more.

Cyrus drew steel without a whisper of sound. He'd wrapped the scabbard throat in leather weeks ago, back on the smuggler's road, and Xanthe had never asked why a man who claimed to remember nothing about fighting knew to do that.

"Passage," Xanthe said aloud, because there was nothing left to lose by speaking. Their voice came out cracked, rusted with disuse — they spoke to the living so rarely. "I'm a carver. I speak for the dead. I ask passage."

The nearest guardian tilted its head. Its skull was old, river-stained, the teeth worn flat by a lifetime of grain bread. A farmer, once. Or a farmer's wife. Someone whose hands had known soil.

And then it answered, in a chorus, because the guardians were not one dead thing but many — dozens of voices braided through borrowed throats, and every one of them spoke directly into the place behind Xanthe's eyes where the hearing lived:

***WHO SPEAKS FOR US?***

"I do," Xanthe said. "Xanthe Amberdale of Hollowmere. Carver of grave-gifts. I have given the dead their last words for twenty years." They swallowed. Lying to the living was easy; lying to the dead was like lying in front of your own mother. "I didn't come to steal from *you*. What I take was taken first."

***THE CHILD-LIGHT,*** said the chorus. ***IT SINGS TO US AT NIGHT. IT HAS SUNG FOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS AND NO ONE HAS EVER ANSWERED.***

Something in Xanthe's chest folded over. They looked at the reliquary in their hands, at the small imprisoned glow, and understood that the guardians were not guarding it. They were keeping vigil. No one had ever explained the difference to them.

"Its father sent me," Xanthe said. "Ashen-Who-Waits. It waits at a shrine in Hollowmere, above ground, under open sky. I'm taking the child home."

Silence. The long, weighing silence of the dead, who had heard every promise ever made and believed approximately none of them.

Then, from deeper in the gallery, from the direction of the great doors they had entered by — bells. Not temple bells. Alarm-bells, rung by living hands. Vael Corvane knew.

***THE PRIEST COMES,*** said the chorus, and there was something in it that Xanthe had never heard from the dead before and would spend years afterward trying to name. Fear. The dead were afraid. ***HE HAS OUR NAMES IN HIS LEDGERS. HE CAN UNMAKE US.***

"Then let me carry your names out with me," Xanthe said, the bargain forming even as the words left them, the way bargains always formed in the bone-lit dark — fast, half-made, dangerous. "Every name in this gallery. I'll carve them. Grave-gifts, proper ones, above ground where his ledgers can't reach. That's my price for passage. Not coin. Names."

The guardians looked at one another — a terrible, slow consultation of borrowed skulls.

Behind Xanthe, Cyrus said quietly, "Whatever they answer, answer fast. Boots. Lots of them."

***

Cyrus counted eleven sets of boots and stopped counting, because counting was a luxury and the corridor was narrow and the first of Vael's guards were already rounding the far arch.

He had told himself, since Thornfell, that whatever skill lived in his hands belonged to someone else — some other man, some knight of a story he'd been erased from. He had held that belief the way you hold a rail over a long drop. In the smuggler's passes, ambushed by outriders, the belief had begun to crack. Here, in the blue-dark of the Bone Gallery with the dead watching and the priest's soldiers coming, it shattered entirely, because there was no one else left to be. There was only his body, and his body *knew*.

The first guard came high, sword rising for a killing cut — and Cyrus was already inside the arc, left hand trapping the wrist, right hand driving the pommel up beneath the jaw with a crack he felt in his own teeth. The man dropped. Cyrus took the sword out of the air before it finished falling.

*Second position of the Seventh Form,* said a voice in his memory that was his own and was not, calm as scripture. *Let them commit. The committed blade cannot follow.*

He had learned forms once. He had learned them in a courtyard with white gravel, from masters whose names were gone, beside students whose faces were gone, and every hour of that training lived in him still, sealed under the wound in his mind like water under ice. Now the ice was breaking and the memories came up through the cracks in pieces — never whole, never kind.

A spearman next, and Cyrus turned the thrust off his own blade's forte, stepped through, elbowed the man into the wall hard enough to crack bone that had nothing to do with the gallery. *Footwork before fury.* Somewhere a master had said that. Somewhere a courtyard. White gravel gone red —

No. Not yet. Not that memory. He shoved it down and it went, sullen, and he hated how easily his mind obeyed, hated whoever had built these locked rooms inside him.

"Left!" Xanthe shouted from the alcove.

He went left without thinking, which was itself an answer to a question he'd been asking for months. Two more guards in the cross-corridor, and between them — worse — a thing of bone and wire and rendered light, one of the court's soul-bound servitors, moving with the wrong smoothness of the puppeted dead. His oath-stone flared hot against his chest, and through the stone, absurdly, he felt *recognition*, like a dog greeting a soldier of its old regiment.

The guards he could handle. He handled them — parry, step, break, the Seventh Form unfolding in his limbs like a letter finally opened after years in the post. But the servitor reached for him with hands that had been someone's mother's hands, and Cyrus hesitated, and hesitation in a corridor is death.

Bone saved him. One of the gallery's guardians — the farmer-skulled one, or another wearing the same chorus — came out of the wall like a wave coming out of the sea and took the servitor apart with a tenderness that was worse than violence, cradling it, unthreading it, laying its stolen bones down gently on the marble.

***GO,*** the chorus said, to both of them. ***WE HAVE DECIDED.***

"Decided what?" Cyrus gasped, dragging Xanthe toward the service tunnel mouth Xanthe had marked on the way in.

***THAT WE WERE NEVER ASKED WHAT WE WANTED. THREE HUNDRED YEARS OF STANDING IN THIS ROOM AND NO ONE ASKED.*** The skull turned toward Xanthe, and the drowned-blue lanterns brightened, just slightly, just enough. ***YOU ASKED. GO, CARVER. THE NAMES ARE YOURS TO CARRY.***

The service tunnel swallowed them — low, dry-smelling, lined with the bones of the court's plumbing rather than its dead — and behind them the alarm-bells changed rhythm, and beneath the bells Cyrus heard, faint and terrible, the measured footsteps of a man who did not run because he had never needed to.

Vael Corvane was coming to see what had been taken from his collection.

Cyrus put his shoulder under Xanthe's arm and ran, and as he ran, another piece of the ice broke open, unprompted, unwelcome: white gravel, a burning chapel, a child's shoe in the doorway, and his own voice — his own voice, young and horrified — saying, *"You gave the order. I saw you give the order."*

He stumbled. Xanthe caught him.

"What did you remember?" Xanthe asked. They were both bent double in the dark, lungs burning. "You look like a man remembering something."

Cyrus pressed his palm flat against the oath-stone, warm as a heartbeat now, and found that his hand was shaking.

"Not something," he said. "*Someone*. I remembered who gave the order, Xanthe. And he's walking down that corridor behind us."
