## 13. The Master's Confession

The service tunnel breathed cold and mineral, the air thick with the taste of old salt and older stone. Xanthe ran with one hand skimming the wall — not for balance, but because the wall was carved, every inch of it, and even now, even fleeing, their fingers read the dead in it like a blind woman reading letters.

*Sold at the solstice. Weighed against a harvest of souls. The lanterns burn brighter for him.*

They did not want to hear it. They heard it anyway. That had always been the shape of their life: hearing what they did not want, carrying what no one asked them to carry.

Behind them, muffled through three turns of stone and one iron door Cyrus had wedged shut with his broken oath-stone's twin fragment, the Bone Gallery was waking. The sound came through the rock like a heartbeat heard through a lover's ribs — slow, vast, wrong. Stone grinding on stone. The guardians turning their heads.

"Left here." Cyrus's voice, low, ahead of them. He had found the tunnel junction by instinct or by some buried map in his ruined memory, and he moved through the dark like a man walking a road he'd walked in another life. "There should be a cistern. Old drainage, pre-court. If it exists."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we learn to pray quickly, and hope someone new is listening."

Xanthe almost smiled. Almost. Their hand found the reliquary where it hung against their chest, strapped beneath their coat, wrapped in Cyrus's cloak, and their heart did its terrible stumbling rhythm against the case's edge. It was warm. It had been warm since they lifted it from its alcove among the sleeping dead, warm as a living thing held too long, warm as guilt.

They reached the junction. Cyrus pressed his palm flat to the left-hand wall, hesitated — and something in his posture changed, a small settling, the way a lock settles when the right key turns. "Drainage channel," he said quietly. "Slopes down toward the deep water. There'll be a maintenance stair. I've—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I've been down here before. I don't remember being down here before. Do you understand how that feels?"

"I understand better than you know." Xanthe squeezed past him into the left passage, and their shoulder brushed his, and neither of them remarked on it. There was no time for remarking. There was only time for running, and for the voices in the walls, and for the thing against their chest that beat like a second heart that was not theirs.

* * *

The cistern existed. Of course it existed; the court beneath the mountain was old the way rivers are old, built atop its own corpses, and every age of it had left drains and ducts and servant ways behind like shed skins. They came out onto a gallery above black water that reflected nothing, lit by veins of pale lichen that pulsed faintly, slowly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Cyrus called a halt with a raised fist, and Xanthe — who trusted no one, who had survived Thornfell's wars precisely by trusting no one — halted. Because his instincts were good. Because whatever else he was, disgraced and hollowed and hunted, the man knew how to keep people alive.

"We rest here," he said. "Ten minutes. No fire. Then we find the smuggler stairs Mother Quill marked for us."

Xanthe slid down the wall to sit, and the reliquary shifted against their chest, and the warmth of it pressed through cloth and leather like a hand laid flat over their heart. Around them, the cistern was quiet — but quiet here was never empty. The dead were in the water. The dead were in the lichen. The dead were in the bones of the mountain itself, murmuring the way water murmurs over stones, wordless and patient.

But the reliquary spoke loudest.

Not the godling inside — the godling's voice, if it was a voice, was sealed deeper than hearing, a pressure rather than a sound, like a note sung below the floor of the world. What spoke was the case itself. The outer casing. Carved bone, old bone, bone worked in patterns Xanthe had known since childhood: the running knotwork that flowed into leaves, the leaves that became hands, the hands that cradled whatever the carver wished to hold safe forever.

They had learned that pattern at a workbench in Hollowmere, aged nine, sanding their knuckles raw while an old man with ink-stained fingers corrected their grip.

*Ambervale,* Master Odric had said. *A carving is a promise. Whatever you seal inside, you must be willing to carry.*

Their master. Their teacher. The man who had taken a gutter-child with too-sharp ears and given them a trade, a name, a place at a bench beside his own. Dead eleven years now, buried under a plain stone in Hollowmere's yard, mourned honestly and buried honestly, so far as anyone knew.

So far as Xanthe had ever bothered to look.

Because they hadn't looked. That was the shame of it, arriving late and cold. They had carved his grave-gift themselves — a small bird, wings folded, at rest — and they had heard his last words in it as they heard every dead soul's last words, and his last words had been *forgive the cold, child*, and Xanthe had thought: the fever, the winter, the poor old body failing. Forgive the cold of the grave. A dying man's confusion. They had wept and carved and never once wondered why a man who died of fever would speak of cold as though he had chosen it.

Now they sat in the dark beneath the mountain with his work strapped to their chest, and there was no more room for not-wondering.

"Cyrus," they said. "I need light. And I need you to watch the water."

He looked at them across the dark, lichen-light washing his face pale and greenish, and whatever he saw in theirs made him ask nothing. He drew his knife and turned to face the black water, and gave them his back, and that trust — unearned, unasked-for — hurt worse than anything Vael Corvane's hunters could have done.

Xanthe unwrapped the reliquary.

It lay in their lap like a sleeping child: a case of bone the length of a forearm, banded in silver gone grey, sealed with wax stamped by no seal Xanthe knew. The carving covered every surface. Knotwork into leaves into hands, the Ambervale flowing line, the sure and loving cuts of a master's final years. They had admired it in the gallery with a kind of horrified pride — *he was here, his work was sold here, how* — and had not let themselves finish the thought.

They finished it now.

They took out their finest knife — the one they'd bought with their first honest wages, the one Master Odric had sharpened for them the day they finished their apprenticeship, saying *a carver's blade is their conscience, keep it clean* — and set its edge to the wax seal.

"Forgive me," they whispered. Not to Cyrus. To the case. To the hands that had made it. "Forgive me, but I have to see."

The wax parted cleanly, the way good work always parts when you cut along its grain. Beneath it, the case opened along a seam so fine they'd missed it entirely — hinged, counterweighted, designed to be opened only by someone who understood the locking-cuts. Xanthe's fingers found them by touch, the way they'd found their teacher's lessons still living in their hands after eleven years, and the outer case sighed open like a mouth about to speak.

Inside the inner lining — the side that faced the godling's prison, the side no buyer would ever see, the side meant for one reader only —

— words. Thousands of them. Cut small and deep and desperate, the letters degrading from master's precision to a ragged scrawl, the work of a man carving as he died.

Xanthe read by lichen-light, and the cistern went very far away, and the voices in the walls went silent, as if even the dead wanted to hear this.

*I, Odric Ambervale, carver of Hollowmere, being in my last days, do set down the truth where only grief will look for it.*

*They came to me in autumn. The priests of the new temple — Corvane's men, though I did not know the name then. They brought the child-godling's remains in an iron box, and they told me what it was, and they told me what would happen if I refused. Not threats. Arithmetic. They were very gentle about the arithmetic.*

*One generation. That is what they offered to buy. One generation in which the usurper does not march on the free cities, because the court beneath the mountain holds his debt and the debt is paid in souls, and the first soul to seal the bargain must be divine, must be worth all the rest. They had already killed it. I could not bring it back. All I could choose was who suffered next, and how long the suffering waited.*

*I am old. I have carved ten thousand grave-gifts and heard ten thousand last words, and not one of them was ever simple, and I taught my apprentice that truth, and now I set it down in my own hand: there is no clean choice. There is only the choice that buys time for others to make better ones.*

*I told myself the free cities would use the generation well. I told myself a child of the gods, already dead, was worth less than ten thousand living children. I told myself many things, and the cold took me before I could tell myself enough of them.*

*If you are reading this, you have opened what I sealed, which means you are either a thief or my apprentice, and my apprentice would never steal.* 

Here the writing wavered, the letters going shallow, the cuts failing.

*Child. If it is you. I taught your hands everything they know. I am sorry for what I made them do. The godling is not dead the way we die. Sealed things wait. Sealed things remember. If it wakes, it will wake hungry for justice, and justice is a fire that does not ask whose house it burns. I chose to spare you that fire. Forgive the cold, child. I chose the cold myself. It is quieter than the arithmetic.*

The cuts ended there. The last letter trailed off unfinished, the knife having simply stopped, mid-stroke — a man's strength ending between one word and the next.

Xanthe sat very still.

The lichen pulsed. The black water made its wordless sound. Somewhere behind them, impossibly far and impossibly close, the Bone Gallery ground through its slow waking.

*Loyalty* was a word Xanthe had used all their life like a coin they believed was gold. Loyalty to the dead, who had made them. Loyalty to Master Odric, who had lifted a gutter-child onto a stool and said *you have ears the way other men have eyes, use them kindly*. Every kindness he had ever done them replayed now in the lichen-light, and every kindness was true, and every kindness was also the architecture of a lie — because a man who could carve this confession, who could feel this guilt, had still done the deed. Had still sat at his bench with the priests' iron box open before him and carved a prison for a murdered child of the old gods, and had sealed his confession where only grief would find it, and had gone home to Hollowmere to teach, and to smile, and to sharpen his apprentice's blade.

*Keep it clean.*

Xanthe's hands were shaking. They watched them shake with a strange detachment — the carver watching the instrument, the instrument betraying the craft. Eleven years of grief, redirected. Eleven years of mourning a good man who had never existed. And yet — and this was the wound within the wound — the man who existed was not a monster either. The arithmetic was real. The free cities had had their generation. Xanthe had grown up in it. Had survived Thornfell's wars in it. Every year of their cautious, neutral, self-protective life had been purchased, in part, with a murdered godling's sealed screaming.

*Sealed things wait. Sealed things remember.*

And the godling was not dead the way we die.

Against Xanthe's palms, the inner case — the true reliquary, nested within the confession-case like a heart within a ribcage — pulsed. Warm. Warmer than before. As if it had heard itself spoken of. As if it had felt the wax break and the words read and knew, finally, that someone had come looking.

"You knew him?" Cyrus's voice, soft, from the water's edge. He hadn't turned around. He had kept his vigil the whole time, guarding their grief without witnessing it, and Xanthe loved him a little for that, in the wary, furious, unwilling way they loved everything now.

"He was my teacher," Xanthe said. Their voice came out sanded flat. "He carved this case. He sold the godling to buy the free cities one generation of peace. And he hid the truth inside his own work and died calling it forgiveness."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. "Was he forgiven?"

"That's not mine to give."

"No," Cyrus agreed. "I suppose it isn't." He turned then, and his face in the lichen-light was the face of a man who had spent months being told he was guilty of a massacre he could not remember, and who understood — perhaps better than any living soul — what it was to owe a debt to the dead that no living court could settle. "But someone's owed the truth. Even if they're dead. Especially if they're dead."

Xanthe looked down at the confession one last time. At the unfinished letter. At the arithmetic written in a dying man's hand, and the love written underneath it, both true, both unforgivable, both his.

They closed the case gently, the way one closes a coffin. They rewrapped it in Cyrus's cloak. But they did not strap it back against their chest the same way; they carried it now in their arms, the way one carries a wounded child, or a verdict.

Above them, through fathoms of stone, the court began to ring — a great bell, or something shaped like a bell, tolling alarm through the roots of the mountain. The lichen flared bright, then dimmed, then flared again, keeping time.

"They know," Cyrus said, drawing his blade. "The stair. Now."

Xanthe rose, the reliquary heavy in their arms, heavier than bone had any right to be. And from somewhere very close — from the wrapping, from the inner case, from the sealed and waiting dark — came a feeling that was not quite a voice, not yet, but was learning to become one:

*hungry for justice*

The stair down was narrow and slick, and the bell rang on, and somewhere behind them, boots were finding the service tunnels faster than any guardian's grinding could explain — as though someone had told Vael Corvane exactly which way two thieves would run.

Someone who knew the tunnels. Someone who had been down here before.

Someone whose memories were coming back piece by piece — and whose memories, Xanthe realized with a cold drop through their gut, might not all be innocent ones.
