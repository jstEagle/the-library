## 14. Mother Quill Collects

The safehouse Mother Quill kept beneath the Saltmarket was a drowned chapel in a former life — Xanthe could tell by the way the ceiling ribs arched overhead like the underside of a whale's jaw, and by the salt crust blooming white along the old font that now served as a washbasin. Someone had hung lanterns of green glass where saints once stood, and their light turned everything the color of deep water. It was the kind of room that made you feel you were already underwater and simply hadn't noticed yourself drowning.

Xanthe sat with their back to the wall, as they always did in Quill's houses, and watched her pour tea.

Mother Quill poured the way she did everything: precisely, without hurry, as though the world had agreed long ago to wait for her. She was small — smaller than Xanthe remembered from the night she'd pulled them out of the canal at Cindermoor with a boat-hook and a curse, thirteen years old and half-drowned, still clutching a commission they'd been too proud to abandon. She'd aged since then in the way of smuggler queens: not taller or grayer but *denser*, as if the years had been compressed into her like coal into diamond. Her rings clicked against the teapot. Her eyes never left your hands.

"You're not drinking," she said.

"I'm waiting," Xanthe said. "You didn't drag us off the road and feed us stew out of hospitality. Nobody who runs the Saltmarket does anything out of hospitality."

Quill smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it frightened Xanthe more than any threat could have, because it meant she liked them, and people who liked you were the ones who could hurt you properly.

"Thirteen," Quill said, setting down the pot. "You were thirteen, and stupid, and you'd carved a grave-gift for a man whose family couldn't pay you, because his daughter cried when she asked. I fished you out of black water because you wouldn't let go of that carving even while you went under." She tapped the table once. "I have thought about that, over the years. Most people let go."

Xanthe said nothing. The reliquary sat between them on the table, wrapped in oilcloth and Cyrus's spare cloak, and it seemed to Xanthe that it hummed — though whether that was the godling's soul inside or their own nerves, they couldn't say. Since the bone gallery, since the master's confession carved in a dying hand inside the case, Xanthe had barely slept. The voices of the dead pressed close these days, eager, chattering about justice and judgment and the priest Vael Corvane's name spoken in a hundred ruined mouths. But here, in this green-lit room, there was only one voice that mattered, and it belonged to a living woman pouring tea.

"Eat something," Quill said. "Then we'll talk business."

"I don't want to talk business."

"Nobody ever wants to talk business with me." She pushed the bread closer. "That's how you know it's important."

* * *

Cyrus was not in the room. Quill's people had taken him — politely, at knifepoint, which in the Saltmarket amounted to the same thing — to be fed and bandaged in another chamber, and Xanthe had let him go because refusing would have cost more than it bought. He'd looked back at them through the doorway, oath-stone in his fist, and mouthed something that might have been *don't* or might have been *be careful*. With Cyrus it was usually both.

So Xanthe ate the bread, because refusing food in the Saltmarket was an insult with consequences, and drank the tea, which tasted of rosemary and something faintly numbing, and waited for the knife to come out from under the warmth.

It came, as knives do, gently.

"The councils know," Quill said. "All seven of them, or enough of the seven. Word travels faster than you walk, carver. A dead godling's soul in a reliquary, carried out of the mountain court by a bone-carver and a disgraced knight? There are men in Vell's Landing who would trade a harbor for it. There are women in Corryn who would trade a fleet." She laced her fingers, rings clicking like small bones. "The free cities are dying, Xanthe. You've seen the columns on the road. The usurper eats a city every season, and the councils sit in their marble rooms arguing about tariffs while the world burns. But give them *this*—" she laid one finger, almost tenderly, on the wrapped bundle, "—give them proof that the old gods live, that their power can be held, bought, *kept* — and they will stop arguing. They will stand. Fear makes men run; hope makes them fight. I am offering them hope, and I will be paid very well for the delivery."

"And you want me to hand it to you."

"I want you to *let* me take it. There's a difference. You'd never survive the roads to Vell's Landing carrying that thing. Every cutpurse between here and the coast already smells it. My people can move it clean. That's what I do, little carver. I move things that cannot otherwise be moved." She leaned back. "And you owe me."

There it was. Xanthe set down the cup carefully, because their hands wanted to shake and they would not give Quill the sight of it.

"Thirteen years old," Xanthe said. "One night. One boat-hook."

"One life." Quill's voice didn't rise at all, which was worse. "I've told you before what my ledger looks like. Everyone in the Saltmarket is in it. Most of them are in it twice. You're in it once, and the entry has never been marked paid, because I never asked. Thirteen years I've let that page lie open, Xanthe Amberdale, and I have never once asked you for so much as a forged seal or a quiet word. Do you know what restraint costs, in my line of work? Restraint is the most expensive thing I own." She opened her hands. "Now I'm asking. And it isn't even a hard price. Not your blood, not your bones, not your hearing — just a box. Let me sell hope to the cities, and we're square, and you walk away free of me forever. Doesn't that sound like mercy?"

Xanthe looked at the reliquary.

They could hear it now — not humming, but *breathing*, if a soul sealed in bone could be said to breathe. Small and slow, like a child asleep in a cold room. Ashen-Who-Waits' child, sold by priests, cased by Xanthe's own master in a carving so fine it had made Xanthe weep the first time they'd understood whose hand had made it. And inside the case's lip, hidden where no buyer would ever look, Master Aldric's confession: *I sold the godling to buy the free cities one generation of peace. Forgive me. Or don't. But remember what peace costs, and who paid.*

One generation of peace. And the generation was up. The usurper's armies were at the passes.

"Who's bidding?" Xanthe asked quietly. "Which council wins?"

"Corryn, most likely. Their navy needs a miracle and their treasury can afford one."

"And what will they do with it?"

Quill shrugged, elegant and terrible. "Put it in a vault. Put it on an altar. Put it behind glass and charge admission. It doesn't matter — it's theirs, honestly bought, and honest purchase is more than that soul has known in three hundred years."

"That's where you're wrong." Xanthe heard their own voice go flat and cold, the voice they used for haggling, the voice that had kept them alive through Thornfell's wars. They hated using it on her. "It matters entirely what they do with it. Because it isn't a box, Quill. It isn't cargo. It's a child — a god-child — and its mother is awake again after three centuries, and its mother sent me. Ashen-Who-Waits named its price, and the price was *me*: my feet on the road, my hands on the case, my silence broken. If I hand that soul to a council vault, I'm not selling hope. I'm selling the same lie your councils have been telling themselves for years — that anything can be owned if the price is right. The priests under the mountain believed that. Look what it built them. Lanterns that burn on rendered souls."

Quill's eyes narrowed, just slightly. "Poetry," she said. "I've bought and sold poetry all my life. It doesn't stop armies."

"No," Xanthe agreed. "But a returned god might. The old gods are waking, Quill. You've felt it — everyone has. Prayers answered for the first time in living memory. What do you think happens to the usurper's soldiers when the ground itself turns against them? When the dead refuse to serve his priests? That's not poetry either. That's the only weapon the free cities have left that he can't buy, because he's spent three hundred years making sure nothing else could stand against him." Xanthe leaned forward. "You want to sell hope? Fine. Sell them the *news*. Sell them the story — a godling freed, a priest of the soul-trade brought down, the old gods walking again. Stories I'll give you freely, and gladly, and you can charge whatever the market bears. But the soul goes home to its mother. That was the debt I took on before I ever walked into your house, and I don't get to renegotiate it. Neither do you."

The silence that followed was long. In it, Xanthe became aware of the dead again — the chapel's old congregation, perhaps, or simply the weight of every grave-gift they had ever carved, pressing at the edges of their hearing like faces at a window. *Say it plain,* one of them murmured, an old woman's voice, patient as sediment. *Debts between the living should be said plain, or they rot into debts between the living and the dead.* Xanthe took a breath.

"But I won't pretend the other debt doesn't exist," they said. "You pulled me out of the canal. You fed me when Hollowmere would have let me starve for being what I am. You never asked me for anything, and that restraint was worth more than gold, and I know it. So hear my counter-offer, and know that I make it with a sick heart, because I would rather owe you forever than refuse you at all." They turned their palms up on the table, empty, the gesture of a carver showing clean hands. "Bind me. Whatever you want done in the Saltmarket that needs a bone-carver who hears the dead — names read off relics, forgeries unmade, graves found, ghosts settled — I'm yours. A year. Two. However long your ledger says the boat-hook was worth, minus what a soul delivered home to its god is worth to every living person in these cities, which is a great deal, and you may price it however you like. Take my labor. Take my craft. Take my future, if you must. But not the reliquary. Never the reliquary."

Quill studied them for a long time. Outside, somewhere above, the Saltmarket clanged and shouted and bargained, the great engine of the smuggler world turning through its day, indifferent to the two small people deciding each other's futures in a drowned chapel.

"You'd bind yourself to me," Quill said slowly, "to save a god you've met exactly once."

"I've heard its mother every day of my life," Xanthe said. "Every piece of bone I've ever carved has whispered with the dead. Ashen-Who-Waits is why I can hear at all — did you know that? I didn't, until recently. I thought it was a curse. It turns out it was an apprenticeship." A ghost of a smile crossed their face and died. "You taught me to swim, Quill. The dead taught me to listen. I'm offering you the swimming lessons back. It's the best I have."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Mother Quill's eyes that was not the merchant, not the queen, not the ledger-keeper. Something older and tireder, the face of a woman who had once hooked a drowning child out of black water for no reason she'd ever admitted, and had spent thirteen years pretending there hadn't been one.

Then it closed over, like water.

"A year and a day," she said. "Your labor, your craft, first claim on your services in all matters touching the Saltmarket. Sealed in salt and witnessed by my people, so there's no wriggling out of it later." She stood, and began unbuckling the rings from her fingers, one by one, laying them in a row — the old way, the way deals were sworn in the Saltmarket before anyone trusted paper. "And when the year is done, carver, you and I are square, and I never want to see that ledger page again. It has cost me sleep."

"It's cost me more," Xanthe said, and meant it, and saw that she knew they meant it.

Quill snorted — an inelegant sound, startling from her — and swept the rings back onto her fingers. "Gods preserve me from sentimental criminals. Go fetch your knight. You leave within the hour, through the Undertow, before Corvane's hunters pick up your scent again. And Xanthe—" she caught their sleeve as they rose, and her grip was stronger than her size suggested, "—when you bring that soul home, and the old gods come up out of the ground like spring, remember who moved you clean through the Saltmarket when every council in the free cities wanted what you carried. Remember it kindly."

"I always remember kindly," Xanthe said. "It's the dead who remember otherwise."

* * *

Cyrus was waiting in the passage outside, leaning against wet stone with his arms crossed, and he straightened the moment Xanthe emerged, reading their face the way he'd learned to read it on the road — badly, but trying.

"Well?"

"She took the year," Xanthe said. "A year and a day of my work, instead of the reliquary. We leave through the Undertow within the hour."

Cyrus exhaled. "That's — good. Isn't it? That's better than I expected."

"It's ruinous," Xanthe said. "She knows it and I know it. A year of a bone-carver's service is worth more than any council's bid, if you spend it right, and Mother Quill always spends things right." They started down the passage, and Cyrus fell into step beside them, and the green light of the drowned chapel fell away behind them like a depth they were surfacing from. "But it was the only coin I had that she'd accept, and the alternative was handing a sleeping child to people who build vaults."

They climbed. Somewhere ahead, the Undertow waited — the smugglers' river-road, dark and fast, running toward the mountain and the court beneath it, toward Vael Corvane and his hunters, toward the reckoning Xanthe could feel gathering in the bones of the world like weather.

"Xanthe." Cyrus's voice, low. "Back there. When you refused her. I heard you through the door, some of it." He hesitated, and then, awkwardly, like a man handling something fragile: "You chose the mission over the person who saved your life. I don't know many people who'd have done it. I'm not sure I would have."

Xanthe kept climbing. "You would have," they said. "You broke your whole life open to chase a truth you couldn't even remember. That's the same choice, Cyrus. Just louder."

Ahead, far above, through a grate of rusted iron, a sliver of gray daylight showed — and with it, faint but unmistakable, the voices of the dead rose like wind through a keyhole, hundreds of them, thousands, all saying the same word in a hundred different mouths:

*Come.*

The godling's mother was calling them home.
