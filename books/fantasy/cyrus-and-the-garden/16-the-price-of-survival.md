## 16. The Price of Survival

The mountain began to die around them, and it died loudly.

Stone groaned in the deep places like a ship breaking its keel. Dust sifted down through the bone-lit galleries in pale curtains, and somewhere far behind them — back along the service tunnels, past the shattered reliquary case and the gallery where the dead guardians still wandered, confused, half-awake — something vast gave way with a sound like the world's spine cracking.

Xanthe ran.

Their lungs burned. Their hands, still black with the soot of Vael Corvane's burning ledger-room, clutched the reliquary to their chest as though it were a newborn — and perhaps it was, they thought wildly, perhaps that was exactly what it was: a child, stolen before its first breath, wrapped in carved bone instead of swaddling cloth.

Ahead of them Cyrus moved like a man who had been born in these tunnels, though he had never seen them before yesterday. He moved like a knight of the old order, which was worse. Every few paces he would stop, tilt his head, listen to some vibration in the stone that Xanthe could not hear, and then choose their path without hesitation. Left at the fork where the ceiling sagged. Down the stair where the air moved warm. Xanthe followed, and hated how much they trusted him now, and hated more how much he had come to trust them.

"The eastern galleries are collapsing," Cyrus called over his shoulder. His voice was rough from smoke and shouting. "If we cut south through the ossuary—"

"The ossuary floods when the deep cisterns crack." Xanthe heard the water already, a distant rushing whisper beneath everything, the mountain's blood let loose. "We go up. The old pilgrim stair."

"There is no old pilgrim stair on any map I've read."

"I didn't draw maps for people who read them."

He almost smiled. Even now, with the mountain falling on them and Vael Corvane's hunters somewhere in the dark behind, he almost smiled, and Xanthe felt the shape of that smile lodge under their ribs like a splinter.

*Don't*, they told themselves. *Don't you dare grow fond of him now.*

Because Xanthe knew something Cyrus did not know. Had known it since the moment the reliquary's outer case cracked open in their hands and their master's dying confession spilled out of the bone in words no living ear but theirs could hear. They had known it since the godling's soul, pressed against their chest through layers of carved casing, had begun to *sing* — faintly, terribly, a note like a struck bell heard through water — and Xanthe's own gift had answered it, voice to voice, resonance to resonance.

The soul needed a vessel. Not a box. Not a shrine. A living thing with an opening in it, a door, a capacity — something that could hold what was divine until the old gods could gather it home.

And there was only one vessel in these tunnels shaped correctly.

Xanthe had spent thirty years learning to hear the dead. The voices lived in them the way marrow lives in bone — not resident, exactly, but structural. Remove them and what remained might stand, might walk, might carve — but hollowly. Lightly. Like a bird's bone, all air.

They could do it. They understood that now, with the terrible clarity that comes in the last hour of a thing. The godling's soul could pour into the space their hearing left behind, fill it like water filling a cup, and ride there safely out of the collapsing dark, out of Vael's reach, up into daylight where Ashen-Who-Waits could take back its child.

And then the voices would be gone. All of them. Forever.

Xanthe stumbled on a broken step and Cyrus caught their arm without looking, steadied them, kept running. His grip was iron and careful at once.

"You're limping," he said.

"It's dust in my boot."

"Liar," he said mildly, and did not slow down.

* * *

They gained the pilgrim stair as the first true collapse came — not the groaning threat of it but the fact, tons of gallery floor folding down into the level below with a roar that turned the air solid. The stair itself was ancient, cut before the mountain court existed, worn hollow in the center by centuries of bare feet. Torches of rendered soul-light guttered in brackets along the wall, feeding on nothing now that their keepers were dead or fled, burning down toward their own extinction.

Cyrus paused at the base, chest heaving, and looked back the way they had come.

"They'll follow us," he said. "Vael doesn't strike me as a man who accepts losses gracefully."

"Vael isn't a man anymore. He sold too much of himself to be one." Xanthe leaned against the stair wall, trying to breathe quietly, trying to think. "But the dead won't follow us. Not his soul-bound legions. I spoke to them. I gave them names and crimes, and the dead remember their names even when they forget their orders."

Something moved across Cyrus's face — that hunger he carried, that wound-shaped want for exactly what Xanthe took for granted. To speak with the dead. To know, finally, what had been done to him and by whom.

"You could ask them about me," he said quietly. It was not the first time. It would not be the last. "The legionnaires. Anyone. You could find out what I did, Xanthe. What was taken out of my head."

Xanthe looked at him — this man who had fought beside them in the bone gallery, who had held a corridor against waking guardians with nothing but a broken oath-stone and a fury older than his memory; this man whose muscle remembered honor his mind had lost. And Xanthe opened their mouth to tell him. About the price. About the vessel. About what freeing the godling would cost, and who would pay it.

And stopped.

Not because lying had ever been easy for them — it wasn't; Xanthe Amberdale had survived Thornfell's wars precisely by never lying to anyone worth trusting — but because they saw, with perfect clarity, what Cyrus would do if they told him.

He would refuse. He would argue. He would say there must be another way, and when there wasn't, he would offer himself — because that was what knights of the old order did, even disgraced ones, even hollowed ones. He would offer his gift, whatever small scrap of divinity-adjacent grace the temple had ever put in him. And the temple had scraped him hollow already; Vael had seen to that when he erased him. There was no door in Cyrus for the godling to enter. Only walls, and behind the walls, nothing.

Or worse — he would try to stop them. Bind their hands. Carry them bodily up the stair and out of the mountain, godling and all, and spend the rest of his life finding a third option that did not exist while the free cities burned and the usurper's armies marched and every day cost lives that a restored godling might have saved.

No. Some truths were like surgery: they went in fast and deep, and only afterward did anyone discuss whether the patient should have been consulted.

"Cyrus," Xanthe said, and made their voice flat and practical, a smuggler's voice, a carver pricing a commission. "When we reach the light, there will be a ritual. The gods will take their child back. That part I understand. The rest — the mechanics of it — the dead explained it badly and I wasn't listening closely." A pause, calibrated. "I'll know more when we're out."

It was the smoothest lie they had ever told, and it tasted like ash, and Cyrus believed it because he wanted to believe that the worst was behind them.

"All right," he said simply. "Tell me when you know."

And Xanthe, who had built a life on never believing anyone, watched this man believe *them*, and felt the lie settle into their chest beside the reliquary like a second burden, heavier than the first.

"Come on," they said. "The stair climbs east. Daylight by midmorning, if the mountain lets us keep our heads."

* * *

They climbed for hours.

The stair switched back on itself through the mountain's bones — through veins of quartz that glowed faintly without being asked to, through chambers where prehistoric roots had forced their way through stone and died there, mummified, like the fingers of giants. Twice the stair behind them ceased to exist entirely, swallowed by settling rock, and each time Cyrus said nothing, only glanced back once, and climbed faster.

Xanthe used the climbing to prepare.

That was the truth of it, though they'd have cut out their tongue before saying so aloud. Every switchback, every rest-stop in the cold dark, they spent talking inwardly to the voices — the dead who had ridden with them since childhood, murmuring in the marrow of every carved thing they touched. Old Maren the ferryman, dead forty years, who commented on the weather. The nameless soldier whose fingerbone ring Xanthe wore, who hummed marching songs when Xanthe was afraid. The dead woman from Hollowmere whose grave-gift had started all of this, whose last words — *they sold her soul* — had pulled Xanthe into the current that was now carrying them toward a waterfall's edge.

They catalogued them. That was the awful, secret work of the climb: Xanthe walked through their own gift room by room, like a householder walking through a house the night before the bailiffs come, deciding what to touch last.

*Maren.* Thank you for the crossings. For telling me which ice would hold.

*Soldier.* I don't know your name. I'm sorry. I wore you well, I hope.

*Grandmother.* You taught me the first knife-grip. You also told me, the week before you died, that the dead don't envy the living anything except the chance to be useful. I understand you now. I wish I didn't.

And underneath all of it, always, the godling singing in its bone prison against Xanthe's chest — that thin, sweet, wounded note — growing slightly stronger with every hour, as if it could smell the surface, as if it knew.

Once, near the top, Xanthe caught themselves humming along.

They stopped. Pressed their lips together. Kept climbing.

* * *

Dawn found them in a chamber just below the final shaft — a natural cave where the pilgrim stair emerged into open air, a crack of grey-gold light slanting down like a blade laid across the stone. Cold real wind moved through it, smelling of pine and snow and the far, faint smoke of a world at war. After the rendered-light stink of the deep court, it smelled like forgiveness.

Cyrus stood in the light with his eyes closed, letting it hit his face, and for a moment he looked young — younger than his years, younger than his scars, a boy knight again before whatever the temple did to him had been done.

"We made it," he said. "Gods above, we actually—"

"The ritual happens here," Xanthe said.

Cyrus opened his eyes. "Here? In the open? Won't Vael—"

"He's buried. Buried or digging. Either way he's hours behind us and the mountain hates him now — the dead down there owe *me*, not him." Xanthe set the reliquary down on a flat shelf of stone, gently, the way one lays down a sleeping child, and knelt beside it. Their heart was going hard. They kept their hands steady through sheer spite. "I need to open it fully. Break the inner seal. And then — there's a step I haven't told you about."

Cyrus went very still. Twenty years of soldier instinct narrowing in him like a pupil contracting against light. "What step?"

Xanthe drew a breath, and found that the lie had run out of road after all, here in the honest daylight, with the godling singing and the dead of thirty years gathered close inside them like family at a deathbed.

"The soul needs a vessel," they said. "A living one. Something with a door in it. Mine opens."

Silence. Wind in the shaft. Somewhere below, very faint, stone still shifting.

"What does 'opens' mean, Xanthe." Not a question. A demand wearing a question's clothes.

"It means the voices go. All of them. The hearing — the thing I am, the thing I've been since I was six years old carving my first grave-gift — it goes, and the godling pours into the space, and rides safe to its father, and I live out my life deaf to the dead." Xanthe kept their eyes on the reliquary. "It's fair. It's cheap, even. One carver's ears against a war."

"No." Cyrus crossed the cave in three strides and dropped to a knee in front of them, forcing eye contact, and his face was terrible — not angry, worse, *afraid*. "No. There's another way. There has to be—"

"There isn't. I've had two days of climbing to look for one and the dead have had three hundred years to look for one and there isn't one." Xanthe's voice cracked, finally, just once, on the edge of something enormous. "Cyrus. Listen to me. This is the only thing I have ever chosen freely — do you understand? My whole life I've been a hinge between worlds, pushed and pulled, used by smugglers and priests and ghosts. Nobody ever asked what *I* wanted to trade. This — this is mine. Don't you take it from me."

His hands had closed over theirs, over the reliquary, and they stayed there a long moment, trembling slightly, knight's hands that had held a corridor alone against the waking dead.

"If you do this," he said hoarsely, "you won't hear them anymore. Your master. Maren. Any of them."

"I know."

"You'll be alone in a way I can't fix."

"I know that too."

"And you're going to do it anyway, right now, while I'm standing here, because you think if you wait you'll lose your nerve."

Xanthe finally met his eyes. And smiled — a real one, crooked and grieving and absolutely resolved.

"I was going to do it while you slept," they admitted. "You've ruined a perfectly good plan."

"Don't—" His voice broke. He bowed his head over their joined hands. "Don't make me watch."

"You're not watching a death," Xanthe said softly. "You're watching a delivery. Thirty years late." They slid their hands free, gently, and turned to the reliquary. "Now hold the light for me. That's all. Hold the light, knight. Let me work."

And Cyrus — who had once held a corridor against the dead with nothing but his oath-stone and his shame — rose, and planted himself in the shaft of dawn-light like a pillar, and held it.

Xanthe set their palms flat against the inner seal, and began, with a carver's patience and a traitor's tenderness, to break their master's last lock.

Beneath their fingers, the bone grew warm.

And from somewhere far below — up through miles of collapsing stone, through drowned ossuaries and empty courts and the long dark throat of the pilgrim stair — came a sound that froze them both.

Horns.

Deep, brazen, wrong. Not the mountain's voice. An army's voice, sounding from the world above — from the southern passes, from the direction of the free cities, from the morning that was supposed to be safe.

Cyrus's head snapped up, oath-stone flaring suddenly hot against his chest.

"That's not a hunting horn," he breathed. "That's a war-call. Xanthe — the vanguard's reached the pass. They're *here*."

Above them, through the crack of daylight, the sky had begun, very faintly, to burn.
