## 17. Armies at the Pass

The wind came down off the Thornfell heights carrying the smell Cyrus had hoped never to smell again: woodsmoke and iron, the breath of an army on the march.

He stood on the shoulder of the southern pass with the morning sun behind him, watching dust rise beyond the foothills in a long, slow curtain — not the scattered haze of campfires but a single vast plume, like a storm front dragged across the earth. The vanguard of the usurper's host. He counted banners by their color through the shimmer of distance: black and gold, black and gold, and once, far to the east where the road bent around a burned granary, a standard he knew too well. The seven-pointed star of the Temple Militant, its points inverted.

His hands remembered that standard. His hands remembered what men did beneath it.

"They'll reach the defile by tomorrow," he said. "Maybe the day after, if they stop to water the horses."

Behind him, no one answered. There was no one left behind him who outranked him; there was barely anyone behind him at all.

* * *

They had come up out of the lowlands for three days, a river of the displaced flowing along the smuggler roads Xanthe knew so well, and Cyrus had watched them from ridgelines and shrine walls as the river thickened. Farmers with their cattle and their grief. Weavers from the mill towns, carrying loom-weights because they could not carry looms. Soldiers, too — deserters from three different garrisons, wearing scraps of uniforms they had cut the badges from, moving alone or in twos and threes, ashamed of their own survival.

At a wayshrine on the second night, an old sergeant of the Hollowmere watch had asked him, not unkindly, "Where do you mean to go, knight?"

"Nowhere," Cyrus had said. "I mean to stop."

The sergeant had looked at him a long time. Then she had spat into the shrine's cold brazier and said, "Well. That's something."

* * *

By noon of the third day, the defile chose itself.

Cyrus had walked this ground before — in another life, under another name, when he had ridden these passes as a knight of the old order and learned their bones the way a surgeon learns anatomy. The southern pass narrowed twice: first at a place the cartographers called the Shepherd's Throat, where the road compressed between scree slopes to perhaps forty paces wide, and then again half a mile further north at the Wolf's Jaw, where an ancient rockfall had left a gap barely wide enough for two carts abreast, walled on one side by a cliff face and on the other by a drop into a ravine full of winter meltwater.

Two hundred men in good order could hold the Wolf's Jaw against two thousand. Two hundred men in poor order could hold the Shepherd's Throat for a day and die there.

He meant to use both.

* * *

The refugees had pooled in a high meadow below the defile like floodwater behind a dam — six hundred souls, perhaps more, too many to feed, too slow to outrun cavalry, and all of them looking at the southern sky as though it might answer questions. When Cyrus rode down among them on a stolen courier's horse, they drew back from him. He could not blame them. He was armored in mismatched plate gone brown with dried mud, his face still carried the bruising of the tunnels beneath the mountain, and he wore a broken oath-stone on a cord at his throat that some of the older soldiers recognized and flinched away from.

Deserters know what an oath-stone means. It means judgment. It means a man who failed.

He dismounted in the middle of the meadow and did not raise his voice. He had learned, somewhere in the erased years of his service, that a man who shouts is asking to be obeyed, and a man who speaks quietly is asking to be believed, and armies run on belief longer than they run on bread.

"I am Cyrus Vane," he said. "Some of you will know the name. Some of you will know worse things about it than I do, because I don't remember them myself." A ripple went through the crowd — recognition, disgust, fear. He let it move and settle. "Three days ago I came out of the mountain court carrying proof of what the usurper's priests have been doing with your dead. In a week, maybe less, their army comes through this pass to do the same with your living. You have a choice. Run north, strung out along the open road, and let their horsemen ride you down one family at a time. Or stand here, at the Throat, for as long as we can make them bleed for every pace, and give the free cities time to wake up."

An old woman near the front said, "Stand with what? Stones and prayers?"

"With what you have." He pointed east, to where the road climbed toward the pass. "There are four hundred fighting-age men and women in this meadow. Most of you are deserters. I know why you ran — I won't ask, and I won't judge. But hear this." He took the oath-stone from around his neck and held it up, letting the light find the fracture running through its heart. "This stone broke the day my temple ordered a massacre and wrote my name on it instead. Everything I served turned out to be rot. And I am telling you that rot can be fought. Not won over — not yet — but fought. Held. Slowed. Every hour the usurper spends bleeding in this pass is an hour the free cities spend arming. Your children are the free cities. Hold here, and you are holding them."

Silence, except the wind and the distant mutter of the army's dust.

Then the old sergeant from the wayshrine stepped forward — Wrenna, he'd learned her name since — and planted her spear butt-first in the turf with a crack that carried.

"I held a wall at Marrow Ford with forty men against a thousand," she said loudly, "and we lived, because our commander was a mad bastard who knew the ground better than the enemy did. Is this one a mad bastard?"

A ragged, disbelieving laugh moved through the crowd. Cyrus found himself smiling without meaning to.

"Worse," he said. "I'm honest."

* * *

The work began at once, and it was the work of desperation dressed in the grammar of siegecraft.

He split the force the way he would have split it in the old days, though he gave the orders now with apologies instead of rank. Three hundred of the ablest — mostly deserters, a few hunters, a handful of refugee women who had handled bows against wolves — he stationed at the Shepherd's Throat under Wrenna, with orders not to win but to yield slowly, trading ground for hours, falling back in stages to the Wolf's Jaw rather than dying in place. The remaining hundred he kept at the Jaw itself with the refugees' carts, building barricades from wagon-beds, felled pines, and the tumbled stones of the old rockfall, lashing everything with rope cut from tent-guylines until the gap became a knot.

He put the archers — eleven of them, with hunting bows and a barrel of arrows fletched from goose-feather and prayer — high on the cliff above the Jaw, where they could shoot down into the press of any assault. He had sappers dig pits along the approach and floor them with caltrops made from broken scythe-blades. He sent the fastest riders north through the pass with letters written in his own hand to every garrison, militia, and council between Thornfell and the coast, telling them exactly where the usurper's host would bog down, and for how long, and where their supply train would be most vulnerable while it was bogged.

And all day, as the barricades rose, he felt it — the thing returning in pieces, the memory that Vael Corvane had tried to bury in him. Not visions now. Something better. Something practical.

He knew this army.

Not its name or its numbers, but its habits. He had drilled beside its officers, eaten from its kitchens, slept in its column during the wars he could not fully recall. He knew that the usurper's vanguard would be heavy cavalry eager for glory, that they would assault the Throat on arrival rather than wait for the main body, because their commander — a Temple colonel named Hask, if the name surfaced correctly from the drowned dark of him — had been passed over for promotion twice and needed a victory. He knew that Hask's men would not scout the cliffs properly, because the Temple trained its scouts to look for enemies and not for geology. He knew that after two failed assaults the column would halt and entrench, and that entrenching troops eat through forage at a monstrous rate, and that the pass country had already been stripped bare by the refugees fleeing ahead of them.

Every fact was a weapon. He stacked them like cordwood.

* * *

Xanthe came up from the south at dusk on the fourth day, walking out of the last light with bone-dust on their sleeves and a look on their face that Cyrus had learned to read as carefully as weather.

"You felt it," he said.

"I heard it." They sat down heavily on a barricade log, close enough that their shoulders touched his. Below them the campfires of the defenders made a sparse constellation in the dark defile, and beyond the southern ridge the usurper's fires burned in a solid line of orange, thousands strong, patient as winter. "The dead in the pass country are restless tonight. Old dead, battle-dead. They keep saying one word over and over." A pause. "*Tomorrow.*"

"Tomorrow, then."

"Cyrus." Xanthe's voice dropped, and in the firelight their face was very still. "When the voices leave me — and it's soon now, I can feel them pulling away like a tide going out — there's something I need to tell you about the price. About what freeing the godling costs—"

"Don't." He said it gently, but he said it. "Whatever it is, you've already decided to pay it alone. I've watched you decide things like that since the mountain. So here's what I'll trade you instead: survive tomorrow, and I'll listen. Survive, Xanthe. Promise me nothing else."

For a long moment they said nothing. Down in the defile, someone was singing — a harvest song, badly, in a language from the eastern valleys — and the sound rose thin and stubborn against the weight of the dark.

"The dead used to say I survived by trusting no one," Xanthe said finally. "They were right. Then they said I'd die the same way." Their hand found his wrist and gripped, brief and hard, bone-carver's fingers strong as roots. "All right, knight. Tomorrow first. Then truth."

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of rain that would not fall.

They came out of the south in column of squadrons, black and gold streaming, lances dipped, the morning fog tearing around their horses' legs like gauze — the vanguard of the usurper's host arriving at the Shepherd's Throat exactly as Cyrus had promised Wrenna they would, hungry for glory, unscouted, unready.

Horns sounded along the defender's line. On the cliff tops, eleven archers crouched behind their stones. At the Wolf's Jaw, a hundred desperate people waited behind a wall of wagons and pine trunks. And in the narrow throat of the pass, Wrenna's three hundred leveled spears against a tide that outnumbered them ten to one, and held.

The first charge broke on the scree slopes at mid-morning, horses screaming on hidden caltrops, and the second at noon, and the third as the light went long and red. Between charges, Cyrus walked the line with a bucket of water and a whetstone, binding wounds and sharpening spears and saying the names of the fallen aloud so that the living would remember them — and because he had begun to suspect, after everything, that the dead were listening.

By dusk, the assaults had stopped. Through the gathering dark, Cyrus watched the usurper's camp begin to dig entrenchments, precisely on schedule, precisely as he had known they would, and he permitted himself the smallest, coldest satisfaction: two days bought. Perhaps three.

Then a rider came pounding up from the south picket, horse blown and rider bloodied, and skidded to a halt before him holding out a scrap of black-and-gold cloth wrapped around something heavy.

"We pulled it off a messenger their side let slip through the outer lines," the rider gasped. "Thought you'd want to see, sir. It's addressed to their commander. From someone called—" the rider frowned, struggling with the name, "—Corvane. High Priest Corvane."

Cyrus unwrapped the cloth. Inside lay a letter sealed with the inverted star, and beneath the seal, in a fine, precise, familiar hand, a single instruction:

**The knight and the carver must not reach the free cities. Burn the pass if you must. Spare nothing.**

And below that, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, as if written by a man who already knew how the war would turn:

**Bring me the oath-stone. It remembers what he cannot.**

Cyrus closed his fist around the paper until his knuckles went white. Behind him, the defenders' fires flickered; before him, the enemy's stretched to the horizon. Somewhere in the dark between them, Vael Corvane's hunters were already moving — and they were not coming for the pass at all.

They were coming for Xanthe.
