## 18. The Court Beneath Rises

The tunnels began to sing before the dead arrived.

Xanthe felt it first in their teeth — a low vibration threading up through the stone, through the soles of their boots, through the reliquary strapped against their chest like a second heart. The godling's soul had been restless since they'd left the bone gallery, humming through its carved prison in frequencies that only Xanthe could hear: a child's voice, wordless, frightened, and beneath it something older, something that sounded like a mountain remembering it had once been a seed.

"They know," Cyrus said behind them. His voice was rough from running, from smoke, from whatever was reassembling itself inside his skull with every mile they put between themselves and Vael Corvane's court. "They've known since we breached the gallery. I can feel that oath-stone burning a hole in my pack."

"Then we keep moving." Xanthe didn't slow. The service tunnels here were old — older than the court above, older than the usurper's dynasty, older perhaps than the gods themselves. The walls were ribbed with strata of pale stone veined with something dark and glistening that Xanthe refused to examine too closely. In places, the ribs looked less like geology and more like the insides of enormous lungs. Everything beneath the mountain breathed.

The voices of the dead pressed in around them as they ran. That was the thing no one understood about Xanthe's gift — or curse, depending on the hour — it wasn't silence punctuated by speech. It was a river, constant, and down here in the deep roads the river had become a flood. Every foot of tunnel held its dead. Miners whose picks had struck something they should not have struck. Couriers who had carried messages between courts and been paid in the only currency the mountain honored. Children, because there were always children, in every place where power pooled like standing water.

*Run*, said a courier who had died three hundred years ago with a sealed letter still clutched to his breastbone. *Run, carver, run. He is waking the legion.*

"The legion," Xanthe breathed, and hated the way their voice shook.

Cyrus caught up to them at a junction where four passages met beneath an archway carved with figures whose faces had been deliberately chiseled away. He was breathing hard but his eyes were clear — clearer than they'd been since the memory had come back to him whole, two days and one lifetime ago. "How many?"

"I don't know yet."

"You always know. You told me you can hear them coming the way other people hear weather."

Xanthe closed their eyes. Opened their hearing wider than was safe, wider than they wanted, and let the flood pour through them.

What came back made their knees soften.

Not dozens. Not hundreds. Thousands upon thousands of the dead, arranged in ranks beneath the mountain's skin, in caverns Xanthe had never mapped because even the smugglers' maps marked them with warnings instead of lines. Soul-bound legionnaires. Soldiers of the old subterranean court who had sworn their deaths into service, whose bones had been rendered down and re-lit with fragments of purchased souls, whose purpose was written into them the way words were written into bone.

And they were moving. All of them. Toward the surface. Toward Xanthe.

"How many?" Cyrus asked again.

"Enough." Xanthe started down the left-hand passage, then stopped, hand flat against the wall, and corrected toward the right. "Enough to bury every free city twice over. Enough that running is just dying somewhere with better scenery."

* * *

They found the chamber by accident, which Xanthe would later understand was no accident at all.

It opened off the service tunnel without warning: a vast hollow where the mountain had been hollowed and never refilled, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, its floor a plain of packed earth scattered with the small white offerings of centuries — knucklebones, milk teeth, the finger-bones of people who had given them willingly, which was rarer than Xanthe liked to think. At the chamber's center stood a broken pillar, sheared off at shoulder height, its stump worn smooth as if generations of hands had gripped it while speaking.

Xanthe stopped at the threshold and listened.

This place had been a court once. Not the soul-market, not the corrupted glittering thing Vael Corvane had built on top of it — this was older. This was where the dead had come to be heard, back when justice beneath the mountain had been spoken aloud rather than bought and sold. Back before the priests of the usurper had taught everyone that a voice could be rendered down into light.

"Cyrus." Their voice came out strange. Steady, somehow, in the way that mattered most when everything else wasn't. "How long do we have?"

He checked the passage behind them, listening with his body the way he'd been trained to — weight balanced, blade loose in his grip, that terrible borrowed skill settling over him again like a coat he'd forgotten he owned. "An hour. Perhaps less. They move fast underground, faster than anything has a right to."

"Then I need an hour." Xanthe stepped across the threshold into the chamber, and the air changed. It pressed against them like water. Like hands. Like ten thousand listeners leaning in.

"What are you doing?" Cyrus's voice sharpened. "Xanthe. We should be climbing. There's a chimney shaft half a mile back—"

"And Vael will follow us up it, and the legion will follow him, and he'll take the godling back and grind us both into lantern-fuel on the way." Xanthe turned to face him, and saw their own fear reflected and doubled in his eyes. "You got your memories back. Your justice. Your name. But there are thousands of soldiers down here, Cyrus, who died for this court and were bound into its service without ever being asked — without anyone ever speaking aloud what was done to them. No one has ever testified for the dead. Not once. Not in all the years the courts have bartered them like grain."

"That's not a fight we can win standing still."

"It's not a fight at all. That's the point." Xanthe unslung their satchel and set it on the broken pillar's stump. Inside were the tools of a lifetime: chisels and burnishing stones, wax and cord, and — wrapped in oilcloth against their own superstition — the rubbings and readings they'd taken from every relic, every grave-gift, every fragment of bone they had ever touched that carried a story worth keeping. A carver kept records. A carver who heard the dead kept testimony.

"You're going to stand in front of an army," Cyrus said slowly, "and talk to it."

"I'm going to stand in front of an army," Xanthe said, "and tell it the truth. Something no one down here has done in three hundred years." They pulled the oilcloth free and began laying out their materials with hands that only trembled a little. "Vael commands them because they're bound. Binding is a chain, and chains hold — until someone gives the chained a reason to pull. The priests wrote their law in blood and contracts. But the old court, the one under all of it? Its law was spoken. Witnessed. Given voice. And I am the last person alive who can give these dead a voice."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, the knight was gone from his tone and something rawer remained. "If it doesn't work?"

"Then we die here, and I'll finally get to ask the legionnaires myself what they want done about it."

Despite everything, something flickered at the corner of his mouth. "There's the smuggler I hired."

"You didn't hire me. You begged, and I took pity, and I've regretted it daily."

He crossed the chamber and gripped their shoulder once, hard, the way soldiers did when words would only get in the way. Then he went to the mouth of the passage, drew his blade, and planted himself there like a door hinge. Holding the corridor. The way he'd held it in the gallery, the way some buried part of him had been trained a lifetime ago to hold it.

"An hour," he said. "Talk fast."

* * *

The dead came like weather.

Xanthe felt them before the sound reached the chamber — a pressure change, a shift in the flood of voices, the way a river changes note before it crests. Then the passage behind Cyrus filled with a grey shimmer, a heat-haze made of bone-dust and rendered light, and the first rank of legionnaires marched out of the dark.

They had been men once. That was the horror and the heartbreak of them. Armor fused to skeletons, helms grown into skulls, each one lit from within by a sliver of purchased soul that burned cold and blue where a heart should have been. Their feet made no sound on the earth. They moved in perfect silence, ranks closing behind them like water filling a footprint, until the far walls of the chamber were lined with them and the darkness above held more, standing on ledges Xanthe couldn't see, patient as sediment.

At their head walked something worse than the rest: a standard-bearer carrying a pole from which hung a banner of woven hair, and wearing upon its breastplate the seal of Vael Corvane's office — a sigil of nine interlocking rings that Xanthe now knew had been carved, originally, by their own master's hand.

The standard-bearer halted twenty paces from the pillar. The legion halted with it. Ten thousand blue flames burned in the dark, fixed on Xanthe like a congregation waiting for a sermon, or a jury waiting for a verdict.

*Xanthe Amberdale.* The standard-bearer's voice arrived directly inside their skull, dry as old parchment, layered with a thousand other voices underneath like sediment in cut stone. *You carry what the Court has bought. Return it, and your death will be quick. This is mercy, by our law.*

"By whose law?" Xanthe asked. Their voice rang strangely in the vast dark, and they made no attempt to soften it. "Yours? Or his?"

Silence. Even the flood of ambient dead — the miners, the couriers, the children in the walls — went quiet, listening.

*I serve the Court.*

"You serve a chain." Xanthe stepped forward, away from the pillar, into the open ground between themselves and the front rank. Behind them, Cyrus shifted his grip on his blade but did not move. "I've spent my life listening to the dead, soldier. Grave-gifts and relics and bones in every border town from Hollowmere to the salt coast. Do you know what I have never once heard? A single dead soul say it wanted to be sold. Not one. Not ever. Every ghost I've ever heard wanted the same three things — to be remembered, to be believed, and to have someone say out loud what was done to them."

The blue flames did not waver. But they did not advance, either.

"So hear me." Xanthe lifted their arms, and let the satchel fall open on the pillar behind them. "My name is Xanthe Amberdale, carver of Hollowmere, speaker for the dead by trade if not by title. I stand outside any court, any law, any contract written in blood or bone, and I bring testimony. Not mine alone. Theirs." They touched the first of the relics laid out — a knucklebone from the charnel cave, taken weeks ago from a legionnaire who had died clutching a letter he'd never sent. "I have read your brothers. I have read your sisters. I have read the ones who fell at the salt gates and the ones who starved in the siege galleries and the ones who were rendered down while still warm because the market price of light went up that season. And every one of them, before the binding took their words away, left something behind. A name. A wound. A truth."

The pressure in the chamber changed. The blue flames guttered — all ten thousand of them, at once, like candles in a draft.

*These words are treason,* the standard-bearer said, but the sediment-voices beneath it had begun to stir, murmuring, shifting, and Xanthe seized that stirring like a rope thrown to a drowning swimmer.

"Treason against whom? Against Vael Corvane, who buys children's souls to light his halls? Against the priests who taught this mountain that the dead are currency?" Xanthe's voice cracked upward, raw and ringing. "I'll tell you what treason is. Treason is a high priest selling a godling's soul and calling it doctrine. Treason is a temple ordering a massacre and erasing the knight who witnessed it. Treason is binding ten thousand soldiers into service after death without asking a single one of them for consent!" They pulled the knucklebone close and held it up, and let their gift do what it had always done, the thing they had spent a lifetime hiding and one long month learning to weaponize.

They gave the dead their voice back.

The legionnaire's last words poured out of Xanthe's mouth in a dead man's cadence: *"Tell my daughter in the lowlands the letter was finished. Tell her the war was wrong from the start and I knew it and stayed anyway, because staying was all I knew how to do. My name was Harren Vosk. Remember it. Someone remember it—"*

The nearest rank of legionnaires shuddered. One of them — a broad-shouldered skeleton with a farmer's calluses still visible on its finger bones — took a single step forward, out of formation. The blue flame in its chest flickered, and for a moment, through it, Xanthe heard something underneath the binding: a whisper, thin and wondering, like a man hearing his own name after decades of silence.

*Harren Vosk,* it repeated. *That is my name. That was my name.*

"Yes," Xanthe said gently, in their own voice again, tears cutting tracks through the dust on their face. "And I know what was done to you. All of you. I can speak every name. I can testify every crime — yours, and the crimes committed against you. But testimony needs witnesses, and judgment needs judges, and there is no law in this mountain anymore except the ones you choose to obey." They spread their arms wide, empty-handed, heart hammering against the reliquary strapped to their chest, the godling's frightened hum rising to meet their own racing pulse. "So judge. Right here, right now, outside any court he built. Judge Vael Corvane by the oldest law there is — spoken aloud, witnessed by the living and the dead together. Refuse his orders. Turn your banners around. And let me walk this godling home."

For a long, terrible moment, nothing moved.

Then the standard-bearer raised its banner, and the nine interlocking rings of Vael's seal caught the cold blue light — and the broad-shouldered legionnaire, the one who had been Harren Vosk, reached up with a farmer's strong hand and tore the seal from its own breastplate. It dropped the sigil onto the packed earth between them, where it lay gleaming like a coin no one would ever spend.

A second legionnaire followed. A third. A dozen, then a hundred, the sound of falling seals rippling backward through the ranks like rain beginning on a tin roof — and beneath it all, rising, growing, a sound that Xanthe had never heard in all their years among the dead and would remember for the rest of their life:

Ten thousand dead voices, finding their words again, saying their names.

But the standard-bearer did not move. And when it spoke again, the sediment-voices beneath its parchment dryness were gone, stripped clean, replaced by something singular and cold and terribly awake.

*Clever carver,* it said. *But the Court anticipated even this.*

Its helm split open along seams Xanthe hadn't seen, and from within rose not a skull but a coil of rendered light — dense, deliberate, wearing the shape of a soul that had been refined past all recognition.

*He put a piece of himself in every standard,* it said, as the torn seals on the earth began, one by one, to glow. *Did your ghosts never teach you that a chain has two ends?*

Behind Xanthe, in the passage, Cyrus swore — and the oath-stone in his pack began, unmistakably, to scream.
