## 20. Silence Given

The reliquary opened like a wound.

Not with violence — with light. The carved bone casing that had held the godling's soul for three hundred years split along seams no eye could see, and what poured out was not fire, not radiance, but something gentler and far more terrible: a sound. A single note, low as a mother's hum, high as a child's laugh at the top of a stair, and it moved through the ruined gallery of the court beneath the mountain the way dawn moves through a valley — touching everything, claiming nothing.

Xanthe felt it in their teeth first. Then in their hands, which had held bone all their life and had never once trembled until now.

The dead legionnaires stood in a broken ring around them, Vael Corvane's soul-bound soldiers freed from their binding by the weight of spoken names and spoken crimes. Some of them were weeping, if the dead could weep — their faces slack, their hollow eyes brimming with something like dew. Others knelt. One, a woman whose breastplate bore the crest of a city that had not existed since before Xanthe's grandmother's grandmother, reached out toward the light with fingers that were only suggestion and memory.

And in the center of it all, the godling rose.

It did not rise the way the living rise. It gathered itself, the way mist gathers off a river at morning, the way grief gathers in a house where someone has just died. It was small. That was the thing that broke Xanthe's heart open — after three centuries in a box, sold like a sack of grain by a frightened man trying to buy peace, it was *small*. Child-sized. Child-shaped. Its face was unfinished, the way young things are unfinished, and its eyes were the color of ash holding the last heat of a fire.

Ashen-Who-Waits had waited for this child. And here it was, rising free and whole.

"Carver."

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the voices of the dead always had, but this one was different. This one was warm. It did not scrape against the inside of Xanthe's skull; it settled there, like a bird coming home to roost.

"You know my name," Xanthe said. Their voice came out rough. They had been speaking with the dead for so long that they had forgotten how to speak to anything else, and now they were speaking to both at once, and neither came easily.

"I have known your name since before you were born," the godling said. "I heard it in the dark of the reliquary. I heard every name spoken over every grave-gift your hands ever made. You gave me stories, carver. Three hundred years in the dark, and you gave me stories."

Xanthe's throat closed. They thought of all the grave-gifts — the little carved birds and dogs and doors, the tokens laid into coffins and crypts across Hollowmere and the free cities, each one carrying a voice, each one whispering its fragment of a life into the dark. They had never known where those whispers went. They had assumed they simply faded, the way all things fade.

They had been feeding a prisoner. All their life, without knowing it, they had been keeping a godling company.

"I didn't know," Xanthe said. "If I had known—"

"You would have come sooner," the godling said gently. "Yes. That is why I chose you. Not because you are strong, or brave, or good. Because you listen. Because even when you buried the warnings, even when you refused and resented and ran — you *listened*. The dead have had no one to hear them for three hundred years, carver. You were the only door left open."

Behind Xanthe, Cyrus shifted. She could feel him without looking — the particular stillness he carried now, the stillness of a man who had gotten his memories back piece by piece and found them heavier than any armor. He had watched her bargain with the dead legionnaires, name by name, crime by crime, until the dead turned on the priest who had bound them. He had watched Vael Corvane fall beneath the weight of his own testimony, dragged down by hands that had been waiting centuries for justice. He had watched the reliquary open.

He did not know yet. Thank all the patient gods, he did not know yet what she had seen in the tunnels, what she understood now with terrible clarity: that a soul freed from a vessel must pass through a living one, and that the price of passage was whatever the vessel held most dear.

For Xanthe Amberdale, that had never been in question. Not really. From the moment Ashen-Who-Waits had spoken through the carved bone and named its debt, some quiet part of her had known how this would end. The voices were her gift and her curse and her whole self, and gifts given by gods are never truly owned. They are only borrowed. And borrowings come due.

"It is time," the godling said.

Xanthe nodded. Her hands, she noticed distantly, had gone very steady. Steadier than they had ever been. There is a kind of calm that lives on the far side of dread, once the decision is made and there is nothing left to do but walk through the door.

"How does it work?" Cyrus asked. His voice was tight. He had moved closer without seeming to move at all — knight's instinct, muscle memory from a war he'd finally remembered, standing between Xanthe and everything, including, apparently, the truth. "The release. What does it require?"

The godling was silent a moment. When it spoke again, its voice had gone soft with something that might have been apology.

"The soul must pass through the living," it said. "That is the old law, older than me, older than my parent who waits in ash. The vessel must give freely what it treasures most. There is no other way. There has never been another way."

Cyrus went rigid. Xanthe saw him arrive at the arithmetic — saw his eyes flick to her hands, to the bone-dust stains worked so deep into her knuckles they looked like shadow, to the ears that had heard the dead since childhood. Saw him understand.

"No," he said. "No. Find another way. Take mine — take whatever I have, I've already lost half of it once—"

"You have nothing I need, knight," the godling said, not unkindly. "Your treasure is your purpose, newly found, and purpose cannot pay this price. Only a listening can. Only the ear that has kept faith with the dead." It turned its unfinished face to Xanthe, and its eyes were kind, and that kindness was the worst part. "You knew. You have known since the charnel cave. Why do you think you read his oath-stone so gently? You were practicing farewells."

Xanthe laughed. It came out cracked and wet and real. "You're a cruel little god."

"I am a *grateful* little god," it corrected. "There is a difference. Cruelty takes without asking. I am asking."

*You will lose the voices forever,* Ashen-Who-Waits had told her, weeks ago, in a shrine that smelled of dust and old prayers. *Or you will bring my child home.* She had thought then that it was a threat. It had been a promise. The god had simply been honest about the shape of the door.

"Yes," Xanthe said.

Cyrus grabbed her arm. "Xanthe—"

"Cyrus." She turned to look at him fully, and let him see it — all of it, the resolve and the fear and underneath both, the strange clean peace. "I have spent my whole life unable to be alone. Do you understand? Every grave-gift, every relic, every carved bit of bone — voices. Whispering. Begging. Accusing. Grieving. I have never once, not for a single hour since I was six years old, sat in true silence. I love them. I love the dead. They made me who I am." Her voice caught. She let it. "But they are not mine to keep. They never were. And this child has been in the dark for three hundred years while I complained about the noise of the light."

"That's not fair," Cyrus said hoarsely.

"No," Xanthe agreed. "Almost nothing worth doing is."

She stepped forward, out of his grip, gentle about it, and knelt before the godling in the ruin of the court beneath the mountain, among the freed dead and the fallen priest and the rendered lanterns guttering out one by one as their stolen fuel finally, finally burned free.

"What do I do?" she asked.

"Open your hands," the godling said. "And listen. One last time. Listen as long as you can bear to. They will want to say goodbye."

* * *

The first voice was her mother's.

Xanthe should have expected it, and in some chamber of herself she had, but knowing a thing and surviving it are different countries entirely. The voice rose out of the gathering light like smoke out of a snuffed candle — warm, tired, smelling faintly of tallow and river water, the way it had on the night she died of the fever while Xanthe, six years old and already hearing things no child should hear, sat by the bed and carved a little wooden bird because she didn't know what else to do with her hands.

"There she is," her mother said. "There's my Xanthe."

"Mama." The word came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh. She had not said it aloud in thirty years. She had heard her mother's voice a thousand times in a thousand relics — in the hair-comb she'd left behind, in the doorframe of the house they'd rented, in the grave-gift Xanthe had carved for her own mother's grave with hands that wouldn't stop shaking. But hearing and answering were different. Answering was alive.

"You grew up crooked and stubborn," her mother said, and Xanthe could hear the smile in it. "Just like I hoped. Listen to me, little bird. I'm only the first. There's a long line behind me, and you will want to rush them through because it hurts, and you mustn't. Hurting is the toll. Pay it gladly."

One by one, they came.

The smuggler Brannoc Hale, who had taught her the mountain passes and died with a usurper's arrow in his throat, laughing about how at least it wasn't the cold that got him. "You kept my secret, girl. The one about the border cache. Never told a soul, not even under Mother Quill's knives. That was decent of you. Decency's rarer than gold. Go on, then. Walk loud in the world. Some of us only ever got to walk quiet."

The dead woman from Hollowmere, the first warning, the one whose last words had started all of this — *they sold her soul* — and who now stood at the edge of the light with her hands folded. "I was angry, when you buried my words. I shouted at you for a week. I hope you heard me shouting." Xanthe had. "But you came around. Slow as a glacier, but you came around. That's more than most of the living ever do. Tell the ones up top—" her voice wavered, the first crack in it, "—tell them the dead remember who listened. We keep a list. It's shorter than you'd hope."

An old soldier from a war Xanthe had never learned the name of. A child drowned in the Merrowfen, wanting only to know if anyone had found her doll. A temple scribe who had copied forbidden texts and been burned for it, whispering *thank you for reading them, thank you for reading them* as though Xanthe had done it on purpose. The master. Last of all, or nearly last — Master Oren Vail, whose hand had carved the reliquary, whose confession she had read in the service tunnels with her heart turning over like a cart in a ditch. He had sold the godling to buy the free cities one generation of peace, and he had spent his dying breath carving the truth into the inside of the case where no buyer would ever look, because he could not bear for the lie to be the last thing.

"Student," he said.

"Master." Her jaw ached from clenching. "You lied to me. Your whole life, you lied."

"I did." No flinching in him. He had never flinched; it was the thing she had loved and hated most about him. "And I will not insult you by asking forgiveness with my last words. I will only say this: I chose the living, because I could not hear the dead, and so their suffering was a story to me. You can hear them. You always could. Whatever you choose, student — choose it with your ears open. That is all I ever taught you that mattered."

Then he was gone, and the light was brighter, and the godling stood before her with its small unfinished face upturned, and Xanthe understood that the line had ended and the moment had come.

"Will it hurt?" she asked. "Losing them?"

"Yes," the godling said. "All the way down. For a long time. Perhaps forever — grief is not a debt that clears." It reached up, and its small hand took hers, and its grip was astonishingly strong, the grip of something that had held on to hope for three hundred years in a box. "But hear me, carver, and carry this instead of the voices: silence is not emptiness. Silence is a room. What you put in it is yours to choose. Most people fill theirs with fear. You — you will fill yours with memory. And memory, unlike my poor stolen self, cannot be bought or sold or sealed in bone. Memory belongs to the one who carries it."

"Promise me something," Xanthe said.

"Anything within my small power."

"When the free cities hold — when the war breaks — go to the graves. All of them. Every grave-gift I ever carved. Let them hear, just once, that they were heard. That someone listened. Let them rest."

The godling's eyes shone like banked coals. "I will spend a century doing nothing else."

"Good," Xanthe said, and smiled, and meant it, and was terrified, and meant that too. "Then take it. Take the listening. It was never mine anyway. It was only ever a loan."

The godling pressed its forehead to hers.

The light went through her like a river through a reed.

* * *

Afterward, there was silence.

Not the ordinary silence of an empty room — Xanthe had never actually experienced that, and so had no idea it would be so *large*. It was silence like deep water. Silence like the space between stars. It rushed into her through the ears that had been doors for thirty years and found them closed, and it filled her up, and it was the loneliest sound she had ever heard, and it was also, God help her, the quietest.

She was aware of Cyrus kneeling beside her. She could see his mouth moving. She could see the question in his face — *are you alive, are you whole, what did it cost* — and she realized, with a wonder that bordered on laughter, that for the first time in her life she had to *guess* what someone was saying. Had to read a face like a stranger reads a face. It was absurd. It was liberating. It was the worst thing that had ever happened to her and she would not have undone it for all the voices in all the bones in all the world.

She raised one hand — *wait* — and pointed to her ear, and shook her head, and watched him understand. Watched his face do several things at once: horror, grief, and underneath them, unwillingly, a fierce and stubborn tenderness, the look of a man deciding to become someone's interpreter whether they liked it or not.

She tapped her own chest. *Still here.*

He exhaled, long and shaking, and nodded.

Around them, the court beneath the mountain was dying its long-delayed death. The rendered lanterns had gone out; the soul-market stalls stood empty, their wares released or scattered; the dead legionnaires were walking — actually walking, upright, unhurried — toward some door in the dark that only they could see, going at last to wherever the dead go when they are no longer held. One of them, the woman with the crest of the vanished city, paused beside Xanthe and inclined her head. Xanthe couldn't hear whatever she said. But she could read the shape of it well enough: gratitude, and a salute, and farewell.

The godling stood at the edge of the light, growing brighter, growing less child-shaped and more *itself*, whatever itself would turn out to be. Before it went, it looked back at Xanthe one last time, and though no sound crossed the silence between them, Xanthe felt the words land somewhere behind her sternum, the way you feel a drum through a floor:

*Rest now, listener. You have earned the quiet. And when the world grows too loud with the living — come find me. I remember everyone who ever kept me company in the dark.*

Then it was gone, upward, through stone, through mountain, through three hundred years of wrong, going home.

Xanthe sat in the ruins of the court beneath the mountain, deaf to the dead for the first time since childhood, and wept — great, ugly, unguarded weeping, the kind she had never permitted herself because the dead were always watching, always needing, always asking. Now no one needed her. Now the silence was hers alone, vast and terrible and clean, and she grieved into it with her whole body, mourning the self she had been, the chorus she had lived inside, the thirty years of whispers that had made her who she was.

And underneath the grief, steady as bedrock, unwavering as a carved thing:

*I would do it again. I would do it again tomorrow. I would do it a hundred times.*

Because the godling was free. Because the dead legionnaires had walked out of their chains with their heads up. Because Vael Corvane's empire of rendered souls lay broken, and the free cities had a chance — a real one — because a bone-carver from Hollowmere had finally stopped burying the warnings.

Grief transfigured, her mother had called it once, in a voice from a hair-comb, speaking of Xanthe's father. *Grief doesn't leave us, little bird. It just changes shape. Sorrow becomes stone, and stone becomes shelter.*

Xanthe wiped her face. Took Cyrus's offered hand. Stood up in the silence, which was enormous, which was empty, which was hers.

Above them, muffled through a mile of mountain, came a sound she didn't need the dead to interpret — a deep, rolling boom, and then another, rhythmic, deliberate. Siege engines. The usurper's vanguard hammering at Thornfell's southern pass, where Cyrus had rallied refugees and deserters into a holding force with nothing but his recovered memory and his own bare courage.

Cyrus looked at the ceiling of the world, then at her, and his expression asked the question plainly enough for a deaf woman to read:

*Can you climb?*

Xanthe Amberdale rolled her shoulders, flexed her scarred and bone-stained hands, and answered the only way that mattered.

She started walking.
