## 22. A Carver Who Listens Differently

The first thing Xanthe carved in the new workshop was a mistake.

It had been four months since the mountain, and their hands had not yet learned the shape of this new silence. The chisel moved through riverbone the way it always had — patient, obedient, finding the line that waited inside the material like a name inside a stone — and when the small figure emerged, a woman with her hands open, Xanthe set down the tools and held it to their ear out of habit.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Bone did not speak. Bone had never spoken; it was only Xanthe who had heard, and Xanthe who no longer did. Still they held it there, pressed cold against their cheek, waiting for something that would never come again, and felt the grief move through them like water through a channel cut long ago and never filled.

Then they laughed — a short, startled sound in the empty room — because the woman's hands were not open. They were cupped, the way hands cupped to catch something falling. Xanthe had carved catching without meaning to. Memory moving through the hands even when the voices were gone.

"That'll do," they said aloud, to no one, and began again.

* * *

The workshop sat at the end of Tanner's Row where the row stopped pretending to be a street and became a footpath down to the millrace. It had been a smokehouse once, then abandoned, then — after the war broke at the pass and the world declined, astonishingly, to end — rented to Xanthe for less than it was worth, because Hollowmere had decided, in its grudging way, that Xanthe Amberdale was worth less than nothing being charged for. The landlord, a widow named Petra whose husband's grave-gift Xanthe had carved for free, refused to discuss the discount and slammed the ledger shut whenever Xanthe tried.

The front room held the bench, the tools laid out in their old order, the oil lamp with its good clean flame. The back room held the bed, the stove, and the shelf where Xanthe kept the things they could not sell: a broken oath-stone bound in copper wire; a reliquary case, empty now, its inner surface covered in a dead man's confession; a single white feather that had fallen from nowhere when the godling rose, and which Ashen-Who-Waits, if the shrines were to be believed, had not asked to have returned.

Xanthe worked with the door open. That was new too. For thirty years they had worked behind closed shutters, because a bone-carver who heard what Xanthe heard could not afford to let anyone watch their face while they listened. Now anyone could come in. Most of Hollowmere took advantage of it, in the way of small towns, drifting in to lean on the doorframe and talk about rain and prices and who had married whom, while Xanthe carved and gave answers in the tone of a person being patient against their will, which everyone understood to be the same as affection.

They came for the grave-gifts. That was the strange mercy of it. In the old days a grave-gift from Xanthe's bench had carried a voice in it — the dead speaking through the carving, a last word sealed in bone, and mourners had found it holy or horrible depending on their constitution. Now the gifts carried nothing but what Xanthe put there: the set of a jaw remembered from life, the fold of a shawl worn forty winters, a shepherd's crook with the exact wear of a particular hand. And the mourners wept anyway. Wept harder, some of them said, because the gift was not the dead person speaking. It was the living remembering, made solid enough to hold.

"You've got sawdust in your hair," Petra said one morning, standing in the doorway with eggs she also refused to discuss. "You know the Vellane boy comes by when you're at market? Sits on your step and looks at your tools through the window. He wants to carve."

"He wants to make knives," Xanthe said. "I've seen his hands. They want edges, not lines."

"Then teach him edges before he takes a finger off doing it badly." Petra left the eggs on the sill. "You're going to die in here alone with a hundred unfinished pieces and someone's going to have to clear it all out, and it'll be me, so think of my back."

Xanthe thought about it after she left. Not seriously. But longer than they expected to.

* * *

Cyrus came on a Thursday, riding a grey mule he had named, absurdly, after a temple saint, and carrying bread wrapped in a cloth that had once been a banner of the usurper's ninth column. Xanthe smelled the loaves before he reached the bridge — warm crust, dark flour, the free cities' eastern recipe — and was already setting out the good knife when he ducked through the door.

"You're supposed to be rebuilding a granary," Xanthe said.

"We finished the granary. Now I'm inspecting roads, which is what temple knights do when temples stop wanting them and towns start needing them." He set the bread on the bench, looked at the half-carved woman on it, and tilted his head. "Open hands?"

"Cupped hands."

"Ah." He did not ask. He had stopped asking, somewhere on the road south of the mountain, what things meant; he had learned instead to wait, and the meanings arrived on their own. It was one of perhaps seven things Xanthe liked about him.

He looked well. That still caught Xanthe off guard sometimes — how well he looked. At Thornfell's southern pass he had been a man holding a wall with his body, hoarse from shouting, blood on him that was mostly not his own. In the weeks after, as testimony piled up around Vael Corvane's fall like driftwood after a flood — deserters' accounts, refugees' accounts, and beneath all of it, whispered through every shrine in the free cities, the testimony of the dead themselves, spoken through any god who would carry it — Cyrus had changed. Not lighter, exactly. Set down. A man who had been carrying a weight so long his spine had grown around it, and who was learning, slowly and with visible suspicion, to stand straight.

"The council at Marrowgate sent word," he said, tearing the bread. "They want the pass road rebuilt all the way to the salt mines. Labor's short. They're asking for guides who know Thornfell."

"Tell them to ask Mother Quill."

"I did. She quoted them a price I won't repeat in your house."

"Of course she did." Xanthe took bread and ate, and thought of Mother Quill as one thinks of a storm that passed east — enormous, remembered, no longer overhead. The debt had died with the reliquary. Or rather: Xanthe had refused her, in the dark under the mountain, with the ground shaking and the dead legionnaires turning like a tide, and Mother Quill had looked at them for a long moment and then done the most Quill thing imaginable — she had helped them escape, free of charge, saying only, "Now we're square twice over, carver, and I collect nothing, ever, from someone who tells me no with their whole chest." There was no debt between them anymore. There was something better and more dangerous: respect. Xanthe slept fine anyway. Mostly.

"And you?" Cyrus asked. "Marlowe says you turned down the Ashen shrine commission. The offering figures. Twelve of them, stone-bone, the priests want them for the solstice."

"I didn't turn it down. I said I'd do it when it was done." Xanthe shrugged. "I carve slow now."

"You carve the same. You just don't lie about how long things take."

That was probably true, and Xanthe resented it, and Cyrus smiled into his bread because he knew.

Outside, the millrace ran loud with autumn water. A cart went by on Tanner's Row, and someone called a greeting to someone else, and a dog answered, and all of it reached Xanthe plainly, cleanly, at exactly the distance and depth that living sound reaches a living ear. No voices underneath. No dead murmuring up through the floorboards of the world, no last words leaking out of keepsakes and doorposts and other people's jewelry. When Xanthe walked through Hollowmere now, they heard only the town. Only the living, arguing and laughing and lying to each other about the price of wool.

People assumed it must be quiet for them now. Xanthe had not found the words to explain that it was not quiet. Silence where voices had been is not silence; it is an absence with a shape, and you learn its outline the way your tongue learns a missing tooth. Some days the shape was rest. Some days it was a wound wearing the costume of rest.

But the godling lived. That was the arithmetic Xanthe performed every night before sleep, and it always summed. Somewhere beyond the shrines, a young god ran green up the hillsides of Thornfell, and the old gods spoke openly with the free cities, and the usurper's armies had broken like a wave on the defile Cyrus had chosen with a memory the temple stole and could not keep. The price had been Xanthe's hearing. The hearing had been thirty years of other people's deaths poured into their skull without consent. On balance — most days, on balance —

"You've gone somewhere," Cyrus said.

"I'm deciding if survival was worth it."

"And?"

"It cost too much," Xanthe said. "And it was worth it. Both are true and I'm keeping both, and if you tell anyone I said the second part I'll deny it to your face."

Cyrus laughed — really laughed, the rare one — and reached for the second loaf, and for a while neither of them talked, because the bread was good and the fire was warm and neither of them had to perform anything in this room. That was the other thing Xanthe liked about him, the one they'd have put above the seven if asked under torture: Cyrus did not require performance. He had seen Xanthe bargain with the hungry dead. He had seen them choose. There was nothing left to pretend.

* * *

He stayed until the light went gold and then left, because he lodged at the refectory and the mule had opinions about dusk. At the door he paused, the way he always paused, and Xanthe knew what was coming because he did it every visit and Xanthe allowed it every visit and neither of them called it a ritual, though it was one.

"Anything?" he asked quietly.

Xanthe shook their head. "Nothing. Same as last time. Same as always."

He nodded. It was for him as much as for her — for the sister he had witnessed die at Coldford, whose name the temple had scrubbed from his mouth along with everything else, and whose face had come back to him whole on the day his memories returned, arriving like a knife coming home. He had hoped, for a while, that whatever Xanthe had given up might have left a crack, a residue, some channel through which the dead could still whisper. Xanthe had checked too, those first weeks. Held every carving to their ear. Sat in the charnel caves with their palms flat on the stacked bones of strangers.

Nothing. The deafness was total, and it was the deal, and it was honored, and somewhere in that totality was a kind of faith: the gods had taken exactly what was promised and nothing more. You could be bitter about that or you could be grateful. Xanthe chose by the day.

"Solstice," Cyrus said instead of goodbye. "Come to Thornfell for the solstice. The godling will be there — Ashen's bringing it up through the shrines, all twelve way-stations, the whole procession. Everyone who was in the mountain is invited, which means you, whether you sulk about invitations or not."

"I don't sulk."

"You're sulking right now, standing up."

"Get out of my workshop."

He went, grinning, and Xanthe stood in the doorway and watched him ride the mule across the bridge with the banner-cloth folded in his saddlebag, and the evening came down blue and cold over Hollowmere, and the lamps lit one by one along the Row like a slow thought.

* * *

That night Xanthe worked late.

The woman with cupped hands stood finished on the bench, and beside her Xanthe had begun a second figure — a knight, kneeling, sword point-down, head bowed, and around the base of it a border of small carved hands, dozens of them, reaching. It was not a commission. It was not for sale. It was for the shelf in the back room, next to the oath-stone and the empty reliquary, where Xanthe kept the story that had unmade and remade them.

Their hands moved in the lamplight, sure and slow, and Xanthe listened — truly listened, the only listening left to them — to the sounds a workshop makes when it is loved: the whisper of steel in bone, the tick of the cooling stove, the wind testing the shutters, the distant argument of the millrace. Living sounds. Enough.

Tomorrow the Vellane boy would come sit on the step, and Xanthe would open the door and put a practice blank in his hands and begin teaching him edges. Next month, maybe, the solstice road east, and a procession of shrines, and a godling running green through the hills. Tonight, the kneeling knight, and the border of hands.

Xanthe carved the last hand of the day, blew the dust from it, and set down the chisel.

Bone held no voices now. It held something better, they had decided — it held what the living chose to remember, and the choosing was the holiness, and it was enough. It had cost too much.

It was worth it.

Both were true. Xanthe kept both, banked the fire, and slept without dreams until morning.
