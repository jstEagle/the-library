## 2. A Face in Old Ink

The collector came to my stall on a Thursday, which was wrong for two reasons. First, Thursdays were slow at the Vell night market—half the lamps shuttered, the charm-sellers packing up early, the fishmongers hosing blood off the cobbles while the gulls argued over scraps. Second, he was dying. I could tell from the doorway of the lamplit arcade where he stood gathering himself, one hand pressed flat against the stone like the city might pitch him over if he let go.

Dying men don't come to buy charms. They come to sell things.

I'd learned that in another life, back when I wore gray and answered to an order instead of a market warden's schedule. The Ninth Order kept ledgers on collectors the way farmers keep ledgers on rain—what they hoarded, what they feared, what they'd trade when the end came close enough to smell. Old habit made me catalogue him before he'd crossed half the arcade: sixty-odd years, scholar's stoop, ink stains gone permanent on his right hand, coat too good to be poor and too worn to be rich. A man who had spent everything on paper.

He reached my stall and stood there breathing for a moment, working up to something. Around us the market murmured its Thursday murmur—a woman haggling over luck-knots she didn't believe in, someone frying onions two stalls down, the ever-present hum of Vell's null-stones eating any stray magic that dared so much as flicker. In Vell, my charms were honest work: knotted thread, river-glass, dried rowan, nothing that required me to be anything other than what I pretended to be. A trinket-man. A quiet man. Jonah Dravenmoor, who had been no one at all for five years, and had gotten very good at it.

"You're the charm seller," the collector said. "The one who does the true ones."

"I do knots and glass," I said. "They're pretty."

"They're more than pretty." He lowered himself onto the stool across from my table without asking, which told me how little time he thought he had left for courtesy. Up close he smelled of camphor and old vellum, and there was a tremor in him that wasn't fear—it was the body simply running out. "I've bought six of them over three years. The fever-charm you sold the cooper's wife worked. The sleep-knot for the harbormaster's daughter worked. You're wasting your talent, sir, but that's your business." His eyes were wet and bright and terribly awake. "My name is Corvus Hale. I was a reader at the Undermount Archive before they closed it. And I'm going to die before winter, and I would like to spend my last coin on something that works."

"What is it you want?" I asked, because the question underneath—I know what you are—was better left unasked by both of us.

"A quieting," he said. "Not for pain. For fear. I want to walk into the dark without it shouting at me. Can you do that?"

In the old days I could have done it with a word and a drop of blood, and the fear would have lifted out of him like steam. Here, in Vell, under the null-stones, with five years of careful nothing behind me, I did it the long way. Rowan bark, steeped; a knot tied seven times with the intent held steady through every turn; a wordless focus that cost me sweat rather than blood. It took ten minutes. He watched every second of it with the hunger of a man watching his last meal being cooked.

When I handed it to him—a small braided cord, smelling of bitter bark—he closed his fist around it and shuddered once, all over, like a horse shedding flies.

"And the payment," I said, "is whatever's in that bag you've been holding like a shield since you walked in."

His mouth twitched. "They said you were sharp." He set the bag on the table between us. It was oilcloth, wrapped twice, tied with string gone soft with age. "I've spent fifty years collecting prophecy. Most of it is fraud, some of it is madness, and maybe one book in a hundred is real—and 'real' only means it was written by someone who saw a piece of the truth and paid for it. This one is real." He untied the string with fingers that shook, not from illness alone. "It cost me my marriage, if you want the full ledger. My wife said I loved the past more than her. She was right. That's why I'm here alone, and that's why I'd rather it went to someone who'll read it than to a nephew who'll burn it."

"Mr. Hale—"

"It's not cursed," he said. "I checked. Believe me, I checked." He pushed it across the oilcloth-wrapped bundle until it bumped against my hand. "But I'll tell you what it is, because I'm not a cruel man and I liked your knots. It's a court-book. Prophecies out of the Hollow, transcribed before Vell sealed its gates. One hundred and twelve years old." He looked at me then with those wet bright eyes, and for one lurching second I felt completely, catastrophically seen. "And I never found a buyer for it because everyone who looked inside too long came back changed. Not harmed. Just—quiet. Like they'd heard their own name called from very far away."

The market noise seemed to recede. The onion-fryer, the haggling woman, the gulls—all of it pulled back like tide.

"Maybe take it to the archive," I said. My voice sounded fine. My hands weren't doing anything at all, which was how I knew.

"The archive is ash, son. Everything under the mountain is either ash or worse." He stood, steadied himself against the table, and managed something like a smile. "You have a good evening. Thank you for the cord. I'll use it well."

He walked away down the arcade, slower than he'd come, one hand around the rowan cord and the other trailing along the wall. I watched him go the way you watch a door close on a room you can't enter anymore. Then I sat alone in my lamplight with a hundred-year-old book I hadn't asked for, and I told myself I'd sell it unread to the first collector who came by Saturday.

I opened it that same night, of course. Curiosity is the one oath I never managed to keep.

* * *

My rooms above the map-shop were cold; Wren hadn't been home yet. That was ordinary. Wren kept cartographer's hours, which meant she arrived when the light died and left before it woke, and we passed each other in the doorway like ships trading cargo—kisses, tea, fragments of the day. Her drafting table took up most of the front room, buried under coastlines in progress. I cleared a space among her maps, lit both lamps, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

The book was heavy the way stones are heavy, as if weight were its native element. Calfskin covers gone the color of tea, brass corners green with age, no title. Inside, the paper was rag-made and soft, and the first forty pages were exactly what Hale had promised: transcriptions. Court-prophecy, the real kind, the kind that costs. The script changed hands four times in those forty pages—four different scribes, four different generations, all copying the same source. Whoever compiled this had chased it across decades. Had given his marriage to it, apparently.

The prophecies themselves were standard Hollow verse: the mountain dreams, the court counts, the debt comes due. I'd heard worse sung in taverns as children's rhymes. I turned pages with the detached speed of a man confirming a purchase is worthless, already composing Saturday's sales pitch—

And then the woodcut.

It was full-page, printed from a block some dead engraver had cut with obsessive care. A mountain gate—the Hollow gate, unmistakable, that split arch like a wound in the rock. Before it, a figure stood with her back to the viewer. Small in the frame. Holding something in one hand.

Underneath, in the old script: *She comes of her own walking, though she does not yet know the road. The hundredth soul, the last of the court's due. Mark her face.*

Mark her face.

The figure in the woodcut faced away. But the engraver had cheated the perspective—cartographer's cheating, the kind Wren used when a client wanted to see more than geometry allowed—and the woman's head was turned, just slightly, just enough.

I knew the line of that jaw. I knew the way her hair came loose at the temple when she was working, the strand she always blew at instead of pinning back. I knew the small scar above her eyebrow from the day a compass clamp snapped and I'd held her hand while she laughed at the blood.

The date beneath the plate read: *Cut in the year 1141.* One hundred and twelve years ago.

"No," I said aloud, to the empty room, in the reasonable tone you use with a dog barking at nothing. "No, that's—" I tilted the page toward the lamp. Ink doesn't lie about faces; that's the whole point of ink. Woodcut portraits are crude, yes, generic, yes—but this one wasn't. Someone had cut this face with love or terror or both, every line deliberate, and every line was hers. Wren. My Wren, standing before the mountain gate in a picture older than her grandmother, holding—I leaned closer—holding a rolled sheet of paper. A map.

I did the thing you're not supposed to do. I kept reading.

The verses beneath the woodcut were in a different hand again, tighter, hurried, the scribe pressing hard:

*Nine orders swore, nine orders paid.*
*A hundred souls to stay the blade.*
*Ninety-nine the mountain keeps;*
*the hundredth wakes, the hundredth—*

The page ended mid-line. The next leaf had been torn out. Not damaged—torn, cleanly, deliberately, sometime in the last century, by someone who had read this far and wanted no one else to follow.

I sat very still. In Vell, stillness is a discipline; the whole city practices it. Stillness so the wardens pass you by. Stillness so the null-stones have nothing to grip. I had made myself a monument of stillness for five years, and now I couldn't remember how it worked. My heart was loud as surf.

*The court's due.* I knew that phrase. Every swordspeaker knew it, though we'd been taught to say it lightly, the way you say a bogeyman's name to prove you're not afraid. When the Ninth Order ended the Border War, they hadn't won it—they'd *paid* for it. That was the story we were given as novices, wrapped in honor: sacrifice, the order's great glory. A hundred souls, delivered to the court beneath the mountain, in exchange for peace. We'd been taught to bow our heads at the number. No one had ever told us whose souls. No one had ever told us how the mountain collected what centuries later, when accounts ran short.

*The hundredth soul, the last of the court's due.*

Wren's key scraped the lock downstairs. I heard her come in, stamp the cold from her boots, call up softly—*"Jonah? You awake? I brought those plums you like"*—and every ordinary sound of her arriving home went through me like wire.

One hundred and twelve years ago, an engraver cut her face into wood, and a scribe wrote down that she would come walking to the gate, and someone tore out the ending because the ending was unbearable. The past hadn't stumbled onto us. The past had set a place at its table and waited, patient as debt, for us to arrive.

Her footsteps started up the stairs. I looked at the open book, at her face looking back at me from the century-old page, and I did the fastest, worst arithmetic of my life: hide it, or tell her.

The door opened. Wren stood there with a paper bag of plums and frost melting in her hair, and her eyes went straight past me to the table—to the book, open, glowing amber under the lamp—and I watched her smile die by degrees, replaced by something I had never seen on her face before.

Recognition.

"Where," she said quietly, setting down the plums without looking at them, "did you get that?"
