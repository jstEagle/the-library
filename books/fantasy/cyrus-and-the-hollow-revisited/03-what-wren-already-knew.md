## 3. What Wren Already Knew

The night Jonah told me, we were in the back room of the map shop, and I was inking a coastline.

I remember that detail the way you remember the last ordinary thing before everything stops being ordinary. The coastline was invented—a client wanted a fantasy of a country that didn't exist, all soft beaches and safe harbors, and I was drawing it with the kind of care I usually reserved for places that were real. My lamp was trimmed low. The kettle had just gone off the fire. And Jonah stood in the doorway holding a book wrapped in oilcloth like it was something that had died.

"Wren," he said. "I need to show you something, and I need you to not look at me while you do it."

That's Jonah. He thinks if he can control the order of things—the order of words, the order of looking—he can control what they do to you. Five years of loving him and I still hadn't cured him of it.

"Show me, then."

He unwrapped the book on the drafting table, next to my ink pot and my blotting sand and the little brass compass my father gave me, and turned to a page near the middle, and I looked.

A woodcut. Old, cracked, the ink gone brown at the edges. A mountain with a gate in its root, and before the gate, a figure. A woman. Short hair. A coat with the collar up. A satchel slung across the body the way I slung mine, because my right shoulder ached if I carried weight on it and I'd been carrying it that way since I was nineteen.

The print was dated one hundred and twelve years before either of us was born.

"That's not me," I said.

"I know," Jonah said.

"It can't be me. I wasn't born."

"I know." His voice had gone careful, the way it went careful when he was handling me, and I hated it, and I hated that I understood it. "But look at the coat. Look at the—"

"The satchel. I see it." I set down my pen. My hand was steady, which surprised me. "Jonah. Why did you bring this to me at nine o'clock at night instead of telling me at breakfast like a person?"

"Because at breakfast you'd have had the whole day to think about it, and I thought—" He stopped. He did that thing where he pressed his thumb against his own wrist, the one he did when he was holding something down. "I thought maybe if I showed you at night, you could sleep on it. Sleep makes things smaller."

"Sleep makes things bigger," I said. "You know that better than anyone. You just hide in the day so you don't have to dream in front of me."

That landed. I saw it land. And then, because I am not a kind person when I'm frightened, I said the thing I had been not-saying for six years:

"I dream about the mountain too."

* * *

He didn't say anything for a long time. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere out in the shop, the draft under the door moved a hanging chart of the northern coast, and it breathed against the wall like something sleeping.

"Since when," he said finally.

"Since I was a child. Since before I could say the word *mountain* properly. There's a gate at the bottom of it, in the dark, and the gate is open, and there are lights inside—hanging lights, hundreds of them, like a festival, except nobody's celebrating." I kept my eyes on the woodcut because it was easier than his face. "And in the dream I know all their names. Every light. That's the worst part. I wake up grieving a thousand people and I don't know a single one of them."

"How often?"

"Used to be every night. Now it's—" I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. "Every night again. Since the autumn."

Jonah sat down across from me. Slowly, the way you sit down when your legs have told you they're only provisionally yours. He turned the book toward himself and read the page below the woodcut, his lips moving, and whatever was written there made him close his eyes.

"Read it out loud," I said.

"No."

"Jonah."

"It calls you—" He stopped. Started again. "It calls you *the court's due*. It says the debt comes to the gate, and the gate knows its own, and—" He shut the book. "It's a hundred years old. It's a madman's woodcut. Prophecy books are cheap at auctions for a reason, Wren, they're—"

"Jonah." I put my hand flat on the closed cover. "What did you see in the dream? Yours."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide. I had watched him decide that a hundred times—whether to tell me the truth, whether I was safe to tell things to—and I had never once made it easy for him, because I didn't want a man who told me things easily. I wanted a man who told me things at cost.

"There are lights," he said. "And one of them goes out. And I know which one."

"Whose?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. I turned my hand over on the book cover, palm up, an old habit between us that meant *yours, if you want it*, and he took it, and his hand was cold.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I have to ask you, and you're going to be angry, and you should be."

"I'm already angry. Spend it."

"When you were a child. Before you came to Vell. Did anyone—did you ever sign anything? A paper, a ledger, anything with your name on it that you didn't understand?"

I laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high, the laugh of a person standing at the edge of something.

"Why would you ask me that?"

"Because the book says the debt was *signed into the bloodline*. Because the heralds collect on signatures. Because—" He stopped, and I watched him choose the cruel version over the kind one, which is the thing I loved most about him. "Because I think you did. And I think you remember."

* * *

Here is what I had never told Jonah, in six years, in a city built on the principle that magic doesn't exist, in a life I had built plank by plank out of ink and vellum and Tuesday evenings.

When I was nine years old, a woman came to our house in the fen country. I remember her boots—they were wrong for the fens, too fine, cracked at the seams from somewhere drier. She sat at our table and she spread out a document, and my mother went white and took my little brother into the other room, and the woman said to me, in a voice like a door closing: *You are the last of the installment. Sign, and your family is paid up in full, forever.*

And I signed.

I remember the pen. I remember it was a good pen, heavier than ours, and I remember thinking, with the strange practicality of a nine-year-old, that we should keep it. I do not remember signing. That is the thing I have never been able to explain to anyone, not the priests, not the doctors in Lowmarket, not myself at three in the morning. I remember the pen and I remember the woman's boots and I remember my mother's white face, and between the taking-up of the pen and the setting-down of it there is a gap, smooth as a page torn out.

I have built my whole life in that gap.

I told Jonah all of it. The boots. The pen. The torn-page feeling. He listened without touching me, which was correct; there are things you can't be touched through.

"So I'm not being collected," I said, when I was done. "I was *paid out*. Twenty years ago. I'm not a debt, I'm a purchase, and the receipt is a hundred years old, and the shop—" I gestured at the walls, at the charts, at the whole small lit life of it. "The shop was me hiding in the margins of my own sale."

"Or it's just a shop," Jonah said. "And you're just Wren, and a mad woodcut and a dream and a woman in bad boots don't get to take that."

"You don't believe that."

"No," he admitted. "But I want to, and I've learned to move toward what I want. It's the only navigation I trust."

I almost smiled. That's the thing about terror nobody warns you: it doesn't crowd out love. It compresses it. Everything true gets smaller and harder and heavier, like a star.

"Here's what frightens me," I said. "It's not the mountain. It's not the lights, or the gate, or whatever's owed. I've been dreaming of it my whole life; the mountain and I are old acquaintances." I turned my palm up again, and this time he didn't take it, so I closed my fingers around the air, around the shape of the thing I was trying to say. "What frightens me is the gap. The torn page. Because if they can take the signing from me, they can take other things. They can take the shop. They can take the coastlines. They can take you, Jonah, not by hurting you but by cutting you out of me the way my signature was cut out, and I'll wake up one morning grieving a thousand lights and not know why one of them was yours."

The lamp flickered. Neither of us moved.

"That's what I'm afraid of," I said. "Not fate. Forgetting. I'm afraid of being *edited*."

Jonah stood up. He came around the table, and he knelt—Jonah, who knelt for no one, who had a swordspeaker's spine even pretending to be a charm-seller—and he took my face in both hands the way you hold a compass, level, precise, precious.

"Then we write it down," he said. "Everything. Tonight. Every dream, the boots, the pen, the gap, all of it, in both our hands, in ink, in duplicate. If they're going to edit you, they'll have to edit a witness too." His thumb moved along my jaw, and his voice dropped into the register I had only heard once before, the night he told me what he was. "And Wren—I swear to you on the last oath I have left, whatever the court bought, whatever's due at that gate, I will go down and get it back. I will un-sell you if it's the last thing my order ever makes me do."

"Your order's dead," I said.

"Then it'll be the first thing I do as no one at all."

I kissed him. It was not a tender kiss. It was the kiss of two people signing something they can read perfectly well and choosing to sign anyway, and when we broke apart, the lamp had steadied, and the invented coastline lay between us, soft and safe and fake.

"Start writing," I said.

He pulled paper toward him. And that was when we both heard it—the front bell of the shop, past midnight, past any honest hour, ringing once and then again, impatient, and boots on my floorboards. Too many boots. And under the door, sliding through the draft like a card, a folded paper with the warden's seal pressed in grey wax.

Jonah went very still beside me.

"They're not here for me," he said quietly. "Wren. They're not here for me."
