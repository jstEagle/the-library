## 4. The Debt of the Ninth Order

Maren Voss lived above a tannery in Saltrow, where the smell of rendered hide and lye soaked into the walls so thoroughly that even the rain came down smelling of old death. Jonah climbed the outside stairs at dusk, each step announcing him with a creak that sounded like an accusation.

He hadn't seen her in five years. He had told himself it was because she'd want nothing to do with him. The truth was simpler and worse: he couldn't bear to see what the order's fall had made of her, because he was afraid it would show him what it had made of himself.

The door was unlocked. Of course it was. Maren had never once in her life locked anything, on the theory that a swordspeaker who needed locks had already lost.

She sat by a window with a bottle of something amber and illegal, watching the tannery workers below with the flat attention of someone who had stopped expecting anything from the world. She was fifty-three and looked seventy. Her hair, once the color of hammered bronze, had gone the gray of old ash. But her hands were still a swordspeaker's hands—scarred across every knuckle, steady even holding a bottle.

"Jonah Dravenmoor," she said without turning. "I smelled you coming. Guilt has a scent. Like copper and unwashed promises."

"Maren."

"Don't 'Maren' me." She turned then, and her eyes did the thing they had always done—went through him like a blade through wet paper, finding everything he'd hidden. "Five years. Five years of silence, and now you climb my stairs at dusk looking like a man whose dog died and whose house is on fire. Sit down before you fall down."

He sat. The room was one room, mostly bottles, a bed heaped with books instead of blankets. He recognized some of them. Order texts. Forbidden ones. She'd kept them through everything—the trials, the disbanding, the purges. Kept them the way you keep a wound open: to remember why it happened.

"You've been drinking since noon," he said.

"I've been drinking since the order fell. Noon is just when I start again." She poured a second cup without asking and pushed it across the table. "Drink. You'll want it for what you're about to ask me."

He didn't touch it. "There's a book—"

"There's always a book." She drank. "Prophecy books are like rats, Jonah. Every city has more than it admits, and they only surface when something's rotting underneath."

"It shows Wren. Standing before the mountain gate. Dated a hundred and twelve years ago." His voice held. He'd trained it to hold. "And it names her soul as *the court's due*."

For a long moment Maren said nothing. Then she set the bottle down with exaggerated care, the way a person does when their hands have started shaking and they don't want anyone to know.

"What else does it say?" she asked quietly.

"That's all. A woodcut and two lines of ink."

"Two lines." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a snapped bowstring. "You came all this way for two lines. You should have come for the whole ledger."

"What ledger?"

"The Ninth Order's ledger." She stood, crossed to the heap of books, and began digging—not searching, digging, like a woman who knew exactly where the body was buried and had spent years deciding whether to dig it up. "Sit there. Don't drink. Listen."

She pulled out a slim volume bound in gray leather gone green with damp. It had no title. It had no need of one; certain things announce themselves.

"Do you know how the Border War ended?" she asked.

"They signed the Armistice of—" 

"The Armistice was the *paperwork*, boy. Wars don't end with paperwork. They end when somebody pays." She set the ledger on the table between them but didn't open it. Her hand rested on the cover like a woman restraining a dog. "Year 1113. The war had eaten eleven years and both kingdoms' sons. Your grandfather's generation. The Ninth Order was sent to end it—that was the whole point of us, in case no one ever told you. Not duels. Not glory. We were the instrument a kingdom reaches for when it wants something done that it can't say out loud."

Jonah felt cold starting somewhere below his ribs. "Done how?"

Maren opened the ledger.

The pages were columns. Names—hundreds of names, written in a hand he recognized, because he'd learned his letters from that same hand. Grandmaster Corvane Aldous. The man whose statue had stood in the order's courtyard. The man whose oath Jonah had sworn at nineteen with his hand on a sword and his heart full of fire.

Each name had a date beside it. And beside the dates, a single recurring notation:

*Rendered unto the Hollow.*

"There are a hundred names here," Maren said. "A hundred souls, sold to the court beneath the mountain. That was the price. The Hollow ended the war in a single night—every soldier on the border woke up unable to remember why they were fighting, and the armies walked home. Miraculous. Celebrated. Songs were written." She tapped the page. "Nobody asked what it cost, because the invoice went to us. To the order. We became debtors. The Hollow doesn't take payment all at once, Jonah. It takes installments. One soul at a time, on its own schedule, and we swore oaths binding our order—and our heirs—to pay."

"Heirs." The word came out of him like a stone coughed up. "There are no heirs. The order is dead."

"The order is *disbanded*. Dead is different." She finally looked at him straight. "An oath sworn on swordspeech doesn't die when the sword does. You know that better than anyone. It sleeps. It waits. And the debt passes down the line of succession until it's paid in full."

"How many installments have been paid?"

She counted silently, her lips moving, and he watched her arrive at a number she clearly already knew by heart.

"Ninety-nine."

The room tilted. Or maybe he did. He gripped the table.

"Ninety-nine souls over a hundred and twelve years," Maren said. "Sicknesses that came too fast. Accidents too clean to be accidents. People who vanished, who drowned in dry rooms, who simply stopped being *themselves* one morning—empty, smiling, gone. I tracked them for twenty years after the fall, thinking if I understood the pattern I could break it. I couldn't. The Hollow chooses. And the hundredth name—" she turned the last page of the ledger, and there it was, fresh ink in ancient handwriting, as though the book had been waiting to write itself—

"—was never a name at all. Look."

The final entry read:

*The hundredth due: one soul, unmarked, unsworn, unbought. The court will choose. When the sign appears—a face before the gate, foretold in ink—the debt comes due within the turning of one season.*

Jonah stared at the words until they blurred. A face before the gate. Foretold in ink.

Wren, standing before the mountain, drawn by a carver's knife a century before she was born.

"No," he said. "No. She's not of the order. She's not sworn. She's not—she draws maps, Maren. She smells like cedar and ink and she hums when she works and she has *nothing to do with any of this*—"

"Which is exactly why they chose her." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "Unmarked. Unsworn. Unbought. The Hollow wanted a soul with no debts of its own—clean collateral. And it wanted the payment to hurt. That's the arithmetic of oaths, Jonah. A debt means nothing unless collecting it ruins someone."

"So it's my fault." He heard his own voice go flat and distant, the way it did in the worst moments. "My oath. My order. My inheritance. She's paying for a war she's never heard of, ended before her grandmother was born, because men with swords got tired of fighting and decided to buy peace with other people's souls."

"Yes." Maren didn't flinch. She never had. It was one of the reasons he'd loved her, and one of the reasons he'd run. "It's your fault. Ours. Mine too—I signed the succession clause myself, thirty years ago, young and stupid and certain the debt would die with us. It didn't. It found you. And now it's found her."

He stood up. His legs worked, which surprised him. Everything felt very far away and very clear, the way the world looks from the top of a fall.

"What do I do?"

"Nothing," Maren said. "That's the honest answer. Run. Take her east past the salt flats, past the wards, somewhere the mountain's reach thins out. Maybe you buy her a year. Maybe ten. The Hollow is patient—it waited a century for this installment; it can wait out your little life."

"And the other answer? The dishonest one?"

Maren smiled, and for a second he saw the woman who had trained him—sharp, brilliant, half-mad with conviction.

"The other answer is under the mountain. The Sovereign holds every oath you ever swore, Jonah. Every promise, every vow, every word you spoke with the sword in your voice—they're all filed down there like deeds to houses. As long as those oaths stand, the debt stands. Break the oaths, and—" she shrugged, and the gesture cost her something; he could see it cost her something— "and there's no precedent. No one's ever tried. Because everyone knows what happens to oathbreakers."

"What happens?"

"They lose everything the oaths gave them." She picked the bottle back up. Her hands were steady again now, which meant she'd made her peace with whatever she was about to say. "Everything. All of it. Down to the last spark."

Jonah looked at his hands. Swordspeaker's hands, scarred and silent, five years of careful nothing. He thought of Wren's face in the old ink. He thought of the way she'd looked at him last night across the market stall, tired and happy, and said, *Some days I think you're the most dangerous thing in this city, Jonah Dravenmoor, and other days I think you're just mine.*

The season was already turning. He could feel it, the way you feel weather coming in a scar.

"Where are the underways," he said. "The old approaches. Maren—you mapped them once. Before."

"I burned those maps." She said it fast, too fast.

"Maren."

"*I burned those maps.*" Then she closed her eyes, and something in her face collapsed—not into drunkenness, into decision. She hauled herself up, went to the hearth, and pried loose the third stone from the left with a knife she kept in her boot. From the cavity she drew an oilcloth packet, small and stiff with age.

"One copy," she said. "I told myself I burned them all. I told myself that for five years." She pressed it into his hands. "Saltrow stairs, beneath the aqueduct, third arch. Follow the water against its flow. And Jonah—if you meet anything down there wearing a face you love, it isn't that thing. Nothing down there loves you. Remember that."

He tucked the packet inside his coat, against his heart, where lies and maps and old sins are usually carried.

At the door he paused. "Why keep them? Why keep any of it?"

Maren looked out the window at the tannery yard, at the workers scraping hides pale and new.

"Because someday someone would climb these stairs asking how to fix it," she said. "And I wanted to be the kind of person who had an answer ready. Even if I was drunk. Even if it was too late." She raised the bottle to him. "Go save your mapmaker, oathbreaker. Make us all liars."

He went down the stairs two at a time, and the city of Vell opened around him, lamplit and ordinary, full of people who had no idea they lived on top of a debt.

He was three streets from Saltrow when he saw the herald.

It stood at the mouth of Copperlane, where the lanterns didn't quite reach, and it wore the face of his dead brother.

* * *

Tomas had been dead eleven years. Jonah had carried him out of the Border War's last battle himself, or what was left of him, and buried him under a rowan tree with his own hands. He knew the set of those shoulders. The crooked half-smile. The way Tomas used to tilt his head when he was about to say something that would get them both in trouble.

"Hello, little brother," the thing said, in a voice remembered rather than heard. "You've been hard to find. Well. No. You've been easy to find. You've been *hard to face*."

Jonah's hand went to his hip, to the absence of a sword, the habit of a lifetime.

"You're not real."

"I'm real enough. Real as a receipt." The herald stepped forward into the light, and it was Tomas, perfectly, terribly, down to the scar on his chin from the fence he fell off when he was nine. "The court sent me because it thought you might listen to family. The debt is due, Jonah. One season. Bring her to the gate yourself and the parting will be gentle. Refuse, and the collection gets creative. The Hollow has a hundred and twelve years of practice at creative."

"She's not yours to collect."

"She was never ours to begin with. She's *yours* to deliver. That's what inheritance means." The smile widened a fraction too far, the jaw hinging a degree past human. "But you already know that. You've known since you opened the book. So run along home, little brother. Kiss her while you still can. And when you decide—run east, or go down—know that we'll be watching either way."

"We?"

The herald's eyes flickered upward, and for one instant the face slid sideways, like a mask slipping, and underneath there was nothing but a smooth gray blankness with a voice behind it.

"The court keeps all its lanterns lit," it said. "Even the ones that haven't been hung yet."

Then the alley was empty, and the lanterns of Copperlane burned ordinary and dim, and Jonah Dravenmoor stood alone in the dark with a stolen map against his heart and one season left to decide what he was willing to become.

He ran home to Wren.
