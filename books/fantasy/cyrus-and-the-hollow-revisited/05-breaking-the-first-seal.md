## 5. Breaking the First Seal

The knock came at half past eleven, and Wren knew before she opened the door that it was wrong.

Not the knock itself—three raps, polite enough—but the hour. Nobody knocked on her door at half past eleven with good intentions. Cartographers kept daylight hours. Her neighbors kept earlier ones. And Jonah, who had left the market an hour ago with his tray of tin charms and his careful, forgettable smile, was two streets away in his rented room above the chandlery, asleep or pretending to be.

She set down her pen. The map under it—half of the Saltreach delta inked in her finest line work, the kind of detail that had won her a commission from the Harbor Guild last spring—stayed where it was. She looked at it for one long breath, memorizing the curve of the river, and then she went to answer.

Two wardens stood on her step. City gray, iron badges, the flat eyes of men who had learned not to blink at anything. Behind them, at the mouth of the alley, stood a third figure in the hooded coat of an inspector, and something about the way the lamplight caught his jaw made Wren's stomach fold over on itself.

"Mapmaker Halloway?" the taller warden said.

"That's me."

"We have cause to search these premises. Licensed under the Vell Charter, article nine." He held up a folded paper stamped with the city's seal—the mountain-and-gate, which everyone in this city wore like a warning. "You may stand aside, or you may make this difficult."

"Search for what?"

The warden's expression didn't change, which told her everything. Men who didn't know what they were looking for said so. Men who did just waited.

She stepped aside.

* * *

They went through her shop the way water goes through paper—not fast, but thorough, soaking into everything. The tall one took the front room: the drafting table, the drawers of instruments, the rolled maps in their cedar tube rack. The other went up the narrow stair to her sleeping loft without asking, and she heard him moving through her things with the unhurried patience of a man being paid by the hour.

Wren stood in the middle of her own life and tried to breathe.

Here was the thing about Vell that every child learned before they learned to read: the city hated magic the way scarred skin hates fire. Not with argument—with reflex. The Charter had been written in blood a century ago, after the Sundering, after whatever had happened under the mountain that the histories refused to name plainly. Unlicensed working was not a crime like theft. It was a crime like plague. You didn't punish it. You burned it out.

And Jonah—

Jonah, who warmed her tea kettle with a word when he thought she wasn't looking. Who had once, drunk on festival wine, hummed three notes of swordspeech against the back of her neck and made the candle flames stand up straight as soldiers, and then gone white as bone and sworn he'd never touched magic in his life. Who sold charms in the night market that were *supposed* to be tricks, sleight of hand, tin and misdirection, except that Wren had watched a fevered dockworker clutch one to his chest and sweat his sickness out by morning.

She had known. She had known for a year, maybe longer, and she had said nothing, because saying nothing was how you kept a thing in a city like Vell. Happiness this good had to be hidden to be held.

Now someone else had done the saying.

The warden upstairs came down holding her washstand mirror. He turned it over in his gloved hands, frowning at the back, and Wren felt the floor tilt beneath her before she understood why.

Because there, scratched into the silvering in a fine, looping hand—*her* hand, from a year ago, a joke, a private little vanity she'd never shown anyone—was a compass rose drawn in a script that wasn't any alphabet used in Vell.

It was a ward. A minor one. Jonah had taught it to her the same week he'd taught her to pick a lock, laughing, calling it *cartographer's insurance*. She'd carved it into the mirror so her reflections would always show her true north. She had forgotten it existed until this exact moment, standing in her ruined shop, watching a warden hold her own handwriting up to the light.

"This isn't licensed," the warden said.

"It's decorative."

"It's script." He turned to the inspector in the doorway, and Wren saw the man's face now fully, and her stomach finished what it had started.

He was young. Younger than her. He had a cartographer's face, actually—a precise, interested face—and she knew him. Tomas Ellery. He'd bought three of her coastal charts two months ago, paid full price, complimented her shading technique. He'd asked her, over the counter, whether she ever drew maps of places she hadn't been. Whether some of her coastlines seemed too accurate for a woman who'd never sailed past the harbor bar.

She'd thought he was flirting.

"Miss Halloway," Tomas said gently, "your coastlines are too good. That's all any of this is. Someone noticed that your unexplored shores match no survey and every survey. We ran the comparison. Then we ran your associates." He spread his hands. "I'm sorry. It wasn't personal."

*It was always going to be personal,* Wren thought. In Vell it was never anything else.

* * *

They found the charms at midnight.

Not in her shop—in his room above the chandlery, because of course they went there next, and of course Wren insisted on coming, insisting with the particular ferocity of a woman who knows exactly what will be found and has already decided how much of it she can absorb herself. The tall warden pried up the floorboard under Jonah's cot and lifted out the oilcloth bundle, unwrapped it on the bare mattress, and there lay forty-one charms in a neat grid, each one humming faintly, each one warm to the touch.

Forty-one small crimes. Forty-one reasons.

Jonah stood in the doorway in his shirt, hair sleep-mussed, playing bewildered, and he was *good* at it—five years of practice good—and Wren watched him do it and understood with terrible clarity that bewilderment would save neither of them. Because the warden was already reading the maker's mark off the largest charm, and Jonah's marks were subtle, but subtle only worked on people who weren't looking, and these people were looking.

"Where did you get these?" the warden asked.

"I buy stock at the river auctions," Jonah said. "Anyone can check my ledgers."

"We'll check your ledgers." The warden weighed the bundle in his hand. "And we'll check the auction rolls. And then we'll check the assay office, because I'm told there's a clerk there who swears he's seen you buying silver that doesn't tarnish."

Silver that didn't tarnish. Spell-silver. Wren closed her eyes.

They were going to take him. Not tonight—there would be questions first, papers, the slow machinery of the Charter courts—but within the week. And the questions in a Charter court were not questions like *where did you buy this.* They were questions like *how long,* and *what else,* and *who taught you,* and they were asked in rooms below ground with instruments that had been legal since the Sundering precisely because no one wanted to know what happened in them.

Jonah caught her eye across the little room. Five years of careful invisibility, and in one glance he said everything he'd never let himself say: *don't fight them. Don't spend yourself on me.*

Wren smiled at him, small and steady, and lied to him with her whole face.

* * *

They released her at two in the morning—"for now," the tall warden said, "the premises are sealed until the inquiry"—and Wren walked home through empty streets with her arms wrapped around herself and her mind moving faster than her feet.

The inquiry would start with the shop. Everything in the shop. Every map, every chart, every instrument. And her maps were not evidence of *his* crime, but they were evidence of *hers*—coastlines too true, mountains too exact, the impossible accuracy that Tomas Ellery had traced back through her pen to something that could only be called sight. If the court dug into her, they would find the mirror-ward, and if they found the mirror-ward they would find who taught it to her, and the thread from her to Jonah would be a short one, and a short thread is easy to pull.

Unless there was nothing left to find.

She stopped at her own door, key in hand, and stood there in the cold while the idea finished forming, whole and terrible, like a coastline rising out of fog.

Her maps. Nineteen years of work. The delta she'd started yesterday. The commissioned chart of the Saltreach that the Harbor Guild expected in six weeks. The atlas of the northern passes she'd begun at fifteen, page by patient page, the thing that had made her *her*, the talent that had lifted a dockworker's daughter into a shop of her own with her name painted on the glass.

Everything she had built. Everything she was, on paper.

Burn it, and there was no evidence. Burn it, and there was no proof of second sight, no impossible coastlines, no thread to pull. Burn it, and she was just a mediocre mapmaker whose shop got raided by mistake, and Jonah was a tin-trinket seller who bought bad stock at auction, and both of them were small enough for the machinery to pass over.

Keep it, and they would take him below the Charter house, and she knew—I mean *knew*, the way she knew coastlines—what came back up those stairs. Sometimes it came back up. Usually it was less than what went down.

Wren opened the door. Sealed premises meant sealed, but seals were wax and wax was hers; she'd drawn the city's own boundary-marks for six years and knew every seal in Vell the way other women knew their neighbors' faces. She slipped the wax off its hinge with a thumbnail and a whispered apology to the law.

Then she lit the stove.

* * *

She fed it one map at a time, because a stove is honest work and deserves honesty.

First the commissions—forgive me, Guildmaster Osen, forgive me, six weeks of promised labor gone to flame. Then the private pieces, the ones nobody had ever seen: the delta she'd finished at dawn yesterday, still tacky with fixative, curling brown at the edges like a living thing dying. The northern passes. The survey of the drowned quarter that had taken her two summers and cost her two fingers' worth of frostbite. Page by page by page.

She did not cry until the atlas. She cried then, steadily and silently, the way she did everything, feeding the fire with fifteen years of herself while the smoke thickened and the room filled with the smell of burning linseed and her own ambition. The compass rose on the cover caught the light one last time before it blackened—true north, her father had joked, drawn by a girl who couldn't find her way to church—and then it was gone, and the fire took the rest.

Last, the mirror. She carried it downstairs wrapped in her apron and broke it against the hearthstone, and the ward-script shattered into a hundred bright fragments that she shoveled into the coals, and she stood there watching her own handwriting melt, and she thought, with a calm that frightened her:

*I am choosing him. Right now. This is what it looks like.*

Not the way it looked in ballads—no sword drawn, no grand refusal, no thunder. Just a woman alone at three in the morning feeding her life into a stove, page by page, because the alternative was a room below the Charter house and a man she loved coming back up those stairs smaller than he went down.

The last thing to burn was a scrap she almost saved: a rough sketch, pencil only, of two figures walking side by side along a road that bent toward a mountain. She'd drawn it weeks ago without knowing why. It had felt important. She'd meant to ask Jonah about it.

She held it over the flames a long moment.

Then she let it go, because important things leave traces, and traces were the enemy now.

By four the stove was cold ash and her shop was a shell—empty racks, bare walls, a ghost of smoke soaked into the plaster that no amount of scrubbing would hide. She sat on the floor amid the wreckage with soot on her hands and her heart beating very slowly, very evenly, like a metronome counting down to something.

Which was when she heard the tap at the back window.

Soft. Twice. A rhythm she knew—*shave and a haircut*—taught to her by the same man who'd taught her the lock-picking and the ward-script, back when they were both pretending to be ordinary people falling in love.

She crossed the dark shop and opened the shutters, and Jonah crouched in the alley below, gray-faced in the pre-dawn, with a packed bag over one shoulder and blood on his knuckles and terror written across him in letters plainer than any script.

"They're not waiting for the inquiry," he whispered. "There's a herald come down from the mountain. Wren—it's asking for you by name."
