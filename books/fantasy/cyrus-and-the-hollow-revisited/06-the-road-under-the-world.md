## 6. The Road Under the World

Maren's directions were written on the back of a tavern bill, in a hand that shook like a man trying to hold down something alive.

*Down through the tannery sluice. Past the third grate, take the left fork where the old water used to run. Don't light anything. Don't speak any word you mean. The underways remember.*

Jonah read it three times by the light of a shuttered lantern while Wren packed her satchel with the efficiency of someone who had already grieved what she was leaving behind. She'd burned the maps. He kept seeing it — her kneeling in the alley behind the charm stall, feeding five years of work into a brazier while the wardens kicked doors two streets over. The ink had gone up green and gold, all those careful coastlines and mountain passes curling into smoke that smelled like cedar and loss.

She hadn't cried. She'd just said, *Go*, and he had gone.

Now they stood at the mouth of the tannery sluice, where Vell's refuse met Vell's secrets, and the smell alone could have stripped paint.

"You first," Wren said. "You're the one who knows how to be quiet."

"I'm the one who knows how to hide," Jonah corrected. "Quiet is different."

"Then do both." She pulled the collar of her coat up against the stink and gave him the ghost of a smile. It didn't reach her eyes. Nothing had reached her eyes since the night she told him about the signature she couldn't remember signing, the one that had lived inside her since childhood like a splinter working toward the heart.

He went first.

The sluice was a throat of wet stone, barely shoulder-width, and they moved through it bent double, hands trailing walls slick with something Jonah refused to name. Behind them, muffled by stone and distance, came the sound of the raid continuing — whistles, boots, a warden's amplified voice reciting the anti-magic statutes as though the city itself needed reminding. Someone had informed on him. Five years of careful nothing, undone by a single denunciation. He didn't know who. He suspected everyone.

At the third grate, Maren's directions held true. Beyond it, the passage opened into a vaulted channel where an ancient river had once run, its bed now dry and furred with pale dust. Their footsteps made no echo. That was the first wrong thing.

The second wrong thing was the warmth.

"It shouldn't be warm," Wren whispered. "Underground. This deep."

"No," Jonah agreed. "It shouldn't."

The underways beneath Vell were older than Vell. Older than the anti-magic edicts, older than the court's treaties, older than the war his order had ended with a hundred souls. They had been dug by people whose names had been eaten, for purposes that had been buried with them. And somewhere far below and far away, beneath the mountain at the edge of every map Wren had ever drawn, the Hollow waited with its ledger open and its interest accruing.

They walked until the dark became total, then lit the lantern anyway, because total dark was its own kind of madness. The flame burned low and steady and cast their shadows long across the dry riverbed, and Jonah tried not to notice that his shadow looked like it was carrying something on its back.

* * *

It started on the second day, or what he guessed was the second day — time down here was a rumor. A tightness in his throat, like swallowing a fishhook.

By the third day, he could feel the words.

Not hear them. Feel them. They sat in his chest like coals banked under ash, and every so often one would shift, and he would taste iron. Swordspeech wasn't like other magic. It wasn't drawn from the air or the earth or the blood of the world. It was drawn from oaths — spoken promises given weight, given edge. Every vow he'd ever sworn as a member of the Ninth Order still lived inside him, coiled and patient, and the order was dead but the vows were not, because vows outlive the mouths that make them.

That was the whole horror of swordspeech. You never stopped paying for your words.

"Stop," Wren said.

They had stopped twice already that day. Each time, he'd found himself standing very still with blood running freely from his nose, or from one ear, or — once, terrifyingly — from the corner of one eye, as if the words were trying to exit through whatever route offered least resistance.

"I'm fine," he said, which was a lie, and lying to Wren had gotten harder lately, because she'd started reading him the way she read terrain — looking for the contour lines underneath.

"You're bleeding again."

"It's nothing."

"Jonah." She took his face in both hands, tilted it toward the lantern light. Her fingers came away red at the tips. "Your magic isn't waking up. It's waking *up* — there's a difference. It's climbing toward something. What?"

He almost told her the truth: that he could feel the covenant pulling at him like a tide, that the closer they drew to the mountain, the louder his dead order's oaths got, each one demanding to be honored, to be enforced, to be *spoken*. That swordspeech was waking because the debt was coming due, and he was the collateral, and the words wanted out.

Instead he said, "The herald will have found the sluice by now. We should keep moving."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then she wiped his nose with her sleeve — a gesture so domestic it nearly broke him — and shouldered her satchel, and walked on.

* * *

The herald found them on the fourth day.

They heard it before they saw it: footsteps in the dry riverbed behind them, unhurried, even, the cadence of someone who has never once needed to hurry. Jonah turned with the lantern raised, and the light caught a figure perhaps fifty paces back, walking with a gait he knew better than his own.

Tall. Broad through the shoulders. Left arm swinging slightly stiff, the way it had after the break that never healed quite right.

"Tobin," Jonah said, before he could stop himself.

His brother smiled. It was Tobin's smile, crooked on the left side, the smile that had gotten them out of trouble and into worse for nineteen years. It was Tobin's face, Tobin's sandy hair falling into Tobin's gray eyes, Tobin's worn soldier's coat with the patched elbow. Down to the scar through the eyebrow from the fence rail, age eleven.

Every detail correct. That was the worst part. Not an approximation. An *inventory*.

"That's not him," Jonah said quietly. To Wren. To himself. "Tobin died at Redford Bridge. I carried what was left of him home."

"I know," Wren said. Her hand found his. Her grip was steady. Hers always was; it was one of the thousand things he loved about her. "I know, love. I see it too."

The thing wearing his brother stopped ten paces away, close enough that the lantern light reached it fully, and Jonah understood why the Hollow had sent this particular shape. Not to frighten him. To *unmake* him. Grief was a lever, and the Hollow knew exactly where to stand.

"Little brother," the herald said, in Tobin's voice, warm and rough and familiar enough to stop his heart. "You've been hiding from us a long time."

"You're not real."

"I'm as real as the debt," it said, tilting its head, and the gesture was pure Tobin, the head-tilt he did when he thought Jonah was being dense. "And the debt is very real. A hundred souls, sold fair and square to end your pretty little war. Ninety-nine collected. One outstanding." Its gray eyes — Tobin's gray eyes — moved to Wren, and there was nothing in them at all, no warmth, no recognition, just appraisal, the way you'd look at a purse. "She's grown into her face nicely. The book didn't lie."

"Don't look at her."

"Or what?" The herald smiled again, and this time the smile went wrong at the edges, wider than a face should allow, and for one flickering instant Jonah saw through the mask — saw something vast and patient and *hungry* wearing his brother like a coat. "You'll speak a word at me? Go ahead, little brother. Speak. Every syllable costs you blood, and we both know you haven't much left to give. Your order bled itself dry making bargains. You're just the last drop in the cup."

Jonah's chest tightened. The coals in him roared. He felt a word rise — a killing word, an oath-word, *stand and be bound* — and he clamped his teeth against it, and blood poured from his nose, hot and fast, and the herald watched with something like delight.

"There it is," it murmured. "The sword in your voice. We've missed it. The court misses it. Come home, Jonah. Bring the girl. Settle the account. Or keep running, and we'll wear faces you love all the way down, until there's nothing left of you but the owing."

Then it stepped backward into the dark between one lantern-flame's flutter and the next, and was gone, and the passage was empty, and only the blood on Jonah's lip proved anything had been there at all.

* * *

They didn't speak for a long time after.

Wren bandaged his nose with strips torn from her spare shirt, her movements brisk and precise, a cartographer's economy of motion. When she finished, she sat back on her heels and studied him.

"It used my face," she said. "In the book. Now it's using yours — your brother's. It collects faces the way I collect maps."

"It collects debts," Jonah said. "Faces are just how it delivers the bill."

"And if we pay? If we go down there and give it what it wants?" Her voice stayed level, but he heard the fault line running through it. "What happens to me?"

"I don't know." He wouldn't lie to her anymore. Not down here, where lies had weight. "I think souls don't die. I think they hang. Like lanterns. Like light that remembers being a person." He swallowed. "I think you'd know, forever, and be unable to do anything about it. I think that's the actual price. Not death. *Awareness.*"

Wren nodded slowly, absorbing it the way she absorbed everything — mapping it, fitting it into the terrain of what she knew.

"Then we don't pay," she said. "We find another way. There's always another way through a range; you just haven't found the pass yet."

"The covenant—"

"Is a contract," she said, "and every contract has terms, and every term was written by someone, and anything written can be *read*, Jonah. I'm a mapmaker. Show me the shape of the thing that binds you, and I'll find the way around it. Or through it. Or under it." She took his bleeding hand in hers. "You broke your oath once already, walking out on the Order. Do it again. Break it all the way down this time."

He stared at her. Something cold and enormous shifted in his chest — not the coals, not the oaths, something else. Hope, maybe. Or terror. Down here, they felt identical.

Ahead, the dry riverbed sloped downward, and the air grew warmer, and far off — impossibly far, impossibly deep — there was a glow on the stone like lamplight through honey. The Hollow, lighting its own halls. Waiting.

And from the dark behind them, faint but unmistakable, came footsteps again. Unhurried. Even. Closing.

This time, they weren't Tobin's.

They were Jonah's own.
