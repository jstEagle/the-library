## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

**Jonah**

The last stair was made of teeth.

Not metaphor—actual teeth, each one the length of a rowboat, set in a jaw of black stone that had been a gate once, before something enormous had decided to eat it and then change its mind. Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Her palm was slick. So were my thoughts.

"Tell me this isn't real," she whispered.

"I can't." I'd stopped lying to her somewhere around the third mile of underways, when the walls started breathing. "But I can tell you I'm not leaving without you."

The stair went down for what felt like a year. And then it didn't.

The Hollow opened beneath us like a held breath finally released.

I have seen courts. I was raised in one—the Ninth Order's hall at Cael Sarrow, all grey granite and honest iron, built by men who believed beauty was a kind of lie. This was the opposite of that. This was beauty as a weapon, deployed at scale.

The cavern was vast enough to have weather. Stalactites hung like inverted cathedrals, and every one of them dripped light—not firelight, not glowstone, but something warmer and more personal, gold shot through with colors that shifted as I watched: blue, then green, then a bruised violet. The floor was polished obsidian that reflected everything twice, so the whole court existed in duplicate, above and below, and you couldn't tell which version was the real one.

And hanging from the ceiling, in their thousands, were lanterns.

I knew what they were before Maren's warnings finished assembling themselves in my head. Souls don't look like anything, when they're free. But caged, contained, *displayed*—they burn. Each lantern was a person. Some glowed steady and content, souls that had forgotten they'd ever been anything else. Some flickered wildly, still fighting. One near the entrance guttered in a rhythm I recognized, and I hated myself for recognizing it—a stutter like a man laughing, like my brother had laughed, right up until the Hollow took his debt out of his chest.

Wren made a sound beside me. Small. Like a hinge.

"They're people," she said.

"Yes."

"And we're standing in their living room."

That was Wren. Even now. Even here. Finding the human furniture in a nightmare.

A path unrolled toward us across the obsidian—literally unrolled, a carpet of woven silver that slithered forward from the dark like a tongue tasting the air. At its end stood a figure I couldn't focus on. Not because it moved fast. Because my eyes kept deciding it was someone else. My mentor. My mother. The recruiting sergeant who'd sworn me into the Order at seventeen. Every face I'd ever obeyed, cycling gently, patiently, like a shopkeeper showing wares.

"Jonah Dravenmoor," it said, in a voice made of all those voices agreeing. "And the due payment herself. Welcome home."

"It's not her home," I said.

"Neither is Vell," said the Sovereign, pleasantly. "You've both just been pretending."

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what nobody tells you about walking into hell: your feet keep working. You keep putting one in front of the other because the alternative is stopping, and stopping means admitting where you are.

Jonah walked ahead of me on that silver carpet with his shoulders squared the way he squared them at the market when wardens passed—performing calm, spending it faster than he earned it. I kept my eyes off the lanterns. If I looked too long at any single one, I could feel it looking back, and some of them flickered in patterns that felt like questions. *Are you next? Are you us?*

The court received us the way a rich house receives guests it has already billed.

There were courtiers—I use the word loosely. Shapes in the reflections rather than the room itself, moving through the mirrored floor, bowing as we passed. None of them cast shadows. All of them smiled with mouths that belonged to other people. I recognized one smile. It was mine—the one from the little mirror over my drafting table, the one I make when a map comes out right. A reflection wearing my satisfaction drifted past and bowed to me, and I understood, with a lurch, that these were the parts the Sovereign kept. The pleasant bits. The bits worth displaying.

The throne, when we reached it, was a scale.

An actual balance, tall as a church tower, brass and bone, its two pans hanging empty. Around its base coiled chains that ran upward into the dark, and every chain ended in a lantern-hook. The whole architecture of the place funneled here. The beauty, the light, the music I only now realized was playing—all of it was decoration on an instrument for weighing souls.

The Sovereign sat upon the crossbeam as if it were a chaise lounge, which should have been impossible and was, apparently, merely rude.

"You've come a long way," it said, and now it wore Jonah's face—older, unlined, the face he might have had if five years of fear hadn't etched him. "Both of you. Most debtors send lawyers. Or armies. You walked."

"We walked," Jonah said. His voice was flat and careful, the voice he used for prices. "We've come to settle the account."

"Oh, I know why you've come." The Sovereign leaned forward, and the lantern-light bent toward it like iron filings to a magnet. "I've known since 112 years ago. I wrote it down. Would you like to hear how it ends? I do so love the ending."

"No," I said.

"Wren," Jonah warned softly.

But the Sovereign turned to me, and it had my father's face now, dead eleven years, and I had to grip Jonah's arm to stay upright. "She dreams of the mountain," the Sovereign said, gentle as a bedtime story. "Has since she was six. She signed a page she can't remember signing, because I let her forget—courtesy costs nothing. She has spent her whole life drawing maps of places she's never been, because part of her already lives here, in the ledger, waiting." It smiled my father's smile. "You didn't fall in love by accident, swordspeaker. You were drawn together. Ink finds ink."

"That's a lie," Jonah said.

"It's a *term*," said the Sovereign. "Everything else is negotiation."

* * *

**Jonah**

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, not Wren, not Maren, not the God I stopped believing in somewhere in the underways: I missed it.

The swordspeech. Five years of silence, and I missed it like a missing tooth—the tongue keeps going back to the gap. When I spoke a word of power, the world listened. Doors unlocked themselves. Men with weapons reconsidered. For a few shining seconds I was never afraid, because fear requires the possibility of being ignored, and the sword-words were never ignored.

The Hollow knew that. Of course it did. It collects souls; it knows what they're made of.

"Let's discuss terms," the Sovereign said, and the great scale behind it began, very slowly, to sway.

It offered first what I expected: Wren's freedom for my service. A century in the court, enforcing debts, hunting runaways. I refused before it finished speaking, and it nodded as if I'd passed a test, which frightened me more than the offer had.

Then it offered the second thing.

"Your soul," it said, "stays where it is—in your body, in your life, yours entire. And hers goes free. No debt. No lantern. No prophecy. In exchange—" it spread hands that were briefly my brother Tam's hands, and I stopped breathing— "you get it back. The speech. All of it. Not the mumbled half-magic you rationed out in Vell's night markets. The full voice. The Ninth Order at its height couldn't have matched what I'll put back into your throat. You'll be the last swordspeaker the way a storm is the last of the rain."

The scale swayed. The lanterns dimmed, leaning in to listen.

"And Wren?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded far away, like someone else's debt.

"She walks out. Free. Remembers everything, including this moment, including the choice you made. She lives eighty more years and dies warm, in a bed, old." The Sovereign tilted its head, wearing Tam's face, wearing my grief like a borrowed coat. "Or: you refuse, we fight, and I take you both. I always take the debt, Jonah. The only question the scale ever answers is *how much*."

I want to be able to say I refused instantly. That love is a wall and I am a wall of a man.

I didn't. That's the truth I carried out of that place, the splinter I still worry at: there was a moment—a long one, three heartbeats, maybe four—when I stood in the glitter of that terrible court and *weighed* it. Her soul against my power. Her eighty warm years against the voice of a god in my mouth. And some cold clerk in the back office of my heart ran the numbers and found them interesting.

Four heartbeats. Then Wren's hand found mine, and her fingers were shaking, and she said, quietly, so only I could hear:

"Whatever you're about to do—do it as the man from the night market. Not the soldier. He's the one I signed up for."

The clerk in my head looked up from his ledger. Closed it.

"No," I said.

The Sovereign sighed, and every lantern in the Hollow flickered at once, a thousand souls flinching. "No to which part?"

"All of it. The bargain, the terms, the account." I stepped forward, and the silver carpet recoiled from my boot. "You want a debtor? I'm not the one who owes you. My order owed you. My order is dead. And I am done paying its bills."

Something moved behind the Sovereign's borrowed faces—something older than all of them, surfacing like a shark under ice.

"Careful, swordspeaker," it said. "There are words you swore me. Oaths with hooks in them. Break them here, in my court, and the breaking will cost you things you cannot imagine."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, and felt, deep in my chest, the first waking stir of the old magic—hot, hungry, terrified, *mine*— "that imagination is the one thing I brought plenty of."

The scale stopped swaying.

The Sovereign rose from its impossible seat, and every courtier in the mirrored floor turned to look at me, wearing my own face, and the Sovereign said, almost kindly:

"Guards. Take him somewhere he can think about his oaths. And bring me the girl—we may as well begin weighing."

Chains came out of the dark like eels.

The last thing I saw before they took me was Wren, standing alone on the obsidian with her chin up, surrounded by ten thousand burning souls—and the last thing I heard was the Sovereign, murmuring to the scale, patient as interest accruing:

"Now then. Let's find out what she weighs."
