## 8. Oathbreaker

They took the sword first, which was a courtesy, and then the name, which was not.

The cell had no door. It had a mouth — a seam of black stone that opened when the Sovereign willed it and sealed like lips after. The walls were veined with silver light that pulsed slow as a sleeping heart, and Jonah understood within an hour that the veins were oaths. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every promise ever sworn to the court beneath the mountain, rendered visible and hung here like meat in a smokehouse.

His own were easy to find. They glowed warmer than the rest, the way old coals hold heat. *I will speak only to bind. I will never unmake what my order has made. I will carry the covenant until my blood stops or my voice does.* Nine oaths, one for each year of his training, each one laid over the last like lacquer until the boy who had spoken them was buried under a man shaped entirely by his word.

He pressed his palm against the wall and felt them hum.

"Comfortable?" said the herald.

It still wore Tam's face. It stood on the far side of the cell's mouth, arms folded, wearing his dead brother's crooked half-smile the way a thief wears a stolen coat — correctly, but not owned. Five years since Tam died at Redford Bridge, and Jonah had spent four of them learning not to flinch at mirrors. This thing made mirrors feel safe.

"You're not him," Jonah said.

"No." The herald didn't bother lying. "But I remember being him. That's the cruelty of it, isn't it? Your order fed us to this place whole. Everything he was is in here somewhere, filed under debt." It tilted its head, and the gesture was so precisely Tam's that Jonah's chest seized. "The Sovereign offers again. Last chance. Your soul back, your power restored, the girl walks out with her body and no memory of the last five years. She'll be happy. She'll draw maps. She'll never know what she lost."

"And me?"

"You'll be magnificent."

Jonah laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's the pitch? Magnificent?"

"It's a good pitch," the herald said, with Tam's shrug. "It worked on better men than you."

Then the mouth closed, and Jonah was alone with the humming walls and the arithmetic of his own life.

* * *

He slept, or something near it, and dreamed of Vell. The night market. Wren's stall two rows over from his, the smell of her ink and the coal she warmed her tea on. The way she'd looked at him across the crowd the evening before everything broke open — not suspicious anymore, not testing, just *seeing* him, the whole inventory, sword-voice and all, and staying anyway.

He woke with wet eyes and a dry throat and made himself do the thing he'd been refusing to do since the mouth sealed: think it through.

Because there was a tenth oath. One he'd never spoken aloud, never written, never told Maren or anyone. He'd found it scratched into the flyleaf of his first training manual, in his own childish hand, the week after Tam died: *I will not become the thing that killed my brother.*

A private vow. An unenforceable one. Which meant, by every law of swordspeech he'd ever been taught, it was worthless.

Which meant it was the only one left worth anything.

Because here was the truth Maren had drunk herself numb rather than finish telling him, the truth the Sovereign had worn like jewelry all through its glittering offer: the covenant that bound every swordspeaker for nine hundred years wasn't a shield. It was a leash. *Speak only to bind* — because binding was how the court harvested. Every oath a speaker enforced fed the Hollow a little light. Every soul the order sold to end the war had been purchased with words, and the words kept paying interest. The magic wasn't corrupted. The magic *was* the corruption, elegant as a loan, and Jonah had been a collector on its behalf his entire adult life.

There was one speech that dissolved instead of bound. The Unbinding. Forbidden before it was even named; the manuals called it the Word That Eats, and said any speaker who used it would lose everything — power, craft, the gift itself. The order taught it as a horror story to keep apprentices obedient.

Maren, three cups deep, had taught it to him anyway. She'd said, *Not because you should. Because you deserve to know what they're afraid of.*

He sat cross-legged in the dark and began to bleed.

* * *

Swordspeech cost blood. Everyone knew that. What the manuals didn't say was that the price scaled with the size of the truth you were trying to say. A binding charm cost a pinprick. The war-oaths his elders had spoken over battlefields had cost them their minds, eventually — that was the real reason the Ninth Order dwindled, not age, not peace. You could only pay so much of yourself into words before the words owned more of you than you did.

The Unbinding required him to mean, completely and without remainder, that every oath he had ever sworn was wrong.

Not mistaken. Not regretted. *Wrong* — wrong at the root, wrong the way a tree is wrong if it grows from a poisoned seed. And he had to mean it about Tam too. About the vow he'd whispered over his brother's grave. About the beautiful, load-bearing promise that had gotten him up every morning for five years.

He tried. He sat in the dark and reached for the meaning and his mind slid off it like rain off glass. Because if the oaths were wrong, then the discipline was wrong, and the discipline was the only shape grief had ever taken that hadn't crushed him.

Hours passed. The silver veins pulsed. Somewhere beyond the walls, the court went on glittering, souls swaying gently in their lantern-light like fruit waiting to be picked.

Then, through the stone, faint as a memory of sound, he heard singing.

Not the court's music. Something rougher. A work-song, almost — the kind cart-drivers sang in Vell's lower streets, tuneless and stubborn. And threaded through it, a voice he knew, saying his name the way she said it, like a word she'd invented: *Jonah. Jonah. Come up. Come up.*

Wren. Somewhere above, alive, fighting, refusing.

And the thought arrived whole, the way true things do: *she doesn't need me to be magnificent. She needs me to come up.*

He put his hand flat against the wall, against the warm coal-glow of his nine oaths, and spoke.

"I unbind," he said, and blood ran freely from his nose, hot over his lips. "Not one oath. All of them. Mine, my order's, the covenant's root and branch. I unbind the bargain that bought a hundred souls. I unbind the leash. I unbind the lie that binding was ever protection." His voice cracked; he pushed through the crack. "Let every word my kind ever swore to this place fall silent. Let the debt die with the debtor. Let the lanterns go out — no. Let the lanterns go *home*."

The wall screamed.

There was no other word for it. The silver veins flared white, then black, and the black spread like ink through water, racing away from his palm in every direction, and everywhere it touched, the humming stopped. He felt each of his nine oaths go out like candles pinched between fingers — not painfully, worse than painfully, *cleanly*, the way a bone sets. Each one left a hole where it had been, and into the holes rushed everything he'd been too bound to feel: five years of fear, ten years of grief, the full uncatalogued weight of Tam's face at Redford Bridge.

He kept speaking. Blood soaked his shirt. His vision tunneled.

"I unbind my brother's pledge. I unbind the harvest. I unbind—" and here his voice dropped to almost nothing, the smallest true thing he'd ever said, "—the vow I made that I wouldn't become what killed him. Because I already did. And I'm done."

The last vein went dark.

The mouth of the cell tore open — not opened, *tore*, the stone peeling back like skin — and the light that came through wasn't the court's gold. It was gray, ordinary, underground gray, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

* * *

The corridor outside was chaos. The lantern-hall where the souls hung was a storm of released light — hundreds of them, thousands, pouring off their hooks and streaming upward through cracks in the vaulted ceiling like sparks reversing a fire. The court's music had curdled into a single sustained note of rage, and beneath Jonah's feet he could feel the mountain itself reconsidering its architecture.

The Sovereign was screaming somewhere far above. He did not go toward it. He was done with bargains, including the bargain of vengeance.

In the lantern-hall, among the streaming lights, one shade lingered.

Tam. Not the herald's stolen version — smaller than that, dimmer, a young man of nineteen with a bridge behind his eyes and mud on his boots. He looked at Jonah with an expression Jonah had spent a decade imagining and never once gotten right. It wasn't anger. It wasn't even peace, exactly.

It was recognition.

"You got old," Tam said.

"Told you I would," Jonah managed. "You said I'd be bald, though. So."

"Yeah, that tracks." The shade drifted closer, and where it passed, the air smelled like rain on a summer road. "You know what I remember most? Not the war. Not the bridge. Tuesday evenings. You practicing your forms in the yard and getting mad at the fence post." A pause. "Go be that guy again. The one who got mad at fence posts."

"Tam—"

"I don't blame you." The shade said it simply, the way you'd report the weather. "You were nineteen and they handed you the bill and told you it was honor. I blamed them. Now they're dust, so we're square." It began to thin, drawn upward with the others, and its last look was Tam's crooked half-smile — but owned now, finally, worn by the right person. "Come up, little brother. Someone's carrying the light for you. Try to be worth it."

Then he was gone, and the hall was empty except for rubble, and the mountain groaned overhead like a ship changing its mind about the sea.

Jonah ran.

* * *

He found Wren at the base of the great stair, ankle-deep in drifting motes of freed soul-light, holding a torch she'd clearly improvised from a curtain and a chair leg, shouting directions upward at a river of confused, half-remembering dead.

She saw him and her face did three things at once — relief, fury, and the third thing, the one she saved only for him.

"You're bleeding."

"I'm *empty*," he said, and heard his own voice come out plain. No resonance. No weight of binding in it. Just a tired man's voice, hoarse and human and entirely his own. "Wren, I can't— the magic's gone. All of it. I'm nothing special now."

She crossed the distance, grabbed his bloody collar, and kissed him hard enough to make the point unnecessary.

"Good," she said against his mouth. "Now help me count heads. There are four hundred souls up that stair and maybe six of them remember their own names, and the roof is coming down, and you're the only person here who knows these tunnels from the *maps*—"

Above them, a crack split the vaulted ceiling from wall to wall, and the first real fall of stone came down a hundred yards behind them like thunder learning to walk.

"—and we are leaving *now*," Wren finished, shoved the makeshift torch into his hands, and turned to face the stair, already calling names she couldn't possibly know, guiding the dead toward daylight.
