## 9. The Long Way Up

The first thing the Hollow did when it died was remember how to be a mountain.

I felt it in my teeth—a deep, grinding groan, like a giant turning over in its sleep. The lantern-souls that had hung from the vaulted ceiling in their thousands tore loose all at once, and the great dark hall filled with light that moved, that drifted, that *wept*. Not the cold gold of the court's chandeliers. This was warmer. Messier. The color of candle flames in drafty rooms, of lamps left burning for someone who might still come home.

Jonah lay across my shoulders, half-conscious, heavier than any man I'd ever carried and lighter than I expected too—like the weight of him had been mostly the sword in his voice, and now that voice was empty.

"Up," I told him. My mouth tasted of ash and old ink. "We're going up."

He made a sound that might have been agreement or might have been the last word of an oath dissolving on his tongue. I didn't wait to find out. Around us, the freed souls were rising in slow spirals, confused as moths, bumping against stone that was beginning to crack and shift overhead. Dust fell like gray snow. Somewhere far below us—I could feel it more than hear it—the foundations of the Hollow were folding inward, the mountain reclaiming the wound that had been cut into it a century ago.

A hundred souls sold by his order. A hundred lanterns snuffed back into flame. And among them, drifting near the shattered dais where the Sovereign had sat, one small light that circled twice, hesitated, and then came toward me like a dog that has decided, finally, to trust someone.

"Tam," I said softly. Jonah's brother's shade hovered at my elbow, flickering. He had forgiven Jonah with his last act as a bound thing—had pushed him out of the way of the collapsing covenant-stone—and now he seemed unsure what he was supposed to do with freedom. "This way," I said. "You remember daylight? Everyone remembers daylight. It's the first thing they put into you and the last thing they take out."

The shade pulsed—yes, or maybe just hunger—and began to drift toward the fissure in the eastern wall, the one Maren's directions had promised would lead to the underways. The other souls followed him. A river of small warm lights, flowing uphill through a dying dark.

I shifted Jonah higher on my back and went after them.

* * *

The underways were worse than the descent had been, because now everything was falling apart.

Maren's map—the one she'd scratched onto a wine-stained napkin with a shaking hand, cursing me and Jonah and the Ninth Order and the wine itself—had been drawn for a Hollow that stood. Now the tunnels were shrugging off their geometry. Staircases ended in rubble. Corridors that should have run straight bent like heat-haze. Twice we came to junctions where three identical passages split off, and both times Tam's shade drifted down the correct one without hesitation, as if the dead kept better maps than cartographers.

"Note for future editions," I muttered, hauling Jonah over a fallen lintel. "Souls navigate by grief. Grief is load-bearing."

Jonah laughed. It was a terrible sound—wet and thin—but it was a laugh, and I felt it against the back of my neck like a pulse.

"You're carrying me," he rasped.

"I'm carrying the man who broke a thousand-year covenant with one sentence. You've earned a shoulder ride."

"My magic—" He coughed. "It's gone, Wren. All of it. I can't feel the swordspeech. I can't feel anything but—"

"Your ribs. Yes. I think two are cracked. Don't talk."

He talked anyway. Of course he did. "You burned your maps."

"I burned my maps." I didn't look back at him. My eyes were busy with the rubble and the drifting lights and the cracks widening in the ceiling. But I felt him go still against my back, the way people go still when they understand the size of something.

"The Vell archive copies," he said. "The harbor survey. Your mother's—"

"All of it. Every original. If the wardens found one line of my hand next to one charm of yours, they'd have traced you through me." I stepped over a dead herald—one of the faceless things the Sovereign had sent hunting us, crumbled now to gray salt. "Paper burns fast. Faster than people. That's the whole trick of it."

"You spent ten years—"

"Twelve." I ducked under a low arch. "And I'll spend twelve more drawing new ones. Maps aren't magic, Jonah. They're just attention. Attention survives anything." I paused at a fork where Tam's light waited, patient, flickering toward the left passage. "Besides," I said, quieter, "you'd have done worse for me."

"No," he said. "I nearly took the bargain instead."

I knew. I'd been there. I'd watched the Sovereign hold out his own soul to Jonah like a merchant offering coin—power restored, every oath refunded, the swordspeech singing in him again forever—and I'd watched my love stand in that glittering light with his hand half-raised, and I had understood, in that endless moment, exactly how heavy the sword in his voice had always been. How much of him wanted it back.

He hadn't taken it. He'd broken it instead. Broken all of it.

"We'll discuss your near-treason later," I said. "Over soup. Walk—well, be carried."

* * *

We climbed for what felt like a day and might have been three hours. Time ran strangely in the underways, pooling in the low places. The souls grew fewer as we rose—not lost, I told myself, just finding their own ways out, slipping through cracks too small for the living, going home to whatever home remained for them. Tam stayed with us. I thought he stayed for Jonah, though Jonah slept most of the climb, breathing shallowly against my neck, and so maybe the shade stayed for me, for the woman who had talked him toward the light like a stray cat.

At some point I started talking to keep myself moving. About Vell. About the night market and the charm stall and the way Jonah used to arrange his little trinkets—luck-knots and fever-beads and nothing that worked, nothing that was ever allowed to work—so they caught the lamplight just right. About Tuesdays. About the way he looked when he thought I wasn't watching: careful, and guilty, and hopeful, like a man reading a letter he expected to be bad news.

"I knew," I told his sleeping weight. "From almost the beginning. Nobody arranges luck-knots that beautifully unless they're hiding something. And then the book—you should have seen your face. Like the past had reached up out of the page and grabbed you by the throat." I stepped carefully over a seam in the stone where the mountain's old bones showed through. "I signed something once. When I was nine. I never remembered signing it, but I remembered the dream after—the mountain, the gate, the ink. For twenty years I thought I was haunted. Turns out I was *purchased.* There's a difference, apparently. The interest alone—"

My voice cracked. I let it. There was no one to perform steadiness for except a sleeping man and a dead boy's ghost, and ghosts have seen everyone cry.

"I'm not afraid anymore, though," I said, when I could. "That's the strange part. I spent my whole life afraid of losing myself—of waking up hollow, of being some court's due. And now the court's dead and I'm still here, still Wren, still tired and filthy and furious, and I know exactly who I am. Do you know what that's worth?" I adjusted my grip. "Everything. It's worth everything. Even the maps."

The tunnel ahead brightened. Not soul-light. Gray, honest, unmagical dawn-light, leaking down through a collapse in the ceiling where tree roots hung through like the fingers of the living world reaching in.

Tam's shade stopped at the edge of it. He flickered—fast, agitated, a candle in wind.

"It's all right," I said. "Daylight won't hurt you. Nothing will hurt you ever again."

He drifted forward, into the beam, and for one moment he was almost solid—a young man with Jonah's jaw and none of Jonah's guilt—and then he came apart into motes, into morning, into gone. But before he went, he turned. And whether it was really him or just light doing what light does, I saw him smile.

I stood there a while, holding an empty-handed kind of fullness, and then I climbed toward the roots.

* * *

We came out on the mountainside above Vell, in a meadow full of early sun and late frost, and I laid Jonah down in the grass like something rescued from a flood.

He woke slowly. Watched the sky. Watched me. Flexed his fingers one at a time, the way he did every morning for five years, checking for the magic the way other men check for feeling in frostbitten hands.

"Nothing," he said. Wonderingly. "There's nothing there."

"No," I agreed. "There isn't."

He turned his head and looked at me, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling, and I realized—with a certainty as clean as a drawn contour line—that this was the version of him I loved best. Not the hidden swordspeaker with his careful charms and his buried thunder. Not the oathbreaker blazing in the court beneath the world. This: a man lying in wet grass with cracked ribs and no power at all, looking at me like I was the only remarkable thing left in the world.

"I heard you," he said. "In the tunnels. Talking to me. Most of it."

"Most of it," I said, mortified. "Which parts?"

"The part about the luck-knots." His smile widened. "Nobody arranges them that beautifully."

"Shut up and let me look at your ribs."

I knelt over him in the frost-wet grass and pressed gently along his sides while he hissed, and there was no spell in my hands and no spell in his body and no covenant anywhere in the world holding either of us to anything, and it was the most magical morning of my life.

Below us, Vell smoked gently in the dawn—chimney smoke, breakfast smoke, ordinary smoke. The wardens would have questions. The market would have rumors. Someone would eventually ask what happened to the mountain, why the anti-magical wards had flickered all night like a guttering lamp, why a hundred faint lights had been seen rising from the peak before sunrise.

Let them ask. We had a stall to reopen, trinkets to sell, honest ones. And somewhere in the ashes of my archive, I had a new map taking shape in my head—not of roads or harbors, but of the underways themselves, the reopened dark beneath the world, waiting for daylight to come down and name it.

"Jonah," I said.

"Mm?"

"When we get down. I'm going to need paper. A lot of paper."

He laughed—still wet, still thin, but easier now, like a door opening—and pulled me down beside him into the grass, and we lay there in the ordinary sun while the mountain, behind us, settled into its long and dreamless sleep.

Then, from the valley below, thin on the wind, came the sound of bells—Vell's alarm bells, ringing in a pattern I'd only read about in the oldest wardens' manuals, a pattern meant for one thing:

Something was coming up the trade road. Something that had been waiting a hundred years for the Hollow to fall.
