## 10. Maps Without Magic

The stall was the same one Jonah had kept for five years in Vell's night market—same crooked leg shimmed with a folded scrap of leather, same oil lamp with the chipped glass that threw a warm smear of light across the counter. But the goods were different now.

No charms. No whispered words stitched into copper wire, no luck-knots that actually worked, no fever-salves that cooled because he asked them to. Just honest trinkets: brass compasses that pointed north and nothing else, tin whistles tuned by ear, small carved animals Wren bought from a woodworker two streets over and sold at cost because she liked them.

A boy haggled him down to three coppers for a whistle shaped like a fish. Jonah let him win. The boy ran off piping badly into the dark, and Jonah sat back on his stool and listened to the sound fade between the market stalls, and thought: *this is what I traded it for.* Not the mountain, not the covenant, not the hundred souls hanging like lanterns in the dark. This. A bad whistle and a Tuesday night.

He caught himself smiling and didn't stop.

"You're grinning at nothing," said the woman at the next stall, an old candlemaker who had never once asked why his stock changed overnight from charms to junk. In Vell, nobody asked. That was the city's whole religion.

"I'm allowed," Jonah said.

"New law?"

"Old one. I just finally read it."

She snorted and went back to her wicks. Above the market, the sky did its ordinary work—a few stars, a thin moon, no omens. Jonah had learned to check for omens anyway, out of habit, the way a man who once broke his leg checks ice underfoot. There were none. There hadn't been any for months. He was starting to believe that wasn't a trick.

Wren arrived at the second bell, as she always did, with ink on three fingers and a rolled sheet of paper under one arm that she carried the way other people carried sleeping children. She set it on the counter without unrolling it.

"Finished?" he asked.

"Finished." She said it carefully, the way you say a word you've practiced. "The lower galleries. All nine levels. Every passage the survey teams opened."

Nine months since the Hollow came down. Nine months since the mountain gate stood open to the sky for the first time in living memory, and the cartographers' guild—the same guild that had laughed Wren's underground theories out of their hall twice—had come to her hat in hand and asked if she would please, please draw what was down there before it all flooded or fell or got claimed by some duke.

She'd said yes. Of course she'd said yes. And then she'd spent most of a year walking those passages with a lantern and a measuring chain, mapping the place where she had almost stopped being herself.

Jonah looked at the roll of paper and understood what it cost her to have finished it. "It's good work," he said.

"It's *accurate* work," she corrected. "Good is a different thing." But she was pleased; he could see it in the set of her shoulders, the way she'd started carrying them again instead of bracing them. "The guild wants to hang it in the main hall. Full size. With my name on it."

"They should."

"They want to put your name on it too."

He blinked. "Mine? I can't draw a straight line."

"Not for drawing." She turned the roll so the end faced him, and there, in the corner where mapmakers put their credits, she'd already lettered it in her careful hand: *Surveyed by Wren Calloway. Guided below by J. Dravenmoor.* "Half those tunnels, nobody could have found without someone who knew how to listen to stone. Even without—" she made a small gesture, the one they both used now instead of saying *magic*, "—you still hear things. You told me yourself. It's just hearing now, not speaking."

Jonah touched the letters of his own name. It had been a long time since anything official wanted his name attached to it. For five years his name had been something to hide, and before that it had been something to spend.

"All right," he said. "But I get a copy."

"You get the first copy. I'm having it printed tomorrow." She paused, and he heard the next thing coming before she said it, because after everything, he always did. "There's one more thing about the deep gallery. Level seven. Where the court used to be."

The market noise seemed to step back from them politely.

"There's nothing there," Wren said. "That's the thing. The whole chamber—it's just gone. Not collapsed. *Gone.* Smooth rock, old rock, like it was never hollowed out at all. The survey team keeps measuring it and getting different numbers, and I keep redrawing it, and last week I just... left it blank. Put a little note. 'Here stood the Hollow. It is not here now.'"

"What did the guild say?"

"That I should fill it in with something." She shrugged. "I told them some places don't owe anyone a picture."

* * *

They walked home through streets that knew them. That was the difference, Jonah thought, between hiding and living—in Vell, five years ago, he had moved through these same lanes like water through cracks, unremarked, unremembered, a man shaped exactly like absence. Now the candlemaker nodded to him. The fish-whistle boy's mother waved. The night watchman, who had once very nearly arrested him, tipped his cap and said, "Evening, Master Dravenmoor," and Jonah said "Evening," back, and neither of them made anything strange of it.

Their rooms were above a bookbinder's shop, two floors up, with a window that caught the morning and a table big enough for maps. Wren lit the lamp while Jonah put water on for tea, and for a while they did the ordinary arithmetic of an evening—her washing brushes, him counting the day's coppers, the kettle finding its voice.

The prophecy book sat on the shelf above the table.

They had argued about keeping it. Wren had wanted it burned the first month, then the second, then quietly stopped wanting it, and Jonah had understood why without her saying: it was theirs now. Whatever it had been—a trap, a ledger, a debt collection notice a century in the making—it had ended as a record of them, and people keep records of themselves. They keep them even when the records frighten them. Especially then.

Most nights they ignored it. Some nights, like tonight, Wren took it down.

She opened it to the last page without hunting for it. They both knew the page by heart now, but knowing didn't dull it. The woodcut had been changing since the night the covenant broke—slowly, the way frost leaves a window. First the mountain gate had emptied of figures. Then the lanterns had gone out, one by one, until the sovereign's hall was only architecture. Then the two small shapes that had stood bound at the gate's threshold had come loose, unstuck from the ink like things waking from a long sleep.

Now the page showed a road. Just a road, running level through open country, drawn in the plain confident strokes of a woodcut artist who had never seen the place and so had drawn every road at once. And on the road, two figures. Blank-faced, as woodcut figures always were—but these more blank than most, featureless as fresh paper, as though the artist had deliberately left room.

Walking side by side. Neither leading.

"They're not us," Wren said. She said it every time, and every time it sounded less like denial and more like a question she enjoyed asking. "I mean, they're obviously us. But they're not *us*. We're right here. We're not in the book."

"No," Jonah agreed. "We're not in the book."

"That's the whole point of you breaking everything, isn't it? That we're not in any book anymore."

"That was the whole point." He set down two cups. "Whether it worked is another question. Books are patient."

She looked at him over the page. "You're worried."

"A little. It's a habit I'm trying to quit, along with the magic." He wrapped his hands around the tea she pushed toward him. "It's just—I keep thinking about what Maren said, near the end. That oaths don't die, they just wait for someone to pick them up. The covenant's broken. The court's gone. But somewhere there's a person standing in front of a dying collector, trading for an old book. Somewhere there's a swordspeaker who hasn't sworn anything yet, deciding whether to."

"And what do we do about that?"

"Nothing tonight." He said it simply, and found, checking himself the way he checked the sky for omens, that he meant it. "Tonight the tea's hot and the map's finished and your name goes in the guild hall. Tomorrow I'll worry about tomorrow. I'm told that's how ordinary people live. I've been meaning to try it."

Wren laughed—that real laugh, the one that had gotten easier all year—and closed the book. But before she shelved it, she looked at the two blank figures one more time, and did something she had never done before.

She picked up her pen.

Just a small mark. Two small marks, actually—one dot of ink beside each figure, high on the face, where eyes would go if the figures had decided to have them. Not features. Not identities. Just the suggestion that someone, someday, might look out of that page and see the road ahead instead of the road behind.

"There," she said. "Now it's a prophecy of people who can see where they're going."

* * *

Later, when the lamp was low and the tea was cold and Wren slept with one arm flung across his chest the way she'd done since the first night he stayed, Jonah lay awake a while—not from fear, but from the unfamiliar weight of a day that had been entirely enough.

Under the floorboards, faint as memory, the old shop's timbers settled against each other with small creaking sounds, and Jonah listened to them the way he had once listened to stone, and heard nothing in them but a house being a house.

On the shelf, the prophecy book waited with its two marked figures on its last page, and beyond that page—for the first time in a hundred and twelve years—nothing written at all.

Whatever came next, it would have to be drawn by hand.
