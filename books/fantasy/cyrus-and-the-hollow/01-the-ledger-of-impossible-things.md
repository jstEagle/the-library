## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There is a particular silence that lives inside bone. Most people never hear it because most people never hold a piece long enough, and the ones who do—the surgeons, the relic-mongers, the guild men with their soft hands and softer consciences—only hear their own pulse knocking against it like a beggar at a locked door.

I hear what's behind the door.

My name is August Dravenmoor, and I am a bone-carver of the Hollow Guild, which means I sit in a cold room above a shop that sells impossible things, and I listen to the dead say their last words to strangers who pay by the ounce. It is honest work, in the way that grave-digging is honest work. Somebody has to do it, and nobody wants to shake your hand afterward.

The widow arrived at dusk, which is when they always arrive. Grief keeps banker's hours in this city; it comes in after the lamps are lit, when the day's lies have been told and there's nothing left between a person and the truth but the dark. She was small and gray as ash, wrapped in a coat that had known better decades, and she held her payment in a handkerchief knotted tight enough to strangle.

"You're the carver," she said. Not a question. The desperate ones never ask questions; questions imply alternatives, and alternatives imply hope, and hope is expensive.

"I'm the carver," I agreed, and gestured her to the chair across my workbench. "You have something of his?"

She produced it from her coat like a woman surrendering a weapon: a finger bone, no longer than my thumb, yellowed at the joint where the body had begun its slow argument with time. Her son's. He'd been dead three weeks—drowned, she said, pulled out of the Graycanal by the dredgers, though the water had kept him two days before giving him back.

"The priest said he went clean," she whispered. "No sins unconfessed. But mothers know better than priests. Mothers always know better." Her eyes came up to mine, wet and fierce. "I need his last words. Whatever they were. Even if—" Her voice snagged. "Even if they weren't for me."

I took the bone. This is the part of the trade nobody warns you about: the weight of it. A finger bone weighs almost nothing. But you hold it and you understand that everything a man ever did passed through those small arches of calcium—every coin he stole or earned, every door he opened, every hand he held—and now all of that is gone except whatever the bone remembers.

"Before we begin," I said, because I say it every time, "you should know how this works. I carve. As I carve, the bone gives up what it holds. Usually it's little—a name, a curse, a snatch of prayer. Sometimes more. You'll hear what I hear, if you wish. Some prefer not to."

"I wish," she said, with the finality of a woman who had rehearsed the word on the walk over.

So I carved.

I won't describe the technique. Not because it's secret—it is, fiercely so, and Vessa Corvane would flay me and sell the flaying—but because describing it would be like describing how to cry. You take the graver. You find the grain of the thing, the long pale rivers of it, and you follow them down into the silence I mentioned, and somewhere along the way the silence stops being silent. The dead don't speak *to* you. They speak *through* the work, the way water speaks through a pipe. You're just the pipe-man. That's the whole dignity of my profession: plumbing for ghosts.

Her son's voice came up through the bone like breath through a keyhole.

*Cold,* it said first. *Cold, cold, the water's got teeth tonight.* Then, clearer, closer to the end: *Mam. Tell Mam the ledger wasn't mine. I copied it but I never wrote it. Tell her the numbers were wrong before I ever touched them.*

The widow made a sound I have heard perhaps four hundred times in my career and will never get used to: the sound of a person receiving exactly what they asked for and discovering that asking was the mistake. She pressed both hands over her mouth. Above them, her eyes were enormous.

"He was a clerk," she managed, when she could speak. "For the Corvane counting-house. He—he said the numbers were wrong?"

"That's what the bone holds."

"But wrong how? Wrong whose?" She leaned across the bench, gripping its edge, and I saw the grief in her curdle into something sharper. Fear, maybe. Or its ugly cousin, suspicion. "Carver. What else does it say? Everything. Every word."

And here I must tell you about my rule.

Every carver has one. Mine is simple: never listen twice. Once the commission is filled—once the client has their words and the transaction is entered—I put the relic down and I do not pick it up again. Not for curiosity. Not for profit. Not for the client's tears or the guild's insistence. Because the dead are like wells, and the second bucket you draw up is never the same as the first, and if you keep drawing, eventually you pull up something with your own face reflected in it. I have seen what happens to carvers who listen twice. There's one who walks the undercity still, technically alive, carving nothing, saying nothing, his eyes full of other people's endings. Nobody remembers his name. Perhaps he doesn't either.

Never listen twice. It has kept me invisible for eleven years, and invisibility, in my line of work, is not a condition. It is an achievement.

"Everything the bone holds belongs to the commission," I told the widow gently. "What I've given you is what it holds."

"It's not enough." She was crying now, quietly, the way people cry when they've learned to do it without disturbing anyone. "He was thirty-one years old. He drowned with his boots on and his ledgers in his bag, and the last thing he thought of was *numbers*, and you're telling me that's all there is?"

"No," I said. "I'm telling you that's all there is *for sale*."

That sounds cruel. It isn't meant to be. Cruelty would be pretending otherwise—letting her believe that for another coin I could dig deeper, that love could be transacted in installments. The dead give what they give. We only keep the books.

She paid me anyway. Eleven silver marks and a copper she tried to add and I refused, because the copper felt like pity and pity fouls the ledger. She stood to leave, then stopped at the door, small and gray against the lamplight from the stair.

"Carver," she said. "When you wrote down his words. Did you write them in your book, or in yours?"

I didn't understand the question. I said something bland and professional—I'm very good at bland and professional; it's practically a trade skill—and she nodded as if I'd answered, and went down the stairs and out into the dark, and I sat there holding her son's finger bone and feeling like a man who has been handed a letter addressed in handwriting he almost recognizes.

*The ledger wasn't mine. The numbers were wrong before I ever touched them.*

Drowned clerks and their numbers. Not my business. My business is the carving, the hearing, the filing. Three movements, like a dance. You learn the steps and you don't improvise.

I filed the transaction.

The Hollow Guild keeps its ledgers in the vault below the shop, and I will confess—since we're alone here, you and I—that I love the ledgers more than any living thing in this city. They are vast, black-bound volumes with spines like coffin lids, and every impossible thing the guild has ever bought or sold is written in them in ink mixed with ash: bottled memories (three hundred entries, category: regret), borrowed years (the interest rates are obscene), names that were never spoken (we keep those in a separate book, because they have a tendency to migrate). Somewhere in those pages is a record of every grief I have piped through bone, every widow, every last word. If the city burned tomorrow and every soul in it fled, the ledgers would remain, complete and indifferent, remembering everyone.

I dipped my pen. I wrote: *One (1) relic, human phalanx, post-mortem resonance. Client: Maren Ostrell, widow. Words recovered: fragmentary, concerning accounts. Fee: 11 marks. Carver: A. Dravenmoor.*

Then I paused, pen hovering, because the dead clerk's words were still sitting in my ears like a stone in a shoe.

Concerning accounts. The boy had worked for the Corvane counting-house. Vessa Corvane owns the Hollow Guild. And in eleven years of filing transactions, I had never once written the phrase *concerning accounts* in the column marked *nature of recovered words*, because in eleven years no bone had ever given me anything that sounded like the beginning of a sentence instead of the end of one.

I looked at the entry for a long moment. Then, because I am careful, because I am invisible, because I did not survive eleven years in this trade by writing interesting things in guild property—I struck the phrase out. *Fragmentary,* I wrote instead. *Personal. No further note.*

A lie, entered neatly in a book of truths. The ledgers forgive everything except smudging.

I closed the volume. I carried the widow's son's bone to the drawer where finished commissions go—not discarded, never discarded; the guild keeps everything, and the guild's keeping is why half the city fears us—and as I slid the drawer shut, I swear to you the bone hummed once against the wood. Faint. Almost polite. Like someone clearing their throat in the next room.

I told myself it was the settling of the building. Old shops settle. Old bones settle louder.

I banked the lamp and climbed the stairs to my room, where I do not keep photographs, because I have none; where I do not keep letters, because I write none; where the only object older than five years is a mirror I keep turned to the wall, because a man in my profession learns early that his own face is the one relic he should never carve.

Invisibility, I have said, is an achievement. Let me amend that. It is an achievement the way a held breath is an achievement. Sooner or later, everyone surfaces.

I fell asleep to the sound of snow beginning—early this year, far too early, fat flakes ticking against the glass like fingernails—and dreamed, for the first time in eleven years, of numbers. Long columns of them, marching off a page, all of them wrong, all of them written in a hand I almost recognized.

Mine.

* * *

Vessa Corvane summoned me at dawn, which was the second strange thing, because Vessa does not summon; she sends notes, and the notes are threats with calligraphy. Summoning is for emergencies, and emergencies, in the Hollow Guild, are for other people.

She received me in the counting-room upstairs, surrounded by her clerks—young ones, quick-fingered, none of whom would meet my eye—and she dismissed them with a flick of two fingers before I'd cleared the threshold.

"August." She never uses my surname. I've often wondered whether she's forgotten it or is saving it. "You filed a transaction last night. Widow, drowned son, clerk at my counting-house."

"Maren Ostrell. Yes."

"What did the bone say?"

I gave her the official version, bland and professional: fragmentary, personal, a mother's comfort purchased at market rate. I have delivered this speech perhaps nine hundred times. I could deliver it asleep. I may, in fact, have been asleep, because halfway through it Vessa crossed the room, took my chin in her cold fingers, and turned my face up to hers like an appraiser examining a suspect antique.

"August," she said softly, "in eleven years you have never once lied to me in a ledger. Don't start in person. It's beneath us both."

Outside the window, the snow was still falling. It had not stopped all night. In eleven years, I thought, it has never snowed this early, and the dead, I remembered, grow restless when the ground freezes before they're ready.

"Tell me what the boy said," Vessa repeated, "and then come with me to the prophecy vault. We're auditing it today—all of it, every shelf—and I want you beside me while I do it."

"Why?" I asked. "Why the vault? Why now?"

Something moved behind her eyes that I had never seen there before, and it took me a moment to recognize it, because it had no business living on the face of a woman who owned impossibility outright.

It was fear.

"Because," said Vessa Corvane, mistress of the Hollow Guild, keeper of the ledgers of impossible things, "last night, for the first time in a hundred years, someone opened the vault who wasn't supposed to be able to open it. And the book they touched was open to today's date."
