## 2. A Date Written in Dust

The prophecy vault smelled the way all old paper smells when it has been left alone too long: like dust that had given up pretending to be anything else. I liked it down there. Dust doesn't ask questions. Dust doesn't weep into your ledger and ask you to confirm that its son died well. Dust just settles, patient as a debt, and waits for you to acknowledge it or not.

I acknowledged it with a broom.

Twice a year, by guild charter, every carver of standing was required to audit the vaults beneath Corvane House—count the volumes, verify the seals, initial the inventory in ink that couldn't be forged because it was mixed with graveyard loam and cost more per ounce than most people earned in a season. Nobody wanted the job. The prophecies were considered the dullest corner of an already dull collection: a thousand years of seers predicting floods, famines, kings dying of things kings die of, none of it ever coming true in any way you could bet on. The guild kept them out of tradition, the way you keep a grandfather's bad furniture.

I volunteered for it every time.

You can see why. An audit is hours alone in a locked room with no one grieving at you, no one watching you, no one expecting your hands to be steady and your face to be nothing at all. It is the closest thing to privacy my life affords, and I hoard it the way misers hoard coins—guiltily, and counting.

So there I was, on the last cold night of autumn, with my lamp trimmed low and my inventory quill behind my ear, working through shelf forty-one of the deep stacks, when I found the book that ruined me.

It didn't look like ruin. It looked like exactly what it was: a ledger-bound codex, calf over boards, spine cracked in three places, titled on the fore-edge in faded gilt—*Conjectures of the Seer Maren Vole, Being Certain Observations Upon the War of Breath and Bone.* A century old, give or take. One of four hundred identical dreary volumes in that section alone. I'd audited it twice before without opening it past its flyleaf. Prophecy books are like drunk uncles: best acknowledged briefly and not engaged.

But the seal on this one was broken.

That shouldn't have been possible. Vault protocol required every volume to be wax-stamped after each audit, and the wax on shelf forty-one was intact on every book but this one, where it lay in two clean halves on the shelf like something had been set down carelessly—or deliberately—and never picked up again. I stood there holding the halves, feeling the particular itch at the base of my skull that I get when bone wants to talk to me, except this was paper, and paper only ever talks about people.

I should have logged the broken seal, re-waxed the book, initialed the discrepancy, and gone home to sleep the sleep of a man with no interesting qualities. That is the whole architecture of my existence: log, seal, initial, sleep. I have built a fortress out of tedium and I live in it quite comfortably.

Instead I opened it.

Maren Vole wrote the way seers always write, which is to say she wrote like a woman being paid by the word and haunted by the deadline. Pages of astrological squinting. Pages of portents involving comets, stillbirths, and the color of rivers. Halfway through, her hand changed—looser, faster, the ink blotted where she'd written too quickly to dip again—and it was there, in that hurried middle section, that I found the entry.

*In the last year of the war,* she had scrawled, *the courts will make a thing neither living nor dead, and they will set it walking among the guilds with no name and no grief, and it will carry the terms of the armistice in its own body, though it will not know it carries them. And on the night the first snow falls upon the city—*

I stopped reading. Not from reverence. From recognition.

Because the next line wasn't a line. It was a date. Circled. Twice. In ink that was brown-black and flaking, ink that had been dry for a hundred years, and the date was—

Today.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only weapon I've ever owned. I did not misread it. I did not confuse calendars; the guild uses one calendar and has used it since before the war, since before the guild, possibly since before anyone thought to argue about it. I checked the conversion tables twice, then a third time, the way a man checks a lock he has already locked. The numerals were clear. The circle around them was confident, drawn in a single stroke, the kind of circle you draw when you are very sure of what you're circling.

A hundred years ago, a dead woman's hand had reached across a century and circled tonight.

And beside it, in the margin, in that same hurried hand, smaller than the rest, almost apologetic:

*the Hollow Bearer opens.*

I sat down on the floor. This was not dramatic. My legs simply filed a formal complaint and the audit stool was too far away. I sat among the dust with the open book in my lap and the lamp guttering beside me, and I experienced what I can only describe as vertigo—not of the body but of the self, the sensation that the floor of your identity has quietly been removed while you weren't looking.

Here is the thing about being invisible. You work at it. August Dravenmoor, bone-carver, second-rank, keeper of ledgers, is a construction the way a wall is a construction: brick by brick, day by day, unremarkable mortar between unremarkable stones. I am punctual. I am competent. I am forgettable in the specific way that makes widows trust me with their dead and masters trust me with their accounts. I have spent eleven years—eleven years, I realized, sitting there, eleven years exactly, to the season—being no one in particular, and I had come to regard that as my natural state rather than my achievement.

And now a book older than my employment, older than my memory, older than anything I could point to and say *this is mine*, had been waiting on shelf forty-one with my date in it.

Not my name. That was the strange part, the part I turned over and over like a relic hunting for its voice. The passage said *a thing neither living nor dead.* It said *no name and no grief.* It described me the way you'd describe a piece of furniture—which, I suppose, is how I'd always described myself. But the fit was exact. No name. No grief. Walking among the guilds. Carrying something in its body that it did not know it carried.

I laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry bark that echoed down the stacks and came back sounding like someone else.

"No," I told the book, conversationally, because when you work alone long enough you start talking to objects and stop apologizing for it. "No, you've got the wrong man. I'm an accountant. I carve knucklebones for weeping women and file them under G for grief-services. I don't carry anything. I barely carry my own supper."

The book, being a book, declined to be reassured.

But here is what I could not shake, what I still cannot shake, writing this now with the lamp burned down to a thumbnail: the phrase *the Hollow Bearer opens.* Opens what? A door? A grave? A ledger? And opened *how*—by force, by accident, by some clerkish act of routine diligence, some man doing his job on an ordinary night?

Some man auditing shelf forty-one because he volunteers for it, because it's quiet down here, because dust doesn't ask questions.

I copied the passage. All of it—the whole hurried section, the date, the marginal words—in my own hand, onto a sheet of vault paper that officially does not exist and which I folded into the lining of my coat, against the breastbone, where a man might keep a letter from someone who loved him. It was the closest thing I had ever owned to a letter addressed to me. It was, in fact, the only document in the world that seemed to know I existed before I existed.

Then I did the cowardly, sensible, unforgivable thing: I closed the book, fitted the two halves of wax back together around the seal, pressed them warm with my thumb until they held, and entered the volume in the inventory as *unopened, seal intact.*

A forgery. My first. Eleven years of immaculate ledgers, and I falsified an entry in under a minute, and my hand never shook once, which frightened me more than the passage had. Apparently the skill was in me all along, waiting. Apparently a great many things were in me all along, waiting.

I climbed the stairs out of the vault two at a time, which I never do, because haste draws eyes. The guildhouse above was settling into its nighttime sounds—Vessa Corvane's boots crossing the hall above me, unhurried, precise, a woman who heard everything that happened in her house the way I hear the dead through bone. I flattened myself against the stairwell wall and let her pass overhead without looking up, invisible as designed, and for the first time in eleven years the invisibility felt less like armor than like a cage whose door I had only just noticed.

Outside, through the high grim windows, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had turned, and there was a smell in it I knew from the relics—from the bones of men who died in winter campaigns—a smell like iron and held breath.

Snow coming. First snow of the year.

Tonight, if the clouds kept their appointment.

*The Hollow Bearer opens.*

I walked home the long way, through streets where the lamps burned low and the dead murmured in their doorways the way they do when the seasons turn, and I told myself I would sleep, and I would wake, and I would report the broken seal honestly in the morning, and whatever the book meant by *opens*, it would not be me, because I was nobody, I had made very sure of nobody, nobody was my finest work.

I was halfway up my own stairs when the knock came at the door below.

Three knocks. Evenly spaced. Patient as dust.

And through the wood, in a voice I knew—knew intimately, professionally, having heard it a dozen times through carved bone saying goodbye to his mother—the widow's son said:

"August. She told me your other name."

* * *

I did not answer. Obviously. Answering doors at midnight is how invisible men become visible, permanently, in ways that involve shrouds.

"August," the voice said again, and it was gentle, the way he had been gentle in the relic, in the last warm seconds of his recorded life. "I know you're there. Dead men hear heartbeats through walls. Yours is going like a rabbit's."

I stood on the stairs with my hand on the rail and my breath coming out white in my own cold hallway, and I understood, with the clarity that comes only at the worst possible moments, that the book on shelf forty-one had not been waiting a hundred years for tonight.

It had been waiting for whoever knocked first.

"I'm coming in," said the dead man politely. "Don't reach for anything carved."
