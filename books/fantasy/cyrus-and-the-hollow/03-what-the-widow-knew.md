## 3. What the Widow Knew

There is a particular knock that grief makes when it comes back a second time. The first visit is all tears and trembling hands and the smell of too much perfume applied over unwashed skin. The second visit is quieter, more dangerous. It means the mourner has been thinking. Thinking is where I make my living, and thinking is also where my trouble starts.

I heard her on the stairs before she reached the landing—Mistress Aldery Vane, three days after her purchase, her boots hesitating on every step as though the stairs themselves might object. I had time to put away the chisel, straighten the ledger, and arrange my face into the expression I keep for clients: attentive, sympathetic, entirely empty behind the eyes.

The door opened before she knocked. That's the thing about bone-carved thresholds; they know their maker's moods.

"Master Dravenmoor." She stood in the doorway with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral that hadn't started yet. "Forgive me. I know the transaction is closed."

"The transaction is closed," I agreed, and did not move aside.

"I need to ask you something." Her eyes were red-rimmed but steady, which was worse than weeping. Weeping I could manage. Steadiness in a widow was a horse that had already thrown its rider once and decided it liked the taste of blood. "When you carved Tam's relic—the locket-bone—you heard him. His last words."

"That is the nature of the commission," I said carefully. "You paid for his final message. You received it."

"Yes." She swallowed. "But he said more than what you gave me."

Here is my rule, the one rule, carved into the inside of my own skull where no client can reach it: *never listen twice.* A relic holds one true utterance, the last thing the dead pressed into their marrow before the marrow went quiet. Listen again and you don't get more truth. You get the dead reaching back through the carving, looking for a hand to hold, and some of them do not let go. Some of them pull.

"Mistress Vane," I said, "whatever else the relic may hold, it isn't yours to hear. And frankly, it isn't mine either."

"He spoke your name."

The words landed in the room like a dropped chisel—small, hard, ringing.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Tam. In the nights since I brought the locket home, he speaks. Not to me—to *you*. He says your name over and over, but it isn't the name on your ledger. It isn't August Dravenmoor." She leaned forward, and I caught the scent of tallow and cold ash on her coat, the smell of someone who had been sitting up nights with the dead. "He says *Cyrus*."

I want to tell you that I laughed. I want to report that I made some dry remark about the unreliability of grieving mothers and showed her the door. What actually happened was this: the room got very slightly tilted, the way a floor tilts when a ship remembers it is on water, and I heard myself say, in a voice that belonged to a stranger wearing my throat:

"Say it again."

"*Cyrus,*" she said. "Cyrus. He says it like an apology. Like he owes you something. Master Dravenmoor—I came because I thought you should know your own name."

I have no past. This is not a figure of speech or a poet's flourish. I woke eleven years ago in a guild cot with clean hands, a ledger under my arm, and a head as smooth and featureless as river stone. Vessa Corvane told me I'd been found half-dead, that the guild had bought my debts and my silence both, that a carver who cannot remember cannot be blackmailed. I accepted this the way one accepts weather. A man without a past has nothing to grieve, and I had built a whole careful life on that foundation: serve the grief of others, permit yourself none, file everything, feel nothing, go home, sleep, repeat.

And now a dead boy—a boy whose last words I had carried through my own two hands—was saying *Cyrus* into the dark of a widow's bedroom like a man calling across a frozen lake.

"I don't know anyone by that name," I said.

"No," she agreed softly. "That's rather what I suspected. But he knows *you*." She reached into her coat and set something on my workbench. The locket-bone. My own carving, pale as a fingernail moon. "It won't stop. Three nights now. I haven't slept. Take it back if you must, refund me, curse me—but take it, because whatever he wants from you, he wants it badly enough to keep a poor woman awake."

She left before I could answer. Grief does that—it spends everything it has in one motion and then needs to sit down somewhere dark.

I stood over the locket for a long time. Bone remembers; that is the first law of my trade. But bone also *listens*, and I have always assumed the listening ran only one direction—from the dead, through the carving, to me. Standing there, I felt the direction reverse. Something on the other side of that pale curve of marrow was waiting to see what I would do.

My rule says never listen twice.

I picked up the locket anyway. Held it flat in my palm. Felt the faint warmth of it, the way you'd feel a held breath.

And then—because I am, above all things, a professional coward—I put it in a drawer, locked the drawer, and went to find Vessa Corvane.

* * *

The Hollow Guild keeps its mistress on the top floor, behind a door of black oak carved with the guild sigil: a keyhole with no key, a lock with no door. Vessa Corvane sat at her desk doing what she does best, which is making money look embarrassed to be seen with her. Silver hair cropped short, rings on six fingers, eyes the color of ledger ink. She looked up when I entered, and for exactly half a heartbeat—less, I would swear less—her face did something complicated before settling into its usual mask of fond contempt.

"August. Twice in one week. People will say I've favorites."

"A client returned a commission," I said. "Aldery Vane. The son's relic. It's... speaking."

"Relics speak. That's the product."

"It's speaking a name. Mine. Or—" I stopped. Committed. "*Or a name that used to be mine. Cyrus.*"

The silence that followed had texture. I have spent eleven years learning to read silences the way other men read faces, and this one said: *he knows, and she knew he might.*

Vessa set down her pen with great care, the way one sets down a live coal. "Where did you hear that word?"

"From the dead boy. Through his mother."

"And you came straight to me." Something almost warm moved through her voice, quickly buried. "Good. That was wise."

"Was it?" I asked. "Because here's what's unwise, Vessa: yesterday I audited the prophecy vault, and I found today's date circled in a book older than the guild charter, beside a phrase about someone called the Hollow Bearer. And today a dead man calls me Cyrus. And just now, when I said the word, you flinched. You flinched *before* I finished the sentence, which means you already knew it, which means—" I took a breath. Eleven years of not-asking rose up in me like floodwater against a door. "Which means there is a past of mine worth hiding. Which means I have one."

"August—"

"Do I?"

She rose from her desk. Vessa Corvane is not tall, but she has a way of occupying space that makes architecture feel decorative. She came around the desk and stood close enough that I could smell the bergamot she uses to cover the smell of the vault dust.

"You are August Dravenmoor," she said, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "Bone-carver, third rank, keeper of ledgers, employed by this guild for eleven years, paid fairly, protected absolutely. You are a man I pulled out of a ditch and taught a trade, and I will thank you not to let a hysterical widow and a dead child rewrite your history."

"Then say it plainly: there's nothing. No records. No origin. Nothing before the ditch."

"There's nothing *relevant*."

"Everything is relevant to someone," I said. "That's the entire business model of this guild."

Her hand came up—fast, faster than a woman her age should move—and gripped my jaw. Not cruelly. Precisely. The way you grip a tool you're afraid of dropping.

"Listen to me, August, because I will say this once, and then never again. There are things in this city that were buried for good reasons, and the reasons are still good, and the people who buried them are still watching. If a name surfaces—and names are surfacing, I can feel it the way you feel weather in old bones—then the wisest thing you can do, the *kindest* thing, is to stop asking questions. Not slow down. Stop. Because the moment you start pulling that thread, you stop being a man the guild protects and become a thing the guild *contains*, and I have seen what containment looks like, and it is not a ditch, August. Ditches are merciful."

I have been threatened by better, or so I assume; I wouldn't remember. But I have rarely been threatened with such evident grief, and that frightened me more than any knife would have. Vessa Corvane was not warning me off for the guild's sake. She was warning me off the way you warn someone away from a well you personally filled.

She released my jaw and stepped back, smoothing her rings, reassembling herself.

"Refund the widow. Reclaim the relic. Melt it down for stock bone. And August—" she paused at the window, snow beginning to sift past the glass, early this year, far too early, "—the vault audit you mentioned. File your report. Note nothing unusual. Can you do that for me?"

"Of course," I said. "Note nothing unusual."

She believed me. That was her mistake, and possibly mine.

* * *

That night I sat in my rooms above the workshop with the locket-bone out of its drawer and a blank page of ledger paper before me, and I did the arithmetic of my life. Eleven years. One ditch. Zero memories. A head like swept ground. I had told myself for a decade that this was freedom—the clean slate, the man with nothing to lose. Sitting there, turning the pale curve of Tam Vane's femur in my fingers, I understood for the first time that a man with nothing to lose is a man who has already lost everything and been billed for the privilege.

Never listen twice.

I set the locket against my ear like a shell.

At first, nothing. Then—not words, not yet, but pressure, the sense of a vast attention turning toward me from a long way off, the way you feel a crowd go quiet in the next room. And beneath that, faint as breath on winter glass, a young man's voice, patient, exhausted, repeating the only thing he had left:

*Cyrus. I'm sorry. Cyrus. They made us forget. Cyrus—*

The candle guttered. Outside, the snow thickened, falling silent and wrong, flakes drifting upward for a heartbeat before remembering which way was down. And in the marrow of a dead boy's leg bone, in a voice that was somehow also my own, something whispered:

*They're coming to ask you what you remember. Please—remember faster.*

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark, holding a dead man's apology, listening to my own heart count down like a ledger being balanced.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would copy the prophecy page.

Tonight, I would finally learn how to be afraid properly.
