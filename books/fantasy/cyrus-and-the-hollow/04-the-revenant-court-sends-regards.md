## 4. The Revenant Court Sends Regards

The dead do not knock. I want that on the record, filed somewhere between *never listen twice* and *do not ask questions you can't afford the answers to*. The dead simply arrive, the way damp arrives through stone—patiently, everywhere at once, and always where it will do the most damage.

I was in the counting room when the candles leaned.

Not guttered. *Leaned.* All forty of them, bending east toward the door like wheat under a wind that wasn't there, and the flame-light went the color of old bruises. I have worked for the Hollow Guild eleven years, and in that time I have developed a private taxonomy of dread. Candle-lean is near the top. It means something is crossing the threshold that has weight without mass.

The doors opened on a woman made of winter.

That's not poetry. Her skin had the grey-blue cast of ice held up to lamplight, and frost bloomed and died across her shoulders in slow cycles, like breathing. She wore court dress—the revenant court's version of it: high collar, long train, buttons of carved bone that I recognized, professionally, as human femur, polished to the color of cream. Her eyes were the worst part. They were kind. Nothing that beautiful should be kind.

"August Dravenmoor," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded like it was being read from a ledger. "Bone-carver. Keeper of ledgers. Man of no past." She smiled. "We've come to admire your bookkeeping."

"We," said Vessa Corvane from the doorway, in the voice she uses when she's deciding whether to sell someone, "is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"Guild mistress." The emissary inclined her head with the precise courtesy of one duelist acknowledging another. "My principals send their regards."

"The dead don't have regards," Vessa said. "They have grievances."

"And tonight they are feeling generous about them." The emissary produced, from her sleeve, a purse that clinked with the particular heavy sound of coin minted before the war. Pre-war gold. There isn't much of it left, and what remains buys things money shouldn't. "A page," she said. "One page, from one book, in your vault. The one dated—" she paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over an incision, "—today."

The room got very quiet. Even the candles stopped leaning.

I should say here that I am very good at being invisible. It is not a talent; it is a discipline. Eleven years of keeping my voice level, my hands clean, my questions unasked. So I did what I have trained myself to do. I looked bored.

"You have the wrong shop," I said. "I keep ledgers. Ledgers aren't sold."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But pages are. Everything is sold, Mr. Dravenmoor. That is the entire philosophy of your guild. You trade in bottled memories and borrowed years. You deal in names that were never spoken. And yet suddenly, shyly, you develop principles?" She turned the purse over in her fingers, letting it speak its silver argument. "Name a price. We will pay it twice."

Vessa's eyes flicked to me—one heartbeat, no more—and I read the whole ledger of her soul in it. She was calculating. Not whether to sell me. Whether selling me would be *profitable enough* to survive whatever came after.

"The vault inventory is sealed," Vessa said smoothly, "by guild law. No single page is alienable. The answer is no—not for lack of interest, emissary, but because the Hollow Guild does not sell what it cannot price."

"A pity." The emissary sighed, and frost bloomed across the counting table, delicate as lace, then melted away as if ashamed. "Then we'll simply wait. We are very good at waiting. Waiting is most of what we do." She turned those kind, terrible eyes on me. "You should know, Mr. Dravenmoor, that we did not send our best negotiator first. We sent our best *listener*. She'll come later, when you've had time to wonder. Wondering softens people. It softened you already, I think. You audited that vault three days ago and you haven't slept since."

I said nothing. Which, of course, told her everything.

She left the purse on the table anyway—a down payment on an answer nobody had given. Then she walked out through doors that opened for her before she touched them, and the candles straightened, and the room remembered how to be warm.

Vessa looked at the purse. Looked at me. Said, with terrifying mildness: "Interesting week you're having, August."

* * *

Here is what the emissary knew that night, walking back through the frozen streets toward the river and the drowned hall beneath it:

Her name had been Lissandra Hael once, and she remembered all of it, which was her punishment. The dead who serve the court remember everything—that is the price of their office, the tax levied on eternity. She had been a negotiator in life too, and she had sat across the table, a hundred years ago, from a young man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like warm bread, and together they had written terms onto parchment in blood because ink could be forged and blood could not.

She remembered his name. That was the unbearable part. She remembered it perfectly, syllable by syllable, and she had walked into the Hollow Guild tonight and stood three feet from the man wearing his face—older, remade, hollowed out like a gourd—and he had looked at her with polite boredom and not a flicker of recognition.

The court's orders were simple: buy the page, or confirm that the bearer cannot remember. If he cannot remember, he is safe. If he begins to remember—

Lissandra stood on the bridge above the black water and admitted the truth to herself, because honesty was another of her taxes: the court did not fear what August Dravenmoor might *say*. They feared what he might *feel*. Terms written in blood bind to blood, and his blood remembered even if his mind did not. She had watched his hands while he refused them. Carver's hands. Steady. But when she'd said *you haven't slept since*, something behind his eyes had moved—something turning over, like a body shifting in shallow ground.

He was beginning to dig.

She should report him as dangerous. The protocol was clear. Instead she found herself lingering on the bridge, listening to the water, remembering warm bread and ink-stained fingers, and thinking—with the small treachery available only to the dead—that perhaps some things deserved to be remembered after all.

She reported nothing. For now. And in the ledger the court kept on *him*, she wrote the only entry that mattered:

*Subject refuses gold. Subject lies badly. Subject is curious.*

*Curiosity, in bearers, precedes memory.*

* * *

I copied the page that night.

I want to be honest about this: it was theft. Guild law says vault contents are sealed, and copying is a form of taking, and taking is the one crime the Hollow Guild punishes without irony. I know all this. I did it anyway, because curiosity had gotten its teeth into me and I have learned that the only way to survive curiosity is to feed it until it sleeps.

The vault opens to my key after midnight—it always has; the guild trusts its invisible man with keys, which tells you something tragic about trust—and I sat in the cold dark with the prophecy book open on my knees and a blank sheet of vellum and my own carving knife dipped in lampblack, because I copy things the way I make them: by hand, stroke by stroke, so the words pass through my bones.

*On the last day of the long winter, the Hollow Bearer opens.*

*What he carries is himself.*

*Let him speak the terms or let the terms speak him.*

There was more, but the rest was smudged—water damage, or tears, a century old. One line survived at the bottom margin, small, almost apologetic:

*The witness is the warranty.*

I sat there with the lampblack drying on the knife and thought: I don't know what any of this means. And beneath that, quieter, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine: *yes you do.*

I finished the copy, folded it flat, and slid it into the false spine of my own ledger—the one place in the guild no one audits, because auditing the auditor is unthinkable. As I closed the vault I heard it, faint, from the shelves behind me: a whisper, dry as paper turning.

Not words. Just my name. The true one. The one the widow's son had spoken. The one I still didn't know.

Sounded like it was being practiced. Sounded like something getting ready to be said aloud.

I locked the vault, pocketed the copy against my ribs, and went back upstairs to not sleep—past the counting room, where the emissary's purse still sat on the table like a threat with a bow tied on it.

Pre-war gold. Minted before the war.

And I stood there in the dark looking at it, and my hands—steady carver's hands, traitor hands—remembered the weight of a pen, and the taste of copper, and a table where two courts sat down to end a war, and I thought, with a calm that frightened me more than panic ever could:

*I was there.*

Tomorrow I would ask Vessa what the guild made me from.

Tonight, I picked up the purse, and hid it, and started planning how to get into the pre-war bone stores—because bone remembers, and I needed to know what *my* bone had been keeping all these years.
