## 6. The Long Winter Falls

The first snow came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophe ought to arrive at night, in thunder, with some ceremony. Instead it arrived while I was eating soup, drifting past the guild's high windows in fat, lazy flakes, and every flake was whispering.

I want to be clear about that: whispering. Not the way snow sometimes carries sound the way old walls do—I'd lived in this city long enough to know its ordinary hauntings. This was different. Each flake that touched the glass left behind a thread of voice, thin as wire: *he knows, he knows, he knows.* A thousand tiny mouths, all saying my name without saying it.

I put down my spoon. The soup had gone cold anyway. Grief does that to kitchens; I suppose dread does too.

"August." Vessa Corvane stood in the doorway of the common room, and for once she wasn't wearing her guild-mistress face—the lacquered one, patient as a ledger line. She wore something rawer underneath it, and I didn't like it any better. "You've seen the sky."

"I've seen the soup."

"Don't be clever today. Cleverness is a luxury." She crossed the room and pulled the shutters closed, one by one, and the whispers cut off mid-word each time the wood swung shut. The last one sounded almost disappointed. "The dead have been restless for three nights. Every carver in the city is reporting the same thing—relics going hot, bones singing in their drawers. The Mire Street guild lost two apprentices to a listening fit. They're still standing at their benches, carving nothing, smiling."

"That sounds like them."

Vessa turned on me with an expression I had never seen her wear. It took me a moment to identify it, because I'd only ever seen it aimed at other people from across a negotiating table.

It was fear.

"Three days," she said. "The date in that book is three days out, and you're making jokes about soup. Do you understand what's converging on this building? The Gray Ledger has offered me money I could retire three times over on. The revenant court has sent notaries. Notaries, August. The dead don't send notaries unless they intend to draw up terms."

"What did you tell them?"

The pause before she answered lasted exactly one heartbeat too long.

"I told them I'd consider their offers."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Vessa Corvane, or thought I knew: she had pulled me out of nothing eleven years ago. That was the story she told, and the story I told myself, because everyone needs an origin and mine came pre-packaged. No family. No history before a certain gray morning when I woke on the steps of the Hollow Guild with steady hands, a talent for bone, and a head as empty as a swept room. Vessa had found me there—or so she said—and taught me the trade, the rules, the ledgers. Never listen twice. Never carve from living bone. Never ask what you were before.

She had been generous, in her way. She paid me fairly. She defended me in disputes with the other masters. Once, when a client accused me of theft and demanded my hands as payment, Vessa had stared the man down until he apologized to *me*, which in this city was roughly equivalent to parting the sea.

She had also, I now understood, kept me like a thing in a drawer. Steady hands don't come from nowhere. Empty heads aren't natural weather.

That evening I climbed to the prophecy vault—not to read the book again; I'd stopped needing to. The date was printed on the inside of my eyelids now. I went because the vault was cold and quiet and the quiet helped me think, and what I thought was this:

If I were a woman who had made a man out of spare parts and a stolen secret, and the secret were about to walk off on its own legs, what would I do?

I would watch him. And then, when watching failed—

I heard footsteps on the vault stairs and pressed myself into the shadow of the shelving, which is where I happened to be when Vessa entered the vault below with a lantern and a man I did not know. He was dressed in the gray-and-silver of the Gray Ledger, the rival guild, and he moved like someone who counted things for a living.

"The price stands," the man said. "Deliver him intact. Memory unspoiled. Our buyers will do the extraction themselves."

"And if the court bids higher?"

"Then sell to the dead and pray they pay in something you can spend."

Vessa laughed, and it was her real laugh, low and warm, and hearing it used in service of this made something in my chest go very still and cold, like a relic cooling after the echo fades.

"You misunderstand me," she said. "I'm not selling him to one side. I'm running an auction. Whoever pays best gets the Hollow Bearer, and whoever pays second-best gets to believe they were close. That's how you survive a bidding war between guilds and ghosts. You make sure both sides lose gracefully."

"And if he remembers on his own first?"

The lantern light caught Vessa's face, and I saw the guilt there—real guilt, worn like a hairline crack in good porcelain. She was sorry. She was going to do it anyway.

"Then," she said quietly, "everyone loses loudly, and I'll have at least been paid."

They went up the stairs. I stayed in the dark a long while, doing arithmetic. Three days. Two buyers. One auction lot, and the lot was me.

I had always thought of myself as careful. Invisible by craft, by discipline, by the simple virtue of never mattering. It had never once occurred to me that I might be invisible the way evidence is invisible—hidden on purpose, by someone else, in a place they chose.

* * *

The next day the city itself began to turn against its own silence.

You learn to read a haunted city the way sailors read weather. Normally the dead are like damp—they seep, they settle, they stay where they're put. But walking to the market that morning, I passed the bell tower where a carver had hung a memorial bell sixty years ago for the war's unnamed dead, and the bell was ringing. No rope moving. No wind. Just a slow, arrhythmic tolling, like a heart remembering how.

In the market square, a memory-broker's stall had collapsed into chaos—bottles rolling everywhere, and every bottle that broke released not the usual fog of somebody's birthday but a single repeated word, over and over, in dozens of borrowed voices: *bearer. Bearer. BEARER.*

An old woman selling roasted chestnuts grabbed my sleeve as I passed. Her eyes were white as the snow. "You," she said, in a voice that wasn't hers—it was older, layered, many throats speaking through one mouth the way voices speak through relics, except no one had carved her. "You carry it. You carried it before. Give it back."

"I don't know what you mean," I said, which was true in the way that a locked box is empty.

Her grip tightened. Chestnuts scattered. "The terms, little clerk. The terms you wrote in your own blood. The dead have waited a hundred years in the cold you brokered. We remember what you chose to forget."

She let go as suddenly as she'd seized me, blinked, and became a confused old woman holding a tongs and apologizing for the mess. Around us, the market pretended very hard that nothing had happened, which is the market's principal skill.

But they knew. All of them knew. The whole restless city of the dead had been waiting a century for a man who didn't exist yet, and now he existed, and he was buying chestnuts.

I bought the chestnuts anyway. If the world was going to end in three days, I saw no reason to do it hungry.

* * *

By dusk, the watchers had arrived.

I spotted the first from my window: a figure across the street who had been reading the same page of a newspaper since morning, in snow, without gloves. Gray Ledger, certainly—only the Ledgers could afford that kind of patience. The second watcher was subtler, a shadow on the roofline opposite that didn't move when the pigeons startled, which meant it wasn't flesh, or wasn't only. The third I only sensed, the way you sense a draft under a door—a cold attention fixed on the building, patient as frost.

Three directions. Maybe more. Vessa's auction had drawn a full house.

So I did what any reasonable man would do, which is pack a bag, steal his own future back, and lie to his keeper.

I found Vessa in her office over the counting room, adding columns by lamplight. The numbers, I noticed, were large. Newer than yesterday's large.

"August." She looked up, and the guilt-crack in her porcelain was wider tonight. "You should sleep. Tomorrow we—"

"We what?" I leaned in the doorframe, arms crossed, wearing the blandest face I owned. "Tomorrow we talk about the offers? I heard you, Vessa. The vault has good acoustics. Intact memory, unspoiled extraction. Very flattering. I've never been described so kindly by anyone trying to sell me."

For a long moment she didn't move. Then she set down her pen with the care of a woman disarming herself.

"How much?"

"All of it. The auction especially. That was elegant. I'd admire it if I weren't the lot."

"August—" She rose, hands open, and for one naked moment she looked less like a guild mistress than like a woman standing at the edge of a grave she'd dug herself, years ago, and only now noticing its size. "Everything I did, I did to keep that secret buried. You were the safest place to keep it. A man with no past, guarded by people paid to have none either. Eleven years of peace. Do you know how many died to buy those eleven years?"

"No. Because you erased that too."

"Yes!" The word cracked out of her like ice giving way. She steadied herself against the desk. "And I would do it again. Whatever comes through that door—Ledger, revenant, the war itself—I will choose the peace over the truth every single time, because I have seen the alternative and it is a century of winter. So yes. I will sell you. To whoever lets me keep burying you."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above us, faintly, through stone and timber, the memorial bell tolled twice.

"Then here's my counteroffer," I said. "I leave tonight. You tell your buyers I escaped—which will even be true—and you collect nothing, and the auction closes, and maybe, if I'm feeling generous, I don't take the secret with me when I go."

Something flickered across her face—hope, or its close cousin, calculation.

"And where would you go, August? You've never been anywhere. You don't know the city outside these walls. You don't know *yourself* outside these walls."

"No," I agreed, shouldering my bag. "But I'm told there's a version of me under the city who did. I'm going to go dig him up."

I was at the door when she spoke again, softly, and it was the softness that stopped me.

"The widow's son," Vessa said. "Tamsin Hale's boy. He spoke your name, didn't he? Before she ever came to my counter." I turned. She was looking at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere in my careful ledgers. "I know because I sent her to you. First domino, August. I've been tipping them for a week, trying to see which way you'd fall."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the snow.

"So run," she said, turning back to her ledger, picking up her pen as though we'd discussed the price of tallow. "Run to the undercity. Run to your own grave. And when you find what's buried there—and you will find it, because it wants finding—remember that everything hunting you wanted you to dig. The only question left is whose hands are on the shovel when you do."

I went down the back stairs, out through the reliquary door, and up into the falling dark.

The snow was thicker now, and the flakes whispered louder as I walked—*he runs, he runs*—and somewhere behind me a bell tolled, and ahead of me the ground sloped down toward the old warrens where the ruins hummed, and I realized, with the peculiar clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, that Vessa had never been my protector at all.

She had been my warden. And wardens, when the prisoner finally walks, always know exactly which gate he'll use.

She'd left it unlocked.
