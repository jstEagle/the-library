## 7. Flight Through the Hollow City

I have a professional rule about running: never do it. Running tells everyone watching that you are worth catching, and in the Hollow City, worth catching is worth killing.

I broke my rule at the ninth bell, when I went out the guild's coal chute with Vessa's ledgers under one arm and her betrayal still warm in my chest.

Let me be precise about that last part, because precision is the only inheritance I own. I had heard her through the door of her study, speaking to someone whose voice sounded like wet gravel poured over silk — a revenant broker, or something wearing the manners of one. *"The Bearer will come to you frightened,"* she said. *"Frightened men sell cheap. But if the Court bids higher, well. I am a businesswoman before I am anything else."*

*Anything else.* That phrase has been sitting in me like a swallowed stone ever since. Because for five years she was everything else to me — the hand that taught me the chisel angles, the voice that told me which griefs were safe to carve and which belonged in the deep vaults. She fed me supper on the long nights and never once asked about my past, and I mistook that mercy for kindness when it was simply good husbandry. You don't ask questions of livestock.

The coal chute spat me into the alley behind the tanneries, and I ran.

* * *

The undercity is where the Hollow City keeps its shame. Above ground we call it history: monuments, plaques, the War Memorial with its bronze soldiers who look, if you squint, mildly embarrassed. Below ground it is just what it was — a battlefield that got built over because nobody wanted to look at it anymore. Old cisterns where companies drowned. A tram tunnel that became a trench for eleven days and then became a grave for four hundred. The masons sealed most of it after the armistice, but the city is old and dishonest, and there are always ways down.

I knew three. The widow's son knew more.

That is not a figure of speech, though I grant it sounds mad even by my standards. Tam Wren died two years ago at nineteen, and I carved his echo into a locket of riverbone for his mother, and I heard his last words, and then — this is the part I am not proud of — on certain nights since, when the guild was quiet, I took out the practice scrap of his carving and listened again. Twice. Three times. Dozens. Every rule I had, broken for a dead boy who spoke kindly.

His echo lived in the bone the way warmth lives in bread. And tonight, as I dropped down through the grating behind the Renderers' Hall with revenants' lantern-light swaying somewhere above, his voice came up through the dark of my coat pocket like a hand finding mine.

*"Down and left,"* said the ghost of Tam Wren. *"Past the flooded stairs. Don't touch the railings. They remember hands."*

"You're an echo," I whispered to him. "Echoes repeat things. They don't give directions."

*"I repeat what mattered,"* he said, and there was something in his tone that no echo should have had — patience, almost, or pity. *"You listened so many times I learned the shape of your listening. Down and left, Hollow Bearer."*

I did not correct him. There didn't seem to be any point, and besides, correcting ghosts is how you end up arguing alone in the dark, which is bad enough without witnesses.

* * *

The war ruins hum. I want that on the record, because everyone above ground insists they don't. The sealed places hum the way a struck bell hums after you've clapped your hand over it — a sound you feel in your teeth rather than hear. Bone-carvers know why, though we're forbidden to say it: the dead don't leave a place just because the living stop looking. They settle. They compress. And compressed grief makes a noise, low and endless, like the city itself grinding its teeth in its sleep.

Tam guided me past it all. Past the flooded stairs, black water breathing against stone. Past a wall where someone had scratched tally marks into the brick — days counted by some soldier who ran out of days — and the marks had gone soft-edged, melted-looking, as if the wall had tried to weep them away. Past a shrine some anonymous mourner had made from a boot, a candle stub, and a name carved so small it might have been a secret apology.

*"She carved me too,"* Tam said quietly as we passed it. *"My mother. In plain wood, on the doorframe. It doesn't hold anything. It doesn't need to."*

"Your mother thinks you told her my true name."

A pause. Echoes shouldn't be able to pause, either. *"Did I?"*

"That's what she says. A name August Dravenmoor doesn't recognize. Which means either you lied, or someone put the lie in your mouth, or—" I stopped walking. The hum was louder here, and underneath it I could hear water dripping in a rhythm that felt deliberate. "Or the name is real and I'm not who I've been telling myself I am for five years."

*"Or all three,"* Tam said, gently. *"Left here. The cache is ahead. You'll know it because it hurts."*

"It what?"

But the passage turned, and I understood.

* * *

It hurt the way a tooth hurts — localized, personal, unmistakable. One particular stretch of corridor, maybe thirty feet, where the hum sharpened into something with an edge on it, and every step I took felt like walking against the grain of the world. My scars — the ones I'd always assumed came from a chisel accident, a fire, some ordinary catastrophe — began to ache in sequence, left hand first, then throat, then sternum, like a lock testing keys.

"There," Tam said.

There was a niche in the wall behind a slab of fallen masonry, and in the niche, a box. Not hidden, exactly. *Waiting.* The stone around it had been carved away in clean, professional strokes — my strokes. I recognized my own technique the way you recognize your own handwriting, which is to say with a lurch, because I had no memory of ever standing in this corridor, and yet my chisel-work sat here patient as a signature.

The box held three things.

A ledger page, brittle as autumn, covered in terms written in a cramped hand I also recognized, because it was mine — the same slant, the same way I cross my sevens. The ink had gone brown. Ink does that over decades. Blood browns faster.

A second sheet: the prophecy page, the one I'd copied from the vault. Someone had already known I'd need it. That thought kept arriving in my head and refusing to leave.

And a bone. White, dense, pre-war — the good material, the kind that holds a voice like glass holds light. It was a hand-bone, a metacarpal, and it had been carved with a delicacy I have never once managed in five years of practice. Along its length, in letters fine as hair, ran the inscription:

*THE TERMS ARE THE WITNESS. THE WITNESS IS THE TERMS. SPEAK THEM WHOLE OR NOT AT ALL.*

I picked it up and it fit my hand. Of course it fit my hand. It had been *my* hand. Somewhere below the wrist, on a night I could not remember, in a life I had been scraped clean of, this bone had been taken from my body and carved by — by whom? By me? By Vessa? By whoever remade August Dravenmoor out of whatever was left?

I sat down on the fallen masonry and did something I permit myself very rarely, which is nothing at all. Just breathing. The hum filled the corridor. Somewhere above, the revenants were still hunting; somewhere above, Vessa Corvane was deciding which court to sell me to; somewhere above, the snow was starting, early and wrong, carrying whispers the way the almanacs used to say it did before the worst winters.

*"Read them,"* Tam said.

"No."

*"August."*

"I mean not yet." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "Do you understand what happens if I read them? If the terms need a living witness to renew them — or void them — then the moment I speak them aloud, I'm not a man anymore. I'm a key. And every locked door in this city, living and dead, is going to want to turn me."

*"Yes,"* said the ghost of Tam Wren, with the terrible honesty of the nineteen-year-old he would never stop being. *"But you're already a key. You've been one since before you had a name you knew. The only question is whether you get turned by someone else or whether you choose the door yourself."*

I looked down at the hand-bone lying in my palm, humming faintly against my skin, and I thought about the widow, Maren Wren, who paid in honest coin and honest grief and believed her son had given her a gift she couldn't use. I thought about five years of careful invisibility, of never listening twice, of being so thoroughly nothing that even I believed it. I thought about how the guild hadn't erased a dangerous man. They'd erased the *last casualty of the war* and handed him an apron and a broom and called it mercy.

Terror, I discovered, is a fuel like any other. Burned properly, it stops feeling like terror at all. It starts feeling like anger, and then like direction.

"Tam," I said. "When the emissary cornered me, it said both courts fear what I might remember. Not what I might *do*. What I might *remember*."

*"Yes."*

"So the memory is the weapon. And right now the memory is in pieces — half in this bone, half in the terms, half in me." I stood, and my knees had stopped shaking, which surprised me. "Which means before anyone can use me, I have to make myself whole. All the halves. Even the ugly ones."

*"Where will you go?"*

I tucked the bone inside my coat, next to Tam's echo, next to the blood-inked terms, next to the prophecy page — a little archive of myself, carried against my chest like contraband, which is exactly what it was.

"To the only person in this city who ever gave me truth without charging for it," I said. "Maren Wren told me her son spoke my true name. She's the one thread I can still pull. And if the courts and the guilds are all converging on me, then I'm done running anyway — my rule, remember. Never run. Make them come to you, and make sure that when they arrive, you're holding something they want worse than your death."

The hum of the ruins seemed to lean in and listen.

Above me, far above, the first snow was falling on the Hollow City, and every flake of it was whispering. And for the first time since I'd found today's date circled in dust, I wasn't afraid of what the winter was saying.

I was going to answer it.

I climbed back toward the dark, toward the hunters, toward Maren Wren's door — and behind me, in the sealed corridor where the war still ground its teeth, something ancient shifted its weight, and began, very softly, to follow.
