## 8. The Price of the Secret

The undercity hums like a struck bell that refuses to finish ringing.

I've been running for what feels like a season and is probably three hours—down through the drowned market where the water still remembers the stalls it swallowed, along the war-tunnels where the mortar between the bricks was mixed with ash because someone, a century ago, thought that was a good idea. The widow's son's echo walks beside me. Not the boy himself—he's been dead nine years—but the shape grief left in him when I carved his last words into bone. He doesn't speak much. He points.

Right now he's pointing at the tunnel mouth ahead of us, and I wish he weren't, because standing in it, patient as winter, is the emissary.

It wears its borrowed body well—a grey coat, a grey face, eyes like coins left too long in a fountain. When I'd met it in the guildhall I'd mistaken it for a man with excellent posture and terrible circulation. Now, this deep, I can see the seams. The dead don't build bodies; they rent them.

"August Dravenmoor," it says, and then, softly, almost kindly: "That isn't your name."

"I know," I say. "Everyone keeps telling me. It's becoming my least favorite sentence."

"Then you know some of it." The echo at my shoulder shivers. The emissary tilts its head toward him, respectful, the way one bows to a colleague's portrait. "You were running from us. From the Court. And yet you came here—to the place your own blood wrote down. Curiosity over safety." Something in the coin-grey eyes flickers. "I remember that about you."

"You remember *me*?"

"I remember everyone who signed," it says. "Come inside. It's cold, and what I have to tell you shouldn't be said standing up in a tunnel full of ash-mortar. Ash listens."

Everything down here listens. That's rather the point.

* * *

From the emissary's ledger—and I use the word deliberately, because whatever else the dead keep, they keep accounts—the entry reads:

*Subject recovered beneath the Old Exchange. Cooperative. Suspiciously cooperative. This should be noted as either a virtue or a symptom.*

I am called Vesper. I have worn eleven bodies since the armistice and disliked ten of them. I was present at the signing. I was present at the unmaking. Both times I stood in the same corner, holding the same candle, because the Courts believe symmetry lends proceedings dignity and I have never had the heart to correct them.

The living think we want war. The dead think the living want war. Both are wrong, mostly, but wrongness is load-bearing—it holds up treaties, marriages, guilds. What we actually wanted, both sides, all those years ago, was an ending that didn't require anyone to admit they'd lost. And so a broker was found. A carver whose gift let him hear both courts at once, who could carry terms across the line without either side having to look at the other.

He was young. He was tired. He believed, with the particular ferocity of the exhausted, that words spoken aloud by the right mouth could hold back anything.

He was right. That was the problem.

* * *

The chamber beneath the Old Exchange is round, and the ceiling is bone—not carved, grown, pale ribs arching overhead like the interior of something enormous that died mid-thought. In the center stands a plinth, and on the plinth lies a hand-bone the length of my forearm, and I know it the way you know your own handwriting: before I see it, I've already seen it.

My name is cut into it. Not *Dravenmoor*. The other one. The true one, the one the widow's son spoke, the one that sits in my chest like a key in a lock that has never once been turned. Reading it does nothing. Hearing it did everything. Which tells me something I don't yet want to understand.

"Sit," says the emissary, and there are chairs. Of course there are chairs. It planned this meeting the way I plan ledger entries—thoroughly, and in advance of anyone knowing an account existed.

I sit. The echo-boy stands behind me, a small grey warmth.

"Tell me what you think you know," the emissary says, "and I will tell you where you're wrong. It saves time."

"I was the last casualty of the war," I say. "I died, someone remade me, wiped me, and set me to carving relics in a city that forgot me on purpose."

"Elegant. Wrong in three places." It raises fingers. "One: you weren't the last casualty. You were the *first* casualty of the peace. Different thing entirely, and the distinction matters more than you can currently afford. Two: no one remade you. You remade yourself—or rather, the terms did. Three—" it pauses, and for the first time the coin-eyes look away from me, which frightens me more than anything else it's done— "three: we didn't wipe you because you knew too much. We wiped you because the secret *is* you. There is no document. There is no cache. There is only the witness. The terms live in your memory, and they renew themselves only so long as a living mind carries them whole."

The ceiling-bone seems closer than it was.

"So erase the memory," the emissary continues, "and the terms don't die. They go dormant. Held in trust. Waiting for the bearer to remember them—which is why neither court killed you, August. Killing you would have voided everything. Instead they erased you, gave you a trade that kept your hands near bone and your ears near echoes, and hoped you'd stay incurious forever."

"Hoped," I say. "And now?"

"Now the century turns. The terms require renewal by a living voice, spoken aloud, witnessed by both courts. Renewal—or voidance. Speak them truly, and the peace holds another hundred years. Speak them falsely, or refuse, or let the date pass—" it spreads its grey hands— "and every debt of the war comes due at once. Every grave opens on its own schedule. No armies required. Just arithmetic."

I laugh. I can't help it; it comes out cracked and awful. "So tonight's prophecy date. 'The Hollow Bearer opens.' It's not a threat. It's a *deadline*."

"It's a contract clause," the emissary agrees. "Prophecy is just law written by people with better handwriting."

* * *

Here is what I do not write in any ledger, because there is no column for it: watching him laugh in that ruined chamber, hearing the crack in it, I felt something I have not felt since the signing.

Shame.

We told ourselves the erasure was mercy. A man who cannot remember his death cannot be haunted by it; a bearer who cannot recall the terms cannot be tortured into surrendering them. We were protecting the peace. We were also, conveniently, protecting ourselves from his face—because he begged, at the end, not to be unmade. He asked to keep *something*. His mother's voice. The smell of the river in summer. One thing. We took all of it, and I held the candle, and I watched, and I recorded the procedure in triplicate because grief without paperwork is just weather.

Now he sits where he sat then, older in a way that has nothing to do with years, and asks the question we always feared:

"Why offer to buy the page? Why hunt me? If renewal serves both courts, why does everyone want me *dead or delivered*?"

Because, I could say: renewal requires truth, and truth requires trust, and neither court trusts the other to hear the terms spoken honestly. Each believes the other will falsify the record, claim advantage, tip the balance. Each wants the bearer renewed *under its own supervision*—which means owning him, which means the peace becomes a leash rather than a bridge. Power, offered, is already corruption. We are merely deciding whose hands shake first.

What I actually say is: "Because everyone would rather win the argument than survive the winter."

And he looks at me—this hollowed man, this walking archive—and says, "And you? Which are you?"

I do not answer. I am a ledger. Ledgers do not answer.

* * *

"The widow's son spoke your name," the emissary says. "That was not accident. Echoes leak. Your memory is eroded, not gone—it sits under the carving the way water sits under ice, and every relic you've ever made has tapped at it. You've been remembering in fragments for years. You simply filed them as work."

The boy's echo shifts behind me. I think of every commission. Every grieving client. Every time I heard a dead voice say something that snagged on me like a burr—words that felt less like messages and more like *memos*. He'd been sending them through whoever would carry. Through me. To me.

"He's been leaving notes," I say.

"For nine years," says the emissary. "Dead boys make patient couriers. They have nothing else scheduled."

I stand up. My legs hold, which surprises me. "Then here's my next question, and I want a straight answer, which I understand may be difficult for your profession. If I speak the terms tonight—if I renew the peace—what happens to *me*?"

The silence goes on long enough that the ceiling-bone seems to lean in.

"The renewal consumes the witness," the emissary says finally. "Not violently. Worse than violently. The terms pass from your memory into the world itself—into every carver, every mourner, every bone that ever held an echo. You become distributed. Remembered everywhere, housed nowhere." Its coin-eyes fix on mine. "Or refuse, run, hide past the deadline, and the terms void, the graves open, and you'll still be alive to watch it, carrying a name no one left alive will have time to say."

"So it's dissolve into everyone, or watch everything end."

"Yes."

"And the Courts' plan?"

"To put you in front of their assembly tonight and *persuade* you. And the Guild's plan—your Mistress Corvane's plan—is to sell them the opportunity." It rises too, smooth as poured ink. "Which brings us to logistics. Two courts await you above. One choice below." It gestures at the hand-bone on the plinth. "Take it or leave it. It's yours either way. It was always yours. It's the only part of you they couldn't wipe, because it isn't memory—it's evidence. Blood doesn't forget; it just stops talking until someone picks it up."

I pick it up.

It's warm. Of course it's warm. It's been waiting a hundred years for a hand, and mine fits it the way a word fits a sentence it was always going to end.

Above us, muffled through a century of stone, the first snow begins to fall on the Hollow City—and with it come the whispers, thousands of them, restless as a crowd finding its seats.

"They're gathering," the echo-boy says. It's the first time I've heard his voice since the tunnel. It sounds like my voice used to. "Both of them. Everyone's coming to hear you speak."

"Then everyone," I say, tucking the bone against my chest, "should probably get comfortable."

The emissary holds the candle high, and for the first time since the signing, its hands are shaking.
