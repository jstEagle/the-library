## 9. Where the Dead Keep Court

They brought me to the Hall of Quiet Terms at dusk, which I suppose was meant as a courtesy. The dead have a sense of occasion the way a knife has a sense of humor—technically present, mostly about the edge.

The hall had been a countinghouse once, back when the city counted things other than its losses. Now the ledgers were gone and the vaulted ceiling was open to the sky, snow sifting down through the broken glass in long, patient columns, settling on the benches where the revenant court sat. They did not breathe. They did not fidget. There were nine of them, robed in the grey of unwritten pages, and their faces were the worst part—not decayed, not beautiful, just *unfinished*, like carvings someone had abandoned halfway through because the commission was too sad to finish.

At the far end of the hall, behind a table that had clearly been carried up from the guild at considerable expense, sat the assembled masters of the Hollow Guild. Vessa Corvane at their center, hands folded, wearing her negotiation face—the one she used when she intended to sell something and wanted you to feel grateful for the privilege.

I stood between them. The emissary stood beside me.

"You may sit," said the eldest of the revenants. Its voice sounded like a door opening in an empty house.

"I'd rather stand," I said. "It's easier to run."

A sound moved through the court that might have been laughter, if laughter were something you could do with dust.

"August Dravenmoor." The eldest pronounced my name carefully, the way you handle a relic you suspect is cursed. "Bearer of the Hollow. Keeper of terms written before your birth and after your death, which is a sentence we are aware makes no sense, and which we ask you to forgive."

"Oh, I've forgiven worse this week," I said. My voice held. I was almost proud of it. Inside, I was doing arithmetic: one of me, nine unfinished faces, six guild masters, one traitor-in-waiting whose traitorship I hadn't yet confirmed but whose negotiation face was already angled toward the revenants like a compass finding north. And one emissary, who had turned on its own court once already tonight, in the undercity, when it put itself between the court's hunters and my throat.

"The armistice," the eldest said. "You brokered it. You wrote its terms in your own blood, and you hid them where no one would think to look—in yourself. Then you let us take the memory of them from you, because a secret no one remembers cannot be tortured out of anyone." It paused. Snow landed on its unfinished cheek and did not melt. "We have come to ask you to remember."

"Not ask," said Master Ondrell, third from Vessa's left, a man whose wealth was exceeded only by his chin. "To *sell*. The Guild offers terms. Protection, position, a workshop of your own—"

"He has a workshop of his own," Vessa said mildly. "He's squandered it listening twice."

There it was. She'd known about the rule. She'd known about everything, probably, since the day they scraped me hollow and handed me a chisel. I looked at her and she met my eyes without flinching, and I understood that whatever guilt lived in her—and I had seen it, in the undercity, in the way she'd hesitated half a heartbeat before selling my location—it had lost to whatever else lived in her, which was older and hungrier and wore competence as a disguise.

"The terms," the eldest continued, ignoring the masters with the ease of long practice, "require renewal by a living witness every hundred years. Or they void. If they void, the war resumes. The living will lose. We say this without pride. We remember losing, and we remember winning, and neither tasted of anything."

"And if I renew them?" I asked.

"Then the winter continues. The border holds. The dead stay dead, the living stay ignorant, and you go back to carving grief into bone for people who will never know your name." The eldest tilted its unfinished head. "It is not nothing. But it is not much."

"And if I speak them aloud instead?"

The silence that followed had weight. Snow kept falling through it, unhurried, indifferent, the way snow does when it has outlasted every argument ever held beneath it.

"If you speak them," the eldest said slowly, "they enter every ear in this hall. Every carver who touches bone tonight will hear an echo of them. Every mourner who whispers to a grave will hear them whispered back. The terms become *common*. And common things cannot be owned, August Dravenmoor. Only obeyed—or broken."

Master Ondrell leaned forward. "Which is precisely why the Guild must control the speaking. A controlled disclosure—"

"There is no such thing," said the emissary.

It stepped forward then, out of the shadow beside me, and the whole hall adjusted around it the way water adjusts around a dropped stone. Up close, in honest light, I could see what I hadn't let myself see before: the emissary's face wasn't unfinished like the others'. It was *worn*, like a coin passed through too many hands. It had been someone. It might even have been someone I knew, a century ago, though the knowing was gone and I had stopped mourning my missing memories somewhere around the third betrayal.

"The court sent me to buy the page," the emissary said. "Then to buy the man. Then, when he refused both, to bury him in the undercity where the terms would rot unread until the war resumed and we could stop pretending we wanted peace." It turned its worn face toward the nine. "I am telling you this in front of the living because I am done pretending. I died in that war. I remember it. That is my entire qualification for this office, and it is more than any of yours."

"You are remanded," said the eldest coldly.

"I am *tired*," said the emissary, and something in the word made even the masters flinch.

Vessa rose. Of course she rose. Vessa always rose when the room was at its most dangerous, because danger was where the prices were best.

"Gentlemen. Ladies. Whatever the appropriate term is." She spread her hands. "The Guild's position is simple. The Bearer speaks the terms under our supervision, into our record, sealed in our vault, disclosed to each party in measured portions over a negotiated period—"

"Vessa." I said her name quietly, and to my surprise she stopped. "How long have you known what I was?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Snow came down between us, catching in her dark hair, and for one unguarded instant she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Since the night I signed the order," she said. "Twenty-two years ago. You were very polite about the whole thing. You thanked us." Her mouth twisted. "That was the worst part. You thanked us, and then you became so *good* at being nobody that I nearly convinced myself we'd done you a kindness."

"You're doing it again right now," I said. "Convincing yourself."

"I'm doing my job." And the shutters came down, and she turned back to the court. "The Guild's offer stands—"

"No," I said.

Every unfinished face turned to me.

"No," I said again, louder, and heard something underneath my own voice—something older, rougher, a voice that had argued and pleaded and died in a room like this once before. Bone remembers. I was standing inside the proof. "No sales. No portions. No vaults. I spent twenty-two years being a locked box, and I'll tell you what I learned in there: a secret doesn't get safer the longer you keep it. It gets *heavier*. And eventually the box breaks, and everything spills out anyway, except by then it's spoiled."

"August," Vessa said, warning in it now.

"You want the terms? Fine." I pulled the hand-bone from my coat—the one carved with my own blood, recovered from the undercity cache, warm against my chest all the way here like a second heart. "But I don't sell them, and I don't trade them, and I don't portion them out to whichever side pays best. I speak them the way they were written. Once. Aloud. To everyone. Because that's what a witness *is*—not a vault, not a merchant. A witness tells the truth and lets the truth do what it does."

The eldest of the revenants regarded me for a long, snow-filled moment.

"Bearer," it said. "Understand what you choose. Speak, and you unmake the arrangement that keeps you alive. The false self, the erased years—they are load-bearing. Remove them and—" It gestured vaguely at me, at all of me. "What remains may not remain."

"I know," I said. "I did the reading. Chapter seven, actually. Very grim."

The emissary made a sound that was definitely, unmistakably, a laugh.

"August Dravenmoor." The eldest rose, and the whole court rose with it, nine unfinished faces catching the falling snow. "The court accepts your terms. Speak."

And then Vessa moved.

I'll give her this—she was fast. Faster than Ondrell, faster than the guards, faster than anyone had a right to expect from a woman who filed paperwork for a living. She crossed the distance between the masters' table and me in six strides, and her hand closed on my wrist, the one holding the bone, and her grip was strong and desperate and shaking.

"Don't," she said. Low, urgent, meant for me alone. "August. Listen to me. The moment you speak, you're gone—I've read the old accounts, I know what happens to bearers who break seal. You'll scatter. There won't be enough of you left to *remember* being remembered. Twenty-two years I kept you safe—"

"Kept me *filed*," I said.

"—and I am asking you, as the only person in this hall who has ever given a damn whether you lived or died—"

"That's not true," I said gently. "The widow gives a damn. Her son gives a damn. Half the undercity hums with people who give a damn, and none of them ever sold me." I turned my wrist in her grip—not breaking free, just turning, until my hand could close over hers. "You gave a damn too, Vessa. That's why this hurts. But you gave a damn *and* kept the ledger open, and I can't afford both anymore."

Her eyes went bright. For a moment—one treacherous, human moment—I thought she'd let go.

Then she wrenched my arm back and shouted, "Take him! The terms die with the bone!" and the guild guards surged forward, and the emissary stepped into their path like a wall deciding to exist, and the hall erupted into motion and noise and snow.

I didn't fight. Fighting was a delay, and delays were how secrets got bought back.

I raised the hand-bone above my head, into the falling snow, into the light of whatever was left of the evening, and I began to speak.

The first words came out in my voice. By the third sentence, they didn't. They came out in the voice from the relics, the arguing voice, the pleading voice, the voice that had died last of all—and the words themselves were simple, terribly simple, the kind of terms that could only have been written by someone young and desperate and certain that fairness was possible:

*Let the border be drawn where the grief is thickest. Let neither side hold more than it can mourn. Let the names of the fallen be spoken yearly, by the living to the dead and the dead to the living, in equal measure, until neither side can tell which is which.*

The snow thickened. The guards slowed. The revenants bowed their unfinished heads, one by one, like a field of grey flowers closing at dusk. Vessa's grip slipped from my wrist—not released, I think, so much as forgotten—and somewhere beyond the broken ceiling, past the snow, past the dark, something vast and frozen and a hundred years old began, very quietly, to crack.

I spoke the fourth term. The fifth. My hands were going translucent at the edges, and I found, absurdly, that I wasn't afraid. I was *lighter*. Every word took weight off me and put it into the world, into the snow, into the ears of everyone in that hall and everyone who would ever hear the echo of this echo, forever.

The last term was the hardest. Not because it was long.

Because it was my name.

I opened my mouth to speak it—

* * *

—and the snow came down through the broken roof like a curtain falling, and the hall went white, and white, and whiter still, and somewhere inside all that softness a voice that was no longer quite mine finished the sentence it had started a hundred years ago.

The winter listened.

And then, all across the Hollow City, the ice began to answer.
