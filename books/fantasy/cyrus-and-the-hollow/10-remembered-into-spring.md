## 10. Remembered Into Spring

I had always imagined that dying would feel like the ledger closing—ink drying, the page turned, the transaction complete. A tidy thing. A filed thing.

It did not feel tidy.

It felt like being read aloud.

The words were still in my mouth. *The terms of the armistice, witnessed in blood and bone, renewed each winter by the memory of the one who brokered them.* I had spoken them standing in the snow-choked hall of the revenant court, with Vessa's betrayal still warm in the air beside me and the emissary's court lying in glittering ruin at its feet, and the moment the last syllable left me, the false self the guild had built—August Dravenmoor, bone-carver, keeper of ledgers, man of careful invisibility—began, quite politely, to come apart.

I want to say I was afraid. I was, at first. Fear is the body's oldest habit, and mine had been manufactured along with the rest of me. But the unmaking did not hurt. It was more like setting down a ledger I had carried so long I'd forgotten its weight. The name *August* peeled away first. I felt it lift from me like a label from an old jar, and beneath it—nothing. Not darkness. Not void. Something worse and better than either.

Possibility.

The hall was full of people watching me dissolve, and I would like to report that they wept. Some did. Vessa did not, though her face did something I had never seen it do in eleven years of employment: it broke. The emissary, half-unraveled itself from turning on its own court, inclined what remained of its head—a courtesy, a salute, an apology, all three.

"You could have taken the power," it had said, minutes before. "Both courts offered. You could have been the hinge of the world."

"I was a ledger-keeper," I told it. "I know what happens to things that get written down twice. They stop meaning anything."

And then I spoke the truth, because it was the only thing I had ever owned outright, and I would not let them have it.

Now the snow was burying the hall, and I was burying myself, and somewhere in the middle of the two burials I stopped being a man and became a sentence.

Here is what it is like to scatter into shared memory: every carver in the city felt me pass through their hands. I know this because I felt them feeling me. The old woman in the Saltmarket who carves memorial spoons—she paused mid-stroke, her knife trembling, and heard a voice that was not a voice say *the war stays buried, the terms are renewed, remember the one who spoke.* The apprentice in the undercity who had been carving a name into a wall since the fighting stopped—he finished it, and it was my name, though he had never learned it, though I had barely learned it myself. Every mourner holding a relic to their ear heard, faintly, beneath their dead, a man saying the same words, and the words were not a spell and not a command. They were a witness statement. That was all I had ever been. That was enough.

The dead heard it too. That was the point. The armistice required a living witness to renew it, and I had been the only one—erased, remade, kept like a coin in a vault, spending my days filing impossible things because I could not file myself. Both courts had thought a witness who couldn't remember was a witness who couldn't testify. They were wrong in the way that powerful people are always wrong: they mistook silence for absence.

I remembered everything as I came apart. That was the cruel joke of it, the guild's last gift to me: the unmaking restored me completely, all at once, the whole of the man I had been before they scraped me hollow. I remembered the war's last day. I remembered sitting in a ruined chapel with representatives of both courts, my hands bleeding onto the page because I had insisted the terms be written in the broker's blood—*so no one can claim they weren't paid for.* I remembered choosing erasure myself, in the end, because a secret that lives in a man's head is a hostage, and I refused to be taken twice.

I remembered my name.

I won't write it here. Not because it's gone—it isn't, it's everywhere now, it's in the grain of every carving in the city—but because some things should be heard, not read. I spent a century as a man who existed only in files and ledgers. I would rather be a sound.

The winter broke that night.

I felt it happen, or I *was* it happening—the distinction had stopped mattering. The snow over the hall turned soft and heavy and then, at some hour no clock admitted to, it turned to rain, and the rain turned the city's frozen gutters to music, and the dead, who had been restless for weeks, went quiet in the way of the finally-heard. Not silenced. Heard. There is a difference, and the whole war had been fought over the difference, and I had spent my whole second life failing to notice it in my own guild's ledgers: we called them *transactions*, every grief, every echo, every last word we sold. We never once called them what they were. Conversations. Interrupted ones.

I am getting ahead of myself. It is difficult not to, when you are everywhere at once. Let me return to the hall, to the last moment I had edges.

Vessa came to me as I thinned into the snowfall. She knelt—Vessa Corvane, kneeling, the guild masters would have struck her from the rolls—and she said, "I was going to sell you. You know that."

"I'd have filed it under *inevitable*," I said. My voice was already an echo of an echo.

"Why did you speak it? You could have run. You could have let them fight over you for another century."

"Because I finally read my own entry," I said. "And I didn't like what the ledger implied."

She laughed. It cracked in the middle. She put her hand where my hand had been and closed it on snow, and I felt that too—felt the cold of her palm, felt the grief she'd been carrying for eleven years under all that competence, grief for a man she had helped make and helped imprison and, in her way, helped survive. I forgave her. It cost me nothing, which is the only reliable exchange rate forgiveness has ever had.

Then the hall was empty of me, and the city was full of me, and spring came up through the stones like a rumor finally confirmed.

* * *

Here is what remains of me, as best I can account for it, and I was always a thorough accountant:

I am in the ledgers. Not the guild's—the guild's books burned that night, Vessa's doing, the last honest act of a dishonest institution. I am in the ledgers people keep now, the plain ones, the ones carvers started keeping after: not *what was sold*, but *what was said*. A widow's son's last words, written down because his mother wanted them written down, not because anyone would buy them. A soldier's joke, recorded by his brother, who laughed while he wrote it and cried after. The terms of the armistice, copied in a hundred hands and read aloud each winter on the night the snow first falls, so that no single witness need ever again be carried like a coin.

I am in the bone. Every carver in the city will tell you the same thing: since that night, the relics speak more freely. The dead are not louder—they were always loud enough. They are *answered*. Someone listens on the other end, and when a carver lifts a relic and hears a last word, there is a faint second voice beneath it, patient, unhurried, saying *go on* and *I have him* and *you are not alone in the listening.*

I am not a man. I want to be honest about that, because I spent a century being an honest man and it would be a poor final entry to fudge the accounts. I have no hands. I cannot carve. I miss it—I miss it the way you miss an argument you were having in a dream, the shape of it without the substance. But I am remembered, and it turns out that being remembered is not the poor cousin of being alive. It is a different country, with different weather, and the weather here is spring.

* * *

The widow Maren Halloway came to her workbench on the first morning the thawwater ran in the streets, and she carved a name into plain wood.

Not relic-bone. Not guild-stock. Plain pine, bought from a lumberyard, the kind of wood that holds no echoes, hears no dead, keeps no secrets. She had thought about this for weeks, since the night the sky broke open over the revenant court and every carving in her shop had spoken at once, and the voice beneath all the voices had said her name—*Maren*—in a tone of such ordinary gratitude that she had sat down on the floor among her spoons and wept until dawn.

He had heard her son. That was the thing she kept returning to. Years ago, in a dim shop that smelled of dust and bone oil, a careful gray-eyed man had carved a keepsake for her and told her, gently, what her boy's last words had been, and then he had looked at her with something she had mistaken at the time for professional detachment. It was not. It was a man listening to grief he was not permitted to feel, filing it away because he had nothing else to file himself into. She had gone home that night and slept well for the first time in a year, and she had never once thought to ask the carver what *he* carried. Who listens to the listener?

Now everyone did. That was the strange arithmetic of it. The city remembered him the way a river remembers rain—not as an event, but as a level it now permanently held. Children carved his name into fence posts without knowing why. The guild was gone, but in its shell someone had opened a school where they taught carving the old way, the honest way, and the first thing they taught was not the knife-work. It was the listening. And every student, on their first day, was told the story of the Hollow Bearer, who had been hollowed and filled and hollowed again, and who in the end chose to be a voice rather than a vault.

Maren set her knife to the pine and cut the letters slow and clean. Not a relic. A marker. A plain, human, mortal thing, the kind you make for someone who is simply, finally, allowed to be gone.

When she finished, she ran her thumb along the grooves, and—because she was a woman who had spent a year talking to a carved keepsake and saw no reason to stop now—she said, out loud, to her empty shop:

"There. Now you're written down somewhere that can't be sold."

The pine held no echoes. Plain wood never does.

But somewhere, faintly, from no direction the room could name—from the thawwater in the gutters, from the grain of the bench, from the space just behind her own hearing—a voice that was not a voice said:

*I know. I read it.*

And then, lighter, wry as a man closing a ledger he is glad to be done with:

*Best entry in the book.*

Outside, the snowmelt ran silver down the Hollow City's streets, and in every shop, in every house, in every hand that held a knife over wood or bone, the listening went on—and was, for the first time in a hundred years, answered.
