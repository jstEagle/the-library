## 1. Charms and Small Lies

The pigeons of Verrin's Grain Market woke before the bells did.

Sable knew this because they told him so — not in words exactly, but in the grey murmur of their waking, the soft complaint of feathers and hunger that drifted through his shutters like smoke. *Cold. Cold. The man with the crumbs is late. Cold.* By the time the dawn bell rang out from the Temple of the Ninefold Sun, he had already dressed, already counted out yesterday's bread into a twist of cloth, and was descending the narrow stair to his shop with the particular weariness of a man about to spend a morning lying to everyone he met.

He had gotten good at it. Five years good.

The shop itself was barely a shop at all — a lean-to of timber and patched glass wedged between a cooper's yard and a chandler who burned tallow so cheap it smelled like a tannery. Sable Reyes, Charm-Seller, purveyor of luck-knots and hearth-blessings and small woven things that promised nothing and delivered less. That was what the sign said, anyway, the letters he'd painted himself on his second day in Verrin, hands shaking, ears listening for boots. *S. REYES — CHARMS & CURES — NO TASK TOO SMALL.*

No task too small. It was almost funny. His whole life was no task too small: a whispered word to a rat to spare the grain sacks; a bargain struck with a pigeon flock to keep the eaves of the widow Anselma's house clear of nesting filth; a murmured agreement with the market cats that they would not scream at night outside the birthing rooms on Cooper's Lane. Small mercies. Invisible ones. Each one paid for in crumbs and fish heads and patience, each one deniable, each one a lie wrapped inside a charm sold to someone who believed in nothing but luck.

He unlocked the door, set out his wares — knots of red thread, river-stones painted with suns that would fade by spring, little bags of salt and dried lavender that did nothing whatsoever except smell nice — and stood a moment in the doorway breathing the market air.

Verrin in early winter smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and the iron tang of the canal. Snow had come three days early this year, dusting the rooftops white and then going grey with soot, and it had not melted since. The old men at the fountain said it was a bad omen. The priests said the Ninefold Sun was testing them. The pigeons said only *cold, cold*, because pigeons were not philosophers.

"Morning, Master Reyes!" called Tam the cooper's boy, hauling a barrow of staves through the slush. "Any luck left today, or did you sell it all to the ferryman?"

"I keep a reserve," Sable called back, with the easy grin that was his armor. "But it'll cost you double if you keep splashing my doorstep."

Laughter, a thrown snowball he dodged with theatrical offense, and then the market swallowed him and he was moving among it, greeting, joking, selling. This was the performance he had perfected: the charming, slightly shabby charm-seller who winked at pretty wives and let children steal dried currants from his tray and always, always had time for a story. Nobody suspected a man who made them laugh. Suspicion required silence, and Sable Reyes never stopped talking.

It was the beasts who talked too much, if anything.

*Cold,* said the pigeons under the eaves, tracking him with orange eyes. *Crumbs? Crumbs?*

"After midday," he murmured, so low it was barely a movement of lips, pitched beneath the market noise. "And you'll earn them. The grain carts come up Tanner's Row at noon — scatter when I pass, make a fuss, draw the tax-man's eye north while the Miller's boy rolls his barrels through the gate. As agreed."

*Crumbs,* they agreed, which was pigeon for *yes.*

A rat beneath the chandler's step said, in its thin scraping way, *the cat by the canal has taken three of my children,* and Sable crouched to fuss with his bootlaces and answered, *I'll speak to her. No more young ones. She keeps her end, your family stays off her street.* Bargains within bargains. A web of tiny contracts holding up a corner of the world, all of it invisible, all of it illegal, all of it his alone.

Because that was the truth under the paint and the patter: the charms were nothing. He was the charm. He had been born able to hear what beasts said and to answer, and in Verrin — in all of the Nine Provinces, since the Edict and the burnings before it — that gift was not a gift. It was a pyre waiting for kindling.

His mother had taught him the first rule before she taught him his letters: *Never let them see you listen.* Not to the dog in the yard. Not to the horses in the lane. Never look at an animal when it speaks. Never answer where eyes can catch you answering. She had died in a province far south of here, in a fire, officially an accident, and Sable had run north until he found a city big enough to be anonymous, and he had built himself a mask out of jokes and red thread, and for five years it had held.

Mostly, he could almost forget. Mostly the loneliness stayed down where he kept it, packed tight as ballast.

Then there were mornings like this one, when a woman bought a hearth-charm against mice and thanked him warmly for magic she thought was superstition, while the actual mouse behind her wainscoting was telling him her floorboards were rotten and would give way by thaw unless someone fixed them — and Sable smiled, took her copper, and said nothing, and felt the silence go through him like a nail.

* * *

Midday came, and with it the pigeons' riot on Tanner's Row, the tax-man's curses, the Miller's boy's grinning salute from the shadows — Sable had a network now, five years deep, of small creatures and small favors, a shadow-guild of rats and sparrows that no Inquisitor had ever thought to look for — and after it came Wren.

She arrived the way weather arrives: suddenly, and changing everything.

She was perhaps nineteen, courier-built, with wind-chapped cheeks and a hound limping at her side on a rope lead. Sable knew her by sight the way he knew half the market — the girl who ran the northern passes for the letter-house, quick-tongued, quicker-striding, the sort who ate standing up. He'd sold her a travel-charm once, a knotted cord against lost roads, and she'd laughed and said her feet were the only charm she needed, but she'd bought it anyway, for her mother's sake. He had liked her instantly and guardedly, the way he liked everyone.

Today she came straight to his stall and set both hands flat on it, and her face stopped his patter in his throat.

"My hound," she said. "Bramble. He's dying."

Behind her, the hound lowered itself carefully onto the cold cobbles, the way old dogs do when lying down has become a negotiation. Grey about the muzzle. Deep-chested. Eyes gone cloudy at the edges but still, Sable saw with a lurch, still turned toward him with terrible attention.

"He's twelve," Wren went on, fast, the words of someone who had rehearsed and hated rehearsing. "Twelve years he's carried my packs over the passes, and now the vets say it's his heart, or his gut, something they can't cut at. And he won't eat, and he cries at night, and—" Her jaw worked. "They say he can't tell me where it hurts. They say he doesn't understand what's happening to him. And I keep thinking, somebody should tell him. Somebody should explain. You hear stories, right? About people who can—"

*Stop,* Sable thought, with a cold clarity that surprised even him. *Walk away. Now.*

"Wren." He kept the grin on, kept it light, leaned on the stall like a man amused. "You've been listening to tavern-talk. There are no beast-speakers. There haven't been for a generation — the crown made sure of that, and good riddance, if you ask me. Half those stories were frauds taking coin off grieving folk, and the other half got the fire they deserved." He heard his own voice say it, smooth and practiced, and hated every syllable, and kept saying them. "What I sell is thread and salt and hope. Hope's worth the copper. The rest is just knotwork."

Something flickered across her face — disappointment, and under it a stubbornness he recognized, because he wore the same thing every day.

"They say there was a speaker in Copperlane last month," she said quietly. "Before the Inquisitor came. They say the man begged."

"They say a lot of things." Sable began gathering his tray, the universal gesture of a closing merchant. "Look — I'm sorry about your dog. Truly. Old dogs are a debt we all owe someday. But I can't help you, and neither can anyone else you're likely to find, and the asking around after speakers isn't safe these days, if you take my meaning. The new Inquisitor's men don't care whether the stories are true. They care who tells them."

That much, at least, was honest.

Wren looked at him a long moment. Behind her, Bramble shifted his weight off a hip and sighed, a long rattling breath, and his clouded eyes never left Sable's face.

*Young one,* the hound said. His voice was deep and slow, like stones settling in a riverbed. *Tell her I am not afraid. Tell her the pain is a country I am traveling out of. Tell her she was the whole of my work, and I was glad of it.*

Sable's hands went very still on the tray.

Every instinct he owned screamed at him. *Don't listen. Don't answer. Look away.* Five years of rules, his mother's voice under all of them. But the hound was dying, and dying creatures spoke plainly, without guile, and their words went into him like rain into cracked ground, no matter how he sealed himself.

*Tell her yourself,* he answered, before he could stop it, barely a breath, lips hardly moving. *You have the tongue for it. I'm just a charm-seller.*

*She does not have our ear,* Bramble said, with the vast patience of the old. *Only you. Oathkeeper's blood knows how to carry a message.*

The world tilted. Sable kept his face pleasant and his grip steady and felt sweat start cold along his spine. *You're mistaken, friend. Just a seller of knots and nonsense.*

The hound held his gaze — a long, cloudy, unfooled gaze — and said nothing more, which was somehow worse.

"—Master Reyes?" Wren was frowning at him. "You've gone strange. Are you ill?"

"Tired," he managed. "Long market. Look — take this." He pressed a sachet of lavender and willow-bark into her hand, the closest thing to a pain-ease he dared sell openly. "For his comfort. Steep it warm. It won't save him, but it may ease the nights." *It won't tell him anything either,* he added silently, to the hound. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

Wren took it slowly. "Thank you," she said, and it wasn't gracious; it was wounded, and watchful, and he understood that he had just become, in her ledger, a man who had refused a dying creature its last kindness. She turned, gathered Bramble's lead gently, and walked back into the market crowd, and the hound looked back over his shoulder at Sable once — only once — before the bodies closed between them.

Sable stood in the cold with his half-packed tray and felt the loneliness rise up through the ballast like floodwater.

This was the shape of it, then. This was the life. A girl asks him for the smallest mercy in the world — *tell him he's understood* — and he says no. Because saying yes means the fire. Because his mother burned. Because one voice, one witnessed word, and everything unravels: the shop, the network, the rats and pigeons and cats who trusted him, the fragile anonymous architecture of five years. Because survival was the whole of his religion and he had never once found the courage to be a martyr.

He was good at the lies. He was so tired of being good at them.

* * *

He closed early. He told himself it was the cold.

By dusk the snow had thickened, coming down in fat silent flakes that muffled the city's edges, and Sable sat alone above his shop with a candle and a cup of cheap wine and the day replaying behind his eyes. Bramble's voice. *Oathkeeper's blood.* Where had a passing hound learned that name? The order was ash and rumor; even the priests spoke of the Oathkeepers only to condemn them. Yet the old dog had said it like a diagnosis, plain as *grey-muzzled* or *twelve years.*

Perhaps it was nothing. Beasts repeated what they overheard, fragments, market gossip filtered through stable-yard and kennel. Some child's nursemaid tale. It meant nothing.

It meant nothing, he told the candle again, and the candle did not believe him either.

Below, through the floorboards, he heard the market cats moving along the walls, keeping out of the snow. *Cold,* they said, in their elegant indifferent way. *Cold, and the two-legs who owes us a debt sits alone and unhappy. Shall we sit on his roof and judge him?*

"Go bother the chandler," Sable muttered aloud, forgetting for one dangerous second that he didn't dare be heard — and then caught himself, laughing softly at his own carelessness, alone as he was. Alone as he would always be. That was the trade. That had always been the trade: every voice he hid cost him a little more of his own, until some days he wondered whether the silence around his gift was eating outward, whether one day there'd be nothing left of Sable Reyes but a grin and a tray of red thread.

He was reaching to snuff the candle when he heard the knock.

Three raps, hesitant, at the shop door below. Then — unmistakable, threading up through the snow-silence — the deep, rattling, patient breath of an old hound, and a girl's low voice saying his name.

"Master Reyes. Please. I know it's late. Please open the door."

Sable sat very still, hand hovering over the flame, and listened to the snow fall on Verrin, and did not move — knowing even then, with the certainty of a man watching the first snow of a winter that will not end, that whatever he decided in the next ten breaths would decide everything after.

Down below, Bramble sighed, and waited with him.
