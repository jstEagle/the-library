## 2. The Proclamation

The morning the criers came, Sable Reyes was selling luck to a woman who did not believe in it, which was his favorite kind of customer.

"Three coppers for the charm," he said, holding it up so the light could do its work. It was a knot of braided horsehair and river-reed, sealed with beeswax and threaded on a leather cord, and it was worth exactly nothing. That was what everyone in Copper Row believed, and Sable had spent five years making sure they went on believing it. A charm-seller's wares were permitted to be useless. Useless was safe. "Tie it to your doorpost and quarrels will pass around your house like water around a stone."

The woman — a broad-shouldered dyer with ink stains up both forearms and suspicion all over her face — turned the charm over twice. "My sister bought one of these off you last spring. Her roof still leaked."

"Your sister's roof leaked because her roof is old," Sable said mildly. "The charm kept her marriage from leaking. Ask her which she'd rather have mended."

That got a laugh out of her, or half of one, bitten off quickly, the way people in Verrin bit off their laughs these days. The winter had been long even by Verrin's standards, and this was a city that measured its winters in grievances. Five years now the cold had come early and left late, five years of ice-thickened harbors and granaries opened at rationing prices, and the mood of the city had gone the way of a well rope: frayed, and fraying somewhere near the middle where no one could reach it.

She bought the charm. Sable made change from a box that had seen better decades, and while she counted her coppers he let his eyes move over the market the way he always did — not browsing, *listening*. Copper Row on a market morning was a riot of small voices most humans never thought to hear. Pigeons muttering territorial grievances from the eaves of the chandler's shop. A dog tied outside the tanner's, complaining with patient dignity about being left. Rats beneath the grain-merchant's stalls, conducting their brisk and ruthless economy in squeaks and whisker-signals, and — this was the part no one ever suspected — gossip. Rats were terrible gossips. They knew which merchant watered his flour, which guard took bribes, which nobleman's household was quietly starving for lack of funds.

Sable had built his whole quiet life on rat gossip and pigeon grudges. He'd learned the hard way that the safest use of his gift was the smallest: a whispered bargain here, a favor traded there. The pigeons kept watch on Copper Row for crumbs; if soldiers came down one end of the street, three birds would lift off at once and he'd know before the boots hit the corner. The rats told him who in the crowd carried sickness, who carried knives, who carried warrants. In exchange he fed them, defended their nests from boys with sticks when he could do it without looking mad, and never, ever spoke to them where a human eye might catch him doing it.

Five years of small bargains. Five years of harmless charms and careful lies. It had worked because it was boring.

He was tying the dyer's charm into a scrap of cloth when the bells started.

Not the market bell. Not the alarm bell either, though his body reacted to it as if it were, spine going cold under his coat before his mind caught up. This was the deep, slow tolling of the temple bells in Oldsquare, rung only for proclamations of state — coronations, declarations of war, deaths of kings. The last time Sable had heard them was the old king's funeral, four years back.

The market did not stop all at once; it stopped in ripples, stall by stall, conversation dying in patches like candles snuffed down a table. Heads turned east toward Oldsquare. Somewhere behind the fishmonger's row, a baby began to cry and was hastily hushed.

And then, threading through the hush, came the sound Sable would remember for the rest of his life, though he didn't yet know it: footsteps. Many boots, in unison, and above them a voice trained to carry across courtyards.

"—by order of His Majesty Aldous the Second, and by decree of the High Chancery—"

The criers came into Copper Row in threes, flanked by soldiers in the crown's slate-grey, and Sable understood, in the first breath, everything he needed to understand, because he had grown up on stories that began exactly this way.

"—that the realm endures a winter unnatural and prolonged, sent by powers old and forbidden. Let every subject of the crown know: the gift of beast-speech, and all arts of the old covenant, are hereby declared heresy against realm and heaven. Any soul possessing such gifts shall surrender themselves to the Chancery within nine days' time. Any soul *harboring* such a one—" the crier's voice lifted, practiced, almost musical, "—shall be judged to share the crime. And any who conceal, abet, or refuse shall face the flame."

The words fell into the street the way snow falls — soft, individual, unhurried, and utterly covering. Sable stood very still behind his little stall with its ribbons and knots and worthless blessed things, and felt the first flakes settle on him one by one.

*Any soul possessing such gifts.* Nine days. Surrender, or the flame.

They had known, of course. He had known, in some cellar of himself, that this day was stored somewhere like a knife in a drawer. You did not hide a thing for five years without understanding that hiding was not the same as being safe. But knowledge kept in a cellar stays cold and abstract, and here it was now, walking down Copper Row in slate-grey coats, reading itself aloud in a pleasant carrying voice.

*Unnatural winter.* He almost laughed, and the laugh would have been bitter enough to cut his own mouth. If they only knew how unnatural — how grief-stricken, how *orphaned* — the cold really was. But he didn't know why the winter had come. He only knew that whatever the beasts felt about it ran deeper than weather. Even the rats had stopped joking about the cold two winters past. There was something wrong under the season, some wound, and now the crown had decided the wound was *him*, and people like him, and the city was going to be grateful for the explanation.

Because that was the thing about fear, Sable thought, watching the faces turn along both sides of the street. Fear was heavy, and people wanted to put it down somewhere.

He watched it happen. He watched a porter near the front shift his weight, jaw working, eyes moving from the soldiers to his neighbors and back — calculating. He watched the fishmonger's wife go still in a new way, the way of a woman filing something away. He watched old Tam Hessler, who had drunk with Sable a hundred times, who owed him eleven coppers and had never once mentioned it, look at Sable's stall for a heartbeat too long, then away, then — the worst part — *back again*, with an expression Sable recognized from the inside: the arithmetic of a man discovering that someone he knew might be worth something dead or chained.

The proclamation ended. The criers folded their papers with a snap and moved on toward Tanner's Way, and the soldiers followed, and the market breathed again — but it came back changed. The noise resumed at half its former volume, conversations dropping whenever anyone passed, smiles arriving late and leaving early. The whole street had pulled its coat tighter.

"Well," said the dyer, taking her charm off the counter and then pausing, awkward. She looked at it, then at Sable, and he saw her decide — visibly, he watched her decide — that a charm bought from Sable Reyes was perhaps a charm she ought not be seen holding. She stuffed it in her basket beneath the bread instead of wearing it, muttered something about thread she needed elsewhere, and left faster than she'd come.

Sable began packing his stall.

His hands were steady, which surprised him, and he catalogued the steadiness the way he catalogued everything now: evidence, testimony, a fact to work with. Steady hands meant he had hours yet before panic made him stupid. He rolled the cloth, boxed the charms, stacked the ribbons. Around him the market packed up early too, hundreds of people performing the ordinary motions of an ordinary day with the extraordinary care of people who had just been told the ground had rules they hadn't known.

A pigeon landed on the awning frame above him. Grey hen, missing a toe, one of his regulars.

*"Bells,"* she said, in that flat compressed way pigeons had, all meaning and no music. *"Bad bells. Soldiers at the north gate too. And Chancery men. Chancery men with lists."*

Lists. His hands did not stop moving, but something behind them went very cold and very quiet, the way the forest goes quiet before the does break cover. Lists meant names already gathered. Lists meant this hunt had begun before today, that the proclamation was not the opening move but the announcement of one already underway.

*"Thank you,"* he murmured, barely breathing it, and scattered a handful of cracked corn where she could reach it without seeming to. *"Stay high. Tell me if grey coats come down this street again."*

*"Grey coats everywhere now,"* she said gloomily, and flew off.

Everywhere now. He finished packing, shouldered his baskets, and made himself walk — not hurry, *walk* — up Copper Row toward his shop, and as he walked he listened with everything he had, because he could not help it, because the whole street was speaking whether it meant to or not.

The dogs were the worst. Dogs heard the tone underneath human words, always had, and every yard he passed held one, and every one of them was saying the same thing in different registers of distress. *"Ours is frightened. Ours smells frightened. Everyone smells frightened. Something is coming for the smelling-of."* They couldn't parse the proclamation but they could parse the fear in it, and the fear was spreading through the city's dogs like a fever through a flock. By nightfall every dog in Verrin would be barking at shadows, and their owners would say the beasts had gone strange, and it would be entered somewhere as further evidence.

At the mouth of Cooper's Lane, two women stood close together, and Sable caught the tail of their talk in passing.

"—my cousin in Harlow Ford, she says they took the miller's boy. Just a boy. Said he was heard whistling the starlings down out of the orchard—"

"—nine days, though. Nine days to get yourself over the border—"

"—and who takes him in? Border lords have to post their own lists now. No, you want my advice, you keep your head down and your mouth shut and you thank heaven you're plain and ordinary—"

Keep your head down. Plain and ordinary. Sable had written those rules himself, had lived by them so long they'd worn grooves in him like a cart path. And here was the thing he could not say to anyone: the plan was good. The plan had worked for five years. If he went home, shuttered the shop, sold nothing but ribbons until spring, charmed nobody, spoke to nothing, gave the rats no reason to visit and the pigeons none to perch — the odds were decent. Better than decent. The Chancery worked from lists and informants, and he had spent half a decade ensuring there was nothing to list and no one informed. He was a charm-seller. A fraud. A harmless, slightly shabby vendor of worthless knickknacks whom the neighborhood tolerated because he extended credit and remembered birthdays.

Except.

Except there was a girl named Wren who had come to his stall a month ago with a dying hound in her arms and a question in her mouth, and he had said no. He had said no kindly, he had said no gently, he had lied to her beautifully — *the vets on Chandler Street are good, truly, sometimes beasts just know when their time has come* — and she had gone away with her dying dog and her unanswered question, and Sable had thought that was the end of it.

But she had looked back. Twice, going down the street, she had looked back. And she had come back once more since, just to buy a ribbon, just to linger, just to ask in an offhand way whether he'd ever — and he had deflected again, smoother the second time, and again she had left looking back.

She knew something. Not everything, maybe not anything provable — but the Chancery did not need proof, not on day one of a hunt. The Chancery needed suspicion, and a name on a list, and a girl with a dying dog and a neighbor who refused her, and suddenly the arithmetic of Copper Row changed. Eleven coppers, Tam Hessler's eyes, the fishmonger's wife filing something away. Every small kindness Sable had done in five years had been done publicly, witnessed, remembered — and every one of those memories had just been converted, by a single sheet of paper read aloud in a carrying voice, from goodwill into ammunition.

He reached his shop — a narrow slot of a building between a cooper's yard and a house that leaned as if listening — and let himself in, and shot the bolt, and stood in the dimness with his back against the door and his baskets still on his shoulder.

The shop smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and old wood. His shelves held his stock: charms, knots, ribbons, bundles of dried herbs, little carved animals he whittled in the evenings. On the sill by the back window, in a clay pot, grew a straggly mint plant that the sparrows had a standing arrangement about. His whole life, arranged on three shelves. He had made it small on purpose. Small things don't draw the eye.

Through the wall, muffled, he could hear the cooper's apprentices talking, and he caught fragments — *nine days... my uncle says the gifted can smell each other... I heard they burned three in Kellin's Reach already, before the decree even—*

Kellin's Reach. Before the decree. So the fires had started first and the law had hurried to catch up, which was how these things usually went.

Sable set down the baskets at last. He unpacked slowly, mechanically, giving his body work to do while the rest of him turned the problem over, and it was during the unpacking that he noticed the sparrow.

It was on the mint pot, a cock bird with a bent tail feather, and it was saying his name.

Not *saying* it — sparrows didn't have the words — but calling the pattern of it, insistent, urgent, hopping wing-to-windowsill, and when Sable crossed the room the bird flew a short loop toward the back wall and returned. Toward the back wall. Where the loose board was. Where he'd cached, over five careful years, a purse of coin, a false-bottomed satchel, papers in a dead man's name, and the other things a cautious man keeps for a day he prays stays hypothetical.

The sparrow had been fed through that window all winter. The sparrows watched everything; that was their whole nature. And they had watched grey coats moving through the city all morning, posting papers at every corner, and somewhere in that small fierce skull a conclusion had formed about what papers and grey coats meant, and whose window they were likely to arrive at.

They know, Sable thought. The beasts know before I do. They've known for days — the birds changing their roosts, the rats running new routes, the dogs feverish since midweek. The city's animals had been whispering about something wrong for a week, and he had filed it under winter-scaries, foxes in the warrens, ordinary animal nerves. He had, for the first time in five years, failed to listen properly, because listening would have meant hearing it early, and hearing it early would have meant acting, and acting meant admitting the quiet was over.

He sat down slowly on his stool among his worthless beautiful charms, and the sparrow settled on the mint pot and puffed itself small and waited, with the patience of a creature that has delivered its message and considers the matter closed.

Nine days. The word sat in the room with him like a third person.

Dread, Sable discovered, does not arrive like a hammer. It arrives like the first snow: one flake, noted idly; another, prettier than the first; then a whitening of the doorstep; then, between one glance and the next, the whole world changed color and the roads beginning to fill. You cannot point to the moment it became serious. You only look up one morning and see that it covers everything, and that it has been falling all night, and that it will not stop falling because you have finally bothered to notice.

Outside, the light was already going grey and low, though it was barely afternoon. Snow weather. Actual snow, perhaps, or only the sky rehearsing. Down in the street someone's shutters banged, and a dog began to bark, and then another, and then half the dogs in the quarter were barking at nothing at all, a ragged chorus of fear with no center and no end.

Sable looked at the loose board in the back wall for a long moment.

Then he got up, took the mint pot off the sill, set it on the floor for the sparrow, and went to fetch his other life out of the wall — because the smart move, the only move, the move he'd rehearsed in his head a hundred drowsing times, was to be gone from Verrin before the ninth day dawned.

He had the board half-loosed when the knock came.

Three soft raps. Familiar rhythm. He knew it before he knew it — knew the light, hesitant cadence of it, the way you know a song you wish you'd never learned.

Wren.

* * *

He stood motionless, hand still on the board, and made himself breathe. Through the gap between shutter and frame he could see her, small and sharp-faced in the failing light, a bundle in her arms wrapped in a man's coat. Not the hound — the hound, if the hound still lived, was too big to carry like that. Something else. Her eyes went up to the shutter seam and he had the sudden crawling certainty that she knew he was standing there, three feet away, not breathing.

"Sable." Her voice, pitched low for a street that might be listening. "I know you're in there. Your lamp's lit."

It wasn't. He checked, absurdly, before he could stop himself. It wasn't.

Another knock. Softer. "Please. It's not — I'm not here about me." A pause, and then, quieter, in a voice that had been crying recently and was done pretending otherwise: "I need you to talk to my dog."

Behind him, in the dark of the shuttered shop, the sparrow stirred on the mint pot and said, softly, the flat compressed syllables of its kind:

*"Bad. Bad coming. Bad at the door."*

Sable closed his eyes.

Nine days, the decree had given the city. Looking at the shape of Wren against the grey, listening to the dogs barking down the darkening street, he understood with a cold settling certainty that he did not have nine days. He had tonight.
