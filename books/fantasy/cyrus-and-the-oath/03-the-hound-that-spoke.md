## 3. The Hound That Spoke

Wren had walked past Sable's shop eleven times in three days before she worked up the nerve to stop.

She counted them because counting was what she did when she was afraid. Eleven passes through the lamplight spilling from his window, eleven glimpses of the charm-seller bent over his workbench, threading river-glass and knotted twine into things the market wives swore by. Eleven small cowardices. On the twelfth, her boots simply stopped moving, and there she was, standing in the doorway of Reyes Charms & Curios with a dying dog shivering against her knee.

The bell over the door gave a soft, apologetic chime.

The shop smelled of beeswax and dried lavender and something older underneath — cold stone, river water, fur. Shelves crowded the walls from floor to rafters, stuffed with jars of feathers and bundles of herbs tied in red string, copper bells, bits of antler, knots of horsehair braided into rings. It was the kind of clutter that looked accidental until you noticed every object had been placed exactly where it meant to be.

Sable Reyes looked up from his bench. He was younger than she'd expected — late twenties maybe, dark-haired, with a smile already assembling itself on his face like it lived there, folded and ready. Market-charm. She knew a performance when she saw one; she'd spent five years as a courier learning to wear pleasant faces at gates where pleasant faces got you through faster.

"Mistress Wren." He said her name like he'd been expecting her all along. That unsettled her more than anything else could have. "You've been circling my door like a gull after fish carts. I was starting to take it personally."

"You noticed that."

"I notice most things." He set down his pliers. "It's an occupational hazard of selling luck. People think luck is random. It isn't. Luck is just attention, wearing a nicer coat."

Bramble shifted against her leg, and Wren felt the tremor run through the old hound's whole body — a long, rolling shudder like the last shiver of something settling. Bramble had been hers for thirteen years, since he was a fat brown pup that chewed through two pairs of courier boots and half a saddlebag. Thirteen years of frost-passes and night-roads, of sleeping back to back in waystation lofts, of him barking wolves off her heels on the Kestrel Ridge trail. Now he was gray at the muzzle and hollow at the haunches, and the healers' hall had given her the look — the flat, practiced pity — and told her to take him home and keep him warm.

Home was a rented room above a tannery that smelled so strongly of lime it made the dog sneeze. Keep him warm meant watching him shake himself apart on her floor.

So here she was. At the charm-seller's door. Doing the one thing she'd sworn not to do.

"I need to ask you something," she said, "and I want you to tell me the truth, which I know is a strange thing to ask a man who sells charms."

His eyebrows went up, amused. "Go on."

"The marketwives say your charms work." She kept her voice low, level. "Not sort-of work. Not the way a rabbit's foot works because you believe it does. They say your fever-knots break fevers. They say the rat-charm you sold Cobb the cooper actually kept rats out of his stores, and everyone knows Cobb's stores are the best-defended in Verrin, and everyone also knows Cobb would sell his own mother for a copper, so he isn't paying you for nothing. They say your weather-twine kept Old Marta's roof-line dry through the whole spring rains while her neighbors' leaked." She paused. "Charm-sellers don't do that. Charm-sellers sell hope in pretty packaging. So either you're the luckiest fraud alive, or—"

"Or?" His voice had gone very quiet. The assembled smile was still on his face, but behind it something had gone still and watchful, like a bird freezing in hedgerow shadow.

"Or there's something else going on in this shop," Wren finished, "and I'm desperate enough tonight not to care what it is."

She crouched, and her hand found the loose skin at Bramble's neck, and the hound leaned into her hard enough that she nearly lost her balance. He was breathing wrong — shallow, quick, a whistle at the top of each inhale. The vet in the Lower Ward had named it: growth in the chest, pressing. Weeks, maybe less. Maybe days. There was nothing to cut and nothing to brew.

"He's dying," she said. It came out steadier than she felt. "And I've heard — I don't know how I've heard, market talk, drunk talk, the kind of talk people don't say twice — but I've heard that you can *talk* to them."

The silence that followed was long enough to have weight. Somewhere out in the street, a cart rattled past, and the lamp in the window guttered and steadied. Sable hadn't moved. But everything about him had changed — the easy slouch gone out of his shoulders, his hands flat on the workbench, the watchfulness no longer hiding behind the smile but standing in front of it, plain as a drawn knife.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"Yes," Wren said. "You do."

"Wren—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, hated the crack, hated the heat behind her eyes. "Don't give me the charming line. Don't sell me a knot of twine and send me home. I have watched that dog die for nine days. Nine nights, listening to him breathe like a cracked bellows. And there is a rumor — one stupid, dangerous rumor — that someone in this city can hear what animals are saying. That he can answer them." She swallowed. "If it's true, then Bramble has been trying to tell me something for nine days and I can't understand a word of it, and that — *that* — is the cruelest thing I can imagine. Not the dying. The not-understanding."

She hadn't meant to say all of it. It came out of her like water through a split seam.

Sable closed his eyes.

For a moment he looked — not angry, not afraid, though she'd learn later that both were there, layered under everything — but *tired*. Tired the way stone is tired, the slow fatigue of holding still for years.

"If I were such a person," he said carefully, each word placed like a foot on ice, "and I am not saying I am, mind — then you should know that this is the most dangerous rumor in Verrin right now. More dangerous than treason. More dangerous than debt to the crown. You should walk out that door, take your dog home, and never speak of this again. To me or anyone."

"Why? Because of the Inquisitor?"

Something flickered across his face. "You've heard the criers."

"I'm a courier. Hearing things is my trade." She straightened. "Gift-Hunt. Surrender or burn. I heard it the same morning you did." She let that sit between them. "Which means if I wanted to sell you to Hale's men, I wouldn't be standing here asking favors. I'd be at the garrison collecting whatever they pay informants these days."

"And why aren't you?"

It was a fair question, and she made herself answer it honestly, because she could see he needed honesty more than comfort. "Because you fixed Marta's roof-charm for free when she couldn't pay. Because Cobb talks about you like you hung the moon. Because in five years of walking this market, I've never once heard a soul say a bad word about Sable Reyes." She looked down at Bramble, whose ribs rose and fell, rose and fell, too fast. "And because if there's even a chance he understands more than I've been able to give him credit for, then he deserves to be understood. Before the end. Everyone deserves that."

* * *

He didn't say yes.

What he did was cross the shop, turn the sign on the door to CLOSED, drop the bar, and pull the curtain over the window. Then he stood with his back to her for a long moment, one hand braced against a shelf of feather-jars, and Wren watched five years of careful living argue themselves to death in the set of his shoulders.

"My mother used to say," he said finally, without turning around, "that the gift doesn't care about timing. Beasts don't wait until it's convenient. They speak when they speak, and you listen or you live the rest of your life knowing you didn't." A breath. "I've been telling myself for five years that not-listening is survival. Tonight I find out whether I believe that."

He turned. Knelt.

"Bring him here. Into the light."

Wren led Bramble across the floorboards, her heart hammering somewhere up near her throat. Sable spread a wool blanket by the stove, its iron face glowing dull orange, and eased the old hound down onto it with a gentleness that undid her a little — the way he supported the bad hip, the way he murmured nonsense words low in his throat, the way Bramble, who growled at strangers, who had bitten a tax collector once and gotten Wren fined for it, went limp and trusting beneath his hands immediately, bonelessly, like he'd been waiting for this touch his whole life.

"He knows you," Wren whispered.

"Animals remember kindness longer than people do." Sable settled cross-legged beside the dog, close, his palm resting flat on Bramble's side, over the laboring lungs. "They remember everything, actually. That's the trouble with them. Nothing ever heals over. Everything stays green under the scar."

"What are you doing?"

"Listening. Properly, this time. Not the way I do at the market, sideways, half-hearing, keeping myself safe. This takes—" He exhaled slowly. "It takes opening a door I've kept nailed shut for a long time. Give me a moment."

Wren held her breath.

She didn't know what she expected. Incense, chanting, some visible working. What happened instead was smaller and stranger: Sable's breathing slowed and deepened, and his eyes — open, fixed softly on middle distance — seemed to lose their focus, the way a man's do when he's listening to a voice far away across a courtyard. The stove ticked. Snow ticked against the shuttered window. Bramble's tail thumped once against the blanket, weakly, and then again.

And Sable's face changed.

Later, Wren would try to describe it to herself and fail. It wasn't shock. It was recognition — the naked, unguarded kind, the kind people only show in the instant before they remember to hide it. His lips parted. His hand pressed flatter against the dog's ribs.

"Oh," he breathed. "*Oh.* Hello, old soldier."

Wren's mouth went dry. "What did he—"

"Hush." But it was gentle, and he glanced up at her, and his eyes were shining. "He says — he says you smell like pine resin and road dust, and that it's his favorite smell in the world. He says the winter you slept in the waystation loft at Cold Ford, when you thought he was keeping your feet warm—" Sable's voice caught, and he laughed, wetly. "You thought he was keeping your feet warm. He was keeping you alive. Your heartbeat kept stuttering in the cold, skipping like a stone on ice, and he lay against your chest all night and matched his breathing to yours until yours steadied. He's done it four times. Four nights you would have died, and you don't know any of them."

Wren put her hand over her mouth.

"Bramble," she managed.

"He knows. He says—" Sable tilted his head, listening to something Wren could not hear, and the tenderness on his face was so unguarded it hurt to look at. "He says don't cry, you great soft lump, crying smells like vinegar. He's very rude. They usually are, the brave ones. All the politeness gets bred out of them by hardship." He smoothed the gray muzzle with one thumb. "He wants to know if the young one is yours. The girl. The black-and-tan pup you gave away in the autumn."

"She went to the miller's boy," Wren whispered. "He wanted a hunting dog. Bramble liked him."

"He didn't like him." Another tilt of the head. A pause. Then, softer: "He approved of him. There's a difference. He says the boy walks heavy on his heels like you do, and that made him feel better about it. He says—" Sable stopped. Swallowed. Listened hard.

The stove ticked. Snow ticked.

"He says thank you," Sable said quietly. "For thirteen years. For never chaining him. For letting him sleep facing the door. He says he was proud to carry your packs and prouder to guard your sleep, and that of all the things a dog gets to be in one life, being yours was the best of them."

Wren was weeping now, openly, silently, both hands buried in Bramble's ruff, and the old hound turned his head and licked her wrist once, tasting salt, and made a sound deep in his chest that she had known for thirteen years and understood without translation.

"How much pain?" Sable asked the dog gently. "Be honest. You're terrible at honest about pain."

Bramble's tail thumped.

"That much." Sable nodded slowly. "I thought so." He looked up at Wren, and his expression was grave and kind and asking permission without words.

"What can you do?" she asked.

"Not save him. I won't pretend otherwise, and he'd be insulted if I tried — he says he's done, Wren. Done and ready. Thirteen years is a long time for a big dog, and he's carried it well, but his hips ache and his chest hurts and he's tired down to the marrow." Sable's hand stayed on the dog's side. "What I can do is ease the road. There's a way of — sitting with them, at the end. Taking the fear out of it. Animals fear death the way they fear anything unknown: loudly. If someone tells them what's coming, in words they understand, it stops being a wall and becomes a door. I can stand at the door with him. I can make sure he's not frightened. I can't go through it with him. Nobody can."

"Do it," Wren said. "Please. Do it."

* * *

It took an hour.

She would remember pieces of it for the rest of her life, in fragments, the way you remember a fever. Sable talking low and constant, not performing, not charming — just speaking, steadily, in that quiet even voice, translating a conversation Wren could feel happening all around her without ever quite hearing. He told Bramble about the door. About what waited past it, or what might — "I'll be honest with you, old man, nobody who hasn't been through comes back to report, and those who have aren't talking — but every creature I've sat with has gone toward it calm at the end, tails up, ears forward. Like a road they recognize. Like home ground." He told him the snow outside was good snow, dry powder, easy running snow. He told him that Wren would eat properly — "she promises, don't you, promise him" — and that the miller's boy would come visit with the black-and-tan pup in the spring.

And Bramble answered. Wren never heard the answers. But she saw them land: the flick of an ear, the slow thump of the tail, the way the old dog's breathing gradually, gradually slowed, the whistle at the top of each breath softening, easing, the tremor running out of his body degree by degree as if someone were banked fires cooling.

Near the end, Sable went very still.

Wren saw it happen — saw the recognition cross his face again, deeper this time, and something else with it. Something startled. Something almost afraid.

Bramble lifted his head. It seemed to cost him everything, that lift, the last strength of thirteen loyal years gathered into one gesture, and he looked at Sable Reyes — really looked, nose to nose, eye to eye — and made a sound. Low. Deliberate. Three notes, rising.

Sable went white.

"*What,*" Wren said. "What did he say?"

Sable didn't answer her. He was staring at the dog as if the dog had spoken in the crown's own voice, and his hand had come off Bramble's side and risen halfway to his own mouth, and he looked — for the first time since she'd met him — genuinely, badly frightened.

"He said—" Sable's voice came out cracked. He cleared his throat. Tried again. "He said, *you smell of the old blood. Oathkeeper's blood. My grandsire served one, on the northern roads, before the breaking. He told us the scent. We don't forget. We never forget.*"

The words dropped into the room and lay there like stones.

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell tolled the third hour, and the sound of it seemed to wake Sable from wherever he'd gone. He blinked. Drew a long, shaking breath. Looked down at Bramble, whose head had sunk back onto the blanket, whose breathing had slowed to something deep and even and unlabored for the first time in nine days.

"He's ready," Sable said softly. "He wants to sleep now. Will you stay with him?"

"Always."

"Then I'll leave you both. He'd like that, I think. Just the two of you at the end." Sable rose stiffly, joints cracking like an old man's, and crossed to the stove to feed it another log, giving them privacy the only way privacy exists in a single room — by turning his back and making himself busy.

Wren knelt and wrapped her arms around her hound and pressed her face into his neck, into the pine-and-dust-and-Bramble smell she had known half her life, and felt his great slow heart beating against her own, steady now, steady as it had been on every frozen pass and midnight road. His tail moved once against the floorboards.

*Oathkeeper's blood.*

She held the words the way you hold a coal — carefully, aware of the burning, aware of what they might ignite. She was a courier. She knew what information weighed, and this piece weighed more than anything she'd carried in five years of carrying contraband letters and sealed writs through guarded gates. A rumor about a charm-seller was gossip. A rumor about a charm-seller was worth a purse of silver from the Inquisitor's men, maybe, if you were hungry enough.

But *Oathkeeper's blood* — that was different. That was the word the criers had burned into the air all week without naming it outright: the Gift-Hunt was for the old blood, the covenant-blood, the heretic lines. And she was standing in a shuttered shop at three in the morning, alone, with proof of it licking her tears off her wrist.

She could sell him tomorrow. One sentence at the garrison and Wren the courier ate all winter.

Instead she kissed Bramble's brow and whispered, "Good night, old man. Best of dogs. Go on ahead, I'll catch up someday, but not soon — you hear me? Not soon."

And Bramble sighed, long and deep and contented, the sigh of a soldier stacking arms at the end of a long campaign, and let go.

When she looked up, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, she found Sable watching her from across the room. The lamp threw his shadow long against the shelves of feathers and bells. His face was unreadable, but his eyes weren't; they were full of something urgent and pleading, and she realized he was about to beg her — this stranger, this woman who had guessed his deadliest secret in under an hour — to keep silent. She could see the plea assembling itself behind his teeth.

She spoke first.

"I heard everything," Wren said quietly. "All of it. Every word."

The fire popped. Snow ticked at the glass. Somewhere across the city, in a tall stone house on Cathedral Hill, a man with a warrant and a ledger was lighting his first candle of the morning's work.

"And," Wren said, rising, her knees aching, her dead dog's warmth still on her hands, "we need to talk about what you're going to do now — because Inquisitor Hale already came to me yesterday, asking questions about you. And I told him I'd think about his offer."

Sable Reyes went very still, and outside, the winter wind began to rise.
