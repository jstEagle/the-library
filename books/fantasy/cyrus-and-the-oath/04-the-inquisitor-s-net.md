## 4. The Inquisitor's Net

The report lay on Hale's desk like something dead.

He had read it four times. He would read it a fifth before the candles burned down, because that was what thoroughness demanded, and because reading it again was easier than thinking about what it meant.

*A charm-seller in the Tanner's Row market. Speaks to animals. The hound named him Oathkeeper's blood.*

Inquisitor Corvus Hale set the paper down and pressed his fingertips against his closed eyes until he saw gold sparks. Outside his window, Verrin was putting itself to bed — shutters closing, watch-bells ringing the third hour, the smell of coal smoke and tallow drifting up from the streets below the citadel. A city settling into winter like an old man into a chair he suspected would be his last.

Five years he had worked this city. Five years of ledgers and informants and quiet arrests made quieter by discretion, and in all that time the Gift-Hunt had been a formality — a proclamation read from the steps, a name crossed off here or there, a hedge-witch in the Low Quarter who had been guilty mostly of being old and strange. The crown had wanted the *appearance* of vigilance. It had not wanted mess.

Now there was a new seal on the warrants, and the king's own voice behind them, and mess was precisely what was wanted.

Hale opened his eyes and looked at the report again. The informant's name was Wren — no family name given, which meant she'd asked for anonymity, which meant she'd asked for protection, which meant she understood exactly what she had done and was afraid of it. Good. Fear kept informants honest. It also kept them from coming back, usually, and there was a part of him — a small, cold, professional part — that noted her fear as an asset to be spent carefully.

The rest of him was not professional at all tonight.

*Oathkeeper's blood.*

He rose and went to the cabinet against the far wall. It was locked with three keys, and he wore two of them on a cord around his neck beneath his shirt, where no one could see, and the third he kept in the heel of his boot like a superstition he had never managed to outgrow. Inside the cabinet, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, were the things he was supposed to have burned seven years ago when he took the gray cloak: a slate and stylus marked with the spiral sigil of the order; a child's primer on beast-speech, its pages soft with handling; and a single hawk's feather, brown-barred, given to him by a woman who had once told him that a gift was not a possession but a *promise*.

He did not take the feather out. He never took it out anymore. He simply stood with his hand flat against the cabinet door, feeling the wood grain under his palm, and let himself remember for exactly as long as it took the candle to gutter and steady.

Then he locked it again, all three locks, and went back to work.

* * *

The archive beneath the citadel was older than the citadel itself, and it smelled of dust and mouse droppings and, faintly, of the tallow they were no longer allowed to burn in quantity. Hale descended the stairs with a lantern and a request chit signed by the Lord Chancellor himself, though he doubted anyone would challenge him. Inquisitors did not need permission to read. That was rather the point of them.

The census rolls for Verrin's market districts filled six shelves. He pulled the volume for Tanner's Row and environs, five years back to present, and carried it to the reading table beneath the one window that looked out onto a lightwell full of dead ferns.

Charm-sellers were licensed tradesmen. That was the irony nobody in the palace appreciated: the crown taxed the sale of charms while burning the people who could actually make them work. Every licensed seller in the city was registered — name, stall location, sureties, tax record. Hale ran his finger down the column for Tanner's Row.

*Sable Reyes. Stall 14, east colonnade. Licensed eight years. Surety: none. Tax: paid, punctually, in coin of small denomination.*

No family name beyond Reyes, and even that had the smell of a purchase — a name bought from some clerk with a loose pen, the way rootless men bought identities the way other men bought coats. No surety meant no one vouched for him, which meant either he had no friends or he had paid extra to be forgotten. Eight years in the same stall, punctual taxes, small coins. A man who wanted very badly to be unremarkable.

Unremarkable men did not survive eight years in a market by being unremarkable. They survived by being *useful*, and usefulness left traces if you knew where to look.

Hale closed the census and sat for a long moment in the greenish light of the lightwell, listening to the archive breathe around him. Somewhere above, water moved through pipes. Somewhere below, mice moved through the walls, and he caught himself — absurdly, shamefully — listening to them as if they might tell him something. As if the old training would ever fully leave his hands. Master Ellery had said once, rapping his knuckles with the slate-stylus: *You will hear them your whole life, boy, whether you speak to them or not. That is the curse of half-learning.* She had been right. She had been burned for less than what he now did for wages, and he tried not to think about that either, and failed, as he failed every night.

He pulled the next ledger instead. Market wardens' complaint records, Tanner's Row.

There. Not complaints — *absences* of complaints. A district that averaged forty-one recorded grievances per season showed eleven across the past five years, and of those eleven, nine concerned a single stall's neighbors reporting nothing but good fortune: rats gone from their grain stores, pigeons off their awnings, a baker's moldering cellar mysteriously clean. Nine reports of miraculous luck clustered around one man, filed by neighbors who seemed, season after season, unable to articulate why their luck had changed, only grateful that it had.

Gratitude without understanding. Bargains struck so gently the other party never realized they'd been parties at all.

Hale wrote in his notebook, in the cramped cipher he used for things he did not want read over his shoulder: *Subject does not sell charms. Subject sells silence. Rats and pigeons are paid to leave. Question: what else has he bargained for?*

And beneath that, after a pause long enough that the candle beside him burned a thumb's width lower:

*Question I am not permitted to ask: is this what the Oath looked like, practiced small?*

* * *

He interviewed the informant on the fourth day, in a room with white walls and one chair, because the Gift-Hunt protocols required informant interviews to be conducted under observation, and because he had learned long ago that fear answered more questions than torture ever did, and cost considerably less paperwork.

Wren was younger than he expected. Nineteen, perhaps twenty, with courier's calluses and the hollow-eyed look of someone who had not slept since she did the worst thing of her life. She sat in the chair with her hands folded tight enough that the knuckles had gone pale, and when Hale offered her tea she stared at it as if it might be a trap.

"You reported a charm-seller," Hale said. "Tanner's Row. Tell me what you saw."

"I told the clerk."

"Tell me again. Clerks write down what they think matters. I want what actually happened."

She told him. Her voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone who had rehearsed until the words wore smooth. A dying hound. A refusal first — *he refused me, he said no, he told me to go away* — and then a night visit, and then the thing she had heard through a door not quite closed.

"It spoke," she said. "The dog spoke. Not — not words like we say them. But I understood it anyway, inside my head, like remembering something. And he answered it. Out loud. In the dog's own voice, almost." Her hands twisted in her lap. "It said he was Oathkeeper's blood. It called him that. And then it died, and he cried, and I ran."

"You ran."

"I ran." She lifted her chin, and there was something underneath the fear now, something harder. "What would you have done?"

It was not a question an informant asked an Inquisitor. Hale let the silence sit between them long enough for her to regret it, and watched her regret it, and found that he could not enjoy it, which was itself a kind of answer.

"What you should have done," he said finally, "is what you did. The law is the law."

"Yes," she said, in a voice that made it clear she thought the law was filth, and he noted that too, and dismissed her with a chit for her payment, and watched her walk out of the white room with her shoulders up around her ears like a woman walking past a grave.

Afterward he sat alone and turned the interview over, the way he turned all interviews over, looking for the seam. There was always a seam. People lied about facts, never about feelings, and Wren's feelings had been wrong. Informants who betrayed a neighbor out of greed were contemptuous of the betrayed. Informants who betrayed out of fear were sick with guilt. Wren had been both, tangled together, but threaded through everything — through the flat voice and the twisting hands — had run something he had not expected and did not know how to file.

Grief. She had grieved the hound. She had grieved, he suspected, the moment the door opened and she heard what was behind it, because some doors, once opened, could not be shut again, and she had known even as she ran to the Inquisition that she was running toward the destruction of the only true thing she had seen in years.

*You saw something holy,* Hale thought, staring at the white wall, *and you sold it, and you know exactly what it was worth, and that knowledge is going to eat you alive.*

He knew because he had done the same thing, seven years ago, in a larger room, with better lighting.

* * *

That night he walked Tanner's Row alone, out of uniform, in a plain wool coat, the way he did before every significant arrest. Protocol called it pre-operation surveillance. Master Ellery had called it something else, once, walking the market paths of the Oathkeeper enclave with a boy of twelve at her side: *Go and look at them living, Corvus. Before you decide anything about them, go and look at them living.* He had never stopped doing it. He had simply stopped admitting why.

The market by night was a skeleton of itself — stalls shuttered, tarps lashed down, the colonnade pillars standing in pools of lamplight like the columns of some poorer, humbler temple. Stall 14 was dark. Hale stood across the lane from it for the better part of an hour, in the shadow of a cooper's awning, and studied it the way he had been taught to study everything: patiently, without wanting, wanting being the enemy of seeing.

The stall was neat. Too neat. Charm-sellers cluttered — feathers and knots and bottles and bones, the visual noise of a trade built on suggestion. This stall was spare. A few genuine trinkets, honestly priced. And on the counter's edge, worn smooth by handling, a length of river stone polished into a worry-stone, the kind of thing a man touched while thinking, and beside it —

Hale narrowed his eyes. Beside it, tucked into the corner of the counter where the shutter met the post, a small clay dish with the ghost of honey in its bottom. Set out, every night, for something. A cat, probably. Or rats. Payment rendered, quietly, to the small powers of the street.

His chest did something complicated and painful that he refused to name.

Because he knew this. He *knew* this. This was the old practice, the small practice, the daily courtesy of the covenant that the primers had taught as a child's first lesson: *Feed the small ones, for they remember what the great ones forget.* Honey for the bees, milk for the hedge-beasts, a share of the loaf for the birds. Not magic. Manners. The thousand tiny courtesies by which the two realms had once acknowledged each other, before the crown tore the parchment and called the tearing progress.

Sable Reyes was not hiding a weapon. He was keeping a habit. Seven years of gray cloak had not burned that out of Hale, and apparently eight years of masks had not burned it out of the charm-seller either, and the tragedy of it sat in Hale's throat like a swallowed stone: that the last embers of the old order were being tended, secretly, tenderly, in a clay dish on a market counter, by a man who would be burned alive for it if Hale simply did his job.

He turned up his collar and walked home through the snow, which had begun sometime during the hour, fat slow flakes that hissed against the lamps.

* * *

The warrant request went to the Chancellor's office in the morning, and Hale wrote it the way he wrote everything: precise, complete, damning. *Subject Sable Reyes, alias uncertain, licensed charm-seller of Tanner's Row, is suspected of the practice of beast-speech under the provisions of the Gift-Hunt Proclamation. Evidence: informant testimony (corroborated detail attached); census irregularities; market warden records indicating anomalous animal behavior within the subject's commercial radius. Requesting authority for surveillance and arrest.*

He read it over twice. Then he did the thing he had never done in five years of service.

He did not send it.

He sat with the paper in front of him, the ink drying, the seal-ribbon waiting, and he examined himself with the same cold patience he brought to suspects, and what he found was this: he did not want proof. Proof was easy — proof was a night arrest, a hidden listening, a voice answering a voice. What he wanted, what he had wanted since the moment he read *Oathkeeper's blood* in a dead hound's testimony, was for the proof to be wrong. For the charm-seller to be a fraud. For the gift to have died out entirely, everywhere, so that the burning of the last of it had already happened years ago without him, and his cabinet full of relics was a museum of a finished thing rather than a betrayal still in progress.

Because if the bloodline lived — if somewhere in this frozen city a man still spoke the old tongue to pigeons and dying dogs — then the covenant was not dead, only sleeping, and everything Hale had done for seven years, every quiet arrest, every ledger entry, every name he had fed into the machine in exchange for his own survival, had been the murder not of criminals but of *heirs*. Of inheritors. Of the last few people alive who still knew the manners by which the world had once been kept.

Master Ellery's voice, from seven years and one lifetime ago: *A gift is not a possession, Corvus. It is a promise. And a promise unkept does not vanish. It waits.*

The winter outside his window had lasted three years now. Three years of harvests failing north of the passes, of granaries opening, of the court muttering about cursed weather while the priests blamed the gifted and the gifted burned. Everyone said the winter was punishment. Hale had always assumed they meant the gods' punishment.

But the beasts remembered what the great ones forgot. And if the covenant was broken — truly broken, torn by royal hands a generation ago — then perhaps the winter was not a punishment at all.

Perhaps it was grief.

Hale looked at the unsent warrant for a long time. Then he folded it, and placed it in his desk drawer, beneath the false bottom where he kept the things that would hang him, and he took out a fresh sheet, and began instead to build the case properly. Slowly. Thoroughly. With every delay dressed as diligence.

He would find the proof. It was his duty to find the proof. But duty, he told himself, as the candle burned down and the snow piled white on the sill, did not require him to hurry.

Three days later, the street-cat that haunted the colonnade of Tanner's Row appeared on the windowsill of his office, four floors up, where no cat had any business being. It sat regarding him through the glass with yellow eyes, patient as winter, and Hale — alone, unarmed, seven years a traitor to everything he loved — got up from his desk and opened the window.

"Well?" he said.

The cat looked at him for a long moment. Then it turned and leapt down into the snow, and Hale understood, with a certainty that felt like falling, that the net he had been weaving around the charm-seller had just begun, very quietly, to weave itself around him.
