## 5. Betrayal in the Market

The snow had stopped by morning, but the cold stayed on like a guest who'd decided the house was his. Sable opened his shutters to a sky the color of pewter and breathed out slowly, watching his own warmth curl away and vanish. Five years in Verrin had taught him that days like this one were best spent indoors with the stove fed and the door barred. Days like this one were when people came looking for comfort, and comfort was the most dangerous thing he sold.

He lit the stove anyway. He set out the tray of charms — knucklebones wrapped in red thread, river stones drilled and strung, dried sprigs of rowan that had never done anything magical in their lives — and he waited for Wren.

She came an hour after dawn, hood up, cheeks raw from the wind, and she did not come alone in any way that counted. He saw it before she'd crossed the threshold: the way her eyes went everywhere at once, cataloguing instead of greeting. The way her hands wouldn't settle. The way she stood just inside the door as though she were standing at the edge of something.

"You came back," he said, and kept his voice easy, shopkeeper's warm.

"I said I would." She pulled back her hood. There were shadows under her eyes deep enough to lose coins in.

"For the hound?"

She flinched. Small, fast, quickly buried — but he'd spent five years reading faces across a counter, and hers told him everything she wished it didn't. "For the hound," she said.

*You've already talked to someone.* The thought arrived cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts do, without preamble or mercy. *Or someone talked to you.*

He should have sent her away then. He knew it even as he smiled and gestured her toward the stool by the stove, even as he poured the cheap spiced wine he kept for customers who might become trouble. Some old instinct in him — the same instinct that had kept him alive through five years of small lies — was ringing like a struck bell. But another part of him, the part that remembered the hound's ribs under his palms, the part that had lain awake three nights hearing *Oathkeeper's blood* repeat itself in a dying animal's voice, wanted her here. Wanted, selfishly, to know what she'd heard. What she'd do with it.

That was the thing about loneliness. It made you greedy for witnesses, even the kind who could kill you.

"Wren," he said, settling onto his own stool, "before we start — look at me and tell me you haven't spoken to anyone about what happened that night."

Her eyes came up. Held. And there it was, plain as writing: guilt, arranged neatly over fear, the way a cloth is arranged over a body.

"No one," she said.

"Ah." He nodded slowly, and set down his cup. "You know, I once had a rat tell me the exact same thing while crumbs from the guardhouse kitchen were still stuck to its whiskers."

"Sable—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, not unkindly. "Don't spend the lie twice. It costs you more the second time."

She was crying now, silent and furious about it, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like she could scrub the tears off by force. "They have my sister," she said. "The Inquisitor's men. They have Marisol, she's fourteen, they took her three days ago and they said—" Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "They said all I had to do was bring you to the Tanner's Row archway at noon. That's all. Just walk ahead of me, that's all, and Marisol comes home, and no one burns."

The bell in him had stopped ringing. In its place was a terrible stillness, the stillness of a man watching winter arrive all at once.

"And if you didn't bring me?" he asked quietly.

"Then they take my mother too. And they said—" She swallowed. "They said what happens to families of the gifted's *friends*. They used words like *harborer*. Like *accomplice*."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. He thought about the hound, the great gray beast whose name had been Bracken and who had loved this woman so completely that his last breath had been spent naming her grief. He thought about a girl who had come to a stranger because she couldn't bear to let a dying animal die alone — and what kind of person the crown had to threaten to turn such a creature into bait.

He found, examining himself honestly, that he could not hate her. That was the bitterest discovery of the morning. He turned the question over the way he'd turn it if it were his own sister behind those walls, his own mother, and he knew — he knew with sick certainty — that he'd have walked into the archway too. He'd have walked anyone there. He'd have hated himself while he did it, and he'd have done it anyway, and he'd have cried exactly the way she was crying now.

"We have time?" he said.

"What?"

"It's not noon yet. How long do we have?"

Wren blinked at him through the tears. "An hour. Maybe less. Sable, you can't run, they'll — they said if you run, if they don't get you today—"

"They'll say whatever works. That's what threats are for; they're not promises, they're levers." He was already moving, pulling his cloak from its peg, sweeping the tray of charms into a sack without ceremony. "But listen to me now, Wren, and listen properly, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're both going to be very busy lying."

She stared.

"You're going to walk me to that archway. You're going to do exactly what they told you to do, because your sister's life depends on their believing you obeyed. And somewhere between here and Tanner's Row, I am going to vanish — not run, *vanish*, so cleanly that no one can prove you did anything but walk beside a man for ten streets. Do you understand? Your story has to be true. Every word of it. You brought me. I slipped away. That's all anyone will ever be able to say."

"That won't work," she whispered. "There'll be soldiers. Hale himself, maybe. They'll have the archway covered on every side—"

"Then it's fortunate," Sable said, shrugging the sack onto his shoulder, "that I know someone who doesn't care about archways, or soldiers, or sides at all."

* * *

Tanner's Row smelled of lime and old blood even in winter, and the archway itself was a great stone mouth left over from some older city, wide enough for two carts abreast, shadowed enough to hide a dozen men. Sable counted four of them without trying — two idling by the chandler's, one apparently asleep against the wall, one pretending to haggle over eels. There would be more he couldn't see. There always were.

Beside him, Wren walked like a woman marching to her own execution, which in a way she was, and he squeezed her arm once, gently.

"Breathe," he murmured. "You're doing nothing wrong. Remember that. Whatever happens in the next quarter hour, you are a girl who did what she had to do for her family, and I will never hold it against you, and neither will anyone whose opinion matters."

"You don't know what they'll do to me afterward. If they think I warned you—"

"I do know. And I'm sorry. Truly." He meant it. It sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. "Now. When I stop to look at the charm-seller's stall — the one with the blue awning, two doors down — that's your moment to step away and go find your sister. Don't look back. Whatever noise you hear, however it ends, don't look back."

"Sable—"

"*Go find your sister,*" he said softly. "That's how you help me now."

They came on. The archway grew. Somewhere above it, pigeons shifted along a gutter ledge, and Sable felt them the way he always felt birds — as small warm sparks at the edge of his mind, curious, half-asleep in the cold.

And lower down, threading between the stalls like smoke with an opinion, came a familiar prick of attention: orange fur, torn ear, a personality like a knife left in the rain.

*Well, well,* said Ash, the street-cat who owned the stretch of Verrin between the fishmongers' and the chandler's, and who had owed Sable a debt since the night Sable had bargained a fever out of her last surviving kitten. *You smell afraid, charm-man. I didn't think you knew how.*

*I need a favor,* Sable thought back, keeping his stride even, his face bored, his eyes on the blue awning. *A big one.*

*Mm. Big favors cost big.*

*Name it.*

*A saucer of cream every third day until thaw. From the good dairy, not the watery stuff. And the fat man at the fish stall stops throwing things at me.*

*Done, sworn, sealed.* And because he might not get another chance to mean it, he added, *Ash — thank you.*

*Save the gratitude,* said the cat, and vanished beneath a cart. *Tell me where you want to be ugly.*

Sable stopped at the blue awning. Picked up a love-charm, examined it with theatrical skepticism, put it down. Behind him he heard boots shift, voices low, the net drawing in — Wren stepping away, someone murmuring *now, now, quietly*, the soft ring of mail under wool.

Then he looked up at the pigeons on the gutter and gave them the only command that mattered: *Fly. All of you. Now. Down the row, hard, into the archway, into their faces.*

What followed was less an escape than a small feathered apocalypse. Forty birds exploded off the gutter in a clattering brown panic, wheeling straight down Tanner's Row and into the archway, and the soldiers — trained men, but men nonetheless, with eyes and faces and an instinctive horror of things fluttering near their heads — broke formation to curse and swat. A lamp went over. The eel-seller's basket went over. In the white flurry of wings and shouting, a lean orange streak shot between the legs of the man nearest Sable, and Ash did what cats do best: she created chaos with total indifference to consequence, yowling, scratching, knocking a brazier of coals sideways into a stack of straw.

Fire caught in the cold air like it had been waiting all winter for permission.

Sable ran. He ran through the fishmongers' lane, vaulted a cart, ducked through the gap behind the tannery vats that only children and cats used, and behind him the noise rose and changed — shouts becoming orders becoming alarm, and above it all the crackle of flame taking hold of dry timber that hadn't seen rain in weeks.

His shop. His little shop with the crooked sign and the stove he'd fed every morning for five years. The fire would reach it, or they'd fire it themselves, and either way everything he'd built to be invisible would burn bright enough to see from the walls.

He stopped in the shadows behind the tannery, lungs burning, and made himself watch the smoke begin to climb. Made himself feel it. Grief needed witnessing, even self-inflicted grief; Maren had told him that once, in another life, in the Burrow's lamplight. *Feel it now,* she'd said, *so it can't ambush you later when you need your wits.*

He felt it. Then he put it away.

*Charm-man.* Ash materialized on the wall beside him, tail lashing, smelling faintly of smoke and enormous self-satisfaction. *Your debt is paid and I would like it noted that I nearly died for cream.*

*Noted. Witnessed. Sworn upon.* He crouched and scratched the intact ear, and she permitted it with the air of a queen tolerating a peasant. *Where can I go that they won't think to look?*

*You already know,* the cat said, and her yellow eyes held his with that unnerving feline directness, as though she could see straight through his skull to the word he was avoiding. *Down. Where the other strange ones went. You smell like them now — did you know that? You smell like the tunnels.*

Sable went still. "Maren," he said aloud, barely a breath.

*The old badger-woman,* Ash agreed. *Follow the water until it smells wrong. Then follow the wrongness.*

Above them, the first of the soldiers' horns sounded — three short notes, repeated, the signal that swept a district. Not hunting a thief anymore. Hunting a name. By tonight every crier in Verrin would have it: *Sable Reyes, charm-seller, Oathkeeper's blood, taken alive if possible.*

If possible. He understood the phrasing perfectly.

He pulled his hood up and turned toward the river, toward the grates and the dark, toward the only people in the world who might understand what was waking up inside him. Behind him his shop burned, five years of careful small lies going up in honest flame, and ahead of him lay the sewers, and Maren, and the beginning of a truth far larger and far more dangerous than anything he'd ever sold.

Ash trotted ahead of him into the dark, tail high, leading the way home.

*Come on, then, Oathkeeper,* she said. *You owe me a lot of cream.*
