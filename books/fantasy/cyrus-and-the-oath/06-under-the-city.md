## 6. Under the City

The cat did not look back to see if he followed. That was the first thing Sable learned about her, and it told him more than any words could have. She moved through the smoke and the shouting of the burning market the way water moves through cracks—unhurried, certain, already knowing the shape of every gap in the world. Behind him, his shop of eleven years folded itself into flame, and Sable found he could not make himself look at it. He looked at the cat instead. It was easier.

"This way, charm-seller," she had said, in that dry rust-and-whisker voice, before the soldiers' boots had even finished hammering on his door. "Unless you'd rather stay and be interesting."

He had rather not. He had gone out the back, over the wall where the ivy grew thick, and down into the throat of the city.

The sewer mouth opened beneath a grating on Tanner's Row, and the smell hit him like a wall—rot and lye and the sweet-sour reek of the tanneries above, all of it stewing together in the dark. Sable had lived five years in Verrin and had never once thought about what ran beneath it. Cities, he had learned, were like people that way. You could love the face and never ask what was underneath, and the underneath went on doing its work regardless, patient and unthanked.

"Keep to the left wall," the cat said. Her name, she had informed him with great dignity, was Bristle, and she was owed for three winters of mice he had never actually asked her to catch, plus interest. "The right side floods when the tannery dumps. And don't step in anything you'd be sorry to wear."

"I hadn't planned to step in anything at all."

"Everyone plans that," said Bristle, and flicked her tail, and said nothing more for a long while.

Sable walked. The dark down here was total except for the thin gray seams of light where grates opened to the streets above, and through those seams he could hear the city changing. Criers, still crying. The Gift-Hunt proclamation, read for the third time that day, the words worn smooth with repetition: *surrendered or burned, surrendered or burned*. Once, a woman's voice, high and cracked with fear, calling a name down through a grate—*Tomas, Tomas, if you can hear me, don't come home*—and then boots, many boots, and silence. Sable stood beneath that grate with his heart hammering until Bristle wound around his ankles and pressed her small warm weight against him, once, briefly, the way cats do when they have decided you are worth the trouble.

"She's talking to a brother," Bristle said. "Not to you. Walk."

So he walked.

He had never thought of himself as a man who would run. That was the thing that kept circling in his head, over and over, as the sewer bent and branched and the dark grew older. Five years he had built his life on the principle that hiding was safety, that smallness was armor, that a man who sold love-charms and luck-knots with a smile and never asked for anything was a man no one would ever look at twice. He had been so careful. He had refused a dying girl's hound rather than be seen. And in one afternoon it had all come apart anyway—not because he had been careless, but because the world had simply decided to stop being the kind of world his carefulness was built for. Wren's face floated up in his memory, pale and stricken in the doorway, and he could not decide what he felt about her. Betrayed. No—that was too clean a word. She had looked like someone who had been made into a knife without her consent. He had looked like that once, he suspected, the day he first understood what lived inside him and what it would cost if it were ever seen.

The tunnel sloped downward, and the air changed. Warmer, somehow, though that made no sense this far underground, and sweeter—under the reek, a thread of woodsmoke and tallow and something green, like crushed herbs. Bristle's pace quickened.

"Nearly there," she said. "Rules, charm-seller, and mind them, because I'm vouching for you and my reputation is older than your whole family line. No names given freely. No gifts shown unasked. And when the elder speaks, you listen with your whole self, because she can tell the difference, and she'll know if you're holding anything back."

"Is that a warning or a threat?"

"It's a kindness," said Bristle. "The last one you'll get free."

* * *

The Burrow announced itself first by warmth.

The tunnel opened without warning into a vaulted space that had been, in some earlier century, a cistern—Sable could see the old waterline scored into the brick, three tiers of it, marking decades of a city's thirst. Now the great round chamber was strung with lanterns of colored glass, and the light they cast was honey-gold and moved like light through water. Racks of dried herbs hung from beams. A smithy's forge glowed in a side alcove, and a woman worked at it with her sleeves rolled, and perched on her anvil, watching the work with fierce professional interest, was a kestrel. Along one curved wall, shelves held hundreds of small clay vessels, each one sealed with wax and marked with a sigil. Somewhere out of sight, a child was singing—a looping, wordless tune that rose and fell like birdsong, and was answered, from somewhere else in the dark, by an actual bird.

There were perhaps forty people in the chamber, and Sable counted them the way a hunted thing counts: exits, hands, eyes. A man mending a net who did not look up. Two old women at a table stacked with candles, talking low. A boy of maybe twelve feeding scraps to a badger the size of a dog, and the badger eating from his hand with the terrible gentleness of something that could take the hand off and was choosing not to.

Every one of them, Sable realized, had that look he knew from his own mirror. The look of a person holding a door shut from the inside.

"They're staring," he murmured.

"You're staring back," said Bristle. "Everyone here learned the same lesson you did: look at the newcomer until you know if they're a door or a knife. Give them a minute. They'll decide you're a door."

A woman was coming toward him through the lantern light, and the chamber seemed to organize itself around her passage—not deference, exactly, but attention, the way a room attends to a fire. She was old, though old in the way of a river stone rather than a dry leaf: worn smooth, dense, hard to imagine breaking. Her hair was white and cropped close, her hands were scarred from decades of work Sable couldn't name, and she walked with a plain ash staff she did not lean on. A raven rode her shoulder with the settled gravity of a creature that had been riding that shoulder for a very long time.

She stopped three paces away and looked at him. Not at his face—at his hands, then his throat, then, briefly and terribly, his eyes.

"You're the charm-seller from Cooper's Lane," she said. "The one who wouldn't speak to the girl's hound."

The words went through him like cold water. "You know that?"

"The pigeons know it. The rats know it. The sparrows have discussed it at length, and sparrows discuss everything." Her voice was dry and unhurried. "You refused a dying animal its last words, boy. Do you know what the street-crows called you for a week after? The Deaf One. They meant it as an insult. I've been trying to teach them it was meant as a kindness, but crows hold a grudge the way mountains hold a shape." The raven on her shoulder made a low croaking sound that was unmistakably, horribly, a laugh. "My name is Maren. And you are standing in my Burrow smelling of smoke and self-pity, and I would like to know your mother's name."

Sable's whole body went still. "Why?"

"Because," said Maren, "you have your mother's way of standing when you're frightened, which is to say you stand like a man at a door he's afraid to open, and I knew your mother, and I have spent twenty years wondering if her child was dead or merely hiding. So." She waited. The lanterns moved. Somewhere in the vaults above, the city went on hunting.

"Elowen," Sable said at last, and his voice came out strange, rusty, like a hinge long unswung. "Her name was Elowen."

Maren closed her eyes.

When she opened them again, they were wet, and she did not trouble to hide it, and somehow that undid him more than anything the day had done. "Elowen of the Wellspring road," she said softly. "Who could call moths out of the dark and once argued a river into holding its flood for an hour while a village got its children to high ground. Who married a tanner's son and came to the city and put her gift away like a winter coat in summer, and never took it out again, and never told me why, and never told her son, I'd wager." She looked at him. "She never told you what you are."

"I know what I am," Sable said. "I'm a charm-seller."

"You're an Oathkeeper," Maren said, and the word went through the chamber like a stone through a window. The net-mender looked up. The candle-women went silent. The boy with the badger turned, and the badger turned with him. "The last one. The blood that made the covenant at the Wellspring before this city had a name, the blood that was meant to hold human and beast in one promise—and the crown has spent a generation teaching people to spit when they hear the word. That's why they're burning the gifted, boy. Not because we're dangerous. Because one of us might remember what the crown wants forgotten."

"I don't remember anything," Sable said, and heard the crack in his own voice. "I remember my mother singing to the moths when she thought I was asleep. I remember her being frightened of something she never named. I remember her dying quietly and being buried quietly, and that's all. I'm not an Oathkeeper. I'm a man who talks to rats for pocket money, and today I lost everything, and I'd like to sit down."

"Then sit," said Maren, and something in her voice gentled. "And eat. And grieve your shop, because it was yours, and grief doesn't care that it was only a shop. But hear me, Sable Reyes, and hear me with your whole self, the way Bristle told you to." She stepped closer. Up close her eyes were pale gray, the color of ice over moving water. "You didn't lose everything today. You lost the hiding place. There's a difference, and the difference is going to matter to you before this winter ends."

* * *

They fed him at a long table near the forge—barley soup with wild thyme, dark bread, tea of something piney and hot that he drank too fast. The net-mender turned out to be named Corvin and to speak mostly to his hands; the candle-women were sisters, Dell and Petra, who had been in the Burrow since the first purges twenty years ago. The boy with the badger was called Finch, and he watched Sable with open, unguarded curiosity until the badger, whose name was Grum, delivered a low rumbling opinion on the subject of staring, and Finch went pink and apologized to Sable and to Grum, in that order.

"You apologize to the badger first," Sable observed.

"He was rude first," said Finch, with the flawless logic of the young, and Grum made a sound that was definitely smugness, and Sable felt something in his chest crack open a little, painfully, like ice on a river in the first thaw.

It was the belonging that did it. That was the thing he kept circling back to, sitting in the lantern light with soup in his hands and smoke still in his hair. Five years he had been alone—not lonely, he had told himself, never lonely, a man with a shop full of customers and a street full of pigeons who owed him favors was not lonely—but alone, in the way of a man who has never once in his life said the true thing out loud in a room full of people. And now here was a room where the true thing was the only currency. Here was a boy who apologized to his badger. Here was an old woman who had known his mother's name and kept it, all these years, like a coal banked in ash.

He should not trust it. He knew he should not trust it. He had been betrayed before the sun went down today by a girl whose hound he had eased into death, and the crown had turned her into a knife with nothing but fear, and fear was everywhere down here too, banked and patient under the warmth like a fire waiting for a draft. Every one of these gentle people would give him up tomorrow if the Inquisitor's men put a blade to their children. He would have given up any of them. That was the arithmetic of terror, and it was honest arithmetic, and he made himself look at it straight on, the way he made himself look at everything.

And still. Still, when Dell refilled his tea without asking, when Corvin wordlessly pushed the bread closer, when Grum the badger heaved himself up onto the bench beside him with a sigh like a collapsing mattress and leaned his heavy warm weight against Sable's hip as if they had known each other for years—still, something in him that had been clenched for five years let go, one finger at a time, and did not know how to clench back.

"Grum likes you," Finch said, awed. "Grum doesn't like anyone."

"I'm very likable," Sable said. "It's my whole profession."

"What was it like?" Finch leaned forward. "Out there. Hiding it. Ma says hiding it eats you from the inside like a worm in an apple."

"Finch," said Maren, from across the chamber, without raising her voice, and Finch subsided—but the question hung in the air, and Sable found, to his surprise, that he wanted to answer it. Truly answer it. Not with charm. Not with the smile he sold alongside the luck-knots.

"It's like speaking," he said slowly, "in a house where you've been told, all your life, that if anyone hears your voice, everyone you love will die. So you learn to whisper. And then you learn to mouth the words. And then, after enough years, you learn to be silent so completely that you forget there was ever anything you wanted to say." He looked down at the badger leaning against him. "And then a dying hound looks at you with its whole heart, and asks you for one word, and you say no. And you tell yourself it was mercy. Caution. Wisdom." His throat tightened. "And some nights you know it was just fear wearing wisdom's coat."

The chamber had gone quiet around him. He hadn't meant to say all that. He hadn't said any of it, ever, to anyone.

"That," said Maren, coming to sit across from him, "is the most Oathkeeper thing you've said all evening."

* * *

Later—much later, when the lanterns had been trimmed low and the Burrow had settled into its night-sounds, the drip of water and the murmur of sleepers and the soft industrial snoring of Grum—Maren found him at the edge of the old cistern, where a low parapet looked down into the dry basin's deepest tier. She came with two cups of the pine tea and handed him one without asking, and the raven stayed on her shoulder, and for a while none of them said anything, and it was the most comfortable silence Sable had shared with another person in five years.

"Your mother came to me once," Maren said at last. "The winter before she died. She stood right where you're standing and she asked me what I would do, if I had a child, in a city that burns what we are." She sipped her tea. "I told her I'd teach the child everything, and trust the child to hide well. She said hiding wasn't a life. I said neither was burning. And then she went home, and she died, and I have thought about that conversation every winter since." The raven croaked, softly. "Hush, Quill. I'm getting to it." She looked at Sable. "She asked me one more thing. She asked me: if the gift wakes in my son, will you find him before the crown does? I made her a promise. I've kept a watch on Cooper's Lane for five years, boy. Five years. And every year you got better at hiding, and I began to hope you'd never need me at all."

"The Inquisitor found me anyway."

"The Inquisitor is the crown's hound, and hounds are why we hide. But hounds can be wrong-footed, and hounds can be turned, and this one—" Maren paused, and Sable caught it, the hesitation, the first he'd heard from her. "This one is a strange case. Hale. He was one of ours once. Apprentice to the last true Oathkeeper before the order broke. He knows what the covenant was. He knows what the winter means. And still he serves the men who broke it, and still he burns us, and I have never decided whether that makes him the most dangerous man in Verrin or the most pitiable." She turned to look at him, and the lantern light caught the gray of her eyes. "You'll meet him, I expect. People like us always meet our hounds in the end."

"Is that meant to comfort me?"

"It's meant to prepare you. Comfort is for afterward, and I'm an old woman, Sable Reyes—I deal in what comes before." She drained her tea and set the cup down on the parapet with a small definitive click. "Sleep now. Tomorrow I'll teach you what your mother never could. Tomorrow we begin with the covenant—the true one, the first one, the words your blood was made to speak. And you'll wish I hadn't, because the truth is heavier than hiding, and you've been carrying the hiding so long it's part of your spine." She was moving away into the dark, staff tapping, raven a black shape on her shoulder, when she stopped.

"Oh," she said, as if remembering something small. "One more thing. The girl. The one who betrayed you."

Sable's hands went still around the cup. "What about her?"

"The crows have been following her since sundown. She's been walking the city for hours, and she keeps ending up in the same place: the mouth of Tanner's Row, by the grate, looking down." Maren's voice was unreadable. "She hasn't gone to the soldiers again. She hasn't gone home. She's just standing there, in the cold, looking down at the dark where you went." A pause, long enough for Sable's heart to do something complicated in his chest. "Crows are gossips, but they're not liars. So I'll ask you plainly, Oathkeeper's blood, and you can answer in the morning: when the girl comes down—and she will come down—do we open the door for her, or do we bar it?"

Sable looked out over the dark basin, at the lanterns guttering low, at the sleepers curled in their blankets with their beasts beside them, at the whole small warm world that had taken him in without knowing what he would cost it.

He thought of Wren's face in the doorway. He thought of a hound's last words. He thought of a knife made without consent.

"Leave the door unbarred," he said. "But leave someone awake behind it."
