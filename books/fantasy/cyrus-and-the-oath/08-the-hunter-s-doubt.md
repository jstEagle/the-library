## 8. The Hunter's Doubt

The interrogation room beneath the Inquisitor's Hall had no windows, which Hale considered a mercy. Windows let in weather, and weather was the one thing the crown could not control and therefore pretended did not exist. Snow had been falling on Verrin for five years without pause. The king called it a season. Hale had stopped believing in seasons.

He sat across a scarred oak table from the girl — Wren, the courier, seventeen perhaps, with chapped hands and the flat stare of someone who had already decided what she was willing to lose. A brazier burned in the corner. It was not for warmth. It was for the light it threw: interrogation worked best when the subject could never quite see the interrogator's face.

"Tell me again," Hale said. "From the beginning. Slowly."

"I've told it three times."

"And I've listened to it three times. Tell it a fourth."

Wren's jaw tightened. He watched her weigh something — fear against pride, probably, or fear against guilt. Guilt was common in witnesses. They always felt they had done the wrong thing, whichever thing they'd done.

"The hound was dying," she said at last. "Old girl. Sixteen years. She stopped eating in the autumn and by midwinter she couldn't stand. I'd heard — people said — that the charm-seller on Tanner's Row wasn't really selling charms." Her eyes flicked up to his, then away. "I thought he could ease her passing. That's all. I didn't know what he was."

"What did you see?"

"He put his hands on her head. And he *listened* to her. Not like a man listens to a dog. Like — " She struggled. "Like a man listens to another man. And then he spoke back. Not words I understood. But she understood them. Her whole body changed. She looked at him the way you look at someone when they've said the true thing you were afraid of saying yourself."

Hale kept his face still. Inside, something old and buried turned over in its sleep.

"And then?"

"She died. Peacefully. Before he touched her she was crying; after, she just... let go. Like she'd been waiting for permission." Wren's voice caught, and she hated it — he could see her hating it. "And then he said something. To himself, I think. He didn't know I was still in the shop. He said, *"Oathkeeper's blood."* The hound said it first. Or — I don't know who said it first. It was like they were both saying it."

The brazier popped. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the floor of the Hall, and snow ticked faintly against a high vent grate, patient as everything else this winter.

"You reported this," Hale said, "to the garrison captain on duty."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The question hung there longer than any answer would have. Wren looked at her own hands. "Because my brother owes money to men who work for the crown," she said finally. "And because I thought — if I gave them the charm-seller, they'd leave the rest of us alone." She laughed, short and bitter. "I thought there was a *rest of us*. A line they wouldn't cross. I'm learning there isn't."

Hale wrote nothing down. He rarely did during the telling; writing made witnesses perform. He wrote afterward, from memory, and his memory was the reason the Inquisitor's office had wanted him in the first place.

"You may go," he said.

She stood, hesitated. "Will you burn him?"

"That depends on what he is."

"He eased a dying dog," Wren said. "That's what he *is*. Whatever word you put on it after."

Then she was gone, and Hale sat alone in the windowless room with the brazier ticking itself down to coals, and did not move for a long time.

* * *

His mentor had been named Corvess. Old Corvess, last apprentice-master of the Oathkeepers before the order was dissolved, a spare dry man who smelled of ink and horse and who had taught Hale, in the years before the crown repurposed him, everything a hunter needed to know about the hunted.

*You cannot catch what you do not love*, Corvess used to say, sharpening a knife that he never seemed to use on anything. *Understand that now, boy, and save yourself twenty years of misery. The gifted are not a species apart. They are us, plus one sense. Hunt them the way you'd hunt your own reflection.*

Hale had been eleven when the covenant broke — too young to remember the world before the winter, though he remembered the last summer, the way children remember things they don't yet know are endings: green hills, bees so thick in the clover the fields hummed, his mother hanging washing that dried in an afternoon. Then the frost came and did not lift, and the priests explained it, and the crown explained it more forcefully, and the explanation everyone settled on was simple and satisfying: *the gifted broke the world.*

It had to be them. Who else? The beasts had gone silent and strange the same season; the Wellspring, that half-legendary heart of the north, had frozen solid; and the only people with power over either were the ones the old stories blamed for everything anyway. The Oathkeepers were disbanded, their hall burned, their records sealed. Corvess had stood in the crowd watching the fire and said nothing, and Hale, fourteen and hungry for certainty, had taken the silence for grief and then, later, for guilt.

Now, thirty years into the winter, Hale ran the Gift-Hunt with a reputation for coldness that he had carefully cultivated and privately did not recognize. He was efficient because efficiency was kind — quick arrests, clean trials, minimal spectacle. He had argued the pyres down to a rare necessity. He told himself this was mercy. Some nights he believed it.

He climbed to his office and pulled out the sealed folio he was not supposed to have copied: fragments of Oathkeeper doctrine recovered from the burned hall, preserved by some clerk's laziness rather than anyone's wisdom. He had read them a hundred times. He read them again now, by lamplight, while the snow ticked at the glass.

*The gift is not possession,* the old hand had written. *No beast speaks through the gifted, nor they through the beast. It is a door held open between two houses. What passes through is only ever what each house already contains.*

A door held open. Not a corruption. A courtesy.

He set the page down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Here was the official account, the one he had sworn to enforce: generations ago, the gifted meddled with powers beyond their station, and their meddling poisoned the land, and the endless winter was the world's wound festering. Every gifted soul taken off the board brought spring a day closer. That was the arithmetic of the Hunt. Simple. Satisfying. Five years ago he had believed it the way you believe a load-bearing wall.

But Corvess — Corvess, who had *been* an Oathkeeper, who had known the doctrine from the inside — Corvess had never once, in all their years together, confirmed it. When Hale, young and zealous, had demanded to know whether the gifted had caused the frost, the old man had looked at him for a long moment and said: *Boy, when a house goes cold, ask who stopped feeding the fire. Don't ask who's shivering in it.*

He had thought it evasion. An old man protecting his own.

Now a courier girl sat in his memory describing a charm-seller easing a dying hound into peace, and the hound naming him *Oathkeeper's blood*, and Hale found himself doing the thing he had trained himself never to do during an interrogation: believing the witness completely.

* * *

Three days later he went down into the holding cells to look at the man himself.

They hadn't burned the shop yet — Hale had delayed it twice with procedural objections he almost meant — but Sable Reyes had been moved to the cells after the market trap went wrong, and the escape, and the fire. Half of Tanner's Row was ash. Two soldiers injured. The charm-seller vanished into the city's underbelly like water into sand, and the garrison wanted blood, and the crown wanted a name on a pyre, and Hale wanted — 

Hale stood outside the wrong cell entirely before he corrected himself. The holding cells were mostly empty these days; the Hunt had thinned the gifted population of Verrin to nearly nothing, and what remained hid too well to be caught. He paused at the bars of a cell holding a drunk, then walked on, annoyed at himself.

But the detour had served its purpose, because standing at the far end of the corridor, talking quietly with the turnkey, was Corporal Dennet — one of the men from the market raid — and Dennet was saying, in the easy horror of soldiers off duty:

"—swear on my mother, sir, the *cats*. Dozens of them. Came off the roofs like they'd drilled it. And the big tom went for Sergeant Ilves's throat, would've had it if Ilves hadn't got his arm up. Beasts don't do that. Beasts run from fire. Somebody *told* them—"

"Somebody what, Corporal?" Hale asked.

Dennet straightened so fast his mail clinked. "Sir. Didn't see you."

"Somebody told them. Told them how?"

"I — well. The way they say the old gifted do, sir. Only—" The corporal's face worked. "Only it wasn't like the stories, sir. The stories say it's like being ridden. Possessed. This was — the cats came *for* him. Like he'd asked. Like they *wanted* to help." He swallowed. "My grandmother used to say her mother talked to geese. Before the winter. Said they'd talk back. Nobody believed her till the priests said it was devil-work, and then everybody believed it all at once." He looked at the floor. "She never sounded possessed, sir. My great-grandmother. She sounded lonely."

Hale dismissed him with a nod and stood a long while in the empty corridor, listening to the drip of meltless ice somewhere in the walls.

*Sounded lonely.*

Corvess again, unbidden, in the voice he used for the lessons he considered most important, the ones delivered while ostensibly doing something else: *The covenant was never magic, boy. Magic is what the priests call it because they can't tax it. The covenant was manners. It was two kinds of creature agreeing to be neighbors instead of strangers. Break the manners and you don't get monsters. You get loneliness. Loneliness on both sides of the wall, forever, and everything that grows out of loneliness.*

Everything that grows out of loneliness. Hale looked down the row of empty cells and saw, for the first time, what they actually were: not a defense against the cause of the winter, but a monument to it. Five years of frost, and in all that time the Hunt had burned perhaps two hundred souls, and the winter had not loosened by so much as a degree. If the gifted caused the frost, where was the arithmetic? Where was the spring?

Unless the story was backwards. Unless the frost came first, or came from elsewhere, and the gifted were not the disease but the — what? The antibody? The memory of the cure?

He thought of the hound. Eased into death by a stranger's kindness, given permission to let go. Was that the act of a man poisoning the world? Was that the work the crown had taught him to hunt?

* * *

That evening he requested the sealed archives under pretext of cross-referencing warrants, and spent four hours among the confiscated Oathkeeper records that had survived the burning of the hall — crates the crown had never bothered to destroy because destroying them would mean admitting they mattered.

Most was inventory. Rosters. Correspondence about feed for messenger-hawks. And then, near the bottom of the third crate, a journal — Corvess's own hand, dated the year of the breaking.

Hale read it standing up, because sitting down suddenly seemed dangerous.

*The summons came from the palace at dusk. The king's grandfather requires the Order's presence at the Wellspring. No reason given. Master Aldery says we go gladly; I say nothing, but I do not like the season of it. Why summon the covenant's keepers in the dead of winter, to the covenant's heart, unless you intend to touch the covenant itself?*

And then, later, in a hand gone ragged:

*It is done and I cannot write it plainly, so I will write it crooked. We were not summoned to keep the covenant. We were summoned to be witnessed breaking it. The king required Oathkeeper hands upon the rite so that the blame, when it came, would come to us. He has taken the binding into the crown's keeping — he says into the crown's protection — and the Wellspring has gone dark, and the frost has begun, and Master Aldery knelt in the snow and wept and said the realms will grieve now, the realms will grieve for a hundred years, and every year of it will be laid at our door.*

*I stayed loyal tonight. God forgive me, I stayed loyal, and loyalty was the shape my betrayal took.*

Hale closed the journal.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the snow went on falling, as it had fallen for five years, as it would fall tomorrow, patient and total, and for the first time since he was fourteen years old Hale did not know what it was. Punishment? Or grief? A wound inflicted — or a mourning sung?

He thought of Wren in the windowless room, saying *he eased a dying dog, that's what he is.* He thought of Dennet's great-grandmother, sounding lonely. He thought of Corvess kneeling in the snow thirty years ago, weeping beside a frozen spring while a king smiled and an order died, and Hale understood with terrible clarity that he had spent his entire career hunting the survivors of a crime, on behalf of the criminals, using methods taught to him by a man whose loyalty had been the shape his betrayal took.

The warrant for the charm-seller lay on his desk. Dead or alive; the garrison preferred dead now, after the fire. Hale picked it up. His seal was already affixed. All it lacked was his signature, and his signature was the one thing in Verrin that had never once failed to appear where duty required it.

He dipped the pen.

He held it over the line.

Outside, far to the north, past the walls, past the passes, the winter stood like a held breath — and somewhere inside it, if the journal was true, a Wellspring waited, dark and grieving, for hands that might still undo what other hands had done.

Hale signed the warrant.

Then he sat looking at it for a long time, and reached for a fresh sheet, and began — slowly, in the careful hand of a man building a case, though this time he did not know against whom — to write down everything Corvess had ever taught him.
