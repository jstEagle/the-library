## 10. The Road North

The road out of Verrin was a scar of frozen mud, and Sable walked it like a man walking away from his own grave.

He had not stopped moving since the Burrow. Three days now. His boots were wrong for snow — good market boots, soft-soled, made for standing behind a counter while charming old women bought luck-knots — and by the second morning his feet had gone from aching to a distant, screaming numbness that he chose not to think about. The city shrank behind him into a gray smudge under the low sky, and the winter closed in around him like a hand.

Maren was dead. That thought arrived every few minutes, fresh as the first time. *Maren is dead because you came to her.* He had held the tunnel open with his gift and his body and finally with his voice, shouting for the others to run while the soldiers' torchlight crawled down the sewer passage, and when Sable had looked back she had been a small dark shape at the mouth of the collapsing brickwork, still standing, still singing to the rats in a language he had only begun to learn.

*North,* she had said, or screamed, or pressed into his mind with the last of her strength. *Wellspring. Before midwinter. You are the last blood, boy. Go.*

So he went. North, along the old drover's road, past farms where the smoke rose thin and desperate from chimneys, past fields that had not given a harvest in three years of endless cold. He kept off the road when he could hear wheels. He slept in ditches and once, gloriously, in a hay barn whose owner's dog — an ancient, half-deaf shepherd named Bracken — smelled him out in the night and did not bark.

*You smell of grief,* Bracken had said, in the slow, warm way old dogs speak. *And of rats. And of something older. Sit. I will watch.*

Sable had sat, and wept into the dog's rough neck until dawn, and Bracken had watched, as promised, and said nothing else, which was the kindest conversation Sable had ever had.

Now it was the fourth day, and the drover's road climbed toward the foothills, and the snow came down in that fine, dry, whispering Verrin winter-fall that never accumulated and never stopped, and Sable realized two things almost simultaneously: that he was being followed, and that he no longer had the strength to care very much.

The follower was clumsy. A city walker, not a woods walker — snapping twigs, breathing too loud, stopping too long whenever he stopped. Sable had noticed the rhythm of it an hour ago and had been cataloguing possibilities since. Soldiers would not be this bad at it. Hale himself would not be this bad at it. Which left —

"You walk like a man who wants to die tired," said Wren, "but I'd rather you didn't do it before I've said my piece."

She stepped out from behind a blackened pine with her hands visible and empty, which was either honesty or a very good trick. Snow clung to her cropped hair and the shoulders of a courier's oilskin, and there was a new bruise shadowing her jaw, yellow-green, days old. Her hound — no. Her hound was dead. He had eased its dying himself, kneeling in his shop with her hand on his shoulder, and the last thing the animal had spoken into his mind was *Oathkeeper's blood*, and she had heard, and she had sold him.

"Say it quickly," Sable said. His voice came out rusted. "I'm told I have a schedule."

"Sable—"

"No." He held up a hand, and was surprised to find it shaking, and hated that she saw it. "No. You don't get my name in your mouth on a frozen road after what you did. You got paid, or you got threatened, or both, and either way you walked me into a square full of Hale's men and then you stood there and watched them burn my shop. So whatever this is—" he gestured at her, at the empty road, at the whole lying shape of her presence, "—it isn't a piece. It's a trap with better manners."

Wren took the words without flinching. That was somehow worse. She stood in the falling snow and let him spend his anger on her like a woman paying down a debt, and when he finished she said, quietly:

"They have my brother."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere far off, a crow complained about the weather, and Sable heard it say, in the flat grammar of crows, *cold-cold-cold-rotten-eggs*, and wished, briefly and violently, that he could not understand it.

"Tomas," Wren went on. "Sixteen. Apprentice cooper. They picked him up the day after your shop burned — not because of anything he'd done, just to keep me walking straight. Inquisitor Hale has him in the Lantern cells. He let me write to him. One letter. It came back with half of it cut out." Her jaw worked. "They told me if I found you again — if I so much as learned which road you'd taken and didn't report it — Tomas would go to the pyre instead of the mines. So I reported a false road south, and then I deserted my post, and now there are men looking for me too, and my brother is sitting in a cell as insurance against a sister who's already failed them once." She laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "I'm very good at failing people lately. Ask anyone. Ask you."

Sable said nothing. The numbness in his feet had crept up to his knees, or perhaps that was just exhaustion talking.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," she said. "I'm asking you to let me be useful. I ran the north passes for four years — Coldford, the Shepherd's Stair, the Hollow Way. I know which villages still have bread and which ones will sell you to the crown for a bounty. I know how to move in snow, which you clearly do not, because I've been following you since yesterday morning and you left a trail a blind man could read." She paused. "You're going to the Wellspring. Maren told the whole Burrow about you before the end; the ones who got out are scattered, but word travels. You're going north, alone, in winter, with half the crown's writ on your head, and you cannot even buy a horse without someone remembering your face from the market."

"And you think I should trust you," Sable said. "Because?"

"Because I'm the only person in the world who knows where you're going and hasn't sold it yet." Something flickered across her face — not quite a smile. "Yet being the operative word. I know. But I figure a man who talks to beasts can tell a lie when he hears one. So listen to me now, Sable Reyes, charm-seller, Oathkeeper's blood, whatever you are: I am not sorry enough, and I am not good enough, and I am the best chance you have. Take it or leave it."

She stood there in the snowfall with her empty hands and her dead hound's name surely somewhere in her heart, and Sable looked at her for a long time.

Then he turned and kept walking north.

Behind him, after a moment, he heard her boots crunching through the snow, following.

* * *

Wren had expected him to send her away properly. A curse, a thrown stone, something. Instead he simply walked, and she followed, and they spent the first day in a silence so complete it felt architectural — a silence with walls and a roof, built jointly, maintained by mutual effort.

It gave her a long time to think, which was not a kindness.

She had betrayed people before. Courier work taught you that early: you carried other people's secrets in your teeth like a dog carrying eggs, and sometimes the egg broke, and you learned to live with the yolk on your conscience. But she had always told herself there was a line. Secrets, yes. Never lives.

Then the Inquisitor's men had put Tomas's name in front of her, and the line had turned out to be drawn in ash.

She watched Sable's back as he walked. He was thinner than he'd been in the market, where he'd had that easy, practiced warmth — the charm-seller's smile that never reached all the way up. Out here the smile was gone entirely and what remained looked younger and more frightened and more stubborn, all three at once. He favored his right foot. He didn't know how to read weather; twice she'd seen him glance at the sky with the expression of a man consulting a document in a language he'd only studied.

Once, near midday, a fox crossed the road thirty yards ahead of them, and Sable stopped dead, and the fox stopped too, and for the space of ten heartbeats the two of them regarded each other across the snow like old acquaintances deciding whether to pretend they hadn't seen each other. Then the fox dipped its head — actually dipped it, Wren would swear to that on any oath she had left — and slipped into the trees, and Sable stood there a moment longer with an expression she couldn't read before walking on.

"What did it say?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

The silence stretched so long she thought he wouldn't answer. "That there are soldiers at the Coldford crossroads," he said at last, not turning around. "Four of them. Asking travelers questions. It thought we should know because it doesn't like dogs, and soldiers travel with dogs."

"Oh."

Another mile. Then: "Why did you come back? Truly. Not the speech. The truth."

Wren considered several answers and discarded each one. "Because when your hound was dying," she said finally, "you could have charged me anything. I'd have paid it. My father's silver, my own blood, whatever. And you refused, and then you relented anyway, and you knelt on that filthy floor and held Bristle's head and told him—" her voice caught, annoyingly, unexpectedly, "—told him he'd been the best of everything, and that it wasn't his fault the sickness came, and that he could go. And he believed you. He went easy. I've watched animals die hard my whole life on these roads, Sable. I know what easy looks like. You gave him that for free, and I sold you for a threat against a boy who probably hates me now anyway." She shrugged, feeling the lie of the shrug in her own shoulders. "I don't know what that makes me. But I know I couldn't live in a world where the Wellspring fails because I wouldn't walk north beside you."

Sable stopped. Turned. Looked at her with those gray eyes that seemed, in certain lights, to hold a whole other country behind them.

"That's the first honest thing you've said to me," he said.

"It's not. I've been honest since the crossroads."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "Honest isn't confessing. Confessing is cheap — it costs a minute of shame and buys absolution. Honest is telling me you're not sorry enough. Honest is admitting you'd probably do it again if it were Tomas on one side and the world on the other." He held her gaze. "Would you?"

"Yes," Wren said. "God help me. Yes."

Something in his face shifted — not forgiveness, nothing so generous, but recognition. The look of a man finding his own reflection in an unexpected mirror.

"All right," Sable said. "Coldford crossroads has soldiers. Is there another way through?"

Relief hit her like warm water, and she had to turn away and fuss with her pack straps so he wouldn't see what it did to her face. "There's the Hollow Way," she said. "Old smugglers' route. Steep, and there's a stretch where the wind funnels between the rocks hard enough to take your breath sideways. But it's unwatched." She glanced back at him. "And there's a courier cache at the top. Blankets. Dried meat. If nobody's emptied it."

"If somebody has?"

"Then we'll be two liars freezing on a mountain," Wren said, "which is still better company than I deserve."

For the first time in four days, Sable Reyes laughed. It was a broken-sounding thing, startled out of him, gone as fast as it came — but it came, and Wren found she would walk a long way in the cold to hear it again.

* * *

The Hollow Way earned its name by the second hour.

The path threaded up between walls of gray stone that leaned toward each other overhead like conspirators, and the wind came down the funnel of them with a sound like a choir practicing rage. Sable walked with his shoulders hunched and his gift pulled close around him like a coat, because the mountains were *loud*. He had never been among so many wild voices — marmots whistling alarm down the scree, ravens trading insults overhead, and beneath it all something vast and slow and patient that he eventually understood was the mountain itself, or the stone of it, murmuring in a register below language, below thought. Old. Older than the covenant. Older, maybe, than the Oathkeepers who had made it.

*What broke,* the stone seemed to say, over and over, in the wordless way of stone. *What broke. What broke.*

"I know," Sable muttered into his collar. "Believe me. I know."

"Talking to yourself already?" Wren called back. She had taken point without discussion, picking the route with a sureness that was its own kind of eloquence. "We're not even at the steep part."

"Talking to the mountain."

She glanced back at him, and he braced for mockery — the market had taught him exactly what people did with his gift when it amused them — but her expression was only tired curiosity. "Does it talk back?"

"Not really. Stone doesn't use words. It's more like... weather that remembers." He scrambled over a fallen slab, wincing. "Birds are the chatterers. Dogs are the gossips. Rats are — well, rats are lawyers. Everything's negotiable, nothing's free, and they always know where the bodies are."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "Bristle used to stare at me for hours. I always wondered what he was working out."

"He was wondering why you talked so slow," Sable said, "and whether you'd ever figure out that 'sit' meant 'sit.'"

This time her laugh surprised them both. It rang oddly off the stone walls, bright and human in all that gray, and above them a raven echoed it back in a croaking parody that made Wren laugh harder, and Sable felt something in his chest loosen by a single degree — the first degree in five days.

They made camp that night in a courier cache that had, mercifully, not been emptied: a hollow behind a leaning boulder, stocked with a wool blanket gone musty, half a brick of dried venison, and a fire-striker. Wren built the fire with quick, economical movements while Sable watched, and the small flame threw their shadows huge against the rock.

"Your turn," she said, settling across the fire. "Truth for truth. That's the toll on this road. Why did you hide it? Five years, Sable. Five years selling love-charms made of pigeon gossip when you could have — what? What does the gift actually do, at full strength?"

He fed a twig to the flames and considered the question, really considered it, maybe for the first time.

"When I was nine," he said, "my mother told me what I was. Oathkeeper's blood. She said the gift wasn't mine — it was a debt, carried down through generations, and one day something would come to collect. She said never use it where anyone could see, never use it for coin, never let it become *known*, because the world had already decided once what to do with people like us." He looked up. "She died in the last purge. The one before this one. I was fourteen. I changed my name, came to Verrin, and buried the gift so deep I nearly convinced myself it wasn't there. Selling charms was — it was a joke I played on myself. Whispered bargains with pigeons dressed up as magic. Enough of the gift to feel it, not enough to be caught by it."

"And now?"

"Now Maren is dead, and my shop is ash, and the crown is hunting me across a winter that won't end, and apparently I'm the last living heir of an order that bound the realms together." He smiled without humor. "So the debt came to collect after all. Mother was right. Mothers usually are."

Wren was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Far above, the wind sang its one endless note.

"My mother died when I was eight," she said at last. "Fever. I don't remember her voice anymore. I remember her hands." She flexed her own fingers in the firelight. "Tomas is all I have of family, and I traded you for him, and I'd do it again, and some mornings I wake up and hate myself so thoroughly I can't stand the smell of my own skin. That's my truth. Toll paid."

Sable looked at her across the small fire — this woman who had ruined him and saved him in the same week, who lied the way other people breathed and confessed like it cost her blood — and found, to his considerable irritation, that he believed every word.

"Wren," he said. "When this is done — if it's ever done — you should tell him. Your brother. All of it. Not to be forgiven. Just so he knows the shape of you."

"And if the shape of me is ugly?"

"Then he'll know why," Sable said. "That's worth more than pretty."

She stared at him a long moment, firelight moving in her eyes, and then she lay back on the musty blanket and turned her face to the rock wall, and said, gruffly, "Sleep, Oathkeeper. Dawn comes early on the Stair."

But she said it softly. And Sable, wrapping himself in his half of the blanket, listening to the mountain murmur its broken refrain and the wind sing above it, thought: *two liars, learning honesty, one campfire at a time.* It was not trust. Not yet. But it was the raw material of trust, and he had survived five years in Verrin on less.

* * *

They crossed the Shepherd's Stair on the third day, a knife-edge ridge where the path narrowed to the width of a coffin and the drop on either side went down into white nothing. Wren went first, talking the whole way — not to fill the silence, Sable realized, but because she knew the sound of her voice steadied him, the way a handler's voice steadies a nervous animal. He filed that observation away and said nothing, and let it steady him.

At the summit, where the ridge widened into a bald dome of wind-scoured granite, Wren suddenly stopped and caught his arm.

"Look," she said. "North."

The clouds had torn open along the horizon, and beyond the last blue foothills the land fell away into a vast whiteness that was not cloud and not plain — a frozen immensity stretching to the edge of sight, gleaming under a sun that gave light but no warmth. And rising out of it, impossibly far away, a single peak crowned with a spiral of pale light that might have been ice-glitter or might have been something else entirely.

"The Wellspring," Sable breathed.

Even at this distance, even through the wind, he could feel it. A pull, faint and insistent, like a voice calling from another room — or like a bell that had been ringing for a generation and was waiting, patiently, furiously, for someone to answer.

Beside him, Wren shivered. "It's beautiful," she said. "And it looks like the mouth of a grave."

"It might be both." Sable squinted northward, and the pull tugged at something behind his ribs, and the mountain beneath their feet murmured its old refrain — *what broke, what broke* — and he opened his mouth to answer it, to say *I know, I'm coming.*

That was when the raven dropped out of the sky onto the granite before them, feathers ruffled, eyes wild, and spoke in a voice scraped raw by distance:

*"Runners on the north road. Crown livery. Moving faster than any soldier should. And behind them —"* the bird's voice dropped, ragged with something like awe or terror — *"behind them walks a man in gray with no warrant and no escort, and the wolves will not go near him. The wolves are afraid of him. He is coming to the Wellspring too."*

Sable and Wren exchanged a look.

"Inquisitor Hale," Wren said quietly.

"No," Sable said slowly, watching the pale spiral of light on the far peak, feeling the covenant's broken bell toll in his chest. "Not anymore. Something's wearing Hale's coat — or Hale has stopped wearing the crown's." He turned north, into the wind. "Either way, we're out of time. Midwinter's in six days. We move tonight."

Below them, far to the south, small and swift and wrong, figures were already climbing.
