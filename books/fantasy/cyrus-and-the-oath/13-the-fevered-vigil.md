## 13. The Fevered Vigil

The bolt had gone in below her collarbone, and by the time Sable carried her off the ice it had already begun to speak.

He heard it first as a whisper under the wind — a thin, wet voice, the voice of a wound talking to itself. He had spent five years pretending he could not hear such things. Now, with no one left to lie to, he listened, and what the wound said was *hot, hot, rot coming, rot riding the iron deep.*

"No," he said aloud, to Wren more than to the wound. "You don't get to die on me. You don't get to leave me with nothing but your lies."

She did not answer. Her head lolled against his shoulder, her courier's braids stiff with frozen blood, and her breath came shallow and fast, each exhale a little cloud that the wind stole before it could finish forming. The ford was behind them now, and Hale's burned warrant somewhere behind that, and ahead there was only the pass road climbing into country where even the crows flew silent.

He found shelter where a courier would have looked for it: an overhang of stone above a dry streambed, half-hidden behind a curtain of icicles, with just room enough for two bodies and a fire small enough to hide. He built the fire anyway. Warmth was worth the risk of smoke. Cold would kill her faster than any soldier.

He cut away her coat, then her shirt, wincing at every sound she made — which was none, which was worse than any sound. The bolt had gone through clean, Hale's quartermaster work, but the shaft had broken against her shoulder blade and a splinter the length of his smallest finger still sat buried beside the bone. He drew it out with his knife heated in the flame and his teeth set so hard his jaw ached, and she woke only enough to say his name — not *Sable*, but something older, softer, a name from a dream — and then sank again.

The wound wept clear fluid, then clouded. By full dark it had begun to smell sweet-rotten, like fruit going bad in a warm room.

Sable sat back on his heels and looked at his own hands.

They were strong hands, for a liar's hands. They had coaxed pigeons down from cornices and talked rats out of granaries and once, memorably, argued a mule out of a stubborn sulk for three hours in the rain. But they were also weaker than they had been a month ago. Every bargain since the Burrow had cost him — sleep, warmth, appetite, color. Maren's death had taken something too, though he could not have named what. And the Wellspring waited north of him, and everything Maren had taught him said he would need every scrap of himself to face it.

And here was Wren, burning up in the dark, needing exactly the thing he could not afford to give.

"You'd tell me to do it," he said to her unconscious form. "You'd make some joke about it. *Don't waste your strength on me, charm-seller.*" His voice cracked on the old nickname, the one she'd used when trust between them was still a coin being tested by biting. "Well, I'm not asking."

* * *

The carrion birds came at midnight, because carrion birds always know.

They came down out of the black sky one at a time, folding onto the rocks above the overhang with the particular patience of things that eat the dead. There were seven of them — six hooded crows and, apart from the others, an old raven with feathers gone grey at the throat like frost on a fencepost. Sable knew him. Every gifted soul within two hundred miles knew him. He was called Ash-Throat in the speech of birds, and he had been old when the covenant broke, and he remembered things the younger birds had only heard sung.

Sable climbed up to them without a word, because words were the coin he was about to spend and he meant to count every one.

*The woman rots,* said the youngest crow, hopping closer, head tipped. *We smell it. We will wait.*

"You'll wait a long time," Sable said. "She doesn't die tonight."

*Everything dies,* said another crow, and there was no cruelty in it, only arithmetic. *We are patient. Patience is our whole nature.*

"I need medicine moss. The gold-veined kind that grows on the north faces of the high stones, where the hot ground breathes." He named the place: the fumarole slopes two valleys east, where snow never settled and the rock steamed faintly all winter. It was half a night's flight for strong wings. "I can't carry enough back fast enough walking. I need you to bring it to me."

The crows shifted. Ash-Throat did not move at all.

*What do you pay?*

That was the question, always the question, and Sable had learned long ago that beasts did not bargain in trinkets. A crust of bread bought a sparrow's gossip; a dead mouse bought a rat's silence. But this — this was a flight through killing cold, for a bird, carrying a burden, for a man. This was not bread.

"I'll pay in voice," he said. "In true speech. I'll answer any question you ask me, honestly, in the old tongue, until dawn."

The young crows rustled with something like hunger. True speech was rare now. True speech was the sound of the world before the breaking, and most of them had never heard a human use it except to curse or command.

Ash-Throat finally turned his head. One eye, black and ancient, fixed on Sable.

*You are the blood,* the raven said. Not a question. *Maren's blood-line. The last of the keepers.*

"I'm whatever's left."

*Then hear my question before you offer so cheaply.* The raven hopped one step closer, and the other birds went still around him, the way water goes still around a stone. *When you reforge the Oath — if you reforge it — what becomes of us? The eaters of the dead. The unlovely ones. When men and beasts spoke together, we were shunned by both. Men feared our mouths and beasts despised our hunger. We kept the roads clean and the plague-years short, and no one ever sang songs about us.*

Sable knelt in the snow, and the cold came up through his knees, and he understood that he was not being asked a question. He was being asked to swear.

"When I reforge it," he said slowly, "the covenant will be written new. Not copied. New. And I will write you into it — you and yours — as wardens of the passage. First honored at every death-feast. No beast-speaker will turn from you again. That is my word, given in true speech, witnessed by the snow and the stars and your six cousins."

Silence. Then the youngest crow made a sound halfway between a croak and a laugh, and Ash-Throat spread his wings — vast, ragged, magnificent in the starlight.

*It is a bargain,* the raven said. *Fly well, keeper's blood. We fly faster.*

They went up all together, seven shadows swallowing the stars, and Sable watched them until the dark took them, and then he climbed back down to the fire and Wren.

* * *

The fever broke its banks sometime before dawn.

He had cleaned the wound as best he could with melted snow and the last of his spirit-wine, packed it with moss from his own dwindling stores — not the good moss, the weak stuff, but better than nothing — and wrapped her in both their cloaks. Then he sat and held her hand and listened to the fever talk through her, because the fever talked, in fragments, in a courier's clipped cadence gone loose and wandering.

"...told them where you'd be... told them, told them..." A sob that wasn't hers, that belonged to some earlier night. "They had my brother's name, Sable. They had his *name*, they knew what street he slept on, and the Inquisitor just — he just *held the paper*, like he was letting me read my own family's funeral..."

"I know," Sable said. "I know. I'd have done the same. I told you I would have."

"...you didn't even hesitate at the ford... you stupid, brave, *stupid*..."

He laughed, and it came out wrong, thick. "Takes one to know one."

Her hand gripped his, sudden and hard, fingers finding his with a soldier's accuracy even lost in fever, and for a moment her eyes opened — unfocused, seeing some other room, some other fire — and she said, clearly, "Don't go north without me. Promise. Promise me, you great lying—"

"I promise," he said. "Sleep. I'll be here."

She slept. The grip loosened. And Sable sat alone with the fire and the enormous quiet of the mountains, and found that the confession he had been carrying since the ford — since before the ford, if he was honest, since the pass, since the wolves, maybe since the first day she'd walked into his shop with her dying hound and her stubborn eyes — rose in him like water finding a crack.

She couldn't hear him. That was the mercy of it. He could say anything to an unconscious listener; it was the one conversation in his life with no price attached.

"Wren." Her name felt strange in his mouth, too large. "Wren Reyes-Villalobos, if you ever let me call you that while you're awake, I want you to know how this started, because you'll never believe it otherwise. It started with your hound. That mangy, wonderful old dog looked at me with your same eyes — that exact look, the one that says *I know you're lying and I'm deciding whether to care* — and I thought, oh no. Oh no, it's you. It's going to be you.

"I've spent five years being charming at people. Do you know what that is? Charm is a wall painted to look like a door. Everyone walks up to it and thinks they've come inside, and nobody has ever come inside. My mother died behind that wall. My whole order died behind it. And then you stood in my shop threatening to report me and feed me to the Inquisitor, and I thought — thank the gods. Someone who sees the wall and isn't impressed by the paint.

"I love you. There. Said it to the only witness who can't hold it over me." He smiled, and it hurt. "You'd be insufferable about it. You'd make me say it twice. You'd probably make me say it in front of Hale, just to watch his face.

"So get better. Get better and be insufferable. That's not a bargain — I learned from the best that bargains need two willing parties, and you haven't agreed to anything yet. It's a request. From a man who's very tired of talking to walls."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the pass like a long exhalation. Wren breathed — shallow, but steady, steadier than an hour ago — and Sable bowed his head over their joined hands and, for the first time since the Burrow burned, slept.

* * *

The crows returned at grey noon, and they brought the moss.

They came down in a ragged spiral, snow-crusted, wings shaking with exhaustion, and between them they carried a bundle of gold-veined moss nearly the size of a loaf — more than he'd dared hope, enough to pack the wound and brew a strong decoction besides. The youngest crow collapsed dramatically on a rock and lay there with its beak open, and Sable suspected, from the glint in its eye, that the collapse was partly theater.

"You're owed," he said to them all, in true speech, formally. "Every one of you. When the covenant stands again, remember this flight. I will."

*We remember everything,* said Ash-Throat, settling on the stone above the overhang. His grey throat was rimed with ice, and he looked, for the first time, genuinely old. *That is our curse and our craft. Now hear the rest, keeper's blood, because we saw things on the wing, and you must know them.*

Sable's stomach tightened. "Say it."

*Smoke on the southern road. Many fires, moving north along the king's highway. Soldiers — a column, days behind you but marching hard. And above them, circling, a hawk that does not hunt for itself.* The raven's eye glittered. *A hawk wearing a jess. Someone rides with that column who speaks to birds, and not as a friend.*

Sable went cold in a way the mountain had not managed. A tamed hawk, a military hawk — the crown had its own beast-handlers, coerced ones, collared ones. Which meant the Gift-Hunt had not stopped at the ford. Which meant Hale's burned warrant had not ended anything; it had only made him a fugitive too, and fugitives run toward the same destinations.

"They're coming for the Wellspring."

*They are coming for what sleeps under it,* Ash-Throat corrected. *There is a difference, and the difference may save you or bury you. Rest today. Walk tomorrow. And keeper's blood —* the raven paused, and something almost gentle entered the dry old voice — *the woman fought the fever all night. We could hear her, even aloft. She is stubborn as a winter oak. She will live to be insufferable, if that is truly what you wish.*

"It is," Sable said. "More than the covenant. Don't tell anyone I said that."

*Your secrets are safe,* said the youngest crow cheerfully. *We will sell them to no one for less than a whole haunch of venison.*

They lifted away, seven specks rising into the pale sky, and Sable turned to the work of the moss — stripping, crushing, steeping it in snowmelt over the fire until the water went the color of weak honey and smelled of pine and hot stone. He fed it to Wren drop by drop, patiently, wiping what spilled, and packed the rest into the wound, and within an hour the sweet-rotten smell began to fade, and by late afternoon the fever's whispering had dropped to a mutter, and by dusk her breathing had found a rhythm deep enough to trust.

He sat watching her face in the firelight — the chapped lips, the frost-burned cheekbones, the small scar on her chin from a fall she'd never explained — and let himself feel, for one guarded minute, the thing he never let himself feel: hope. Real hope, with weight and edges, dangerous as a drawn blade.

Because the Wellspring was four days north. Because a column of soldiers was on the road behind them. Because the price of the reforging waited under the ice, unnamed, and he had already begun spending himself in installments — he could feel it, a thinness in his chest where his gift lived, a sense of standing in a room with less air in it than yesterday.

But she was breathing. The moss was working. The crows were sworn. And tomorrow they would walk north together, into the teeth of the winter and the crown and the ending of everything he had been.

Wren stirred. Her eyelids fluttered — and did not open, but her lips moved, shaping words the fever still held hostage. Sable leaned close to catch them.

What she whispered, soft and slurred and unmistakable, was his name. Just his name. Said the way people say the thing they mean most, when they think no one is listening.

Sable closed his eyes, and added fuel to the fire, and began counting the hours until dawn.
