## 15. The Wellspring

The mountain stopped pretending to be climbable on the third morning.

Sable felt it before he saw it — a pressure in his chest like the moment before a sneeze, or the moment before grief. The wind had died two hours ago, which was wrong; wind did not die in the passes, it only changed its mind about direction. Snow hung in the air without falling, each flake suspended as if the whole sky were holding its breath.

"It knows we're here," Wren said. She stood a few paces ahead, her courier's cloak crusted white at the shoulders, her breath pluming and then — strangely — not dispersing, but hanging beside her like a second, smaller ghost.

"The mountain?"

"Whatever's under it." She didn't look back at him. She'd been quieter since the ford, since the bolt, since the fever broke on the fourth night of his vigil and she woke to find him asleep sitting up with his hand still wrapped around hers, moss-stained and shaking. She hadn't mentioned what he'd said to her while she burned. He hadn't asked whether she'd heard. Some words, once spoken into fever-dark, belong to whoever they landed on.

They climbed the last ridge together, boots crunching through a crust that sounded less like ice than like thin glass over something deep. And then the ridge crested, and the world opened, and Sable forgot how to breathe for an entirely different reason.

The Wellspring lay below them in a bowl of black stone, and it was frozen — but not the way water freezes. Water freezes in chaos, in feathers and ferns and jagged blooms. This was frozen the way a held note is frozen: one perfect sheet of ice, clear as cathedral glass, curving down into depths that glowed faintly from beneath with a light that had no color Sable could name. It was the color of the moment just before dawn. It was the color of a word about to be spoken.

Around its rim stood pillars — some whole, some shattered — carved with figures that might have been beasts or might have been people or might have been both at once, wearing each other's shapes. Snow had drifted against their bases. Nothing else moved.

"There it is," Wren whispered. "Five years I've carried letters over these passes, and I never knew this was here."

"No one did." Sable's voice came out rough. "That was rather the point."

* * *

Wren watched him go down the slope ahead of her and thought, not for the first time, that he walked like a man approaching a gallows he had built himself.

She had known Sable Reyes for six days now, properly speaking — six days of grudging trail-silence and shared fire and the slow, embarrassing business of two liars learning honesty — and in those six days she had catalogued his tells the way she catalogued pass-conditions: the way he touched his collar when he lied, the way he went still and soft-voiced when he was most afraid, the way he looked at animals as if they were old friends he owed money to. Right now every tell was firing at once. His shoulders were drawn up around his ears. His hands kept opening and closing at his sides, flexing around nothing.

"You could wait here," he said, without turning. "I mean it. Whatever happens down there—"

"I'm coming." She said it flatly, the way she said *the pass closes at dusk* and *don't eat the yellow lichen*. "You nearly killed yourself buying moss off carrion birds for me. You don't get to do the noble-alone thing now. Besides—" she lengthened her stride until she drew level with him, "—someone has to remember the way back."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, worn thin by the road, but it was his.

The bowl of the Wellspring was warmer than the air above it, which made no sense and which Wren had stopped expecting the world to make. As they descended the last stretch of stone, the suspended snow behind them finally released, falling all at once with a sound like applause. Ahead, the ice sheet waited, vast and patient, and standing upon it —

Wren's hand found her knife before her mind caught up.

It was a stag. That was the only word for it, though no stag she had ever seen — and she had seen the red deer of the northern forests, huge as oxen — prepared her for this. It stood taller than a horse at the shoulder, and it was pale the way moonlight is pale, the way frost on a window is pale: not white so much as *absent*, as if someone had cut the shape of a stag out of the world and left the shining behind. Its antlers rose and branched and branched again, and in the crooks of them snow gathered and did not fall. Its eyes were open. They were fixed on Sable.

It had been watching them, Wren realized, since the ridge. Perhaps since the ford. Perhaps since Verrin.

"Sable," she breathed.

"I see it." His voice had gone very quiet, very gentle — the voice he used with frightened animals, she realized. With dying hounds. "Don't run. Please don't run. If it wanted us dead we'd have died on the glacier."

"That's comforting. Truly. I feel better already."

He huffed — half laugh, half nerves — and then stepped forward onto the ice.

The sound his boot made was wrong. Not the crack of frozen water but a deep, resonant tone, like a finger run round the rim of a glass, and it rolled outward across the whole sheet and came back changed. The stag did not move. But the light beneath the ice brightened, and Wren felt the warmth on her face strengthen, and somewhere under her feet — under miles of stone and older dark — something enormous turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen.

* * *

Sable had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road north, and in none of those imaginings had he been struck dumb.

He had rehearsed speeches. Formal ones, learned from Maren's fragments and Hale's stolen pages — *I am come of Oathkeeper blood, I am come to answer* — and defiant ones of his own devising, and even, embarrassingly, humble ones. Now, standing three lengths from a creature that had been alive when the covenant was young, every word he owned deserted him at once.

The Pale Stag regarded him. Its eyes were the exact color of the light under the ice, and looking into them was like looking into the moment before dawn, and Sable understood suddenly and completely that this creature had witnessed everything. Every oath sworn and broken. Every voice given and taken. Five generations of human kings and beast elders, and it had stood witness to all of it, unmoving, unblinking, keeping the record no one else had survived to keep.

"You know why I'm here," he said. It came out barely above a whisper.

The stag's head dipped — a fraction, no more. Then it stepped forward, and the ice sang under its hooves, and it lowered its great crowned head until its breath washed over Sable's face. It smelled of high places and cold stars and, underneath, faintly, impossibly, of his mother's kitchen: woodsmoke and dried lavender.

Then it touched its nose to his forehead, between the eyes.

The world went white.

* * *

He was eleven years old, and his mother was dying, and there was a dog.

Not their dog — a neighbor's, a big stupid mastiff named Bracken who had followed the sickness into their house because dogs do that, because they cannot be kept from the people they love. Sable crouched by his mother's pallet with her hot hand in both of his, and Bracken pressed his broad head against the boy's shoulder, and Sable heard him say — plain as rain, plain as anything — *"Make her stay. You can talk to things. Make her stay."*

And Sable, eleven years old and drowning, had looked at the dog and lied.

*"I can't,"* he said. *"It doesn't work on people."*

Bracken believed him. Dogs are terrible liars themselves and therefore trust everyone. The boy let him believe it, and held his mother's hand while she left, and told himself afterward, for years, that he had done nothing wrong. That there had been nothing to do. That the gift worked on beasts and birds and the small creeping things and never, ever on people, and so the lie had cost nothing.

But he had known. Even at eleven, some cold honest corner of him had known that he hadn't tried. That trying meant finding out, and finding out meant either hope or the end of hope, and an eleven-year-old will choose the numbness of certainty over the terror of maybe, every time. He had let a loving creature believe a lie because the truth required courage he did not have.

The vision released him, and the ice was singing, and the stag was watching.

* * *

He was sixteen, and he sold a charm he knew was false.

Not false exactly — that was the architecture of the lie, the load-bearing wall of it. The bargains were real. The pigeons really did carry word, the rats really did turn keys, the sparrows really did watch doors. But the woman who came to his stall that day, gray-faced, clutching a locket with her son's portrait in it — her son had been conscripted, marched east, gone eight months without a letter — and Sable took her coin and called in favors from every gull and sparrow within a mile, and they brought back nothing, because there was nothing to bring back, because the boy was dead in a ditch outside Marrow Ford and the birds had already told him so.

He could have told her. He could have given her the truth, ugly and final, and let her begin the long work of grieving instead of waiting. Instead he smiled his market smile and pressed her palm closed around a copper charm and said, *"He's alive. The birds say he's alive. Hold onto hope, madam — hope is the strongest magic there is."*

She came back every month for two years. He took her coin every time. When the Gift-Hunt came, when the customer betrayed him, when Hale's soldiers dragged him through the streets — some bitter, honest part of Sable had thought, *this is fair. This is the bill arriving.*

The vision released him. The stag had not moved. The light beneath the ice had deepened, gone from pre-dawn gray to something darker, more patient.

* * *

There were others. He lived them all in the space between two heartbeats, though each one felt like a full life spent:

The rat he had promised safe passage through the granary district and then, when the guards offered silver for information on the Burrow's sympathizers, considered selling — considered for three full days before disgust won, and told himself the three days hadn't counted.

Wren herself, at the ford — no. Not Wren. But the night before the ford, when she slept and he kept watch, and he had briefly, quietly calculated how much easier the road north would be without a wounded woman slowing him, and had hated himself so thoroughly and immediately that the calculation lasted four seconds. Four seconds. The stag showed him all four.

The girl in the market, Wren, five years ago, asking him to speak to her dying hound, and his refusal — not kindness, whatever he'd told himself. Fear wearing kindness's clothes. He had let that animal suffer alone in its last hours because he was afraid of being seen, and he had dressed the cowardice up as prudence and worn it for five years.

And beneath all of them, oldest and deepest, the foundation stone: the choice, at nine years old, to hide. To bury the gift so deep that even he sometimes couldn't find it. To spend his whole life telling the world a lie about himself, one small convenient falsehood stacked on another, until the tower of them was taller than any truth he owned.

The visions ended. Sable came back to himself on his knees on the singing ice, and he was crying, and he did not remember starting.

* * *

From the rim of the bowl, Wren watched him kneel and could do nothing.

She had tried to follow him onto the ice at the first vision — she had gotten three steps before the sheet flared under her boots with a warning tone like a struck bell, and the stag's head had turned toward her with an expression she could only call *courteous refusal*, and she had retreated, heart hammering, to the stones. So she stood and watched the man she had married by firelight four nights ago kneel alone on a mirror of ice, shaking, weeping, while the light under the surface shifted through colors she had no names for, and every instinct she owned screamed at her to cross that ice and hold him and every wiser instinct held her back.

This was his to do. She understood that the way she understood weather. Some passes you walk alone or not at all.

But God, it was hard. Harder than the bolt. The bolt had been one decision, one body-length, one instant of *not him, please not him*. This was minutes of watching him be flayed by something invisible, and all she could offer was her presence on the shore and her refusal to leave it.

So she stayed. She planted her feet where the ice began and she stayed, arms crossed against the cold, watching, and when his shoulders shook she shook too, in sympathy, like a rope feeling a strain at its far end.

* * *

When the last vision released him, the stag was standing directly over him.

Sable knelt in the wash of its shadow, wrung out, emptied, stripped of every comfortable story he had ever told about himself. And here was the strange thing, the thing he would carry the rest of his life: he did not feel condemned. He felt *seen*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Condemnation says *you are worthless*. The stag's long silence said only, *this is what you are. All of it. Now choose.*

He raised his head. His voice, when it came, was wrecked and small and entirely honest.

"I'm not worthy of this," he said. "I'm a liar and a coward and I hid for twenty years while the world froze. Maren should be here. Anyone should be here. Anyone but me."

The stag regarded him for a long moment. Then it did something it had not done in all the time since the ridge: it moved — a slow turning, deliberate, ceremonial — until its whole pale body faced him fully, antlers spread against the colorless sky like a winter tree, like a crown, like a door.

And it spoke. Not aloud. The voice arrived inside Sable's chest the way beast-speech always had, but vaster, older, resonant as the ice itself:

*"None are worthy. That was never the covenant's demand. Only blood, and willingness, and the price paid whole. Your ancestors were liars also. Your ancestors were cowards also. They came anyway."*

The light beneath the ice began to rise.

*"One thing remains to be spoken,"* the Stag said, and its voice grew grave, and the warmth in the air drew back like a tide going out, *"and it is not a small thing, Oathkeeper's blood. It is the price. Hear it before you swear anything."*

The glow beneath the Wellspring gathered, brightened, focused — rising toward the surface directly beneath Sable's knees, close enough now that he could see movement in it, shapes, countless pale shapes circling slowly in the deep like fish in a lantern-lit pool. Like voices. Like every voice ever given.

The Stag lowered its head until its eyes were level with his.

*"Hear the price,"* it said, *"and ask yourself what your voice is worth."*

Behind him, at the rim of the bowl, Sable heard Wren take a sharp breath — and he turned, and saw her face, and saw that she had heard it too.
