## 17. One Night of Words

The Stag's hoofbeats faded into the pines, and the silence it left behind was the loudest thing Sable had ever heard.

They made camp in the hollow beneath the Wellspring's overlook, where a shelf of stone broke the wind and an ancient fire-ring still held the bones of some traveler's last blaze. Wren gathered wood without being asked. Sable built the fire with hands that would not quite obey him, fumbling flint twice before the sparks caught. It was not fear that shook him, he told himself. It was only the cold.

The lie tasted like all his others.

Wren sat down across the flames from him, her wounded shoulder propped against her pack, and looked at him for a long time without speaking. She had gotten good at that, these weeks on the road — at reading him in the firelight, at knowing when his silences were walls and when they were doors. Tonight, he thought, she was trying to decide which kind this was.

"You could sleep," she said finally. "I'll take first watch."

"I won't sleep."

"I know." She pulled her knees up. "I just wanted to say something kind."

A laugh escaped him — short, cracked, real. He fed a branch to the fire and watched the light climb the walls of the hollow, gilding the frost-ferns on the stone. Somewhere above them, through a mile of ice and older magic, the Wellspring breathed its slow frozen breath. Tomorrow — or the day after, if he found his courage failed him at dawn — he would walk down to it and give away the only voice he'd ever had.

But tonight was his. The Stag had given him that much: one night of words.

"Wren," he said.

"Don't," she said quickly. "Don't start saying things like it's the last chance. You'll make it a funeral."

"It isn't a funeral."

"Then what is it?"

He turned the question over honestly, because she deserved honesty now more than she had ever deserved anything, and lying to Wren had stopped working somewhere on the north road, around the time she'd taken a crossbow bolt meant for him and he'd realized there would never again be a version of his life that did not have her in it.

"It's a wedding," he said. "Or I'd like it to be. If you'll have it."

* * *

She stared at him so long that the fire popped twice, three times, throwing sparks up toward the black teeth of the pines.

"That's not funny," she said at last.

"No. It isn't."

"Sable. You're giving up your voice tomorrow. Your *voice*. You'll never—" Her breath caught, and he saw her do the arithmetic he had been doing since the Stag spoke the price aloud: no more words between them, ever. No arguments. No whispered jokes over the cookfire. No name spoken softly in the dark, *Wren*, like a hand reaching out. She would live thirty, forty, fifty years beside a man who could never again say her name.

"And you want to marry me the night before?" Her voice climbed. "You want to bind me to a silence?"

"I want to bind myself to you while I still can." He leaned forward. "I've spent five years hiding half of what I am, Wren. Five years selling charms and telling small lies and letting people believe exactly what was comfortable for them to believe. I'm done with comfortable. Tomorrow I choose the Oath. But tonight—" His throat tightened. "Tonight I choose you. Out loud. While out loud is still mine."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It isn't. None of it is. But it's true, and true is what we've got."

She was crying now, angrily, wiping her face with the heel of her hand the way she did everything — briskly, like weather to be endured. He moved around the fire and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and she let her head fall against him, and for a long while neither of them said anything at all, which was its own kind of speech.

"The life we won't have," she said eventually, into his coat. "That's what you were going to say. I could see it in your face. Say it anyway. We might as well spend the whole night grieving things that haven't happened yet."

So he said it. He told her about the little shop he'd have reopened in Verrin, in spring, with a new sign and honest prices — not charms anymore, just candles and dried herbs and the kind of harmless trinkets that meant nothing and promised nothing. He told her about the room above it with a window facing east, where she could drink her morning tea and watch the market wake up. He told her about children — and here her breath went ragged against his shoulder — children who would grow up thinking their father was quiet by nature, who would never know why he wrote everything on slate, why he smiled the way he did when sparrows landed on the sill.

"A daughter," Wren said thickly. "With your eyes. And she asks you questions every hour of the day just to watch you suffer."

"She'd get answers," Sable said. "Just not spoken ones. I'd be the most patient father in Verrin. I'd have to be."

"And I'd translate," Wren said. "Every argument. 'Your father says you can't have the last honey-bun.' 'Your father says you absolutely may.'"

"He'd never win an argument again."

"He'd never need to. He'd have me."

The grief of it moved through them both then, slow as thaw water, and they let it come. Wren talked about the courier routes she'd run — the high passes, the winter shortcuts, the inn at Two Rivers where the keeper's wife made plum brandy that could strip paint. She talked about the house she'd once imagined renting above the chandlery on Tanner's Row, how she'd stood in the empty room at nineteen and decided she would live there someday with someone worth coming home to. "I never found him," she said. "I found something else. Someone the crown wants dead and the beasts want crowned and who talks to rats. Somehow that's better."

"It's better," Sable said.

"It's *worse*, and it's better," she said, and laughed wetly, and he laughed too, and the sound of both of them laughing in that frozen hollow felt like defiance, like the two of them standing up in the face of a winter that refused to end and insisting on joy anyway, joy as an act of war.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to a bed of orange coals, Sable reached into his coat and drew out the thing he had been carrying since the Burrow: a thin ring of braided copper, salvaged from Maren's effects, the kind of simple band an Oathkeeper's spouse had worn in the old days. Maren had pressed it into his hand during those first hours in the hidden enclave, back when he'd still believed he might refuse the inheritance entirely.

"I don't know the rites," he admitted. "Maren taught me fragments. There was a vow the old order used, but I only remember pieces."

"Then we'll make one up," Wren said. "We're good at making things up. You made a living off it."

"We're good at lies. This has to be true."

"Then keep the parts you remember and mean the rest." She held out her hand, palm up, steady despite the tremor he could feel in it. "Go on, charm-seller. Sell me something."

So he took her hand, and closed the copper ring inside it, and spoke the fragments Maren had given him — *two voices, one covenant; two paths, one road; what I hold I do not hold alone* — and then, because the fragments ran out and truth did not, he kept going in plain words, his own words, the last long sentences he would ever weave:

"I can't promise you ease. I can't promise you a life that makes sense to anyone in Verrin, or a husband who can tell you good morning. I promise you this: whatever I am after tomorrow, every part of it will be yours. When I speak to the beasts, I'll be speaking for us. When I'm silent, it will only be my mouth that's silent — everything else in me will still be talking to you. I'll find ways. Slate and chalk and hands and looks. You'll wish I could shut up."

Wren laughed through her tears. Then she slid the ring onto her own finger, because he had no left hand free to do it — he was holding hers with both of his — and she spoke her vow without any borrowed liturgy at all:

"I, Wren, who betrayed you and got forgiven for it, who lied to you and got trusted anyway, take you, Sable, voice and silence, gift and price, winter and whatever comes after winter. I'll be your tongue when you have none. I'll be your witness when the world calls you liar or traitor or king. And I will never — *never* — let you feel sorry for yourself about this in peace. You'll hear about it daily."

"Daily," he agreed, voice breaking. "I expect nothing less."

There was no priest. There was no crowd. There was only the fire, and the frost, and far above them the faint blue glow of the Wellspring seeping through the ice like a heart beating under snow. But when Sable leaned forward and kissed his wife — his *wife*, the word rang in him like a struck bell — the coals flared bright without wind, and somewhere in the dark pines a nightbird began to sing, low and deliberate, a song he had never heard it sing before.

He listened the way only he could listen.

It was singing a blessing. Old words. Words from the covenant, half-forgotten even among beasts, sung at the joining of pairs under the open sky since before the first wall of Verrin was raised.

"They know," he whispered. "Wren — the birds know. The old vows are waking up. Everything is listening tonight."

"Good," Wren said, leaning her head against his. "Let them listen. Let the whole frozen world hear it. Somebody married us, and nobody's permission was required."

* * *

They did not sleep. As the night deepened, they talked the way people talk when they know the well is running dry — faster, deeper, hoarding nothing. Sable told her about his mother at last, truly, for the first time: how she'd hummed while she worked, tunes no human song used; how she'd vanished when he was nine, taken in one of the first quiet purges before the Gift-Hunt had a name; how he'd spent years angry at her for leaving and one terrible afternoon in his twenty-third year understanding, all at once, that she hadn't left — she'd been *found*, and she had let herself be taken without a fight so the soldiers wouldn't search the house, wouldn't find her son asleep in the loft with a sparrow sleeping warm in his cupped hands.

"She bought my silence with her voice," he said. "In a way. She never spoke a word when they took her. Not one. I asked everyone in the street afterward. Everyone remembered the same thing: she walked out quiet as snowfall. I used to think she was brave. Now I think she was already practicing for me. Practicing the price so I'd recognize it when it came."

Wren held him while he said it, and when he finished she said, "Then tomorrow you finish what she started. You pay it forward instead of paying it down. That's not tragedy, Sable. That's a relay."

And Wren told him about her family — the parents in the river district whose safety Hale had leveraged to make her betray him, the younger brother with the quick grin who didn't know what his sister had done to survive. "If I die on this mountain," she said, "write them something. Oh — you can't. Fine. Have Hale write them something. Make the Inquisitor earn his redemption."

"Hale will outlive us all out of sheer stubbornness," Sable said. "Men like him don't die. They just change sides until the side changes."

"Speaking of which—" She tilted her head. "Do you hear that?"

He did. Beneath the nightbird's song, beneath the wind, there was another sound now, far off down the southern slopes: the creak of harness leather, the muffled ring of iron-shod boots on frozen ground, a column of men moving through the dark with the particular discipline of soldiers who have marched all night on someone's orders.

Torchlight flickered between the trees, far below. A lot of torches.

"Crown troops," Wren breathed, sitting up. "Coming up the pass. They're marching on the Wellspring."

Sable rose slowly to his feet, his wife's copper ring glinting on her finger in the firelight, and looked south toward the torchline snaking up through the pines like a burning vein. Behind them, the Wellspring pulsed its patient blue. Ahead of them, midwinter was hours away. And somewhere down in that darkness, he knew with a certainty that needed no gift at all, marched a man who had burned his warrant and chosen his side — and behind that man, men who had chosen theirs.

"The Stag said midwinter," Sable said quietly. "The troops arrived early. Which means tomorrow we don't get our battle at a time of our choosing."

He turned to Wren, and took her hands, and gave her the last smile he would ever deliver in words.

"No more night of words, love. Dawn's coming — and it's bringing an army with it."
