## 18. Hale's Last March

The snow had stopped falling somewhere behind him, in the low country, and Hale could not decide whether that was mercy or omen. Above the tree line the air was simply cold — a clean, ancient cold, the kind that did not drift or gust but stood in place like a wall, as if the mountain itself had been holding its breath for a generation and intended to keep holding it.

He climbed with the warrant in his breast pocket and the king's seal pressed against his heart, and both felt heavier than the iron at his hip.

Three days behind him came Captain Vess and forty soldiers of the Crown's Second Column. He had watched their fires from the ridgelines, counting cook-smokes the way he had once counted, as an apprentice, the candles lit in the Oathkeeper chapterhouse on vigil nights. Forty men. Two crossbow ranks. A sapper with fire-oil, because the crown had learned that fire was cheaper than argument. They were not pursuing a traitor, whatever the orders said. They were pursuing a door, and Hale was the man who knew where the door stood.

He had burned his warrant at the ford. He had thought, in that strange bright moment with Wren falling and Sable screaming her name into the white water, that the burning had settled everything. It had not. Warrants were paper. What he carried now could not be burned, because it was not an order. It was the memory of his master Aldric's voice saying *the covenant is not a law, boy, it is a promise, and promises outlive the people who make them* — and the memory of the day, twenty years ago, when he had watched the chapterhouse seals melted down for crown silver and had said nothing, because he was nineteen and ambitious and the Inquisitor's offer had glittered like a coin.

He had spent his whole life since then mistaking obedience for honor. The road north had been peeling that mistake away, layer by layer, like bark from a dead tree.

* * *

He found the pass at dusk on the fourth day, and he knew it at once, though he had never seen it. Maren had described it to him once — no. That was not true. He corrected himself even now, out of habit, the way a man corrects a lie he has told so long it has grown calluses. His master had described it. Aldric, in the last winter of the chapterhouse, drawing the mountain's shape in spilled tea on the table: *the Wellspring lies above the shoulder of the world, where the ice never learned to melt. If you ever have to choose, Hale — and you will, I have known you long enough to know it — choose the spring.*

He had not chosen the spring. He had chosen the crown. And now he was climbing toward it with his lungs full of knives and his old master's ghost walking beside him in every gust of wind, and the tea-stain mountain rising ahead exactly as it had been drawn.

The pass narrowed between walls of blue ice. And there, in the last of the light, he saw them: figures on the slope above, small and dark against the snow. Two of them. One leaning on the other.

He should have kept climbing past them, should have circled wide and gone ahead alone to prepare the ground. Instead his feet carried him up the switchback as if they had been waiting twenty years for permission, and when he came close enough to see faces — Sable Reyes, hollow-eyed, his lips cracked and dark with cold, and Wren, gray-faced, her shoulder bound in stained bandages, walking on a leg that plainly did not want to hold her — Hale stopped, and pulled back his hood, and waited to be hated.

Wren's hand went to the knife at her belt. Sable's arm came out across her, slow, protective, and his voice when it came was a ruin of itself, scraped thin by the altitude and by whatever he had spent in the carrion-birds' country.

"You."

"Me," Hale said. "I'm alone. Vess is three days back with forty men and fire-oil. They're not coming for you, Reyes. They're coming for the spring. The king wants the covenant the way a man wants a well — he wants to own the water."

Sable stared at him. Behind the exhaustion there was something else, something Hale had seen in a hundred interrogations and had never once wanted to see directed at himself: the flat, measuring look of a man deciding how much of you to believe.

"Why come ahead?" Sable said. "Why warn us?"

"Because I spent twenty years breaking the thing you're trying to build," Hale said. "And because at the ford a girl took a bolt meant for you, and I stood there and did nothing, and I have run out of ways to do nothing." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me. Put me between the soldiers and the spring. It's the only place I've ever been worth anything."

Wren laughed — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it at all. "You hunted us. You burned the Burrow's location out of Maren's people. You—"

"I know what I did." Hale said it quietly, and found, to his surprise, that the words did not break him. They had broken him weeks ago, somewhere on the road, and what was left was only a man standing in the wreckage of his own career, cold and oddly light. "I'm not asking for forgiveness. Forgiveness is for afterward, if there is an afterward. Right now there are forty men coming up this mountain to chain a miracle to a throne, and I am the only person alive who knows exactly how Vess will come at you, because I trained him."

The wind moved through the pass, and far above them, at the crown of the ice, something vast and pale shifted — a shape at the edge of sight, antlers like bare winter branches. The Stag. Watching. Hale felt its attention pass over him the way a searchlight passes over a wall, and he did not look away, and after a long moment the attention moved on, and he understood that he had been weighed and found — not good. Never that. But perhaps useful. Perhaps, at the very bottom of the scale, honest.

Sable looked at him for a long time. Then he said, "Can you shoot?"

"I was Inquisitor of Verrin," Hale said. "I can do a great many things I'm not proud of."

"Then come up to the spring," Sable said. "And be proud of this one thing."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the ice wall, and Hale learned the shape of what he had joined.

He learned that Wren's fever had broken two days ago but the wound beneath the bandage was still angry, and that she refused with a courier's stubbornness to let it slow her. He learned that Sable slept in snatches with his ear pressed to the ice, listening to something beneath the mountain that Hale could not hear, and woke each time with an expression like a man returning from a long way off. He learned that there was almost no food, and that Sable had traded the last of his strength for the moss that had saved Wren, and that this — the arithmetic of it, a gift spent down to the stub before the one act that would need all of it — frightened Hale more than the forty soldiers did.

"You've nothing left," he said quietly, on the second night, while Wren slept. The fire was small; they dared little more. "The spring will ask something of you. What if there's nothing left to give?"

Sable turned his hands toward the flames. They were a working man's hands, charm-seller's hands, and they shook faintly, and he looked at them as though they belonged to a stranger.

"Then I'll give the shaking," he said. "It doesn't ask for strength, Hale. Maren told me that much before the end. The covenant never asked the strong for anything. It asks for everything you have, whatever that is. That's the whole trick of it. That's why the crown can't hold it." He glanced up, and in the firelight his eyes were old. "You know that. You knew it when you were nineteen. That's why they had to melt the seals."

Hale said nothing. There was nothing to say that the wind had not already said to him, all the way up the mountain, in his master's voice.

He took the first watch. Below them, far down the darkening slopes, he watched the column's fires kindle one by one — forty points of orange in the blue dusk, orderly as a rosary. He counted them twice. Then he counted them a third time, and his stomach went cold.

Fifty-three fires.

Vess had been reinforced.

* * *

They came at dawn on the third day, and they came the way Hale had taught Vess to come at everything: in three waves, crossbows high, shields low, the sappers held back until the way was bought with bolts. Hale stood at the narrow throat of the pass with the wind at his back and the Wellspring's pale glow above him like a second dawn, and he raised his voice, and it carried in the thin cold air the way only a voice trained in courtrooms and interrogation rooms can carry.

"Captain Vess! Hold your column!"

The line stopped. Fifty-three fires' worth of men, ranked on the snow, breath steaming, and at their head a broad, careful figure in a captain's half-plate that Hale had personally recommended him for, six years ago, in a report he could no longer read without flinching.

"Lord Inquisitor." Vess's voice was flat. He did not use the title kindly. "The warrant says you're relieved. The warrant says you're to be taken alive if convenient."

"I burned the warrant at the Gray Ford. Ask your scouts; they'll have found the ashes." Hale stepped forward, alone, into the open ground between the lines. "Vess. Look above me. Look at the spring."

"I've looked. It's ice and light. The crown says it's power, and the crown says it's ours."

"The crown says a great many things." Hale pulled the packet from inside his coat — the evidence he had carried out of the winter court with arrows in the door behind him, the old chapterhouse ledgers, his grandfather's-king's own correspondence, the sealed confession of a dead king's steward. He held it up where the front rank could see it. "Your grandfather's grandfather broke the covenant on purpose, Captain. Not carelessly. *Deliberately.* He unbound the beasts to take their strength for the throne, and the winter we've all been freezing in is not a punishment sent by the gifted. It's a debt. And these people—" he did not turn, but he felt Sable and Wren behind him, felt them like a hearth at his back, "—are the only ones trying to pay it."

Silence, except the wind. Somewhere in the ranks, a man shifted his weight, and the snow creaked.

"Those are pretty words, my lord," Vess said at last. "I have orders."

"I know," Hale said. "I wrote orders like them for eleven years. I know exactly how heavy they are, and I know exactly how light they turn out to be the moment you set them down." He drew his sword — slowly, deliberately, and turned it, and drove it point-first into the snow between his feet, and let go of it. It stood there, upright, absurd, a fence post of surrender. "I'm not going to fight you, Vess. I'm asking you to look at the spring and think about what your orders are actually *for*."

And that was when the horns sounded — not Vess's horns, but horns from the rear of the column, and the ranks broke open as riders forced through: the crown's own marshals, with a fresh warrant and a fresh set of chains, sent not to take the spring but to take *Hale*, because a loyal man who had seen the king's grandfather's confession was worth more in a cell than a hundred gifted in chains.

Hale watched Vess read the new warrant. Watched the captain's face do the arithmetic Hale had done on the road north — the slow, horrible arithmetic of a soldier discovering that the sum of his obedience had been spent on something rotten. Watched him look up, at the ice, at the pale glow, at the man standing unarmed in the snow with a sword planted before him like a grave marker.

"Lord Inquisitor," Vess said, and his voice had changed, gone quiet, gone uncertain, "step away from the traitors."

"No," Hale said.

It was the smallest word he had ever spoken, and the largest. Twenty years of rank and rule and careful, obedient silence, and it all came down to one syllable, said into the wind, at the top of the world.

"No," he said again. "I'm done stepping. I'm standing here. And if the crown wants the spring, it goes through me first — and through him." He stepped aside, at last, and gestured up the slope, and Sable Reyes came forward out of the shadow of the ice wall, thin and worn and shaking, with nothing left to give but the shaking, and stood beside him.

The marshals drew. The crossbow ranks wavered, caught between two sets of orders and one unbearable sight. And high above, the ice of the Wellspring began — softly, terribly, like a bell being struck — to sing.

The Pale Stag stepped out onto the ridge, antlers full of light, and every man in the column stopped breathing at once.

"Last chance, Vess," Hale said, not turning around, watching the light. "Whose side of the winter do you want to be standing on?"
