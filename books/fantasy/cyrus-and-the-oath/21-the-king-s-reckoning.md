## 21. The King's Reckoning

The throne room of Verrin had not been swept properly in a generation. Hale noticed this as he walked the long aisle toward the dais, boots ringing on stone still gritted with last autumn's ash — ash from the pyres, though the court had never admitted it. The king's grandfather had ordered the braziers lit for the Gift-Hunt's founding, and the soot of those fires had worked its way into every crack of the ceiling above, a gray stain shaped like a spreading bruise. Hale had stood beneath it for twenty years without truly seeing it. Now he could see nothing else.

He carried the evidence against his breastbone, wrapped in oilcloth: the private correspondence of Aldric the Second, the grandfather, three letters in the royal hand that named the covenant, named the Wellspring, and named the price the crown had been unwilling to pay. *Let the beasts keep their winter,* Aldric had written to his chancellors. *A king who shares his realm with wolves is a king in name only. Break the order. Burn the records. Let the gifted be made criminals, and their magic heresy, and in a hundred years no one will remember there was ever an Oath at all.*

It had taken ninety years, not a hundred. And the realm had paid the difference in a winter that would not end.

The court was assembled — not the full court, but enough. The king, young and hollow-eyed, wrapped in furs that did not warm him. The Lord Chancellor Vess, whose family had held the seal for two generations and whose hands, Hale had learned in the last week, shook when they were not gripping something. The captains of the hunt. The clerks. And along the walls, in places of honor they had not been given but had simply taken, the beasts: the Pale Stag standing in the great doorway with frost steaming off its flanks, and behind it a delegation of wolves who sat in the aisle like gray-robed elders, and ravens along the window ledges in numbers that made the light itself seem to have feathers.

The king had not wanted this audience. Hale had left him no choice. You could refuse a traitor's testimony; you could not refuse a living witness older than your dynasty, standing in your own doorway, breathing on your own guards until they stepped aside.

"Lord Inquisitor Hale." The Chancellor's voice was dry as old vellum. "You stand accused of treason. Of burning your warrant. Of aiding a fugitive. Of—" he consulted the scroll with theatrical slowness, "—*conspiring with beasts.*"

"I confess to all of it," Hale said. "And I will prove it was not treason. Treason is betrayal of the crown. I have not betrayed the crown. I have betrayed the *lie* the crown has been telling, which is a different crime, and the only cure for it is truth."

He unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

Wren stood at the back of the hall with her arm still bound against her chest, the crossbow wound three weeks healed but the shoulder stiff, and she watched Hale lay the letters out on the reading table with the same care he'd once used to lay out instruments of interrogation. She had hated this man. She had good reason. He had put her family's name on a list, put a knife against her throat in the form of a threat, and made her the instrument of Sable's ruin. And then he had stood in a frozen ford and watched her take a bolt meant for another man, and had burned his own warrant with hands that shook, and had walked north beside them through the passes like a penitent who did not expect forgiveness and did not ask for it.

She still didn't forgive him. But she had learned, on the road north, that forgiveness and alliance were different animals, and you could feed one without the other.

"Read it," Hale said, and the Chancellor's clerk read it, because Vess would not touch the letters himself. The clerk's voice started strong and thinned as it went, the way a voice does when it hears itself saying something unforgivable. *Let the beasts keep their winter.* *Burn the records.* *No one will remember there was ever an Oath at all.*

The hall was very quiet. Outside, the wind had changed in the last week — everyone in Verrin had felt it, the first thaw in five years, icicles dripping from the eaves in the mornings like the city weeping — but inside the throne room the cold seemed to gather and hold its breath.

"The Gift-Hunt," Hale said, "was founded on a lie. Not a mistake. A *lie*, deliberate and documented, told by a king who broke the covenant that kept this realm alive and then made the victims of his breaking into criminals. Every pyre. Every arrest. Every child taken from its parents for speaking to a sparrow. All of it, the cover-up of one man's greed, maintained by his heirs out of shame." He turned to face the young king directly, and Wren saw that his voice did not soften. Hale had never learned to soften. "Your grandfather did this. Your father hid it. The question, Your Majesty, is whether you will be the third king in the line of the lie, or the first king of the truth."

The king looked at the Stag.

Everyone looked at the Stag. It had not moved since it entered, and its antlers — Wren had never seen antlers like them, wide as a wagon, pale as the ice they'd been carved from — seemed to hold the gray light of the windows and give it back changed. When it finally spoke, its voice was the sound a frozen lake makes at night, deep and cracking and impossibly old.

"I was there," it said.

Three words. The hall's breath went out all at once. Wren heard a captain of the hunt sit down without meaning to, his legs simply deciding for him.

"I witnessed the first covenant, sworn at the Wellspring before your people had walls or kings. I witnessed its keeping for nine hundred years. And I witnessed its breaking, on the night Aldric's soldiers put the torch to the Oathkeeper hall while the order slept, believing themselves safe under the king's peace." The Stag's dark eyes moved across the court, and where they touched, people looked down. "I have walked the winter you made. I have counted your pyres. I do not come to accuse you. The beasts do not accuse. We only *remember*, and we have come to make sure that you remember too."

The king stood. He was young, Wren thought, younger than her, and he had inherited a throne built on a pyre, and no one had ever told him. His voice, when it came, was thin but did not break.

"The Gift-Hunt is revoked," he said. "Effective this hour. All warrants burned. All prisoners of the hunt released. The laws of the Gift—" he faltered, looked at the letters, looked at the Stag, "—the laws of the Gift are struck from the code, and the records of Aldric's reign opened to the public record. Let the historians have all of it. Let them have everything."

It was not a triumph. Wren had imagined, on the long road south, what this moment might feel like — imagined trumpets, imagined the crowd in the square outside roaring. But the hall only exhaled, the way a sickroom exhales when the fever finally breaks, and the sound was not joy. It was the sound of a city beginning a reckoning it would not finish for years: the trials, the reparations, the mothers who would want to know where the ash of their children had been scattered and would never be told. Justice, Wren was learning, was not a door that opened. It was a wall that came down one stone at a time, and every stone was heavy, and someone had to carry each one.

The Stag inclined its great head — the smallest possible bow — and turned, and the wolves rose with it, and the ravens lifted from the ledges in a gray spiral that went out through the high windows and up into a sky that, for the first time in five years, had weather in it instead of only cold.

* * *

They gave Hale a ruined hall in the oldest quarter of the city, which was the court's way of saying *we do not know what to do with you yet.* The Oathkeeper hall — or what remained of it. Aldric's soldiers had burned it ninety years ago and the crown had left the shell standing as a warning, a roofless rectangle of blackened stone that the poor of Verrin had used as a quarry and the children as a dare. When Wren walked into it behind Hale a week after the reckoning, there were sheep grazing in what had been the refectory, and a tanner had set up his racks against the east wall, and someone had built a cookfire against the scorched base of the great hearth, which was the only part of the building that had never stopped being what it was made to be.

"It's a graveyard," Wren said.

"It's a foundation," Hale said. He was already walking the perimeter, and she saw that he was not seeing the ruin. He was seeing the hall that had been — his old mentor Corvan had described it to him a hundred times, in the years before the hunt, in the low voice men used for things they loved and had lost. "The hearth is original. The covenant was sworn in this room. The stones remember, even if the court doesn't."

"You can't rebuild an order out of one traitor and a burned building."

"No," Hale agreed. "But I'm not going to be the only one for long." He stopped at the hearth and put his hand flat against the stone, and Wren saw his shoulders change — saw the twenty-year weight of the hunt settle differently on them, not lighter, but *aimed* now, which was a different thing to carry. "There were forty apprentices in the last cohort. Corvan scattered them the night of the burning. Some died. Some fled north. Some did what I did — took the crown's coin and told themselves they were keeping the old knowledge safe by serving the men destroying it." His jaw worked. "I'm going to find them. Every one. And I'm going to offer them what no one offered me: a way back."

"And the gifted of the city? The ones in hiding? You hunted them, Hale. They're not going to walk into the hall that burned their families because you've had a change of heart."

"No," he said. "They're not." He turned to face her, and for once there was no coldness in him at all; there was something rawer, and it cost him to show it. "Which is why I'm not asking them to come to me. I'm asking them to tell me what justice looks like from where they stood. Reparation before rebuilding. The order doesn't get to be the hero of its own restoration. It has to be the *debtor*." He looked back at the hearth. "Corvan taught me that the Oath was never an order's property. It was a promise between two peoples. We were just the ones who kept the ink wet."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "Sable won't come back to this. You know that. He gave his voice for the covenant. He's not going to stand in your hall and be your first recruit."

"I know." Hale's voice was very even. "Which is why the first thing the rebuilt order will do is what it should have done ninety years ago. Nothing. Stand witness. Keep the records. Be *present*." A ghost of something that was almost a smile. "And the second thing it will do is learn to listen to a man who can't answer it. He'll teach us more than I ever taught my apprentices. He just won't be able to tell us when we're being fools. We'll have to work that out ourselves."

"You'll have help with that," Wren said. "Wren's very good at telling people when they're being fools."

"I've noticed." He looked at her then, fully, and the old interrogator's flatness was gone from his eyes. "I owe you a debt I can't repay. You took a bolt for a man I was trying to hang."

"You were doing your job."

"My job was the lie." He said it without flinching, which was, Wren thought, the bravest thing she'd ever watched him do — braver than the ford, braver than the burning warrant. "I'd like to spend the rest of my life doing a better one. If the order will have me. If the city will have me. That's not mine to decide, and I know it." He picked up a fallen rafter-end, tested its weight, set it down again with the care of a man handling something that might yet be useful. "It's going to take years. Maybe the rest of my life, and not finish even then."

"Good," Wren said.

He looked at her, surprised.

"Good," she said again. "Because if it were quick, it would be a story. And stories let people off. This way it's just work, and everyone has to keep showing up for it." She shrugged with her good shoulder. "I'm a courier. I know what things cost to carry. This one's going to be heavy the whole way."

Outside, through the roofless walls, the sky had gone the soft gray-pink of a thawing evening, and from the direction of the market came a sound the city had not made in five years: bells, rung not for alarm or proclamation but for the sheer fact of spring. Somewhere in the tanner's racks a sparrow began to sing, and Wren thought of Sable, who could understand every word of it, and of the young gifted child who had come to their door that morning holding a sparrow with a question — and of the answer that was coming, slowly, one stone at a time, toward them all.

Hale stood in the middle of his ruined hall, a man rebuilding a promise, and did not yet know that the first stone was already on its way to him — carried in the beak of a bird, in the hands of a child, in a question no one in Verrin had heard asked aloud in ninety years.

*What is my gift for?*

The order would have to learn to answer. That was the work. That was the whole of it. And it began tomorrow, at dawn, with the first stone.
