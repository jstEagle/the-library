## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The quail arrived at the fifth bell, glazed in honey and rosemary, and it was going to kill the king.

Mara Lockridge knew this the way she knew most things—quietly, and before anyone else. She stood in the antechamber off the royal dining hall with a silver fork in her hand and a portion of the bird cooling on her tongue, and somewhere beneath the honey, beneath the fat and the char and the rosemary that the kitchens always overdid because His Majesty liked it that way, there was something else.

Bitter almond.

It came late, the way cyanide always did—a ghost of sweetness first, then the bitterness curling up from the back of the throat like smoke finding its way under a door. Most people wouldn't have caught it. Most people's tongues had been ruined by wine and strong cheese and thirty years of living carelessly. Mara's tongue had been ruined differently.

She swallowed. She did not spit. Spitting was for amateurs and for people who could afford to look frightened.

"Again," she said to the serving boy, who was fourteen at most and sweating through his livery.

"Milady?"

"The quail. Give me another piece. From the other side of the bird."

He fetched it with hands that shook, which told her he hadn't done it. Poisoners' hands didn't shake when they watched you taste. Poisoners' hands were very still. She'd learned that in her second year, from a man who had since hanged, and she'd never forgotten the stillness of him—the terrible calm of someone watching their work go down another person's throat.

She tasted the second piece. Same honey, same rosemary, same ghost of bitter almond, fainter this time, as if whoever had done the work had been more careless on the left side of the bird than the right. Or as if they'd wanted the king dead on the third bite rather than the first, so that no one would ever trace it back to the kitchen at all.

Mara set down the fork. She wiped her mouth on the linen provided for exactly this purpose—it was embroidered with the crown's crest, which she had always found funny in a dry, distant way—and she looked at the boy.

"What time did the birds come up from the larder?"

"Fourth bell, milady."

"And who dressed them?"

"Brannic and me, milady. And Cook oversaw the glaze."

Cook. Head cook, a broad soft-spoken mountain man named Tam Vessel whose hands smelled permanently of flour and whose prayers—she had noticed this, filed it away, thought nothing of it—were said too quietly and to no god anyone named aloud anymore.

"All right," Mara said. "Go back. Touch nothing. Tell no one."

"Are we—are we still serving it? The king's expecting—"

"No one is serving anything," Mara said, "until I say so."

She walked into the dining hall without ceremony, because ceremony was not part of her job description. The Taster's Office had no seat at court, no banner, no title worth pronouncing. It had a chair in the antechamber, a linen napkin with a crest on it, and a salary paid out of the same ledger line as the kennelmaster's. When courtiers passed her in corridors they looked through her the way one looks through furniture, or weather, or a servant carrying something unpleasant. Nine years had taught her to prefer it that way. Invisibility was a wage all its own.

King Aldric sat at the long table alone, which was itself unusual—he usually took his evening meal with at least six courtiers, because a king who dines alone is a king admitting something—but tonight the hall was empty except for two guards and a page, and the king was staring at nothing with the particular blankness of a man listening to a conversation happening somewhere very far away.

Mara had seen him do this before. She'd seen it perhaps a hundred times over nine years, and each time she had catalogued it and dismissed it: fatigue, grief, the weight of ruling a kingdom whose winters were getting longer and whose harvests were getting thinner. Kings stared at nothing. It came with the crown.

"Your Majesty." She stopped three paces from the table, because closer than three paces was familiarity and further was insolence, and both got tasters dismissed—or worse. "The quail is poisoned. I'll have the kitchens turned out and the larder sealed tonight. You'll want the pheasant instead; I've tasted it twice and it's clean."

The king looked at her.

This was the part Mara hated—not the poison, not the risk, but this: the moment when the most powerful man in the kingdom remembered she existed, and looked at her, and she saw in his face that he did not know her name. Nine years. Two hundred and some dishes tasted, four assassinations foiled—one by blade, three by chemistry—and the King of Aldermere did not know her name.

He was a handsome man gone slightly hollow, the way good timber goes hollow—sound on the outside, eaten within. Grey at the temples now, deep lines around the mouth, eyes the color of a winter sky that had gotten tired of being looked at. Everyone said he had aged strangely, faster than a king should, but everyone said it quietly, the way people say things they've decided not to think about.

"The quail," he repeated.

"Poisoned. Bitter almond under the honey glaze. Someone with kitchen access and a light hand—probably applied after the glaze, which narrows it to four or five people, and I'll know which one by morning."

"You tasted it," the king said. "You put it in your mouth knowing—" He stopped. Something moved behind his eyes, some flicker of the man he might once have been, some old reflex of decency surfacing like a fish breaking water. "How many times have you done that?"

"I don't count, Your Majesty. Counting makes it heavier."

"That's a lie," the king said, and there was something almost human in the flatness of it, almost wry. "Everyone counts."

Mara said nothing. He was right. She counted. She counted everything: every dish, every dose, every night she'd spent curled around a chamber pot wishing for death to hurry up or leave her alone entirely. Four hundred and eleven tastes in nine years. Nineteen of them had nearly killed her. Three had killed pieces of her that had never grown back.

Her tongue was the proof. Anyone who looked would see it—scarred white along the edges where the worst doses had burned it, mapped with little ridges of tissue that had healed wrong, a topography of survival that made even soft bread feel like wet leather on bad days. She had learned to read flavor through the scarring the way a blind woman learns to read through her fingertips. The bitterness lived in her now below language, below thought. Her body knew poison the way a sailor's body knows weather.

"They'll hang whoever did it," the king said.

"If you find them. Poisoners are rarely caught, only promoted." Mara heard herself say it and wished, distantly, that she hadn't. But nine years of swallowing death for a man who didn't know her name had worn her discretion thin as old cloth, and sometimes things leaked through.

The king studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, dry sound with no joy in it, like a cough wearing laughter's clothes.

"Promoted," he said. "Yes. That has a familiar ring." And then his gaze slid away from her again, back to whatever far conversation he was listening to, and he waved one hand—dismissal, or surrender, or both. "The pheasant, then. And thank you."

*Thank you.* Two words, nine years overdue, delivered like a man paying a debt he resented acknowledging. Mara bowed and withdrew, and if her chest did something complicated on the way out, she ignored it the way she ignored everything: professionally, and on schedule.

* * *

Her rooms were in the east wing, three floors above the kitchens and one floor below the servants who serviced the queen's chambers, a position chosen deliberately: close enough to smell what the kitchens burned, high enough that no one visited. Two small rooms, a narrow bed, a window that looked out on the inner courtyard where the guard drilled at dawn. A hearth. A lock she'd improved herself with a bit of filed iron that cost her a week's wages.

And, beneath the third floorboard from the window, a map.

She knelt now, as she did every night, and pried it up with her thumbnail. The board came away without sound—she'd shaved the join herself, sanded it smooth, waxed it against swelling. Beneath it lay a oilcloth packet, and inside the packet, folded eight times, a map of the Thornfell mountains drawn in her own hand.

Nine years of work. That was the thing nobody understood about escape plans—they took longer to build than the life you were escaping from. Every route she'd run for the crown, every smuggler trail she'd mapped while hunting poisoners through the border country, every shepherd's path and goat track and dried-up riverbed, she had recorded here in ink fine as hair: forty-one ways out of Aldermere that no king's road and no king's tax collector knew about. Which passes held snow until midsummer. Which villages sold horses and asked no names. Where the shrine-keepers lived—abandoned now, all of them, their fires cold for a generation, their gods unspoken—and how a person could move between them without ever touching a road wide enough for two riders abreast.

She didn't unfold it tonight. She just knelt there with her hand flat on the oilcloth, feeling the map's thickness under her palm, the way other people touch a talisman or a lover's letter. It was her real wealth. Her true country. On the day she'd had enough—if the day ever came when the counting grew too heavy, when the nineteenth near-death became a twentieth she didn't walk away from—she would lift this packet, tie it to her belt, and be three mountains gone before the palace finished missing her.

Nine years, and she had never taken it out past the window. That was the discipline of it. An escape plan you used was an escape plan you lost.

Below her, faintly, she could hear the kitchens: the clash of pans, the barked orders of a household in crisis. By now the steward would know. By morning the whole palace would know that the taster had saved the king again, and by afternoon the story would have softened into anecdote, and by week's end it would be forgotten, because that was the shape of gratitude at court—it arrived late, diluted, and never touched the hands that earned it.

Weary pride. That was the phrase she'd settled on, years ago, for what she felt. Not the clean pride of a soldier or a mason, whose work stood visible against the sky. Hers was the pride of a sewer inspector, of a plague-grave digger, of anyone whose competence consisted of absorbing filth so others didn't have to see it. The king ate because she had already eaten. The kingdom breathed because she had already breathed its poison. Nobody thanked the well for holding what it held.

She replaced the board. She pressed it flush with her thumb and sat back on her heels, and that was when she heard it—faint, from the direction of the kitchens, threading up through three floors of stone like woodsmoke through a keyhole:

Singing.

Someone in the kitchens was singing, low and wordless, a melody she half-recognized from somewhere she couldn't name—an old tune, older than the court, older maybe than the crown—and underneath it, barely audible, a voice murmuring words in a cadence she knew from nowhere at all. A prayer-cadence. The kind of rising-falling rhythm that human throats fall into when they're speaking to something they believe is listening.

Tam Vessel, she thought. Cook. Praying over his ovens at midnight like a man whispering to a fire that had gone out a lifetime ago.

Mara sat in her dark room and listened until the singing stopped, and she found—to her mild professional surprise—that the sound had raised the fine hairs on her arms. Not fear. Nothing so useful as fear. Just the cold prickle of a taster's tongue detecting something on the wind of the place: a flavor where there should have been none.

Bitter almond under the honey. Something old under the ordinary.

She banked the hearth, lay down, and began, as she did every night, the inventory that let her sleep: forty-one routes out of Thornfell. Four hundred and twelve tastes. One king alive who didn't know her name.

Tomorrow she would find the poisoner. Tomorrow the routine would resume, seamless and gray, and the thirteenth floorboard would keep its secret, and she would remain invisible, indispensable, and unthanked.

Tomorrow, she thought, as the hearth ticked down to embers.

She was wrong about tomorrow, though she had no way yet of knowing it. Tomorrow the astrologer would climb his tower and find something in the moon's count that no almanac could explain, and a silence would begin moving through the court like dye through water—and Mara Lockridge, who had spent nine years tasting death so that others needn't, was going to learn what it meant when the dead things started answering back.
