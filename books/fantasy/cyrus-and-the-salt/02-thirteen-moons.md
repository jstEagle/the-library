## 2. Thirteen Moons

The astrologer's name was Master Corvel, and he had the voice of a man reading his own obituary with interest.

Mara stood at the back of the council chamber with the morning's tasting tray balanced on her forearm—honeyed bread, figs, a pot of plum preserve she'd already dipped into twice—and watched the court pretend to be interested in astronomy. It was one of her favorite spectator sports. The lords and ladies of Aldric's court were magnificent pretenders. They could look attentive through tax riots, border skirmishes, and the slow visible decay of their king's health, all while arranging their faces into expressions of mild scholarly curiosity.

Corvel unrolled a chart across the long table, weighted its corners with brass instruments, and cleared his throat three times before anyone granted him silence.

"The moon," he said, "will turn thirteen times this year."

A pause. Somewhere near the windows, a page shifted his weight and the floorboard complained.

"Thirteen turns," Corvel repeated, as though volume might improve comprehension. "In the forty-one years of His Majesty's reign records have been kept, the moon has completed twelve full cycles annually, with the thirteenth beginning but never finishing—a partial turn, a stub of a moon, gone by winter's end. This year, for the first time since the reign of King Osric the Second, the moon will complete a full thirteenth turn. A true thirteenth moon."

He said *Osric* carefully, the way a man says the name of a dog that once bit someone.

The chancellor, a soft-bellied man named Harrow whose gout flared whenever bad news arrived, leaned forward. "And what does it mean?"

"Nothing good is ever named quickly," Corvel said.

Mara almost smiled. She liked Corvel in the distant way one likes any honest man in a palace, which was to say from across a room, never close enough to be implicated when honesty turned fatal.

He traced the chart with one finger. "I have consulted every almanac in the royal library. Under Osric—the last reign with a full thirteenth moon—there are no surviving records of what followed. None. An entire year of court chronicles, missing. Either they burned, or someone wanted them forgotten." He looked up, and his eyes found the king at the head of the table. "Or both."

King Aldric sat very still.

That wasn't unusual. The king had grown still over the years the way a pond grows still—surface unbroken, everything underneath going dark and slow. Nine years ago, when Mara had first knelt to taste his food, he'd been a restless man, drumming his fingers, laughing too loud, standing mid-meal to pace. Now he sat like furniture that had learned manners. The physicians called it dignity. The gossips called it melancholy. Mara, who watched him swallow more often than anyone alive, called it something else, though she hadn't yet found the word.

She was watching him now, and so she saw it.

When Corvel said *thirteenth moon*, the king's right hand twitched. Not a flinch of surprise—Mara knew surprises; she dealt in them daily, in flavors and poisons and the small betrayals of other people's faces. This was different. This was a hand trying to do something and being stopped halfway, like a dog yanked back by an invisible leash. The fingers curled, uncurled, then settled flat on the table with deliberate care, as if the hand itself had been told to behave.

Then the king spoke, and his voice was perfectly normal, warm even, the voice of a tired father presiding over squabbling children. "Omens are like beggars, Master Corvel. They appear wherever there's coin to be made from them. We'll mark the moon and move on. What else does the chart say?"

"It says nothing else, Majesty. That's precisely what frightens me."

"Good," said the king, smiling. "A man who fears nothing says nothing worth hearing. Next matter."

The council moved on to grain tariffs. Mara moved on with them, tray steady, face smooth. But the prickle stayed under her skin like a splinter too deep to grip.

* * *

She tasted lunch in the antechamber off the great kitchen: cold pheasant in aspic, a leek soup, wine from the southern vineyards the queen favored. Nothing bitter but the chicory. Nothing that whispered. She signed the ledger with her usual notation—a dash, a dot, a second dash meaning *clean, serve it*—and carried the tray through herself, because protocol said the taster must present each dish personally, and protocol in this palace was the only religion anyone practiced reliably.

Protocol gave her access. Access gave her eyes. Eyes gave her the map under the floorboard in her narrow room above the mews, inked with nine years of Thornfell smuggler roads, and the plan folded inside it like a letter never sent. One day. Not yet. But the day needed watching for, and so she watched.

Which was why she noticed things.

She noticed, delivering the pheasant, that Lord Harrow's gout had migrated to his left foot overnight, which meant he'd been drinking the physician's tonics again instead of resting. She noticed that two of the queen's ladies wore matching green ribbons, which meant either a new fashion or a new faction, and factions dressed faster than fashions did. And she noticed that when Chancellor Harrow mentioned, in passing, the harvest blessings—"the old shrines sending down their salt, at least the gods remember us"—the king's cup stopped an inch from his lips.

Stopped. Held. Then continued, smoothly, and he drank, and smiled at something the queen said.

But in that held inch, Mara had seen his jaw tighten. Seen his eyes go somewhere else—not inward, exactly. More like outward. Like a man listening through a wall.

Old gods. Salt. Harvest blessings. Ordinary words. Words spoken in that chamber ten thousand times, words as harmless as flour. And the king of the realm had flinched at them the way a beaten dog flinches at a raised hand.

She filed it away. Filing things away was most of her job. Poison was rarely the danger you tasted first; it was usually the danger someone mentioned casually three days earlier, if you'd been paying attention.

* * *

The afternoon light came slantwise through the west colonnade, and that was where she saw the shadow.

She was crossing from the kitchens to the mews with an empty tray, taking the long route because the short route passed the guard barracks and the guards had recently discovered opinions about her. The colonnade was empty except for the king, walking alone—he did that now, more and more, solitary circuits of the palace like a prisoner counting his bars—and the late sun threw his shadow long and sharp across the pale flagstones ahead of him.

Mara slowed. Stopped, at a respectful distance, and inclined her head. He didn't acknowledge her, which was normal; kings don't see tasters, they only see what tasters have approved.

His shadow stretched out before him, black and crisp on the stone.

She took another step, angling slightly to keep him in view, and the sun caught the moment exactly right: the king lifted his left arm to touch the balustrade—

—and the shadow lifted its left arm half a heartbeat later.

Mara stood absolutely still.

Sunlight doesn't lag. Light is faster than flesh; shadow arrives when the body arrives, or a hair before. Every instinct she owned—the instincts that had kept her tongue and throat intact through nine years of other people's murder attempts—told her what she'd just seen was impossible. Shadows do not trail their owners. Shadows do not arrive late to their own appointments.

She watched him walk on. Watched the shadow slide along the stones beside him. And there—again, faintly, at the edge of certainty—the delay. The arm moving and then the darkness moving, a half-second behind, like a reflection in water too thick to ripple honestly.

The king paused at the colonnade's end. Turned his head. Looked back at her.

His face was kind. Tired. Familiar. He said, "You're the taster."

"Mara Lockridge, Majesty."

"You've served me a long time." Something crossed his expression then—something quick and lost, like a fish flashing under ice. "Do I pay you well?"

It was such an odd question. She blinked. "Generously, Majesty."

"Good," he said. "That's good." And he walked on, and his shadow followed, and Mara would have sworn on her mother's grave—which was ash and a rumor, but still—that the shadow reached the corner after he did.

She stood in the emptying light for a long time, tray cold against her hip, doing the arithmetic of what she'd seen and finding no sum that came out sane.

Tiredness. That was the reasonable answer. She rose before dawn, tasted a dozen dishes a day, slept badly in a room that smelled of horse and copper. Eyes played tricks. So did ears, and so, famously, did tongues—her own least of all, but still.

Except her tongue didn't play tricks. Her tongue was the one instrument in this palace that had never lied to her. It had cost her everything else—softness, sleep, the ability to eat a meal without cataloguing it—but it told the truth, always, and it had taught her to trust the rest of her senses the same way. When the honeyed quail had whispered *almond* to her a month ago, she hadn't doubted it. She'd acted, and the king had lived, and nobody had thanked her, and that was fine. That was the shape of her life.

So when her eyes told her a king's shadow moved late, she believed her eyes the way she believed her tongue.

Which meant something was wrong with the king.

Not ill. Illness didn't lag shadows. Not mad—madness lived entirely inside the skull and couldn't reach the sunlight. Whatever moved a half-second behind King Aldric was something attached to him closely enough to borrow his outline and carelessly enough not to bother keeping time.

Mara walked back toward her rooms the long way, past the chapel, where evening bells were being readied, and past the little alcove where servants sometimes left offerings—candles, mostly, and once a doll, though dolls were forbidden. The old gods' alcove. Officially it was decorative. Unofficially, half the palace still left candles there, the way people scratch a doorframe passing through a house they grew up in.

There were more candles than usual. Dozens. New wax, fresh flames, and kneeling before them, a scullery girl no older than sixteen, whispering something with her eyes screwed shut and her hands pressed together hard enough to whiten.

Praying. Actually praying, in the old way, to gods whose names hadn't been said aloud in the palace since before Mara came.

The girl opened her eyes, saw Mara watching, and went scarlet. "It's nothing, mistress. Habit. My grandmother taught me—"

"I know the habit," Mara said. "My grandmother too."

The girl relaxed, grinned, fled. Mara looked at the candles a moment longer. Thirteen of them burning in the alcove—she counted without meaning to, the way she counted drops, grains, heartbeats between courses. Thirteen little flames for a thirteenth moon.

Her skin prickled again, cold, beneath the routine warmth of the evening.

* * *

Night. Her room above the mews. She pulled up the floorboard and took out the map, not to add anything, just to hold it, the way some men hold knives. Ink lines running through Thornfell like veins: goat tracks, dry washes, a smuggler's tunnel under the old watch-station, three passes that closed with snow and two that never did. Nine years of quiet theft from the kingdom's geography. Her exit. Her someday.

She should have been thinking about the map. Instead she thought about a hand curling around nothing. About a cup stopping an inch from lips at the word *gods*. About a shadow arriving late to its own body.

And about the question the king had asked her in the colonnade, which she kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it:

*Do I pay you well?*

Not *thank you*. Not *you've been loyal*. He'd asked whether he paid her—as if confirming a transaction. As if checking that she was bound to him properly. Or as if he genuinely didn't know, the way a man who has been asleep doesn't know what his hands have done.

She put the map away. Pressed the board down flat. Lay on her cot and listened to the horses shifting below, and the wind coming off Thornfell, and the far-off bells marking hours that suddenly felt less certain than they'd been that morning.

Outside her window, the moon was rising. Waning gibbous, three-quarters lit, ordinary, punctual. She watched it clear the rooftops and thought about Corvel's missing chronicles, an entire year of records burned or buried under Osric the Second. Thought about how the oldest lies aren't hidden. They're just filed where nobody reads.

Something was coming with the thirteenth moon. Corvel feared it and couldn't name it. The court ignored it expertly. And the king—

The king flinched at the names of dead gods, and his shadow couldn't keep up with him.

Mara closed her eyes and made herself a promise in the practical grammar of her trade: *watch the food, watch the king, watch the moon.* Three things to taste. She'd survived worse menus.

Below in the mews, a horse stamped once, sharply, as if something had touched it in the dark.

She decided not to think about that either.

She failed.
