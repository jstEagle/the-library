## 3. Salt on the Tongue

The salt arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone but me.

Tuesdays were delivery days at the palace's east gate—grain from the lowlands, wax from the chandler's guild, and once a season, salt from the mountain shrines. Most of the court treated the consecrated salt as a formality, a white dusting of piety over their food that cost them nothing and meant less. They sprinkled it on their eggs and never thought about where it came from or why.

I thought about everything I put in my mouth. That was the job.

The shipment came up from Thornfell in three sealed barrels, escorted by two shrine-keepers who looked half-starved themselves, all hollow cheeks and patched cloaks. That was wrong for a start. The mountain shrines had always been poor, but they'd been poor the way old things are poor—threadbare but intact, proud of their poverty even. These two looked like men who hadn't eaten properly since midwinter.

"You're late," I said, because the autumn shipment usually came before first frost, and this was well past it.

"Roads were bad." The older one wouldn't meet my eyes. He kept his gaze on my collarbone, which is where people look when they've been told not to look at someone's face. "There was trouble at the passes."

"What kind of trouble?"

"The kind you pray about." He almost smiled when he said it, and the smile frightened me more than the words did, because it wasn't bitter. It was hopeful. Nobody who'd traveled those roads in winter had any business looking hopeful.

I cracked the seal on the first barrel and took up my testing spoon.

Salt testing is simple in theory. You're checking for contamination—ash, sand, the cheaper rock salt that unscrupulous merchants sometimes cut into shrine salt because the difference is invisible until it touches your tongue. Consecrated salt has a particular bite to it, a clean mineral sharpness that sits at the back of the throat like a held breath. Cut it with anything and the breath comes out ragged.

I took a pinch, pressed it to my tongue, and waited.

It was pure. Better than pure—it was the finest batch I'd tasted in nine years, crystals like cut glass, sharp enough to make my eyes water. Whatever trouble the shrine-keepers had passed through, it hadn't touched their craft.

And then the whisper started.

*Come home.*

Two words. A woman's voice, or something shaped like one—the way a cave is shaped like a mouth without being one. It didn't come through my ears. I felt it behind my sternum, in the space where my heartbeat lives, as though the salt itself had dissolved past my tongue and was speaking directly into the meat of me.

My hand didn't shake. Nine years of training saw to that; I could hold a spoon steady while my throat closed around henbane. But every hair on my arms stood up under my sleeves, and somewhere very far away, the east gate courtyard went quiet and gray, as if the world had stepped back to give this moment room.

"Is something wrong, mistress taster?" The younger keeper. Watching me now. Both of them watching me.

"No," I said. My voice came out flat and professional, which I counted as a small miracle. "The salt is clean. Finest I've tested."

They nodded and began hauling the barrels toward the pantry stairs, and I stood there with the taste still blooming on my tongue, listening to an echo that was already fading.

*Come home.*

I'd heard voices before. Every taster does, eventually. Certain poisons sing on the way down—there's an ergot strain that makes you hear bells, a mushroom from the southern marshes that fills your skull with your mother's voice calling your name. I knew how to catalog them. Sound, source, duration, fade. Hallucination, chemical origin, four seconds, gone.

This one didn't fit the chart. There was no bitterness underneath it, no metallic tang of toxin. It tasted of nothing but clean salt and cold stone, and it hadn't faded so much as paused, the way a singer pauses between verses.

I spat into the testing basin, rinsed my mouth three times with well water, and wrote *pure, superior grade, no adulterants* in the ledger with a hand that only shook on the last word.

That evening I ate plain bread in my room and didn't salt it, and lay awake listening to my own heartbeat, wondering what else might be listening back.

* * *

The kitchens at night are a different country.

By day they roar—forty cooks and scullions and spit-turners shouting over the clatter, the air thick enough to chew. By night, after the king's supper has gone up and the last pots are scraped, they empty down to embers and shadow, and the only souls left awake are the ones with reasons to hide there.

I had my reason: the bread ovens stayed warm until dawn, and warm stone eases an ache I carry in my right hand from an old poisoning—a nerve thing, the physicians called it, permanent, the body remembering what was done to it long after the poison itself is gone. On bad nights I'd sit on the oven bench with my hand against the bricks like a cat at a hearth and let the heat work its slow argument.

On the night after the salt, I came down expecting an empty kitchen.

Tam Voss was praying.

He knelt in the middle of the flour-dusted floor with his back to me, a big man gone soft around the middle from years of tasting his own cooking, his hands folded so tightly the knuckles showed white through the flour. He was facing the old bread oven—the original one, the huge domed relic from before the palace expansion, bricked shut decades ago because nobody baked the old way anymore—and he was saying words I hadn't heard since I was a child on the border.

Not the formal prayers. Not the ones the chaplain mumbled over state occasions, all incense and obligation. These were the border prayers, the kitchen prayers, the ones grandmothers whispered over rising dough and sick children. The ones that named the old gods by their small names.

*Halven of the Hearth. Halven Who Waits. Halven Who Was Before.*

Nobody spoke that name. Not because it was forbidden—there'd never been an edict—but because the new faith had come through a century ago with its single god and its tidy doctrines, and the old names had simply dried up like wells, one by one, until everyone agreed without deciding that they'd never existed. Tam Voss was fifty if he was a day. He couldn't have learned those prayers anywhere living.

"Halven," he said again, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, big man kneeling in flour like a boy, "Halven, I'm sorry. We let you starve. We forgot to feed you and you starved, and now—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "Now the crops came up overnight. Old Marta's boy, fevered three weeks, sat up whole on Thursday morning. The priests say it's the new god's mercy, but the new god never answered anyone I ever knew, and Halven—" His hands unclenched, opened, palms up, offering. "Halven, if it's you. If you've come back. I'm here. I remember."

And the kitchen answered him.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only discipline I have left. I did not see anything. I did not hear a voice, not exactly. What happened was this: the banked coals in the sealed oven—I could see them through the iron grate, dull orange, ordinary—brightened. All at once, evenly, like a lung filling. The warmth against my aching hand deepened by degrees, gentle as a blanket drawn up. And the whole room acquired a quality I can only call attention, the way a room changes when someone enters it, when the air itself decides to listen.

The flour on the floor stirred. Not blown—there was no draft, every door and shutter sealed against the cold. It lifted in a slow spiral around Tam's knees, a ghost-white ring turning like the year turns, and settled again.

Tam laughed. That was the worst part. This enormous broken-sounding laugh of joy, wet at the edges, and he bowed his head over his open hands and said, "Thank you. Thank you. I'll bring the first loaf. First loaf's always yours, you know that, you always—that's how it goes, first loaf for Halven—" and he was crying now, freely, a man reunited with something he'd mourned too long to hope for.

I should have announced myself. That's what a sensible person would have done: coughed, shifted, let him know he had an audience, given him the dignity of choosing to be seen. Instead I stood in the doorway with my hand against the doorframe and watched, because some animal part of me understood that whatever was in this room with us, I did not yet know whether it was safe to interrupt.

*Come home,* the salt had said. And now I watched a god come home to a kitchen, and the wonder of it filled me up like light fills a jar—

—and then, beneath the wonder, cold.

Because I was a taster. Because nine years of swallowing death had taught me the lesson that governs my whole life: *anything strong enough to help is strong enough to harm, and the dose is the only difference.* The same leaf heals and kills. The same wine toasts and poisons. And the same presence that warmed an old cook's grief could, if it chose, do something else entirely, and I had no idea what it wanted, or how large it was, or why it had waited a hundred years to answer.

The crops rising overnight. The dying healed. Everyone at court had celebrated the news like weather, like luck. But luck doesn't whisper *come home* through consecrated salt, and gods don't return from a century of silence without a reason, and reasons that size are never comfortable for the people standing nearby.

The warmth faded. The coals dimmed to their ordinary orange. Tam Voss knelt alone in his ring of flour, wiping his face with a forearm, leaving pale streaks through the dust, and I slipped backward out of the doorway and up the servants' stair with my heart going hard against my ribs.

I told myself I'd imagined it. I was very good at telling myself things; it's most of what immunity is.

But halfway up the stair I stopped, because I'd remembered something. Small. A detail from the delivery yard that had slid off me at the time and now came back with edges.

The older shrine-keeper, when he handed over the manifests, had asked me one question beyond the usual. He'd leaned close, under cover of the barrel noise, and said, low and quick, like a man passing contraband:

"You taste for the king, yes? Everything he eats, everything he drinks—you take it into your body first?" I'd confirmed it, puzzled, assuming some bureaucratic point. And he'd gripped my wrist—gripped it, a stranger, a shrine-man daring to touch the royal taster—and said, "Then you're already drinking from the cup. Be careful what the cup holds. And mistress—if anything whispers to you, don't answer. Not once. Not kindly. Not ever. Promise me."

I'd promised, the way you promise madmen on roads, to make them let go of your wrist.

Tonight I stood in the dark of the servants' stair with the ghost-warmth of a returned god still on my skin, and understood that I had just heard the whisper myself—and worse, that I'd spent the whole evening wanting to answer it. Wanting it the way a starving girl wants bread. *Come home*, it had said, and some buried part of me, the border child, the girl whose grandmother's prayers those were, had leaned toward that voice like a plant toward a window.

Tomorrow I would test the rest of the shipment grain by grain. Tomorrow I would start writing down dates—the crops, the healings, the salt, the cook—and find the pattern underneath. Tomorrow I would ask Tam Voss, carefully, over the breakfast breads, who taught him those prayers and whether anyone else remembered them.

But tonight, one more thing, and it's the thing that sent me to bed with the lamp lit.

When I'd backed out of that kitchen doorway, when the warmth had faded and Tam's laughter had gone quiet—at the very last moment, just before the stair swallowed me—I had felt it turn its attention from him to me. Briefly. The way a diner pauses between courses.

It knew I was there.

And somewhere far below, faint as a coal's last glow, I heard it say—not *come home* this time, but something else entirely, something patient and pleased, in a voice like a host greeting a guest it has been expecting for nine years:

*"Ah. The taster. She'll do."*
