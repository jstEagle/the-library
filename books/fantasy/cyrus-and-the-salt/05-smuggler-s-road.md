## 5. Smuggler's Road

The summons came at dawn, folded inside a laundry list, because that was how Sera Vane's office communicated with people it didn't want seen talking to itself. *Grain shipment from Thornfell. Reports of contamination. Ride out, meet the source, confirm or deny. Come back quiet.*

Mara read it twice, burned it in the kitchen fire with the tongs she used for toasting spice, and went to pack.

Nine years she had kept a bag ready under the loose floorboard—next to the map, rolled in oilcloth, inked with every goat track and collapsed mine shaft and smuggler's cut through the Thornfell range that a border girl could learn before the crown hired her tongue. The bag held two shirts, dried meat, a vial of universal antidote she'd brewed herself and trusted more than any physician, and forty silver crowns sewn into the hem. She had never once used the bag. It existed for the day the palace stopped being survivable.

Today it was going outside the walls for an errand. That felt like lending your escape route to a stranger.

She told no one where she was going, which was standard. Tasters traveled light and unannounced; a food-taster's face was not famous but it was known in certain kitchens, and there were men who'd pay to know which dishes the king's poisoner had already cleared. She rode out through the east postern before the bells, leading a mule that looked too poor to rob, wearing a coat that had been good ten years ago.

The city let her go the way water lets go of a leaf. Nobody watched. Nobody ever watched Mara Lockridge. She had spent a decade making herself into something the eye slid past—a servant's stoop, a taster's gray cloak, a face arranged into permanent mild boredom—and it worked so well some days she wasn't sure she could be seen even if she wanted to be.

She did not want to be. Wanting things was how you died in a palace.

* * *

The Thornfell road was three days of climbing switchbacks and pretending the air didn't taste different.

It did taste different. That was the thing nobody warned you about when they romanticized mountains—the smell came first, pine and cold stone and snowmelt, and then the taste arrived underneath it, mineral and clean, like drinking from the world's own cup. Mara had grown up on that taste. She had swallowed palace cooking for nine years and forgotten, mostly, that food was supposed to have a flavor that wasn't sauce.

She also knew, professionally, what poisoned grain smelled like. Ergot had a sourness like wet rye left in a dark room. Arsenic dust carried a faint metallic ghost that most tongues missed and hers catalogued automatically, the way a musician hears a flat note. She spent the climb sampling wild water at every stream, chewing a leaf of this and a berry of that, running her tongue over the country like a hand over a familiar face.

That was when she found the first fire.

It sat in a hollow below the pass road—an old shrine, one of the waystone shrines the mountain folk had kept before the crown absorbed their gods into the state liturgy and then quietly starved the state liturgy of everything but taxes. Mara remembered these shrines as ruins. She'd camped in three of them over the years, dry-stone shelters with their god-stones worn smooth and faceless, good for getting out of the rain.

This one had a fire burning in front of it. A woman stood at the fire with her sleeves rolled up, feeding it pinewood, and the god-stone had been scrubbed clean of moss, and someone had traced its worn features again with fresh chalk so that it had a face for the first time in decades.

Mara should have ridden past. Invisibility was a discipline; you did not break it to gawk.

She slowed the mule anyway. And the woman turned, saw her, and did the last thing Mara expected.

She smiled. Wide, unguarded, glad.

"Traveler," the shrine-keeper called. "Come and warm yourself. We've got broth."

"I'm for the high passes," Mara said, which was true and deflecting at once.

"All the more reason." The woman ladled broth from a pot without waiting for an answer, and here was the second strange thing: she made no move toward a blessing over it. No invocation, no gesture at the four points. Just food, offered plainly. "You look like you've come a long way on short rations."

Mara dismounted. Later she would tell herself it was professional caution—never refuse offered food, taste everything, know your country—but the truth was simpler. The broth smelled like childhood.

She tasted it before she drank, automatically, and the shrine-keeper watched her do it and nodded as if that too were a courtesy.

"Careful tongue," the woman said. "Good. You'll want one, up there."

"Why?" Mara asked. "What's up there?"

The shrine-keeper looked up the valley, at the white shoulders of Thornfell going gold in the morning, and her expression was something Mara had no category for. Joy with a wound under it.

"Answers," she said. "After thirty years, the gods are answering again."

* * *

She heard the stories all the way up the mountain, and each one was worse than the last, if worse meant harder to dismiss.

At the mining village of Colder's Rest, a boy of six who'd been born with his heart on the wrong side of a fever—Mara had passed through that village two years ago and seen him propped in a doorway, blue-lipped, breathing in increments—was carrying firewood. Running with it. His mother talked about it to strangers in the road, unprompted, hands shaking.

At the third switchback above the tree line, a shepherd walking down told her the flock losses had stopped. Not lessened. Stopped. Wolves were still wolves, he said, but now the ewes lambed in weather that should have killed them, and every lamb lived, and he wasn't grateful in the soft way people were grateful for luck. He was grateful the way a man is grateful to a creditor. He said the word *Hirven* as he said it—the old name of the mountain god, a name Mara hadn't heard since she was small enough to be shushed for asking about it—and he said it like setting coin on a table.

"You shouldn't say that name on the open road," Mara heard herself tell him.

The shepherd just looked at her with mild, sorry eyes. "Why not? He's listening now. First time in my whole life anyone's said that."

That was the sentence that stayed with her up through the passes: *he's listening now.* For thirty years the old gods of Thornfell had been a dead language, museum pieces, names ground off the waystones by decree and neglect. Mara's own grandmother had still whispered to them over the cookfire, one palm flat on the earth, and Mara had inherited the habit the way you inherit a limp—something done low and fast and never explained. But nothing had ever answered. You whispered to the dark because your mother's mother had, and the dark kept its silence like a debt.

Now the dark was paying out. Crops rising overnight. Fevers breaking. A dead language walking around in living mouths.

And somewhere behind all of it, a cold small voice that had survived nine years at court kept saying: *nothing answers prayers for free.*

* * *

Her informant was waiting where informants always waited, in the burned-out shell of the High Hollow waystation, two days up—a smuggler's rest with a roof half gone and walls thick enough to hold warmth, and a view down both approaches. Dags was sixty and shaped like a knot in wood and had been moving untaxed goods through Thornfell since before Mara was born. He'd taught her three of the passes on her old map. He owed her, and she owed him, and between those two weights sat a working trust that had never once required sentiment.

He was not alone, and that was the first wrong note.

Three other men sat by the fire, mountain men by their boots and their silence, and none of them looked at her the way men looked at the crown's people. They looked at her the way men look at a priest.

"Mara." Dags rose, took her arm, walked her a few steps toward the door. His voice was pitched low and urgent, and underneath the urgency was something she had never heard from him. Fear. Not of her. Of the men behind him. "The grain. You're here about the grain?"

"Contaminated shipment headed downslope. Court wants confirmation."

"It's confirmed." He rubbed his jaw. "Ergot, and worse than ergot—there's ash mixed in. Shrine ash. Someone's salting the grain with burnt offerings and it's gone bad in the sacks. I've got forty measures sitting at Kell's Ford that will kill a village if it's milled. That part's honest trade news, take it back, use my name."

"And the other part?" Because there was always another part, with Dags.

He glanced at the three men by the fire. One of them had laid something on the floor between himself and the flames: a strip of cloth embroidered with a spiral, the mark of the high shrines, stitched by hand. An offering. Laid out in a smuggler's den like tribute.

"They want you to carry something," Dags said. "Downhill. To the palace."

"Dags. I'm a servant. I carry dishes."

"You're the king's taster." His eyes came up and held hers, and there was no apology in them. "You stand next to the throne every night. You're the closest thing to the king's ear that walks up this mountain. And these people—" he lowered his voice further "—these people believe the mountain god woke up. They believe it because their sheep stopped dying and their kids stopped coughing. And they want to give thanks properly, the old way, and they need the crown to allow it. Publicly. Formally. So they've written a petition, and they need it carried by someone the court can't accuse of sedition. Someone invisible."

The word landed harder than he could have known. He'd meant it as a compliment. Nine years of craft, summarized by a smuggler as a job qualification.

"Give it to a merchant," she said. "Give it to a priest. There are dozens of priests suddenly, I hear."

"There's exactly one priestess," Dags said, "up at the First Salt shrine, and she won't come down. Says the mountain comes to no one. And the merchants won't carry it because the last man who spoke up for the old rites in a market square was arrested within the hour—Sera Vane's people are watching every road for exactly this." He paused. "They're not watching you. Nobody watches you, Mara. That's worth money, what you've got. Worth more than money."

*Invisible,* she thought, and couldn't tell anymore whether the word tasted like safety or like burial.

The three men rose. The oldest came forward, holding a packet wrapped in oilcloth—the petition, sealed with wax stamped by a signet ring she recognized from twenty years ago, from a time when mountain elders still had the right to use such rings. He held it out in both hands.

"We ask nothing for ourselves," the old man said. "Only that the king permit the fires. Let us light the shrine fires openly and pray to Hirven by name, as our fathers did. That's the whole of it. Read it, if you like. There's no treason in it. There's only thanks."

"Thanks for what?" Mara heard herself ask, though she'd been collecting the answer for two days.

The old man's face opened like a door. "For coming back. For answering. Lady, we prayed for thirty years into a hole in the sky, and three moons ago the sky started talking back." He pressed the packet toward her, gently, the way you offer food to someone you suspect hasn't eaten. "You have a careful tongue, Dags says. Then taste this too. Taste what's in the air up here. It's grace. It's grace, and it's real, and we will kneel to whatever king lets us keep it."

Mara looked at the packet. Looked at the offering cloth by the fire, at the men who moved goods illegally through the dark for a living and had laid out embroidery for a god.

Every instinct she owned said *take it to Sera Vane, turn it in, stay invisible, stay alive.* Every instinct, and beneath the instincts, faint as a coal under ash, something older—her grandmother's palm flat on the earth, whispering to the dark.

She took the packet. "I make no promises what the court does with it."

"Just carry it," the old man said. "Carrying's enough. Someone has to bring the mountain's words to the low country. That's how it's always been. That's why the roads exist."

* * *

She camped that night in a shrine.

Not the chalk-faced one from the way up—one higher, smaller, a beehive of dry stone at the shoulder of the pass, with a god-stone inside worn nearly featureless. She'd slept in it once, seven years ago, running a message for Dags through early snow. Then it had been a ruin with a rock in it. Now the rock had fresh butter smeared across its brow—old rite, older than writing—and someone had left a clay cup beside it, filled with clear water, offered to whatever came by.

Mara sat with her back to the wall, the petition under her hand, and did not sleep.

The trouble was that she kept almost praying.

Not deliberately. It rose in her the way it rises in anyone alone at altitude with a long drop and a short life: fragments. *Let the grain be the worst of it. Let the court leave the fires alone. Let me get down the mountain.* Small asks, reflex asks, the mental litter of a frightened animal—and each time she caught herself mid-thought and strangled it, and each time felt absurd for strangling it. Who cared? The gods had been mute her whole life. Whispering in a ruin couldn't matter.

Except the shepherd had said *he's listening now*, and the shrine-keeper had said *answers*, and nine years of tasting had taught Mara one law above every other law:

Nothing that powerful ever feeds you for free. You just haven't seen the bill yet.

Somewhere past midnight, near sleep at last, she heard footsteps on the pass trail below—many feet, unhurried, singing. Low voices, a walking song, one of the old harvest rounds that hadn't been sung aloud in her lifetime except in whispers. She eased to the doorway with her knife out and watched a line of pilgrims come up the moonlit trail: a dozen of them, farmers and miners and one child on a man's shoulders, all climbing through freezing dark toward the high shrines, singing softly, faces turned up.

They passed below her ruin without seeing her. Of course they didn't see her. Nothing saw her; that was her whole design.

But as the last singer went by, an old woman at the rear of the line stopped, lifted her head as if catching a scent, and looked directly up at the doorway where Mara stood in shadow. Looked straight at her, for the length of one breath, and smiled—not at her, Mara told herself, not *at* her, at the shrine, at the night, at the listening dark—and then walked on after her people, still singing.

Mara stood in the cold doorway a long time, the petition heavy in her hand, doing sums she didn't like.

Thirty years of silence. Three moons of miracles. Thirteen turns of the moon until the astrologers' unnamed omen came full. And now a mountain full of ordinary people climbing willingly into the dark to feed something that fed them back, while down in the palace a king flinched at the names of old gods and his shadow lagged a half-second behind his body.

She had come up for poisoned grain. She was going down carrying a prayer.

By the time she reached the low country, she had decided one thing only: she would deliver the petition, yes—but she was not giving it to the court. Not yet. First she needed to understand what, exactly, had woken up in her mountains. And there was exactly one place to start looking, and it was the last place her instincts approved: the palace kitchens, where a head cook named Bram had been humming, lately, tunes Mara had only ever heard in whispers.

She found out later how badly she'd chosen her starting point. By then it was too late.
