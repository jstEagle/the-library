## 6. The Devouring Kind

Sera Vane kept her ledgers the way other people kept knives: close, oiled, and always within reach.

The one open before her now was bound in grey calfskin, unmarked, and contained nothing but dates and names. That was the point. Anyone who broke into her office—and three people had tried in nine years; two were buried under the orchard wall—would find a spymaster's records of grain shipments and border tolls. Boring enough to survive discovery. The real ledger lived behind her left eye, in the place where she stored things she could never write down.

Tonight's entry was short.

*Father Anselm, shrine-keeper at Lowmark, last seen 4th day. Brother Cade, itinerant priest, missed his circuit. Sister Maren of the Riverhouse—gone.*

Three priests in eleven days. All of them quiet men and women, the kind who mended roofs and argued about tithes. And all of them, in the weeks before they vanished, had said the same thing to the same people: *the prayers are working again. Someone should ask why.*

Sera dipped her pen, thought better of it, and set it down.

She stood at the window of her tower office instead, watching the palace below. The evening shift of guards changed at the gate. Lamplighters worked their way along the colonnade. In the great hall, servants laid the king's table for a feast that had no announced occasion—which was itself an answer, if you knew how to read it. Aldric had held four feasts in two months. Before this spring, he'd held four in two years.

A king does not feast when he is content. A king feasts when he is hungry.

That phrase had come to Sera unbidden three nights ago, waking her at the hour she privately called the wolf hour, and it had not left since. She distrusted phrases that arrived on their own. They were either wisdom or infection, and it took years to tell which.

Below, crossing the colonnade with her head down and her step unhurried, went the food-taster. Mara Lockridge. Grey dress, grey expression, carrying the little ledger in which she recorded every dish she tasted for the king's table.

Sera watched her the way she watched everything: without appearing to, and completely.

* * *

The trouble had started, as most trouble did, with arithmetic.

In the first week of the answered prayers—the week the barley came up green in the drought fields, the week a fevered child in the lower city walked out of a deathbed that three physicians had signed—Sera had done what she always did with miracles. She looked for the fraud.

There was always a fraud. In twenty-two years of intelligence work, she had catalogued forty-one miracle claims in the capital alone. Thirty-eight had been con artists, harvest-failures misremembered, or diseases that simply broke early. Two had been political theater staged by ambitious clergy. One had been a genuine mystery she'd never solved, and it still bothered her at night, like a tooth.

But this was different, because this wasn't one miracle. It was weather. Reports came up from every province at once: crops rising overnight, wells refilling, the lame walking. And the pattern underneath the reports was not random. The answered prayers clustered around the crown's roads. Around the king's progresses. Around places where crowds gathered to see Aldric pass.

Devotion, distributed geographically, produced results. Devotion elsewhere produced less.

Sera had built a map. She was very good with maps. She'd once tracked a smuggling ring through six provinces using nothing but the price drift of lamp oil. This map used pins: white pins for answered prayers, black pins for the vanished priests. When she finished, she stood back and looked at it for a long time.

The white pins ringed the capital like a tide coming in.

The black pins sat inside them, at the shrines.

It was almost elegant. Almost beautiful. Whoever—or whatever—was answering the kingdom's prayers was doing it the way a farmer sows a field: thickly near the house, thinly at the edges. And the only people who had noticed the pattern, the only ones asking *why* instead of *praise*, were disappearing at a rate that would have been statistically invisible if Sera hadn't been the sort of woman who counted everything.

Three priests. Eleven days. At that rate, by the thirteenth turn of the moon—the omen the astrologer had named with such trembling ceremony—there would be no one left in the kingdom trained to notice anything at all.

Except, Sera reflected, turning from the window, the people whose profession was noticing.

Which brought her, as everything lately seemed to bring her, back to Mara Lockridge.

* * *

Here is what Sera knew about food-tasters, because knowing the shape of every office in the palace was her trade:

They were chosen young, usually poor, usually orphaned—children nobody would miss, dosed with graduated poisons until their bodies learned the alphabet of death. Most lasted five years. Their tongues scarred, their nerves frayed, and they retired to some damp cottage to die slowly of the accumulated harm, pensioned off with a silence clause. It was not a cruel system, exactly. It was worse than cruel. It was a system that had never once been examined, because examining it would require admitting what the crown considered a child's life worth.

Mara Lockridge had lasted nine. That was unusual. What was more unusual was her reporting.

Every taster kept a log: dish, ingredients, taste-notes, verdict. Over the years Sera had read hundreds of these logs, skimmed mostly, filed away. Tasters were chatty in ink even when silent in person. They complained about the cook. They noted when the salt was damp. They developed little superstitions—this spice from the eastern trader was always suspect, that vintage gave false bitterness. Their handwriting drifted. Their opinions drifted. They were human, and their pages showed it.

Mara Lockridge's logs never varied.

Not steady—Sera knew the difference. Steady meant disciplined. Never varying meant something else entirely. Nine years of entries in the same hand, the same dry cadence, the same verdicts rendered in the same seven-word formula: *No adulteration detected. Fit for the king's table.* Through two assassination attempts she'd flagged (correctly). Through the year the kitchens changed hands and half the court got flux from bad shellfish. Through the arrival of a new under-cook whom Sera's own agents had later caught selling palace schedules to the hill clans—a man Mara had described, in her log, exactly once, as *new under-cook, adequate.*

Nine years. Adequate.

At first Sera had filed this under competence. Then, as the priests began to vanish and the prayers began to land, she had taken out all nine years of Lockridge logs and read them straight through, one after another, in a single sitting, and by the end of it her hands were cold.

Because there was one more thing in those logs. Something so small she'd missed it the first dozen times.

Once a month, regular as rent, Mara tasted a shipment of consecrated salt from the mountain shrines. Standard procedure; the salt went into the king's ceremonial dishes, and everything ceremonial was tasted twice. Every other taster in the palace's history had noted the same thing about consecrated salt: it tasted of nothing. Clean, mineral, blank. There were decades of entries confirming it.

Mara's entries said otherwise. Not dramatically. Just a single word, once a month, in the taste-notes column, in the same hand, in the same dry voice:

*Bitter.*

And then, beginning this spring—beginning precisely when the prayers started landing—

*Bitter. Faintly sweet beneath.*

Sera sat back from the logs and looked at her wall of pins for a long time.

Either the taster's tongue was failing, which mattered, because that tongue was the last mechanical check between the king and a poisoned cup. Or the taster's tongue was working perfectly, and the salt itself had changed, which mattered far more, because it would mean the old gods' rites—the rites the crown had quietly starved for a generation—were no longer inert. Something had gotten into the consecrated salt. Something that tasted bitter to one woman in the whole palace, and faintly sweet beneath, like fruit going over.

And there was exactly one person alive who could taste the difference.

* * *

She started the file that night. Grey calfskin, unmarked, one page.

*MARA LOCKRIDGE. Taster, 9 yrs. Origin: Thornfell border, village unnamed in intake papers—flagged. No family listed. No debts. No vices found. No friends found. Correspondence: none. This is not normal. Everyone corresponds with someone.*

Then, beneath, the questions she intended to answer:

*Where does she go on her rest-days? (Answer: nowhere visible. Nobody vanishes into nowhere for nine years without a road. Find the road.)*

*Who taught her to write reports that never vary? (Nobody teaches that. That is self-taught. What happened to her that she decided variation was dangerous?)*

*What does "bitter, faintly sweet beneath" mean?*

And at the bottom, the question she wrote last, pressing hard enough to score the page:

*If the crown is what's drinking the kingdom's faith—watch the one servant who puts everything in her mouth and tells the truth about none of it.*

She stopped. Read it back. Crossed out *tells the truth about none of it* and wrote instead: *tells a truth no one has thought to ask for.*

That was fairer. Sera prided herself on fairness the way other women prided themselves on piety. Fairness was just accuracy applied to people, and accuracy was the only god she'd ever served.

But fairness cut both ways, and she knew it. If the salt had changed, then the taster had noticed before the spymaster. If the taster had noticed, she had either reported it upward—in which case someone above Sera had buried it, a possibility she did not enjoy considering—or she had kept it in her own mouth, alone, for months, the way she apparently kept everything.

A woman like that was either the best asset in the palace or the worst enemy in it. In Sera's experience the two categories overlapped more often than her predecessors had liked to admit.

* * *

Three days later, she made her first move, and it was small, because first moves should be.

She arranged for the kitchen deliveries to be rerouted—one clerk's signature, one bored guard reassigned—so that the monthly salt shipment from the mountain shrines would pass through the gate at dusk instead of dawn, in full view of the colonnade. Then she waited in her window and watched who looked.

Most of the court didn't look. Salt was salt. The queen's ladies glanced up, decided it was dull, returned to their embroidery. The captain of the gate checked the manifest and yawned.

Mara Lockridge stopped.

Not long. Four breaths, maybe five. She was crossing the colonnade with her evening ledger, and she slowed—not obviously, never obviously—and her eyes went to the salt crates the way a hound's nose goes to wind. Then she continued on, head down, step unhurried, and was gone into the kitchens.

Four breaths. Sera wrote it down that night, in the grey book, in code.

*Subject registered the shipment. Subject knows what changed in it. Subject is pretending not to know. Question: is she protecting herself, or protecting it?*

She underlined the question twice, then sat for a while with the pen idle, listening to the tower wind.

Because here was the thing she had not written down, the thing that lived behind her left eye with the rest of the unwritable truths: Sera Vane had spent her whole career believing that every darkness in the kingdom had a human face. Greed, ambition, fear, lust—the four horses she rode daily. She had broken conspiracies built on each of them. She knew their gaits, their smells, the way they lied.

But the pattern on her pin-map had no human gait. It spread like appetite. It concentrated where feeding was richest. It removed observers with the tidy patience of a thing that had removed observers before and expected to remove them again. And it wore, if her worst suspicion was right, a crown.

Her informants in the lower temples had given her one fragment before the last of them went quiet—a wine-stained scrap of a sermon, preached by a priestess who was now among the missing, and copied out by a clerk who didn't understand what he'd heard:

*"You ask why the gods are generous again. Children, generosity is a mouth. Ask what it is eating."*

Sera had read that scrap perhaps forty times.

She did not believe in gods. Belief was a tool she issued to others and withheld from herself, like the poison rings hidden in her signet and the knife in her boot. But belief was not required for the arithmetic. Three priests gone. Prayers clustering like flies. A king feasting four times in two months. Consecrated salt turned bitter, faintly sweet beneath, and one grey woman in the whole palace who could taste it and said nothing.

Generosity is a mouth.

Sera closed the grey ledger, snuffed the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark of her tower, listening to the wind worry the shutters. Below, the palace glowed with feast-light. Somewhere in that light, a king was raising a cup, and a thousand throats were singing his health, and whatever wore him was drinking it in.

Tomorrow she would begin pulling Mara Lockridge's threads properly. The border village. The rest-days. The nine years of nowhere. She would do it gently, patiently, the way she did everything—and when she finally sat across a table from that grey, unvarying woman, she would offer her the only currency Sera had ever needed: the truth about what happens to people who know too much and hold it alone.

They hang, usually. Or they vanish, like priests.

Or, Sera thought, blowing out the last candle, they learn to bargain.

She was already composing the opening line of the approach as she climbed into bed—she composed approaches the way composers composed tunes, in the bath, in the saddle, at the wolf hour—and the line was this:

*You have tasted something bitter, taster. So have I. Let us compare notes before one of us is on the missing list.*

She slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of pins on a map closing inward like a ring of teeth, which was new.
