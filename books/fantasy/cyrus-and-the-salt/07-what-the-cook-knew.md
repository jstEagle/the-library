## 7. What the Cook Knew

The smell reached her before the shouting did.

Mara had spent nine years learning to read the palace by its smells—tallow and wet stone in the morning, roasting meat at midday, the green bite of cut herbs in the evening—and so she knew, before her feet were fully on the stairs down to the kitchens, that something was wrong. Not spoiled. Not burned. Wrong in a way she had no word for yet, a sweetness under everything, like fruit kept too long in a closed room.

She found the kitchen doors already open and a knot of scullery boys pressed against the far wall, white as uncooked dough. Nobody was screaming anymore. That was worse. The screaming had happened, apparently, and then stopped all at once, the way a bird stops singing.

Bramble Hodge—the head cook, Bramble, who had fed Mara her first real meal in this palace eleven years ago when she was nothing but a border girl with a scarred tongue and no name worth saying—was kneeling in the center of his own kitchen.

He was smiling.

That was the thing that put ice down Mara's spine, the thing she would still be seeing behind her eyes at midnight for weeks afterward: not the death, but the smile. It sat on his face like something placed there, patient and pleased, the smile of a man listening to music no one else could hear. His hands rested open on his thighs, palms up, in the posture of a man receiving a gift. Flour dusted his knuckles and forearms—he'd been baking, she realized. He'd been baking bread for the king's table, and somewhere between the kneading and the rising he had knelt down here on the cold flagstones and died with his face turned up toward the ceiling beams as if expecting them to open.

He wasn't wasted away. That was the second wrongness. Dead men in this kingdom lately came in two kinds: sickly and sudden. Bramble was neither. He looked *hollowed*. Like a fruit whose flesh had been eaten out through a hole too small to see, skin still round, still holding its shape, still faintly sweet-smelling—but empty inside. When one of the undercooks finally worked up the courage to touch his shoulder, the man swayed like a dried husk, and everyone stepped back at once.

"Nobody touches him," Mara said, and was surprised to hear her own voice come out flat and certain. "Nobody prays over him either. You hear me? No prayers. Not one."

They stared at her. A girl with flour on her boots and no business giving orders in Bramble's kitchen. But there was something in the way she said it—not hysteria, not grief, just the flat authority of a woman who tasted poison for a living and had learned that panic was a flavor you could refuse—that made them obey. The scullery boys filed out. The undercooks drifted after them. Within minutes she was alone with the body and the ovens, which still held their warmth, and the smell of that terrible sweetness.

She should have called someone. The steward, the guard captain, anyone. Instead she stood there in the quiet kitchen where she had eaten a hundred stolen meals, and she grieved the way she did everything: silently, efficiently, without letting it reach her face.

* * *

Bramble Hodge had been the closest thing to a friend the palace allowed her.

Not a friend, exactly. Friends required evenings, confidences, the dangerous luxury of being known. But Bramble had fed her. In the early years, when she was new and the other servants treated the taster like furniture that could die, he had always found extra for her plate. A heel of good bread. The end of the honey cake nobody claimed. Once, when she'd been sick for three days after swallowing something meant for the king—a slow arsenic that the physicians swore shouldn't have touched her, but did, because immunity is a wall with cracks in it—Bramble had sent up broth every day without being asked, and never once mentioned it after.

"You keep us honest," he'd told her once, flour to the elbows, laughing. "Whole court knows if the food's clean enough for Mara Lockridge, it's clean enough for a king." And then, quieter, in a different voice she hadn't understood at the time: "Though I wonder sometimes what else gets past you, girl. What's in the air. What's in the *prayers*."

She'd laughed at that. She remembered laughing at that, three weeks ago, an entire lifetime ago.

Now she knelt beside him on the flagstones, careful not to touch, and looked at his open hands again. Palms up. Receiving.

There was flour on his right palm, thick, fresh, and beneath the flour—she leaned close, closer than she wanted to—there were marks. Letters. He had written something on his palm in flour paste before it dried, or written it in flour itself, dusted into the creases of his skin the way bakers dust a board.

Mara took out her knife and scraped the flour carefully onto a scrap of parchment she always carried, folding it twice, tucking it into her bodice. Then, because the note might not be the whole of it, she searched the kitchen the way she'd been taught to search a meal: methodically, without hope, expecting nothing.

She found it in the flour bin. Of course she found it in the flour bin. Bramble had been a cook for forty years; if he'd wanted to hide something from the palace, he would have hidden it where only another cook would think to look, and even then only a cook who knew his habits. The bin's false bottom lifted out, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with age, was a packet of papers.

The top sheet was in Bramble's hand, big and blunt, the letters of a man who wrote the way he cooked—generously, without ornament:

*It drinks the faithful. Do not pray where it can hear.*

Below that, more pages. Recipes, she thought at first—his handwriting, quantities and methods—but the recipes were wrong. Consecrated salt measured against hellebore. Prayers written backward. Notes on which shrines still answered and which had gone silent, dates going back years, decades, in two different hands. Someone had started this collection long before Bramble, and Bramble had inherited it the way cooks inherit everything: from the last person who held the job.

The last entry was dated four days ago.

*It heard me tonight. I felt it listening while I prayed for my daughter's fever to break. It answered. Her fever broke. God forgive me, it answered, and now I know what the answering costs, and I cannot stop praying anyway. It knows that. That is how it holds you.*

Mara sat down on the cold floor next to a dead man and read that line six times.

Her daughter. Bramble had a daughter in the river towns with a fever. He'd told Mara about it in passing, months ago, the way men told things they couldn't fix—her name was Tilly, she had her mother's temper, the physicians said wait and see. And Bramble, faithful old Bramble, who still crossed himself toward the mountain shrines when he thought no one saw, had done the one forbidden thing. He had prayed where it could hear.

And it had answered. And the answering had eaten him hollow from the inside out, and left him kneeling and smiling, and the whole court would call it a seizure or a bad heart or the will of whatever god was fashionable this season, and no one would ever know—

No one but her.

* * *

She took the papers back to her room and read them until the candle guttered, and what she understood by the end made her hands shake in a way poison never had.

The recipes weren't recipes for food. They were recipes for *protection*—half-remembered, half-mad, assembled across generations by people who had each glimpsed a piece of the truth and died before they could pass on the whole of it. Salt from the mountain shrines, laid in thresholds. Names spoken backward. And threaded through all of it, in margins and footnotes in five different hands, the same warning repeated like a refrain:

*It feeds on devotion. Starve it. Never pray aloud indoors. Never pray where the crown can hear.*

The crown. Every note circled back to the crown, to the king, to the throne room where the court knelt daily in ranks and poured out their love for Aldric like wine into a cup.

Mara thought of the king's shadow moving half a beat late. She thought of the flinch when the astrologer mentioned the old gods. She thought of the shipment of consecrated salt, and the whisper she'd heard in it that no one else had, and the way the whisper had sounded almost grateful.

*You taste truly,* it had seemed to say. *How rare.*

She got up and pried up the floorboard where her map lived—nine years of smuggler roads through Thornfell, her escape plan, her private country—and looked at it for a long moment. Then she didn't burn it. She folded it smaller instead, and tucked it into the oilcloth packet with Bramble's papers, because a woman who was about to start asking dangerous questions needed two things: a way out, and proof of what she'd found.

Before dawn she went back down to the kitchens one last time. The body had been taken away in the night; the flagstones had been scrubbed; the ovens were cold. Everything was ordinary except the silence where Bramble's voice used to be.

Mara stood in the doorway of his kitchen and let herself feel it—one breath, two, the full weight of a good man kneeling dead and smiling—then she closed the door on the feeling the way she'd been taught to close doors on everything soft.

Grief was a luxury. Resolve was a tool. She had spent nine years turning herself into a tool, and tools did not weep over flour bins.

But they remembered.

"Who taught you to pray, Bramble?" she asked the empty kitchen quietly. "And who taught you to be afraid of it?"

The ovens said nothing. But somewhere above her—up through the floors, up through the council chamber and the chapel and the throne room where a thousand devoted hearts beat in rhythm—something vast and patient shifted its weight, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound it finds interesting.

Mara felt it notice her, the way you feel weather change.

She pulled her collar up against a chill that had nothing to do with temperature, and walked out into the grey morning to find out who else in this palace had been praying—and who else had been answered.

The list, she suspected, would be short.

The survivors' list, shorter still.
