## 8. The Tenth Turn

The moon was ten days from its thirteenth turn, and Aldric knew it the way a drowning man knows the surface is getting farther away.

He knew it because he could still know things. That was the horror of it—that he hadn't been erased, only buried. The thing that wore his crown and signed his name and sat his body on his throne had left him exactly where he was: underneath. A cellar under a house where someone else kept the lights burning. He could hear everything through the floorboards. He could feel everything—the cold of the throne room stone through his own knees, the taste of wine he wasn't allowed to swallow, the weight of his own hand when it moved without him.

He had stopped screaming a long time ago. Screaming took strength, and strength was what the thing drank.

*Prayer,* it said sometimes, in a voice that was not a voice but a warmth spreading through his skull like spilled honey. *Listen to them pray.*

And Aldric listened, because he had no choice. All across Vareth, all across the lowlands and the river towns, people were praying again—old prayers, forbidden prayers, prayers to gods whose names hadn't been spoken aloud in a generation. And every prayer rose like smoke and found its way to the thing in his head, and every prayer made it stronger, and every prayer made him smaller.

He had been a king once. He tried to hold on to that. He had been a king who rode his own borders and knew his stewards by name and could read a grain ledger faster than his chancellors. He had a daughter—

He reached for her face and found nothing.

That was the worst of it. Not the throne stolen, not the body borrowed, not even the years—he didn't know how many years—of watching his own hands sign edicts he'd never read. The worst was that somewhere in the last decade the thing had eaten his daughter's face out of his memory, carefully, the way a child eats the middle out of a pastry and leaves the crust. He remembered that he had a daughter. He remembered loving her. He remembered a small hand in his, and a laugh like bells, and none of it attached to anything anymore. Just love with no face, drifting in the dark like a lantern over deep water.

*She prays for you,* the presence murmured, savoring it. *Every morning. Such devotion. Such a devoted little queen.*

Isolde. His wife prayed for him every morning at the dawn altar, publicly, beautifully, and each prayer went down the thing's throat like warm wine. He had understood that only recently—that her devotion was not comfort but feeding, that the more she loved him in public the deeper he sank. Whether she knew what she was doing, whether she had known all along, he couldn't tell anymore. He no longer trusted anything he thought. His thoughts came to him secondhand, warmed over, like food someone else had already tasted.

*Taster,* he thought, and the word surfaced from nowhere, from some deep stratum the thing hadn't bothered to dig through yet. Why that word? Why now?

Because somewhere below the honey-warmth of the god's attention, in the part of him that was still Aldric of Vareth, something had begun to count.

---

It counted in minutes now.

In the early years there had been nothing. Then, occasionally, seconds—a blink that was his, a breath taken at his own rhythm. Lately, as the moon fattened toward its thirteenth turn, the thing grew drunk and careless. It fed so heavily, so constantly, on the rising tide of answered prayer that it left gaps in its grip, the way a drunk king leaves doors unlocked.

Three days ago he had gotten eleven seconds. He'd used them to weep, which was wasteful, and he'd hated himself for it.

But today—today there would be a council meeting. And council meetings were long, and dull, and full of grain figures and border petitions, and the thing found them as tedious as any mortal king did. It sat in his skull and half-slept while chancellor after chancellor read aloud, digesting the morning's harvest of devotion from a hundred village altars, its attention pooled fat and slow.

Aldric lay beneath it and counted.

*Two hundred souls at the Wellshrine of Ansel gave thanks this morning,* the thing purred. *They wept. They knelt in the mud. Oh, they knelt so beautifully.*

*Fourteen days,* Aldric thought, pressing the words down flat where the thing wouldn't look for them. Fourteen days until the thirteenth turn, when the binding became permanent, when whatever remained of him would be pressed flat as a coin and spent forever. He didn't know how he knew the number. He simply did, the way you know a wound is mortal.

He needed three minutes. Three minutes of his hands being his hands.

He began, as he always began, by going still. Not fighting—fighting was noise, and noise drew attention. Stillness. He made himself small and quiet and boring, a dead coal in the ash of his own mind, and he waited for the thing to gorge itself toward sleep.

Outside, the council chamber filled. He felt his body settle into the high-backed chair at the head of the table. Felt Chancellor Ovett begin his drone about tariffs on Thornfell wool. Felt the thing's attention drift, heavy and honeyed, rolling over the morning's prayers like a man counting coins.

*One minute,* Aldric counted. *Two.*

His fingers twitched. His. For one glorious, terrifying instant, his right hand was his hand again, and it trembled against the armrest like a bird with a broken wing.

*Three.*

He opened his eyes.

The council chamber swam into focus—oak table worn pale at the elbows, tall windows, gray light, the faces of men who had served a stranger for years without knowing it. Chancellor Ovett mid-sentence. Lord Perren nodding along. And near the wall, the servants: a boy with a ewer, and a woman in palace gray with a tray, dark-haired, watchful, standing very still.

The taster. Mara Lockridge. The name arrived whole, unbidden, and Aldric nearly lost his grip on the moment out of sheer shock. He knew her. Nine years she had stood at his elbow tasting death in small doses, and he had never once thanked her, and now her name came back to him like a gift dropped through the floorboards of heaven.

There was no time. There was never time.

He moved his hand. It shook badly—the muscles unfamiliar, the nerves complaining—and he dragged the council ledger toward himself, pulled the quill from the inkwell, and wrote. Fast. Ugly. The letters cramped and slanting, the hand of a man writing with a rope around his wrists.

*THE CROWN IS NOT MINE. IT DRINKS THE FAITHFUL. THIRTEEN MOONS AND I AM GONE. FIND THE ONE WHO TASTES. SHE WILL KNOW THE SALT.*

He got as far as *SHE WILL KNOW* before the honey-voice stirred above him, puzzled, attentive, a sleeper turning over.

*What are you doing?*

Aldric slammed the ledger shut. His hand went slack. The chamber tilted away from him like a boat losing its mooring, and the warmth poured back in, thick and forgiving, smoothing everything over.

*You dreamed,* the presence said fondly. *Kings dream at their councils. It is nothing. Sleep, little king. The prayers are sweet today.*

The last thing he felt with his own senses was the scratch of the quill still between fingers gone foreign again, and the sound—faint, at the edge of hearing—of a serving woman setting down a wine cup very quietly beside his hand.

Then the honey closed over him, and the king of Vareth went back to being furniture.

* * *

Mara noticed the ledger before anyone else did, because noticing things no one asked her to notice was the only hobby her life allowed.

She had come in with the second wine service, moving through her routine: pour, taste, wait, count to sixty, pour again. The king had not looked at her. The king rarely looked at anyone. But when she set his cup down, his hand had twitched toward hers—not reaching, just twitching, like a man dreaming of grasping—and then he had lunged upright in his chair, hauled the ledger close, and written three lines in a scrawl that shook like a leaf in rain. Then his eyes had gone soft and distant again, and Chancellor Ovett had kept talking about wool tariffs as though the king of Vareth scribbling madly mid-council were the most natural thing in the world.

No one else had reacted. That was the frightening part. Lord Perren hadn't glanced up. Ovett hadn't paused. As if the court had long ago learned not to see anything the king did that didn't fit.

When the council emptied, Mara cleared the cups. She was nobody; nobodies could linger. She let her sleeve brush the ledger open.

Three lines, in a cramped, slanting hand:

*THE CROWN IS NOT MINE. IT DRINKS THE FAITHFUL. THIRTEEN MOONS AND I AM GONE. FIND THE ONE WHO TASTES. SHE WILL KNOW THE SALT.*

Her skin went cold from the scalp down.

She knew that handwriting. Not well—she was a food-taster, not an archivist—but nine years in a palace teaches you to read documents upside down across a desk. In her first year, bored and invisible in the record room where she waited out the evenings, she had sorted old ledgers, and King Aldric's hand had been on a decade of them: firm, level, confident, a ruler's hand. This was not that hand. This was that hand *dying*. Same bones, same slant, written by a man whose fingers were being held by someone else.

*Find the one who tastes. She will know the salt.*

She looked up. The king sat gazing out the window, his profile smooth and empty as a coin. His shadow fell long across the floor in the gray light—and Mara watched it, deliberately, for the length of ten heartbeats.

It lagged. Half a heartbeat behind his hand when he lifted the cup. Half a heartbeat behind when he turned his head. Like a shadow attached by a fraying string.

Mara set the ledger down exactly as she'd found it, picked up her tray, and walked out of the council chamber at the pace of a woman carrying nothing heavier than wine.

Inside, she was running.

* * *

That night, in the dark of her narrow room, Mara pried up the floorboard and took out her map.

Not to add to it. To look at it. Her nine years of smuggler roads and shrine passes and hidden fords, inked in her own tight script, the escape plan she'd built plank by plank since her first winter in the palace. She spread it across her knees and told herself she was checking routes, distances, the fastest line from the palace gate to the tree line.

She was lying to herself, and she knew it. She was looking for a reason to stay.

*It drinks the faithful.* The cook's whisper in the kitchen. The dead priest who'd smiled too much. The salt that spoke. The king's dying hand writing *find the one who tastes*—and how had he known? How did a king buried alive inside his own skull know that the taster mattered? Unless he didn't know. Unless it was a guess, a drowning man grabbing at anything. Or unless it was true, and she had been part of this all along without knowing it.

She thought of the founder of her office—Cyrus, the name every taster learned first, the man who'd invented the craft and vanished from history two generations later. She thought of consecrated salt, and why a king's last free thought would reach for it.

Under the floorboard, next to the map, was a small cloth bag of the mountain salt she'd bought from a peddler years ago and never used. She took it out and held it in her palm. It was quiet tonight. Ordinary salt. But it had whispered once, and things that whispered once could whisper again.

Somewhere above her, in the high tower, the king was being worn like a coat by something that ate worship. Somewhere in the kitchens, a good man had died kneeling and smiling. Somewhere in the mountains, fires were being lit at shrines that had stood cold for thirty years.

And the moon was ten days from its thirteenth turn.

Mara folded the map along its old creases, slid it back under the floorboard, and lay down without sleeping. She listened to the palace breathe around her—the guards' boots, the wind in the flags, the vast slow hunger overhead drinking its evening prayers—and she made herself a promise she didn't yet believe:

Tomorrow she would find out what Cyrus knew about salt.

And if the answer was what she feared—if the founder of her office had left anything behind worth finding—then the escape plan under her floorboard might finally get used.

Not for her.

For the man in the chair whose handwriting she recognized, and whose daughter's face he could no longer remember, and who had spent his last three minutes of freedom writing a message meant for no one in the world but her.
