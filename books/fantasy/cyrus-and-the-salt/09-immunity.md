## 9. Immunity

The first poison Mara ever swallowed was her own choice, and she had been proud of it for exactly four days.

She was nine years old, and the winter had come down off the Thornfell passes like something with teeth. She remembered that much clearly—the way hunger made everything sharp-edged and simple. Her mother had sold the good blanket in Harrow's Cross, then the pot, then the loom's iron fittings one bolt at a time until the loom was just wood, just useless, just a thing to burn. After the loom there was nothing left to sell except the obvious.

Mara had solved it the way starving children solve things: by making herself smaller than the problem. She ate pine bark. She ate boiled nettles that stung her throat raw. She ate a handful of rye that had gone black with ergot because throwing it away felt like an insult, and spent three days in the corner of the hut watching the walls breathe and her dead father walk through the door eleven times. On the fourth day she got up, shook, and decided that whatever else happened, she would never again be hungry enough to eat poison.

So when the recruiters came through Harrow's Cross in the spring—grey-cloaked men from the capital asking after girls with strong stomachs, offering bread on the spot, actual bread, white and soft as snowfall—she did not cry or cling. She took the bread. She ate half and hid the other half in her sleeve, because a girl who has starved does not ever fully trust a full plate again.

Her mother watched her go from the doorway of the hut and did not wave. That was the bargain they'd silently struck, the two of them: better a living daughter far away than a dead one close. Neither of them said it out loud. Neither of them cried where the other could see. Mara walked out of Harrow's Cross with half a loaf down her front and her chin up, and she told herself she was going to the palace to serve the king.

She was not so foolish as to believe she was going to be a maid.

* * *

The Taster's Office sat behind the palace kitchens like a tumor grown onto the side of something healthy—a low stone building with one window, three cots, and a locked cabinet that Master Corvus opened only with both keys, his and the steward's. There were four tasters when Mara arrived. By the time she understood what the office was for, there were three.

Corvus was an old man with a wine-stained smile and hands that shook only when they were empty. He had been tasting for thirty years and looked like a man who had been buried alive and had decided, somewhere in the dark, to make himself comfortable. He fed her the first week. Real food, constantly, relentlessly—eggs, cream, lamb in caper sauce, little honey cakes. When she asked why, he said, "Because we're going to take that body of yours, girl, and teach it a new language. I need to know what it sounded like healthy."

On the eighth day he opened the cabinet and took out a small brown vial, and Mara—who had survived ergot and bark and nettle-sting, who had hidden bread in her sleeve like other girls hid ribbons—looked at it and understood.

"You'll start small," Corvus said. "A drop in broth, no more than you'd get from a bad mushroom. You'll tell me everything you feel. Everything. The tongue is honest but stupid; it needs a narrator."

"And if it kills me?"

"Then you were too slow a learner," Corvus said, "and the kingdom needed someone quicker." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Sit down, girl. Eat your egg first. Always eat first. A poisoned gut on an empty stomach dies twice as fast, and I didn't buy all this cream for nothing."

She ate the egg. Then she drank the broth.

The drop was nothing—a bitterness at the back of the tongue, a warmth in the ears, ten minutes of her heartbeat sounding too loud, like footsteps following her. She narrated all of it. Corvus wrote it down in a ledger in a cramped hand, and when he finished he looked at her over the rim of the vial and nodded once, the way a man nods at a horse with good conformation.

"That," he said, "is the last easy day you get."

He was right.

* * *

They dosed her every third day for the first year.

There is no way to describe the curriculum of the Taster's Office to someone who hasn't lived it. Corvus was thorough the way famine is thorough. Bitter almond, so that thereafter the smell of marzipan made her gag for the rest of her life. Hemlock, which taught her the taste of cold rising up her own spine. Foxglove, which turned the world golden at the edges and made her heart stutter like a bird thrown against a window. Arsenic in trace amounts, weekly, for years, until her skin took on the faint grey undertone that palace ladies called "the taster's pallor" behind their fans. Nightshade, once, badly—an accident of dosage—and she woke three days later with Corvus asleep sitting up beside her cot and a new scar on her tongue where she'd bitten through it convulsing.

That scar was the first. There would be others. Her tongue became a document of her education: the smooth pale line from the nightshade, the rough patch on the left side where chronic dosing of a corrosive mineral tonic had worn the papillae down to velvet, the slight crookedness where it had healed wrong after the second seizure. By twenty she could curl her tongue behind her teeth in company so no one would see. By twenty-five she didn't bother. Nobody looked in a taster's mouth anyway. They looked at their own plates.

Something else happened over those years, something Corvus had predicted and Mara had not believed: the poisons stopped being enemies.

It happened gradually, the way a river cuts a canyon. Each dose hurt less. Each recovery came faster. Her body learned the language Corvus promised, then grew fluent, then grew native. A dose that would kill a guardsman outright gave her, at worst, a headache and a strange metallic lucidity, a cold clean feeling like standing alone on a mountaintop. By the time she was sworn formally into the king's service—at nineteen, kneeling before a steward who could not look at her mouth—she could swallow hemlock tea with her breakfast and taste, separately and distinctly, the seven ingredients of the royal soup, plus anything that shouldn't be there.

Poison became her constant companion. That was the phrase Corvus used at the end, when he was dying—not of poison, but simply of being old, which even the Taster's Office had no antidote for. He held her hand with shaking fingers and said, "You've done it, girl. You've made yourself a home in the one house nobody else can live in. It'll keep you safe." And then, because he was honest at the end the way most men are, he added: "It'll also keep you alone. Mind the difference."

She hadn't minded it. Not for years.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about immunity: it is paid for in installments, and the currency is everything soft.

Not just the body. The body was only the down payment. But poison gets into everything if you let it steep long enough, and Mara had steeped. She learned to read people the way she read broth—for adulterants. A compliment had an aftertaste. An invitation had a texture. Kindness, when it came, she held on her tongue like an unknown substance, waiting for the burn, and it always, always burned, eventually, so she learned to spit it out politely before swallowing.

There was a kitchen boy named Tam who brought her sweetcakes on feast days for three years running, and she never once let herself wonder why until the year he didn't, and she found out he'd married a laundress and moved to the river quarter, and she realized she had spent three years declining a friendship without ever admitting one had been offered. There was a fellow taster, Jeyna, quick and funny, who died at twenty-six from a slow cumulative poison none of them caught in time—not assassination, just a bad harvest, just arsenic in the grain from a field too near old mine workings. Mara tasted the porridge that morning and pronounced it clean, because it was clean by every measure she knew, and Jeyna drank the cup, and Mara had stood at the funeral unable to decide whether she was guilty or merely unlucky, and deciding, finally, that the distinction didn't matter, that guilt was a luxury for people whose bodies still belonged to them.

She stopped attending funerals after that. It seemed cleaner.

Even her name thinned out. Tasters are deliberately unremarkable—the office survives on invisibility, Corvus had taught her, the way fish survive on water. No livery. No titles. No seat at any table, only standing positions near the king's shoulder. Servants learned to talk around her as if she were furniture. Courtiers forgot her face within a season of arriving. Once, at a banquet, a foreign ambassador asked aloud, in front of her, whether the king employed a woman or a mute in the grey dress, and the whole table laughed, and Mara laughed too, easily, because it was true, because being nobody was armor, because the ambassador would never think to poison a piece of furniture.

She told herself it was a fair trade. She told herself that often, the way you tell yourself anything that needs telling more than once.

Look at what she had, she reasoned, in the ledger-keeping voice Corvus had taught her. Warm rooms. Steady food—irony noted, filed. Wages saved against a future she planned in obsessive detail. Nine years of maps drawn in her own hand under a floorboard in her room: every smuggler road through the Thornfells, every goat track and shrine path and dry ravine, every water source and waypoint between the capital and the border, bought piecemeal from informants and remembered from a childhood spent hungry below those same peaks. One day—when her savings were enough, when her nerve was enough—she would walk out of the palace with her maps and her immunity and her savings and be no one anywhere, free as weather.

That was the dream. That was the whole of it. Freedom defined, in its entirety, as the continuation of solitude in a location of her own choosing.

And the cost? Also in the ledger. Her mother had died in Harrow's Cross six years back, and Mara had learned of it a month late, from a passing merchant, standing in a corridor that smelled of roasting pheasant, and she had felt—she had checked, carefully, professionally, the way she tested everything—almost nothing. A bitterness at the back of the tongue. A warmth behind the ears. Ten minutes of her own heartbeat too loud.

Grief, it turned out, presented exactly like a mild poison. She recognized the symptoms. She had been trained to endure them.

* * *

Now, lying awake in her narrow room above the Taster's Office with the moon counting toward its thirteenth turn outside the shuttered window, Mara did the arithmetic she had avoided for nine years.

She did it because of the salt. Because of the whisper in it, thin as wire, patient as debt. Because of old Brann the cook on his knees in the flour dust, praying to a name out of the deep past and getting an answer while the candles leaned toward him like listeners. Because her immunity—which had been bedrock, which had been the single fact she built her freedom on—had suddenly become a question mark.

Corvus's voice, dead these eight years, kept saying in her memory: *Mind the difference.* Safe and alone. Alone and safe. He had warned her they were not the same word, and she had spent a career pretending not to hear him.

Because here was the thought that had crept into her bed tonight and would not leave: the poisons had not only taught her body a language. They had evicted her from her own species, room by room, dose by dose, and called the vacancy protection. She could drink what killed kings. She could also stand in a room full of weeping people and feel only a professional interest in their tears. She had a tongue that could distinguish seven ingredients in a soup and could not, reliably, distinguish anymore between caution and fear, between prudence and cowardice, between being unkillable and simply being unreachable.

She had wanted to never be hungry enough to eat poison. She had become a woman for whom nothing on any table could do harm—and nothing at any table could reach her either. Immunity was not a wall around her. It was her, walled. She was the keep, and the keep was empty, and she had decorated it with maps of everywhere except home.

And yet—

She rolled onto her side and pressed her palm flat against the floorboard over her maps, the way other women pressed palms against sleeping bellies or cold hearths. The wood was cool. The maps were hers. Nine years of secret roads, and every one of them ran away from here.

But the cook had prayed and been answered. The salt had whispered. The king's shadow lagged behind him like something reeled on a string. And some animal part of her, the part older than Corvus's training, older than the scars on her tongue—the part that had eaten ergot rye at nine years old and walked out of Harrow's Cross rather than be eaten—had begun to understand that the thing answering prayers in her kingdom was not a god at all, not yet, but a mouth. And mouths, whatever else they did, whatever names they wore, shared one universal grammar.

She knew that grammar intimately. She had spent her whole life learning how things got swallowed.

Tomorrow she would taste the king's breakfast, as always, and pronounce it clean or not, as always. Tomorrow she would stand at his shoulder, furniture-grey and invisible, and watch that lagging shadow, and say nothing, because saying something meant belonging to something, and belonging was the one poison she had trained a lifetime never to swallow.

She lay in the dark and admitted it at last, quietly, the way you admit a taste you've been pretending not to notice:

She was already afraid. She had been since the salt whispered. And fear, after nine years of immunity, felt almost new—sharp and unwelcome and horribly alive, like a flavor she'd forgotten existed.

By morning she would know what to do about it. She already suspected what it would cost.

What she did not yet know—could not have known, lying there cataloguing her scars like coins—was that within a fortnight a spymaster would put a knife to the one secret she had left, and the road she'd mapped for escaping her whole life would turn out to run, in the end, straight back into the palace.
