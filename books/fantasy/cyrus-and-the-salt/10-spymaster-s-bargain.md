## 10. Spymaster's Bargain

Sera Vane had read ten thousand confessions in her career, and she had learned that the guilty ones always touched their faces.

Mara Lockridge didn't touch her face. She sat across the desk in the spymaster's office with her hands folded like a woman waiting for bread to rise, and she looked at the papers spread between them without flinching, and that was the first thing that made Sera uneasy. The second thing was the papers themselves.

"Read them," Sera said. "Aloud, if you like. I've already read them enough times to recite them."

Mara picked up the top sheet. A ledger page, copied in a fair hand: three shipments of mountain grain routed through Thornfell over two years, each one signed for at a waystation that officially did not exist, each one received by a name that appeared nowhere in any census Sera's people could find. *Harrow. Harrow. Harrow.*

"You know this name?" Mara asked.

"I know it's not a name. I know it's a hole in my records shaped like a person." Sera leaned back. "I know someone inside these walls has been feeding information through it, because the shipments only started after you were promoted to first taster, and they only stopped—notice this—the month the palace changed its grain supplier. Which is a fact no one outside the provisioning office would have known in time to matter."

Mara set the page down. "You're saying I'm a smuggler."

"I'm saying you're either a smuggler or the best-informed innocent woman in Aldric's court, and I've been doing this long enough to stop believing in those."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below them, the kitchens were already at work on the evening meal Mara would taste before the king touched it, and Sera watched her glance toward the window at the sound of a bell and then look away again, and thought: *she counts the hours by the king's stomach.* Nine years of that. Nine years of reports that never varied. *No poison detected. No poison detected. No poison detected.*

That was the thing that had brought Mara into this room, though Mara didn't know it yet. Not the smuggling. The smuggling was leverage; the reports were the wound.

"Say something," Sera said.

"What would you like me to say?"

"The truth would be novel."

Mara's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "The truth is that I grew up on the wrong side of the Thornfell passes, and the roads up there don't care whose flag flies at the bottom of them. People move wool and cheese and sometimes grain, and if a waystation wants to call itself Harrow so the toll collectors stay bored, that's mountain law, not treason. I haven't carried anything for anyone since I took the king's salt. You can check my hands. Tasters' hands get inspected monthly—you wrote the rule yourself, Lady Vane."

"I did write the rule." Sera had also written the rule that tasters' quarters could be searched with cause, and she had searched Mara's room four days ago, and what she'd found under a loose floorboard was currently locked in the drawer beside her knee. "And your hands are clean. It's your floorboards that concern me."

She saw it land—a single blink, half a heartbeat too slow—and then Mara's face went smooth as poured cream, and Sera felt the cold particular pleasure of a trap closing. She opened the drawer and laid the map on the desk between them.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Every pass, every goat track, every dry wash and collapsed mine shaft in forty miles of Thornfell, inked in a hand so fine it looked engraved, annotated in a cipher Sera had spent three days breaking and still only half understood. Water sources marked with tiny stars. Guard rotations marked with tiny skulls. And along the eastern edge, in letters smaller than the rest, a sequence of dates going back nine years, like a diary kept in geography.

"This," Sera said, "is treason. This isn't wool and cheese. This is every back door into the kingdom, drawn by someone who knows them well enough to walk them blindfolded, hidden in the rooms of the woman who tastes the king's food."

"Yes," Mara said.

Sera had prepared for denial. She had prepared for tears, for bargaining, for the trembling-voice plea about a sick mother in the mountains. She had a whole architecture of threats built to withstand resistance, and Mara's flat little *yes* knocked all of it sideways.

"That's it? Yes?"

"It's a map. Maps aren't treason, Lady Vane. They're insurance." Mara turned the sheet so it faced her, and her finger came down on the eastern dates. "Nine years ago I made a promise to myself that whatever happened in this palace, I would always know the way out. You keep files on everyone in this building. I keep a file on the mountains. Which of us do you think sleeps better?"

*Me,* Sera almost said. *Because my file has an office behind it and yours has a floorboard.* But she didn't say it, because there was a second document in the drawer, and the second document was the reason this conversation was happening tonight instead of next week, and the second document had nothing to do with smuggler roads.

She put the map aside and laid down the intercepts instead.

Three of them. Copies of prayers, if you could call them that—petitions, really, written by shrine-keepers in the high passes and sent down-mountain through channels Sera had only begun to map. The first asked the old gods for rain. The second asked for rain again, and noted that the last request had been answered. The third was newer, its ink barely dry when her courier pulled it from a dead drop in the foothills, and it said: *The thirteenth moon comes. We ask not for gifts but for restraint. Whatever walks among us wearing devotion, we ask it to be patient until we are ready.*

"Your informant network is better than mine," Sera said. "Or worse. Depending on how you look at it."

Mara read all three pages twice. When she reached the third, Sera watched her thumb press white against the paper, and thought: *there. There's the face you didn't make about the map.*

"Where did these come from?" Mara asked.

"A place I'm not going to tell you, from a courier I'm not going to name, about a subject I am now going to ask you about directly, because we're past the point where pretending costs either of us anything." Sera stood and walked to the window. Below, the inner court was emptying of daylight; somewhere a priest was ringing vespers, and the sound of it made her jaw tighten in a way she'd stopped questioning weeks ago. "Prayers are being answered, Lockridge. Real ones. Crops rising overnight. Fevers breaking. A miller in the valley provinces who was dead on Tuesday and walking around on Thursday, according to six witnesses I've interviewed and disbelieved and then, reluctantly, believed. And every priest who's tried to find out *how* has gone missing. Four of them. In two months. Men and women who asked careful questions and then simply ceased to appear in the world."

"And you think the shrines know why."

"I think the shrines are frightened, which is more than can be said for anyone inside these walls. Everyone here is *happy*, Lockridge. Have you noticed? The whole court is glowing like a bride. The queen prays six hours a day. The king—" Sera paused. She had watched the king at the autumn council, had watched his shadow lag his body by half a heartbeat in the candlelight, and had told herself she'd imagined it, and had then spent a week not imagining it. "The king has never seemed more beloved."

Mara said nothing. Her silence had a texture to it, Sera noticed. Not blankness. Restraint. Like a woman holding a full cup very steady.

"Here's what happens now," Sera said. "Tomorrow morning I take the map and the intercepts and your confession—if you give me one—to the chancellor. Treason against the crown carries a specific penalty, and the gallows on the south wall are tall enough that the whole city gets a good view. Or—"

"Or," Mara said.

"Or you go back to being what you already are. A woman with friends in the mountains. Except now you're my woman with friends in the mountains. You'll carry messages to your Harrow. You'll visit the shrines. You'll find out what the old priests knew, and why answering prayers and asking questions seem to end in the same disappearances, and you'll report to me weekly, in writing, in the cipher I know you're clever enough to improve."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow you hang, and I find another mountain girl, and she'll be slower and less careful and probably dead within the year, and none of that will matter to you because you'll be hanging." Sera let that sit. Then, because she was honest with herself even when she couldn't afford to be honest with anyone else: "I don't want that. For what it's worth. You're the most competent person in this building and I include myself, and I did not build my career on wasting competent people."

"No," Mara agreed. "You build your career on owning them. It's cleaner."

Something in Sera's chest twisted—not quite anger, not quite recognition. "Yes. It is. Welcome to it."

Mara looked down at the map for a long moment. Sera had interrogated traitors and fanatics and one truly memorable assassin who had wept throughout his confession and lied during every word of it, and she trusted herself to read faces better than any living soul in the kingdom, and what she saw on Mara Lockridge's face now was not fear and not calculation but something older and wearier than both: the expression of a woman watching a door close that she had always known would close eventually.

"One condition," Mara said.

"You're not in a position to bargain."

"Then take it as advice, freely given, from a condemned woman." Mara raised her eyes. "Whatever you send me to find in those mountains—when I find it, and I will find it, because I'm good at finding things—you should decide *now* what you'll do if the answer is bigger than the crown. If it turns out the rot goes up higher than the chancellor. Higher than the throne itself. Because I've tasted the king's food for nine years, Lady Vane, and lately—" she stopped, and chose her words with the care of a woman defusing something, "—lately the meals have been strange. Not poisoned. Strange. And I've started wondering whether the thing I'm protecting is the man or the chair he sits in."

The fire popped. Down in the city, the vespers bell finished, and the silence afterward felt heavier than the sound.

"That," Sera said slowly, "is either treason or loyalty, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Neither can I," Mara said. "That's the condition. Don't ask me to choose until you've seen what I see."

* * *

They settled terms by lamplight. Weekly reports, left in the hollow behind the third caryatid in the east colonnade, written in a cipher Sera dictated and Mara memorized in a single reading, which annoyed Sera more than she showed. Money, grudgingly accepted. A cover story: the taster would travel to Thornfell to source wild herbs and consecrated salt for the royal kitchens—an errand plausible enough that no one would question it, in a court where consecrated salt had suddenly become fashionable at every table.

"Fashionable," Mara repeated, as they finalized the story. "Is that what they're calling it."

"People want blessed things right now. Salt, water, thread. My own tailor asked me to bless his scissors." Sera said it flatly, but she watched Mara's face while she said it, and the flicker that crossed it confirmed what Sera had suspected for a fortnight: the taster knew something. Not everything. Something. Enough to be afraid in a specific direction.

"You'll leave in three days," Sera said. "Take the pilgrim road as far as Vell's Crossing, then your own paths from there. Report by the first of the month. If you miss a report, I assume you've turned, and I act accordingly."

"And how does the crown act accordingly against a woman who knows every back door out of the kingdom?"

"Poorly," Sera said. "Let's not find out."

Mara rose, gathered her shawl, and paused at the door with her hand on the latch. For a moment Sera thought she would say something true—something about the king, or the shadows, or the third petition with its frightened plea for restraint—but what she actually said was:

"The honeyed quail tonight. Tell the kitchens to use the clover honey, not the heather. The heather batch smells faintly of smoke, and the king has been off his food, and a nervous king makes a nervous court."

Then she was gone, and Sera sat alone with the map and the petitions and the small cold fact that the palace's most invisible servant had just given her unsolicited counsel on the king's appetite, and that the counsel had sounded, unmistakably, like concern.

*She'll play both sides,* Sera thought, snuffing the lamp. *Of course she will. I would. It's the only sane move available to a woman owned by two masters.* The question—the question that would keep her awake past midnight, staring at the ceiling of her office, listening to the palace breathe—was which side Mara would betray first, and whether the answer would arrive in time to matter.

In the dark, the intercepted petition lay on her desk, its final line facing up:

*We ask it to be patient until we are ready.*

Ready for what, old men of the mountains? Sera blew out the candle and did not sleep.

* * *

Three days later, at dawn, she watched from the armory window as Mara Lockridge rode out the north gate with a herb basket strapped behind her saddle and nothing else visible at all—which was, Sera reflected, exactly how the most dangerous woman in the kingdom ought to travel.

The report was due on the first of the month.

On the fourth day of the month, the hollow behind the third caryatid held no paper. On the fifth, it held a single sheet, unsigned, in a hand Sera did not recognize, containing one line:

*Ask your archives who founded the taster's office, and what happened to her after thirty years.*

Sera read it twice, burned it in the brazier, and went down to the records vaults, where the oldest ledgers slept under dust and lock and key—and where, she would realize much later, much too late, she had just taken the first step down a road that did not lead anywhere she could come back from.
