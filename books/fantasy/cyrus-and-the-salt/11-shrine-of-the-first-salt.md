## 11. Shrine of the First Salt

The mountain did not want me there.

I'd known it three days in, when the smuggler path I'd mapped at nineteen thinned to a goat-track and then to a suggestion, and the wind started carrying a sound I couldn't file anywhere—half avalanche, half throat. The Thornfell passes had always been indifferent to human traffic, but indifference was a thing I understood and could work with. This was different. This was the specific, patient dislike of something old being disturbed.

I climbed anyway.

Nine years of tasting had taught me that the things worth avoiding were rarely the things that killed you. It was the slow poisons, the ones you built a tolerance to grain by grain, that hollowed you out while everyone called you lucky. Whatever was happening in the palace—dead gods waking, cooks praying, kings whose shadows lagged behind their bodies like bad dancers—I could not taste it from inside the walls. So I went up, into the thin cold air, following a rumor I'd paid a shepherd two silver marks to confirm: *there's still one at the First Shrine. She keeps the salt.*

* * *

The shrine sat in a bowl of stone above the tree line, small as a shepherd's hut and twice as old, its walls fitted without mortar by hands that must have been patient or desperate or both. Salt crusted the doorframe in a thick white rime, layered so deep it had become geological—decades of it, centuries maybe, each generation adding a coat like a family adding paint to a fence until the wood underneath is more memory than timber.

The priestess was on the roof.

She was mending thatch with the unhurried competence of someone who had done it every autumn of her life, and she did not stop when my boots crunched onto the salt-scattered gravel below. Grey braid, grey eyes, hands like root-stock. Sixty if she was a day. She looked down at me the way you look at weather.

"You're the taster," she said.

Not a question. My hand went to my knife before my brain caught up. "How do you know that?"

"Because you walk like your tongue is more important than your feet." She came down the ladder with a creak of old joints, brushed straw from her skirts, and studied me with an expression I couldn't read—until I realized I could. It was pity. Pity, aimed at me, precise as a surgeon's mark. "Mara Lockridge. Nine years in the king's household. You've eaten more poison than any living soul in this kingdom and they've never once thanked you for it."

"That's not information a shrine-keeper should have."

"It's information a shrine-keeper has always had." She turned toward the door. "We keep records here too, girl. Ours are just older than anyone's. Come in out of the wind. You've walked far enough to earn a fire, and you're going to need to sit down for what I have to tell you."

"I haven't told you why I came."

"You came because the salt whispered to you." She held the door open. "They always do, in the end. The ones who can hear it."

* * *

Inside, the shrine was one room and mostly salt. A low fire. A table worn concave by generations of elbows. And along the back wall, stacked in clay jars sealed with wax and prayer-marks, the salt itself—the consecrated shipment I'd tasted in the palace cellars, the same mineral, but here in quantities that made the air taste faintly of the sea and faintly of something under the sea.

The whisper was louder here. Not words. More like standing outside a door where people argue in a language you almost know.

Ivenna set water on to boil and spoke with her back to me, which somehow made it worse, made it confession-shaped.

"How much do you know about the famine?" she asked. "The real one. Not the one in the histories."

"The histories say three failed harvests. The Sorrow Years. Half the border country starved, the rest was saved by—" I stopped. Because the histories said it plainly enough, I'd just never heard it the way she wanted me to hear it: "—by the king's covenant. The crown prayed, and the crops returned."

"Mm." She poured two cups of something dark and resinous and pushed one across the table. "And who taught the crown what to pray to?"

"I don't—"

"No. Nobody does. That's rather the point of everything." She sat, folded her hands, and looked at me across the steam with those grey eyes that had already weighed me and found me insufficient and forgave me for it. "Sit down, taster. I'm going to tell you how your kingdom was bought, and what the price turns out to be, and you're going to want to be sitting."

I sat.

"There were gods here before the crown," she said. "Old ones. Small ones, mostly—gods of the hearth-stone and the field-edge and the birthing shed. They didn't ask much. A bowl of cream. A song at harvest. They answered when it suited them and sulked when it didn't, which is what you want from a god, honestly. Manageable." A dry pause. "But there was one in the high places that wasn't small. We don't know what it was before. Maybe it was always hunger wearing a shape. Maybe we *made* it hungry—the way you can make anything hungry by feeding it. What we know is that during the Sorrow Years, it answered."

"Answered what?"

"A prayer made out of desperation. The border folk were eating their dead, taster. Do you understand? That's not a story I tell lightly and it isn't a story anyone wrote down because no one wants their grandmother remembered that way. And into that came the thing in the mountain, and it offered a bargain to whoever would carry the word down. Crops overnight. Rain on schedule. The dying healed. In exchange—" her mouth twisted, "—worship. Devotion. Faith, poured into it like wine into a cup, forever."

"And the crown took the deal."

"The crown *brokered* the deal." Her voice flattened. "That's the part your histories got wrong, and wrong on purpose. The king didn't pray. Kings don't pray; it looks weak. The crown negotiated. Terms. Rates. A tithe of devotion, collected through state religion, funneled upward. And the god—it kept its side of the bargain beautifully. Three generations of perfect harvests. The kingdom forgot famine ever existed. They put it in the histories as a miracle and built the capital on top of the gratitude."

I drank. The tea was bitter and grounding. "So where does the binding come in? If it was all aboveboard—"

"Because worship is never enough." She leaned forward. "This is the thing, Mara. This is the thing you have to understand before anything else makes sense. A god fed on devotion doesn't stay fed. Devotion is smoke. It fills the belly and vanishes. Every prayer bought less than the last. Within a generation the god was starving again—and a starving god with a covenant gets *creative* about collection." She tapped the table, once, hard. "So the priests of the day—my predecessors—did the only thing left. They bound it. Salt and rites and a vessel. They couldn't kill it; nothing kills hunger but feeding, and feeding it was the problem. They locked it into a container strong enough to hold it and starved it slowly, rationing the devotion through the shrines, keeping it asleep but alive, because killing it might crack the covenant and take every warded field in the kingdom with it."

"What container?"

Ivenna looked at me for a long moment. The fire popped. Outside, the wind found some new register of displeasure.

"You already know," she said quietly. "You felt it flinch when the old gods were named at court. You've seen his shadow arrive late. Don't make me say it and take away the few seconds you get to not-know."

"The king." My voice came out flat, professional, tasting something I couldn't spit out. "The binding is the king."

"The royal line. Body to body, crown to crown, down through every reign since. The god wears them, taster. Has worn them all. Aldric is just the current coat it's borrowed." She said it without cruelty, which was the worst way to hear it. "Every prayer at court, every candle lit for the king's health, every hymn in his honor—it goes straight into its mouth. Your palace isn't a government. It's a feeding trough, and it's been running for longer than anyone alive can remember."

The room tilted slightly. I gripped the table's edge and thought, absurdly, about the honeyed quail, the bitter almond, all those small deaths I'd caught and swallowed. Nine years of keeping one man alive, and the man had been a glove.

"Why now?" I managed. "Why is it waking?"

"Because someone let the shrines starve." Ivenna's jaw tightened. "Sixty years ago the court stopped sending consecrated salt. Stopped the ration rites. Decided the old gods were superstition and the covenant was settled history. So the binding thinned. Slowly—so slowly nobody noticed. And now the moon is counting toward its thirteenth turn, and on the thirteenth turn, if nothing intervenes, the binding completes. Permanent. Not a king wearing a god anymore—a god wearing a kingdom. Every soul in it, praying without knowing why."

I thought of the cook on his knees in the kitchen, praying to a nameless god and getting an answer. I thought of the queen's elaborate devotions. I thought of the astrologer announcing the omen no one could name, and the whole court shivering pleasantly, like cattle smelling weather.

"The salt," I said. "The shipment. Why did it whisper to me?"

Ivenna rose and crossed to the wall of jars. She lifted one down, broke the wax with her thumbnail, and held it out to me—a fistful of coarse white crystals catching the firelight.

"Because salt remembers what it seals," she said. "And because the founder of your office bound this god the first time, and every taster since has carried a trace of that binding in their blood. You don't just tolerate poison, Mara Lockridge. You're the last living piece of the lock." Her hand stayed steady; mine, reaching for the salt, did not. "Which means either you're here because you were sent—"

"I sent myself."

"—or because the mountain called the lock back to its door." She closed my fingers over the salt. It was cold. Cold as a cellar, cold as a coin from a dead man's pocket, and beneath the cold, faint as a pulse, the whisper. "Thirteen moons, taster. We're past the third. There is a rite that can rebind it properly, or break it entirely, and both cost more than I can pay alone. I'm eighty-one years old and I am the last priestess who knows the words."

The scale of it settled over me then—not the danger, danger was familiar, danger fit in a goblet—but the *lie*. Generations of it. A kingdom built on a feeding trough and told it was grace. Every harvest festival, every prayer for the king, every history book with the covenant printed as miracle. My whole life spent guarding a body that wasn't a body, serving a crown that wasn't a crown, invisible in a palace that was one enormous mouth.

I had escaped in my head a thousand times, down smuggler roads to a life where I owed nothing. But there was no down anymore. There was only the mountain, and the thing in it, and the thirteenth moon coming on like a tide.

"What do you need from me?" I asked.

Ivenna smiled, and there was no comfort in it at all.

"First? Finish your tea. Then sleep—you leave before dawn." She took the salt back, resealed the jar, and turned to the fire. "You're going back down to court, taster. And you're going to find out who restarted the prayers—because the god didn't wake on its own."

"Somebody opened the trough," I said.

"Somebody opened the trough." She glanced back at me, firelight carving her face into ridges. "And Mara—if it's who I fear it is, then the queen already knows your name."

* * *

I slept badly, on a pallet that smelled of salt and old smoke, the whisper in the jars keeping time with my pulse. Before dawn I stood at the shrine door with the wind pulling at my coat, and Ivenna pressed a twist of paper into my palm—salt, wrapped like a poison dose, the first thing in nine years I'd carried that was meant to go in my mouth and do exactly what it said.

"Taste nothing up there without thinking of me," she said.

I went down the mountain the way I'd come, faster, my own map unspooling under my boots, and the whole descent I turned one thought over and over like a stone in the hand:

For nine years, I'd sworn nothing got past my tongue.

And the largest poison in the kingdom had been sitting on the throne the entire time, and I had swallowed for it daily, and called it loyalty.
