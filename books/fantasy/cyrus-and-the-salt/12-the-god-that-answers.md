## 12. The God That Answers

Ivenna had kept the shrine of the First Salt for forty-one years, and in all that time the mountain had never once spoken to her.

It spoke now.

Not in words. The mountain did not waste itself on words. It spoke in the way the frost climbed the eastern wall overnight, climbing *up* instead of down, against every law of cold she knew. It spoke in the spring that had run sweet for a century and now ran faintly bitter, like water that had passed through a mouth. It spoke in her goats, who would not graze the high meadow anymore and stood all night facing the summit, silent, attentive, like an audience waiting for something to begin.

She was mending a net over the drying racks when she felt it: a pressure behind her sternum, gentle as a hand laid flat. Not pain. Attention.

"Ah," Ivenna said aloud, to no one, because there was no one left to say it to. "So you're awake."

* * *

The old texts called it the Famine-Father, the Hunger Below, the One Who Answers. The founders of her order had used softer names when they wrote to kings—*the covenant partner*, *the deep benefactor*—because soft names opened purses and hard ones closed them. But Ivenna had read the original ledgers, the ones written in the century when the shrine still had thirty keepers instead of one old woman and a leaky roof, and the ledgers were honest in a way doctrine never is.

It answered prayers. That was all it had ever done. It answered them, and it ate what it was owed afterward, and for three hundred years the kingdom had decided that was a fair price, right up until the price came due in full and some desperate taster with a scarred tongue had stuffed the god's hunger into a box of consecrated salt and bought everyone thirty years.

Thirty years that should have been followed by renewal rites. Renewal rites that had never come, because the court had found it cheaper to let the seal thin than to send a king up the mountain to kneel.

Ivenna had spent four decades maintaining a lock whose keyhole faced inward, praying to gods who never answered, keeping the salt blessed, keeping the count of moons. She had thought herself a warden of a sleeping thing.

She understood now, standing in the goat pen with frost climbing the wrong direction, that she had been a nurse at the bedside of something that was not sleeping at all. It had been listening. All these years of thin prayer and thinner faith, it had been listening the way a starving man listens to a neighbor's dinner through a wall.

And lately the neighbors had started cooking again.

* * *

It began, as these things do, small.

A shepherd named Colm—she'd known him since he was a boy stealing plums from her orchard, a wide-faced man now with a wife and two daughters and a laugh you could hear across two valleys—came up the shrine path on a gray morning with a dead lamb in his arms and tears freezing in his beard.

"The vet can't come till the thaw," he said. "I know it's foolish. I know nobody answers anymore. But my girls prayed over it, Ivenna. My little girls knelt down in the byre and prayed like their grandmother taught them, before anyone told them it was foolish, and I couldn't—" His voice broke. "I couldn't tell them no."

Ivenna looked at the lamb. It had been dead long enough to stiffen; she could see that from six feet away. Cold, bloodless, done.

"Bring it inside," she said, because grief deserved a roof.

She meant to give him tea and a blessing and send him home to bury the thing. Instead, while Colm sat with his head in his hands, the lamb breathed.

There was no light. No thunder. The fire didn't even gutter. It simply happened, the way a cramp happens, sudden and wrong: the small body shuddered, drew air, and bleated—a thin, bewildered sound—and stood up on legs that had been stiff as sticks a moment before, and walked to its mother's scent on Colm's coat and began to nuzzle it.

Colm made a noise Ivenna had never heard a grown man make. He fell to his knees on her shrine floor, in front of the sealed salt cist that her order had guarded for three hundred years, and wept, and thanked—not her, not the old gods of the litany, but *something*, generously, wildly, with his whole chest.

And Ivenna felt the cist warm under her palm like a body waking.

* * *

Afterward she could not stop thinking about the mechanics of it. That was her flaw, always had been; other keepers had been mystics and she had been a bookkeeper with incense. The lamb had been dead past any margin the old gods' mercy allowed. The old gods—the true ones, the quiet ones, the ones who took a season to answer and asked nothing but patience—did not raise the dead. They ripened crops and softened fevers. They worked like farmers, slow and within limits.

This had been different. This had been fast, and showy, and free.

*Free* was the word that frightened her. Nothing below the frost line was free.

She went into the archive room—that cold stone chamber where the founder's own hand still marked the walls—and pulled the ledgers of the first binding. And there it was, in the cramped script of a woman who had signed herself only *Cyrus, Taster*:

*It cannot create. It can only return what has been given to it. Every miracle is a withdrawal. Every withdrawal has a depositor.*

Ivenna sat with that for a long time, the lamp burning low, the frost ticking against the shutters.

Every miracle is a withdrawal. So who had deposited a lamb?

Then she remembered Colm's face, the wild gratitude, the prayer poured out without ration or restraint—*thank you, thank you, thank you*—and she understood with a lurching sickness that the deposit had been Colm himself. His devotion. His faith, drawn out of him in one great gulp, converted into a resurrection, and the change handed back to a weeping man as proof.

The god wasn't answering prayers. It was *buying customers.*

* * *

Within a fortnight there were three more.

A fevered child in the valley village, healed overnight—the mother's hair gone white where it had been brown, though she swore she felt fine, better than fine, better than she'd felt in years. A lame carter walking. A widow's frozen well running sweet again. Each miracle small enough to be deniable, public enough to be witnessed, each one arriving exactly where it would be seen by the most people. And after each one, Ivenna checked the ledger-marks she'd begun keeping, the tally of devotion drawn and spent, and the arithmetic was always the same: the god gave back a fraction of what it took, and the difference went somewhere.

Below. Into the seal. Against the salt.

She began sleeping in the archive room with the founder's notes spread around her like a fortification. On the ninth night she found the passage she had read a hundred times and never understood:

*The covenant completes on the thirteenth turn of a waking moon. Thirteen turns of open devotion and the bond sets like mortar. What was lent becomes owned. What was worn becomes flesh. Guard the count.*

Guard the count.

Ivenna counted. She had always counted; it was the one discipline her order demanded above all others. The first answered prayer—the lamb—had come on the night of the new moon. She laid out her tallies against the almanac with shaking hands, and the pattern rose off the page like a smell.

One turn of the moon had passed since the lamb. Two miracles in the second turn. Five in the third. The curve was not growth. It was acceleration. It was a debt compounding. And if the thirteenth turn completed the covenant—if thirteen turns of open devotion set the bond like mortar—then the counting had already begun, and it had begun the night a grieving shepherd carried a dead lamb up her path and a starving god had decided to spend lavishly on its first customer.

Eleven turns remained. Less than a year. And every prayer answered between now and then was another stone in the mortar, another mouthful of the kingdom swallowed ahead of schedule.

* * *

She tried to warn them. That was the part she would be ashamed of longest, in the years that came after: how hard she tried, and how completely it failed.

She sent letters to the court—to the king's own secretary, to the archivist, to the office of the spymaster, Sera Vane, whose name appeared in old shrine correspondence as a friend of the order. She wrote plainly: *The seal thins. The covenant wakes. Do not pray openly. Do not gather. Do not accept miracles.* She paid a drover good silver to carry them down through Thornfell, and she watched his mule disappear down the switchbacks carrying words that might save a kingdom.

The drover came back a week later. Alone. His mule had fallen on the ice, he said, and the letters with it. All of them. Every satchel. He said it smoothly, without meeting her eyes, and Ivenna—who had lived alone with a god for forty years and knew every species of silence—looked at this sweating man and understood that the letters had never reached the bottom of the mountain. Something between here and the court did not want them to arrive.

Or something at the court did not want to hear them.

That night she dreamed of the palace for the first time in her life. She had never seen it; she saw it now with terrible clarity, the long hall, the throne, and on the throne a shape wearing a king like a coat, and the shape turned its head—slowly, with vast patience—and looked directly at her across a hundred miles of mountain, and smiled.

She woke with frost on the inside of her window and her own breath hanging in the air like a confession.

* * *

On the last day of autumn she performed the test she had been dreading.

The raising of the dead shepherd happened at dusk, and Ivenna did not witness it, which somehow made it worse. She heard it: bells from the valley chapel ringing at the wrong hour, then dogs, then voices, many voices, rising up the slopes in the blue half-light with a joy in them she had not heard since childhood.

They carried Tam Hesk down the mountain road on a door torn from its hinges. Tam had been dead nine days—buried, buried properly, she had helped dig the grave herself when the rockfall took him—and now he walked among his neighbors, pale and puzzled and *smiling*, smiling the same broad unlined smile the healed mother wore, the smile that did not reach anything behind the eyes. The villagers pressed around him touching his sleeves. Someone was singing. Old songs, pre-court songs, songs to gods whose names hadn't been spoken in decades, sung badly and loudly and with total abandon.

Tam's wife threw her arms around him and wept, and Tam patted her back the way a man pats a doorframe. Absent. Patient. Waiting.

Ivenna pushed through the crowd and took his wrist. His pulse was slow and even and utterly calm, calm as a held note, and beneath her fingers she felt—or imagined she felt, and would spend the rest of her life unsure—a faint rhythm underneath his heartbeat, a second pulse, deeper, slower, keeping time with something far below the mountain.

"Tam," she said. "Do you know me?"

He looked at her with mild, pleasant eyes. "You're the keeper," he said warmly. "You keep the salt." And he smiled that smile again, and added, with a tenderness that curdled her blood: "He says you've kept it well. He says you must be tired."

The crowd cheered. They thought she had blessed him. They lifted Tam onto their shoulders and carried him toward the village lights, singing, and Ivenna stood alone in the trampled snow with the dark coming down and the mountain humming under her feet like a struck bell that would not finish fading.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She sat in the archive with the founder's ledgers and did the only thing she knew how to do, which was count.

Twelve miracles confirmed since the lamb. Devotion drawn: she estimated it in the old units, souls'-worth, the measure Cyrus had used, and the number appalled her. Twelve withdrawals, each one larger, each one more public, each one buying more believers, each believer's faith a fresh deposit, a wheel turning itself faster with every revolution. The god was not merely feeding. It was recruiting. Every healed child was a herald. Every raised corpse was a sermon.

And the thirteenth turn was coming. She counted backward from the winter solstice, checked it twice, checked it a third time hoping she'd erred, and the answer did not change.

Ten turns remained. Ten turns of accelerating miracles, ten turns of a kingdom falling to its knees in gratitude, ten turns until the mortar set and the covenant completed and whatever wore the crown wore it forever, and the kingdom's faith became not a meal but a *feeding trough*, permanent, hereditary, eternal.

Ten turns. And she was one old woman on a mountain with a leaking roof, and her letters did not arrive, and the last keeper of the First Salt had just understood, sitting alone in a cold room full of dead women's handwriting, that she had spent forty-one years guarding a lock while the thief picked it from the other side.

She was too late. The thought arrived without drama, the way a diagnosis arrives, plain and final. Too late to stop the waking. Too late to warn anyone who mattered. Perhaps too late entirely.

But she had read every page Cyrus had ever written, and there was one line she had always taken for a mourner's sentimentality, a founder's grief dressed up as instruction. Now, with the mountain humming beneath her and a dead man's patient smile still hanging in the air, she read it again by lamplight and understood it for the first time as what it actually was:

*A seal is only as strong as the vessel that holds it. If the hunger must be moved again, it must move into a living thing. It must move into someone who has already survived being poisoned by everything else. God help us, it will need a taster.*

Ivenna closed the ledger. Her hands were steady. Forty-one years of solitary winters will do that; they teach you that panic is a luxury and the work comes first, whatever the work turns out to be.

Somewhere south of her, beyond the passes, in a palace full of kneeling courtiers and a king who flinched at the old gods' names, there was a food-taster with a scarred tongue and poison in her blood and no idea what she was for.

"Hold on," Ivenna said to the empty room, to the humming mountain, to whoever might be listening—including, she supposed, the thing below, which was listening to everything now. "Just hold on. I'm coming to find you."

Outside, the moon rose—waxing, fat, and counting.
