## 13. Court of Whispers

The trick to lying well was the same as the trick to tasting well: never flinch first.

Mara had learned that in her second year of service, when she'd reported a taint of wormwood in a stew that turned out to be nothing but over-thyme'd mutton and her own imagination. The kitchen master had laughed at her for a week. She'd decided then that she would rather be wrong silently than right loudly. Nine years later, silence was her native language, and lies sat on her tongue like salt — clean, dissolving, leaving no trace.

Sera Vane thought she owned her now.

Three times a week since the shrine road, Mara climbed the narrow stair to the spymaster's office above the armory, where the arrow-slits cut the light into blades and the room always smelled faintly of iron and old paper. Three times a week she reported on the mountain shrines: which fires burned, which keepers prayed aloud, which villages had begun leaving offerings at crossroads instead of chapel doors.

And three times a week she fed Sera a diet of carefully cultivated falsehood, grown in the greenhouse of her own skull.

"The keeper at Hollowmere burns tallow, not oil," she said today, watching Sera's quill move across the page. "Cheap man. His fire goes out by midnight. If you want to catch anyone traveling the high pass, watch after the Hollowmere light dies."

"Names?" Sera didn't look up. Her handwriting was small and viciously neat, like everything else about her.

"Peddlers mostly. A woman with red yarn in her hair who buys candles in pairs." Mara let a beat pass. "No one from court, if that's what you're asking."

"I wasn't asking."

"You were always going to ask eventually."

Sera set down the quill. In the blade-light her eyes were the color of wet slate, and just as warm. "You have an interesting relationship with the truth, Lockridge."

"I have a professional relationship with it. Same as you." Mara folded her hands. "The difference is I taste what I'm given before I swallow. You might try it sometime."

Something flickered at the corner of Sera's mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one, buried like a body under a floor. "One day you'll say something like that to the wrong person."

"Most days I already do."

* * *

The trouble with double games was arithmetic. Every lie had to be paid for with another lie, and interest compounded daily. The red-yarn woman existed; Mara had invented her details, but invention required maintenance. Candle-buying habits. Routes. A face Sera's people could look for and fail to find, which would itself become evidence — of caution, of cunning, of a network worth chasing deeper into the mountains. Away from the palace. Away from the king.

Away from whatever wore him.

Mara had seen it twice since her return, and both times it had been small things, the kind of things only someone who watched for poison in every spoonful would catch.

The first time: a council steward handed the king a petition concerning grain tariffs, and Aldric read it top to bottom without moving his eyes. Not skimming — not moving them. Like a portrait reading. Then he signed at the bottom, in his own hand, and the signature was perfect. Too perfect. She remembered his real hand from the old ledgers, the leftward slant, the ink-heavy downstrokes of a man who wrote fast because he thought faster than he wrote. This signature was balanced. Practiced. Worn, like a coat worn so long it fit better than the skin beneath.

The second time: he'd laughed at a joke the fool made about bishops, and the laugh came half a heartbeat after everyone else's, like thunder arriving late behind lightning. And when the laughter died, he looked at his own wine cup as if he couldn't remember lifting it.

She'd started keeping a list. Small, coded, written in the shorthand she used for her maps, tucked under the same floorboard beside the roads out of Thornfell. It was getting long.

* * *

But none of it — not the dead-eyed councils, not the delayed laughter — prepared her for the queen's chapel hours.

Queen Isolde prayed publicly twice a day, and had for as long as Mara had served. That was unremarkable; queens prayed the way fountains flowed, decoratively, for atmosphere. What was remarkable was that the court had begun to gather. First a handful of ladies. Then dozens. Then the antechamber outside the royal chapel stood full every dawn and dusk, servants pressed against the doors, guards craning their necks, everyone drawn by the rumor that ran through the palace like water through chalk: that the queen's prayers were being answered.

That the king was well again.

He did look well. That was the horror of it. The grayness that had clung to him for years — the tiredness everyone blamed on statecraft — had burned away. He walked the corridors upright. Color lived in his face. Physicians pronounced him restored, and the court, hungry for any miracle that wasn't famine or tax, decided this was one.

Mara watched the evening devotion from the gallery above the chapel floor, a tray of untouched sweetmeats in her hands, her official reason for being anywhere always food.

Below, Queen Isolde knelt on the cold stone in cloth-of-gold, and prayed.

She prayed beautifully. That was the word Mara kept circling back to and hating. The queen's voice had been trained by decades of ceremony into something that filled stone rooms without effort, and she spent it lavishly — blessings on the harvest, on the army, on the king's health, on the kingdom's soul. The words were orthodox enough, dressed in acceptable scripture, but underneath them ran older rhythms. Mara heard them now that she knew to listen: the cadences the cook had used, the cadences Ivenna's fires answered. Names folded inside names. Invitations dressed as praise.

And the king sat beside her, listening, and grew stronger while she spoke.

Mara saw it happen. There was no other honest word for it. As the prayer went on, the king's color deepened. His shoulders squared. His eyes — flat all week, flat as coins — began to shine. And around him the gathered court swayed slightly, like wheat, like anything that bends toward what feeds it, faces softening into expressions of love so uniform it looked rehearsed, though no one had rehearsed it.

Devotion, pouring uphill. Into him.

Her stomach turned over, slow and cold. She had spent nine years swallowing poisons so that no one else had to feel what they did. She knew what toxins looked like in a body: the flush, the dilated pupil, the sweat, the tremor. What she was looking at was a poisoning in reverse. The court was the cup. The prayer was the draught. And the king drank and drank and glowed with it, and everyone who loved him was, without knowing it, holding him down and pouring.

The queen rose at last, and turned, and her eyes swept the gallery — a queen's habit, accounting for her audience — and stopped on Mara.

Hollow. That was the second word Mara kept coming back to. Isolde's face above her beautiful gown was hollowed the way the cook's had been hollowed, except the cook had been empty and smiling, and the queen was empty and awake. Awake and knowing. Whatever she poured out twice a day, she knew exactly what she was pouring, and into what, and why.

Their eyes held for two full seconds. Then the queen looked away, and the court exhaled, and Mara carried her untouched sweetmeats out of the gallery with her hands shaking so badly the silver rattled.

* * *

"You're pale," Sera said the next morning, when Mara came up her stairs. "I thought nothing got past that famous tongue of yours."

"Bad sleep."

"Mm." Sera poured two cups of wine from the flagon she kept for exactly these conversations, and slid one across. Old habit between informants: drink together, so neither can claim the other's cup was safe. Mara tasted hers automatically — almond, cherry, the ghost of tartrate preservative — and set it down untouched anyway.

"You've been watching the chapel," Sera said.

"I've been watching everything. It's my job."

"It's my job. Yours is dinner." Sera leaned back. "But yes. You've been watching the chapel, and you've noticed what I've noticed, and you haven't put it in your reports."

"What have you noticed?"

"That the king's recovery tracks the queen's devotions. Day for day." Sera said it flatly, the way she reported weather. "That the crowds grow. That three priests who raised questions about the theology of it have requested transfers, and one of them hasn't arrived at his new parish. That my agents in the lower town tell me the street preachers are saying the old gods walk again, and that the palace is where they walk first." She turned her cup slowly. "And that you, Lockridge, come down from those mountains smelling of woodsmoke and consecrated salt, and report to me exclusively about candle merchants."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the armory drill yard shouted counts in unison.

"You think I'm hiding something," Mara said.

"I think you're the most consistent source I've ever had, and consistency is its own kind of information." Sera's slate eyes lifted. "Every taster's reports vary. Fear varies. Fatigue varies. Yours never do. Either you're the calmest creature ever bred, or your reports are curated. And I've recently begun to suspect curation."

Mara weighed her answers the way she weighed flavors: bitter, sweet, salt. She could deny. Denial would buy time and cost credibility. She could confess the double game — and hang. Or she could give Sera a truth shaped like a lie, the most nourishing dish there was.

"There are things moving in Thornfell," she said. "Old things. Shrine-keepers lighting fires that shouldn't answer, and answering. I've been careful in my reporting because careless words about gods start panics, and panics start purges, and I've watched this court eat its own people before." She let her voice drop, wearied, honest-seeming. "You want to know what I know? I know the prayers are working again. I know it frightens me. And I know that whatever is making our king healthy again, it isn't medicine."

There. True, true, and true — arranged like stones across a stream so Sera would step exactly where Mara wanted and never notice the path was built.

Sera studied her for a long moment. "The queen," she said finally. "What do you see when you watch her?"

"Hollow eyes," Mara said. "And a woman performing a sacrament she doesn't believe in."

"And the king?"

Mara hesitated. And in the hesitation, she made a mistake she would remember: she told the truth. "His shadow moves late."

Sera's quill stopped.

For a moment neither of them breathed. Then Sera wrote one line, folded the paper, and burned it in the candle flame without ever taking her eyes off Mara. "Get out," she said quietly. "Come back Thursday. Bring me something true about the shrines — actually true — or don't bother coming back at all."

Mara went. On the stair, heart hammering, she inventoried the damage. The shadow comment had been a gift too valuable, a coin flashed where thieves could see it. But there was a use even in that: Sera would now watch the king herself, closely, with her own instruments. Let her see what Mara saw. Let the spymaster's dread do its work. A frightened Sera was a distracted Sera, and a distracted Sera checked fewer candle merchants' stories.

Probably.

* * *

That night Mara lay awake in her narrow room above the tasting office, listening to the palace breathe.

It breathed differently now. She'd noticed it on her return from Thornfell and hadn't known how to name it until tonight. The palace had always murmured — servants, soldiers, the sea-sound of a hundred private conversations. But the murmur had changed pitch. People whispered less about wages and lovers and more about blessings. Crops rising overnight in the river valleys. A chandler's daughter in the lower town whose fever broke on the ninth night. A lame ostler walking. Each story passed mouth to mouth and grew a little larger, the way stories did, and each retelling ended the same way: *and they say the king has the blessing on him.*

They say. They pray. They pour.

She got up, pried up the floorboard, and read her list by candle stub. Dead-eyed councils. The perfect signature. The late laughter. The shadow. And now the chapel, the swaying crowd, the deepening color — she'd started recording the devotions like doses, time and duration and visible effect, because that was how her mind worked, because everything in her life eventually became a ledger of what went into a body and what came out.

Nine years ago they had trained her tongue so thoroughly that poison had become her only constant companion, the one thing in the world that never lied to her. Bitter meant bitter. Almond meant almond. And what she had tasted in that chapel, in the air itself, thick enough to coat the throat, was the flavor of something being fed.

The court thinks it's loving him, she wrote at the bottom of the list. It's fattening something.

She blew out the candle and lay back down and stared at the ceiling beams, and the hunger of the thing below her — the vast patience drinking faith through a crown — seemed almost audible, a low hum under the palace's breathing, patient as tide.

Sleep wouldn't come. Around the second bell, footsteps stopped outside her door. Soft ones. Paused. Moved on.

She was reaching for her knife when the slip of paper appeared under the door instead — slid through the gap with a delicacy that told her more than the words did. No knock. No name. Paper of good stock, cream-colored, scented faintly with the queen's own orris water.

Four words, in a fine, controlled, exhausted hand:

*I must speak with you.*

Mara sat on the edge of her bed in the dark, the note in her fingers, and understood that the game had just changed shape entirely. She had been playing informant for Sera, playing courier for the mountains, playing invisible for nine years.

Now the queen wanted a meeting. The hollow-eyed woman at the center of the feeding, kneeling twice a day at the mouth of the thing, had looked up from her prayers and chosen the taster.

Mara turned the note over. Nothing on the back. No time, no place — only the assumption, chilling in its confidence, that Mara would come, and alone, and soon.

She touched her tongue to her finger and wetted the corner of the page, some old taster's reflex, testing even this, even a scrap of paper, for bitterness.

It tasted only of orris water and fear.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow she would go wherever queens summoned, carrying her scarred tongue and her coded lists and the growing, sickening suspicion that the person who knew the most about what wore the crown was the person kneeling closest to its mouth — and that Isolde had finally decided to talk.
