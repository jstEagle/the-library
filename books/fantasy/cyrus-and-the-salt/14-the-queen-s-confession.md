## 14. The Queen's Confession

Isolde had learned to pray the way other women learned to hold their breath underwater: quickly, efficiently, and with a private understanding that the surface was never far away.

She knelt at her private altar in the east tower while the palace slept, hands folded over a bowl of consecrated salt, and she gave the thing in her husband's skull exactly what it wanted. Devotion, measured out in careful spoons. Love, portioned like medicine. Enough to keep it fed. Never enough to make it strong.

It was a balance she had been keeping since she was nineteen years old, a bride from a family that had brokered the covenant three generations before her birth, and in all those years she had never once prayed for herself. She did not dare. The god heard its name even when it was unspoken, and Isolde Vane-Aldric—Isolde of House Merrow, before that—had long ago stopped believing there was any part of her it could not reach.

Tonight, though, her hands were shaking.

The salt in the bowl had gone gray at the edges. She had noticed it three days ago and told herself it was damp, told herself it was age, told herself every lie a woman tells when the truth requires her to act. But the salt was not damp. The salt was dying. And when the salt died, the leash went with it.

*He is getting stronger,* she thought, and corrected herself immediately, because that was the trap, wasn't it—the pronoun, the comforting fiction that the king and the hunger were separate things, that somewhere inside that broad silent body her Aldric still sat behind his own eyes like a prisoner at a barred window. *It is getting stronger.* The thirteenth moon was coming. The astrologers had named it an omen no one could interpret, which was the court's way of saying they knew perfectly well and preferred death to saying it aloud.

Isolde rose from the altar. Her knees ached. Everything ached these days, in the particular way of a body that has spent twenty years pretending to be comfortable.

She needed a taster.

* * *

Mara Lockridge came to the queen's solar at the appointed hour looking exactly as she always looked: clean, quiet, forgettable, a woman built out of beige and good posture. Isolde had studied her for years across the length of banquet tables—watching the taster sip from the king's cup, pause, swallow, nod—and had never once seen her face change. Nine years of swallowing poison, and nothing moved on that face but the throat.

That was why Isolde had chosen her. Not because Mara was loyal. Loyalty could be bought and bought back. She chose her because Mara Lockridge was the only person in the palace who had made a career of putting terrible things in her mouth and reporting honestly on what they were.

"Sit," Isolde said.

Mara did not sit. "Your Grace."

"I said sit." Isolde poured wine herself—no servants, the doors barred, the windows shuttered against the ears of a palace that listened with more than ears. "You will want to be seated for this."

Mara sat. Her hands settled in her lap, palms down, the stillness of a woman conserving everything. Isolde had seen soldiers do that. She had seen her husband do it, once, a lifetime ago, before the thing behind his eyes learned to smile with his mouth.

"You have served this crown nine years," Isolde began.

"Eight years and seven months."

The correction startled her. She had expected silence or flattery, not arithmetic. "Then you have earned the truth more than most." She set the wine between them, untouched. "I am going to tell you what is wearing my husband's crown. And then I am going to ask you to help me kill it."

For the first time in nine years, something moved on the taster's face. Not fear. Interest, maybe—or the recognition of interest, quickly buried. Isolde found herself absurdly grateful for it. At least one person in this gilded tomb was capable of surprise.

"You may find," Mara said quietly, "that I already know some of it."

*Of course you do,* Isolde thought. *You taste his food. You watch his shadow. You are the only servant in this palace who looks instead of kneels.* But she had rehearsed this confession for two nights, pacing her tower with her hands pressed over her mouth like a woman holding in a scream, and she would deliver it whole or not at all. A confession interrupted was a confession abandoned.

"My family brokered the covenant," she said. "House Merrow. You will not have heard of us—we were never great lords, we were *necessary* ones, which is worse and better. Three generations ago the borderlands starved. Do you know what the histories say caused it?"

"Blight," Mara said. "Three years of blight."

"The histories say that. The histories are wrong." Isolde's voice came out flat as ledger paper. "There was no blight. There was a god—a famine-god, old beyond naming, chained under Thornfell by priests whose names the mountains ate—and it broke loose. Or was let loose; the archives disagree, and I burned my copy of the archives myself, eleven years ago, so I suppose now they only say what I remember. It walked the valleys and the grain died standing upright in the fields. Whole villages. Whole *counties*. My grandmother watched her sisters eat bark and then eat each other's children, and she was a practical woman, so she did what practical people do."

"She negotiated," Mara said.

"She negotiated." Isolde laughed, and hated the sound. "Yes. Worship for survival. Faith for bread. The god agreed to sleep beneath the mountain if the crown above it fed it devotion—regular, rich, *royal* devotion. And the price of that arrangement, the part no history records, is that the god required a vessel. A body through which to receive its meals. A king."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, a dog began barking and then thought better of it.

"Every king since," Isolde said, "has worn it. My husband's grandfather. His father. And Aldric—" Here her voice did finally break, just slightly, a hairline fracture in good masonry. "Aldric was twenty-two. He rode out to be crowned and came back crowned, and by the time anyone understood what had happened, the binding was done, and the priests who might have undone it were dead or scattered or bought. My father arranged the marriage. He told me my duty plainly, the night before the wedding: *the queen's prayers keep the king gentle*. That has been my entire reign, Mara Lockridge. Twenty years of feeding a god by spoonfuls so it would not take the table whole."

Mara had not touched the wine. Her eyes had not left Isolde's face. "And the salt," she said. "The shipments from the shrines. The consecrated salt."

"Is the leash." Isolde turned the bowl toward her, showing the gray creeping edges. "Salt blessed at the first shrine holds what the founder bound. Every meal the king eats is seasoned with it. Every prayer I pour into the altar passes through it first, filtered, rationed, diluted until the god drinks thin gruel instead of feasting. It is the only reason my husband has been able to sit through a council meeting without the court watching him eat their worship alive." She pushed the bowl aside. "And it is failing. Look at it. Tell me what your tongue says, taster, because mine says the blessing is going out of it, and when it goes entirely—"

"When it goes," Mara said, "there is no leash."

"There is no leash, and there is no Aldric either. Whatever is left of him after twenty years inside that thing—it dies with the binding. The covenant completes on the thirteenth moon. The god stops wearing him and starts being him, permanently, and this kingdom becomes a congregation whether it consents or not." Isolde stood, because sitting had become unbearable, and crossed to the window where the moon hung over Thornfell like a coin someone had nailed to the sky. "I have prayed to keep it docile for twenty years. I have poured out devotion like a tavern-keeper pouring beer, measured to the drop. I have watched my husband's face for any flicker of the boy I married and told myself each flicker was real." She turned. "I am telling you this not because I trust you, taster. I don't trust anyone. I am telling you because I need a poisoner, and you are the best in the kingdom."

Silence held the room like a held breath.

Finally Mara said: "You want to poison a god."

"I want to poison its supper. There is a difference, and the difference is everything." Isolde came back to the table and leaned on it, close enough to see the fine scarring on the taster's tongue when she spoke. "The god feeds through ceremony. It cannot refuse a formal offering—that is written in the terms of the covenant itself, my grandmother's cleverest clause, *worship freely given shall be received in full*. On the eve of the twelfth turn there will be a feast. The court will pray over it. The king will preside. And whatever devotion rises off that table will go straight into the thing wearing my husband's face, and it will drink, and it will grow, and in one moon's turn it will finish growing forever." She laid her hands flat on the wood. "Unless the offering is poisoned. Consecrated salt and hellebore, laced through the feast—not enough to harm the court, the doses can be managed, you would manage them—but enough to sicken the thing that swallows the whole meal's worth of faith in one gulp. To make it recoil. To make it *weak*, for one hour, one wretched stolen hour, while whatever is left of my husband claws toward the surface."

"And in that hour?"

Isolde hesitated. This was the part she had not rehearsed, the part that shamed her, because admitting it meant admitting how little she had done in twenty years of complicity.

"In that hour he wrote to me once—years ago, a note hidden in a ledger, three minutes of himself scribbled onto paper. He asked me to find the shrine-keepers. The last true priestess, up in the high passes. Ivenna." Saying the name aloud felt like opening a door she had nailed shut. "I did nothing. I told myself the risk was too great, that a weak god was manageable, that my duty was stability. I told myself that for eleven years." She straightened. "I will not tell it to myself for a twelfth. In that hour, the king must be taken to the shrine, or the priestess brought to the king, and the binding must begin to come apart before the god recovers. You are the only person in this palace who travels the Thornfell roads. You are the only person who knows them well enough to move unseen."

"You've had me watched."

It was not a question, and Isolde did not insult her by pretending otherwise. "Sera Vane watches everyone. Including me. Especially me. Which is why this conversation never happened, and why you will answer me now, in this room, with the doors barred." She slid the bowl of graying salt across the table until it rested between them like a treaty awaiting signature. "Will you help me poison a god, Mara Lockridge? Knowing that if we fail, the god will know our names, and knowing what it does with names?"

Something passed over the taster's face then—something old and tired and strangely amused, the look of a woman being offered a dangerous job by a woman who should have done it decades ago.

"You're doing this for him," Mara said. "Not for the kingdom."

"Do not mistake me for a heroine." Isolde's chin came up, and twenty years of guilt put on its uniform and stood at attention. "I did not act when action might have saved him cleanly. I acted when stability required it, and I stayed silent when silence was safer, and I prayed prettily for twenty years while the thing I helped bind ate my husband from the inside. If I move now, it is because I can no longer bear the sound of my own prayers. Call that love if it comforts you. Call it guilt. Call it duty. I have worn all three like crowns and none of them fit." She drew a breath. "But I am asking. Kneeling, if you require it. A queen kneeling to a food-taster, behind barred doors, begging her to commit treason against heaven."

For a long moment Mara said nothing. Then her hand moved—slowly, deliberately—and touched the rim of the salt bowl.

"The hellebore will need preparing so it doesn't bitter the honey," she said. "The god tastes through the court's tongues, in a way. If the food turns bad on the palate, the devotion sours with it. We'll need the poison sweet." She looked up. "How many days until the feast?"

"Six."

"Then I'll need four of them, and access to the queen's kitchens, and a reason for Sera Vane not to ask why Her Majesty suddenly wants her taster baking." Mara rose, smooth as water finding its level. At the door she paused, one hand on the bar, and glanced back with something almost like pity. "Your Grace. When the hour comes—if we get our hour—you should be the one to speak to him. Whatever is left of him. Wives get the first words. Even guilty wives."

The door closed behind her.

Isolde stood alone in the solar with the dying salt and the nailed-up sky, and for the first time in twenty years, she did not pray. She simply breathed—in, out, the rhythm of a woman who had finally, finally stopped holding her breath—

—and in the silence, from the direction of the king's chambers, faint as a landslide deciding whether to fall, she heard something singing her name.
