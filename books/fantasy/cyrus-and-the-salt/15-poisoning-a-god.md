## 15. Poisoning a God

The queen's letter came tucked inside a hollowed wheel of cheese, which told Mara two things: that Isolde had a sense of humor she'd never once shown at court, and that somewhere in the palace there was still a servant brave enough to carry treason in dairy.

*Midnight. The old nursery. Come alone. Tell no one—not even your shadow.*

Mara ate a sliver of the cheese first, out of habit, and waited an hour to make sure it was only cheese.

* * *

The old nursery sat at the end of the east wing, past rooms where the dust had been undisturbed so long it had developed opinions. No child had slept there in twenty years. The cradle by the window had been carved with mountain goats, and someone—Isolde, presumably, or a version of Isolde from before—had painted their horns gold. It was the kind of room that made grief feel like furniture.

The queen stood at the window with her back straight as a pikestaff, wearing plain wool instead of court silk, and when she turned, Mara saw that the hollowness behind her eyes had cracked open into something worse: purpose.

"You received my note."

"I tasted your note."

A ghost of a smile. "Good. You'll do." She crossed to the cold hearth and crouched, working loose a stone with her fingernails. From the cavity she drew a small wooden box, and from the box, three items laid out on a fold of linen like relics: a twist of grey-white crystals, a dried stalk of flowers with black throats, and a scrap of vellum covered in cramped, ancient handwriting.

"Consecrated salt from Ivenna's shrine," Isolde said. "Hellebore root, ground fine. And the recipe my grandmother's grandmother wrote down after the last time anyone tried this."

"The last time anyone—" Mara kept her voice flat. "Queen Isolde. How many people have tried?"

"One. A taster, actually." The queen's mouth did something complicated. "It didn't work. But he learned things worth writing down. Things about *dosage*."

Mara looked at the salt. At the flower. She thought of the cook kneeling in his own flour with a smile carved into his face, and of the king's shadow lagging half a heartbeat behind him, and of the way prayer had started moving through the palace like water through a breached hull.

"Poisoning a god," she said aloud, because saying it quietly would let it sound sane.

"Poisoning his *meal*." Isolde set her hand on the box. "He eats through us, Lockridge. Every hymn, every candle, every weeping peasant at the feast of the eleventh turn—that's his supper. So we serve him something bitter for once. Salt to cut him open. Hellebore to slow him down. And for one night—one night—the man underneath gets to breathe."

"Why me? You have poisoners of your own. Sera Vane has whole cellars of people who do this sort of thing."

"Sera Vane reports to the crown." Isolde's voice went very quiet. "And the crown is not Aldric. As for my own poisoners—I don't know whose ears they belong to anymore. You are the only person in this palace whose loyalty I can't trace, because you have none. Everyone says so. You taste his food and feel nothing." Her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "I need someone who feels nothing, Lockridge. For one night, feeling nothing is the safest thing in this kingdom."

Mara thought about telling her. About the map under the floorboard and the smuggler roads and the nine years of careful, cowardly nothing. Instead she said, "When?"

"The Feast of the Turning. Six nights from now. The whole court will kneel and sing, and the thing will drink until it's drunk." Isolde closed the box. "And you will be standing behind the king's chair with a goblet, exactly where you always stand. No one ever looks at you. It's the most useful curse in this palace."

It wasn't flattery. It was inventory. That was why Mara believed her.

"I'll need the kitchen," she said. "Unsupervised access. And I'll need you to pray harder than usual that week—loudly, visibly. If the thing notices us preparing, we're both dead, and probably several cooks besides."

"Praying is what I'm best at," the queen said, and there was no pride in it at all.

* * *

Six days. Mara spent them the way she'd spent nine years learning to spend them: invisibly, precisely, one harmless motion stacked on another until the pile became a plan.

She toured the kitchens three times on legitimate taster's business, cataloguing. The Feast of the Turning ran on ritual, and ritual meant fixed points: the ceremonial broth blessed at the altar rail, the boar roasted with consecrated herbs, the wine poured over the king's hands and then drunk by the court, because the god apparently liked its worship with a vintage. Twelve courses. Forty servants. A thousand places for a mistake.

She ground the hellebore herself, in a mortar borrowed from the apothecary's dead stock, wearing gloves and breathing through a wet cloth. Hellebore slowed the heart; in a man it stopped one. In a god, according to the founder's scrap of vellum, it did something stranger—it made him *heavy*, the way a drunk man is heavy, too muddled to hold what he'd caught. The salt did the cutting. Salt was honest; salt remembered being sea; salt refused to dissolve quietly into anything. Together they wouldn't kill the hunger. They'd stun it.

The problem was the broth. Everything else could be dosed lightly, spread across forty dishes, deniable. But the broth was served first, to the king alone, ladled by the high priest's own hand—and the high priest stood within arm's reach of the king all evening.

So Mara did the thing she was actually good at. Not poisons. People.

She learned that the high priest's brother held debts in the lower city. That the head cellarman's daughter wanted a place at court. That the second undercook—a nervous boy named Tam who reminded her unpleasantly of another boy, one she'd known on a mountain road—was devoted to the old gods in a private, terrified way, and prayed every night to a shrine-keeper's name that hadn't been spoken aloud in decades.

She used none of them cruelly. She simply arranged, with quiet suggestions passed through quiet mouths, that Tam would be carrying the broth on the night, and that Tam's route from the altar rail to the dais would pass directly behind the queen's chair, where a taster might pause to adjust a sleeve, or steady a tray, or drop—no, never drop, dropping was amateur—one small pinch of grey-white honesty into a bowl of soup.

Tam never knew. That was the point. The best poisonings were the ones where only two people in the room understood what was happening, and one of them was pretending to be furniture.

On the fifth night, Isolde came to the tasting chamber herself, unannounced, which sent two junior stewards into visible cardiac events. She watched Mara work through the evening meal—quail, again, always quail—without touching anything.

"He asked about you today," the queen said suddenly. "The king. He asked who the woman at the wine was."

Mara's spoon paused over the sauce. "What did you tell him?"

"That you were nobody." Isolde's jaw tightened. "And he looked at me for a long moment, and something behind his eyes moved, and he said—very gently, the way you'd speak to a stranger—'Nobody. How wonderful for her.'" She pressed her lips together. "It's getting stronger. The feasts feed it faster than the daily prayers. Tomorrow night it may not surface at all, even with the hellebore. Are you certain about the dose?"

"I'm certain about nothing," Mara said. "I've read one scrap of paper written by a dead man who failed. But yes. The dose is right."

Isolde laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it. "You know what I prayed for, when I was a girl? To marry a kind man and live in a house near the sea." She looked at the quail, the candles, the whole gilded machinery of the room. "Be careful tomorrow, Lockridge. If it wakes while you're standing there—if it looks at you the way it looked at the cook—"

"It won't look at me. Nobody does."

This time the queen didn't laugh. She put her hand briefly, awkwardly, on Mara's shoulder—the first unprompted touch Mara had received in years—and left.

Mara sat alone with the cooling quail and realized her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with detached professional interest, the way she'd watch any unfamiliar symptom, and waited for them to stop.

They took longer than usual.

* * *

The Feast of the Turning began, as all the worst things in her life had begun, with everyone smiling.

The great hall blazed with four hundred candles. The court wore its grief dressed as joy—she could see it now, now that she knew what to look for: the nobles laughing a beat too loudly, the ladies' eyes shining with tears they hadn't chosen, the whole room humming with a devotion that felt less like love than like weather. The god drank it in. She could almost see it happening, the air above the dais thickening, sweetening, like the smell of fruit going over.

King Aldric sat beneath the antlered crown and smiled his perfect smile, and did not blink enough.

The broth came first. Tam carried it with the trembling reverence of a boy holding his own heart, and his route took him past the queen's chair, and Mara adjusted a sleeve, and the pinch of salt went into the bowl between one heartbeat and the next, and then Tam was bowing and the high priest was blessing and the spoon was rising to the king's mouth.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. Nothing would happen for an hour. That was the design—hellebore worked slow, salt worked slower, and the founder's notes had warned in his cramped hand: *it will fight. It will seem awake. Do not believe it. Wait for the heaviness.*

So the feast rolled on. Boar and song. Wine and weeping. The court sang the hymn of the turning on the third course, a thousand voices rising in unison, and Mara stood behind the king's chair and felt the sound go through her chest like a physical weight, feeding something, fattening something, and she thought: *this is what a barn full of cattle sounds like.*

The fourth course. The fifth. On the sixth, mid-laugh, the king stopped.

It was nothing, at first. A pause. His hand hovered over his cup. Then his smile flickered—actually flickered, like a guttering lamp—and his shoulders dropped half an inch, as if someone had cut strings. He blinked, and when his eyes opened they were wrong in a new way: not empty, not hungry, but *lost*. A man waking in a room he doesn't recognize.

The hall didn't notice. The hall was busy adoring.

But Queen Isolde noticed, and her fan stilled. And Mara noticed, and stepped closer to the chair.

"Aldric?" Isolde said softly. Just the name. His name.

His head turned toward her slowly, like a ship coming about. And then King Aldric did something he had not done in public for years, perhaps decades: his face *changed*. Grief moved across it. Wonder. Horror. His hand rose and touched his own cheek as though checking whether the skin belonged to him.

"Isolde," he whispered. "Gods' mercy. Isolde, how long—"

"Hush." She was gripping his arm below the table, knuckles white. "Hush, love. Not here. Not yet."

"How long," he said again, and his voice cracked down the middle, and around him the court laughed at some joke Mara hadn't heard, because the music was loud and the god was drinking and no one—no one—was watching the king except a taster and a queen.

His gaze swept the table, the candles, the thousand faces, and Mara watched comprehension arrive in him like a tide: the scale of it, the years of it. He swayed. The heaviness was taking hold; she could see the hellebore dragging at him, the salt doing its patient corrosive work. His eyes were filming. His breath came thick.

But he fought it. She saw that too. Somewhere inside the drowning man, a hand found a rope.

"Who—" His head turned, hunting, and found her. Standing behind his chair. Invisible. Except that his eyes locked onto hers with the desperate accuracy of a falling man grabbing a branch, and he said, clearly, in a voice pitched to reach exactly two rows:

"You. The taster. What's your name?"

Every instinct she owned screamed at her to say nothing, to be furniture, to be nobody. A thousand faces. A high priest three steps away. A god overhead, drunk but dreaming, and dreams have edges.

"Mara," she heard herself say. "Mara Lockridge."

"Mara." He said it like a man memorizing a password. His hand shot out and caught her wrist—quick, weak, burning hot. "Listen. Listen to me while I can still—" His eyes lost focus, snapped back. "There is a woman in the mountains. Ivenna. Shrine of the First Salt. She knows what I am. She knows how it ends. Go to her—before the twelfth turn—do you hear me? Before the twelfth turn, or there won't be a me left to save. There won't be anything left at all. It's *almost strong enough*—"

His grip crushed her wrist. His pupils blew wide, then shrank, then widened again, two lanterns fighting a wind.

"Please." The word came out wrecked. A king, begging a servant, in front of a kingdom that couldn't see him. "I remember my daughter's face now. I want to keep it. Please. Find her. Find Ivenna. *Tell her the vessel must be willing.*"

Then his face smoothed over like water closing above a sunk stone, and the smile reassembled itself, and the king of the realm raised his cup and toasted the harvest with a voice like warm honey, and the court roared its love, and his fingers slid from Mara's wrist.

She stepped back into her place behind the chair. Furniture. Nobody.

But her wrist burned where he'd gripped it, and in her ear those words rang like a struck bell: *the vessel must be willing.* Willing to do what? She thought of the founder's scrap, of the dead man who had tried and failed, of the phrase she'd read six times and not understood—*the hunger takes a host or it takes nothing*—and a cold thread began to pull tight somewhere beneath her ribs.

Across the hall, Sera Vane was not singing. The spymaster stood by the eastern pillars with a cup she wasn't drinking, and she was looking—at the king, at the queen, at the space behind the king's chair where Mara stood.

Their eyes met across four hundred candles and a thousand voices.

Sera raised her cup, very slightly. A toast. An acknowledgment. A promise that this was not over.

Mara raised nothing. She folded her hands, invisible, obedient, and began counting the hours until dawn, and the miles to the mountains, and the twelve turns of a moon that was running out faster than any road she'd ever mapped.
