## 16. Hunted Through Thornfell

The pigeon reached her at dusk, which should have been the first warning. Sera Vane's birds were punctual to the minute; this one arrived two hours late, and it arrived alone, without the counterweight cipher that meant *this message has been checked.*

Mara read it in the loft above the taster's quarters, by the light of a single stub of tallow, and felt the words go through her like a fishhook.

*Report to my chambers at your earliest convenience regarding shrine intelligence. Bring your maps of movement patterns for the northern passes. — V.*

Maps. She had never given Sera maps. She had given Sera three carefully curated names from her smuggler contacts, two of whom were dead, one of whom had never existed. Sera knew that. Sera was many cold things, but she was not sloppy. Which meant the message wasn't an invitation.

It was a net, drawn closed with courtly grammar.

Mara sat very still in the dark of the loft and did the arithmetic she'd been doing in fragments since the night she'd slipped Ivenna's name into a false report and watched Sera swallow it whole. Nine years of building a version of herself that never varied, never stumbled, never gave anything away—and she had gotten greedy. She had wanted the game too much. She had fed Sera a lie with just enough truth in it to be delicious, and Sera Vane did not eat lies. She dissected them.

The bird shifted on its perch. Mara looked at it for a long moment—brown, ordinary, one clouded eye—and then she took it gently in both hands and broke its neck, because whatever else happened tonight, no second message was going out behind the first.

Then she went downstairs, and began to run.

* * *

Nine years of small thefts. That was what it came down to, laid end to end: a copper bit here, an hour there, a favor owed and never called in. A dry shirt rolled tight and hidden in the hollow of a chimney flue that hadn't been swept since the old king's reign. A knife sewn into the hem of a servant's cloak nobody would miss. Sixteen silver marks distributed across four hiding places, because a woman who kept all her money in one purse deserved to die poor.

She moved through the palace the way she'd learned to move through it as a girl: not quickly, quickly being loud, but *smoothly*, like water finding the cracks in stone. The kitchens first, because the kitchens connected to everything. Old Breva was dead now—kneeling, smiling, hollowed—but his scullions still worked the night ovens, still left the south corridor unwatched between the second and third bread turns, because bread waited for no man's murder investigation.

She went down the servants' stair with her shoes in one hand, past the wine cellar where she'd spent so many evenings pretending to check casks, and out through the ash gate, where the gatekeeper owed her for the winter he'd been quietly poisoned by his own brother-in-law and she had tasted his food for a week without telling him why.

"Mistress Lockridge." He blinked at her, candle-stupid. "Late errand?"

"Late supper," she said. "For the spymaster."

He let her through without another word. Everyone let her through, everywhere, always. That was the whole architecture of her life: she was the woman whose reports never varied, and therefore she was furniture, and furniture passed through doors.

Tonight the furniture was running.

* * *

The city gates were the problem, as she'd known they would be. Sera would have sealed them within the hour of deciding Mara was a traitor, and Sera decided things fast—that was her one virtue. So Mara didn't take the road. She took the river.

There was a culvert under the east wall where the millrace ran out of the lower town, and nine years ago a boy named Tam had shown it to her for the price of a meat pie. Tam who ran messages along the Thornfell passes. Tam who was fourteen years old and laughed like a door hinge and trusted her completely, and who was going to learn soon enough that the woman he'd shown the culvert to had used it exactly once before tonight, on a practice run, and had told no one, ever.

The water was snowmelt and it hit her like a fist. She let it. There was no way through the culvert that didn't involve drowning halfway, and the trick—which Tam had demonstrated with a grin, walking upright along the ledge under the arch while the current tore at his waist—was to stop fighting the cold. Cold was information. Cold told you where the walls were.

She came out the far side in the willow flats below the town, soaked to the bone, lips blue, and lay in the mud gasping while torches bloomed on the wall above her like a crop of terrible orange flowers.

They were looking for her already. Good. That meant she'd been right to run.

She got up and went north.

* * *

The thing about smuggler roads—the real ones, the ones that existed only in Mara's head and on a map folded into eight layers beneath a floorboard she had, tonight, pried up and taken with her—was that they weren't roads at all. They were *arguments*. A smuggler's path was a sustained contradiction: here is a cliff face, and yet men cross it; here is a gorge with no bridge, and yet the grain gets over; here is a border that exists on paper and nowhere else in the world.

She climbed the goat track above Miller's Delve in the dark, hands finding holds her body remembered though her mind had filed them away years ago, and thought about how she had built all of it for one purpose: to leave. Nine years, and every secret path, every cached coin, every bribable mouth had pointed one direction—out. Over the passes, down the far side, into some valley where nobody knew the word *taster* and she could be a woman instead of a function.

And now here she was, using every inch of it, running for her life with Sera's hunters behind her—and the direction was the same but the purpose had turned itself inside out. She wasn't leaving. She was *delivering*. Somewhere ahead of her, up in the high white country where the shrines stood, a priestess named Ivenna held the answer to what was eating the king alive, and Mara was carrying the only thing more dangerous than the truth: the method of ending it.

She had written it all down in the last hour before fleeing—everything the archives said, everything about Cyrus the founder-taster, everything about the seal and the thirteen days—compressed onto three scraps of oilcloth in a hand so small it would take Sera's cryptographers a week to read them without firelight and patience. The scraps rode against her sternum now, inside her shift, warm as a heartbeat.

If the hunters caught her, they would have to catch all of her first.

* * *

Dawn found her at the Hollow Ford, lying flat in the reeds on the near bank while six riders picked their way along the far shore with lanterns still lit, which told her they were palace men, lowland men, men who hated the dark and trusted horses too much.

She watched them and did the arithmetic of pursuit, which was the same arithmetic as poison, really: dosage, timing, tolerance. Sera would send riders down the honest roads first, because honest roads were fast. Then, when those came back empty, she would start pulling the informants' network apart thread by thread, and every thread led to Mara's private geography. By tomorrow night, Sera would know about the culvert. By the day after, the goat track. Sera was patient the way winter was patient.

Which meant Mara had perhaps three days of advantage, and the high passes took five in good weather.

So she wouldn't take the high passes.

She ate a strip of dried lamb from her cache at the Delve—cached eleven years ago, still edible, because salt forgave almost everything—and studied the ridge to the west where the shrine-fires had started appearing again, little orange stitches in the black hem of the mountains. Between her and them lay the Scythe, a spine of rock that no honest guide would attempt and every smuggler feared, and beyond the Scythe, the old drover tunnels under Thornfell itself, abandoned since the famine years, unmapped except by her.

Three days, if the tunnels held. Two, if she dared the Scythe at night.

She dared it. Of course she dared it. She had spent nine years being careful, and careful had bought her nothing but the ability to watch people she liked die kneeling in flour.

* * *

The Scythe at midnight was less a climb than a negotiation with gravity conducted entirely in fear. Wind came off the icefields in long flat shoves that tried to peel her off the rock like bark, and twice her foot found purchase that wasn't there, and twice her body did the ancient animal arithmetic and solved it without consulting her. Above her the stars wheeled, indifferent. Below her, once, she saw a lantern swing along a trail she'd abandoned hours ago, and understood that Sera's men were good—better than she'd priced—and that the race had narrowed to something like a throat.

Near the summit she found a shrine.

Not one of the new fires—a true one, ancient, a cup of carved stone bolted to the rockface itself, holding a crust of salt that the weather had worn into the shape of a wave. Offerings. Her father had been a border man; she remembered the custom from before the famines, from stories. Travelers left salt for safe passage.

She stood on a ledge above a mile of darkness, frozen, starving, hunted, and she looked at that little cup of stone, and she did not pray.

That was the discipline now. That was the whole of it. She could want—God, she wanted, she wanted safe passage with a desperation that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal—but wanting was the hook, and the hunger that wore the king's crown fished with want. Cyrus's notes had been explicit: *the vessel must belong to nothing, hope for nothing, address nothing.* Thirteen days of being no one's.

She scraped a pinch of salt from the cup with her thumbnail anyway—not an offering, a *withdrawal*—and put it on her tongue. It tasted like the sea tastes when you've never seen it: vast, and cold, and older than anyone's grief.

"Nothing asked," she told the wind. "Nothing answered. We understand each other."

Then she went over the top.

* * *

The drover tunnels were worse than the Scythe, because at least the Scythe was honest about trying to kill her.

They ran under Thornfell for miles, cut in the famine generations to move grain the crown couldn't tax, and they were collapsing in slow motion, ceiling dust sifting down like grey snow at every step, support beams gone soft as bread. Mara walked them by touch and memory, following the tally-marks she'd scratched beside the way-stations on her only previous passage, seven years ago, when she'd mapped them in a single terrified day and sworn never to return.

Somewhere behind her, muffled through half a mountain of stone, she heard the hunters reach the Scythe and begin to argue about it. Their voices carried down the tunnels like insects in a wall. They would come. Sera's men always came; it was why she hired them.

At the third way-station Mara found water dripping through a fault in the rock, cold and mineral-bright, and she drank, and while she drank she allowed herself—for exactly ten heartbeats, timed against her own pulse—one thought of the palace. One thought of Isolde's hollow eyes. One thought of Aldric scribbling his three stolen minutes of warning in a council ledger, handwriting like a ghost's. One thought of Breva, kneeling in flour, smiling, empty.

Then she shut the door on all of it, because attachment was poison now, and she was the kingdom's foremost expert on poison.

Ten heartbeats a day. That was her ration. She had survived on stricter rations than that before.

She pressed on into the dark, and the mountain settled around her with a sound like a long-held breath, and somewhere far above, the twelfth moon was climbing toward its fullness whether she walked or slept or died in the dark.

* * *

She surfaced at dawn on the far side of Thornfell, into a country that had gone wrong.

She knew it immediately, the way you know a room has been searched even before you see the drawers hanging open. The high valley should have been empty—sheep country, hard country, two shepherd families and a shrine at most. Instead there were people on the paths. Dozens of them. Walking uphill, unhurried, in silence, carrying nothing.

Not refugees. Refugees carry things.

These walked with their hands loose at their sides and their faces turned toward the peaks, and as Mara dropped behind a boulder and watched, an old woman at the rear of the column paused, lifted her head like a hound scenting rain, and smiled. Not at anything. Just smiled, the way Breva had smiled, kneeling in the flour.

Mara's stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like something shifting in its sleep.

The god wasn't waiting for the thirteenth moon anymore. It was calling them out early—calling the faithful up the mountains to be eaten in advance of the feast, a glutton tasting the meal while it cooked. And these people were coming *willingly*. That was the part that put ice down her spine, past fear into something colder. Fear you can run from. But a hunger that people loved—

Behind her, far below at the tunnel mouth, she heard shouting: the hunters, through at last, close enough now that running was the only conversation left. Ahead of her the hillsides crawled with the smiling and the devout. And somewhere above it all, past the white shoulders of the peaks, stood the Shrine of the First Salt, and Ivenna, and the only door out of this story that didn't end with everyone kneeling.

Mara checked the oilcloth scraps against her chest, put her head down, and started to climb.

The escape plan she had built across nine years of invisible servitude—the routes, the caches, the borders of her imagined freedom—was finally carrying her somewhere after all.

Just never, not once, in the direction she had drawn it for.

And on the wind behind her, thin as a wire, she heard a voice that was not the wind say, quite clearly, using the mouth of some distant smiling pilgrim:

*"You're late, taster."*
