## 17. The Price of the Pass

The rain started an hour after dawn and did not stop.

Mara climbed through it with her hood down, because hoods muffled sound and she needed every sound there was: the drum of water on stone, the groan of wind through the pines, and beneath it—when the wind fell—the voices of the men following her.

Four of them. Sera Vane did not send amateurs.

She had counted them at the ford below Hallowmere, two hours back: four riders, then four dismounted men moving on foot through the trees with the unhurried competence of people who had done this before. They weren't shouting her name. They weren't crashing through the underbrush like the border reivers of her childhood. They were simply walking, in a loose crescent, the way you walk when you know the quarry is tired and the mountain is on your side.

Nine years she had spent building her escape out of these mountains. Nine years of walking the smuggler roads under the pretext of provisioning runs, of memorizing which ledges held frost and which streams ran shallow enough to cross in boots, of scratching route-marks into bark in a cipher of her own invention. She had imagined this flight a hundred times—herself, a satchel of coin, the border by the third dawn, a new name in a town where no one had ever tasted anything for anyone.

She had not imagined doing it with Sera Vane's hunters behind her and a dead king's plea in her ears.

*Find Ivenna before the twelfth turn.*

The path narrowed where it cut across the face of the ravine. Mara stopped in the lee of an overhanging slab and let the rain hit her bare face, cold enough to sharpen her, and made herself look down at what she'd been walking toward for an hour.

The crossing.

Every smuggler on Thornfell knew the Rope Span. It wasn't a bridge, exactly—it was what happened when the ravine narrowed to ten feet of sheer-sided granite and someone, a hundred years ago, had driven iron pins into the rock on either side and strung chains and planks between them. The planks were mostly gone now, rotted or stolen, but the chains remained, slick with rain, and the smugglers' trick was to cross hand over hand along the near chain, boots on the far one, with a hundred feet of white water and broken stone underneath. Mara had crossed it perhaps forty times. The first time she'd been thirteen and so frightened she'd vomited on the far bank. The last time she'd done it in the dark, carrying a satchel of contraband salt, and hadn't thought about it at all.

Below the crossing, the ravine walls leaned together like a book closing. There was a shelf of stone—a shelf, not a bridge—wedged across the gap forty feet down, and above it, on the southern wall, a scar in the rock face where a slab the size of a cottage door hung by a lip of older stone and a tree root that had been dying for years.

She had noticed that slab on her third crossing, at sixteen, and had thought, with the cold practicality that had already begun to replace everything soft in her: *one good lever and the crossing dies with it.*

She had never thought she'd be the one pulling the lever with people behind her.

*They'll find another way,* she told herself. *It's a delay, not a grave. There's a ford two days north. They'll go around. They'll be angry and wet and alive.*

She knew, even as she thought it, that she was lying. Sera Vane did not send men who turned back at inconveniences. Sera Vane sent men who would cross a broken ravine on a fallen slab, or climb down the walls, or find the smuggler's ladder on the eastern side that only three living people knew about. Delay was not the same as safety. Delay was the only thing she had.

And there was the other thing, the thing she kept not looking at directly: the hunters had seen her face. They knew her route. If any of them reached Ivenna's shrine ahead of her—or reported her heading back to court—everything she'd climbed the mountain to do would rot before it started.

*Survival first,* said the girl who had been dosed with arsenic at nine years old and had learned that the world kept only one commandment. *You can grieve later. You can grieve when you're alive.*

She had said that to herself so many times over nine years that it had worn smooth as a river stone, and she no longer felt it as a lie.

She checked the ravine once more, memorizing angles, and then turned north along the wall to the old shepherd's cache where she'd left rope and a pry bar two winters ago, on a provisioning run, for reasons she hadn't examined at the time.

* * *

The cache was intact. The rope was dry. The pry bar was rusted at the tip but sound, and Mara sat under the stone overhang for a full minute, out of the rain, and let herself eat: hard cheese, a heel of black bread, snowmelt from a puddle in the rock. Her hands were steady. That was the thing about her hands—poison had trained them, and fear had never once made them shake. She could hold a cup of cyanide-laced honey wine without a tremor and smile at the court while she waited to see if she'd live.

She wished, not for the first time, that her heart could learn what her hands knew.

She heard them before she saw them—boots on wet stone, and then a voice, young and carrying, pitched low but not low enough for rain-muffled air.

"—says she'll take the high track past the Span. It's the only way north that isn't the valley road."

Mara went cold in a way the rain couldn't explain.

She knew that voice.

*Wick.*

Tam Wick. Sixteen years old, maybe seventeen now—she'd last seen him two years ago, a wiry border boy with a gap in his grin who had run messages for her along the valley road for three winters. Not for coin, mostly. For the pleasure of being trusted with something. He'd carried her sealed notes to the shrine-keepers when the shrine-keepers still had fires to keep, and he'd never once asked what was in them, and once, when a toll-man had grabbed his collar, he'd swallowed the note rather than give it up and spent a night in a cell for it. He'd come to her after, black-eyed and delighted, and said, *Did I do it right, mistress Lockridge?*

She'd told him: *You never ran messages for me. If anyone asks, you never knew me.*

And he'd grinned through the split in his lip and said, *Never heard of you, mistress.*

Now his voice came up the ravine trail with three grown men, and one of them answered him in Sera Vane's clipped cadence filtered through a stranger's throat: "Then we take the Span. Move."

Mara pressed her back against the stone and did the arithmetic she did not want to do.

If she ran north now, they'd reach the crossing within the hour, cross it within two, and be a half-day behind her on a trail she could not leave—there was no other way to Ivenna's shrine from here; the high track was the high track, and the shrine sat at the end of it like a knot at the end of a rope. They would follow her to the door. They would find the shrine. They would find Ivenna, and the rites, and the last priestess of the old gods, and they would put an end to the only plan in the world that might save the king and everyone the god would eat after him.

If she cut the crossing now, while they were still below the shelf, the slab would come down and the trail would close. Days, maybe a week, before anyone found another route—and Sera would not send more men through the passes in winter on a rumor. She'd recall them. She'd have to.

And the men on the trail would be under the slab when it fell.

*They'll hear it groan,* she told herself. *They'll see it move. They'll run. Wick is quick—Wick is the quickest boy on the border—he'll run.*

She knew the geometry of it. She had stood under that slab. There was no warning you could outrun at that distance. The rock came down in the time it took to turn your head.

Mara closed her eyes and saw, absurdly, the king's face in his one clear hour—the poisoned feast, the terrible waking, his hand gripping her wrist with the strength of a drowning man: *Find her. Before the twelfth turn. Everything else is already eaten.*

One boy against a kingdom. One boy against the thing that wore the crown and drank the faithful and had eaten the cook from the inside out until he knelt smiling in his own kitchen, hollowed like fruit.

*You can grieve later. You can grieve when you're alive.*

"Gods forgive me," she whispered, and heard, in the rain, no answer at all. The old gods were waking, everyone said so. Crops rose overnight. The dying sat up in their beds. But no god answered her, then, and she would remember that, later, and wonder if they had simply chosen not to listen, or if they had listened and let her do it, and which was worse.

She went up the wall.

* * *

The scar in the rock face was a scramble of forty feet, wet and ugly, and she did it with the pry bar slung across her back and her fingers reading the stone the way her tongue read wine: here a hold, there a lie, here the crack that went all the way down. The dying root was where she remembered it, a thick brown cable running out of the slab's underside into the wall, and beside it the lip of old stone that was the slab's only other grip on the world.

She wedged herself into the crack beside it, rain streaming off her hoodless head, and looked down.

The trail below was empty. They were coming. She could hear Wick's voice again, closer now, laughing about something—one of the men must have slipped, and the boy had laughed, because Wick laughed at everything, at the cold and the wet and the toll-man's cell, because he was sixteen and the border had not yet beaten it out of him the way the palace had beaten everything out of her.

Mara set the bar under the slab's lip, above the root.

Her hands were steady.

That was the horror of it, she thought. Not the act ahead, but the steadiness. Nine years of the palace had built her into a tool, and tools did not shake. The taster's tongue that knew bitter almond in honey. The taster's hands that never spilled. The taster's heart that had been dosed and dosed and dosed until it, too, was proof against feeling—except that it wasn't, was it, because here, now, wedged in a crack in a mountain with a boy's laughter rising up the trail, she felt everything, all of it, the whole drowned ocean of it, and her hands did not shake anyway, and she understood that this was not the same thing as not feeling. It was feeling and doing it regardless, and no one had ever told her that this was what courage was, because no one had ever thanked her for anything, and courage without witness just looks like competence.

The laughter came around the bend. She saw them: three men in oiled cloaks and a boy in a wool cap, Wick's gap-toothed face tipped up toward the ravine, and for one insane moment she thought he looked up at her, straight up, forty feet of rain between them, and she waited for him to see her.

He didn't. He was looking at the crossing ahead, the chains and the missing planks, and he said something to the man beside him—she read the shape of it more than the words—*I've done this one, it's not so bad, you keep your weight on the far chain*—and the man said, "Then you go first."

*No,* Mara thought. *No, don't—wait—walk back, go around, take the valley road, forget you ever knew me—*

*Never heard of you, mistress.*

She put her weight on the bar.

The root went first. It parted with a sound like a wet bone breaking, and the slab shifted, groaned, hung—and the men below looked up, all four of them, and in the half-second before it fell she saw Wick's face change, saw him understand, saw him turn and push the man beside him toward the wall with the quick selfless instinct of a boy who ran messages and swallowed notes and laughed at everything—

The rock came down.

The sound was enormous. It swallowed the ravine, the rain, her own scream—she hadn't known she was screaming until it was over and her throat was raw. Dust and spray went up in a white wall. Stones the size of fists hailed down the trail. The shelf of stone below the crossing took the slab's weight and held, and the gap where the trail had been was now a ramp of broken rock, passable, eventually, with time and rope and no one buried under it.

Two of the men had made the wall. She saw them pressed against the southern face, alive, shouting each other's names through the dust.

Wick and the fourth man had not.

The fourth man she could not see at all, and some cold part of her noted that she would not lose sleep over him—he was Sera's, he'd chosen his trade—but the boy was visible, or part of him: an arm, a shoulder, the edge of a wool cap, pinned at the trail's edge where the slab's shoulder had come down across the shelf. Not crushed whole. Caught. And one hand, the hand that had pushed his companion clear, still open, still reaching, fingers spread against the rain as if the gesture hadn't finished.

Mara stayed in the crack and made herself look. She owed him that. She had spent nine years making herself look at everything—the king's plate, the court's rot, the poison in the honey—and she would not flinch from this, because flinching was how the palace got you, flinching was how you ended up kneeling smiling in a kitchen while something ate you from the inside.

*It drinks the faithful. Do not pray where it can hear.*

She had not prayed. She had made sure of it. And the gods had not answered, and the boy was dead, and she had killed him with a pry bar and nine years of practice at doing the necessary thing.

The two living hunters were scrambling toward the slab's shoulder, toward the arm, digging at the rock edges with their hands. One of them looked up—straight up the wall, into the rain, into the crack where she was—and their eyes met across the falling water.

He didn't shout. He didn't reach for a weapon. He just looked at her, and his face, through the dust and rain, was the face she imagined she was wearing: the face of someone who had done arithmetic with a human life and gotten an answer.

Then he went back to digging.

Mara climbed down the far side of the wall, the side away from them, and took the high track north with her hands finally, finally shaking—shaking so hard she had to press them flat against the stone to climb at all, shaking the way they had never shaken over poison, over treason, over Sera Vane's hunters, shaking now because the steadiness was a tool and the tool had done its work and underneath the tool there was still a girl from the border who had once vomited on the far bank of the Rope Span because she was thirteen and frightened, and that girl was screaming, and Mara let her scream, silently, all the way up the mountain, because someone had to.

*You can grieve later,* she told the girl, gently, for the first time in nine years. *You can grieve when you're alive. So be alive. Be alive, and then we grieve.*

* * *

She made six miles before the light failed, and camped in a hollow under a leaning pine with no fire, wet through, eating nothing because her throat wouldn't accept food. The rain had turned to sleet. Somewhere behind her, days back now, two men were digging a boy out of a rockfall, and she made herself hold that thought the way she held poison on her tongue: fully, without flinching, until it burned.

It was full dark when she saw the light.

Not a torch—a shrine-light, small and gold, high up the track ahead, where no light had burned for twenty years. A fire in a stone niche, tended, deliberate, the old signal of the mountain shrines: *safe road, warm hearth, come in from the weather.*

Someone was lighting the fires again.

And beneath the sleet and the wind, so faint she might have imagined it, Mara heard—no, felt—a whisper against her ear, the way she'd heard it in the consecrated salt, the way the cook must have heard it in his kitchen: a voice like grain sliding through a hole in a sack, patient, vast, and horribly gentle.

*Come up, taster,* it said. *I know your name.*

Mara stood in the sleet with her dead boy's mountain at her back and the god's fire burning ahead of her, and she understood that the thing in the king's crown had just made its first mistake.

It had called her by name—and she had spent nine years learning to survive exactly one kind of thing.

Something offered in a cup.
