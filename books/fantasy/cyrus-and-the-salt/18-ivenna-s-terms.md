## 18. Ivenna's Terms

The last mile nearly killed her.

Mara had climbed the Thornfell passes in fog and in fever, in the gray years before the roads were mapped, before the smugglers trusted her enough to show her their shortcuts. She had thought she knew what the mountain could do to a body. But she came up the final switchbacks with Sera's hunters' arrows still singing in her memory, her boots split at the seams, her hands shaking from three days of short rations—and the mountain did not care. It simply rose, patient as arithmetic, until the air thinned to almost nothing and every breath cost more than it paid.

The shrine appeared the way important things do: suddenly, and only when you stopped looking for it.

It was smaller than she expected. She had carried a picture of it in her head all these years—some vast temple hewn into the rock, pillars and bells and gold gone green with altitude. Instead there was a low stone building crouched under an overhang like a shepherd's hut that had decided to be holy, its roof weighted with flat slabs against the wind. Smoke rose thin from a vent. Salt-crusted jars stood in rows along the wall outside, each one sealed with wax stamped by a hand long dead. The door was open. Of course it was open. Nothing up here needed locking except the cold.

Mara stood in the wind a moment longer, catching her breath, cataloguing herself the way she'd been trained to catalogue anything that might be poisoned: pulse fast but steady, vision clear, legs functional, judgment—she'd find out. Then she went in.

* * *

The interior smelled of salt and tallow and something older underneath, something mineral and clean, like the inside of a geode. Ivenna sat at a plain table near the fire, and she did not look surprised.

She was older than Mara had imagined from her informant's descriptions—the last true priestess, the woman who kept fires lit on shrines no one visited—but age had not bent her. She sat the way stone sits. Her hands were folded on the table beside a bowl of barley porridge and a single spoon, and her eyes, when they lifted, were pale as weathered slate and just as hard to read.

"You took the high pass," Ivenna said. "Not the road."

"The road has hunters on it." Mara stayed by the door. Old habit. Never let a room close around you until you know where its exits are.

"Yes. Two of them will wake tomorrow at the tree line with sore heads and no memory of why they climbed so far. I'm sorry about the third. He was past helping when he reached my boundary stones." She said it without apology or accusation, the way you'd report the weather. "Sit. Eat. You've been feeding yourself like a fugitive, which is to say badly."

"I don't take food from strangers."

"Good instinct. It's the reason you're alive." Ivenna nudged the bowl across the table. "It's barley, water, salt from that jar there—you can watch me season it if it helps. And I'm not a stranger, Mara Lockridge. I've known your name for nine years."

Something cold walked down Mara's spine. "How?"

"Because Cyrus knew it first." The priestess's voice didn't change at all. "Or rather, Cyrus knew what you would be. Sit down. This is going to take a while, and you look like a woman who falls over when she stops moving."

Mara sat. She ate—watching Ivenna the whole time, and Ivenna let her watch, even smiled faintly at the thoroughness of it—and the porridge was the best thing she'd tasted in months, which annoyed her, because it meant she'd been hungrier than she'd admitted.

Then Ivenna began to talk.

* * *

"You already know most of it," the priestess said. "Your cook's note told you the shape of it. Your queen's confession filled in the edges. So I'll tell you the part nobody else knows, the part that lives only in this room and in one book under this table."

She reached down without rising and brought up a ledger bound in gray leather gone soft with handling. It was old—not ancient, not crumbling, but old the way a well-worn tool is old, kept alive by use. She laid it flat between them.

"Cyrus," she said. "Founder of the Taster's Office. The first person in this kingdom's history whose job description was *swallow what might kill the king and survive it.*"

"I know the name," Mara said carefully. "Every taster learns it. First of the office. Died in service."

"Died in service," Ivenna repeated, tasting the words. "That's what the palace record says, isn't it. A quiet death. Honored. A tomb somewhere with a plaque."

"Isn't that true?"

"It's true that she died here, in this shrine, in this chair I'm sitting in." Ivenna tapped the table. "But she didn't die of service, girl. She died of *holding*. And before she died, she wrote down everything—what the binding was, how it was made, how it could be unmade, and what it would cost. Because she was a careful woman, and careful people leave instructions."

Mara looked down at the ledger. Her hands had gone still in her lap, the way they went still before she tasted something dangerous. "Tell me."

"The crown made a covenant with a famine-god generations ago," Ivenna said. "You know this part. Worship in exchange for survival. Fields that didn't fail, granaries that didn't rot. And the price was that the god got to wear the king—that every king since has been a glove it puts on. What nobody wrote down is *how* the covenant was sealed. A covenant is just words. A binding is architecture. Someone built it. Someone designed the lock."

"Cyrus."

"Cyrus. Who understood, better than any priest or king, what hunger actually is. Not a metaphor. Not a spirit's mood. A thing with weight, with appetite, with *mass*. And a thing with mass can be moved." Ivenna leaned forward. "When the covenant turned rotten—when the god stopped being satisfied with worship and started eating worshipers—Cyrus did the unthinkable. She opened herself the way a taster opens a dish. She let the god pour its hunger into her. All of it. She held it. And because she held it, the king woke up empty-handed, and the kingdom bought itself thirty years."

Mara's mouth had gone dry. "Thirty years. Then what?"

"Then she died, and the seal weakened, and the god crept back into the crown through the next coronation. And here is the detail that matters, Mara, the detail Cyrus underlined twice in her own hand—" Ivenna turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table. "Read it."

Mara read.

The handwriting was cramped, practical, unlovely—a working hand, a hand used to writing reports that had to be read quickly and believed completely. *The hunger cannot be destroyed. It can be held. To hold it requires a vessel that has already survived what it pours out. It feeds on devotion; therefore the vessel must be emptied of devotion. But above all—it must have a body that has learned poison as a language.*

Below that, underlined twice:

*Only a taster can carry it. Only a taster has a tongue trained to recognize it. Only a taster's body knows how to refuse.*

Mara set the page down very carefully, the way you set down a cup you suspect contains bitter almond.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You're about to." She stood up. Her legs held, which surprised her. "You're about to ask me to do what Cyrus did. To hold it. To die in this chair."

"I'm about to tell you what the terms are," Ivenna said. "What you decide afterward is yours. That's the whole point of terms—they can be refused." She didn't rise. She didn't reach out. She sat in her stone way and let the silence do its work. "So hear them. All of them. Then decide."

Mara stood at the door again, hand on the latch, and hated herself for staying. She stayed.

* * *

"The hunger is not a demon," Ivenna said. "I need you to understand that first, because everyone gets it wrong. Demons can be cast out. This can't, because it doesn't want anything evil. It wants exactly what it was built to want. It was made by human need—generations of starving farmers pouring their desperation into the old shrines until the desperation pooled and thickened and *woke up*. It answers prayers. That's all it does. It answers prayers. Everything monstrous it has done grew out of that one honest function, like a tree grown crooked from a seed that wasn't crooked at all."

"So it can't be killed."

"It can be starved. It can be poured out of the king and into a vessel, and the vessel can hold it, and the holding weakens it, the way a fire weakens when you shut it in a box with nothing to burn. Cyrus proved that. Thirty years of proof." Ivenna's eyes fixed on Mara. "But the vessel must meet three conditions. Not two. Three."

"Name them."

"First: a body trained to endure poison. Because taking the hunger in is being poisoned—it is being force-fed a decade of famines, every empty granary and dead field and mother's grief it ever consumed, all of it arriving in your blood at once. An ordinary body dies. Yours won't. Nine years of swallowing small deaths have taught yours the grammar of it."

Mara's stomach turned over slowly, like something rolling in its sleep.

"Second: the vessel must be willing. Not brave. Willing. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Bravery is a performance for witnesses. Willingness is private. If you do this to prove something, or to spite Sera Vane, or because you're ashamed of the boy in the ravine, the binding fails and the hunger slides off you and finds the nearest open devotion to drink—which would be the entire court, kneeling in love they can't refuse. It has to be a choice made in the dark, alone, with no audience. Do you understand? No one can ever know whether you were truly willing. Except you."

"And the third condition?" Mara's voice sounded far away to her, like someone else asking.

Ivenna looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried the roof slabs. Inside, the fire ticked.

"Thirteen days," the priestess said. "After the taking, thirteen days without prayer, hope, or belonging. Thirteen days of being nothing to anyone and nothing to yourself. Because the hunger feeds on attachment—any attachment—and if you give it so much as one warm memory to grip, it will feed, and strengthen, and tear its way back out of you into whoever loves you most. You'll have to burn your map, Mara. Refuse farewell. Become a person nothing can grip." She paused. "Cyrus managed eleven days before she reached for someone's hand. On the twelfth day the seal cracked. On the thirteenth she was dead and the god was free to wait for the next coronation. Her notes say she regretted nothing except the reaching."

The fire ticked. Somewhere under the floor, in the mountain's bones, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

Mara found that she was laughing. It came out of her low and awful, and she pressed the back of her hand against her mouth to stop it, and couldn't.

"Of course," she said. "Of course it's this. Nine years learning to be invisible. Nine years burning away everything soft so the poisons wouldn't find purchase. I built myself into exactly the shape this needs—I *knew* I was doing it, I told myself it was escape, it was freedom, it was my map and my road out—and the whole time I was quarry being cut for this exact moment. Destiny dressed up as a death sentence, delivered by a priestess who doesn't even ask nicely."

"I don't ask nicely," Ivenna agreed, unmoved, "because nice questions get nice lies. Now answer honestly. Would it change anything if I begged?"

"No."

"Would it change anything if I ordered?"

"I don't take orders from priests."

"From queens?"

"Isolde sent me here to save her husband, not to die in his place."

"And yet," Ivenna said softly, "here you are. Fugitive, hunted, half-starved, having burned your escape plan behind you to shake pursuit. Tell me, taster—what exactly were you planning to do with the rest of your life? Go back to the palace? Take the throne road east? Start over in some port town where nobody knows your face?" She shook her head slowly. "You spent nine years becoming invisible. I'm offering you the one work in the world that invisibility was preparing you for. That's not destiny. That's craft recognizing craft."

Mara turned and looked out the door at the wind-scoured light, the rows of salt jars standing sentinel, the passes falling away below toward a kingdom that knelt weeping before a thing wearing its king.

"If I refuse," she said, "you'll find another way."

"No," Ivenna said. "I'll find another century of villages walking smiling into the hills, another generation of kings drowning in their own skulls, another thirty years of prayers answered by a thing that never once lied about what it was. There is no other way. There is only you, and the next taster after you if you die too soon, and the one after her. Cyrus left a recipe, girl. Recipes are written to be followed."

Mara closed her door-hand into a fist. Opened it. Closed it.

"How long do I have to decide?"

"The twelfth turn of the moon is six days away," Ivenna said. "After that, the covenant completes itself, and no vessel in the world will matter. So: less time than wisdom needs. More than despair does."

Mara nodded once, sharp, and stepped out into the wind, and walked to the edge of the shrine's shelf of rock, and stood looking down at the world she'd spent nine years learning how to leave.

Behind her, Ivenna did not follow. She sat in Cyrus's chair, in front of Cyrus's ledger, and waited with the patience of a woman who had spent her whole life keeping a fire lit for someone she'd never met—and who understood, better than anyone alive, that the hardest part of any recipe is the moment the cook must decide to taste.

Down in the valleys, the moon was already fattening toward its twelfth turn.

Mara counted the days left to her. Six.

And in her scarred, poison-hardened, thoroughly trained tongue, she caught herself tasting the word *yes* — bitter almond, faint but unmistakable — and wondering, for the first time in nine years, whether she should swallow.
