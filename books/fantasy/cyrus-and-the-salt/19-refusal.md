## 19. Refusal

Mara stood at the shrine door for a long time after Ivenna finished speaking, watching the wind comb the high grass the way a hand combs a child's hair before bed.

"No," she said.

The word came out smaller than she'd intended. She said it again, louder, to the mountains themselves. "No."

Ivenna didn't argue. That was the worst part. The old priestess had laid it all out with the terrible gentleness of a physician describing a tumor: the vessel, the hunger, thirteen days of nothing, and then—maybe—a life afterward, hollowed into something that could hold a god without being eaten by one. She had described it the way you describe weather to someone standing in its path.

"You're not required," Ivenna said now. "I want that understood. No god can compel what the covenant never bought. Your refusal is yours."

"Then I'm refusing." Mara pulled her cloak tight. The air up here tasted of stone and snowmelt and consecrated salt, and every breath of it felt like a debt being itemized. "I've spent nine years swallowing poison so other people could live comfortably. Do you know what they paid me for it? A room. A title nobody uses. The right to be ignored." She laughed, and hated the sound. "And now you're asking me to swallow *that*—"

"I'm asking no one. The kingdom is asking. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"The kingdom." Mara turned away from the shrine, from the salt-crusted altar, from the priestess whose eyes held forty years of watching gods be fed. "The kingdom prayed to it, Ivenna. Did you see them? Did you hear? They didn't pray because they were deceived. They prayed because it *worked*. Crops overnight. Fevers broken. Their dead grandmother's rheumatism gone in an hour. You don't trick people into worship with results like that. You just hand them the spoon and let them feed themselves."

She started down the path. Her legs knew these mountain trails better than her own heartbeat; nine years of secret maps lived in her calves. Behind her, Ivenna said nothing, which was its own kind of agreement.

*They chose it,* Mara thought, descending through the scree. *Every farmer who whispered thanks over a doubled harvest. Every mother who pressed her fevered child's forehead and found it cool. Every priest who vanished for asking questions—their neighbors must have noticed, must have wondered, and prayed anyway, because the prayers kept working.* 

The kingdom had made its bargain the way all bargains are made: gladly, greedily, and without reading the terms. And when the bill came due on the thirteenth moon, it would not be collectors at the door. It would be the whole household, smiling, holding out their plates.

Let them eat. Let them choke on it. Let the thing wearing Aldric's crown drink itself fat on a nation's devotion until there was nothing left but the cup.

Mara had a map burned into her memory and a body trained to survive things that killed better people. She would go south, past Thornfell, past the border, past the reach of any crown or covenant. There were smuggler roads that ended at harbors where no one asked a woman's name. She'd spent nine years earning that exit. It was time to use it for herself.

The thought should have felt like freedom. It felt like setting down a weight and discovering the weight had been holding her shape.

* * *

By late afternoon she reached the tree line, where the pines stood thick and dark and the air went green and close. Here the smuggler paths braided together like roots, and here—she stopped.

Smoke. Not one fire but many, thin columns rising through the trees ahead, too evenly spaced for camps, too orderly for flight.

Mara left the trail and moved the way she'd learned to move in these hills: silent, low, reading the ground. She crested a mossy rise and looked down into the valley village of Harrow's Bend—and her stomach dropped as if the hillside had opened beneath her.

The village was empty.

Not sacked. Not burned. Not scattered by raiders or emptied by plague. The doors stood open. Laundry still hung on lines, shirts and shifts turning gently in the breeze, waiting for owners who had walked away mid-chore. A pot of porridge sat congealing over a cold hearth in the nearest house, its door wide to the world. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in the dust of the lane, abandoned mid-game.

And the people—she found them on the road above the village. All of them. Three hundred souls, maybe more, strung along the mountain track in a loose procession, moving upward toward the high passes. Toward the shrine. Toward the god.

They were smiling.

An old man walked with his hands folded behind his back like a gentleman taking the air, nodding pleasantly at the scenery. A young woman carried an infant wrapped against her chest, and the infant was laughing at the swaying, and the woman was laughing with it. Two boys chased each other in circles around the procession and back again, shrieking with joy, and no one told them to stop, because no one wanted anything anymore except to keep walking.

Mara crouched in the bracken and watched, and felt the moral floor drop away beneath her like a trapdoor.

*They weren't taken.* That was the phrase that kept repeating, useless, insufficient. *They weren't taken.* No soldiers, no chains, no weeping dragged through lanes. This wasn't the covenant consuming the unwilling. This was the covenant receiving volunteers.

She thought of Ivenna's voice: *the hunger answers prayers.* Of course it did. That was the whole architecture of the thing. You prayed for your sick daughter, and your sick daughter healed—and what does a healed daughter cost against a God who asks only that you love Him? You prayed for rain and got rain, and gratitude is just prayer with the fear removed. The god hadn't stolen these people. It had *courted* them. Fed them miracles until devotion became appetite, until walking up a mountain to be eaten felt like coming home.

One of the boys ran off the path to pick flowers—stupid, bright alpine flowers growing wild at the tree line—and Mara's hand closed on her knife before she understood why. The boy gathered an armful, grinned at nothing, and ran back to join the procession. He passed within ten feet of her hiding place. Close enough that she could have grabbed him. Could have whispered *run, boy, run south, don't look back.*

She didn't move. Later she would tell herself it was tactical—one girl against three hundred worshippers, against whatever watched over them—but in the moment, in the truth of the moment, she simply couldn't make herself matter enough to intervene. She was invisible. She had spent nine years becoming invisible, and invisibility, it turned out, was exactly as useful as it sounded.

The procession wound upward and thinned into the trees. The last figure was the old man, who paused at the bend, turned back toward his empty village, and bowed to it—deeply, formally, the way you bow to a host after a good meal. Then he followed the others into the dark of the pines.

Mara stayed crouched in the bracken until the light went long and gold, until the smoke columns faded and the mountain swallowed the last of them. Then she walked down into Harrow's Bend and stood in the middle of its empty street, and the silence there was worse than screaming.

In the nearest house, the porridge pot had a ladle resting across its rim, set down carefully, as if the cook meant to come back. On the table beside it, a half-finished letter:

*Dear sister — the crops came in twice this season and little Tam's cough is gone and I know what they say about the old god but I have seen with my own eyes — we are going up to give thanks properly, all of us, the whole bend of us — do not worry — we have never been happier —*

The ink stopped mid-word. Not interrupted. *Completed*, in the way that a sentence is completed when the writer looks up from the page and sees something that makes the rest unnecessary.

Mara read it twice. Then she folded it, put it inside her coat, and walked out of the house, and her hands were shaking, and she could not have said whether it was fear or rage.

* * *

She camped that night in a smuggler's cache she'd mapped years ago, a hollow beneath a granite overhang with a rusted iron box hidden under stones. She built no fire. She ate hard bread and dried meat and stared at the valley below, where a dozen villages showed their evening lights—and counted, helplessly, how many of those lights might go out tomorrow, willingly, joyfully.

*The kingdom chose its god when it prayed.*

She'd believed it on the mountain. She'd descended the whole day wrapped in that belief like a cloak, using it to justify the word *no*, to justify the harbor roads, the new name, the clean escape. But Harrow's Bend had taken the cloak off her. Because the people down there hadn't chosen anything—not really, not with open eyes. They'd been fed miracle after miracle until their judgment drowned in gratitude, and now they were walking up a mountain to die with smiles on their faces, and calling it faith.

That wasn't choice. That was fattening.

And if it was fattening—if the god had spent a season deliberately cultivating love the way a farmer cultivates geese—then the thirteenth moon wasn't a deadline. It was a harvest festival. And the guest of honor was everyone.

Mara sat with her back against cold stone and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the shrine. If the god completed its covenant, it wouldn't stop at the court. It wouldn't stop at the mountain villages. It would drink the borderlands, the cities, the ports where no one asked names. There would be no harbor far enough. There was no *south* far enough, because the thing wearing the king's crown was already everywhere the prayers were, and prayers were everywhere.

Her escape map, nine years in the making, had one fatal flaw she'd never noticed until tonight: it assumed there would be somewhere to escape *to*.

She thought of the cook, kneeling dead with a smile, hollowed like eaten fruit. *It drinks the faithful. Do not pray where it can hear.* He'd known. He'd tried to warn her, and she'd grieved him and moved on, the way she moved on from everything, because grief was a luxury and survival was a trade.

She thought of the boy in the letter—little Tam, cough cured, marching up a mountain with his family, laughing.

She thought of Aldric, buried alive in his own skull for a generation, and of the three minutes he'd clawed free to scratch a warning no one read.

She thought of Ivenna's face when Mara said *no*: not disappointed, not angry. Just noting it, the way you note weather. As if the priestess had already seen a hundred refusals and knew precisely how many of them lasted.

Mara pulled out the folded letter and read it once more by moonlight. *We have never been happier.*

"They're not happy," she said aloud, to the dark, to no one. "They're *fed*."

The distinction mattered. It was maybe the only distinction left in the world, and she was one of perhaps four living people who could still feel the difference between a smile and a meal.

She looked south, where her roads ran, where her freedom waited, patient as a harbor light.

Then she looked north, up the black mass of Thornfell, toward the shrine, toward the salt, toward the founder's recipe and the thirteen days of nothing and the vessel willing to hold a famine in her ribs.

The wind moved through the pines, and in it—for one vertiginous moment—Mara heard something vast and patient and pleased, a sound like a feast being enjoyed two valleys over.

It wasn't speaking to her. That was the horror of it. It didn't need to. It had three hundred volunteers from one village alone, and the moon was waxing, and the whole kingdom was setting its own table.

Mara folded the letter small and put it away. Then she took out the charred stub of pencil she carried and, on the back of her oldest smuggler map—the one drawn in a starving girl's careful hand—wrote a single line:

*Day one of thirteen starts whenever I decide it does.*

She blew out the candle stub. In the dark, the hunger in the mountain hummed its contentment, and Mara Lockridge, royal food-taster, professional survivor, began—quietly, furiously, with no ceremony at all—to plan her own poisoning.
