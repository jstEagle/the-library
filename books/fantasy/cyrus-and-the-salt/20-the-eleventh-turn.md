## 20. The Eleventh Turn

There is a room at the bottom of me where the light doesn't reach.

I know it the way a drowning man knows the surface—by its absence, by the memory of air. I have lived in that room for eleven turns of the moon, or eleven years, or eleven minutes; time down here moves like honey through a cracked jar, slow and sweet and wrong. The presence doesn't bother to hide from me anymore. It used to, in the beginning. It used to wear my voice so well that I mistook its thoughts for my own, and I would stand at my council table agreeing with a hunger and calling it policy.

Now it doesn't need the disguise. Now it lets me watch.

That is the cruelest mercy it has ever shown me: the watching.

* * *

They brought me out onto the Sun Terrace on the morning of the eleventh turn, and the crowd below the walls made a sound I had not heard in a generation of reigns—not the polite roar of a coronation, not the dutiful cheer of a bread day. It was a sound with teeth in it. A sound like a city falling to its knees all at once.

*Aldric. Aldric. Blessed Aldric.*

I stood at the balustrade because the body stood, and I looked down at ten thousand faces turned up toward me like flowers toward a sun that had finally remembered them. The winter wheat had come in green and heavy through the Frostwold. The fever that had gutted the river towns had broken in a single night. A child in Lower Marsh, they said, had been pulled from a well after two days drowned and had walked out of the temple asking for supper.

They were not cheering a king. They were cheering a god, and the god was wearing my hands.

I felt it drink.

There is no honest way to describe what that feels like from the inside except to say it is the opposite of drowning. It is being *drunk*. The presence swelled in my chest like a sail catching wind, and my lungs filled with something that was not air, and for one vertiginous moment I could feel every one of those ten thousand prayers at once—each one a small warm thread running up from a mouth or a heart, and every thread terminating in me. In us. In the thing behind my eyes.

*Look how they love us,* it said, and its voice was my voice, warmed through, the voice I'd had at thirty, before the grief and the gray. *Look how they feed us, Aldric. You could never have made them love you like this.*

It was right. That was the horror of it. In forty years on the throne I had bought love with granaries and garrisons and a marriage that was a treaty with a signature on it, and I had never once—not once—heard my name said the way they said it now, from the throat, from the gut, from the place where people keep the things they would die for.

The thing wearing my crown raised my hand, and ten thousand people knelt, and I stood inside my own skull like a prisoner at a window, watching my body bless them.

* * *

I have been trying to remember my daughter's face.

I want to write that down plainly, because down here in the dark room at the bottom of me, plain statements are all I have left. My daughter's name is Elira. She is—she was—she is nine-and-twenty. She has her mother's brow. She laughs through her nose when something is truly funny, a small snorting sound she is ashamed of. She used to sit on the arm of my chair during council when she was small and whisper terrible advice about the border lords, and I pretended not to hear it, and twice I took it, and twice it was better than my ministers' counsel.

I know all of this. I know it the way you know a list.

I cannot see her face.

The presence took it. I don't know when—somewhere in the first years, when I still fought, when I still had hands to grip with inside my own mind. It ate her face the way it ate my father's voice and my mother's lullaby and the smell of the orchard at Harrowmere where I grew up. It keeps the memories, I think, the way a man keeps coins in a purse it doesn't intend to spend. It wears them. *Borrowed clothes,* I called it once, in the early days, and it laughed with my laugh and said, *They fit better than you ever noticed.*

But today, on the terrace, with ten thousand voices pouring love into the thing that was me, something happened.

A bubble rose through the black water.

It was small. It was stupid. It was the smell of bread.

Not temple bread, not the consecrated loaves the kitchens had been baking since the prayers started working again—real bread, the flat dark barley loaves of the Thornfell foothills, the kind that tastes of ash and patience. And riding on the smell of it, uninvited, absurd, came a name.

*Mara.*

I did not know, at first, where the name came from. It surfaced and hung there, glistening, and I turned it over like a coin found in a gutter. Mara. Mara. And then the coin opened like a locket and there she was—not her face, thank every god old and new, the presence hadn't thought to take hers, because who would bother hoarding the face of a servant—but her *hands*. Scarred knuckles. The way she held a tasting spoon the way a surgeon holds a lance. The way she stood at my shoulder at every evening meal for nine years and never once looked at me, and somehow that never-looking was the most honest thing in the whole rotten palace.

The taster. The quiet one. The one whose reports never varied, day after day, year after year, *no bitterness, no bitterness, no bitterness*, in a court where every other word was a knife.

*No bitterness,* I thought, in the dark room at the bottom of myself, and for the first time in eleven turns of the moon, I laughed.

The presence felt it.

* * *

It came for the laughter immediately, the way a spider comes for the tremor in the web. The swell of it receded—the terrace, the kneeling crowd, the warmth draining out of my chest—and I felt it turn its attention *inward*, down through the floors of my mind, down toward the little dark room where I lived, and I did the only thing a hostage can do when the jailer looks his way.

I went still. I made myself small. I thought about nothing with all the discipline of a man who has spent nine years watching a woman think about nothing at my dinner table.

*What was that,* it said.

*Nothing,* I said. *A cough.*

*You have not coughed in a year. I do not permit it.*

*Then it was a ghost,* I said. *This body is full of them.*

It considered that. I felt its consideration the way you feel a hand pressed flat against a door you're holding shut—patient pressure, enormous weight, no anger, because it had never once needed to be angry with me. Then it withdrew, satisfied or pretending to be, and rose back up through the floors of my skull to preside over the day's devotions, and I sat in the dark and held the name like a coal in cupped hands.

*Mara.*

Why her? I have had eleven turns of the moon and nothing to do but think, and I still don't fully know. But here is what I believe: the presence eats devotion, and it eats memory, and it eats love, but it cannot eat *witness*. It cannot eat the plain act of a person looking at another person and recording what is true. That is what Mara did, every evening, for nine years. She looked at the king's food and told the truth about it, and the truth was always small and always the same, and the smallness and sameness of it was like a stitch, and nine years of stitches make a seam, and a seam is a thing that can be *found*.

I think I am holding on to her name the way a man in a landslide holds on to a fence post. Not because the post is strong. Because it is *there*.

* * *

They paraded me through the Lower City at noon.

I want to be exact about this, because down here, exactness is the last property I own. The presence has grown strong enough now that it does not merely occupy the body; it *inhabits* it, the way a tenant inhabits a house it intends to renovate. My stride had changed. My gait had lengthened into something processional, something liturgical. When I raised my right hand, the fingers spread in a gesture I did not know—a blessing-sign, old, older than the crown, older than the kingdom—and the crowd along the Processional Way answered it with the same sign, ten thousand hands blooming open like the petals of one enormous flower.

They had learned it in a season. A season. The old gods' signs had been dead for sixty years, driven out by my grandfather's edicts, and now children were drawing them in the dust of the gutters, and their parents were not stopping them, because their parents had seen the wheat come in green through the Frostwold and had seen the drowned child walk out of the temple asking for supper.

A woman broke through the cordon. I saw her come—a big-boned farmwife in a gray shawl, wild-eyed, carrying a bundled shape in her arms, and the guards moved to take her and the presence *stayed their hands*. I felt it do it, felt it reach out through my raised palm the way a puppeteer twitches a single string, and the guards simply stopped, and the crowd hushed, and the woman came to the foot of the steps and held up her bundle.

A baby. Gray as ash. Not breathing. Not for hours, by the look of it, by the terrible stillness of the small mouth.

*Please,* the woman said. *They said the king—the blessed king—*

And the presence, wearing my throat, said: *Bring her up.*

I fought it then. I want that written down too, somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of such things. I threw everything I had against the inside of my own body—every memory I had hoarded in the dark, Elira's laugh-through-the-nose, my father's voice, the orchard at Harrowmere—and for three seconds, three real seconds, my arm was my own and it was *falling*, the hand coming down to refuse, to say no, to say *I am not a god and I cannot do this and you are asking a corpse—*

The presence crushed me flat without effort. It didn't even pause. It took the baby out of the woman's arms with my hands, and it held the child up before the crowd, and it *breathed*—and I felt where the breath came from, felt it drawn up out of the ten thousand kneeling hearts along the Processional Way, a tithe of warmth taken from every soul in the square, so slight no one would miss it, so total that all of it together was enough to wake the dead.

The baby cried.

The square made the sound with teeth in it. The woman collapsed to her knees, sobbing the name *Aldric, Aldric, blessed Aldric*, and the presence handed the child back with my hands and smiled with my mouth and said, in a voice pitched to carry to the walls:

*The thirteenth turn comes. And on that day, I will never leave you again.*

The crowd roared. They thought it was a promise.

It was a confession. No one heard it but me.

* * *

That evening, in the privy chamber, the presence sat me at the window with wine I did not drink and let me watch the sun go down over Thornfell. It does this sometimes. I have a theory—I have had eleven turns of the moon to build theories, and they are the only furniture in my dark room—that it likes to let me see the world precisely because seeing it and being unable to touch it is a kind of seasoning. Despair, I think, tastes like something to it. My despair. It salts its meals with me.

The mountains went purple, then gray, then black. Somewhere up in those black folds were the old shrines, the ones my grandfather stripped, the ones the salt had come down from before anyone now living could remember why. Somewhere up there, I had a dim recollection, was a woman named Ivenna, a name from a treaty my grandfather signed and burned. Somewhere down in the city was a cook who prayed and a queen who prayed too much and a spymaster whose reports had gone strange and thin.

And somewhere in the palace, between the kitchens and my dinner table, was a woman with scarred hands and a tasting spoon and reports that never varied, and her name was a coal in my cupped hands, and I had decided—there in the dark, with the purple light dying on the mountains—I had decided what a hostage can decide.

Not how to escape. I had stopped believing in escape around the fourth turn.

But how to *pass a message*.

It would have to be small. It would have to be deniable. It would have to be the kind of thing the presence would not bother to eat, because the presence does not eat small truths; it has no palate for them. A scribbled line in an old ledger. A word spoken in my own buried cadence, in a council meeting, to a room full of men too frightened to listen. A hand trembling at the wrong moment over a wine cup, in front of the one servant in this palace who has spent nine years learning to notice trembling.

*No bitterness,* I thought, at the window, in the dark, holding the coal. *Mara. The twelfth turn. Find the priestess before the twelfth turn.*

The presence stirred behind my eyes, drowsy and vast and fed, and it said, without malice, the way you speak to a dog dreaming at your feet:

*You are murmuring again, old man. Who is Mara?*

And I did the hardest thing I have done in eleven turns of the moon. I let the coal go dark. I thought about nothing. I thought about bread, ordinary bread, barley bread, ash and patience, and I let the name sink back down through the black water, down past the floors of my stolen mind, down into the little dark room where I would keep it, and feed it nothing, and wait.

*No one,* I said. *No one at all.*

*Good,* said the thing wearing my crown, and turned my face from the window, and began, in my voice, to hum a lullaby it had eaten from my mother thirty years ago.

Down in the dark, in the bottom room, I held the shape of the name without saying it, the way you hold a coal after the light goes out of it—by the warmth, by the memory of warmth, by the stubborn animal faith that warmth means fire, and fire means someone, somewhere, is tending it.

The twelfth turn was coming. I had one turn of the moon left to be found.
