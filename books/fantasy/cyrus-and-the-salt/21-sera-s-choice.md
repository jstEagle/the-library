## 21. Sera's Choice

Sera Vane had been hunting the taster for six days, and she had learned one thing for certain: Mara Lockridge was better at this than Sera was.

That admission cost her something. She had spent twenty years building a reputation as the woman who found anyone—the court jester who fled with a cardinal's secrets, the treasurer's son who'd embezzled a duchy into his own pockets, the assassin who'd waited eleven days in a chapel rafters to reach the king. None of them had lasted four days against her.

Mara had lasted six, and Sera had only caught her because she'd stopped following the roads.

*She'll use her own roads,* Sera realized on the fourth night, sitting in a tavern in Lower Hallow with a cup of wine going cold in front of her. *The smuggler routes. The ones nobody else knows.*

So Sera stopped following roads and started thinking like a woman running from herself.

The problem was that every instinct told her to think like a woman running toward something. The shrine of the First Salt sat high in the Thornfell passes—three weeks' climb from the capital, two if you knew the hidden ways, and Mara Lockridge knew the hidden ways better than any living soul except perhaps the dead smugglers who'd first cut them. If the taster was fleeing to the mountains, she was fleeing to Ivenna. And if she was fleeing to Ivenna, then everything Sera suspected was true: the binding was real, the king was a vessel, and the thing wearing Aldric's crown was about to finish its meal.

Sera should have reported this. Should have sent word to her network in the capital—a coded phrase to the chandler on Candlemaker's Row, another to the woman who sold roasted chestnuts outside the treasury. One phrase and three riders would be dispatched, and the mountain pass would close like a fist.

She hadn't sent word.

She kept telling herself it was tactical. A rider could be intercepted; a rider could be read by the wrong eyes; the palace itself was compromised in ways Sera was only beginning to map. Better to handle it alone. Better to keep the knowledge close.

But lying awake on the fifth night in a shepherd's hut above the tree line, watching frost form on the inside of the door, Sera admitted what she'd been refusing to admit since the third village.

She hadn't sent word because she no longer knew whose side she was on.

* * *

It had started with the villages.

On her second day of pursuit, Sera had come through a place called Merrin's Ford—a river town of maybe two hundred souls, built around a grain mill and a shrine so old its god's name had been sanded off the lintel. She'd stopped there because her informants said a woman matching Mara's description had bought bread at the miller's.

No bread had been sold. The mill was silent. The mill was not merely closed; it was abandoned mid-work, grain still heaped in the hopper, a child's shoe by the door.

Two hundred people, gone.

Not slaughtered. Not scattered. Sera had seen slaughter; she'd catalogued it for the crown for two decades. There would have been blood, flies, the specific silence of places where violence had happened. Merrin's Ford had none of that. The houses were tidy. Meals were set out on tables, uneaten. A cradle held a sleeping cat. In the shrine, the old stone altar had been scrubbed clean and strewn with fresh-cut flowers, and someone had lit a fire in the hearth that was still warm.

They had walked away. All of them. Willingly.

Sera found the miller's ledger open on his desk, the last entry dated the day they vanished. Beneath the numbers, in the same careful hand, a single line:

*We are called home. Praise to the giver of plenty.*

Her skin crawled. She'd read enough fanaticism in intercepted letters to know the tone—that bright, emptied certainty. But these weren't zealots driven mad by a street preacher. These were farmers and millers and grandmothers. Ordinary people did not abandon their children's shoes.

Except they had taken their children. That was the worst part. The cradle held a cat because the baby was carried off in arms. Whatever had called them, it had called families, whole and together, into the hills.

Sera rode on. And over the next four days she passed through three more towns in various stages of the same disappearance. In the second, half the village remained—and those who stayed looked at her with a pity that frightened her more than hostility would have. An old woman pressed a heel of bread into her hand at the well and said, "You'll understand soon, spymaster. He's almost done being patient."

She hadn't told anyone her name or office. The woman had simply known. Word traveled strangely in these hills now, as if the ground itself gossiped.

By the fourth village, Sera had stopped pretending she was pursuing anything but answers. She sat in an inn where the innkeeper's family had left mid-meal, ate cold stew from a pot someone had abandoned, and laid out her reports on the table like cards in a losing hand.

Because here was the thing she could not stop turning over: her own reports were in the pile.

Six months ago, the palace had begun requesting detailed assessments of religious sentiment across the eastern provinces. Which shrines were active. Which priests drew crowds. Where devotion ran hottest. Standard work—Sera had assigned it without a second thought, compiled it from her network, sent it up through the proper channels to the king's council.

Now, looking at the map of disappearances laid over her own maps of devotion, the correlation was obscene. Every emptied village sat within two days' walk of a shrine her reports had flagged as "resurgent." The worshippers hadn't scattered toward the god randomly.

*They'd been herded.* Her intelligence—the product of her network, her craft, her twenty years of professional excellence—had been handed to whatever wore the king's face, and used as a menu.

Sera Vane had spent her career believing that information was a blade that cut only where it was aimed. Sitting alone in a dead man's inn, she understood for the first time that a blade is also a thing that can be taken from your hand.

She thought about the priests. Four had disappeared since winter—Father Ambrose of the Riverwalk, the twin sisters at Candlemas Shrine, old Brother Hollen who'd spent fifty years tending the tomb-shrines below the palace. She'd investigated each disappearance herself, dutifully, and filed conclusions that satisfied her superiors: flight, debt, natural death. She had been so proud of how neatly the questions resolved.

They hadn't resolved. They'd been buried. By her.

And underneath all of it, older and colder: the memory of her last audience with the king, three months ago. How he'd received her kneeling report without once blinking. How his shadow had seemed to arrive at the wall slightly after he did. How, when she rose to leave, he'd smiled at her with perfect warmth and said, "Your service nourishes us all, Lady Vane," and she'd walked out flushed with pleasure at the compliment.

*Nourishes.* She'd thought about that word every day since.

* * *

She caught up with Mara on the sixth day, at a place where the smuggler road crossed a ravine on a fallen pine trunk.

Sera came around a bend in the trail and there she was—small, mud-spattered, moving with the economy of a woman who had been walking for days on hard rations—already halfway across the trunk, forty feet of empty air beneath her boots.

"Stop," Sera said, and drew her pistol.

Mara stopped. Turned. Looked at Sera across the ravine with an expression that contained no fear at all, which somehow made the pistol feel absurd in Sera's hand.

"You've been behind me since Hallow," Mara said. Her voice was hoarse with dust. "I heard your horse on the second day and let you follow. I wanted to see where you'd break."

"And where did I break?"

"Lower Hallow. You stopped tracking my trail and started tracking my logic." Something that was almost approval moved through her eyes. "You're the only one at court smart enough to do that."

"The archive room at the shrine of Saint Verity," Sera said. "Three nights before you fled. Someone copied documents and burned the copies badly. Ashes in the grate, half a page surviving."

Mara's face didn't change. "You're saying that was me."

"I'm saying whoever it was took pages concerning the founding of the Taster's Office. And I'm saying you're standing on a smuggler's bridge heading for the First Salt shrine, and I'm the only person in Thornfell who knows both those facts are connected." Sera stepped onto the log. It flexed under her weight. Forty feet down, white water churned. "So before either of us does something permanent—tell me what you're doing, taster. And don't lie. I've had six days of lies. Mine."

For a long moment Mara just looked at her. Then she laughed—a short, exhausted sound with no humor in it.

"You first," she said. "You've ridden four days through empty villages to catch me, and you haven't signaled your network once. I've watched for it. So tell me why Lady Sera Vane, spymaster to the crown, is alone on a smuggler's bridge with a drawn pistol and no backup." Her eyes dropped to the pistol. "And put that away. If you wanted me dead you'd have shot before I saw you. You want something else, and you've wanted it since Merrin's Ford."

"Merrin's Ford," Sera repeated quietly. "You know about it."

"I know about all four." Mara's voice went flat. "Do you know what I found at the third one? A man's boots, set side by side at his door, toes facing uphill. He'd taken them off before he walked barefoot into the mountains. Like entering a temple."

The wind moved through the pines. Below them the water kept up its white noise, indifferent.

"I wrote those reports," Sera said.

Mara tilted her head. "What reports?"

"Devotion surveys. Six months of them. Which shrines burned hot, which priests drew crowds, where the faith ran strongest." Sera heard her own voice go strange—careful, precise, the voice she used for dictating things she couldn't afford to feel. "Every village that's emptied sat near a shrine I flagged. My work. My network. I built the map, and something used it to set its table." She holstered the pistol, slowly. "I've spent twenty years believing information serves the crown. I never asked what the crown serves."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then: "Now you have."

"Yes." The word came out smaller than Sera intended. She cleared her throat. "I've spent the last two days trying to construct an alternative explanation. A cult. A plague of hysteria. Mass poisoning. I am very good at constructing explanations—it's most of my trade. And every single one collapses, because I was at the palace when the king's shadow lagged. Because I watched the queen pour out devotions like a woman feeding a fire. Because I've read the archives, Lockridge. Or I read what remains of them, before someone burned—" She stopped. Started again. "Before the relevant pages were removed."

"I removed them," Mara said. "I needed to know what Cyrus did. The founder-taster. What the binding actually requires."

"And?"

"And I'm not telling you on a bridge." Mara started forward again, crossing the rest of the log with her arms out, sure-footed as a cat. When Sera followed, Mara was waiting on the far bank, repacking her small bundle. "Walk with me. Ask your questions. And when we're done asking, you decide which of us is prisoner."

"That's generous."

"It's not generosity." Mara shouldered her pack and turned up the trail. Over her shoulder, without heat: "It's arithmetic. I can't outrun you forever and I can't fight you. So either I convince you, or I lose. Those are the options."

Sera fell into step behind her. Old habit put her at Mara's flank rather than her back—not quite trusting, not quite enemy. The trail climbed steeply through pines, and the light came down green and cold.

They walked for an hour before Sera spoke again.

"The king," she said. "Aldric. Is there anything left of him?"

Mara's stride hitched—just slightly, just once.

"There was," she said. "At the feast. For one hour, I spoke to him. He begged me to find Ivenna before the twelfth turn." Her jaw tightened. "He remembered his daughter's name. He cried when I told him the cook was dead—he asked who'd make the honeyed quail, and then he understood, and then he wept like a man whose house has burned while he slept in it."

"The queen knows."

"The queen has always known. Her family brokered the covenant." Mara glanced back, and her eyes were hard and tired. "Isolde isn't a villain, Vane. That's the trap of it. She poisons the god's offerings and prays herself hollow trying to keep it fed and docile, and she genuinely believes she's saving him. Devotion as anesthesia. She'd rather share her husband with a monster than risk killing him to free him."

"And you'd take the risk."

"I already have. I'm walking up this mountain to learn how much it costs."

Sera was silent for several steps. Then she stopped, unslung the leather case from her shoulder—the flat courier's case she'd carried out of the capital sewn beneath her riding cloak, the one thing she'd taken besides weapons—and held it out.

"What is it?" Mara didn't take it.

"The archives. The ones that matter. I pulled them the night before I left—originals, not copies, and I burned the registry entries so no one at court knows they're gone. Correspondence between the first Aldric's chancellor and the shrine-keepers, three hundred years back. The covenant terms. Records of every renewal." Sera's arm didn't waver. "There's a letter in there written by the famine-god's first priest. It describes what happens at the thirteenth moon. What becomes permanent."

Mara looked at the case for a long moment. "Why give them to me?"

"Because you'll read them and do something. I'd read them and file them." Bitterness cracked her voice on the last word. "Twenty years, Lockridge. I've filed away every horror this kingdom produced and called it order. I kept the peace by keeping everyone ignorant—including me. Especially me." Her hand stayed steady, but the knuckles had gone white around the strap. "My reports fed it. My discretion protected it. My competence is the reason it's eaten four villages without a sword ever leaving its scabbard. I'm done being useful to it. Give me something to be useful against."

Mara took the case. Weighed it in one hand, the way she might weigh a dish whose contents she didn't yet trust.

"If I read these," she said, "and they say the binding can't be broken without destroying the king—you'll accept that? No appeal to the crown's survival? No argument that a living hostage beats a dead god?"

"I watched a grandmother walk barefoot uphill carrying her granddaughter," Sera said. "She was smiling, Lockridge. There was flour on her hands. She'd been baking when it called her, and she left the loaves to burn." She swallowed. "Whatever the price is, the alternative is a kingdom that walks smiling into its own mouth. I'll pay the price."

Mara studied her a moment longer—searching, Sera realized, for the flaw, the tell, the residue of courtly calculation. Twenty years of Sera's tradecraft had taught hundreds of people to hide their hearts; apparently it had taught Mara to find them.

Whatever the taster saw, it satisfied her. She tucked the case under her arm and turned uphill.

"Then walk," she said. "But understand what you're joining. This isn't a faction. There's no side to defect to—Ivenna's the last priestess of a dead faith, I'm a runaway servant, and you're a traitor with excellent filing skills. If we win, history will probably call us criminals."

"Historians are written by survivors," Sera said. "I intend for us to survive."

Something flickered at the corner of Mara's mouth—not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Then keep up, spymaster. The passes get ugly above the tree line, and the god watches pilgrims on the open trails. We go the smuggler way."

They climbed. Behind them, far below in the valleys, the villages stood empty and tidy, tables set for meals no one would eat. Ahead, the peaks of Thornfell stood up white against the sky, and somewhere in their high folds waited a shrine, a priestess, and three hundred years of buried truth in a leather case that grew no lighter with altitude.

Near dusk, Sera asked the question she'd been holding since the bridge.

"The founder. Cyrus. What did the archives say she actually did?"

Mara didn't slow her stride, but her voice, when it came, was quieter than the wind.

"She swallowed it," she said. "The hunger. Whole. And lived." A pause. "Mostly."

Sera looked at the small, scarred, stubborn woman climbing ahead of her through the blue shadows of the pines—at the taster who had tasted everything the kingdom feared and never once flinched—and felt the shape of the next few days settle over her like snowfall.

"How mostly?" she asked.

Mara kept climbing. "Ask me at the shrine."

Above them, on a ridge black against the last light, something stood watching. It was shaped like a man. It did not move. When Sera blinked, it was gone—and in the silence that followed, both women walked faster, and neither mentioned it, and the moon, one turn short of its thirteenth fullness, rose thin and hungry over Thornfell.
