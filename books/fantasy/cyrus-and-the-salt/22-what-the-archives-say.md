## 22. What the Archives Say

The archive beneath the Spymaster's Wing had no official name. Officially it did not exist at all, which was why Sera Vane had spent eleven years quietly making sure the locks on its door answered only to her.

She went down before dawn, carrying a lamp and a key ring that would have gotten her hanged if anyone counted what it opened. The stairs were narrow, worn concave by generations of feet that were never supposed to be there. At the bottom, the air tasted of dust and tallow and old paper gone soft as cloth.

Three rooms. That was all. Three rooms under the palace where the kingdom kept everything it had decided to forget.

Sera lit two more lamps, set her satchel on the reading table, and pulled off her gloves finger by finger. She liked the archive best at this hour, when the palace above was still asleep and the dead could speak without interruption.

She had come for one thing: the covenant.

* * *

She'd known something was wrong with the crown for a month now. Wrong in a way that made her professional instincts scream and her personal ones go very quiet. Priests who asked questions about the answered prayers turned up missing—four of them, each disappearance cleaner than the last, each file she built on them thinner than it should have been, as though someone above her rank was tidying behind her. And every report she sent upward vanished into the king's council like a stone into a well.

So she'd stopped sending reports up. She started sending them down instead—into these three rooms, into the oldest ledgers, into the reigns of kings whose portraits hung in the long gallery with their eyes painted slightly too knowing.

The famine came first. She'd found that much already. Sixty years ago, a hunger so total that the chronicles stopped recording deaths because there wasn't paper enough. Grain rotted in the fields while the fields stood green. Children born that year were listed in the parish rolls with a single word where their names should have been: *fasting*.

And then, abruptly, the famine ended. No harvest recorded. No relief column. No explanation. Just a line in the privy purse accounts, dated midwinter:

*Paid to the Office of the Taster, extraordinary disbursement. Item: salt, consecrated, forty measures. Item: fees of binding. See sealed folio.*

Sealed folio. Sera had been looking for it for nine days.

She worked methodically down the row of document chests, each labeled with a reign in faded clerk's hand. King Aldric's grandfather. His great-grandfather. Back past the war, past the plague years, back to the famine itself—and there, at the bottom of a chest that smelled of cedar and something older, a folio wrapped in oilcloth and tied with wire.

The wire had been sealed with wax stamped by an office that no longer existed: *the Office of the Taster, founded by appointment of the Crown.* Sera broke the seal without ceremony. Seals were for people who expected things to stay hidden. She had never once in her career expected that.

Inside were two documents. The first was a warrant, signed by the starving king of sixty years ago, granting authority to "our sworn taster, Cyrus of the Border Marches, to do whatsoever shall be needful to end the famine, cost unto himself notwithstanding."

Cost unto himself notwithstanding. Sera read that phrase three times. Clerks didn't write like that unless someone had told them to make sure the words were on the record.

The second document was not written by a clerk.

It was written fast, in a hand that slanted downhill like a man walking into wind, on paper so cheap it was nearly cloth. A confession, or a recipe, or both. It began:

*I am Cyrus, taster to the crown, and I am writing this because whoever holds my office after me will need to know what they are actually tasting for.*

Sera sat down slowly on the reading stool.

*You will have been told your duty is to die for the king if the food is poisoned. This is a lie we tell the court so they will respect us. Your true duty is stranger. Listen.*

*The famine was not weather. It was appetite. There is a thing under Thornfell — older than the shrines, older than the names we gave it — that eats devotion the way locusts eat barley. In the hungry years the people prayed to it out of desperation, and it answered, and every answer cost more than the prayer. It grew fat on a whole kingdom's faith and still it starved us, because what it wants is not worship. Worship is only how it gets through the door. What it wants is to be fed until nothing else is ever needed again.*

*The crown made a covenant. I watched them make it, in the shrine under the mountain, and I will tell you plainly: it was a mistake dressed as mercy. They offered it a king. Not the king's death — the king's *wearing*. A body to live inside, a court to preside over, a nation's prayers funneled through one throat. In exchange it would let the crops grow and the children eat.*

*But covenants with hungry things are never finished, only postponed. So I did the thing the warrant gave me leave to do. Cost unto myself notwithstanding.*

*I took it into myself.*

Sera's lamp flame stood perfectly still. She realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start again.

*Not the god. The god is too large; a man is a thimble trying to hold a river. But a god that feeds must first taste, and taste can be stolen. I drank the hunger — the edge of it, the sharpness, the part that does the eating — and I carried it in my body for thirty days without praying, hoping, or belonging to anything, and on the last day I poured what remained of it into consecrated salt, and the salt held it, and the famine ended.*

*Understand what this means. Understand it completely, because your life depends on understanding it.*

*The seal is not the salt. Salt is only a cup. The seal is the office. Every poison you swallow, every tainted thing you take into your trained flesh, every morsel from the god-touched table that passes through you instead of the king — you are drinking the edges of the hunger, the same as I did. You are keeping the cup empty so the salt keeps holding. The founder of your office did not build a wall against the thing under the mountain. He built a mouth. And you are it.*

Sera set the pages down flat on the table and pressed her palms against them, as if they might slide away.

Every taster since. Nine years of Mara Lockridge swallowing small deaths in a chair beside the throne. The woman reported the same thing every single day — *no anomalies* — in a voice like a ledger, and Sera had marked her early as either the most honest servant in the palace or the most compromised, and had never decided which.

Now neither answer was right. Now the truth was worse and better than both: Mara Lockridge had been the seal. Unknowingly. Unthanked. Eating the god's leavings for nine years so the kingdom wouldn't have to notice it was still on the menu.

*The binding lasts as long as the office does,* the downhill-slanting hand continued. *When I died, my successor took up the work without knowing it, because the king of that day decided knowledge was a burden better carried by no one. He was wrong. Knowledge is the only thing that keeps a burden from being a trap. So I am writing this in cheap ink on cheap paper and hiding it where only a spymaster or a thief would look, which is to say, where it might actually be found.*

*If the seal weakens — if the tasters stop tasting, or the office falls empty, or the salt is disturbed — the hunger will begin to pull toward the crown again. You will know it by three signs. Prayers answered too easily. The faithful vanishing into joy. And the king's shadow moving late.*

Sera closed her eyes.

Prayers answered too easily — the crops rising overnight, the dying healed, half the city lighting candles again. The faithful vanishing into joy — villages walking up into the hills smiling, according to the reports she'd read two nights ago with her skin crawling. And the king's shadow moving late.

She had seen that herself. Three weeks ago, in the council chamber, she'd watched Aldric cross from the window to his chair and his shadow had arrived a half-second after he did, like a dog that had forgotten whose heel it followed. She'd filed it as fatigue. She'd filed four priests' disappearances as coincidence. She'd filed the whole unraveling world into neat little folders because that was her craft, and her craft had been helping the thing hide.

Her own reports. Her own network. She had thought she was hunting the leak, and she had been the pipe.

*One more thing,* wrote Cyrus of the Border Marches. *The vessel matters. If the seal breaks in your time and the hunger must be taken up again, do not send a priest, a knight, or a king. Send a taster. Only a body trained to endure poison can hold the hunger without being eaten by it, and only a person with nothing left to lose can survive the thirty days. We are not chosen because we are pure. We are chosen because we are already ruined in exactly the right way.*

*Tell them. Whoever finds this — tell the tasters what they are. Tell them before the telling is too late. A servant who knows her service is a wall. A servant who doesn't know is a meal.*

There was a final line, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*I did not regret it. I want that on the record too. But I wish someone had asked me first.*

* * *

Sera sat in the lamplight for a long time after the pages ran out.

Then she did what she always did with unbearable information: she organized it. She took out fresh paper and wrote, in her own tight hand, a summary with numbered points, because numbered points were how she kept the world from sliding.

1. The crown has hosted a devouring entity since the famine, renewed each reign.
2. The current binding fails permanently at the thirteenth turn of the moon.
3. The Office of the Taster is the maintenance mechanism of the seal.
4. The current taster, M. Lockridge, is therefore the single most important person in the kingdom and does not know it.
5. I have spent six weeks treating her as a suspect.

She looked at point five for a while.

Six weeks of surveillance files on Mara Lockridge. Six weeks of noting her movements, her contacts in Thornfell, her floorboard, her silences. Sera had built a case against the one person standing between the kingdom and the thing under the mountain, and she'd done it competently, thoroughly, and in writing — which meant somewhere in her own locked cabinets sat a dossier that, if it reached the wrong desk, would hand the god its next meal gift-wrapped.

Because that was the other thing she understood now, cold and clear as well water. The disappearances hadn't been random. Four priests, each one asking questions about the answered prayers, each one vanishing a little cleaner than the last. Someone with access to intelligence channels had been feeding names to the crown. Feeding them *through* the crown. Her channels. Her reports, going up the chain, being read by something that used them the way a fisherman uses a net.

She thought of the petition the informant in Thornfell had begged Mara to carry. She thought of the emptied villages, walked willingly uphill. She thought of the queen's devotions, elaborate and public and somehow always timed to the king's weakest hours.

Everything fit now. Everything fit horribly.

Sera gathered the folio, the warrant, and her summary, and wrapped them in the oilcloth. Then she paused, lamp in hand, and looked down the long dark rows of forgotten paper — sixty years of a kingdom's memory, sealed and shelved, waiting for someone to come down the stairs and ask it questions.

"Someone should have asked," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the dark, the first words spoken down here in maybe a decade. "Someone should have asked her."

She climbed the stairs two at a time.

* * *

Dawn was graying the windows of the Spymaster's Wing when she reached her office, and Mara Lockridge was sitting outside her door on the bench where petitioners waited, hands folded, face unreadable, exactly as composed as she had been every day for nine years.

Sera stopped.

"You're up early," she said.

"I'm always up early." Mara's eyes went to the oilcloth bundle under Sera's arm, then back to her face. Nothing moved in them. Nothing ever moved in them. "You've been in the archive."

It wasn't a question, and Sera felt the floor tilt a degree beneath forty years of professional certainty. "How do you know about the archive?"

"Because I know about everything in this palace that isn't supposed to exist." Mara stood. Up close, in the gray light, Sera saw for the first time what she'd missed in six weeks of watching: the woman was exhausted in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "And because yesterday I found my head cook kneeling dead in his kitchen with a smile on his face, and a note in flour dust that said *it drinks the faithful*, and I have run out of people to be quiet with."

The bundle felt suddenly heavy under Sera's arm. Heavy as sixty years.

"Come inside," Sera said, and unlocked her door. "I think you'd better read what your office founder wrote."
