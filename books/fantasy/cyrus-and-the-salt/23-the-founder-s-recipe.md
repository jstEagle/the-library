## 23. The Founder's Recipe

The archives smelled like the inside of a dead man's mouth.

Mara had expected grandeur—Sera had described the vault beneath the eastern wing as the memory of the kingdom itself—but what she found was dust, and damp, and shelves of ledgers stacked in no order she could discern. Sera lit three lamps before she found the shelf she wanted, low against the far wall, behind a rack of tax records so boring they had survived two centuries untouched.

"Here." Sera pulled a ledger bound in cracked green leather. "The founding registry of the Taster's Office. Year one of Aldric's grandfather's grandfather."

Mara took it. It weighed more than it should have.

"Cyrus," Sera said. "That's the name in the oldest payroll rolls. First taster ever paid from the royal purse. And the last entry in his hand is this." She slid a second book across the table—a slim thing, no bigger than a prayer manual, its cover stained dark with something old. "We found it sewn into the binding of the registry forty years ago when a clerk tried to reback the spine. The clerk went missing shortly after."

"You're telling me that to frighten me."

"I'm telling you that because it should frighten you. If it doesn't, you haven't understood anything yet."

Mara opened the little book.

The first pages were inventory. Salt weights. Grain tallies. The fussy handwriting of a practical man—Cyrus, presumably—who counted things because counting kept the world predictable. She recognized the habit. She did the same with poison doses, with the number of steps between her chamber and the king's table, with the days remaining on every one of her private escape plans.

Then the handwriting changed.

It happened midway through the fourth page, mid-sentence almost—the neat script suddenly slanting, pressing harder into the paper, as if the man writing it had begun to run out of time or ink or faith. The words came faster after that, some crossed out, some written over twice.

*The hunger is not death,* Cyrus had written. *I want that written plainly, for whoever comes after me, because I spent eleven days believing it was death and nearly let the world burn for my cowardice.*

Mara read the sentence three times. Her hands were steady. Nine years of tasting had taught her hands never to do anything interesting. But something behind her sternum had gone very loud.

*Read on,* said the look Sera gave her. Sera said nothing at all. She had posted herself by the vault door, ostensibly keeping watch, actually giving Mara privacy in the only way a spymaster knew how: by pretending not to watch.

*It can be held,* the founder wrote. *Held the way a taster holds poison—not by being stronger than it, but by being made for it. I swallowed the god's hunger whole. It is sitting in me now as I write this, coiled under my ribs like a second heart, and it is hungry, and I am alive.*

"That's not possible," Mara whispered.

"Keep reading," Sera said quietly.

*It feeds on four things,* Cyrus wrote. *Devotion, first—prayer, worship, the directed attention of the faithful; that is its bread. But it will accept substitutes when bread is scarce. Hope, which is devotion pointed forward. Belonging, which is devotion turned inward toward others. And love, which is all three braided together and is the richest meal it knows.*

*I starved it once, briefly, during my retreat to the mountain—by accident, you understand, not wisdom. Alone, friendless, praying to nothing, hoping for nothing, I felt it shrink in me like a worm in a dry season. That is how I learned the shape of the cage we must build.*

*A vessel who takes the hunger must survive thirteen days without feeding it.*

*Thirteen days without prayer.*

*Thirteen days without hope.*

*Thirteen days without belonging to anything living.*

*On the thirteenth day, if the hunger has been starved thin enough, it can be poured out into consecrated salt—the salt of the first shrine, mind you, no other—and sealed there. The seal holds as long as it holds. Mine has held thirty years. It may hold thirty more. It may fail tomorrow. I am not selling certainty. I am selling a chance, which is more than the god is selling anyone.*

Mara set the book down.

The lamp flame bent slightly, though there was no draft. Or perhaps there was; the vault was old, and old places breathe. She pressed her palms flat against the table and looked at the grain of the wood until the room stopped swimming.

Thirteen days.

She had survived nine years of swallowing poison. She had survived the border famine that took her family. She had survived the palace, which was worse than either, because at least poison was honest about wanting you dead.

But thirteen days without hope—

"I can't," she said aloud.

"You haven't finished reading."

"There is no finishing this. There's just dying slowly in a stone room while everyone I—" She stopped herself. The word *everyone* had too much weight in it. Everyone was a list of exactly zero people, if she was strict about it, and yet the list had names anyway. Sera, standing guard at the door. Ivenna, up in her shrine, waiting. Even the cook, dead, even the boy in the ravine, dead—the list grew names in her chest without her permission, the way a wound grows infection.

"You have people now," Sera observed. "When did that happen?"

"Shut up."

"It's relevant to your survival, so no."

Mara picked the book back up. Because there was more—of course there was more. Men like Cyrus always wrote more, men who counted things, who believed that enough numbers laid end to end would build a bridge across any abyss.

*On method,* the next page began. *You cannot simply refuse to hope. Hope is reflexive in the young and the healthy. So: do not attempt abstinence. Attempt replacement. The vessel must fill the space where hope and belonging lived with something the hunger cannot digest.*

*I used arithmetic. I computed the value of pi to two hundred places, over and over, because numbers neither hope nor belong nor pray. Find your own arithmetic. Every vessel has one.*

*You will dream. The dreams are not the hunger; the dreams are your own mind, grieving in advance. Let them come. Do not mistake grief for temptation.*

*You WILL know temptation. On about the ninth day, when the hunger is thinnest and angriest, it will offer you exactly what you most want. It did not offer me wealth or power. It offered me my mother's voice, saying my name. I refused. I have regretted refusing every day for thirty years, and I would refuse again, and both those things are true, and you will have to live inside that contradiction, and it does not get lighter.*

Mara's throat closed.

She thought of the cook's flour-dusted hand. She thought of the ravine, the crack of stone, the boy—Tally, his name was Tally—he'd run messages for her for three years, he'd called her *Shadow* because she moved quiet, and she had dropped a mountain on him to save a kingdom that had never once said thank you.

And now a dead man's recipe was asking her to spend thirteen days in a cell refusing the offer of their return.

"How long did Cyrus live?" she asked. "Afterward."

"Thirty-one years," Sera said. "He died in his sleep. Of old age." A pause. "Alone, incidentally. He dismissed every servant he'd grown fond of, year by year. Kept moving lodgings. Never married. His personal correspondence reads like a man writing letters to people he refuses to meet."

"He practiced," Mara said slowly. "He didn't just endure the thirteen days. He spent the rest of his life staying empty enough that the hunger never woke again."

"Yes."

"And the salt seal—his held thirty years. What breaks it?"

"Deliberate unsealing. Or the vessel dying while still bound to it." Sera crossed her arms. "Which brings us to the part I've been dreading telling you, so I'll tell it plainly: the seal passes through the vessel's body. You pour the hunger into the salt yourself, on day thirteen, with your own hands. Which means that for the rest of your life, you are not a person who contains a secret. You are a person who contains a door."

Mara laughed. It came out wrong, a dry click.

"A door," she repeated. "Wonderful. I've spent nine years being invisible and now I'm architecture."

*One final note,* Cyrus wrote, in handwriting gone small and cramped, as if he were running out of paper or courage. *To whoever does this after me—I assume someone must, since nothing I've learned suggests the covenant dies with me—you will wonder, in the dark hours, whether you have already failed, whether taking the hunger at all was the failure. It was not. Someone must carry it. The kingdom does not get to choose a world where no one carries it; it only gets to choose who, and how willingly.*

*Be willing. It is the only part of this that cannot be forced, bought, or stolen. Ivenna—that is, the priestess of my own day, whose name I will not write lest it complicate her successors' lives—told me that willingness is the hinge on which the whole rite turns. A coerced vessel cracks. A willing one bends.*

*Choose it, then. Choose it the way you chose to swallow the first poison: because the alternative was watching someone else die.*

*Yours in the great quiet,*
*Cyrus, first taster, last of his office*

There was one more page. It was a recipe.

Not a metaphor—an actual recipe, written in the same fussy early hand as the inventories, dated thirty years after the main text. Ingredients, measures, timings. Consecrated salt, one measure, from the first shrine, ground fine but not powdered. Hellebore, a trace, "to teach the hunger that the door is bitter." Spring water. A bowl of black clay, unglazed.

And at the bottom, in a different ink, added later:

*Day 13, hour of dusk. Pour slowly. It will fight at the end—it always fights at the end. Sing if you know any songs. I sang a grain-grinding song my mother taught me. It seemed fitting: the god of famine, sealed to the sound of food being made.*

Mara closed the book with hands that finally, finally shook.

"So," Sera said, from the door. Her voice was carefully level, which meant it wasn't level at all. "Now you know it isn't suicide. Congratulations. Was the truth kinder than the fear?"

Mara considered the question honestly, because lying to a spymaster was a waste of good lying.

"No. Fear was simpler. Fear was just *you'll die.* This is—" She searched for the word and found several, all inadequate. "This is *you'll live, but only as an empty house with a locked room.* Thirteen days with no hope. Do you understand what that means? Not no comfort. No *hope*. I have to want nothing, expect nothing, belong to no—"

She stopped. Because she had heard herself. Nine years of practice at hearing the lie under the words, and here was the biggest one, served to her on her own tongue:

She had done this before.

Not the hunger. Not the cell. But the starving. The emptying. The girl on the border who survived the famine by wanting nothing, expecting nothing, belonging to no one—because everything she wanted died, everything she expected was taken, everyone she belonged to was buried. She had built her immunity to poison and her immunity to hope out of the same starvation, and the palace had hired the whole package without knowing it, and neither had she.

"Oh," she said softly.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something Cyrus wrote. *Find your own arithmetic.*" She looked up at Sera, and for the first time in nine years, let the spymaster see all the way to the bottom of her, where the terror lived braided with a single thin thread of something that might, if she squinted, be possibility. "I've been training for this my entire miserable life. I just didn't know the exercise had a name."

Sera studied her for a long moment. Then she did something Mara had never seen her do: she crossed the vault, sat down across the table, and poured two fingers of the archive's contraband brandy into a cup she'd apparently brought for exactly this occasion.

"You're going to refuse it," she said.

"I'm going to what?"

"The brandy. And everything else, going forward. No more of this—" she gestured at the cup, at the two of them, at whatever had been growing, unwelcome and unnamed, between spymaster and taster these past weeks. "From tonight, I can't be your friend. Ivenna can't be your teacher. The king—gods help us—can't owe you gratitude. You go into that shrine as nobody's anything."

Mara looked at the brandy. It caught the lamplight the way honey caught candlelight, the way the poisoned quail had, nine years ago, on the night she'd first saved a king who wasn't really a king.

She pushed the cup away.

"There's my first lesson," she said. "Refusing things I want."

"First of hundreds." Sera drank Mara's portion herself, in one swallow, like a woman closing an account. "When do you leave?"

"At dawn." Mara stood, tucked the founder's little book inside her coat, against her ribs—against the place where, in thirteen days, a god's hunger would sit coiled like a second heart. "Before sunrise. Before anyone prays."

At the vault door she paused.

"Sera. The ninth day. If I send word that I've changed my mind—"

"You won't send word. You'll send nothing, and I'll interpret nothing as resolve, because that's what it will be." Sera's face was perfectly composed. Only her voice betrayed her, and only barely. "But if you break, Mara—if that thing offers you the cook and the boy and you say yes—then I will come up that mountain, and I will do what has to be done, and I will hate every minute of it for the rest of my life. That's my arithmetic."

Mara nodded. It was, she reflected, the kindest threat anyone had ever made her.

Dawn was two hours away. Above the palace, past the smoke and the bells and the kneeling crowds, the twelfth-turn moon was already climbing—fat, impatient, nearly done counting.

She climbed the stairs carrying a dead man's recipe against her ribs, terror and possibility twisted together so tightly she could no longer tell which was which, and thought, with the strange clarity of the truly frightened:

*Thirteen days. Then I find out whether I was ever a person at all, or only ever a cage that learned to walk.*

Behind her, in the dark of the vault, the founder's book lay closed on the table, and the lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as if the room were holding its breath.
