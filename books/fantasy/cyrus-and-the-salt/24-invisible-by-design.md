## 24. Invisible by Design

The map burned fast.

That was the first surprise. Nine years of ink — every smuggler path through Thornfell, every goat track, every collapsed bridge I'd noted with a taster's caution, every shrine marked with a small salt-white cross of my own devising — and it all went up in less time than it took to boil water for tea. The vellum curled like a living thing, edges blackening first, then the whole sheet lifting on its own heat, blooming into flame the color of old honey. I fed it to the stove in the taster's quarters piece by piece, because I'd promised myself I would watch all of it die.

I had copied it twice in my life. Once onto a second skin, hidden under a floorboard in my old room in the servants' wing — that copy had gone into the fire three days ago, before Sera could find it during one of her increasingly thorough searches of my things. And once into memory, which no spymaster could seize and no god could burn.

Memory was the problem now. Memory was an anchor.

I stood at the stove until the last fleck of charcoal crumbled, then raked out the ash and scattered it in the herb garden where the cook used to grow thyme. Let the mountain roads feed something green. It felt like a funeral. It felt like nothing at all. Both, probably. Grief and numbness had been sharing a bed inside me so long they'd stopped bothering to introduce themselves.

* * *

Resigning was harder than burning.

I'd rehearsed the words on the walk down from the shrine road — *I resign my office, effective immediately* — simple, clean, four words any clerk could carve into a ledger. But offices like mine don't end with clerks. They end with kings, or with the things wearing them, and the thing wearing Aldric had already tasted my name.

So I did it properly. I knelt in the antechamber off the council hall, in full view of two guardsmen and a nervous undersecretary holding a tray of documents he clearly didn't understand, and I laid my taster's seal on the stone floor between us. Silver, worn thin at the edges from nine years of pressing into wax over cups I'd already survived.

"I resign my office," I said. "Effective this hour."

The undersecretary blinked at me as though I'd spoken in tongues. Perhaps I had. Tasters don't resign. Tasters are dismissed, promoted into quiet deaths, or buried. Resignation implied choice, and choice implied that I had somewhere else to be, and being somewhere else was the one thing the palace could not permit anyone to be.

"Her Majesty will wish—" the undersecretary began.

"Her Majesty knows why I'm going." That much was true. Isolde had helped me lace the hellebore into the feast; she knew what I carried back down from the shrine; she knew what Ivenna had told me about vessels and hunger and thirteen days. What she didn't know — what nobody knew yet except me, Sera, and a priestess who smelled of cold stone — was how completely I intended to unmake myself before the twelfth turn rose.

The seal stayed on the floor. I left it there. Someone would have to pick it up, and whoever did would have to decide whether it meant surrender or abdication, and I found I liked leaving that question behind like a coin on a counter.

Walking out of the antechamber, I passed the door to the council hall. Through the gap beneath it I could hear voices — the low, wet murmur of men agreeing with a king who was not a man. I did not stop. Stopping was attachment. Attachment was food.

* * *

Sera found me within the hour. She always did.

She came into the little room they'd given me near the chapel — not my old quarters; those had been searched, turned out, their floorboards pried up and replaced by hands that thought themselves thorough — carrying a lantern she didn't need and a scowl she very much did.

"Burning your maps," she said. "Resigning your seal. Refusing to see anyone. Do you know what this looks like from where I sit?"

"A woman preparing to die," I said.

"It looks like a woman preparing to disappear." She set the lantern down and sat across from me, uninvited, the way she'd been sitting across from me since the day she caught the thread of my double game and decided, in her cold arithmetic way, that I was more useful alive than hanging. "And disappearing is exactly wrong."

"Sera—"

"No. Listen." She leaned forward, and for once the spymaster's mask slipped enough that I could see the person underneath: tired, frightened, genuinely afraid for me in a way she'd never admit aloud. "I've read the archives. All of them. Cyrus — your founder, our founder — went into that cell alone and came out alive. Barely. You know what the records say about the years after?"

I did. I'd read them twice, once for the recipe and once for the warnings. Cyrus had come out of thirteen days of nothing weighing half what she went in with, her hair gone white at thirty, her hands shaking for the rest of her life whenever she held a cup. She'd lived another eleven years and spent most of them unable to remember why she'd bothered surviving.

"She nearly didn't come out at all," Sera said. "There's a marginal note in her own hand, late in her life. One line. I memorized it because it scared me." She recited it flatly, the way she recited everything, but her voice cracked on the last word despite herself. *'A vessel with nothing to hold her has nothing to hold onto.'*

"That's the point," I said. "Nothing to hold onto. The hunger feeds on devotion — on hope, on love, on belonging. Ivenna was clear. If I walk into that cell loving anyone, missing anything, wanting anything back — it will find the thread and pull."

"And if you walk in with *nothing*, Mara, what pulls you back out?"

I looked at her. Really looked. Sera Vane, who had hunted me through my own smuggler roads, who had cornered me with evidence of treason and offered me the rope or the work, who had burned her own files when she learned what those files had been feeding — Sera, asking me to stay tethered to her.

"You," I said. "You're asking me to keep you."

"I'm telling you that you'll die without something to come back to, and I'd rather you hated me for saying it than thanked me for staying quiet." She stood, restless, pacing the width of the narrow room in three steps. "Ivenna agrees with me. Don't look surprised — we've been corresponding. She thinks the founder's notes are incomplete. She thinks Cyrus survived because of the salt rites, yes, but also because someone came to the shrine every third day and sat outside the cell doing absolutely nothing. Just sitting. A witness. The records call her 'the silent sister.' No name. Ivenna thinks the silence was the point — presence without attachment. Someone nearby who wasn't praying for you, hoping for you, needing you. Just... there."

"So send a silent sister."

"I'm volunteering," Sera said.

I laughed. It came out ugly. "You? You can't go five minutes without interrogating a door hinge."

"I can sit still for thirteen days if the kingdom depends on it." Her jaw set in that way it did when she'd already decided and was merely informing me. "And Ivenna is sending one of her own besides. Two witnesses, neither of whom will speak to you, pray for you, or so much as glance your direction more than duty requires. Anchors without hooks. That's the compromise. Take it or I start pulling rank I don't technically have."

"There's no rank over a dying woman," I said.

"Then consider it rank over a stupid one." She paused, and something in her face softened by a degree I might have imagined. "Mara. I've spent nine years watching you do the impossible quietly. Let someone watch you do this one."

I didn't answer. Not because she was wrong — God help me, she was probably right, the archives were right, Cyrus's shaking hands were right — but because answering meant agreeing, and agreeing meant admitting that even now, even deliberately, even *by design*, I couldn't quite make myself into nothing. There was a person in me still. She kept surfacing like a cork pushed underwater.

I told Sera I'd think about it. She took that for a yes. She was usually right about people, which was the worst thing about her.

* * *

Ivenna came down from the mountain the next morning, which she never did, which meant the situation had frightened her more than she'd let on at the shrine.

She arrived smelling of juniper smoke and old snow, wrapped in the grey wool of her order, and she did not waste time on greetings. She stood in the doorway of my borrowed room and studied me the way she studied everything — long, unhurried, as if the truth were written somewhere on my face and she simply needed patience to read it.

"Sera tells me you're cutting yourself loose," she said.

"Sera tells everyone everything. It's her gift."

"It's the right instinct and the wrong method." Ivenna sat on the floor — the floor, not the chair, because priests of the First Salt sat where the earth was — and folded her hands. "Listen carefully, child, because I will say this once and then I will say nothing more until the cell. The hunger does not feed on love. Love is only its favorite door. It feeds on *grip*. On anything that holds you to the world. And here is what the founder's notes leave out, because Cyrus died before she could correct them: a person with no grip at all doesn't starve the hunger. She dissolves. She becomes mist. Thirteen days of true nothing doesn't leave a woman — it leaves a vacancy, and vacancies attract."

I hadn't considered that. I'd been so busy severing — burning, resigning, refusing — that I hadn't asked what remained at the center of a person once everything peripheral was cut away. Ivenna was telling me the answer was: possibly nothing. Possibly not even a person anymore.

"The vessel must be hollow," she said, "but not empty. Hollow like a bell. Empty like a grave. Do you hear the difference?"

I heard it. I wasn't sure I could achieve it.

"So what do I keep?" I asked. My voice sounded younger than I wanted it to. "Tell me what survives the cutting."

Ivenna was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a cart rattled past on the causeway; somewhere below, the palace kitchens were already beginning the obscene preparations for the twelfth-turn feast, and the smell of roasting meat drifted up through the window like a taunt.

"Keep the craft," she said finally. "Not the office — the craft. The tasting itself. The attention. The habit of putting a thing on your tongue and asking, honestly, *what are you?* That is not attachment. That is discipline. The hunger cannot grip a question. Keep your hands working — bread, salt, the daily small labor of survival. Keep your body moving, your mind counting, your tongue honest. Those aren't ties. Those are bones."

"And people?"

"People are ties." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "But a bell needs a frame to hang from, or it cannot ring. Choose one frame. One. Not a web — a single line, strong, deliberate, chosen. Sera offers herself. Take her, if you must take anyone, but take her knowingly: tell her that for thirteen days she may exist at the edge of your world and never once step inside it. If she can honor that, she is a frame and not a hook. If she cannot, send her away, and I will say nothing against you."

I thought about Sera sitting outside a cell door for thirteen days, forbidden to speak, forbidden to hope, forbidden to matter. I thought about her jaw setting. I thought about how badly she would fail, and how hard she would try anyway, and how both of those things were, in their different ways, forms of love I had spent nine years refusing to let anyone feel for me.

"One frame," I said.

"One frame," Ivenna agreed. Then she rose, brushed the dust from her wool, and paused at the door. "One more thing. The hunger will offer you visions. Your mother's kitchen. The cook's laughter. The boy from the ravine, alive, calling your name. These are not memories. They are bait shaped like memories. When they come — and they will come by the fourth day, they always come by the fourth day — you will want to pray. Not to any god. Just to *anything*. To the vision itself. To the idea that the past could open and let you back in. That prayer is the door. Do not open the door."

"How do I not open a door inside my own head?"

"You count," Ivenna said. "Salt grains. Heartbeats. Words in the founder's recipe. Counting is prayer's opposite — it fills the mouth so nothing can be spoken. Cyrus counted. We found her tallies scratched into the cell wall after she died, forty years later. Thousands of them. She counted her way out of a god's throat." She touched the doorframe once, a blessing or a warning, and left.

* * *

The farewells were the hardest part, so I made sure there weren't any.

I declined to see the undersecretary again. I declined the queen's request for a final audience — sent back word through a maid that I would see Her Majesty at the feast, and not before, and that this was not disrespect but necessity. I declined the chaplain's offer of a blessing. I declined a boy from the kitchens who brought me honey cakes because the cook used to make them, and I sent the cakes back untouched with thanks, because thanks are a thread and threads get pulled.

Word spread, the way it does in palaces, that the taster had gone strange. That she'd burned her papers. That she spoke to no one. That she'd been seen standing in the herb garden scattering ash with bare hands, murmuring. The murmuring detail was invented — I hadn't murmured anything — but I let it stand. A reputation for strangeness was armor too. People grip what they understand. Let them fail to understand me.

Only two farewells happened despite me.

The first was Isolde's, delivered by letter, sealed with her own signet rather than the chancellery's, which meant she'd bribed or bullied someone to bypass protocol. Four lines. *I have prayed every day of my marriage to keep him docile, and I am done pretending it was duty. Whatever happens in that cell, you will not carry it alone in the ways that matter. Come back. — I.* I read it twice and fed it to the stove. Not because it meant nothing. Because it meant too much, and I could not afford the weight. I told myself she'd understand. I told myself a queen who had spent twenty years loving a hollowed man knew exactly what it cost to refuse a kindness.

The second was Sera's, and it wasn't a farewell at all. She simply appeared on my last evening in the palace, in the corridor outside my room, and stood there while I finished packing — a satchel, a knife, a change of clothes, a pouch of consecrated salt from the First Shrine that Ivenna had pressed on me against all doctrine. She watched me close the satchel buckle and said, "Thirteenth day. I'll be at the tree line. Sitting. Saying nothing. Whether you want me there or not."

"That's not how this works," I said.

"It's exactly how this works. You get to choose your frame. You don't get to choose whether I hold still." She turned and walked away before I could argue, which was, I suspected, the entire strategy. Sera Vane had spent her career learning that the best way to win an argument was to leave before it started.

* * *

On the last morning, before I walked out the gate, I did one final thing, and I did it alone.

I went down to the old servants' wing, to the room I'd kept for nine years, and I pried up the floorboard where the second copy of my map had hidden. The board came up easy; I'd loosened the nails years ago, planning for exactly this kind of exit. Underneath, in the shallow cavity, there was nothing — I'd taken the map days ago — but there was dust, and in the dust, the faint impression the vellum had pressed into the wood over years of lying there. Roads. Rivers. The Thornfell passes rendered in negative, ghost-pale, like a memory of a map rather than a map.

I could have scraped it out. I should have scraped it out. Instead I knelt there for a long moment, looking at the ghost of the escape plan that had kept me sane through nine years of swallowing poison for strangers, and I understood what I was actually doing.

Every severed tie was supposed to make me harder to grip. But the truth underneath the ritual was simpler and worse: I was trying to become a person the hunger couldn't *want*. No loves to leverage. No hopes to poison. No belonging to threaten. Invisible by design — not hidden, but *uninteresting*. A bell with no clapper. A cup with no bottom.

And some cold, honest part of me whispered that it wouldn't work. That the hunger had worn kings for generations and would find purchase anywhere, that grief itself was a handle, that even my refusal was a shape it could pour itself around. Ivenna's bell needed a frame. Sera's silence needed a door it could knock on. Nothing human was truly gripless, and I was still, stubbornly, inconveniently, human.

I put the floorboard back. I left the ghost-map in the dust. Some things you don't burn; you just stop carrying.

Then I walked out the gate with my satchel and my salt and my single chosen frame waiting somewhere ahead at a tree line, and the guards at the gate looked through me the way they always had, and for the first time in nine years I was grateful for it.

Behind me, the palace bells began to ring noon. Ahead, the road climbed toward Thornfell, and the moon overhead was fat and pale and counting.

Eleven days to the twelfth turn. Thirteen days of nothing after that.

I started walking, and I did not look back, and I counted my steps, and by the four hundredth step I realized I was already praying without words — *let me be nothing, let me be nothing, let me be nothing* — and stopped, horrified, and started counting again.

The hunger, wherever it coiled in its mountain dark, drinking the devotion of a kingdom kneeling toward its feast, must have felt that small stumble of mine like a fingertip brushing a spiderweb.

It knew I was coming.
