## 25. The Twelfth Turn

The god stopped pretending on a Tuesday.

I remember that because Tuesdays were the days the council met, and the council was where I still clawed hardest, where I fought for every word the way a drowning man fights for the surface of a lake. Tuesdays were mine, mostly. Three minutes, sometimes four. I had learned to spend them like coins hoarded in a beggar's fist.

So I remember. I was there, under the skin of my own face, watching through my own eyes as my own mouth opened and something else began to speak.

It had been getting stronger for weeks. I could feel it the way you feel a fever coming—first the heat behind the eyes, then the ache in the joints, then the whole body burning from the inside out. Every prayer in the kingdom was a log on its fire. The queen's devotions, the villagers walking smiling into the hills, the priests in their restored shrines ringing bells for a god they thought had finally decided to love them back. It drank all of it. And it grew.

And I shrank.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. I did not lose control the way a drunk loses control, gradually, with deniable intervals. I lost it the way a man loses a house to the sea—one storm at a time, each one taking a wall, a floor, a room, until the house was a memory the water told better than he could. The god wore my memories like borrowed clothes. It knew my daughter's laugh because I had taught it to her, syllable by syllable, in the first years, when I still believed I could bore it into boredom. It knew the weight of my father's crown because it had been wearing the weight of my father's crown for thirty years before me.

It could not remember her face. Neither could I. That was the first thing it took, and the last thing I noticed, and I still do not know which loss was worse.

But Tuesdays. The council chamber. Let me tell you what happened, as much of it as I saw from the bottom of my own skull.

* * *

The morning of the twelfth turn, I woke before dawn with my hands already folded in prayer.

I had not folded them. I want that understood. I had gone to sleep the way I always went to sleep—flat on my back, arms at my sides, a dead man's posture I had taught myself because it gave the thing in me nothing to work with—and I had woken with my palms pressed together, my lips moving, shaping words in a language I did not know and that my mouth knew perfectly.

The chamberlain found me like that. He knelt. They all knelt now. The whole palace had gone soft-kneed and wet-eyed, servants weeping with joy in the corridors, guards laying their halberds against the walls so their hands were free to press to their hearts. I walked through my own palace like a man wading through honey made of love, and every drop of it was being swallowed by the thing behind my teeth.

I tried, that morning, in the grey hour before council, to do what I had done before. I went down into the part of myself where I kept the three minutes. It was harder now. It was like diving into a lake that had frozen over, breaking the ice with my forehead, sinking through black water toward a light I could no longer be sure was a light and not just the memory of one.

But I found it. I found it because of her.

Mara Lockridge.

I do not know when I first noticed her. That is the truth, and I am ashamed of how long it took. Nine years she had stood at my table, swallowing my deaths, and I had looked at her the way kings look at furniture. But in the black water, in the frozen lake, her name had begun to surface. Like a bubble. Like something rising that could not be kept down.

The night of the poisoned feast—the night I was, for one hour, myself—I had begged her. I remember the taste of my own voice, raw from disuse, saying *find Ivenna, find the priestess, before the twelfth turn*. I remember her face. It was the first human face I had truly seen in years, and it was not kind. It was sharp and tired and watching me the way she watched everything, which is to say the way a woman watches who has learned that everything can be poisoned.

I had loved her a little, in that hour. The way you love a rope thrown into deep water.

And now, on the morning of the twelfth turn, with the god swelling in me like a tide, I dove for the light one more time because I knew—I knew the way you know a thing in your bones before your mind consents to know it—that she had found the priestess. I knew it the way the god knew things, through the roots of it, through the covenant that ran between us like a taproot. Something had moved in the mountain. Something had begun to be planned. The god could feel it too. That was the terrible symmetry of us: what I hoped, it feared.

I broke the surface for three minutes.

I was standing in the council chamber. The lords were seated. Chancellor Ovist was reading from a scroll about grain tariffs, his voice the grey hum it had been for twenty years. The queen sat at my right hand, her hands folded, her face the beautiful smooth mask she had worn for so long that I had forgotten there was ever anything under it.

I moved. My body moved—it was my body, for three minutes, and every motion cost me like lifting stones—and I took the inkwell and I wrote.

I wrote fast and crooked, the way I had written as a young king, before the god taught my hand its patient elegance. I wrote on the margin of Ovist's grain scroll, because it was the only paper in reach, and I wrote what I had no time to make sense:

*Mara. Salt. The mountain. She knows. Trust the taster.*

And then the three minutes were gone, and the ice closed over my head, and I sank.

But here is what I saw, from the bottom, as the black water took me: I saw the queen's eyes flick to the scroll. I saw her fingers go white on the table's edge. And I saw—I will swear this on whatever is left of my soul—I saw her mouth move, just slightly, forming a word. Not a prayer. A name.

*Isolde,* I thought, sinking. *Isolde, what are you doing.*

I had stopped asking that question with anger, years ago. I asked it now with something worse. Hope.

* * *

The council meeting did not end. That is the only way I can describe it. Ovist read his grain tariffs, and the lords nodded, and the afternoon light crawled across the floor, and none of it ended, because the god did not want it to end. It had sensed the plot—I could feel its attention turning toward the mountain the way a great beast turns toward a wound in its flank—and it had decided to stop being a king and become a coronation.

My body stood up. I felt it stand the way you feel a ship move under you when you are below decks.

"My lords," my mouth said, and the voice was wrong.

I want to describe that voice, and I am going to fail, but I will try. It was not loud. That was the horror of it. It was not a shout or a roar or any of the things you would imagine. It was deep the way a landslide is deep—the sound of a mountainside deciding, after a thousand years of standing, to move. It came up through the floor before it came through the air. The goblets on the table rattled. The lords felt it in their spines, in their back teeth, in the soles of their feet, and every one of them knew, in the animal part of the brain that predates language, that something vast had just spoken.

"The twelfth turn is come," my mouth said. "The moon is nearly full. And you have prayed, and I have answered, and your children are fed, and your dead are risen, and your faith has been a river, and I have drunk."

Chancellor Ovist stood up. To his credit—Ovist, who had spent thirty years bending like a reed—to his credit, the old man's face had gone the color of tallow and he was standing anyway, one hand on the table, his mouth opening around some procedural objection, some appeal to order, some small human insistence that councils have agendas.

My body turned its head and looked at him.

I have seen men look at Ovist with contempt. I have seen them look at him with boredom. I had never seen anyone look at him the way the god looked at him, which was the way a man looks at an ant that has had the misfortune to walk across a document of state. There was no anger in it. There was no appetite, even. There was only scale.

"Sit," the god said, and Ovist sat, and the sound he made sitting down was the sound of a man's whole life of procedure collapsing into the shape of a chair.

And then the god did the thing I will never forgive, though I no longer know if forgiveness is a currency I have any right to spend.

It made them love it.

I felt it go out from me like heat from an opened oven. It moved through the chamber and it did not compel—not exactly, not the way chains compel. It *offered*. It opened in each of them a door they had spent their whole lives pushing against, the door that says *you are small and afraid and alone*, and behind the door was warmth, was answer, was a vast patient thing saying *I see you, I have always seen you, your fear is heard, your hunger is holy*. I felt it pour into the lords like water into cracked ground. I felt their resistance dissolve—not break, dissolve, the way salt dissolves, becoming part of the very thing it resisted.

Lord Cassel wept first. Old Cassel, who had opposed my father, who had opposed me, who had opposed everything on principle because opposition was the only love he knew—he wept like a child and slid from his chair to his knees, and his hands came together, and he prayed. Out loud. To the thing wearing my face.

Then they all did. One after another, like lanterns going out in a wind that was also a blessing. Ovist. Master of Coin Redgrave. The young lords who had come to council to whisper and scheme. They went down one by one, and the chamber filled with the sound of weeping and prayer, and the queen—

The queen did not kneel.

She stayed in her chair. Her face was white as the salt they had once shipped from the mountain shrines. Her hands were locked together so hard the knives of the knuckles showed through. And I saw, in her eyes, the thing I had been too drowned to see for years: not devotion. Horror. Horror that had learned to dress itself as piety, horror that had knelt and prayed and poured out her elaborate devotions because her family had brokered the covenant and her prayers kept me docile and the alternative was watching the thing in me wake up hungry.

She had known. All this time, she had known, and she had prayed anyway, and now she was watching the thing stop needing her prayers, and she had just heard me write *trust the taster*, and she was the only person in that room still standing upright in her own will.

"Isolde," the god said, through my mouth, gently, and the gentleness was the worst thing yet. It turned my body toward her and it smiled with my smile, the smile she had married thirty years ago, the smile of a young king on a wedding day. "You look tired, my love."

"I am tired," she said. Her voice did not shake. I marveled at that, from the bottom of the black water. I marveled the way you marvel at a tightrope walker. "My king. The twelfth turn. The court will want a feast."

"A feast," the god agreed. "Yes. A feast. Let them come and love me. Let them come and be loved. The moon is nearly full, and I am nearly—" and here it paused, and I felt it savor the word the way a glutton savors the last course, "—*home*."

The court spent the rest of the day preparing the feast. You have never seen joy like it. I mean that literally; you have never seen it, and I hope you never do. The servants sang while they worked. The cooks wept into their sauces. Guards embraced in the corridors and spoke of the king's love for them, of how he had always loved them, of how they had always known. It moved through the palace like a festival and a plague at once, and no one could tell the difference, and neither could I, and that was the final horror: from the inside, kneeling in the black water, I could feel how good it felt to be loved by it. I could feel why they wept.

If you had asked any of them—the lords on their knees, the singing servants, the weeping cooks—if you had asked any of them whether they were free, they would have said yes. They would have said they had never been freer. That is what it does. That is what it has always done. It only ever answers prayers. It never lies. Humans built it with their need, and it has never once had to force a single soul to its knees.

I lay under all of it, under my own skin, and I did the only thing left to a drowned man.

I hoped.

* * *

Night came. The feast preparations glowed through the palace like an infection of light. And in the queen's chambers, behind a door the god did not think to watch—because the god did not think anything in the palace could refuse it—Isolde sat down at her writing desk.

I was there. I was always there now; the god had stopped bothering to submerge me cleanly, and I floated in the shallows of my own mind like a man face-down in a pond, watching the world through the blur. The god let my body walk where it willed. There was no more pretense of privacy. When the queen's door closed, the god sent my body to stand in her sitting room, motionless, hands folded, smiling faintly at nothing, a king-shaped ornament. It thought it was a threat. It thought it was a guard.

It did not know it was a witness.

Isolde looked at me—looked at the thing wearing me—and then at the door, and then at the window, where the twelfth moon was rising, fat and white and one turn from full forever. And she took out paper, and ink, and she wrote.

She wrote small. She wrote fast. Her hand was steady—thirty years of complicity will steady a hand—but I saw the tremor come into it at the end, when she signed no name at all, only pressed her thumb to the corner, leaving a print in ink the shape of a small dark seed.

Then she melted wax, and sealed it, and did not call a servant. She crossed the room, and she did the bravest thing I have ever seen anyone do, and I include in that every battle I ever rode in, when I was allowed to ride in battles, when I was still a man who rode anywhere.

She walked to the window, opened it, and gave the letter to the cold night air and a girl on the roof below—a girl I did not know, a plain-faced girl with a courier's satchel, who took the letter and was gone across the tiles like smoke.

And as the girl vanished, Isolde turned back to the room, to the thing standing in it wearing her husband, and she looked it full in the face—full in *my* face—and she said, quietly, in the voice of a woman setting down a weight she has carried so long it has grown into her shoulders:

"I know what you are."

The god smiled. It was a beautiful smile. It was my smile, and it was beautiful, and it said: "You know what he is. I am what you prayed to."

"I know what I prayed to," she said. "I have always known. That is not the same as loving it."

"No," the god agreed, and it stepped closer, and it took her hand, and it pressed my lips to her fingers with the tenderness of a bridegroom. "But you will kneel at the feast tomorrow like all the others. And you will weep. And your weeping will taste like everyone else's."

She did not answer. She stood very straight while it held her hand, and she did not kneel, and she did not weep, and when it released her and turned toward the door, she looked at its back with an expression I had never seen her wear in thirty years of marriage.

It was the expression of a woman doing arithmetic.

* * *

I do not know what the letter said. I was face-down in the black water, and the water was rising, and the moon was one turn from full.

But I know what I hoped it said. I lay in the shallows of myself, in the body of a king being fitted for his coronation robes, and I hoped with the last of my three minutes, the last of my small human hoard, and my hope went down the mountain roads, through the Thornfell passes, along the hidden ways a taster had mapped with her own feet, to a shrine where a priestess kept a lid that could be opened.

*Mara,* I thought, into the dark, into the water, into the thing that was wearing me and could not hear a drowning man's last coin. *Mara Lockridge. Come and taste what they have made of me. Come and tell them what is in the cup.*

The moon rose higher. The palace sang.

And somewhere in the dark, on a road I could not see, I chose to believe a woman was walking toward me with salt in her pockets and no intention of kneeling.
