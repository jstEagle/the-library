## 26. Storming the Feast

Mara had dressed for a funeral and arrived at a wedding.

That was her first thought, standing in the antechamber off the great hall while a chamberlain fussed at her collar. The palace had spent nine years teaching her to be furniture—plain wool, plain face, plain name—and now she stood in borrowed silk the color of wet slate, with her hair pinned up in a style she'd last seen on courtiers who died young. The chamberlain tugged the collar straight and stepped back, and his eyes did the thing she'd spent a decade training out of people: they slid past her. A guest, but not quite a guest. A shape the room hadn't decided to see.

*Good*, she thought. *Stay undecided about me as long as you can.*

The invitation had come by Isolde's own hand—a folded card left where only Mara would find it, tucked into the seam of a market stall in the lower city, sealed with wax the queen had pressed herself. *Come as a guest. Sit where I put you. Trust me this far and no further.* No signature. No explanation. Just the faint smell of consecrated salt on the paper, which was either a message or a warning, and after everything, Mara had stopped distinguishing between the two.

Beyond the antechamber doors, the hall roared. Two hundred of the kingdom's finest, feasting on the eve of the twelfth turn, laughing too loudly, drinking too deeply, because the whole court could feel what was coming the way animals feel weather. The god inside their king had stopped pretending three weeks ago. It spoke through Aldric's mouth now when it pleased, in a voice like rockfall, and the court knelt weeping with love they could not refuse, and every day the moon swelled a little closer to full.

Tomorrow night it would be full. Tonight was the last rehearsal.

"Mara Lockridge." The chamberlain announced her name like he was tasting something unfamiliar, and she walked into the light.

Two hundred faces turned. She felt them the way she used to feel poison on her tongue—not the flavor, but the weight of it, the certainty that something in the room wanted her dead or changed or kneeling. She kept her chin level and let the silk do its work. Let them look at the guest and not the taster. Let them wonder why the king's food-taster—the one who'd vanished from the palace months ago, the one Sera Vane's hunters had chased through half of Thornfell—was walking down the center aisle of the feast like she owned the floor.

She found her seat. Third table from the dais, close enough to see the throne, far enough to be deniable. Isolde's arrangement, exactly as promised. The place card bore her name in the queen's precise hand, and beneath the plate someone had set a small dish of salt.

Not for seasoning. Mara understood. For courage. For memory. For the thing they were about to do.

She sat, and folded her hands, and waited for the music to change.

* * *

Sera Vane had killed better men than Captain Dorn, and she'd done it with less regret.

She stood in the guard barracks corridor with a ledger under her arm and her heart beating like a fist on a door, watching the captain of the feast guard read the orders she'd forged four hours ago. Dorn was a solid man. Loyal, dull, devoted to the crown—which, tonight, made him the most dangerous person in the palace outside the throne room itself. Because the crown was not the crown anymore. Because loyalty, these days, was just another word for feeding.

"Redeployment to the river gate?" Dorn frowned at the page. "On feast night? Whose order is this?"

"The king's." Sera let the lie sit there, fat and calm. "His majesty wants the approaches cleared for tomorrow's procession. He's expecting pilgrims. Thousands of them."

That part wasn't even a lie, which was the trick of good forgery—you built your false document on true bones. Pilgrims were coming. Had been coming for weeks, walking into the hills smiling, emptying villages, answering a summons no one had written down because no one needed to. The god didn't send letters. It sent hunger, and hunger traveled faster than any rider.

Dorn looked at her. Sera looked back with the flat patience of twenty years of spycraft, and somewhere behind her eyes she counted the seconds—she'd learned long ago that most men decided whether to obey within five breaths, and everything after that was theater.

At breath six, Dorn saluted. "I'll move two squads tonight."

"Do it quietly," Sera said. "No drums. His majesty dislikes noise before a holy day."

He went. Sera stood alone in the corridor and allowed herself one long exhale, then another, then a third, because her hands were shaking and shaking hands were a luxury she couldn't afford until the work was done.

She had burned nothing yet. That came later, if they lived. But she had spent the afternoon doing something almost as dangerous: she'd gone into the archive vaults with her own key and pulled every file that mattered—the covenant records, the worship ledgers, the routing orders that showed how her own intelligence network had been turned, year after year, into a shepherd's crook, herding the faithful toward the thing that ate them. Her reports. Her words. Her craft, bent into a feeding trough.

She'd carried them out in a satchel against her ribs, and the weight of them was worse than any blade she'd ever smuggled.

One more piece remained. She checked the small knife at her wrist, the signal whistle in her sleeve, the vial of shrine-salt Ivenna had given her at the tree line three days ago—*for the guards' wine, if it comes to that; salt will make them sleep and remember nothing*. Then she walked toward the great hall, past the emptying barracks, past the guards already saddling horses for a gate they'd never reach, and thought, with a clarity that frightened her:

*I spent my life learning everyone's secrets so the crown could use them. Tonight I spend it all back.*

* * *

Ivenna had not been inside a walled town in thirty years, and the walls of Heartspire closed over her like a hand closing over a moth.

She'd come in with the salt carts at dusk, hooded, bent, an old woman among old women, and no one had looked twice—no one ever looked twice at the people who carried their food. That was the joke of it, she supposed, the joke she'd been telling her goddesses for decades: kingdoms rise and fall on what crosses their thresholds unseen. Armies get counted. Salt gets carried.

Now she stood in the undercroft beneath the great hall, in a corridor that smelled of candle grease and old stone, beside a door marked with the seal of the First Shrine—the original covenant-chapel, bricked shut for a generation, the place where the founder-taster Cyrus had once poured a god's hunger into salt and bought the kingdom thirty years. The bricks were still there. The door beneath them was still there. And tomorrow night, if the rites held, that door would need to open.

Tonight, her job was simpler and worse: hold it. Hold the threshold. Keep anything from crossing in either direction until Mara was ready.

She set her hands flat against the cold brick and began the oldest prayer she knew—not a prayer of asking, but of naming. The old gods answered names. That had always been their nature and their danger; they were gods of the specific, the particular, the called-upon. The famine-thing wore the crown because generations of kings had called it *majesty* until the word stuck like a burr. So Ivenna named other things instead, quietly, into the dark: the shrine fires on the passes. The shepherd boy raised dead and walking. The cook, hollowed out and smiling. Every name was a nail. Every nail was a wall.

Halfway through the third litany, the bricks warmed under her palms.

She stopped. Listened. Below the floor, below the foundations, something vast shifted its weight—the way a sleeping animal shifts, the way a mountain settles—and she felt it notice her the way you feel a draft from an open grave. Not seeing. *Tasting.* The god had drunk so much devotion in these last months that it had become a kind of weather, and weather did not need eyes.

*Little keeper,* it said, without sound, without mouth, straight into the marrow of her. *You are very far from your mountain.*

"I am exactly where I have always been," Ivenna said aloud, because speaking grounded her, gave the dark a shape it couldn't wear. "Between you and the door."

A pause. Then something that was almost laughter, rolling through the stone like distant thunder deciding whether to be a storm.

*You are all between me and doors. It is what your kind is for.*

She pressed her hands harder against the brick and kept naming names, and the warmth under her palms grew, patient as winter, and she prayed faster.

* * *

The feast unfolded the way feasts do when everyone present is pretending: beautifully, and slightly too fast.

Mara watched it with a taster's attention, which is to say she watched nothing and everything. The courses came—quail again, honeyed, and she nearly laughed at the cruelty of the menu—and she tasted each dish reflexively, tongue cataloguing bitter and sweet and wrong, nine years of habit she couldn't switch off. Nothing was poisoned. Nothing needed to be. The whole room was marinating in something worse.

King Aldric sat the high seat and did not eat.

That was the detail that made her stomach clench. In all her years of service, the king had eaten everything she cleared—heartily, gratefully, once telling her that hers was the only job in the palace that fed him twice. Now the body on the throne lifted a goblet now and then, smiled at the right moments, laughed at the jester's jokes with a timing that was almost human—but the food went untouched, rearranged, discreetly removed. The thing wearing him didn't need bread. It needed the two hundred faces turned toward the dais, glowing, adoring, afraid in the way that reads as love when you're starving.

And the court glowed. Mara watched a baroness weep openly with joy as the king praised her son's military record. Watched the lord treasurer mouth *thank you, thank you* when Aldric nodded in his direction. Watched devotion move through the room like heat through a kiln, and understood—Ivenna had told her, but understanding arrived differently when you saw it—that every flicker of that love was a swallow. They were being drunk. All of them. Happily.

Isolde sat beside her husband, silver-crowned, serene, pouring out elaborate devotions with the practiced grace of a woman performing a role she'd rehearsed for twenty years. Only Mara, seated close enough, saw what the performance cost: the queen's hands trembled around her cup. Her eyes, when they swept the room, moved like a woman counting exits.

They met, once. Isolde's gaze touched Mara's and held for exactly two seconds—long enough to say *soon*, short enough to say nothing to anyone watching—and moved on.

Mara ate a bite of quail she didn't taste, and waited for the toast.

It came near midnight, as she'd known it would. The god loved ceremony; ceremony was concentrated worship, worship distilled to its proof. Aldric rose, and the hall rose with him, two hundred bodies moving as one, and the king lifted his goblet and the firelight caught his face and for one horrible moment Mara saw what the court refused to see: the smile that didn't fit the face, the eyes lit from behind like windows of a house with a fire in it.

"To the kingdom," the thing said, in a voice like gravel sliding, "and to the faith that sustains it. Drink deep. You are all so *generous*."

The hall drank. The hall cheered. Somewhere a woman sobbed with happiness.

And under the cheering, quiet as a floorboard creak, Mara heard the god add—only to her, only in the marrow-deep way Ivenna had described, because it knew she was here, it had always known—

*Taster. You brought salt to my table. How thoughtful. Come to the dais after the toasts. We should speak about tomorrow.*

Her hands, flat on the table, had gone white at the knuckles.

* * *

Sera reached the hall's west door as the toasts ended, and found the corridor beyond it exactly as she'd arranged: empty. Dorn's redeployment had stripped the interior guard to a skeleton, and the skeleton had been thinned further by a forged rotation order and a barrel of shrine-salted wine that was currently putting six men to sleep in the armory with dreams they wouldn't remember. The path from the great hall to the undercroft was clear. The path from the undercroft to the sealed chapel was clear. Everything, for perhaps an hour, perhaps less, was clear.

She slipped inside and took her position by the wall, a spymaster at her own feast, watching the room she'd served and betrayed and been betrayed by, and found Mara's eyes across the crowd without difficulty. Some things you never lose: the ability to find one face in a hundred.

Mara gave her the smallest nod. *Ready.*

Sera did not nod back. Instead she touched her sleeve, where the whistle hung, and let her hand fall, and hoped that was answer enough. Her heart was hammering—not fear, or not only fear, but the strange grief of a professional watching her life's architecture come down. Every lock she'd picked, every network she'd built, every careful lattice of informants and codes: it had all been scaffolding around a feeding trough, and tonight the scaffolding came down with the building.

She thought of the files in the satchel hidden beneath the floorboards of her office—her own floorboards, her own hiding place, mirroring a taster's map of smuggler roads she'd once mocked as treason. She thought of the villages. The smiling, emptied villages.

*This is what shame is for*, she told herself. *Shame is fuel. Burn it.*

Across the hall, the king set down his goblet and turned his head—slowly, mechanically, a head turning on a hinge that wasn't bone—and looked directly at Sera Vane.

Smiled.

*Yes*, said the voice in her bones, courteous as a headsman. *You too, spymaster. I keep excellent records. We will discuss yours.*

* * *

Below, Ivenna felt the moment the god turned its attention downward, and sweat broke cold along her spine.

It had noticed the others—had tasted Mara's intent, Sera's treachery, the queen's guilt simmering under her serenity like a covered pot. Of course it had. It was a god of appetite, and appetite misses nothing edible. But noticing and stopping were different matters, and here the old gods' nature cut both ways: a thing made of devotion could not act except through the devoted, and the devoted were, tonight, scattered, drugged, redeployed, or secretly armed.

Still, the warmth in the bricks was rising. The foundation stones hummed. Somewhere above, two hundred hearts beat in adoration, and each beat was a drop falling into a cup that was nearly full.

Ivenna spat on her palms, pressed them to the sealed door, and began the final litany—the binding-litany, the one Cyrus had written, the one that had held for thirty years and would, if the girl upstairs had the courage, hold forever after.

*Hold*, she told the door, and the dark behind it, and the hungry thing wearing a king. *Just until moonrise. Just one more night. Hold.*

The bricks pulsed under her hands like a heartbeat. Like a countdown.

* * *

In the great hall, the musicians struck up a dance tune, and the court began to pair off and sway, relieved to move after hours of sitting in their own adoration. The king did not dance. The king descended from the dais.

Mara watched him come and discovered that her body—the body trained to swallow poison without flinching, to kneel for nine years without bitterness, to walk out of a burning building at a measured pace—wanted, for the first time in her life, to run.

She did not run. She picked up her cup of watered wine, because a guest holds a cup, and because tomorrow she would need every ounce of the woman who had learned, one swallowed horror at a time, how to stay.

Aldric stopped before her table. Up close, the wrongness was worse and stranger: the king's face was still handsome, still tired, still *human*—and behind the human face, visible now only because she knew to look, something vast leaned against the inside of his skull like a child pressing its face to a window.

"Lady Lockridge," the god said, using the king's voice, wearing the king's courtesy. "You honor my feast."

"Your majesty honors me," Mara said. Nine years of court polish, and the words came out smooth as poured oil. Inside, her heart hammered like a fist on a door.

"You taste for me still?" The king's head tilted. "Even retired? Even *rebelled*?"

"I taste everything," Mara said. "Habit."

Something moved behind the king's eyes—amusement, or appetite, or both, for the god had never distinguished them. It extended one royal hand toward the dais, toward the high seat, toward the goblet waiting there on a cloth of gold.

"Then come," it said, and the whole turning hall fell silent, two hundred faces swinging toward them like flowers toward sun. "Sit beside me. Tomorrow we are wed to eternity, you and I and all this lovely faithful country. Tonight—" the smile widened a fraction past the face's capacity— "tonight, share my cup."

Every thread she'd followed for a year—every smuggler road, every whispered name, every swallowed truth—converged on this moment, on a goblet raised in a silent hall, on the twelfth turn's eve with the moon one night from full.

Mara set down her own cup, rose from her chair, and walked toward the throne.
