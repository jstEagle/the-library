## 27. The Toast

The distance from her chair to the dais was forty paces. Mara knew because she had paced the hall once, years ago, at three in the morning, measuring everything in this palace the way a prisoner measures a yard. Forty paces, then seven steps up, and every one of them would be walked under the eyes of two hundred courtiers who had spent a year learning to love the thing on the throne.

She counted them as she went. It was what she did. She counted stairs when fear came, counted breaths, counted the bitter notes in a sauce. Numbers were the floor that never moved beneath her.

The court watched in a silence so complete she could hear the tallow hiss in a hundred candelabra. No cutlery clicked. No silk rustled. Two hundred faces turned toward her, and not one of them wore suspicion. They wore joy—loose, wet-eyed, terrible joy, the kind she had seen on pilgrims and beggars and once, memorably, on a man watching his own house burn because the god in the flames had told him it was beautiful.

That was the worst of it. They would watch anything tonight and call it holy.

At the base of the dais, Sera Vane stood where she'd been seated among the honored guards, dressed in the king's black and gold, her face arranged into perfect courtly blankness. Only her eyes moved—flicking to the east door where Ivenna held the shrine entrance under a porter's grey cloak, then to Mara, then down, briefly, to the salt pouch knotted at Mara's hip beneath the folds of her borrowed gown.

*Not yet*, those eyes said. *Not yet.*

Mara gave the smallest nod and climbed.

Seven steps. The throne had been reupholstered since spring. Someone had chosen wine-dark velvet, and she thought, with a detachment that felt almost like laughter, that someone had very probably been made to enjoy choosing it.

King Aldric sat in it like a man poured into a mold. He was larger than he had been—a year of feasts and worship had thickened him, filled him out—but his hands lay on the armrests wrong, too still, fingers curled inward like dead leaves curling toward their own stems. His face was her husband-king's face, handsome still, silver at the temples, but the expression on it belonged to no living man. It was an expression designed. It was a smile that had been engineered to fit between two hundred loving eyes.

And underneath, deep, at the very bottom of him—was there anything? She searched his eyes the way she tasted wine, looking for the bitter note under the honey. There. A flicker, quick as a fish turning in dark water. Something at the bottom of those eyes that was not glad.

*Hold on*, she told whatever it was. *I'm coming for you.*

"You honor me," the god said through the king's mouth. Its voice had gotten worse since winter—or better, depending on your view of landslides. It no longer bothered to sound human except when it wished to. Tonight it wore the king's voice loosely, the way a hand wears a glove a size too big. "The taster who never varied. Nine years of faithful poison-swallowing, nine years of reports as flat as still water. I have wanted to meet you properly for so long."

"Your Majesty honors me," Mara said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"I have watched you, you know." It gestured with one curled leaf of a hand, and a page—smiling, weeping faintly, poor boy—came stumbling up the steps bearing a tray. On the tray: a single goblet of gold, filled with wine so dark it looked like a hole cut in the world. "Every meal you tasted. Every death you cheated me of." The smile adjusted itself by a degree. "You understand, of course, that every poison you ever swallowed for the crown was a prayer offered to me. Fear, faithfully consumed. You were my most devoted servant in this whole hungry kingdom, Mara Lockridge. You simply didn't know whose table you were keeping."

The hall sighed at that—a soft collective exhalation of wonder—and Mara felt the old familiar anger, the cold kind, the kind that sharpened rather than heated.

"That's a lovely story," she said. "But I've heard better from cooks."

Someone gasped. The god laughed, and the laugh rolled through the hall like a barrel down stairs, and the court laughed with it, helplessly, two hundred people laughing because they could not do otherwise, and the sound was the ugliest thing Mara had heard in nine years of palaces.

"Drink," the god said, and lifted the goblet from the tray, and held it out to her.

And here it was. The whole shape of the night folded into a cup of wine.

It knew. Of course it knew. Sera's hunters had torn through Thornfell after her; Ivenna's rites were hidden but not invisible; the god had tasted the plot the way she tasted hellebore, somewhere in the last week, some small note gone wrong in the vast flavor of its kingdom. So it had done what clever things do with poison: it had dressed the confrontation as a courtesy. Offered her the oldest comfort of her life—the taster's cup, the ritual she had performed ten thousand times, wine first, always wine, sip and hold and read. It would let her taste. It would let her prove her famous immunity before the assembled faith of a nation. And then it would feed—not on her body, which was a locked house, but on the spectacle. On two hundred witnesses watching the last doubter kneel. Devotion by proxy. Faith served at a feast.

Or it was poison. Or it was both. Or it was nothing at all and only theater, because the god did not need to kill her; it only needed tomorrow, the thirteenth turn, one more full moon and the covenant sealed past any taster's craft.

Mara looked at the wine. Dark as a hole in the world. She thought, absurdly, of her first cup at eleven years old, in the cellar under the border garrison, a sergeant's rough fingers prying her jaw open: *Drink, girl. If you live, you eat upstairs.*

She had built her whole life on this exact moment repeated ten thousand times. Walk forward. Take the cup. Read the truth in it. Swallow what must be swallowed.

There was no version of her, in nine years, that had refused a cup.

She took it.

The gold was warm. Too warm, as if the metal had been held near a fire, or as if the thing holding the tray had been sweating devotion into it. She raised it. The hall leaned in—one motion, two hundred bodies tilting like grass in wind—and somewhere below and behind her she heard Sera shift her weight, one boot creaking, a woman deciding how many heartbeats she had before she started killing people.

Mara sipped.

Wine first. Always wine first. Hold it on the tongue. Read it.

Sweetness first—summer and black cherry and something floral, honeyed—and beneath the sweetness, faint as a fingerprint on glass, bitterness. Not almond. Not hellebore. This was older. Mineral. Salt-adjacent and wrong, a bitterness like the smell of a shrine left shut for thirty years, like the inside of a sealed room where something had been kept and fed.

She knew this taste. She had met it once before, in a shipment of consecrated salt from the mountain shrines, on the night she first heard the whisper no one else heard. This was the god's own savor. The wine was steeped in it—steeped in prayer, in the residue of two hundred worshippers' rapt attention, in the same essence that had been poured into the king for twenty years.

This was not poison. This was communion. This was the god offering to share its meal with her—to make her complicit in front of everyone, one swallow of the faithful, and afterward no testimony of hers would stand. You cannot accuse the feast from inside it.

The bitterness sat on her tongue, and the whisper came, faint and intimate, the whisper she'd heard in the salt: *you are already mine, you have been mine since the cellar, drink, daughter, drink, every mouthful you ever swallowed was mine, belong, belonging is so easy, look how easy—*

Mara thought of the cook. Kneeling. Smiling. Hollowed out like a fruit eaten to its skin, flour still dusting his hands. *Do not pray where it can hear.* He had been the first person in this palace to show her kindness that asked nothing back, and the god had eaten him through his own kneeling.

She thought of a boy on a message road in Thornfell, laughing, calling her Fox, running her routes like they were games. A ravine. Her own hands on the timber prop.

She thought of her map, burned two weeks ago in a brazier in her own rooms, page by page by page, until she was a person nothing could grip.

The whisper pushed against her like tide against a door.

And Mara Lockridge, royal food-taster, holder of the bitterest tongue in the kingdom, did the thing she had been doing since she was eleven years old.

She swallowed.

The hall made a sound. All of it. One sound, half sigh and half sob, two hundred throats releasing two hundred held breaths, and the god's smile widened past the face's capacity again, and it began to speak—*there, daughter, there, now we—*

"Hold the wine," Mara said, loudly, clearly, in the voice she used to stop a kitchen mid-clatter. She had not touched the goblet to her lips again; the first swallow still sat in her stomach like a coal. "Your Majesty. I've read it."

Silence. Real silence now, different from the performed silence, the court sensing that the script had slipped its page.

"Read what?" the god said, mild as milk.

"The vintage. I'm a taster. It's my trade to name what I find in a cup." She turned, deliberately, slowly, so that she faced not the throne but the hall—all those loving faces, all that wet joy. She let them see her look at them. "You should know what you're drinking, my lords and ladies. It's part of the service."

"Mara—" Sera's voice, low, warning.

"No," Mara said, without turning. "I've looked away for nine years. That's enough."

She set the goblet down on the step beside her, unhurried, the way she'd set down ten thousand cups, and stepped past the throne to the head of the dais so the whole hall could see her plainly, small and scarred and plain-faced in a borrowed gown, with nothing between her and two hundred worshippers but a voice trained to carry across a kitchen.

"My lords and ladies of Aldmere," she said. "I am Mara Lockridge, and for nine years I tasted the king's food so that none of you had to know what was in it. That was my work: swallowing truth so the court could keep its appetite. I was good at it. Nobody thanked me, and I didn't expect it. But tonight there's something in the king's cup that no one has tasted and reported, and I find I can't swallow that one. So hear my report."

The god moved. Not much. A tilt of the head, the way a hawk marks a vole. Below, at the doors, she saw Ivenna begin to walk—slowly, grey cloak open now, white hair loose—timing herself to Mara's voice.

"There is a thing wearing your king," Mara said. "It has worn him for twenty years. Before him it wore his father, and before that his grandfather, back and back to a famine your great-great-grandparents bought their way out of with a covenant nobody wrote down honestly. It eats devotion. It drinks faith the way a leech drinks blood—slowly, patiently, and leaving you weaker each time. Every answered prayer this year was it feeding. Every miracle was an appetite. The crops rose so you would kneel. The sick were healed so you would kneel. It is raising you a religion the way a farmer raises cattle, and tomorrow, on the thirteenth moon, the binding finishes, and your king will be gone entirely, and so will your choice, forever."

She heard the hall's reaction as weather: a storm-front of murmurs, shock cracking through love like ice on a pond. Some faces curdled with doubt. More—many more—simply hardened, refusing, love snapping shut around the lie like a hand around a coin. A man in the second rank began to shout that she was a liar, a poisoner, hang her, and others took it up, ragged, and the god raised one curled hand and the hall went quiet instantly, obediently, horribly.

"You have drunk my wine," it said through the king's mouth, gentle as a landslide settling. "Before all these witnesses. Sweet child. Sit down."

"I drank," Mara agreed. "Because I'm a taster, and tasting means swallowing. That's all it meant." She opened her hand and showed the empty palm. "Your bitterness is sitting in my belly right now, and I want you to know what I taste besides it. I taste fear. Yours. You dressed this as a toast because you needed witnesses—you, the great devourer, needed an audience to prove a taster knelt to you. Twenty years wearing a crown, and you're still hungry in front of a crowd. Gods don't need to be believed by force. Famine does."

The king's face convulsed.

For one instant—one heartbeat—the engineered smile tore, and what showed through was vast and old and starving, a hunger with a history longer than the dynasty, and the candlelight guttered in every candelabrum at once, and two hundred people saw it, just for that heartbeat, saw what smiled at them from the throne—

—and then it composed itself, smooth as poured cream, and the court wavered, and Mara watched the most important thing she would ever watch: she watched them *not look away*. A woman in the front rank made a small broken noise. A guard captain's sword-arm shook. Doubt ran through the hall like dye through water, and no miracle came to settle it, because the god understood, suddenly and completely, that any miracle performed now would be a confession.

"Clever," it said quietly. The glove of the king's voice had split; the landslide showed through every word. "Clever, bitter girl. Do you know what I did with the last taster who spoke out of turn?"

"You ate him," Mara said. "Thirty years ago. A taster named Cyrus, who wrote down what you were before your priests burned it. I've read his hand. I know what you cost him." She let that sit. "And I know what he left unfinished. I'm here to finish it."

"You are one woman with a pouch of salt."

"I'm one woman who has spent her whole life being fed things that were meant to kill her." Mara reached to her hip, untied the knot, and drew out the salt pouch—consecrated salt of the First Shrine, Ivenna's blessing worked through every grain—and held it up where the light caught it, white and ordinary and absolute. "You've never once managed to kill me. Every poison ever put in my path, I outlasted. You think that's an accident? You think Cyrus chose your vessel at random, a thousand years before I was born? My office was founded as a key and let to rust into a lock. Tomorrow is your thirteenth moon. Tonight is mine."

Below, Ivenna reached the foot of the dais and began to climb, and at the great doors Sera Vane stepped out of the ranks of the guard and put her hand visibly on her sword-hilt, and along the walls Mara saw the four guards who were hers, truly hers, turn to face the room.

The god stood.

It rose out of the throne like something surfacing from deep water, taller than Aldric had ever been, and the king's body strained and creaked with it, and the whole hall felt the temperature drop as the thing drew in—not air, but attention, the worship of two hundred souls trying to pour itself into defense.

"Then come to me, taster," it said, and its voice was no longer pretending to be anything. "Come and be swallowed."

"In front of everyone?" Mara said. "Gladly."

She pulled the cord of the salt pouch.

Behind her, at the center of the dais, Ivenna began the first word of the unbinding rite—an old, heavy, geographic word, a word shaped like mountains—and the salt came down onto Mara's open palm in a thin white stream, and the god took one step forward off the throne, and King Aldric's buried eyes found Mara's over the thing's borrowed shoulder.

They were wet.

* * *

The hall's candles all bent sideways at once, though there was no wind.

Mara stood her ground on the top step, salt in her palm, and watched the hunger come for her the way she had watched ten thousand plates come toward her—still, reading, refusing to flinch.

Whatever happened next, she had already done the only brave thing available to a taster: she had named what was in the cup, out loud, in front of everyone, and made a thousand witnesses choose whether to keep looking.

Most of them still were.
