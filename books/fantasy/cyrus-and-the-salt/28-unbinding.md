## 28. Unbinding

The hall smelled of tallow and fear.

A thousand candles had been lit for the feast of the twelfth turn, and every one of them guttered now, as if the air itself were being drawn toward the dais where the king sat wearing his crown like a lid on a pot about to boil. The court knelt in rows—lords and ladies, clerks and chambermaids, faces slick with a love they could not refuse. I had watched that love work its way through the room all evening, moving from body to body like wine passed hand to hand, each drinker weeping with gratitude for the privilege.

I walked down the center aisle in a dress Isolde had chosen, grey as road dust, because she said I should look like what I was: no one. A taster. A servant. The last person in the world anyone would expect to stop a god.

Sera Vane stood at her post by the eastern doors, hands folded, face composed into its usual professional blankness. She had neutralized the guard with three forged orders and one very real threat, delivered quietly to the captain of the watch an hour before the feast began. Somewhere beyond the shrine door at the north end of the hall, Ivenna waited with salt in both fists and forty years of prayer in her throat.

I did not look at any of them. Looking is attachment, and tonight I could not afford a single thread of it.

The god saw me anyway.

It rose out of the king's body the way heat rises off summer stone—a shimmer, a wrongness, a pressure behind my eyes. When Aldric's mouth opened, the voice came out like a landslide deciding to speak.

*The little taster.* It said it the way a man says the name of a dish he has been waiting all evening to eat. *You came back to your old work. How faithful you are.*

"I came back to finish my employment," I said, and my voice carried further than a servant's voice should have. The candle flames leaned toward me. "There's a debt outstanding. Nine years of wages paid in poison. Tonight the crown settles its accounts."

The court stirred. They were not permitted to rise—the god held them kneeling—but their eyes moved, following me, and I understood suddenly why the founder-taster had done this in front of witnesses. Not for legality. For memory. A thing fed on devotion can be starved by attention spent elsewhere. Every eye in this hall was about to stop looking at the king and start looking at me, and the god would feel the difference like cold water poured down its spine.

I stopped three steps from the dais.

"Your Majesty," I said, "your supper has arrived."

* * *

What happened next, I will tell plainly, because someone must write it down honestly or the whole thing becomes a priest's fable.

Ivenna came through the shrine door carrying a basin of consecrated salt the size of a shield, and she set it down between me and the throne without hurry, the way a woman sets down bread dough she has kneaded ten thousand times. She drew a circle around us both with her heel, dragging a line of salt across the marble floor, and she spoke the first words of a rite older than the kingdom—words in a language that made my back teeth ache and the candles burn blue at their tips.

The god screamed.

Not with the king's throat. With everything. The pressure in the air doubled, tripled; two courtiers fainted where they knelt; somewhere a window cracked along its length like ice giving way. The voice filled the hall from every direction at once:

*You would unmake a covenant older than your grandmother's grandmother? You would steal the faith of a thousand thousand faithful?*

"It was never theirs to give," Ivenna said, calm as tide. "You took it while they slept."

*I ANSWERED THEM.*

"You ate them," I said. "There's a difference."

Then I stepped inside the circle, knelt opposite the king, and put my hands on his face the way I had once tested fruit for ripeness in a border market—and Ivenna began the extraction.

* * *

They tell me afterward that it took eleven minutes. It felt like eleven years, and then like eleven centuries, and then like no time at all, the way pain eventually burns past measurement and becomes geography. You do not experience it. You live in it.

Imagine swallowing a famine.

That is the truest sentence I know how to write. Imagine the hunger of a failed harvest entering your body not as sensation but as certainty—as the knowledge, absolute and physical, that nothing will ever grow again, that the granaries are empty, that the wells are dust, that your children's cheeks will hollow and their bellies swell and their eyes go glassy with a patience no child should have to learn. Now imagine swallowing that not once but for every harvest the thing had ever eaten. Thirty years of covenant. Generations of devotion converted directly into appetite. A decade of famines, force-fed, course after course after course, with no plate cleared between them and no end declared.

My immunity, hard-won over nine years of small poisons, held the door open. That was the horror of it. My tongue, scarred and numb, tasted everything—the god's hunger was, at root, a flavor, and I recognized it the way I recognized bitter almond. It tasted like the smell of a house after everyone has left. It tasted like prayers going unanswered. It tasted, God forgive me, almost sweet at the edges, because devotion is sweet to the thing that drinks it, and for one appalling moment I understood why the court loved the king so desperately, so helplessly. I was tasting their love from the inside.

I heard myself screaming. It seemed to come from far away, from some other woman's throat.

Ivenna's voice went on above me, steady as a metronome, reciting names—the names of every priest who had vanished, every village emptied, every soul the covenant had consumed. She was feeding the hunger a ledger of itself, making it carry its own weight as it crossed into me. Salt stung my lips. Someone—I think Sera, though she denied it ever after—held my shoulders when my body tried to fold.

And through all of it, under all of it, the god spoke to me alone, in the private dark behind my ribs where it now lived.

*You are doing exactly what I do,* it said, almost gentle. *You are taking what others cannot hold. You are the vessel. You have always been the vessel. Why fight the shape of your own life?*

Because, I wanted to say. Because someone has to. But there was no breath for words, only for the scream, and the scream was running out too, and still the hunger kept coming, wave on wave, the granaries emptying into me, the wells going to dust in my chest—

* * *

Then it stopped.

The silence after was so total that I heard a single grain of salt roll across the marble floor, six feet away, and come to rest.

I was on my hands and knees inside the circle. My hair had come loose. My hands, when I looked at them, were white as the salt itself. Inside my chest something vast turned over slowly, testing its new walls, and found them thick—found them scarred, calloused, dosed for nine years against exactly this—and settled, grudging, like a wolf lying down in a cage it despises.

Above me, the crown slipped.

It slid sideways off Aldric's head with a sound I will hear until I die—a bright, cheap clatter, gold on marble, ringing out across the silent hall—and rolled, wobbling in slower and slower circles, until it fell flat at the edge of Ivenna's salt line.

The king collapsed.

He went down like a puppet whose strings had been cut mid-performance, and for one terrible heartbeat no one breathed, because everyone in that hall had spent a generation watching that body move and none of us had ever seen it simply fall. Falling was human. Falling was mortal. We had forgotten the king could do either.

But he was breathing.

I crawled to him through the broken circle—my knees leaving wet prints on the marble, though I never learned whether it was salt water or blood—and pressed my ear to his chest. His heart beat slow and stunned, like a man waking from a long illness in an unfamiliar bed. His eyes opened. They were brown. Ordinary brown. I had served this man nine years and I had never once seen his actual eye color, because whatever looked out through them had always been wearing them like borrowed clothes.

"Mara Lockridge," he rasped. His voice was wrecked, thin, entirely his own. "You taste everything, don't you?"

"Everything, Your Majesty."

"What does freedom taste like?" And then he laughed—a cracked, disbelieving, human laugh—and wept, and I let him grip my wrist hard enough to bruise, because bruises were proof. Bruises meant he was alive and I was alive and the thing between us was gone.

Around us the court remained kneeling, but the quality of their kneeling changed. The compelled love drained out of the hall like water from a breached hull, and what rushed in behind it was confusion, terror, grief—the honest emotions of people realizing they had spent thirty years feeding their hearts to a parasite. A countess near the front began to sob without knowing why. An old clerk stared at his own folded hands as if they belonged to a stranger. No one could explain what they felt. That was the point. That had always been the point. The god had never needed their understanding, only their devotion, and now even that was taken from it.

Ivenna stood at the circle's edge, salt-white to her elbows, watching the empty crown on the floor with an expression I couldn't read—triumph, maybe, or mourning, or the particular exhaustion of a guardian who has guarded too long and finally, finally set down the wall.

"It is in you," she said quietly. Not a question.

"It's in me." I touched my sternum. Beneath the bone, the hunger lay coiled and furious and—for now—starving. "It doesn't like the accommodations."

"It won't. Your body is the worst lodging it has ever had." Her old eyes searched my face. "Thirteen days, taster. No prayer. No hope. No belonging. Nothing it can feed on. Can you do that?"

I thought of my burned map. My resigned post. The floorboard with nothing beneath it now. I had spent nine years becoming invisible, and the last month learning that invisibility had been preparation all along—that some part of Cyrus, the founder, had known a taster would stand here one day and need to be a person with no handles for a god to grip.

"I've been practicing my whole life," I said.

* * *

They carried the king out on a litter—he insisted on walking two steps and then fainted with dignity, which I am told is very kingly—and the court dispersed in silence, each man and woman carrying home the strange lightness of a heart returned to its owner. Sera Vane found me at the base of the dais, still kneeling beside the salt, still holding the king's dropped glove for no reason I could name.

"Well," she said. "Your reports certainly vary now."

I laughed. It hurt. Everything hurt. Eleven minutes of swallowed famine does not leave a body quickly.

"The shrine cell is ready," she went on, and for the first time since I'd known her, Sera Vane looked uncertain. "Ivenna's orders are strict. No visitors. No letters. Nothing that anchors you to—" she gestured vaguely at the ruined feast, the toppled crown, the world— "any of this."

"I know the terms."

"Mara." She used my given name for the first time, and the sound of it went through me like a needle finding a vein. "Whatever it offers you in there—it offered me things, during the rites, standing at the edge of the circle. It showed me my dead brother's face and asked permission to bring him back. I nearly gave it. From six feet away, outside the circle, I nearly gave it." Her jaw tightened. "So whatever it shows you, remember that some of us out here would rather grieve honestly than be bought with ghosts."

I nodded. I could not yet speak.

Inside me, in the dark behind my ribs, the hunger shifted its weight and began, very softly, very patiently, to hum a lullaby I had not heard since I was five years old—in a kitchen that no longer existed, in a voice that had been ash for twenty years.

Thirteen days, I told myself. Thirteen days of nothing.

The hunger hummed on, and it knew every word.
