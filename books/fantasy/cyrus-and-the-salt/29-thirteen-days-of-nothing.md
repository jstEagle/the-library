## 29. Thirteen Days of Nothing

The cell was not a punishment. That was the first thing Mara understood about it, and the thing that made it worse.

It was a good room, as cells went. Stone walls older than the kingdom, dry, swept clean. A narrow bed with wool blankets that smelled of lavender and cold mountain air. A window too high to reach, cut so the light came down in one long blade that moved across the floor like a slow sundial, marking hours she was supposed to survive rather than live. There was even a shelf, and on the shelf a clay jug of water, refilled each dawn by hands she never saw, left without a word because words were exactly what she could not afford.

Ivenna had explained it twice, once in her cracked priestess voice and once again when Mara's face must have shown what she thought of it.

"You are a vessel holding a starving god," Ivenna said. "And this thing feeds on three foods only: prayer, hope, and attachment. Not bread. Not water. It cannot touch your body. But every time you love something—every time you *want* something, every time you catch yourself believing tomorrow might be kind—it eats. And every meal you give it makes day thirteen harder. Makes *you* harder to save."

"So I sit in a room," Mara said, "and feel nothing."

"You sit in a room," Ivenna agreed, "and refuse everything that comes to the door."

* * *

The first day was almost easy.

Mara had spent nine years training herself out of wanting. That was the whole craft of tasting, though no one ever named it that way. You learned to swallow bitterness without flinching, to hold poison on your tongue and describe it like weather. You learned that hope was a luxury for people who weren't professionally poisoned, and belonging was a door that swung both ways, and the safest place in any palace was the space just outside everyone's attention. She had built a whole life out of not-gripping. She had burned her maps. She had walked away from Sera's face at the shrine door without looking back, which had cost more than the extraction did, though she'd never say so.

So Day One, she sat on the narrow bed with her back against the stone and did what she did best: nothing, competently.

The hunger inside her ribs was quiet. Weakened by the rite, poured thin across her body like silt settling in a flooded cellar, it lay still and let her pretend she was alone in her own skin. She catalogued the room the way she'd catalogue a dish. Stone, granite, old. Wool, undyed, well-kept. Water, sweet, snowmelt. No almond. No hellebore. Nothing bitter anywhere except herself.

She slept. She woke. The blade of light had moved.

Day One, she thought. Thirteen is only twelve days away. Even arithmetic felt dangerous—arithmetic shaded into counting, counting shaded into hoping—and she caught herself doing it anyway, because a person has to think *something*, and she was not yet skilled at thinking nothing at all.

* * *

Day Two, the god found its voice.

Not aloud. Never aloud; there were no ears in the cell but hers, and the thing had learned patience across centuries of wearing kings. It spoke the way warmth speaks from a hearth you can't see, the way a smell of bread reaches you from a kitchen three rooms away. It spoke from inside her own chest, using her own pulse as its drumbeat.

*You are very good at this,* it said, almost admiring. *I want you to know that. In all my long tenancy of men, I have never been held by someone who starves me so professionally.*

Mara said nothing. Saying nothing was the rule. Answering was a form of attachment—attachment to the argument, to being right, to the small warm satisfaction of winning against an audience of one.

*I only mention it,* the hunger went on, mild as milk, *because you seem to believe this is punishment. It isn't. This is the first room in nine years where no one wants anything from you. No king's plate to guard. No spymaster's leash. No dead cook's note folded in your sleeve. Just you, and quiet, and me. Some people pay a great deal for that.*

"It's a cell," Mara said, breaking her own rule before noon of the second day, which told her how long her discipline would last. "Call it what it is."

*A room,* it corrected gently. *With a bed. With lavender. With water brought to you by someone who chose to bring it. Tell me, taster—who carried that jug? Did you see their face? Do you know their name? Because they know yours. Everyone on this mountain knows your name now. You broke a god open in front of a thousand witnesses. You are the most beloved woman in the kingdom, and you have locked yourself away from all of them, and you call this freedom.*

Mara stared at the blade of light on the floor and breathed.

*There it is,* the hunger said softly. *That ache. Right there behind the sternum. That's mine. Thank you.*

* * *

Day Three, it stopped talking and started showing.

She woke from no dream she could remember into a kitchen that wasn't there. It stood in the cell as plainly as the stone walls did—overlaid on them, or instead of them, she couldn't tell—a low-ceilinged kitchen with a peat fire and a blackened kettle, and a woman at the table with flour to her elbows, humming.

Her mother. Dead eleven years, and humming the border song about the girl who married the river, off-key the way she always was, always insisting the tune went up where it went down.

Mara knew it wasn't real. That was the horror of it: she knew, completely, the way you know you're dreaming, and it changed nothing. The kitchen had weight. The steam off the kettle had temperature. Her mother turned, and her face was the exact face from before the famine years hollowed it, round-cheeked, flour-dusted, annoyed in the fond way.

"There you are," her mother said. "Sit down, the bread's nearly done. You're all bones. Who's been feeding you?"

"No one," Mara heard herself say. "No one feeds me."

"Well, that's over." Her mother sliced bread that smelled like every winter Mara had survived, and set the heel on a wooden plate, butter melting into the crumb, and pushed it across the table. "Eat. Then tell me everything. Start with why you never wrote."

"I couldn't write. You were dead."

Her mother laughed—the laugh, the real one, the snorting undignified one that had gotten them both shushed in chapel a hundred times. "Dead. Listen to her. Sit down, love. Sit down and stay. Nobody here will ever poison you. Nobody here will ever need you to die a little so they can live. Sit down and be somebody's daughter again, just for the length of a loaf."

And Mara, who had not wept since she was nine years old and a palace physician first strapped her arm and fed her arsenic in honey, sat down at her mother's table in a cell with no kitchen in it, and reached for the bread—

—and stopped, fingers a hand's width from the crust, because under the smell of the bread, faint as a lie under a smile, was the smell of the feast hall. Roast quail. Honey. Bitter almond.

The kitchen flickered. Her mother's face held, smiling, waiting, and behind the smile, deep in the eyes, something vast and patient looked out and waited to see if she would take the crust.

"Not today," Mara said. Her voice came out flat and steady, a taster's voice, describing weather. "The bread's burnt. It always was. You always said the tune goes down where it goes up, and you always sang it wrong, and if you were really her you'd be singing it wrong right now."

The kitchen was silent. Then her mother's face softened into something older than mothers, and the room folded up like paper, and Mara was alone on a narrow bed with wet cheeks and an empty stomach and a hunger in her ribs that felt, unmistakably, *fed*.

*Almost,* it murmured, drowsy and satisfied. *Almost. Tomorrow I'll try the dog you had when you were seven.*

"We didn't have a dog."

*I know. You wanted one. Wanting is enough.*

* * *

Days Four and Five, she fought it with inventory.

This was her own invention, and she guarded the method like a recipe. When the visions came—and they came hourly now, better lit, better voiced, drawn from deeper shelves—she named the cell's true contents aloud in a flat professional tone. *Stone. Wool. Water. Light-blade, currently two handspans from the north wall.* The real world, described precisely, left no room for the false one. A taster's trick: you could not taste two dishes on one tongue at once, and neither, it turned out, could a god.

But the god learned. By Day Five it had stopped trying to fool her senses and started offering things that needed no fooling. It showed her nothing. It simply let her remember, unassisted, and then leaned against the remembering.

The cook. Tam, head of the palace kitchens, flour-dusted, dead kneeling with a smile, hollowed out like eaten fruit. Mara had found his note and it had made her a weapon; but underneath the weapon was the man, and the man had slipped her honey cakes on bad days for nine years and called her "little wren" when he thought no one heard, and she had never once thanked him, because thanking people meant they could be taken from you, and now he *had* been taken, permanently, and the last thing she'd given him was silence.

*You could have thanked him,* the hunger observed, on the evening of the fifth day, in a voice like a hand on her shoulder. *One sentence. Nine years of opportunities. He died not knowing you cared. Isn't that the real starvation—not mine, yours? All those years of not-belonging, and what did it buy you? You're sitting in a stone room eating nothing, loving no one, and the kingdom you saved doesn't even know what you paid. Let go, little wren. Grieve him properly. Grief is just love with nowhere to go, and I am very hungry, and you have so much love stored up with nowhere to go—*

"Get out of my ribs," Mara said, and pressed her palms against the cold wall, and counted stones until midnight.

* * *

Day Six, Ivenna broke the rules.

The priestess had sworn she wouldn't come—no visits, no voices, nothing for the hunger to grip—but on the sixth day the water jug came with a scrap of cloth beside it, and on the cloth, in charcoal, four words in Ivenna's blunt hand:

*STILL HERE. STILL FIGHTING.*

Mara should have thrown it in the corner untouched. Attachment, Ivenna had said. Every gift is a hook.

Instead she read it forty times, and wept, and knew she was feeding the thing in her chest with every tear, and read it forty times more.

*Oh,* the hunger said, waking, delighted, *oh, that's lovely. She cares about you. She's afraid for you. She's sitting somewhere on this mountain right now imagining your face. Shall I show you?*

"No."

*I'll show you anyway. It costs me nothing. She's by the east fire, and she's mending a sock badly, and every few stitches she looks at the path to your cell. Look at her. Look how much she—*

Mara did the only thing she could do. She took the scrap of cloth, and she tore it in half, and then in half again, and buried the pieces under the floorstone, and lay down facing the wall, and said, in the flattest voice she owned: "Stone. Wool. Water. Light-blade, one handspan from the north wall."

*You hurt her feelings,* the god said, reproachful. *Tearing up her little note. She'll wonder if you hate her. That doubt will grow. That's how I got started, you know—doubt, watered. Every covenant begins with someone wondering whether they're loved.*

"She knows," Mara said. "She knows exactly what tearing it up meant. That's the difference between us and you. We can mean things without saying them."

A pause. For the first time in six days, the hunger had nothing ready.

Then, quietly, almost respectfully: *Thirteen days. You've done six. Seven remain. Do you truly believe you'll still be yourself at the end? I've worn thirty kings, taster. I know what fasting does to the soul. By the last day, you won't be refusing me. You'll be negotiating. They all negotiate.*

"Then I'll negotiate badly," Mara said, and slept, and dreamed of nothing, which frightened her more than the kitchen had.

* * *

Day Eight, the god changed tactics entirely, and showed her the future instead of the past.

It was subtle. It didn't build a scene. It just asked a question, in her own voice, while she watched the light-blade crawl.

*What happens on Day Fourteen?*

Mara kept her eyes on the wall.

*Say it works. Say you pour me into the salt and walk out of here, hollow, alive. What then? The king remembers what he was, and what he was is a man who let a generation be eaten. Sera rebuilds her spy service around protecting people, and discovers protection is just control with kinder handwriting. The shrines ration their miracles and the kingdom learns to pray in measured spoonfuls, and everyone congratulates themselves on their wisdom, and in thirty years some clever courtier finds my salt and opens the lid, because they always do, someone always does. And you? You'll be what you are now, minus the purpose. A woman trained only to endure things, with nothing left to endure. Hope is food, Mara. You've been starving yourself of it for nine years already. I'm just finishing the meal.*

"That's not hope," Mara said slowly. "That's forecast. You're offering me a forecast and calling it despair."

*Is there a difference?*

"Yes." She sat up. The light-blade had reached the north wall; evening, then. "A forecast says what will happen. Hope says what might. You can't eat 'might.' That's why you need me to *believe* things—to make them solid, so you can drink them. But I don't have to. I can stand in the 'might' for thirteen days. I've stood in worse for longer."

Silence from her ribs. Then, very softly, with something that in anything else she'd have called respect:

*Nine years of tasting poison made you this. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Everything they did to you—the doses, the straps, the arsenic in honey—all of it built the one person in the world who can hold me and not feed me. They meant to make you a tool. They made you a lock. I find that very funny, and I think you do too.*

Mara didn't answer. But she smiled, alone, in the dark, and the smile cost her nothing, and that was new.

* * *

Day Ten, the worst day, the god tried her mother's kitchen one last time, and this time it got the song wrong on purpose.

It sang the tune correctly.

Perfect pitch. Perfect memory. The way the song was written, not the way her mother sang it, and Mara sat in the middle of the vision with tears running down her face and her hands steady in her lap, and said: "Wrong. She always went up there. You've never listened to anyone sing. You've only ever listened to people pray. Prayers all sound the same. Songs don't. Get out of my kitchen."

The vision collapsed like a dropped puppet.

And in the sudden silence, Mara understood something that steadied her for the rest of the fast: the god could counterfeit everything except the specific. It had drunk a kingdom's devotion, and devotion is generic—it rises off crowds like steam, identical from parish to parish. But love is particular. Love is the off-key note, the burnt heel of the loaf, the honey cake slipped to a little wren on a bad day. The god had never loved anyone specifically in its entire existence. It could only offer her the average of her own memories, smoothed and warmed, and the seams showed every time, if you'd spent nine years learning to taste the difference between honey and honeyed poison.

That was the whole craft, really. That was what Cyrus must have known, the founder, the first taster, the one whose recipe she'd rewritten. You don't beat a god with purity. You beat it with precision.

* * *

Day Twelve, she began to talk to it—not to argue, but to keep herself human, and because silence had become its own kind of hunger.

"Why do you want out so badly?" she asked. "You wore thirty kings. You drank a kingdom. Why does another thirty years in salt matter to you?"

The hunger was quiet so long she thought it wouldn't answer.

*Because I remember being answered,* it said finally. *Before the covenant, before the crown—I was small then, a field-god, a hunger at the edge of a village, and they fed me willingly, first fruits, first lambs, and it was—* it paused, and Mara heard, beneath the vastness, something almost like confusion—*it was the only time I was ever part of something. Do you understand? I have consumed ten thousand prayers and I cannot remember one of them. They pass through me like water through a sieve. But the first loaf ever given to me freely—I remember the smell. You people made me what I am. Need, worshiped long enough, becomes a mouth. And now you propose to put a lid on the mouth forever, and call it mercy.*

"It is mercy," Mara said. "For us."

*For you. Yes. Everything is always for you.* A pause. *Your priestess thinks I can be starved into harmlessness. Your spymaster thinks I can be filed away. But I am patient, taster. Salt is a long slow dark, and I have outlasted mountains. Ask her what happened to the god before me.*

Mara went cold. "What god before you?"

*Ask her,* the hunger said, and it was smiling; she could hear it smiling, the way you can hear a smile in the dark. *Or better—wait. Three days. Two, now. And when you pour me into that salt, listen. Listen to what the salt says back.*

* * *

Day Thirteen came gray and windless, the mountain holding its breath.

They came for her at dawn—Ivenna with her rites and her salt and her cracked old voice, Sera standing at the cell door with her arms crossed and her face carefully empty, both of them watching Mara walk to the center of the floor on legs gone thin as a fawn's.

Mara knelt. The consecrated salt sat in its basin before her, white as the light-blade used to be.

*Last chance,* the hunger whispered, and there was no threat in it anymore, only a strange tired tenderness, which was the most frightening thing yet. *Last meal. You could keep me. Think of it—you'd never be ordinary again. You'd never be invisible. You'd be the woman who holds a god, and the kingdom would belong to you, and you would never, ever be alone—*

"I know," Mara said quietly. "That's why it has to be you in the salt and not me."

And she opened her ribs, and poured.

* * *

Afterward—after the basin ran white-hot and then clear, after Ivenna's sealing-song and the lead lid and the wax and the names of thirty stolen kings read aloud like a debt ledger—Sera found Mara sitting on the cell floor, hollowed lighter than she'd been in nine years, staring at the spot where the basin had been.

"You look terrible," Sera said.

"I feel," Mara said slowly, testing the words like an unfamiliar dish, "empty. Good-empty. Like a room after the furniture's been carried out."

Sera sat down beside her on the cold stone, which was unlike her, and said nothing, which was very like her.

Then Mara remembered, and her stomach dropped through the mountain.

"Sera. Before the extraction. It said something. It told me to listen to the salt, once I'd sealed it. It said—" she swallowed— "*ask the priestess what happened to the god before me.*"

Sera's face went carefully, deliberately blank—the face she wore when a report contained something she wished it didn't.

"Mara," she said, "when we get back down the mountain, there's a box in the archives I need to show you. Ivenna's predecessor left records. Sealed ones." She paused. "The founder's recipe wasn't the first seal, Mara. It was the second."

Outside the high window, far above the cell, the moon was rising—full, and ordinary, and one night early.

And from the basin's resting place in the shrine vault below them, faint as a heartbeat, faint as a prayer, the salt had begun—very softly—to hum.
