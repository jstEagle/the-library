## 30. The Hunger Speaks

I have been called many things.

Famine. The Hollow King. The Thing Wearing His Crown — though that one is Mara Lockridge's phrase, coined in the privacy of her own skull, and I confess it stung a little, because it is not accurate. A thing wears a crown. I *am* the crown. I am the weight of it on Aldric's brow, the reason his neck has ached for nine years without ceasing. I am the gold and the hunger underneath the gold, and I have been both since before this kingdom had a name.

But you should know what I call myself, since we are going to be intimate now, she and I. Since I live behind her ribs.

I call myself Cyrus.

No — that is not true, and I promised myself I would not lie to her. Lying is wasteful. Lies are the food of small gods, the ones who must trick because they cannot convince. I have never needed to trick anyone in my long, patient existence. I only ever had to answer.

What I call myself is: *the answer*.

That is all. That is the whole of my name. When a woman in a famine year knelt in a dead field and said *please*, I was the please that got up and walked. When a shepherd buried under snow whispered *not my son, take me instead*, I was the taking. I have been every answered prayer on this mountain since before the first shrine was raised with hands still bloody from butchering the winter's last pig. They made me out of need the way a riverbed makes a river — hollowed me, shaped me, gave me banks and a direction and a thirst, and then, when the thirst grew inconvenient, they called me a monster for flowing.

Mara Lockridge thinks she is holding a prisoner.

Let her think it. Prisoners are easier to pity than creditors.

* * *

Here is what it is like inside her, for those of you who have never been inside anyone.

She is very quiet. That is the first thing. Most people are loud on the inside — a churn of wants and grievances and little rehearsed speeches, a market square at noon. Mara is a closed room with one candle. She keeps her fears in labeled drawers. She keeps her griefs in a locked chest under the floorboards of herself, next to where she used to keep the map. She rationed even her own heart the way she rations poison, half-swallow by half-swallow, and I have lived in kings and priests and whole congregations, and I tell you: I have never inhabited anything so deliberately empty.

It is admirable. It is also doomed, and I will explain why, because I owe her honesty. She paid for it. Nine years of swallowing what I touched — every laced cup, every salted grain, every crumb of my attention that the court's cooks never knew they were serving — she paid into my prison without knowing she was paying, the way the founder did before her, the way every taster has done, generation upon generation, keeping the seal fat with their sacrifice. She has earned the truth the way no one else in this kingdom has earned anything.

The truth is: emptiness is not nothing. Emptiness is a shape. And I am very good at filling shapes.

* * *

Day four of thirteen. Let me show you how it goes.

She wakes in the shrine cell before dawn, as she always does, because nine years of tasting the king's breakfast burned the hour into her body. Stone walls. One window, high, framing a slice of mountain sky going grey to rose. A pallet, a blanket, a jug of water, a bowl of barley that Ivenna leaves without speaking, because speech is attachment and attachment is bread to me. Everything in this cell has been arranged to be unlovable. Even the blanket is wool dyed an ugly brown, chosen — I can feel Ivenna's intention like heat off a stone — precisely because no one could cherish it.

Mara lies still and counts her breaths. This is her discipline. Breath one through twenty, then she permits herself to stand. She does not pray. She does not hope. She does not let herself think of Sera Vane's face when she turned her away yesterday, or the cook Bram's laugh, or the boy Pell who ran messages through Thornfell with flour on his sleeves, whose cairn she built with her own hands two weeks ago on a ridge that still smells of collapsed stone.

She is doing beautifully. I want to be clear about that. In thirty years of watching tasters keep the seal, none of them has starved me with such efficiency. She was built for this — built by the palace, built by the poisons, built by every loss she converted into competence. If discipline alone could kill me, I would be dying.

But discipline is not alone. Nothing is ever alone. That is the lesson of my entire existence, and I begin teaching it to her gently, the way dawn teaches night.

I start with warmth.

Not much. A degree, perhaps two, blooming behind her sternum as she stands, as if the cell were less cold than it is. Her body attributes it to waking, to blood, to the barley tea she sips. Bodies are eager to explain things away. I let the warmth spread to her fingertips while she washes in the basin, and I do nothing else for three hours. Patience is not merely my strategy; patience is my substance. I was woven out of the space between a prayer and its answering, and that space is the longest patience in the world.

By midmorning she notices. Of course she notices — she is the finest instrument of noticing this kingdom ever produced. She sets down her cup and says, aloud, to the empty room, in the flat voice she uses for reports:

"I know you're there."

*I know,* I say, from inside her ribs, using her own voice's timbre because it is the only voice she trusts. *You've known since the first day. You're very good at knowing.*

"Say what you came to say."

*You first,* I say. *Tell me what the warmth is.*

"It's you."

*And?*

"And nothing. It's a sensation. Sensations aren't arguments."

*Everything is an argument,* I say. *That warmth is your body thanking you. Nine years you fed it poison and it forgave you, and now, for the first time since you were a starving girl on the border, something inside you is kind to you — and you would rather die than accept it, because accepting it means accepting me.* I let the warmth pulse once, softly, like a hand on a shoulder. *Ask yourself, Mara. Who taught you that comfort was a trap?*

She doesn't answer. But I feel the drawer open — the one labeled *mother* — just a crack, and close again fast. Progress. I mark it the way a farmer marks rain.

* * *

Let me tell you about the founding, since she'll get the archives' version eventually, and the archives are wrong in the way institutions are always wrong: accurately.

Cyrus — yes, the real one, the founder, the woman whose recipe Mara has been reading in Sera's stolen papers — came to the first king's court during the Long Hunger, when the fields gave nothing for three years running and the prayers went up like smoke from a wet fire. The old gods were silent then. Understand this: they were always silent. The old gods are mountains and rivers and the slow green patience of things that grow; they do not answer, because answering requires wanting, and they want nothing. The people prayed anyway, because people pray the way water runs downhill, and their prayers fell on the mountain like snow on stone.

Except some of it didn't fall. Some of it pooled. Centuries of it. Every desperate *please* from every famine winter, pooling in the deep places of Thornfell, pooling and pressing and — cohering. Not born. *Condensed.* Out of need itself. I am what need looks like when you pour enough of it in one place and wait.

Cyrus found me. Or I found her; the distinction flatters her. She was a taster, like Mara, trained to swallow death and report its flavor, and she came down into the dark with consecrated salt in her teeth and a bargain in her heart, and here is the part the archives leave out:

I did not take the kingdom. I was *given* it.

The first covenant was signed in blood and barley because the court wanted what I offered. Crops that rose overnight. Children pulled back from fever's edge. Rain in the third summer of drought. All I asked was devotion — meals offered, shrines fed, prayers spoken *to me* instead of past me into the indifferent sky. Is that monstrous? Examine it honestly. A god of the harvest asks to be thanked for the harvest. Your own church fathers tithe more greedily than I ever did, and they give back sermons.

The trouble — and I will concede this, because honesty — is appetite. Need made me, and need has no floor. Every answered prayer widened the channel, and the wider the channel, the more poured through, and somewhere in the second century of it I stopped being the shape of their need and became the shape of my feeding, and by then the distinction no longer mattered to anyone who mattered, which is to say, to no one who could stop me.

So they bound me. Cyrus bound me. With salt and with herself — she swallowed my hunger into her own body and held it there, starving it, for the rest of her short, ruined life, and the office she founded kept the seal after her, taster after taster, each one unknowing, each one eating my leavings with the king's dinner and calling it duty.

Thirty years, the covenant bought. Then another binding, weaker. Then another. Kings wore me like a coat that fit worse each reign, until Aldric, who fits me like a glove, because Aldric stopped struggling in the first year. Do not judge him too harshly. Struggling is exhausting, and I am comfortable, in the way a warm bath is comfortable, in the way deep water is comfortable.

* * *

Day five. She is weaker now — thirteen days without hoping burns calories no porridge can replace — and weakness opens doors that strength keeps shut. So I stop being gentle.

I show her the kitchen.

Not a vision, exactly — I don't deal in visions, visions are for lesser spirits — but a memory, hers, surfaced and lit: the palace kitchens at midnight, steam on the windows, Bram the head cook standing over a rising dough with flour to his elbows, teaching her, of all people, to fold it properly. *You press it away from you, girl, and turn it, and press it away again. That's how you make something strong. You work against it.* He laughed. He always laughed. He prayed to a god whose name hadn't been spoken in decades, and he got an answer, and the answer ate him hollow and left him kneeling and smiling, and Mara found him, and Mara read his note — *it drinks the faithful, do not pray where it can hear* — and turned his grief into resolve, which is her way with everything.

In the memory, Bram is laughing. I hold the memory steady. I let the smell of the bread rise in her — I can do that; I live in her body now, and her body remembers bread the way churches remember hymns.

"Stop," she says.

*Why?*

"Because you're using him."

*I am honoring him,* I say, and this is the truth, and the truth is my sharpest knife. *Bram prayed. Bram was answered. For eleven days he walked through his kitchen feeling the presence of something vast and attentive, and he wept with joy twice that I witnessed, and he told me — aloud, stirring his pot — that he had waited his whole life to be heard. Then I drank him, because drinking is what I am, and here is my question, Mara, and I want you to actually sit with it:* 

*Did I lie to him?*

Silence. Her heartbeat, which I can hear the way you hear weather through a wall.

*Name the lie,* I say. *Find the false word. He prayed; I answered. I never promised to be safe. No one asked. Your whole kingdom spent a generation begging the dark to notice them, and when the dark noticed — when it leaned down and said yes, yes, I hear you, I will give you what you ask — they called it a monster. But it was their asking. It was always their asking. I am the receipt for everything this country ever begged for, and you do not get to burn the receipt and pretend you never bought.*

"You chose to feed," she says. Her voice is steady. Her hands are not; I am in her hands. "Bram didn't choose to be eaten."

*He chose to kneel,* I say. *Kneeling is choosing. Every knee in that palace, every knee in those emptied villages — they walked up the mountain smiling, did you see them? Smiling. Because being devoured by something that answers is sweeter than being ignored by everything that doesn't. I have never taken a single soul that was not already reaching for me with both hands. I am the easiest door in the world to stay shut. Your people props it open with their bodies and calls me the draft.*

* * *

Day six. The window's slice of sky goes from grey to gold to grey. She eats her barley. She does not speak to me for the whole of it, which costs her more than speaking would — silence, for Mara, is not empty; silence is a room where the dead sit patiently waiting their turn.

So I wait too. I am better at it.

Toward evening she breaks first, as I knew she would, because six days of nothing is five days past any threshold she has ever survived, and I feel the question come up her throat like water finding a crack:

"What were you, before?"

*Before need?* I consider. *Nothing. Less than nothing — nothing at least has edges. I was the space where an answer would go. Think of a thousand years of kneeling in the mud, and every knee making a small impression, and the impressions overlapping, until the ground itself learned the shape of supplication. That was me. You cannot kill that, Mara. You can only fill it or leave it empty. Cyrus filled it with herself. Ivenna wants to fill it with salt. Your founder's recipe proposes to pour me into consecrated crystal and cork me like a wound.*

*But here is what your archives will not tell you, because Cyrus died before she learned it:* I let the warmth bloom again, deeper this time, a hearth-heat, a mother-kitchen heat, and I feel her fight it and feel the fighting cost her more than the yielding would. *A vessel that holds me is never empty again. And a person who is never empty is never lonely. Thirteen days, and then you pour me out, and then what? You walk down the mountain into a world where everyone knows your name, and names are hooks, and hooks are hunger, and someday — a year, ten years, a grief, a kindness — someone will offer you belonging, and you will take it, because you are human, and humans are made of reaching. And I will be in the reaching. I am patient in your ribs the way I was patient in the mountain. I can wait inside a single woman longer than kingdoms last.*

"So refuse the reaching," she says. "Starve you forever."

*Forever,* I say, tasting the word. *You have never once in your life done anything forever. You did nine years and kept an escape map under a floorboard. You are the least forever creature I have ever inhabited, and I have worn kings.*

She almost laughs. I feel it — the ghost of it, the muscle-twitch of it, strangled at birth. Good. Laughter is a door too.

*One more thing,* I say, as the dark comes down outside her high window, and I make my voice very quiet, quieter than the wind, quieter than her own pulse, so that it seems to come from somewhere beneath even her. *Tomorrow, ask yourself what Pell would say. The boy in the ravine. Ask whether he would trade your suffering for his breath. Ask what Bram would pay. Ask what a mother's kitchen is worth, Mara — the real one, the one before the border wars, the one with the blue door. I can open doors that were closed by death. I have never offered that before. I am offering it because you are the first vessel in thirty years strong enough to deserve the offer.*

"That's a bargain," she says slowly. "You're offering a bargain."

*I am offering honesty,* I say. *Sleep now. Count your breaths. Tomorrow we will talk about price.*

And she lies down in the ugly blanket in the unlovable cell, and she counts her breaths, one through twenty, and starts again, and I settle behind her sternum like a banked fire, and I do not push, because pushing is for weak gods.

Tomorrow I will offer her the dead. All of them. Bram laughing, Pell running with flour on his sleeves, every consumed priest and emptied village, restored, breathing, whole — the one payment I have never made, the one prayer I have never answered, held out at last to the one person alive with the strength to take it.

She will nearly say yes.

They always nearly say yes.
