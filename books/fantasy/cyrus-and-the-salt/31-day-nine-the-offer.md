## 31. Day Nine: The Offer

The hunger had learned her mother's voice by the third day.

By the ninth, it had learned everything else.

Mara sat in the shrine cell with her back against the cold stone, knees drawn up, hands flat on the floor where Ivenna had chalked a circle of salt she was not permitted to cross. Nine days without praying. Without hoping. Without belonging to anything or anyone. She had thought, walking into this cell behind Ivenna's lantern, that the hardest part would be the hunger itself—the god coiled in her ribs like a second heart, gnawing at the walls of her. She'd been dosed with enough poisons over nine years to know how to endure what lived inside you.

She had been wrong. The hunger barely touched her body. It went for the doors instead.

*Your mother's kitchen,* it murmured now, in a voice like warm bread and woodsmoke, in an accent from the border country she hadn't heard since she was seven years old. *The one with the crooked shutter. You used to stand on a stool and stir the pot while she sang. You've forgotten the song. I haven't.*

"I never stood on any stool," Mara said to the empty cell. "I don't remember a kitchen."

*I do.* A pause, savoring. *That's the difference between us, little taster. You spent nine years learning not to want things. I have spent a thousand years learning what people want. And I keep every prayer ever prayed to me, the way your tongue keeps every poison. Nothing given is ever lost. It only waits.*

The salt circle glittered faintly in the grey light from the high slit window. Day nine. Four days left. She counted them the way she'd once counted drops of hellebore in a king's wine—mechanically, precisely, because counting was a thing she could belong to without it belonging back.

"You're wasting your breath," she said. "I don't want anything."

The silence that followed had texture. It was the silence of something very old and very patient deciding to stop being gentle.

*Do you not?*

And then it showed her.

---

Not words this time. Not voices. The hunger reached into her the way a hand reaches into water, and the cell fell away, and Mara Lockridge—who had trained herself out of flinching, who could swallow arsenic without changing expression—felt her whole self lurch sideways like a woman missing a stair.

A kitchen. Her mother's kitchen, real down to the grain of the table, the chip in the blue bowl, the smell of onions in goose fat. A woman stood at the hearth with her sleeves rolled up, humming, and when she turned her face was worn smooth by time but unmistakable—Mara's own jawline, her own dark brows, softened by a smile Mara had no memory of ever earning.

"Hello, love," the woman said. "You're late. Wash your hands."

Mara's throat closed. *This isn't real,* she told herself, and the knowledge sat there, correct and useless, the way a map is useless to a drowning woman.

*It is more than real,* the hunger said gently, still in her mother's cadence. *It is owed. That is the word you keep circling and refusing to say. Owed. Your kingdom traded worship for survival for a generation, and the covenant consumed the faithful as payment—your cook who prayed with flour on his hands, your shepherd boy who ran messages through the ravines, hundreds whose names were written in my ledgers before your founder ever mixed her first salt. They are not gone, Mara. They are held. As I am held. And a held thing can be returned.*

The kitchen dissolved. What replaced it was worse, because it was plainer.

The cook—Bramm, his name was Bramm, she'd known him for seven years and eaten his bread every day of them—kneeling in the palace pantry, smiling, hollowed out. But in this vision he stood up. He turned. He brushed the flour from his apron and said, in his ordinary grumbling voice, "You're standing in my doorway again, girl. Either come in and eat or shut the door, you'll let the heat out."

Mara made a sound she hadn't made since childhood. Something small and involuntary.

Then the ravine. Grey morning, screeslope, the crack of the collapsed crossing—and Tam. Sixteen years old, all elbows, quick as a weasel, the boy who had carried her maps rolled in oilcloth strapped under his coat for three years because he was small enough to pass for a shepherd and fast enough to outrun suspicion. He lay on the rocks with his eyes open, and then he wasn't lying anymore—he was sitting up, brushing gravel off his palms, looking at her with mild reproach.

"You dropped me," he said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way he'd note weather. "You could catch me on the way back."

---

The vision released her and Mara came back into her body on the stone floor of the cell with her face wet and her hands shaking and the taste of copper in her mouth, which was strange, because nothing in this cell could poison her except herself.

She wiped her face with her sleeve, methodically. She breathed. She did the thing she had done ten thousand times in the tasting chamber: name the sensation, isolate it, refuse it purchase. Grief, she named it. Old grief, freshly ground. Do not swallow.

"That was cruel," she said hoarsely.

*That was honest,* said the hunger, and its voice had shifted—no longer her mother, no longer anyone, just itself: vast, level, reasonable as arithmetic. *You wanted me to lie. Everyone wants me to lie; lies are easier to refuse. But I will tell you plainly, as I told your founder plainly, as I told every king I wore: I can give them back. All of them. Bramm with his bread. Tam with his quick feet. The priestesses starved off my mountains, the faithful poured out over a generation like wine from a cracked jug. I hold them still. I have always held what I consume—I am not a fire, little taster. I am a cellar. And you are four days from pouring me out into the dark forever, and I am offering you the keys.*

"Because you want out."

*Because a bargain must be worth taking,* it said, unbothered. *I learned that from humans too. Hear the terms. Release me—not free, I ask no freedom; even I know better—but release me from your ribs, let me go back to my mountain unfed and dreaming, and I will open the cellar. Every soul the covenant consumed returns to their life at the moment they left it. The cook wakes in his pantry annoyed at losing an afternoon. Your boy walks home across a mended ravine. No one remembers dying, because they didn't. You undo it, Mara. All of it. Every grave you ever stood over with salt in your fist.*

Mara pressed her back harder against the wall. The salt circle threw pale light up at her. Four days. She had endured nine days of this thing wearing her mother's voice, and she understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, why the founder's notes had insisted on thirteen days without attachment.

Because attachment was the hinge. Because the hunger couldn't force the door—it could only be *invited*.

"And the cost," Mara said. "There's always a cost. I've read the covenant. I've read your old bargains. Nothing you give is free."

*The cost is me,* the hunger said simply. *I remain bound. Starved. Sealed in salt as your founder sealed me, dreaming hungry for another thirty years, or fifty, until some other kingdom grows desperate and some other crown grows clever. You lose nothing. I lose everything. That is why no one has ever taken this offer, and why I have only ever offered it to someone like you—someone who has already paid so much that she cannot bear to have paid it for nothing.*

The words landed exactly where they were aimed. Mara felt them sink in like needles finding old scar tissue.

Because that was the wound, wasn't it. Nine years of swallowing poison so the crown wouldn't have to. Three years of a boy running her roads. Seven years of a cook's bread. A lifetime of paying forward costs that no one tallied and no one thanked her for—and here was the thing in her chest offering her a receipt. *Here. Take it all back. It was not wasted. It need not have been lost.*

Her eyes burned. She let them.

"I nearly believe you," she said quietly.

*Nearly,* said the hunger, with something almost like respect, *is how every covenant begins.*

---

She didn't sleep that night—or she slept the way a knife sleeps in a drawer, edge-up, ready. In the grey hours she got up and paced the length of the salt circle, heel to toe, twenty paces, the rhythm she'd kept since day one, and she talked to it because silence was a door too.

"If you can restore them," she said, "why haven't you? You've had kings kneeling to you for a generation. You've had queens pouring devotion into you by the bowlful. You were three days from permanent binding. You could have opened your cellar anytime and bought their love forever."

A long quiet. When it answered, it had stopped pretending to be anything but what it was.

*Because restored souls buy love for a season,* it said, *and devoured souls feed me forever. If I gave them back, I would be giving away my stores. I offered you this bargain knowing you will refuse, little taster, because refusal is also food—it is the richest meal there is. Hope, refused at the last moment. Do you know what that tastes like? No one does. Only I do. It tastes like the moment a starving man pushes away bread.*

Mara stopped pacing.

"There it is," she said softly. "Thank you."

*For what?*

"For telling me the truth badly." She sat back down against the wall, and for the first time in nine days, she smiled—a thin, tired, genuine thing. "You just showed me your ledger. You can't restore them without unmaking yourself. The cellar opens only if the cellar ceases to be a cellar. Giving them back would be your death, dressed up as my choice. You weren't offering me a bargain. You were offering me your suicide and calling it mercy, hoping I'd be too grief-drunk to do the sums."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far below, wind moved through the mountain passes, and the consecrated salt in the shrine jars above her head hummed faintly, like distant bees.

*Clever,* said the hunger at last, and there was no warmth in it at all now. *Cyrus was clever too.*

"I know," Mara said. "I've read her notes. She refused you as well."

*Cyrus did not refuse me,* said the hunger, and its voice dropped, and for the first time in nine days Mara heard something underneath it that she recognized from the tasting chamber, from the faces of men drinking wine they suspected: fear. *Cyrus took me in, starved me thirteen days, and poured me into the salt. And do you know what she said at the end, little taster? On her last day, with her hands in the jar? She said, "Forgive me. I could not carry it longer." Your founder died believing she had failed. Because she remembered us both, in the end—you and me. She remembered that a seal is only a promise postponed.*

Mara wrapped her arms around her knees. Outside, the light through the slit window had gone gold with morning. Day nine, ending. Four days left.

"I'm going to finish the sum," she said. "All thirteen days. And then I'm going to pour you out, and I'm going to live with what I couldn't save—Tam, Bramm, all of them—because living with it is the price of the only kind of saving that lasts. That's the recipe. That's what Cyrus actually wrote, under all the grief."

*And if the seal fails,* the hunger whispered, sweet as rot, *in thirty years, or fifty—if some starving border girl swallows me again and lacks your spine—will you tell yourself it was worth it, from whatever dark holds the dead?*

"Yes," Mara said. "Now be quiet. I'm counting."

And the hunger laughed—one low roll of it, deep in her ribs, like a landslide very far away—and settled, and waited, as it had always been willing to wait.

But that evening, when Ivenna brought the day's ration of water and plain barley, the old priestess paused at the cell door. She looked at Mara a long moment—at the tear tracks, at the smile that hadn't fully faded, at the way Mara's hands were steady in a way they hadn't been since day one.

"It spoke to you," Ivenna said. Not a question. "What did it offer?"

Mara looked down at the salt circle, and thought of a boy catching her on the way back across a mended ravine, and made her voice come out level.

"Everything," she said. "It offered me everything."

Ivenna went white. "And you said—"

"Not yet," Mara said. "Ask me again tomorrow."

And somewhere in her ribs, patiently, the hunger began to count along with her.
