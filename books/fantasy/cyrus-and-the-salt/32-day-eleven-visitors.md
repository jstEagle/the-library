## 32. Day Eleven: Visitors

Sera Vane had broken rules for thirty years. She had lied to kings, poisoned informants' trust, buried evidence, and once forged an entire war to buy a fleet three days. She was, by her own accounting, very good at doing the wrong thing correctly.

So it said something that standing outside a shrine cell door with a lantern and a sack of dried plums felt like the hardest transgression of her life.

Ivenna's orders had been explicit. *No visitors. No gifts. No names spoken at the door. The vessel must be nothing to anyone.* The old priestess had said it the way she said everything — flat as a ledger line, like a woman reading terms of a contract she hadn't written and couldn't renegotiate. Sera had nodded. Sera had agreed.

Sera had also spent two days watching the mountain weather turn and thinking about what happened on day nine.

She knew because she'd made Ivenna tell her. Day nine, the hunger in Mara Lockridge's ribs had offered its bargain: everyone back. The cook. The boy in the ravine. All of them, breathing, laughing, whole — for one word of surrender. And Mara had nearly said yes. Ivenna admitted it in that same flat voice, staring into the middle distance, hands folded like a woman at a funeral that hadn't started yet.

*Nearly* was a word Sera understood professionally. Nearly was where every betrayal she'd ever prevented had lived. You didn't stop a traitor by trusting him not to betray. You stopped him by removing the temptation.

And here was the thing Sera could not get past, the splinter under her nail: no one was watching the door.

Ivenna trusted. Ivenna prayed — carefully, rationed, witnessed, but she prayed — and faith, Sera had learned over a lifetime of reading men, was just confidence wearing robes. Sera did not have faith. Sera had contingency plans. And her contingency plan required knowing, with her own eyes, that the woman who had swallowed a god was still holding the lid down.

The plums were the excuse. Dried plums from the valley, the kind Mara had eaten by the fistful in the palace kitchens, according to the cook's old ledgers — Sera read everything, even dead men's shopping lists. It wasn't food that mattered. It wouldn't sustain anything. But hunger of the body was the one appetite the fast didn't forbid, and if Mara had gone eleven days without complaint then the least Sera could do was stand in the cold like a fool and offer something no one would accept.

That was the other reason she'd come, though she wouldn't name it even to herself. She wanted to be refused. She wanted to stand there while Mara turned her away, because if Mara could refuse her — refuse the spymaster who held her life in a file drawer, refuse the only ally left inside the walls of her own former kingdom — then Mara could refuse anything. Then day thirteen would arrive and the salt would hold and Sera would not have to learn what the world looked like when it ended.

She lifted her hand to knock.

The door opened first.

Mara stood in the frame, thin as a winter branch, wrapped in the grey wool Ivenna had given her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Up close the change was worse than Sera had braced for. Eleven days of starving a god showed on a face like frost shows on glass — not dramatic, not wounded, just a slow opaque creep across something that used to be clear. Mara's eyes were steady. That was the worst part. They were the steadiest eyes Sera had ever seen, and they looked like eyes that had been offered everything and had priced it.

"You're supposed to be in the valley," Mara said.

"I don't follow orders from priests."

"No," Mara said. "You follow leverage. What's yours this time?"

Sera held up the sack. "Plums."

Something moved behind Mara's eyes — not warmth, Sera noted, not quite; more like a door opening onto a lit room and immediately closing. "From the kitchen stores?"

"The valley orchards. Third terrace. The ones with the crooked trees."

"I know the ones." And now the steadiness flickered, because of course Mara knew the ones. Everyone from the border knew the ones. You climbed those terraces as a child with your pockets full of stolen fruit and your mother's voice behind you saying *not the crooked trees, girl, they're sour* — and you ate them anyway, because sour was better than nothing, which was the first lesson the border taught and the last one it ever let go.

"They're sour," Sera said.

"I know."

They stood there. Wind came up the pass and flattened the grass against the shrine wall and made the lantern flame lie sideways.

"You should close the door," Mara said.

"In a moment."

"That's not a moment, Sera. That's the beginning of a conversation, and conversations are—" Mara stopped. Chose again. "Attachment feeds it. You know that. She told you that."

"She told me a lot of things. I verify."

"Verify from down the mountain."

"I've spent my career watching people in rooms," Sera said. "Watching is how I love things. It's the only way I've got." She heard herself say it and was briefly furious — thirty years of tradecraft and this starved woman in a borrowed robe pulled the confession out of her like a pickpocket. "I'm not asking you to talk. I'm asking to stand here. Ten minutes. You don't have to look at me. I'll count it as surveillance."

"It isn't surveillance."

"No. It isn't."

Mara's hand came up and pressed flat against the doorframe — not reaching, Sera saw, but bracing. Holding herself in place the way you hold yourself in place against a current. "Do you know what it's been showing me?" she asked quietly. "At night. In the dark. It doesn't scream. It doesn't threaten. It sets tables."

"Sets—"

"My mother's kitchen. A pot on the stove. Two bowls. It knows I can't remember what was in the bowls anymore, so it lets me choose. That's the cruelty of it — it's generous. It gives me the empty bowl and says, *fill it with whatever you loved.*" Mara's thumb pressed white against the wood. "Day nine it added a third chair. It put you in it, Sera. It set your place. It said, *she came all this way, she's standing at the door right now, all you have to do is open it.*"

The wind moved through the pass. Sera became aware, with the cold professional clarity she trusted more than her own heartbeat, that they were having exactly the conversation the fast could not afford.

"So I opened the door before you knocked," Mara finished. "Because I'd rather it be my hand than its answer to yours."

Sera looked at the sack of plums. Sour plums from crooked trees, carried up a mountain by the most dangerous woman in the kingdom, who had burned her files and rebuilt her craft and defected with the court's secrets in her arms — all of it suddenly, absurdly small against a doorframe and a starving woman's braced hand.

"You're going to send me away," Sera said.

"I'm going to send you away."

"And it's going to cost us both something we don't get back."

"Yes."

"Then say it well," Sera snapped, and hated the crack in her own voice. "If you're going to spend us, spend us clean. Don't give me priestess language. Don't tell me it's for the kingdom — I've forged kingdoms, I know what they weigh. Tell me why *me*. Why does my standing here feed it, when Ivenna prays ten feet away every morning?"

"Because Ivenna prays to the gods," Mara said. "You pray to me."

Sera said nothing.

"Don't look like that. I've done it too. Nine years in that palace, I watched it happen to everyone — the king, the queen, half the court. Devotion is just love that's decided it can't survive without the thing it loves. It stops being about the beloved. It becomes feeding." Mara's voice stayed level, and Sera realized the levelness itself was costing her — that every calm word was bought with effort visible only in the braced hand, the too-steady eyes. "It felt you coming yesterday. It's been humming since dawn. Not hungry-humming. Pleased. Like a host hearing guests on the road."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. Apology is attachment with guilt on top. Just go."

"I brought plums."

"Eat them yourself. On the walk down. One at each switchback." A ghost of something crossed Mara's mouth — not a smile; the memory of the shape of one. "Sour ones. So you remember the lesson."

"Which lesson?"

"That sour is better than nothing." Mara's hand dropped from the doorframe. "That's the last inheritance I've got worth passing on. Everything else I owned, I burned."

"You didn't burn me."

"No," Mara said softly. "I'm spending you instead. There's a difference. Burning is quick."

For a long moment Sera stood with the wind pulling at her coat and the sack heavy in her hand, and she understood — fully, finally, in the way she understood a decrypted letter, all at once and irreversibly — that this was what love looked like when it was being used correctly: not kept, not clutched, not fed. Spent. Deliberately. Like coin you'd rather lose than let a god count.

She put the plums on the threshold stone. Not handing them — placing them, the way you leave offerings, which is to say the way you leave things for people who cannot afford to take them from your hand.

"If day thirteen comes and you're still you," Sera said, stepping back, "I'm going to spend the rest of my career making sure no one ever puts you in a room like this again."

"If day thirteen comes and I'm still me," Mara answered, "you won't need to. I'll have learned how to be a room myself." Her eyes closed. Opened. Steadier than ever, and emptier, and Sera thought: *that's not peace, that's excavation.* "Go down the mountain, Sera. Eat your plums. And whatever it whispers to you on the way — and it will, it's started learning your voice, it's had two days of your footsteps on my doorstep to study it — do not answer it."

Sera went.

She counted the switchbacks as instructed. At the third she ate a plum, and it was sour enough to make her eyes water, and she stood in the wind with wet eyes and told herself it was the fruit.

At the seventh she heard it — faint, beneath the wind, pitched perfectly between Mara's level calm and Sera's own clipped cadence: *she's alone in there. She's so tired. One visit wouldn't—*

She did not answer.

She walked, and ate, and did not answer, and the voice tried twice more and grew quieter each time, the way voices do when their audience starves them. By the bottom of the pass the sun was setting over Thornfell and the crooked trees were black lines against it, and Sera Vane sat down on a wall like an old woman and allowed herself, for exactly sixty seconds, to grieve a friendship she had never gotten to have properly.

Then she stood, wiped her face, and began composing in her head the report no one would ever read — the true one, the one that said: *Day eleven. Vessel holding. Temptation attempted via visitor; refused. Recommend immediate hardening of protocols, because on day twelve, the thing in her ribs is going to stop whispering to her and start shouting to us.*

Behind her, far up the mountain, the shrine bell rang once — Ivenna's evening ration of prayer, measured and witnessed.

And somewhere below it, in a grey cell with a closed door, a woman sat down on a cold floor with an empty bowl in her mind and chose, again, not to fill it.

* * *

The twelfth moon rose that night, swollen and wrong-colored, and Sera watched it from the valley inn window and did not sleep.

She did not know yet — could not yet know — that in the lowlands the feast halls were already lighting, that riders were already on the roads, that the god's remaining strength had found one last door to push against. She only knew that the wind had changed direction, that it was blowing uphill now, toward the shrine, carrying with it on its back the smell of woodsmoke and roasting meat and a thousand throats preparing to sing.

Faith, traveling toward the vessel like a flood toward a dam.

Sera dressed in the dark, saddled her horse, and rode for the shrine to warn them: the god was done waiting for day thirteen.
