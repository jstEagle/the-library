## 33. Day Thirteen: Salt

The last morning came gray and thin, like everything else in the cell.

Mara knew it was the thirteenth day because Ivenna had scratched a mark into the stone beside the door each dawn, and there were twelve marks now, and the priestess's shadow had just passed the gap beneath the door on her way to light the shrine fires. Thirteen days without prayer. Without hope. Without belonging to anything.

She had done it. She was almost certain she had done it.

*Almost* was a word she'd learned to live inside for nine years. Almost poisoned. Almost caught. Almost dead. Almost was where tasters lived.

She lay on the pallet and took inventory of herself the way she'd once taken inventory of a suspect dish: what was present, what was absent, what had changed since yesterday. Present: her body, lighter than it had ever been, a scaffolding with opinions. Present: the cold. The cell had no brazier; warmth was an attachment, Ivenna said, and attachments fed it. Absent: hunger — not hers, *its*. For twelve days the thing in her ribs had screamed at her in her mother's voice, in Tam's voice, in the cook Brannic's booming kitchen laugh, offering her bread, home, forgiveness, rest. On the eleventh day it had offered her the boy from the ravine, alive, calling her name across a green field.

She had not answered. That was the whole discipline. Not strength. Just — not answering. A lifetime of swallowing things without comment, finally turned inward.

Absent, too: hope. That one had been harder. Hope crept in through cracks she hadn't known she had. She'd caught herself on day six imagining the end of this — imagining walking out, imagining salt and bread and Sera's dry voice complaining about paperwork — and the hunger had swelled against her ribs like a belly filling with wine. She'd spent that night reciting poison antidotes instead, the founder's recipe, the bitter litany of hemlock and hellebore and the four signs of aconite, until the swelling went down. A woman could survive on bitterness. Mara was living proof.

What remained, when you stripped out prayer and hope and love, was surprisingly small. Breath. The taste of the air, which was stone and old salt. And a kind of attention so clean it frightened her. This, she understood suddenly, was what Cyrus must have felt. This hollow clarity. This was the tool the office had been grinding toward for thirty years without knowing why.

The hunger stirred under her sternum. Weaker now. Much weaker. It moved like something at the bottom of a well, turning over in its sleep.

*You will pour me out,* it said. Its voice had lost the trick of sounding like her mother. Now it sounded only like itself, which was worse — vast and patient and very tired. *And then you will be empty too. You know this.*

"I know," Mara said aloud. Her voice came out rusted. "I've been empty before. It's survivable."

*It is not living.*

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

Footsteps in the corridor. The bar scraped. Ivenna came in with a clay lamp and two other priests behind her, their faces drawn from thirteen days of keeping vigil, and behind them, uninvited, unannounced, came Sera Vane.

Mara sat up.

"You're supposed to be in the capital," she said.

"The capital can burn for one morning." Sera looked terrible — travel-stained, hollow-eyed, her spymaster's composure hanging off her like a borrowed coat. "I rode three days to watch you do this, and if you tell me my being here feeds it, I'll wait outside."

"It does feed it," Ivenna said quietly. "But not enough to matter anymore. Look at her." The priestess crouched, took Mara's chin in a hand rough as bark, and peered into her eyes the way a farrier looks at a hoof. Whatever she saw made her exhale. "It's nearly starved. Another week and it would have gone dormant on its own. But dormant isn't sealed. Dormant waits." She stood. "Today we finish it."

They brought the salt in on a litter, because it took four men to carry: the great basin of the First Shrine, older than the kingdom, its stone worn into a shallow bowl by centuries of hands. It was already half full of consecrated salt — white-gray, coarse, glittering dully in the lamplight. Salt from every shrine in Thornfell, carried down over the past days by pilgrims who didn't fully understand what they were carrying it for. Ivenna had told them the truth, or most of it. Enough truth to make the carrying a devotion. Devotion, Mara thought wryly, was the one food the god wouldn't get today.

Around the rim of the basin, carved so deep the grooves held shadow, ran the founder's script. Mara had copied it onto her skin during the long nights — not ink; she'd had no ink — but with a pin of bone, pricking the letters into her forearm so she could read them by touch in the dark. *Hold. Starve. Pour. Seal.* Four words. Thirty years of her life had been preparation for four words.

"Sit," Ivenna said.

Mara sat on the cold stone floor beside the basin. The priests formed a ring around her, and Sera took the place directly opposite, arms folded, jaw set, watching Mara with an expression she refused to soften into anything as honest as fear.

Ivenna knelt at Mara's side. "Last chance," she said. "Once I open the way, it will fight. It will show you everything you've ever wanted. It will lie with your own mouth. You have to keep pouring even while it screams. Do you understand?"

"I've swallowed worse," Mara said. "With better manners than it has."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the old priestess's face. Then she drew a knife — not iron, but black glass, older than iron — and cut a line across her own palm, and let the blood fall into the salt.

The cell changed.

There was no better word for it. The walls went further away and closer at once. The lamplight bent. And under Mara's ribs, the hunger woke all the way and understood, in the first instant, exactly what was about to happen.

It began to scream.

Not with sound. With memory. Her mother's kitchen bloomed around her — woodsmoke, barley porridge, a woman humming off-key — so vivid Mara could smell the crust on the pot. *Stay,* the kitchen said. *Stay and none of the rest ever happened. No palace. No poisons. No ravine. Stay and be a child who is fed.*

Mara gripped the rim of the basin. Her knuckles were white as the salt.

"Pour," Ivenna said. "Now. Hold the way open and *pour*."

Mara reached inward.

She had no training for this. There was no training. What she had was nine years of doing the impossible thing calmly: putting death in her mouth, recognizing it, and swallowing anyway because the king needed to eat. So she did the same motion, inverted. She found the coiled weight beneath her sternum — a knot of cold the size of a fist, or a heart, or a crown — and she pulled.

It came the way a tooth comes. Rooted. Resistant. Screaming.

The visions hit her in waves as she dragged it up through herself. Brannic the cook, laughing, flour to his elbows — *you could have saved me, you ate at my table* — she poured that wave into the salt and kept pulling. Tam, the boy from the ravine, thirteen years old, holding out a message he'd run through snow for her — *you built the road I died on* — into the salt. The border village of her childhood, burning or not burning, she honestly couldn't remember anymore which — into the salt. Every offered hand, every warm room, every version of a life where someone had loved her: she took each one as it rose, named it a lie or a grief or both, and gave it to the basin.

The hunger fought hardest at the end, when it was small enough to be desperate. It stopped offering and started taking — flooding her with its own memories, a thousand years of famines, fields of people kneeling in dust, mouths opening like fledglings, *feed us, feed us, we made you, we are only what you needed* — and Mara, sobbing now, hands buried to the wrist in nothing, heaved the whole cold mass up her throat and out.

It left her the way a decade leaves. All at once and forever.

She felt it pass through her teeth — she would swear to her dying day she felt it pass through her teeth — and fall into the salt, and the salt *took* it. The basin flared white. Every grain lit at once, a bowl of captured lightning, and the screaming stopped mid-note, and the silence afterward was so total that Mara heard her own blood moving.

Then the light sank into the salt and became, simply, salt again. Coarse. Gray-white. Still.

Mara fell forward. Hands caught her — Ivenna's, Sera's, four priests' — and for a moment she was held above the basin like something being lowered into a grave or raised out of one, she genuinely could not tell which.

"Seal it," Ivenna said hoarsely. "While it's stunned. Quickly."

The lid was waiting. They'd made it together, in the days before the fast began, and it was the part Mara had insisted on — the part where she differed from Cyrus. The founder had sealed her basin with a flat stone and a binding meant to hold until the world ended, and the world had chipped at it for thirty years, and the seal had leaked, and the leak had worn a king hollow. Mara's lid was stone too, but underneath it, set into the rim like teeth, were channels cut to Ivenna's design — channels that could be filled with fresh consecrated salt, renewed each season, opened deliberately by rites written down in a book that would live in the shrine and be taught to every keeper. Not a tomb. A larder. A thing you could open, carefully, on purpose, when the kingdom genuinely starved — and close again after.

Faith as a meal shared, not a feast devoured. Ivenna's phrase. Mara had stolen it and cut it into stone.

They lowered the lid. It fit with a sound like a door closing in an empty house. Ivenna packed the channels with new salt, spoke the four words of the founder's script — *hold, starve, pour, seal* — and then, adding her own line in a voice that shook only slightly, a fifth word that hadn't existed thirty years ago:

"*Witness.*"

The basin went quiet. Not dormant-quiet. Sealed-quiet. Mara had spent her life learning the difference between a poison that waited and a poison that was finished, and she knew, with her whole scarred tongue's worth of certainty, that this was the second kind.

* * *

Afterward they carried her into the shrine proper, into daylight for the first time in thirteen days, and the light hurt so much she laughed, or cried, or both — she wasn't sure where one ended. They gave her water first, then broth, then, when Ivenna judged her stomach ready, bread. Actual bread. She ate it slowly, tasting every stage of it the way she tasted everything: the wheat, the yeast, the ash from the oven, the salt on the crust.

Salt. She paused, crust halfway to her mouth.

"It's fine," Ivenna said, watching her face. "Ordinary salt. The basin holds it. It doesn't spread."

Mara finished the bread.

Sera sat down across from her, uncharacteristically silent, and for a long moment the two women looked at each other over the remains of the meal.

"You look awful," Sera said finally.

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. He's in a bowl." Sera's mouth twitched, then steadied. "Thirteen days. You actually did it. I've watched people crack in thirteen *hours*, Mara. Under far less."

"I've had practice being nobody." Mara looked at her hands. They were shaking now — they hadn't shaken once inside the cell, and she supposed that was the point; there had been nothing in there worth trembling for. "That's the whole trick, if you're wondering. You can't hold a god if the god can find a handle on you. So you take the handles off."

"That's not living either," Sera said quietly. "You told it so yourself. I heard you say it through the door on day eleven, when I wasn't supposed to be there."

Mara looked up. "You heard that."

"I hear everything. Occupational disease." Sera leaned forward. "So here's what happens now, since you've been too busy starving to plan. Aldric is alive. Barely — whatever was driving him collapsed the night you began the extraction; the physicians say his heart is its own again but his body's been a rented horse for twenty years. He wakes for minutes at a time. And when he wakes, he asks for you. By name. Every time." She said it flatly, but her eyes weren't flat at all. "The court doesn't know what happened at the feast. Half of them think you're dead. The other half think you're a saint. I've let both rumors run, because confused courts are manageable courts."

"And Isolde?"

"Gone to the shrines. Voluntarily, before anyone could make it a sentence. She asked Ivenna to assign her to the poorest one." Sera shrugged, a small economical motion that contained, if you knew how to read her, an entire opinion about queens and penance. "The kingdom's going to need months to sort out what it believes. Which means it needs one thing immediately, before the stories harden."

"What?"

"A witness." Sera held her gaze. "Someone who was inside the god and walked out. Someone whose whole profession is telling the truth about what's in the food. The court will believe you. More than that — the court will *need* to believe you, because the alternative is believing what they did for twenty years, and people will kneel to almost any story that spares them that."

Mara set down the cup. Her hands had mostly stopped shaking.

"I'm not going back to being invisible," she said. "Whatever comes next, I go as someone they can see."

"Good," said Sera, and for the first time in nine years of knowing her, Mara watched the spymaster smile without calculating it first. "Because the king's awake. Right now. This minute. And he's asking for you again — and this time, Mara, he remembers why."

Outside, the fourteenth moon was still weeks away, but the morning sun came through the shrine's high window and struck the sealed basin, and the salt inside glowed — faintly, briefly, like something sleeping rather than something dead — and settled again.

Mara stood up. It took two tries.

"Take me to him," she said.
