## 34. The King Who Remembers

The first thing Aldric learned about being free was that freedom had a taste.

Salt. Faint, mineral, at the back of the throat where thirty years of something else had lived. He woke on the floor of his own feast hall with his cheek against cold stone and a thousand faces staring down at him, and the first breath he drew tasted like the mountain shrines of his boyhood—like snowmelt and consecrated rock—and he understood, before anyone told him, what had been taken out of him and who had paid for it.

He tried to say her name. What came out was a croak, a rusted hinge of a sound, because he had not used his own voice in longer than he could count. The court physician leaned over him, weeping without dignity, and behind the physician stood a woman with a scarred mouth and eyes like a ledger's margins, watching him with no expression at all.

Mara Lockridge. He knew her name the way he knew the names of rivers he'd never sailed. She had knelt at his table for nine years, tasting his food, and he had never once looked at her properly. He looked at her now. She held his gaze for exactly as long as courtesy required, then turned and walked out of the hall like a woman leaving a room she'd already memorized.

He would spend the rest of his life learning what that walk had cost her. But that came later. That morning, all he could do was lie on the stone and breathe salt air in a body that felt like a house after a fire—standing, but gutted, every room stripped to the joists.

* * *

They gave him three days before they let him see the ledgers.

Sera Vane brought them herself, which told him everything. His spymaster did not carry documents; she carried conclusions. But she carried these in both arms like an armful of wounded birds, and she set them on the council table one by one, and she did not soften any of it.

"Thirty-one years of your reign," she said. "I've been through the archives. Every disappearance, every village that stopped tithing, every priest who asked questions and then stopped asking anything. I can give you the summary or the whole weight."

"The whole weight," Aldric said. His voice was coming back. It sounded older than he remembered it sounding. "I spent thirty years not carrying it. I don't get to choose the light load now."

So she read to him. For six hours she read, and he sat in his chair—the throne felt obscene, so he sat in a plain council chair—and listened to the accounting of his reign.

Not his wars. Not his laws. His hunger's harvest.

The god had been careful, in the early years. It had worn him well. It had signed sensible decrees, smiled at the right ambassadors, fathered a daughter whose face he still could not summon—he had tried, in the first hour of clarity, and found only a warm blur where a child should be, and that loss hurt worse than anything else the thing had done, worse than the decades stolen outright. It had been careful for years. And then it had grown comfortable, and comfortable things grow greedy, and the disappearances began. Priests first. Then shrine-keepers. Then whole families who prayed too fervently, too specifically, whose devotion had a richness the god could taste. They didn't die. That was the part Sera made him hear twice. They were *consumed*—walked smiling into the hills, poured out like wine, their faith drunk down to the lees while their bodies wandered home empty and sat by windows until winter took them.

"Two hundred and eleven names," Sera finished. "That's what I've confirmed. There may be more in the mountain parishes. The records there are..." She paused, and for the first time since he'd known her, Sera Vane looked away from him. "The records there are partly my work. I'll account for that separately. To you, and to whoever else requires it."

"Sera."

"My king."

"What do you remember of the last thirty years? Of me?"

She considered the question with the same care she gave troop movements. "A king who was kind in small ways and absent in large ones. A king who never remembered a servant's name twice. A king whose shadow—" she stopped. Started again. "I noticed the shadow four years ago. I filed it as an optical curiosity. That filing is the single greatest failure of my career, and I intend to wear it."

"You couldn't have known."

"I could have *wondered*." She gathered the ledgers. "Wondering is the job. Everything else is decoration." At the door she paused. "There are families waiting in the outer court. They've been waiting since the unbinding. They want to know if you're going to receive them or hide."

"I'm going to kneel," Aldric said.

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she nodded once, sharply, and left.

* * *

He knelt in the outer court on flagstones still scarred from the night the god had spoken through his mouth with a landslide voice. Two hundred and eleven names, and forty-three of them had someone left to come—a widow, a brother, a grown daughter clutching a christening gown that had never been worn.

His chamberlain had begged him to receive them seated. Had begged him, at minimum, to use a cushion. Aldric had dismissed the man with more courtesy than he deserved and walked out onto the stones in his plainest clothes, and when the first family was ushered into the courtyard—the widow Maren, seventy years old, holding her dead husband's fishing knife because it was the only thing of his the god hadn't eaten—Aldric went down onto his knees in front of her, on the stone, in front of the whole assembled court, and stayed there.

She stared at him. The courtyard held its breath.

"I cannot return him," Aldric said. His voice carried; the acoustics of the court had always favored kings, even disgraced ones. "I cannot tell you it wasn't me, because it wore my face and used my hands and slept in my bed, and you deserve better than a king who hides behind that distinction. I will not ask for forgiveness. Forgiveness is yours to give or withhold, and either way I will owe you. What I ask is this: let me work. Let me spend what remains of my life paying down a debt I can never finish, starting today, starting with you, and let the kingdom watch me do it so that no throne ever makes this covenant again."

The widow Maren looked at him for a long time. Then she spat at his feet—not in rage, he thought later, but in the manner of a woman settling an account, marking the debt as real—and said, "Then start, king. My roof leaks and my nets rot and I am too old to climb ladders."

And that was how the restitution began: with the King of Veyland on his knees in his own courtyard, agreeing to mend a fisherman's widow's roof, while his chancellor made strangled sounds and his guards discovered urgent business elsewhere.

He mended the roof himself. Three days later, with his own hands, badly, under the instruction of a neighbor boy who corrected him without deference. His palms blistered. He found he was grateful for the blisters. Pain was honest. Pain was his own.

* * *

The mountain shrines came next, and they nearly broke him.

He traveled light—twelve riders, no banner, because a banner invited crowds and crowds invited worship and worship was the one thing he could never again allow near himself. Sera rode beside him and said little. Mara Lockridge, he learned, had gone ahead days earlier by her own roads, and he did not ask how she'd known to; some debts arrange themselves.

The Shrine of the First Salt sat in a high pass where the wind came off the ice fields clean enough to drink. It was smaller than he'd imagined. A stone building the size of a barn, a bell with a cracked lip, terraced gardens gone to weed. And graves. Rows and rows of graves behind it, tended with desperate neatness by a handful of surviving keepers, each marker carved with a name and a single word: *Remembered.*

Ivenna met him at the gate. She was old the way mountains are old—not diminished, just patient beyond human scale—and she looked at him the way one looks at weather.

"You're the vessel it wore," she said.

"I'm the man it ate," Aldric answered. "The vessel was a girl from the border marshes who swallowed poison for thirty years so the seal would hold, and none of us knew, and none of us thanked her."

Something moved behind the priestess's eyes. Approval, maybe, or its austere cousin. "You've been talking to the taster."

"I've been trying. She's a difficult woman to thank. She leaves rooms."

"They all do, at first." Ivenna stepped aside and let him through the gate. "Come and see what your crown starved."

He saw. He spent two days seeing. The empty reliquaries where the consecrated salt should have stood ankle-deep. The scriptorium where the rites had been copied by candlelight for generations by keepers who no longer believed anyone above the tree line remembered they existed. The last true priestess's cell, with its single blanket and its wall of names—hundreds of names, added year by year, the consumed, recorded in a hand that had never wavered even when hope must have.

On the second evening he found her in the garden, kneeling in the dirt among the weeds, and he knelt beside her without being asked, and pulled weeds, and neither of them spoke until the light went blue.

"Why didn't it kill me?" he asked finally. "Thirty years. Why keep the body alive?"

"Because a god that eats its king loses its throne," Ivenna said. "It needed you the way a thief needs a fence. You were the shape it wore to make the eating look like governance." She sat back on her heels. "You want to know the worst of it, king? In thirty years, it never once had to force a prayer. Your court volunteered. Your queen volunteered hardest of all. The covenant runs on willing mouths."

He thought of Isolde—her elaborate devotions, her hollow eyes, the way she had poured herself out nightly at the altar like a woman bailing a sinking ship with her own hands. He had learned, in the weeks since, fragments of what she knew and when she'd known it. Her family had brokered the original covenant. Her prayers had kept the god docile. She had fed her husband's captor with love because the alternative was watching it wake hungry.

"I don't know how to be married to her now," he said. It came out before he could stop it, the first true personal sentence he'd spoken aloud since waking on the feast hall floor.

"No one does," Ivenna said. "Do it anyway. Badly. Repentance isn't a skill. It's just showing up."

* * *

He worked the shrines for nineteen days.

He restored the salt stores with his own treasury—stripped, over Sera's vigorous objection, of the ceremonial plate and half the crown regalia, melted down and traded to salt merchants in Thornfell. He copied rites in the scriptorium with ink-stained fingers while a keeper who was younger than his reign corrected his calligraphy. He dug a drainage channel in the rain because the terraced gardens flooded every spring and had flooded every spring for thirty years because no petition for repairs had ever survived the journey from the mountains to the crown.

Every petition had survived the journey. He found them, later, in a box in his own chancellery—forty years of requests from the shrines, unopened, tied with string. The god had not even bothered to read its victims' letters. He sat with that box for most of a night. In the morning he ordered it placed, unopened strings and all, in the new hall he commissioned that same week: the Hall of Accounts, where the kingdom's debt to the faithful would be written on the walls, name by name, petition by petition, until every line was answered or honestly marked unpaid.

Some of his council resigned over the Hall of Accounts. Lord Cassel called it "a monument to weakness that will haunt the dynasty." Aldric received the resignation standing, thanked the man for thirty years of service, and noted that haunting was rather the point.

"A king who cannot blush," he told the remaining council, "should not be trusted with a kingdom. We are going to learn to blush. All of us. It will be undignified and it will take years and we are going to do it anyway."

* * *

The dreams were the price.

Every night, the god came back—not as presence, not as hunger, but as memory. It showed him the years from inside. The coronation feast where the covenant had sealed itself into his blood with a taste like honeyed quail. His daughter's face, seen clearly one last time before it blurred. The specific flavor of ten thousand prayers, catalogued and savored. It showed him what devotion tasted like from the divine side of the table, and the horror was that some of it had been beautiful—that faith, drunk at the source, was the finest thing he had ever tasted, and some traitorous cellar of his soul still missed it.

He told Ivenna about the dreams on his last night at the shrine.

"It will do that," she said. "For years, probably. The palate remembers. This is why the seal matters, and why the salt must be tended, and why the taster's office must become what the taster intends it to become." She studied him. "You dream of missing it. Do you dream of her? The taster. Taking it out of you."

"Every night. Same dream. She's pouring me out like a cup and filling me with snowmelt, and in the dream I'm saying thank you, and in the dream I mean it."

"That's not the god," Ivenna said. "That's just the truth. They sound similar at first. You'll learn to tell them apart."

* * *

He rode home through the passes on roads Mara Lockridge had mapped in secret for nine years—roads that existed, he now understood, because a starving border girl had planned her escape from his palace down to the switchback, and had never once needed to use them until she left to save the kingdom that owned her. He asked Sera, somewhere past the second pass, what restitution looked like for a woman like that. What do you pay someone who had her whole life taken as a fee for keeping you alive?

"You don't pay her," Sera said. "You build the world where the next one doesn't have to. She'll check your work. Trust me. She checks everyone's work."

In the lowlands, the villages turned out to watch him pass. He had forbidden kneeling—had sent heralds ahead with the instruction—but they lined the roads anyway, silent, holding salt in open palms. An old custom, Ivenna had explained: salt offered to a traveler meant *may your road be honest.* They offered it to their king, who had dined on their faith for thirty years, and he rode through that silent corridor of open hands with his jaw tight and his eyes wet and did not look away from a single one of them.

Looking away was the old habit. The god's habit. He was done with it.

* * *

The capital received him with a wariness he found bracing. No cheering. No banners. Just the city going about its business while its king rode through it plainly, and one small girl on a corner who pointed and said, loudly, "Mama, that's the king who was eaten," and was hushed, and shouldn't have been, because she was right.

Back at the palace, the work stacked up like winter wood. The Hall of Accounts rising. The refounded Taster's Office—Mara had agreed to lead it, on terms, and the terms alone filled a document Sera described as "the most quietly radical text in the kingdom's law"—with its first apprentices already arriving: sharp-eyed border children, mostly, children who knew hunger from the inside, because Mara insisted that a taster who had never been hungry would miss half of what mattered.

And the letters. Every morning, a tray of them. Some were curses, and he read those aloud to himself in full, because the writers had earned an audience even if it was only him. Some were petitions, and those went straight to the Hall of Accounts. And some—more of them every week—were stranger. Letters from priests in distant parishes reporting that the old gods were answering again, carefully, sparingly, through the sealed salt. A blind woman in Millbrook who could see her garden at dusk. A blighted orchard in the Vale that had fruited overnight, modestly, just enough. Grace rationed like bread in a hard winter, witnessed and written down.

He read those letters longest. Because in each one he could hear the question underneath, the question the whole kingdom was learning to ask: *how much is safe to want?*

On the last evening of autumn, Sera came to his study with a folder under her arm and an expression he had learned to dread, because it preceded either catastrophe or confession, and lately it had usually been both.

"The reckoning files," she said, setting the folder down. "Mine. The reports I wrote that steered worshippers toward the shrines. The ones I burned copies of and kept originals of, because I don't destroy evidence even when it hangs me." She sat across from him, spine straight, hands flat on the folder. "The court will want this handled quietly. Bury the file, censure me privately, move on. That's the traditional remedy."

"And you're bringing it to me instead."

"I'm bringing it to you because you knelt on flagstones and mended a roof, and I have decided—provisionally, pending further review—that this administration might actually survive the truth." She pushed the folder across the desk. "But understand what you're holding, my king. What's in here touches the queen's family, three sitting councillors, and the machinery of the spycraft itself. Open it, and the reckoning stops being yours alone. It becomes everyone's."

Aldric looked at the folder. Thirty years of shame had taught him the exact weight of a thing he did not want to read.

He opened it.
