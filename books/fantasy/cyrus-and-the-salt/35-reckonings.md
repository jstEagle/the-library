## 35. Reckonings

The archives smelled of ash by morning, and Sera Vane had not slept.

She sat at the long table in what had been her office for eleven years—the one with the good locks and the better view of the courtyard—and watched the last of the files burn in the iron brazier she'd had carried up from the guardroom. The paper caught quickly. Old paper does. It was dry as bone down here, dry as the men who wrote it.

*House Vellin, grain ledgers, year of the Long Frost.* The entries showed a surplus that never existed, routed through three ghost merchants to villages that would starve on schedule and then pray on schedule, because starving people pray with their whole chests. She'd signed off on the routing herself. Her seal was still visible where the ink hadn't curled yet—a small neat *V*, like a bird's footprint in snow.

She fed it to the fire.

There were two hundred and fourteen files in the cabinet behind her. She knew because she'd counted them twice last night, once out of habit and once out of something that didn't have a professional name. Each file was a road map: which village prayed hardest after which engineered hunger, which shrine was left unburned because its priest could be turned, which family's grief was noted—*noted*, in her own office's shorthand—as *high-yield*. The god hadn't found its worshippers. Sera's predecessors had herded them, generation after generation, and Sera had inherited the flock book and kept it balanced without ever asking what the columns meant.

No. That was a lie she'd told herself for three days now, and she was done telling it. She had asked. Eighteen months ago, when the prayers started landing, she'd pulled the oldest grain ledgers and seen the pattern and felt the cold walk up her spine. And then she had closed the drawer, because the pattern pointed upward, toward the crown, and Sera Vane did not point things upward. That was the job. You pointed down and outward, at smugglers and spies and inconvenient priests, and you never once looked at the hand that fed you.

She looked at it now. It was all there was to look at.

* * *

King Aldric received the court on the fourth day after the unbinding, in the great hall, under his own banner, and he did not sit on the throne.

He stood beside it instead, one hand resting on the armrest like a man steadying himself against a horse that had thrown him, and he told them everything.

Sera watched from her usual place along the wall, half in shadow, cataloguing the room the way she always did—which lords stood rigid, which wept openly, which ones' eyes flicked toward the doors. But for the first time in eleven years she was cataloguing herself too, and the finding was ugly: she was waiting to see if he'd name her.

He named everyone else.

He told them about the covenant, in plain words, no astrology, no euphemism: a famine-god bound into the crown, wearing every king since the founding, fed on worship that the crown itself had manufactured through hunger, quarantine, and quiet murder. He told them his daughter's name—he had one, a girl dead thirty years, and Sera saw three old lords go gray when they realized they'd forgotten her too—and he said, "I cannot tell you what I remember and what was put in me to remember. I know that I signed orders. Whether my hand was mine when they were signed, I will spend the rest of my life determining."

Then he knelt. On stone, before a thousand witnesses, the king of the realm went down on both knees and pressed his forehead to the floor, and stayed there long enough that the whispering died and the fear began, and then rose and said, "Begin restitution with the mountain shrines. Everything they were starved of, returned with interest. Start today."

Afterward, in the antechamber, Isolde found Sera by the window.

The queen looked like a woman who had been scrubbed. That was Sera's first thought—not broken, not relieved, but scoured, raw-pink, as though the complicity had been a skin she'd finally managed to peel off and now everything underneath was exposed to air. She wore no jewels. Sera couldn't remember ever seeing her without jewels.

"You're burning your files," Isolde said. No greeting. Queens don't greet spymasters; they summon or they avoid.

"Two hundred and fourteen," Sera said. "I'm keeping the list of names, though. The consumed. Someone has to read those aloud someday."

Isolde's jaw tightened. "My house brokered the original covenant. Did you know that?"

"I suspected."

"My grandmother signed it. My mother renewed it. I—" The queen stopped. Looked out the window at the courtyard, where workmen were already loading wagons with grain for Thornfell. "I told myself I was keeping him docile. That my prayers were a leash, not a meal. Twenty years of telling myself that." She turned back, and her eyes were dry, which somehow was worse than tears. "I'm going to the shrines."

Sera said nothing.

"The families are asking for reckonings. Aldric can offer restitution. I can't offer anything they'd take from his hands anyway—they blame the crown, and they're right to. So I'll give myself to the places I helped starve. Let Ivenna decide what I'm worth. Let the women whose shrines went dark while I burned candles in a gilded chapel look at me every day." A pause. "It isn't penance. Don't call it that. Penance implies an ending."

"What do you want me to say?" Sera asked. It wasn't deflection. She genuinely wanted to know. Eleven years of saying exactly the right thing to exactly the right person, and she found she had nothing left in the inventory.

"I want you to keep the records this time," Isolde said. "All of them. Even the ones that shame us. Especially those. My family's mistake wasn't the covenant—it was believing that a thing hidden is a thing controlled." She almost smiled. It didn't survive. "You're burning yours. Good. Burn them, and then build something that doesn't need secrets to function. If such a thing can exist."

She left. Sera went back upstairs and burned forty more files, and kept the list of names, and did not sleep again.

* * *

The trouble with rebuilding an intelligence service, Sera discovered, was that she only knew how to build the old kind.

She tried drafting new protocols and produced, by candlelight, documents that were her old documents wearing cleaner clothes. *Surveillance rotation for high-risk persons.* *Informant incentive structures.* *Compartmentalization doctrine.* She read them over at dawn and heard her predecessor's voice in every line, and his predecessor's under his, all the way back to whoever first decided that a kingdom survived by knowing everything its people did—and telling them nothing.

She burned the drafts too. The brazier was getting a workout.

On the seventh day she walked down to the taster's office.

It had been refounded already—that was Mara Lockridge's doing, done with terrifying speed—but the room itself was the same room Sera remembered from her early years: low-ceilinged, smelling of charcoal and herbs, a table scarred by decades of small cups. Mara was there with a girl Sera didn't know, sharp-faced, border-born by the look of her, who watched Sera enter with an assessment so frank it bordered on insult. Good instincts. Sera made a note to have someone watch her.

"I'm not here about the girl," Sera said.

"No," Mara said. "You're here because you've rewritten your charter six times and burned it each time, and you want to know how I rewrote mine."

Sera blinked. "How do you—"

"Because I did the same thing." Mara pushed a cup across the table—not wine, just water, but Sera watched the girl's eyes track the gesture and understood that even water moved differently in this room. "Sit down. Here's what I learned. You can't rebuild a thing by reversing it. Reverse a knife and you still have a knife. You have to change what it's *for*."

"And what is your office for?"

"Witnessing." Mara said it simply, the way you'd say a word you'd fought to earn. "We taste. We question. We testify—in the open, before the court, under our own names. The old tasters were invisible so the poison could hide in them. Mine are visible so nothing can. Your office, Sera—it shouldn't know everything and tell nothing. It should know what threatens people and tell *them*. Publish the threats. Let the villages see the maps. A secret that protects someone is a tool. A secret that protects the palace—" she shrugged— "we've both seen what grows in that dark."

"It'll get my people killed," Sera said. "Openness gets sources killed."

"Some of them, yes." Mara didn't flinch from it. "But the old way got *kingdoms* eaten, and nobody even knew until the moon counted wrong. Choose your losses honestly, for once."

Sera sat with that the whole walk back. Choose your losses honestly. It was, she realized, the entire discipline of intelligence reduced to one sentence, and no one had ever said it to her because the old service ran on losses no one chose—losses incurred quietly, deniably, filed under *acceptable* by men who never tasted poison or famine or a ravine collapsing on a boy who ran messages.

That night she wrote the new charter in one sitting. Three pages. It began: *This office exists to protect the people of the realm from hidden harm, including harm committed by this office.* And beneath it, the clause that would get her laughed out of every court on the continent: *All operations shall be recorded and open to inspection by any subject of the crown.*

She signed it. Then she took the last file from the cabinet—the oldest one, the founding compact of her service, written by a spymaster whose name history had politely forgotten—and burned it, and let the smoke go out the window toward the mountains.

* * *

The reckoning hearings began in the second week, and they were worse than anyone predicted, which Sera privately considered the best possible outcome.

Aldric had insisted they be public. The hall filled with farmers and shrine-keepers and widows who'd traveled weeks on bad roads to stand in front of the throne and say what the quiet years had taken. Sera attended every session. She sat where she always sat, along the wall, and she listened to her own work read back to her—the grain reroutings, the disappeared priests, the informant networks that had reported on prayer meetings—and she did not defend herself, and she did not resign.

Resignation, she'd decided, was the coward's version of accountability. You resigned and the machine continued with your face painted on someone else. She would stay and run the thing she'd helped break, under rules that let everyone watch her do it, and some mornings she thought that was integrity and other mornings she suspected it was just the only punishment she could devise that fit. Both could be true. Mara Lockridge had taught her that much: hold two truths and keep tasting anyway.

On the ninth day of hearings, a woman from the Thornfell valleys stood up—gray-haired, hands like tree roots—and asked not for restitution but a question: *"Who told you where to send the hunger?"*

And the hall went silent, because everyone knew the answer was the crown, but the woman was looking directly at Sera.

Sera came off the wall. Walked to the center of the floor. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to manage this, to deflect, to give the calibrated non-answer that had kept her alive through three reigns. Instead she said:

"My office did. We mapped which villages would starve and which would pray, and we called it stability, and I read the reports for eleven years without asking what fed on them. I can't return what was taken. I can only make sure the machinery that took it is dismantled in front of you, piece by piece, while you watch." She paused. "My name is Sera Vane. It's in every file I haven't burned. Check my work."

The woman studied her for a long moment. Then she said, "The boy from the ravine. Tam's mother wants to know why."

And Sera—who could answer every question about supply chains, about doctrine, about the architecture of complicity—took a breath and gave the only honest answer left: "I don't know why. There isn't a why that fits in a sentence. Come to the archive. Read everything. That's all the why there is, and it should have been public thirty years ago."

The woman nodded slowly, as if filing the answer away for her neighbors, and sat down.

That evening, a message reached Sera's desk. Not official. A scrap of paper in a hand she was learning to recognize—Mara's, terse as a ledger line:

*Hearings going better than expected. Isolde left this morning for the shrines. Ivenna writes that the salt is holding. One more thing: the apprentice found something in the founder's papers we missed. Come to the office tomorrow. Bring nothing.*

Sera read it twice. *Bring nothing.* From anyone else, paranoia. From a former food-taster who'd spent nine years surviving a court, it was simply good practice—and the fact that it made her feel safer rather than insulted told her how far she'd already traveled from the woman who used to trust nothing except her own sealed reports.

She looked at the brazier. Cold now, ash-cold, two hundred and fourteen files gone to smoke, one list of names remaining. Institutions learning to blush, Mara had called it once, or would have; Sera was starting to understand that the blushing never stopped. You just built the office so the blush was part of its circulation, blood coming to the surface where everyone could see whether the body was healthy.

Tomorrow, then. Something the founder's papers had missed—something Cyrus had hidden deep enough that it took a sharp-tongued border girl to find it.

Sera banked the ash, locked the empty cabinet—for the first time in its existence, locking nothing—and went out into the corridor carrying, for once, no secrets at all except the one waiting for her in the morning.
