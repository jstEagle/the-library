## 36. The Taster's Office, Refounded

The summons came on a morning of ordinary rain, which felt like a small miracle in itself. For a year the palace had produced nothing but extraordinary weather—omens, portents, moons behaving badly—and here at last was rain that meant nothing except that the gutters needed clearing.

Mara walked to the king's study with her hands empty for the first time in nine years. No tasting spoon. No slate for reporting. No little vials of antidotes riding against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She had resigned her post twice now: once in writing before the thirteenth turn, and once more after, when she'd come down from the shrine hollow-eyed and thirteen pounds lighter and unable to say what she was anymore.

Aldric had refused both resignations. He had not, however, asked her back.

She knocked. He said "enter" in a voice that was still learning its own weight, still careful around the edges where the god had worn it thin. He looked better than he had any right to. The restoration had put flesh back on him slowly, like frost melting off a window from the inside out, and what remained was a man in his middle years with a taster's caution in his eyes—which was, Mara thought, exactly the right look for a king who remembered what had been drinking through his mouth for thirty years.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Thank you, Majesty."

"Sit down, Lockridge. I have a proposition, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to do it anyway, because you're the only person in this kingdom who understands why it matters."

She sat. Rain ticked against the leaded glass. On his desk lay a book she recognized—the archives volume Sera had carried out of the sealed stacks, the one with Cyrus's hand in the margins—and beside it a single dish of consecrated salt, covered with a linen cloth, the way Ivenna's people covered anything sacred or dangerous.

"The Taster's Office," he said. "Tell me what it was."

"A cage," Mara said. "A very well-built one. We were fed poison until we couldn't be fed anything else, and then we were fed the kingdom's food and told to swallow our reports whole. We weren't witnesses. We were instruments. Instruments don't ask questions. They don't testify. They taste and they die quietly if they're wrong." She paused. "It worked, though. Thirty years. Cyrus bought you thirty years with that cage."

"And it nearly cost me everything else." Aldric turned the archive book toward her. "I've read every word. Do you know what struck me? Not the ritual. Not the salt. This." His finger rested on a margin note in faded brown ink, the founder-taster's cramped hand: *The office must outlive the officeholder. Write down everything. A secret kept by one person dies with that person.*

"He knew," Mara said quietly. "Cyrus knew the seal would need keeping long after he was gone. And instead they made his office into a pantry."

"They made it into a pantry because a pantry asks no questions." Aldric leaned back. "So I'm going to unmake it. Refound, chartered under the crown but answerable to the shrines as well—Ivenna has already agreed to co-sign the charter, which frightened half my council into silence, which I confess I enjoyed. A new order of tasters." He watched her. "Not immune servants. Witnesses. Trained to taste, yes—but trained also to question, to investigate, to testify before the court and before the gods' own keepers. If someone poisons my wine again, I want a taster who can tell me *who*, not merely *that*. If something wearing a friendly face starts answering prayers again, I want an office that will say so out loud while it can still be heard."

Mara looked at the salt dish. Under the linen, faintly, she could feel it—the old pressure behind her sternum, the ghost of a hunger she'd carried for thirteen days and poured out of herself like dirty water. It never fully left. Ivenna said that was normal. Ivenna said, with her flat mountain pragmatism, that a vessel remembers the shape of what it held, and that remembering was useful, and also that Mara should stop making that face when the subject came up.

"You want me to build it," Mara said.

"I want you to *be* it. First Taster Witness of the new office. You'll write the training yourself. You'll choose your own people. You'll report to me, to Sera, and to the shrine-keepers, in that order of inconvenience." He almost smiled. "And you'll refuse, of course. Go ahead. Get it over with."

"I was going to say no."

"I know."

"Because I spent nine years being property, Majesty. I'm not eager to sign a charter that makes me property with a fancier title."

"That," Aldric said, "is precisely the argument I intend to put in your mouth when I present the charter to the court. Word for word, if you'll allow it. Let them hear what the old office was. Let them squirm." He slid a blank sheet of paper across the desk, and an inkpot, and a pen. "You're not property, Lockridge. You're the only person alive who knows both sides of this work—the swallowing and the surviving. So write me the thing Cyrus should have written. Not a recipe for enduring poison. A craft. Start today."

*

She started today, and it was harder than breaking a god.

The problem, she discovered within the first hour, was that everything she knew about tasting had been built backwards. Her own training—what little of it had been training rather than ordeal—had begun with dosing. A starving border girl, brought inside the walls, fed carefully calibrated poisons in her gruel until her body learned to treat arsenic like weather. Then the tasting. Then the silence. The order of operations had been: break the vessel, fill the vessel, use the vessel.

She wrote that sentence at the top of a fresh page, stared at it, and crossed it out so hard the pen tore the paper.

The new order would begin differently. She sat in the old Taster's Office—a narrow room off the kitchens that still smelled of copper and old fear, its walls hung with the ledgers of dead tasters, columns of meals and symptoms and nothing ever written about the tasters themselves, not one name, not one life between those covers—and she began tearing it apart. Literally. Sera sent two clerks at her request, and by evening they had stripped the room to the stone and carried out forty years of silent ledgers to be burned.

"What goes in their place?" one of the clerks asked, a nervous young man named Pell who flinched whenever she picked up anything sharper than a pen.

"Chairs," Mara said. "A table big enough for six. Windows that open. And a shelf for books we're allowed to read." She tapped the wall where the ledgers had hung. "The first thing a taster learns here will be her own name. Out loud, in front of the others, every morning. You'd be amazed what that does to a person who's been taught she's furniture."

Pell blinked. "Is that... part of the training?"

"It's the foundation of it. Everything else is built on top." She pulled fresh paper toward her and began, at last, to write—not the recipe, not yet, but the charter principles, the bones of the craft. Her pen moved faster than it had moved in years.

*I. A taster is a witness first. The tongue is evidence; it is not the verdict.*

*II. No taster shall be dosed without consent freely given and fully informed. Immunity is earned by choice or not at all. An office built on broken bodies deserves what happened to it.*

*III. Every taster keeps two records: what was tasted, and what was wondered. The wondering is not idle. The wondering is the work.*

*IV. A taster may refuse a duty that requires her silence. Refusal is not treason. A court that punishes refusal has already been poisoned by something worse than hellebore.*

*V. When a taster finds rot, she names it aloud, in daylight, before witnesses, however high it sits. We have seen what grows in the dark places of a court that tastes and says nothing.*

*VI. The office answers to the crown, the spymaster, and the shrines, and to none of them alone.*

She stopped there, pen hovering, because principle seven was forming itself underneath the others, rising like a bubble in black water, and she almost laughed at where it came from.

*VII. No taster shall belong to nothing. The enemy of this office once required a person to be utterly alone in order to hold it. We are done doing its work for it. Every taster will have people—colleagues, confidants, someone who knows where she sleeps and what she fears. Solitude is a tool, used deliberately, briefly, and never as a home.*

Thirteen days of nothing, she had endured. She would not train children to do it as a way of life.

*

Aldric read the principles that evening and did something she didn't expect: he argued.

"Not the content," he said, pacing the length of the refounded office—it already looked different with the table and six chairs and the open window letting in kitchen-smells and rain-washed air. "The content is better than anything my council will produce in a decade. But principle five, Lockridge. *Names rot aloud.* You understand you're asking tasters to accuse ministers, generals, possibly queens. The old office survived because it was toothless. Yours won't survive its first honest report unless—" He stopped. Turned. "Unless it has teeth. Unless refusing to hear a taster carries a cost."

"So give it one. Charter clause: a ruler who silences a witness forfeits the office's protection. We stop tasting. Publicly. Let him eat his own cooking."

The king stared at her, and then he laughed—a rough, startled sound, rusty from disuse, and Mara realized with a small cold shock how rarely she had heard Aldric laugh even before the binding, back when he was just a distant figure at the head of a table whose food she tested. The god hadn't only drunk his faith. It had drunk everything that made him worth following.

"You realize," he said, "that you've just designed an office capable of holding its own king hostage."

"I've designed an office capable of telling its king the truth," Mara said. "Whether that feels like a hostage situation depends entirely on the king."

He sat down heavily in one of the six chairs—hers, technically; the chair at the head of the table, though she'd ordered it identical to the others, because hierarchy had done enough damage in this room. "Thirty years I ate from silver and never once wondered who stood in the shadows counting my heartbeats after each meal. I didn't know your name, Lockridge. Nine years, and I didn't know your name until the thing wearing me started saying it."

"You knew my reports."

"I knew *bitter almond, sire, the quail is lost*." He shook his head. "That wasn't a person to me. That was a bell that rang when the world tried to kill me. I mean to fix that. In the new office, the king takes supper with the tasters once a month. In this room. Where you can see my face while I eat, and I can see yours. If we're asking the court to trust its witnesses, the crown can start by trusting them in return, at a table, like human beings."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the light coming through the open window had gone gold and ordinary.

"That's principle eight," she said, and reached for the pen.

*

The recipe took three weeks.

Cyrus's original—the document Sera had pulled from the archives, the one that had saved Mara's life by explaining the fast—was a masterpiece of survival engineering and a moral catastrophe. Read it plainly and it instructed a human being to amputate everything soft: burn the maps, refuse the farewells, starve the attachments, become a person nothing could grip. It worked. It had worked twice now, separated by a century. But Cyrus had written it as a weapon for one desperate occasion, and the old office had turned it into a personality—a whole institution of people shaped like ammunition.

Mara rewrote it at the big table, with the window open, with Pell and the other clerks reading over her shoulder and objecting freely, which she encouraged, because a text no one argues with is a text no one has actually read.

The fasting rite survived, but changed. Thirteen days of nothing became thirteen days of *chosen* solitude—entered knowingly, witnessed by colleagues, with a door that could be opened from outside if the vessel broke. The severance of ties became temporary and ceremonial, a laying-down rather than a burning-up, with a named day of return. The section on immunity she rebuilt from the ground up: graduated exposure, freely consented, reversible, with antidote research moved from rumor to formal study in partnership with the shrine healers. No more starving girls fed arsenic in their gruel. If someone chose this work, they would walk toward the danger with their eyes open, step by documented step, and they could always walk away.

And at the end of the rewritten recipe, where Cyrus had left nothing but a warning, Mara added a final page:

*The hunger we guard against is not only in the salt. It is in every institution that feeds on devotion—on loyalty given blindly, on service that swallows and never speaks, on love that demands silence. Our office was such an institution for ninety years. We do not atone for that by forgetting it. We atone by remaining the kind of people who notice.*

She signed it. Then, beneath her name, she wrote the words no taster's ledger had ever carried, and felt the room change around her like a held breath released:

*Mara Lockridge, First Witness, born in the Thornfell foothills, daughter of a smuggler's runner, formerly invisible.*

Pell read it twice. "You're allowed to write that?"

"No one's ever tried," Mara said. "That's rather the point."

*

The first candidates arrived with the autumn rains.

Sera vetted them—her defection had remade the spycraft into something watchful on behalf of people rather than against them, mostly, though Mara noticed Sera still enjoyed surveillance a little too much to be entirely reformed—and the shortlist came to eleven names. Orphans of the covenant years. A cook's daughter from the river districts who had watched the emptied villages march past her father's window. Two former shrine novices whose temples had closed when the old gods went quiet the first time. A boy of sixteen who had run messages along the Thornfell passes and knew the mountain roads the way Mara once had.

Mara interviewed them at the big table, all six chairs full, windows open.

She told them the truth. All of it—the cage, the dosing, the god, the thirteen days, the ravine. She told them about the cook kneeling dead with a smile, about the note in flour-dusted handwriting, about the taste of bitter almond and the weight of meals that decided whether strangers lived. She watched their faces the way she had once watched the king's plate, and most of them flinched, and the flinching pleased her, because a person who flinches honestly at horror is a person who will name it later instead of digesting it.

Six stayed. She took them all.

On their first morning, standing in the refounded office with the rain loud on the sill, she had each of them say their own name aloud—full name, place of birth, mother's name if they knew it, and one true thing about themselves that had nothing to do with the crown.

The cook's daughter said her father made the best honey-bread in the city and sang off-key while he worked.

The older novice said she was afraid of deep water and had entered the shrine partly to avoid crossing the river ferries.

The message-boy hesitated longest. Then he said, "My brother died in a rockslide in Thornfell two years ago. Nobody ever found enough of him to bury. I took this posting because I heard the new tasters go into the mountains, and I thought—if anyone could find him, find where he lies, it'd be someone who knows those roads." He lifted his chin, braced for dismissal.

Mara kept her face still, and her hands, and her voice. "You'll learn the roads," she said. "All of them. That's a promise of this office—we finish what grief starts." She wrote his name in the register with steady ink, and only afterward did she allow herself, privately, in the space behind her ribs where a hunger had once lived, to feel the shape of what was coming. Because she knew the ravines of Thornfell. She knew one ravine in particular, and what lay at the bottom of it under two years of scree and rain, and she had been meaning to make that walk since the day she came down from the shrine.

The new class's first field exercise, she decided, would be in the passes. There was a cairn that needed building, and a grave that needed salt, and a lesson about the cost of this work that no ledger could teach.

She closed the register. On its cover, in letters deep enough to read by touch, she had burned the office's new motto—her own words, ratified in the charter that morning, spoken aloud by the king before the assembled court while Isolde's empty seat stood witness to what devotion costs.

*Taste honestly. Testify loudly. Belong fiercely.*

Rain on the window. Six names in the register. Somewhere north, in the high grey country where the shrines kept their lights lit, a road waited for her with a boy's bones beside it.

She sharpened her pen, and began the lesson plans.
