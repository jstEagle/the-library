## 38. Prayers, Answered Carefully

The first prayer Ivenna answered after the unbinding was a shepherd's, and it nearly broke her heart with how small it was.

*Let the river not take my ewes in the spring melt.* That was all. No crown, no kingdom, no grand petition for rain over three provinces or victory over some enemy across the sea. A woman with cracked hands and a smell of lanolin about her, kneeling at the shrine door with her cap in her fists, asking for six animals and the sense to keep them out of the water.

Before the thirteenth moon, that prayer would have gone into the dark and stayed there. Or worse—it would have been heard by the other thing, the hunger in the crown, which would have answered it too well. The ewes would have lived, and so would every lamb for nine seasons, and the shepherd would have told her neighbors, and the neighbors would have prayed harder, and the feast would have grown one mouth larger.

Ivenna took a pinch of the sealed salt from the lead-lined box, held it to the shrine stone, and said the old words her grandmother's grandmother had used before anyone decided the gods were embarrassing.

The river rose that spring exactly to the height of an ordinary bad year. Not one ewe lost. Not one miracle a passing merchant could point at and start a pilgrimage over.

That was the whole craft of it now, she thought, watching the woman walk down the mountain lighter than she'd come up. Enough. Never more than enough.

* * *

The salt had changed when Mara poured the hunger into it. Ivenna had expected—she didn't know what. Corruption, maybe. A black grain in the white, something you could see if you looked closely enough, the way you can see rot in fruit before you bite. Instead the salt had simply become *attentive*. She kept it in the box the founder Cyrus's notes described, lead-lined, cedar-lidded, packed in ash, and when she opened it at dawn each day she had the distinct feeling—not thought, feeling—that it was listening. Like a dog that had once bitten someone and knew it. Like a prisoner who had accepted the cell but still came to the bars.

"It only ever answered prayers," Mara had said, in that cell on day eleven, with her voice scraped down to wire. "It never lied. Remember that when you decide what to do with it."

Ivenna remembered. It was why she did not simply drop the box into the deepest tarn in Thornfell and wash her hands of the whole covenant. The hunger had been made by human need, fed by human faith, shaped like a god because humans needed one so badly they'd built it out of the shape of their own wanting. Drowning it wouldn't kill that need. It would just leave the need standing there, empty-handed, looking for someone new to feed it.

So: rations. Witness. The discipline of enough.

She had rebuilt the shrine's practices from Cyrus's recipe and from memory, and where memory failed, from argument with Sera Vane, who arrived every third week with saddle-sore horses and a courier's case full of reports and opinions she had no rank to have opinions with anymore, having burned half her own archives. Between the two of them they had written something new—a rulebook thin as a hymnal, because thick rules got people killed or corrupted, and thin ones could be memorized by a girl watching sheep.

Three prayers answered per shrine per week, no more. Never anything that would raise the dead, heal the dying outright, fill a granary overnight, or otherwise make a crowd go quiet with awe. Small mercies only: a fever broken a day early, a storm turning aside by a hand's width, a lost child found cold but alive. And every answer witnessed by two named souls who signed the ledger afterward, in ink, with the date, so that in fifty years nobody could stand in a court and claim the salt had spoken to them personally, that they alone were chosen, that the god wanted things from them specifically. That was how it started last time. That was always how it started.

Ivenna wrote the first entry herself, in her crooked hand:

*Spring melt. Six ewes preserved. Witnessed: Ivenna of the First Salt; Halla, widow, of Coldbrook.*

It was the least impressive holy book ever compiled, and she loved it more than any scripture she'd ever been taught.

* * *

Not everyone agreed, of course. Grace rationed tastes like insult to the starving.

A delegation came up from the valley towns in early summer—good people, she reminded herself, farmers and millers and a physician who had watched children die of things the old god used to pluck away like thread from cloth. They stood in the shrine yard with their hats off, which was respectful, and their jaws set, which was trouble.

"You have it in a box," said the physician, a grey woman named Doctor Rell. "You have a god in a box and you dole it out like medicine. Three prayers a week. My colleague in Lowmarsh buried four infants last month. Four. And you're telling me the answer is *rationing*."

"The answer," Ivenna said, "is that the last time we let it answer everything, it ate a king from inside his own skull and wore him for thirty years, and when it finally stopped pretending, a thousand people knelt in a courtyard weeping with love they couldn't refuse." She let that sit. "Doctor. How many infants did you bury *before* the thirteenth moon? Before the famine-god started answering? Because I was here. The number wasn't zero. The gods never promised us no graves. Only this thing we built promised that, and look what the promise cost."

Rell's jaw worked. Behind her, a miller shifted his weight, and Ivenna saw the dangerous thing in his face—the thing she'd seen in villages all her life, the hunger for someone to blame for the arbitrariness of loss. Faith's ugly twin.

"Give us the box, then," the miller said. "Let each town keep its own salt. We'll be careful. We'll do it right."

"That is word for word what the court said to the first covenant-makers," Ivenna said, "four hundred years ago. I've read the treaty. They were very sincere." She turned and fetched the ledger, set it on the stone table between them, open to its thirty pages of small mercies. "Read it. Every answer, witnessed, dated, modest. This is what *careful* looks like. It looks like less than you want. If your prayers are being answered in the amount you want, you are being eaten, and you should check who's doing the chewing."

They went down the mountain unconvinced. That was fine. Ivenna hadn't climbed to her position by convincing everyone; she'd climbed it by being right slowly, in front of witnesses.

But that night she opened the salt box at the hour of no moon, and the attention in it felt different. Sharper. Turned toward the valley, toward the miller's town, toward the four graves. Listening for the loophole in her own rules.

She closed the lid and sat with her hand flat on the cedar until dawn.

* * *

Mara Lockridge came to the shrine in autumn, walking openly, which still startled Ivenna every time she saw it. Nine years of invisibility shed like a skin, and underneath was a woman people noticed—a broad-shouldered, plain-spoken woman with a scarred tongue she'd stopped hiding and an apprentice trailing her like a suspicious cat.

The girl was perfect. Sharp-tongued, half-feral, hungry in the way that either ruins a person or forges them. Ivenna had taken one look and thought: *Cyrus.* Whoever the founder had been, before the archives flattened her into a name and a recipe, she must have been exactly this.

"Mara Lockridge," Ivenna said at the door, "you look terrible."

"I slept four hours," Mara said, "in a barn full of apprentices, plural, because apparently one wasn't punishment enough. Tea?"

They drank it in the shrine kitchen, and Mara told her the news in her flat border way: the taster's office refounded under royal writ, twelve candidates, most of them washed out already because Aldric—*the* Aldric, the man who remembered—had insisted the new order taste, question, *and testify*, and testifying turned out to be harder than swallowing poison. Half the noble houses were furious. The other half were frightened. Both halves sent gifts, which Mara refused on principle, because refusing gifts was now doctrine: a taster who belonged to anyone tasted for that someone, and no one else.

"And Isolde?" Ivenna asked.

"At the Lowmarsh shrine. Tending the ledger there. She asked for the posting herself." Mara turned her cup. "First entry she witnessed, she cried for an hour. Second one, she just wrote the date. I think that's what penance actually looks like. Nobody writes poems about it."

Ivenna nodded and led her down to the vault, because Mara hadn't come for tea and they both knew it.

The salt box sat where it always sat, on the stone shelf, in its ash bed. Mara stood over it for a long moment. Ivenna watched her face do the thing it did—the taster's face, the one that had learned to find truth in bitterness—and then Mara laid two fingers on the cedar lid, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was asleep.

"It knows me," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"What does it feel like? From the outside. Tell me honestly."

Ivenna considered the question the way she considered all questions now: carefully, with witnesses in mind, even when there were none. "Attentive," she said. "Patient. Like something that has learned that patience is the only door left open to it." She paused. "And sometimes, at night, lonely. Which is worse. Loneliness in it sounds reasonable. Everything reasonable it says is a hook, but loneliness is the best-tied one."

Mara took her hand off the lid. "Good," she said. "Keep writing that down. The next keeper will need to know it isn't a monster under there. Monsters are easy. It's the *reasonable* thing in the box that gets people killed."

* * *

Winter brought the hard test, as winters do.

A fever came down the passes—sweating sickness, ordinary, mortal, the kind that ran through valleys every few generations and killed perhaps one soul in twenty, mostly the old and the small. By midwinter the valley towns were begging. Not praying yet, properly—begging the shrines to *do something*, which amounted to the same thing with better manners.

Sera Vane arrived through snow with the weekly reports and found Ivenna at the ledger, quill dry, having written nothing for nine days because she had answered nothing for nine days, because the rules said three a week and the fever said ten thousand prayers, and the gap between those numbers was where a keeper's spine got tested.

"You could bend it," Sera said. She'd shed her spymaster's certainty somewhere over the past year, and what remained was blunter and better. "Nobody would know. One mass mercy. Break the fever a week early across three towns. Save forty lives, maybe sixty. Who signs a ledger against that?"

"Every person who then decides the salt loves their town best," Ivenna said. "Every priest who starts wondering why we're hoarding a god. Every farmer who prays bigger next year because it worked this year." She rubbed her eyes. "You know the worst part, Vane? The hunger argued this exact case with Mara in that cell. Give them back their dead. Undo the grief. It was reasonable. It was *right*, by every arithmetic except the one that counts what happens after. That's the trap. Mercy is its favorite bait, because mercy multiplies."

"So we do nothing."

"We do the small things. We break the fever in Coldbrook, where the ledger shows the prayer was asked first and hardest, and we turn the storm that would have carried it east into the sea. We save the ones the rules allow, and we bury the rest, and we hold the hands of whoever needs holding, and we write down every grave so that in fifty years nobody can say the salt abandoned them—only that the world is the world." She looked up. "Faith is a meal shared, Vane. Not a feast devoured. I didn't write that. Mara did, in the margin of the founder's notes, in ink, where the next hundred years will read it."

Sera was quiet a long time. Then she pulled off her gloves, sat down at the table, and said, "Then I'll witness. Two signatures, yes? Let's see what your god does when it's watched."

What the god did, when it was watched, was give exactly enough. The Coldbrook fever broke four days early—four days, witnessed, dated, entered in the ledger in two hands. The eastern storm bent seaward by a width two fishermen swore to and a magistrate confirmed. Forty-one lives, give or take, saved by the smallest permissible mercies, and in the spring the towns did not build the hunger a temple.

They built the shrine a granary instead. Unasked. Ordinary. For keeping food in.

Ivenna wept over that granary more than she had wept over any miracle, and she never told anyone why: because it was the first offering in four centuries that fed people instead of feeding on them.

* * *

On the eve of the fourteenth moon's turning, she climbed to the high ledge above the shrine where the first covenant had been cut into living rock, four hundred years of carved names and terms, most of them lies dressed in legal language. She brought the season's ledger and read it aloud to the mountain, all ninety-one entries, every small mercy and every honest burial, because the old rites required testimony and she had decided the mountain counted as a witness.

When she finished, the wind moved through the pass, and the sealed salt, far below in its box, attended.

And it spoke—not aloud, nothing so crude, but in the way the ledger's margins sometimes showed a word she hadn't written, faint as breath on glass, legible only if you already suspected it: a single line, patient, almost humble.

*Enough?*

Asking. Checking the boundary, the way it checked every dusk. Not pleading. Not yet bargaining. Just the oldest question a hungry thing knows how to ask, asked with four hundred years of appetite behind it and thirteen days of starvation in front of it.

Ivenna closed the ledger. Below her, torches were coming up the road—Mara's party, back early from the low country, and Mara was not walking like a woman carrying good news. Behind her rode strangers: a boy in ravine-mud boots, a woman wearing the cook's apron, faces Ivenna knew from cairns and graves, walking upright and asking questions with their eyes.

The wind came again off the high snow, and the salt-box question hung in it, unanswered, patient as winter.

*Enough?*

"Not yet," Ivenna said to the mountain, and started down toward the torchlight to learn what the dead were doing out of their graves.
