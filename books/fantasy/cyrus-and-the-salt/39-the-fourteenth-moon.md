## 39. The Fourteenth Moon

The road out of Thornfell had never once, in nine years of walking it, felt like this.

Mara stopped where the path bent around the shoulder of the mountain and let herself feel it: no glance over her shoulder, no counting the seconds until she needed to be a shadow again. The morning was cold enough to bite, clean as rinsed glass, and she was walking it in daylight, on the open road, wearing her own face.

She kept waiting for the old reflexes to kick in—the flattening of her shoulders, the practiced dullness that made guardsmen's eyes slide off her like rain off slate. They didn't come. Her body had spent a decade learning to disappear, and now it seemed to have forgotten how, or decided to forget, which amounted to the same thing.

A mule train came up behind her, salt sacks and grain, the driver whistling something tuneless. She stepped aside to let him pass, expecting nothing, and he tipped his cap.

"Morning, lady."

"Morning," she said, and he was gone up the road, and she stood there a moment longer than she should have, absurdly moved by two words from a stranger.

*Lady.* Not *you there*. Not the flat non-greeting reserved for servants and ghosts. He'd looked at her. Seen a person. Filed her away under *lady* and whistled on.

She walked on.

* * *

The first shrine sat in a fold of the hills above the river road, small as an overturned boat, its stone walls furred with moss. Nine years ago she'd passed it forty times without stopping—shrines were doors you didn't knock on when your business was smuggling grain past excise men. Now smoke rose thin and gray from its little chimney, and someone had planted turnips along the south wall in neat, hopeful rows.

Ivenna had sent word ahead through the shrine network—Mara still wasn't sure how the messages traveled so fast; probably the same way prayers did—and so she wasn't surprised when the keeper came out to meet her. A broad woman with sunburned forearms and flour to the elbows, wiping her hands on an apron already more flour than cloth.

"Mara Lockridge." Not a question. The woman said the name the way you'd set a precious thing down carefully on a table. "We've been waiting on you since the moon turned."

"I'm not late, then."

"For what?" The cook-keeper smiled, and there was something in the smile that snagged at Mara's chest—a shape of mouth she'd seen across a kitchen for years, laughing at her own terrible jokes. "You're not late for anything. That's rather the point."

Inside the shrine smelled of woodsmoke and rising bread and, underneath, faintly, of consecrated salt—that mineral sweetness Mara could taste in her sleep now, the flavor of the thing she carried sealed in the vaults below the First Shrine, the flavor of the hunger she'd held in her own body for thirteen days and poured out into stone.

They fed her bread and hard cheese and a broth with wild garlic, and the keeper asked questions the way Ivenna had taught her people to ask them—not about the unbinding, not about the thirteen days, but about ordinary things. Did the roads hold? Was the ferry running at Greywater? Had she eaten?

It was the last question that undid her a little. *Had she eaten.* As if she were a granddaughter home from the lowlands instead of a woman who'd swallowed a god's appetite and lived.

"You're staring," the keeper said kindly.

"At the bread."

"It's good bread."

"It's—" Mara stopped. Started again. "No one's cooked for me in a long time. That's all. I'm used to being the one who checks whether it's safe."

"And is it?"

Mara tasted it anyway—old habit, tongue reading the crumb like a page—and found only bread: wheat, water, salt, the ghost of honey, and nothing else. Safe. But she found herself saying, "Thank you for asking," and meaning it in a way that surprised them both.

When she left, the keeper pressed a twist of paper into her hand. Inside: three pinches of shrine salt.

"For the road," the woman said. "And because—" she hesitated, first awkwardness breaking through her ease, "—because we wanted to give you something and didn't know what you'd take."

Mara closed her fist around it. Salt. Of course they'd chosen salt. What else do you give the woman who holds the seal?

"The road will tell me," she said, which wasn't an answer, and the keeper laughed like it was.

* * *

By afternoon she'd climbed into the high passes proper, where the smuggler roads ran—her roads, mapped over nine years in ink and memory, every goat track and collapsed bridge and dry-stone shelter catalogued against the day she'd need to run. She walked them openly now too. It felt strange, like wearing a coat inside out: all the linings showing, every secret seam displayed to the sky.

At the second shrine—a cave-mouth shrine, older than the others, where the First Salt had been mined before anyone alive remembered why—she met a girl.

Not just any girl. A shrine-keeper's daughter, maybe twelve, sitting cross-legged on the flat rock before the cave with a ledger open on her knees, copying out prayers in a careful child's hand. She looked up when Mara's boots crunched the gravel and stared with the frank, total stare of children who haven't yet learned that staring is rude.

"You're her," the girl said.

"I'm someone."

"You're *her*." The girl pointed at the ledger. On its flyleaf, in a grown-up's careful script, someone had written a title: *On the Tasting of What Gods Touch — after Cyrus.* Mara recognized the handwriting. It was her own. "Da says you wrote the book everyone's copying. He says before you there was only one taster who ever mattered and then nobody for thirty years."

"Cyrus mattered plenty," Mara said automatically, and then heard herself, and almost laughed. A month ago she couldn't have said her predecessor's name aloud without her throat closing. Now here it was, passing her lips on a mountainside like a common word.

"What's it like?" the girl asked. "Tasting for gods?"

Mara crouched down to her level. Behind her, the cave breathed its cold mineral breath. Somewhere far below—in the sealed vault beneath the First Shrine, in the salt that held it—the hunger stirred the way a sleeper stirs, turning over, dreaming. She felt it the way you feel weather coming. Distant. Contained.

"It's like tasting anything else," she said. "You pay attention. You tell the truth about what you find. The rest is just nerve."

"Da says you nearly died."

"Da's right."

"Was it worth it?"

And there it was—the question, the one the villages asked in different words, the one Isolde had asked in exile at the shrines with her hollow face slowly learning peace, the one Sera had asked over brandy the night before Mara left the capital, in her dry spymaster's way: *Would you do it again, Lockridge, or are you going to be insufferable about it forever?*

Mara thought of the king kneeling in the market square with his crown in his hands. Of the cook's grave, and the salt she'd left there instead of flowers. Of the boy in the ravine whose name she'd finally spoken aloud last week, standing at the cairn with the wind pulling her voice apart.

"Worth it isn't the right scale," she said. "Nothing that costs that much comes back weighing the same. But I'd do it again. Ask me in ten years and I might lie to you. Today: yes."

The girl considered this with enormous seriousness, then wrote something in the margin of her ledger. Mara would spend years wondering what it was. She never asked.

She slept that night at the cave shrine, on straw the keeper's family laid out, and dreamed—not of famines, not of the hunger whispering in her ribs, but of a kitchen with flour in the air and someone laughing, and woke with wet eyes and no grief attached to them, which was new, and which she decided not to examine too closely.

* * *

The third day brought her to the pass where she'd collapsed the ravine crossing.

She hadn't planned the route this way. Or told herself she hadn't. But the road forked above the high meadows—one branch dropping toward the valley and the long way round, the other skirting the broken crossing where the scree still showed the scar of her work—and her feet chose before her head could vote.

The ravine was quieter now. Snow filled the bottom in winter; in summer the stream ran thin over the rubble. Someone had built a second cairn beside hers—smaller, newer, stones still bright with quarry-fresh edges. Offerings lay on the flat rock beside it: a clay whistle. A strip of dried apple. A child's boot, worn through, placed upright and careful.

Mara stood a long time.

The boy who'd run messages for her—Tam, Tam with the quick grin and the faster feet—had carried her maps, her warnings, her bribes, for two years before the ravine took him. She'd killed him with her own hands, in effect, pulling the keystone that dropped the crossing on Sera's hunters, and two of the three bodies had been strangers. The third hadn't been.

She'd built the cairn in autumn. She'd spoken his name in the spring. And now some family—he must have had family somewhere in these hills, someone who'd known him, missed him, grieved him in their own grammar—had come and added their stones and their offerings and their unbearable little boot.

She knelt and added a pinch of shrine salt to the offerings. Then, because the silence seemed to ask for it, she spoke.

"They named a road after you," she said. "Down in the valley. The mule drivers call the new crossing Tam's Ford. Nobody remembers why. I remember why." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I don't think it works like that. I'm just—here. Where I can be seen doing it. That's the whole prayer. That somebody sees."

Wind moved through the pass. Nothing answered, and for once nothing answering felt like enough.

She got up, wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a child, and walked on—and noticed, some miles later, that the weight in her chest had shifted. Not lighter, exactly. Settled. The way a load sits differently once you stop fighting it and just carry it.

* * *

The fourth shrine was the largest, built into the shoulder of the mountain itself, and by the time she reached it word had outrun her—which meant half the valley knew she was coming, which meant, when she crested the last rise, there were people waiting.

Not a crowd. Worse: a handful. A dozen villagers and keepers and one enormously pregnant shepherd's wife, arranged on the shrine steps with the self-conscious air of people who've rehearsed something.

"We're not going to make speeches," announced the shepherd's wife, who was clearly in charge. "Ivenna said you hate speeches."

"Ivenna," Mara said, "is wise."

"But we did want to say—" The woman faltered, pressed a hand to her belly, rallied. "Before, when the god wore the crown, we prayed because we were afraid not to. Or because we were hungry and it was the only door that opened. And now—" she gestured at the valley, the terraced fields, the shrine smoke, the ordinary astonishing daylight, "—now we pray because we choose to, rationed and witnessed, like the priestess teaches, and it's enough. It's actually enough. And we know why. We know what it cost." She looked at Mara straight on. "So. Thank you. From all of us. There. No speeches."

There was a ragged moment where Mara was quite sure she was going to cry in front of a dozen strangers, and then the shepherd's wife's husband produced a flask and the moment drowned honorably in plum brandy, and Mara drank healths she couldn't quite follow and laughed at jokes in a dialect thick as porridge, and when the baby kicked the shepherd's wife seized Mara's hand and pressed it flat against the swell without asking, because that was apparently a thing you did with people now, touched them without checking whether they'd dissolve.

"A girl," the woman said. "We think. We'll name her when she comes, but her grandmother wants Mara, and I want to know if that's allowed."

Mara looked at her own hand on the warm, kicking evidence of a future that wouldn't be devoured.

"It's allowed," she said. "Ask me again when she's born. I'll answer honestly. It's what I do."

* * *

She summited the high pass on the fifth evening, and there, at the top of the world, the last shrine of the road waited—hers, in the sense that everything up here was hers: the routes, the shelters, the sightlines she'd memorized against the day she'd need to vanish. The shrine itself was tiny, one room, one keeper—an old man with a white braid who'd tended it alone for forty years and who greeted her like weather arriving.

"You're the taster," he said.

"I was."

"You're the taster," he repeated, with the immovability of the very old, and handed her tea.

They drank it watching the light go gold and then rose and then violet over the ranges, and the old man talked about the salt stores, the rationing, the careful witnessed prayers that Ivenna had organized into something between liturgy and accounting. Every petition logged. Every granting observed and measured. Faith as a meal shared, never a feast devoured.

"It holds?" Mara asked. "The seal?"

"It holds." He sipped. "Some nights it knocks. You'll know the nights—the dogs won't settle, the salt sweats in the boxes. We sing to it those nights. Old songs. It quiets." He glanced at her sideways, shrewd despite his years. "It knows your name, though. Down there in the dark. Some nights it says it."

"I know."

"Doesn't frighten you?"

Mara considered. Somewhere far below the mountain, in the salt, the thing she'd carried for thirteen days turned over in its prison and murmured her name like a man murmuring in sleep—and she felt it, faint as a scar aching before rain, and let it pass through her and away.

"No," she said, and found it true. "It's not my fear anymore. It's my job. There's a difference."

The old man nodded as if she'd said something profound, or possibly as if she'd said something obvious; with the very old it was hard to tell.

* * *

The moon rose while they sat there.

Full, fat, unhurried—the fourteenth moon, the first ordinary moon of her adult life. Under the old covenant it would have been the twelfth or thirteenth turn, the count toward permanence, the hourglass running. Now it was just a moon. It did what moons did. It silvered the snowfields and made the world legible and asked nothing of anyone.

Mara watched it come up over Thornfell and realized her hands were loose in her lap. Not clenched. Not hidden. Just resting, palms up, the way hands rest when they've nowhere to hide and have finally stopped wanting to.

Nine years invisible. Nine years of flattened shoulders and sliding eyes and a map under a floorboard and a tongue scarred numb by other people's poisons. Nine years of being the thing that stood between the king and death, unseen, unthanked, unbelonging. And now: a mule driver's greeting. A child copying her words into a ledger. A boot placed upright by a cairn. A name promised to an unborn girl. Smoke from five shrines, rising.

Visible. Named. Greeted at every door.

It should have terrified her. For most of her life it would have. Visibility was exposure; exposure was death; she'd learned that young and paid for the lesson in everything soft. But sitting here with bad tea and a stranger's company and the whole silvered world spread out below her, she found the fear simply wasn't there. She checked thoroughly. She was very good at checking.

Set down. Like a pack at the end of a long walk. Not forgotten—she'd need it again someday, maybe, the wariness, the shadow-walking—but set down tonight, deliberately, on purpose, hers to pick up or leave.

"The moon's ordinary," the old man said, following her gaze.

"Yes."

"That's the miracle, you know. Everyone always wants the extraordinary. The crops overnight, the dead walking, the voice like a landslide." He refilled her cup. "But an ordinary moon, after all that? That takes work. Somebody did that work." His eyes twinkled, insufferably. "Somebody tasted honestly."

Mara laughed—a real laugh, surprising in her own mouth, loud enough to startle a marmot off the rocks—and the sound went ringing down the mountain, and nothing in the dark below flinched, and nothing above reproached her, and the full moon climbed higher, patient and plain and beautifully, ordinarily full.

She raised her cup to it.

"To the fourteenth," she said. "May they all be this boring."

Far below, deep in the salt, something sighed in its sleep and settled, and the night went on being ordinary, and Mara Coldharbour—who had spent nine years learning to be no one—sat on top of the world being someone, drinking tea, entirely, gloriously seen.
