## 40. Cyrus and the Salt

The girl arrived in the third week of autumn, on a mule too big for her, wearing boots that had been resoled twice and a mouth that had never been resoled at all.

Her name was Petra. She was fourteen. She came from the border villages below Thornfell, where the salt roads ran and the shrines had learned to ring their bells again, and she had walked most of the way because her father's mule could carry grain or a daughter but not both. When Mara asked her why she wanted to be a taster—because you always ask, because the asking is the first lesson—the girl looked her dead in the eye and said:

"Because I'm mean enough to spit it back out."

Mara laughed for the first time in longer than she cared to count.

"Wrong answer," she said. "Come inside."

* * *

The Taster's Office, refounded, occupied three rooms above the old kitchens where Bren the cook had prayed to a god whose name no one spoke. Mara had chosen them deliberately. Sera had called it morbid; Ivenna had called it right; Aldric, when asked, had only said, "She should decide what we remember," which was either wisdom or an abdication of a king's duty, depending on how generous you were feeling about kings that year.

The first room was the tasting room: plain table, plain chairs, water, bread, a brazier for warming honey so it would tell its truths more willingly. No silver. Silver flatters food. The second room was the library, where the founder's notes lay open under glass now, Cyrus's cramped hand preserved behind panes that Isolde had paid for out of her exile stipend—a detail Mara found either penance or vanity, and had decided not to decide. The third room was empty, and every apprentice was told why before they were told anything else.

"That room stays empty," Mara said, standing in the doorway with Petra shifting her weight beside her like a bird on a wire. "One day it will hold something dangerous. Until then, emptiness is a discipline. Learn to be comfortable in it."

"What's going to go in there?"

"A god," Mara said, "if anyone ever gets careless."

Petra was quiet for exactly four steps down the corridor. Then: "Which god?"

And Mara thought, *oh, she's going to be insufferable,* and thought also, with a warmth that still startled her some mornings, *good.*

"Cyrus's god," she said. "Sit down. I'll start at the beginning."

* * *

The beginning, as Mara told it, was not a battle or a crown or a mountain. It was a kitchen.

"There was a girl once—you'll hear her called the Founder, the First Taster, Saint Cyrus if the shrines have been drinking—but she started as nothing. Starving border girl. Ate anything, because eating was better than watching someone else eat. The old king's men found her during a famine year, when half the court was convinced the cooks were poisoning them, and they gave her a choice between the gallows and the tasting spoon."

"The gallows," Petra said, "obviously."

"Obviously. So she took the spoon. And here's the part they leave off the shrine plaques: she was good at it in a way that frightened people. Not just brave. Honest. Most tasters learn to swallow and say nothing—that's what the job is, really, swallowing and silence. But this girl tasted everything twice. Once for poison. Once for the truth underneath. She could taste fear in the wine. She could taste a lie in the honey. She kept records no one asked her for, and one night she tasted the king's evening meal and found something in it that wasn't poison at all."

"The god."

"Not yet. Patience." Mara poured the tea—chamomile and a pinch of consecrated salt, the office's small daily sacrament—and watched the girl watch the salt dissolve. Good. She noticed. They always noticed eventually; noticing was the whole trade. "What she found was hunger. An old thing, older than the crown, that had been eating the kingdom's faith a spoonful at a time for a generation. It wore the king like a coat. Every prayer in the realm went into its mouth, and every answered prayer made it stronger, and the thirteenth moon was coming when it would own him outright forever."

"So she killed it."

"She couldn't. That's the first hard lesson, write it on your bones: you cannot kill a thing that people are feeding with their love. You can only change what it eats. Cyrus took the hunger into herself—willingly, knowing what it would cost—and held it thirteen days without praying, hoping, or belonging to anything, and then she poured what was left into the consecrated salt of the First Shrine, and sealed it."

"With a lid that opens," Petra said. "You said. In your letters to the shrines. Everyone reads your letters."

"I know they do. Yes—with a lid that opens. Deliberately. By hand. Not forever, because forever is how you get complacent, and complacency is how gods get out." Mara turned her cup slowly on the table. "Cyrus survived. Barely. She lost her immunity—everything she'd built, all those years of swallowing poison until poison became a companion—it burned away taking the hunger in, and she came out the other side ordinary. Able to be killed by a bad oyster like everyone else. She wrote her notes afterward in a hand that shook. Then she founded this office, trained six apprentices, and died old, in bed, complaining about the weather."

Petra frowned. "Then why does nobody know her name? If she saved everyone."

Mara smiled, and it was the smile she'd been saving for this exact moment since spring.

"Because she asked them to forget. She said a savior's name becomes a prayer, and prayers feed things, and she'd spent thirteen days learning exactly what feeding feels like from the inside. So she wrote herself out of the record. Took her own name off the founding charter. The court needed a story about the office, so they invented one—a harmless little legend, tasters as servants, invisible by design." She tapped the glass case, over Cyrus's shaking script. "I spent nine years being that legend before I learned there was a woman under it. You get to start knowing. Consider that your inheritance."

* * *

They worked through the autumn.

Mara taught her the way Cyrus's notes taught, amended by everything Mara had paid to learn: how bitterness hides in fat, how almond lies sweetest, how consecrated salt whispers to anyone who has ever carried hunger in their ribs. Petra learned to hold wine on her tongue for ten full seconds without flinching. She learned the ledgers—every meal, every guest, every variance, written plainly, signed honestly, because *the record is the testimony and the testimony is the shield.* She learned to report to the council chamber without lowering her eyes, which took three weeks and one memorable afternoon in which she informed Lord Chancellor Dree that his tribute wine tasted, quote, "like a donkey had stood in it," unquote, and the room had gone so quiet you could hear the ink drying.

Aldric, presiding, had pressed his lips together very hard and later told Mara it was the finest moment of his restored reign.

Sera taught the girl spycraft on the side—how to watch a room while tasting a soup, how to read a servant's hands, how to lie with your face so you never have to lie with your reports. Ivenna came down from the high passes twice, smelling of woodsmoke and cold stone, and taught the theology: what the salt holds, why faith must be rationed and witnessed, why every answered prayer now goes through a sealed vessel and two signatures. *Faith is a meal shared,* she'd say, *not a feast devoured,* and Petra would recite it back with the flat sarcasm of the young, and Ivenna would grin like a woman who had waited thirty years for someone worth annoying.

Isolde sent a letter once a month from the mountain shrines where she served her exile. Her handwriting grew steadier each time. In the last one she'd written: *Tell your apprentice that guilt is a poor teacher but an honest one. Make sure she gets both.*

Mara read it twice and put it in the drawer with the others.

* * *

Winter came early off the Thornfells, the way it always did, and on the first properly cold evening Mara found Petra in the empty third room, sitting on the floor in the dark, arms around her knees.

"You're supposed to find the emptiness uncomfortable," Mara said from the doorway. "That's the point."

"I'm not uncomfortable. I'm thinking." The girl didn't turn around. "About the seal. About whether it holds."

Mara came in and sat down against the opposite wall, because the floor was cold and honest and she'd long since stopped needing furniture to feel authoritative. Through the narrow window, the moon was rising over the mountains—full, ordinary, the fourteenth moon come and gone and another after that, and the sky entirely unbothered by any of it.

"Tell me the argument," Mara said. "Both sides."

Petra was quiet a moment, marshaling. She did that—gathered her words like stones before throwing them.

"One side says: the hunger is in the salt, the salt is guarded, the rites are sound, and the kingdom prays carefully now instead of greedily. Fine. Other side says—" she hesitated, then committed— "other side says the god wasn't wrong. When it talked to you. In the cell. Day nine, you told Ivenna. It said it only ever answered prayers. It said humans built it. People *wanted* it. Villagers walked up that mountain smiling, Mara. Willingly. What happens the next time there's a famine, or a plague, or a war, and somebody desperate decides a lid that opens is an invitation?"

Mara let the silence sit. This was the question. She'd known it was coming for months, and she'd decided long ago she wouldn't insult the girl with a comfortable answer.

"It might happen," she said. "Probably will, someday. Desperation is patient and memory is short. Someone will crack the seal, or beg Ivenna's successor to, or find a famine-god seed somewhere none of us ever looked. That's the honest answer."

"Then why seal it at all? Why not kill it, or—"

"Because the alternative isn't a world without hunger-gods. The alternative is a world where nobody admits they're hungry. That's how the last one got so big—thirty years of a whole kingdom pretending its devotion was free." Mara pulled the small pouch from her belt, the one she carried everywhere now, and loosened the drawstring. Inside: coarse grey-white crystals, consecrated at the First Shrine, faintly whispering even now if you held them to your tongue and listened. "Cyrus understood something it took me thirteen days of nothing to understand. You can't starve need out of people. Need is the oldest ingredient there is. All you can do is make sure it gets measured. Tasted. Witnessed. Poured into vessels that can be opened *on purpose*, by someone who knows the cost, instead of leaking out wherever it wants."

She held the pouch out. "Taste."

Petra took a single crystal, set it on her tongue, and went still. Mara watched her listen—the slight widening of the eyes, the breath held, the long slow exhale as the whisper faded.

"It sounds sad," Petra said quietly. "Not evil. Just—sad. Like something waiting for dinner that never comes."

"Yes. Hold onto that. The day you stop hearing the sadness is the day you've stopped tasting honestly, and the day that happens, hand in your badge, because you're dangerous." Mara took the crystal back and closed the pouch. "That's the whole craft, girl. Everything else—poisons, ledgers, spycraft—is technique. The craft is this: you taste what's actually there, including the parts that argue with you. Especially those."

Petra sat with it. Outside, the moon climbed clear of the peaks, white and round and utterly ordinary, and somewhere below in the city a bell rang the hour, and somewhere above on the mountain the shrines rang theirs in answer, two conversations keeping polite distance across the dark.

"Mara," Petra said finally. "Will the seal hold?"

And Mara looked at her—at the sharp tongue and the steady eyes and the resoled boots, at the fourth generation of a lineage that had begun with a starving girl choosing the spoon over the gallows—and gave the only true answer there had ever been.

"As long as someone keeps tasting honestly."

Petra considered this with the gravity of the very young confronted with their first real responsibility. Then she nodded once, businesslike, and said:

"Well. That's convenient. Because I'm very honest and extremely difficult to fool."

"I know," Mara said, getting up, joints announcing themselves the way they did now, in the voice of a body that had been used thoroughly and mostly forgiven. "It's why I picked you."

They climbed the stairs together. Below them the kitchen fires were being banked; above, the moon rode high and harmless; ahead lay thirty years of meals to test, ledgers to keep, a kingdom learning, slowly, imperfectly, to share its faith like bread instead of devouring it like a feast. Somewhere in a drawer lay a letter from a queen growing steadier. Somewhere on a mountain stood a cairn for a boy who ran messages, and a grave with salt instead of flowers, and both were tended.

Mara blew out the lamp.

"As long as someone keeps tasting honestly," she repeated softly, to the dark, to the salt, to the woman whose name the plaques didn't carry and whose work went on anyway.

Behind the glass in the next room, the shaking handwriting waited patiently for morning, and the empty room stayed empty, and the seal held.
