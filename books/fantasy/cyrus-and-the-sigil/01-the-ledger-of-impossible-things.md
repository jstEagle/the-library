## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to surviving in the Vellum Guild's counting house, and I wrote all three myself, though I'd never admit that aloud.

One: never be the most interesting thing in the room. Two: never finish your work before the hour bell, because finished people get asked questions, and asked questions lead to answers, and answers, in my experience, are where the trouble starts. Three — and this is the one I keep carved somewhere behind my ribs where no auditor will ever find it — never, under any circumstance, let them hear you talking to something that isn't a person.

I was breaking rule three when Master Hollis cleared his throat behind me.

"—and the river account," he said, "is still showing a surplus."

I closed my mouth. Under the counting-house floor, in the dark between the foundation stones, the guild's oldest resident went on murmuring to itself, a sound like coins being counted by someone very patient and very far away. The sleeping river. They kept it in the cellar, coiled in a stone channel no wider than a grave, dreaming its slow way from wherever rivers dream. It talked in its sleep. Most nights it only recited the names of fish it had known. Tonight it had been saying my name.

*Theo. Theo. Little ledger-keeper. Theo.*

"Surplus," Hollis repeated. He is not a cruel man; he is worse. He is a thorough one. "A sleeping asset does not generate income, Crane. Where is the excess coming from?"

"Interest," I said, keeping my eyes on the page. "Accrued interest on the original purchase. The river was bought forty years ago; the principal has been compounding ever since."

Hollis looked at me for a long moment with the expression of a man doing arithmetic on his own suspicion. Then he nodded, made a small note in his own book — everyone at the Vellum Guild keeps a book; we are an institution that believes nothing exists until it is written down — and moved on down the aisle of desks, his shoes clicking like a metronome set to *unease*.

Under the floorboards, the river chuckled.

*I like him,* it said. *He tastes of copper and old rain.*

"You would," I murmured into my inkwell, so quietly that even I barely heard it.

That is the shape of my life. That is the whole shape of it: a desk, a lamp, a ledger, and a voice in the floor that no one else can hear. Twenty-three years old and I have never once said a true sentence out loud.

Let me tell you what I do, since apparently someone must. The Vellum Guild trades in impossible things. Not metaphorically — literally, itemized, receipted impossible things. Caged griffins. Bottled storms. A sleeping river. The left-hand shadow of a man who sold it to pay a debt (it sits in Vault Six, folded like laundry). A winter that got loose from its own season and had to be caught before it froze a county. We buy them, we sell them, we lease them to collectors and kings, and every coin that moves through any of it passes across this desk, through these hands, through my careful, invisible, lying arithmetic.

I am good at it. That is the joke nobody gets but me: they gave the beast-speaker the books. Because I can't be caught doing what I don't do, and because numbers are the one language in this building that doesn't have a pulse. Numbers don't look at you. Numbers never ask why you flinch when the griffin cages come up for audit.

The griffins came up for audit today.

* * *

They keep them on the fourth floor, which used to be the aviary back when the guild still pretended to be a library. Six of them now — there were nine last spring, but three were sold south to a collector whose name appears in the ledgers as *M. Ashgrave* and nowhere else, which should tell you everything about how honest our client list is. The remaining six live in iron-and-wicker enclosures built to guild specification, which means built to hold a creature designed by spite and aerodynamics, and built badly, because guild specifications are always written by men who have never met the thing they're specifying against.

I went up with the assessor's slate because it was my turn, and because refusing would have been interesting, and rule one.

The griffins were quiet when I came in, which was wrong. Griffins are never quiet. They complain the way storms do — constantly, and with an audience in mind. But as I stepped onto the walkway between the cages, all six of them went silent at once, six pairs of gold eyes tracking me, six sets of talons shifting on their perches, and the air got that pressed-in feeling of held breath.

*I know what you are,* said the eldest, the one they call Half-Crown because of the silver crescent over her left eye. She is old for a captive griffin and wears her years like armor. *The rats told us. The rats tell everyone.*

Rats. Of course. I keep the grain accounts; the rats keep me. There are perhaps two hundred of them living in the walls of this building, and I have spoken to exactly four of them in my entire life, each time swearing it would be the last, each time meaning it. Apparently word travels. Apparently word travels *fast*, through walls, among creatures who cannot testify and therefore cannot betray you — but who also cannot keep anything to themselves, because secrecy is a human invention and beasts have never seen the use of it.

*I don't know what you mean,* I thought at her, as if thinking counts, as if she couldn't smell the truth on me the way I could smell the oiled iron of her cage.

Half-Crown tilted her head. Somewhere below us a cart rattled past, and the youngest griffin — a male, barely fledged, sold to the guild as an egg — pressed himself against the bars toward the sound of open sky.

*You speak,* she said. Not accusing. Just stating, the way you'd note the weather. *And you count our cages and write the numbers down and say nothing.* She roused her feathers, a slow ripple from shoulders to tail. *We wondered when one of you would come who could hear. The last one was your mother.*

My pen was in my hand. My hand was steady. I have spent twenty-three years making sure my hands are steady.

*Your mother,* Half-Crown repeated, watching me not react. *Before the Guild processed her.*

*Processed.* The guild's word. It appears in the archives in tidy columns: *acquired, assessed, processed.* What it means, when the asset in question is a person with a forbidden gift, is something the ledgers do not say, because ledgers record transactions, and whatever happens in the processing rooms is not a transaction. It is a subtraction.

"I'm here for the audit," I said aloud, to no one, in the flat voice I use for inventory. "Condition of enclosures. Feed consumption. Health of stock."

Half-Crown laughed. It is not a sound I recommend hearing. It is the sound of a thing that used to own the sky finding one more reason to despise the ground.

*Count, then, little keeper. Count well.* And then, softer, in the tone of someone laying a card face-down on a table: *We know you hide it. We won't spend it. But someday soon, someone will ask you what you are, and you'll have to decide whether you're a ledger or a life.*

I did the audit. Feed consumption, correct. Enclosure integrity, adequate. Health of stock — and here my pen hovered, because the youngest griffin had a broken primary feather on his left wing, unreported, untreated, and writing it down meant a requisition order meant a visit from the guild's warden meant questions, and leaving it off meant the records stayed clean and the bird stayed hurt.

I wrote it down. Rule of mine, unwritten, unbreakable: the books stay true even when I don't. It's the only honesty I have room for, and I guard it the way other people guard gold.

Then I went back downstairs and balanced accounts for another four hours, and the whole time the river murmured my name, and the whole time I did not answer.

* * *

Here is the part I have never written anywhere, not in the ledgers, not in the diary I keep locked inside a hollowed-out accounting manual (the irony is deliberate), not in the safe-deposit box of my own skull where I store everything true:

I remember my mother's voice.

Not speaking to me — speaking to the sparrows. I was four, maybe five. We lived in the guild's orphan wing, which is where the guild puts the children it acquires, and there was a courtyard with a dry fountain, and my mother used to sit on its rim at dusk and talk to the sparrows like they were colleagues. Not commands. Conversation. The sparrows would land on her wrists and argue with her — birds argue constantly; I had forgotten that until just now, writing it — and she would laugh, and I would laugh, and none of it seemed like anything except a mother being strange and lovely at the end of a long day.

She was gone within the month. The guild told the orphans she had been reassigned. The word they used in the actual file — I found it years later, when I learned which locks open for a patient clerk — was *processed*. I was five years old. I was reassigned too, in the sense that children get reassigned: handed downward, fed, housed, taught letters and figures and the guild catechism (*all things may be owned; ownership orders chaos*), and given a desk at fourteen when the aptitude examiners noted my exceptional head for accounts and my total incuriosity about everything else. Total incuriosity. I cultivated it like a garden. I watered it daily.

What the examiners never noticed — what no one has ever noticed, because noticing would be the end of everything — is that I hear them. All of them. Beasts. Not in words, exactly, though words come; more like meaning arriving fully dressed, wearing whatever voice the creature happens to have. The rats sound like dockworkers. The river sounds like an old woman remembering. The griffins sound like exiles, because they are.

Beast-speakers are culled. That is not a rumor; that is policy, written in the guild charter itself, in the same iron hand as the catechism: *the tongue that speaks with beasts divides the realm, and division is disorder, and disorder is loss.* The realms signed their covenant centuries ago and the covenant requires silence between them — that much I've pieced together from fragments, from things the rats let slip, from the way the oldest ledgers go blank for whole decades as if the pages themselves were afraid. Humans on one side of the line. Beasts on the other. Trade between realms handled only through licensed intermediaries, which is to say, through the guild, which is to say, through theft with paperwork.

And anyone born able to cross that line with a word — anyone like my mother, anyone like me — is a flaw in the covenant. A leak. And leaks get processed.

So: rule three. Never let them hear you talking to something that isn't a person.

I have kept it for eighteen years. Eighteen years of walking past kennels and stables and the storm-vaults with my ears full of voices and my face empty as a ledger line. Eighteen years of answering no one, of humming when the hounds greet me by name, of pretending deafness is a natural condition instead of the hardest work I do. Do you know what it costs, to be silent in two directions at once? To never speak to the creatures who see you clearly, and never speak of them to the humans who don't? There are days I feel like a bridge with both ends dismantled. There are days I feel like nothing at all.

Mostly, though, mostly it is this: quiet dread, worn smooth by repetition, like a stone in a pocket you stop noticing until you put on a different coat.

* * *

The hour bell rang nine, and the counting house emptied around me one desk at a time. Hollis left last, as always, pausing at my table to glance at my columns — clean, complete, boring, perfect — and give me the nod he probably believes is warmth. The lamps were trimmed. The doors were barred. I sat alone with the ledgers and the lamp-flame and, beneath the floor, the river.

*Theo,* it said. It says my name the way tide says shore. *Theo. You wrote the young one's wound into the book. Good. True books are rare. Theo. Something is coming.*

"Something is always coming," I whispered. "It's a trading house."

*No.* The sound of it moved through the floorboards like cold. *Something is coming tonight. It runs. It bleeds. It carries paper. Theo — the paper is afraid.*

Paper isn't afraid. Paper is pulp and sizing and ink. I know paper; I am the guild's leading authority on paper, if authority can be measured in reams. Paper does not feel anything, and neither do I, officially, and tomorrow is audit day for the southern leases, and I have a great deal of work—

But the river had already turned over in its sleep, sinking back toward its dreams of fish, and I was left sitting in the lamplight with my pen uncapped and my heart doing something unauthorized.

I told myself it was nothing. Rivers dream nonsense; everyone knows that. I told myself the tightness in my chest was the season changing, the coal smoke, the long hours. I told myself all of it in my flattest, most incurious voice, and I almost believed it.

Then someone hammered on the salt door.

Three blows, hard, uneven — not the knock of a delivery, not the knock of authority. The knock of a body using its last strength on wood. It came again, weaker, and beneath the counting house the river woke fully for the first time in forty years and said, clear as a bell across water:

*Theo. Open the door.*

I sat there in the lamplight, alone in the Vellum Guild, with every rule I had ever made standing in a row behind me like auditors.

Then I got up, and broke all three.
