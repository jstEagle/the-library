## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

There are three hundred and eleven ways out of the Vellum Guild, and I know every one of them, because knowing the ways out of a place is the first thing you learn when you're not sure you're allowed to stay.

The salt door is my favorite. It's tucked behind the counting house, down a flight of steps that smell of brine and old tallow, and it opens onto the alley where the fish-carts come through at dawn. The door gets its name from the crust of white that blooms along its iron hinges — the guild's founders sealed it with salt and oath-wax so that nothing from the beast-realm could pass through uninvited. A superstition, probably. But then, most of what the guild does is superstition wearing a very good coat.

I was taking the salt door because it was nearly midnight, and I had just finished balancing the ledger for the third quarter's bottled storms, and I wanted to stand in an alley where nobody could ask me whether column nine reconciled with column ten. It did not. Bottled storms never do. You can cage a tempest in glass, but it sulks, and sulking storms shed lightning into their own stoppers, which plays havoc with depreciation.

That was my whole life, in those days: depreciation, reconciliation, and the careful, practiced art of being nobody. Theo Crane, keeper of ledgers. A person shaped like a filing cabinet. Ask anyone.

I had one hand on the salt door when the courier fell out of the dark and broke against me.

* * *

People think being knocked down by a dying man is dramatic. It isn't. It's mostly elbows and the sudden, intimate knowledge of how much a stranger weighs. We went down together onto the wet cobbles, and my first thought — I am not proud of this, but honesty is the whole point of a ledger — was that he'd stained my second-best coat, the grey one, the one I wore precisely so that no one would ever look at me twice.

My second thought arrived a heartbeat later, when I felt the heat coming off him. Not fever-heat. Forge-heat. As if someone had carried him in a closed fist like a coal.

"Up," I said, uselessly, because I am a clerk, and clerks say "up" and "sir" and "if you'll just sign here" to people who cannot hear them. "Sir. You need—"

He got an arm under himself. He was young, or he had been once; the road had sanded that down to something older. His cloak was scorched across the chest in a shape I didn't have a name for yet, though I would soon, and would spend years wishing I didn't. Under the burn, blood. A great deal of blood, more than a body is meant to hold, pooling on the stones with the patience of ink filling a pen.

His eyes found mine. They were doing something eyes shouldn't do — searching, specifically, as if my face were a document and he was checking it against a list.

"You're guild," he rasped. "Vellum. I can smell the wax."

"I keep the accounts," I said, which is what I always say, which is what keeps me alive. "If you need a healer—"

"I need a thief." Something that might have been a laugh moved through him and cost him dearly; I watched the price of it leave in red. "Or a coward. Either will do. Anyone who can *not be caught.*"

Behind us, the salt door stood open on the counting house's warm light, and beyond that, somewhere up through the floors, the guild slept its fat, secure sleep. I should have called for help. That is what a loyal member does. That is what the institution that fed me, clothed me, taught me my letters and my numbers and my silence would have wanted. There is a bell rope by the door for exactly this purpose.

Instead I heard myself say: "Not be caught by whom?"

His hand shot out and closed around my wrist with a grip that had no business surviving inside that body. He pressed something into my palm — folded, stiff-edged, hot. Not warm. *Hot*, the way a stone stays hot long after the fire that held it has gone out.

"The map," he said. "It charts the last dragon-fire. Do you understand? The *ember*. Everything hangs on it, everything, the covenant, both realms—" A cough took him and shook him like a dog shaking a rat. When he surfaced, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, a secret passed across a deathbed. "They're draining it. The masked one. He wears such a kind face."

Somewhere above us, faint through the stone, a floorboard creaked.

"They're coming," he said. "Seconds. Maybe less." His fingers tightened on mine, and his eyes — those searching, checking eyes — went wide and fierce. "You have a hiding face. I can tell. You've spent your whole life learning it. So hide this. Hide it better than you hide yourself."

"I don't know what you—"

"*Hide it.*"

And then, because fate has a taste for bad timing, the alley filled with lantern-light.

* * *

They came around both ends at once, which is guild training; you can always spot enforcers by the way they never leave you one direction. Four of them, grey cloaks, iron badges stamped with the guild's sigil — a quill crossed over a key. Brannic was in front. Brannic, who'd shared orphan-bread with me in the under-hall twenty years ago, who still owed me three coppers from a card game we played the winter we were twelve.

"Theo." Brannic's voice was flat, professional, and underneath the flatness, worried. "Step away from him."

"He collapsed," I said. And then, hearing how thin that sounded: "I was taking the night air. He came out of nowhere."

Brannic's gaze flicked to the blood spreading black between the cobbles. To the scorch-mark on the man's chest. Back to me. I watched him do the arithmetic — I could see him doing it, the little movements behind his eyes, because I'd known him since before either of us had anything worth hiding — and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that whatever I said in the next ten seconds would follow me for the rest of my life.

The map sat in my right fist, pressed against my thigh, radiating heat like a small accusing sun.

"The healer's stairs are closer if we go through the counting house," Brannic said, and gestured two of his men forward. "Get him up."

The courier chose that moment to die.

I want to be precise about it, because precision is all I have. He didn't cry out. He didn't clutch at anyone. His grip simply left my wrist the way a tide leaves a shore — gradually, and then completely — and he sagged sideways against the cobbles with his scorched cloak falling open, and his face went slack in the particular way faces do when the person who was borrowing them has finished with it. His other hand had crawled toward me as he died. Toward the map. Or toward me. I've decided since that it doesn't matter which.

One of the enforcers knelt and pressed two fingers to the man's throat, waited the decent interval, and looked up at Brannic. "Gone."

Nobody spoke. The fish-cart smell drifted through the alley. Somewhere a cat was complaining about something cats find worth complaining about, and I stood there with stolen heat burning a hole in my coat and lied with my entire posture.

Because here is the thing they never put in the oaths: guilt has a weight, and bodies betray weight. My right arm hung wrong. My shoulder angled wrong. Every instinct I owned was screaming *look natural*, and every instinct I owned was making me look like a woman holding a live coal and pretending it's a plum.

"Theo," Brannic said again, softer now. "Did he give you anything?"

And I heard myself say — clear, level, bored, the voice I used for column nine — "He grabbed my wrist. Asked for a healer. Died." A beat. "That's all."

It was the first lie I had ever told the guild. Twenty-six years of careful invisibility, of being furniture, of balancing impossible accounts without once asking where the griffins came from or where the sleeping river dreamed — and the first time the institution that raised me asked me a direct question, I answered it false, smooth as signing my name.

Brannic looked at me for a long moment. Lantern-light moved on his badge. Quill over key. Words over locks.

"That's all," he repeated.

"That's all."

He believed me. I want to tell you he didn't — that some part of him saw straight through to the truth trembling under my skin — because that would be a kinder story. But he believed me, or he chose to, and he turned and started giving orders: search the body, log the effects, notify the Master's office, no word to anyone below the fourth floor. The machinery of the guild engaged around the corpse with terrible smoothness, gears I had spent my life oiling from the inside without ever asking what they ground.

As they lifted the courier, his scorched cloak fell open fully, and I saw the wound beneath it properly for the first time.

It wasn't a blade cut. It wasn't any wound I had a name for. Across his chest, burned into the flesh itself, was a mark — a circle broken by seven lines, each line ending in a small hooked barb. A seal. A sigil. And I knew — the way you know a word in a language you swear you've never learned — that it was a brand of pursuit. Someone had marked him so he could be tracked. Someone had reached across miles and opened him like a letter.

Someone who wore a kind face.

* * *

They let me go back inside at half past one. I signed the incident ledger — *unidentified male, dead on arrival, cause pending examination* — and my signature came out steady, which frightened me more than shaking would have. Steady hands mean practice, and practice means I had been lying longer than I knew.

My room is on the fifth floor, under the eaves, chosen years ago precisely because no one visits the fifth floor. I bolted the door. I sat on my narrow bed with the map in my lap, and only then, only when the bolt was shot and the candle was lit and the eaves pressed close overhead like cupped hands, did I allow myself to unfold it.

It was drawn on something that was not paper. Too thick, too smooth, faintly scaled under my fingertips, with a grain that ran in patterns no tree ever grew. It was scorched along one edge — the same seven-barbed circle as the courier's wound, seared into the corner, the hunter's signature — but the rest of it was intact, and it was beautiful, and it was wrong. Roads that bent where roads don't bend. A coastline I almost recognized, except the sea was drawn inland where no sea is. And at the center, rendered in pigment that seemed to hold light the way a coal holds heat:

A mountain with an open heart. A flame inside it. Small, patient, and — I pressed my palm flat over it and snatched my hand back — *warm.*

*Dragon-fire,* the courier had said. *The last. The covenant. Both realms.*

I am a bookkeeper. I know exactly what I'm qualified to believe in, and it is arithmetic. But I also know what a debt looks like when it comes due, and a dying stranger had paid one into my hands with his last breath, and debts do not care whether the debtor feels qualified.

Below me, through five floors of stone, the guild slept on — the cages, the bottles, the vaults, the masked master in his high rooms with his kind, kind mask. Somewhere in that building was an office where a clerk was already drafting the report of tonight's death. Somewhere was a desk where orders would be written about loose ends. I had seen the machinery work a thousand times. I had simply never been the thing caught in it.

The map pulsed gently under my hands, patient as a heart.

I thought about the bell rope I hadn't pulled. I thought about the lie sitting in my mouth like a coin I couldn't spit out. I thought about a man who had looked at my careful, hidden, invisible face and known in one glance exactly what I was: someone who could hide things. Who had been practicing her whole life.

Outside my window, far off across the rooftops, a hawk cried once — sharp, urgent, almost like a word.

Almost like a warning.

I blew out the candle and sat in the dark, holding the hottest thing I had ever touched, and listened to the guild breathe beneath me, and understood that whatever happened next, I had already chosen. The lie had chosen for me.

The rats would know more. The rats always knew more.

But that was tomorrow's sin.
