## 3. What the Rats Said

I want to be clear about one thing before I tell you what I did: I had rules.

Not guild rules — the guild has rules the way a river has banks, mostly decorative and always negotiable if you're rich enough. I mean *my* rules. Rules I'd built for myself brick by brick over twenty-three years, mortared with caution, roofed with a healthy terror of dying badly. Rule one: never let anyone see you flinch at a bird's song or a dog's growl, because you might hear something in it worth answering. Rule two: never speak back. Rule three, the load-bearing wall of the whole structure: *never, ever talk to the rats.*

Every building has rats, but a counting house that trades in impossible things has rats like nowhere else on earth. Ours were fat on spilled griffin-feed and bottled-storm residue, clever as tax collectors, and they knew things. That was the problem. Knowledge is a debt in the Vellum Guild, and debts get collected.

For eleven years I had walked past them in the cellars and thought, very carefully, about grain prices. For eleven years they had watched me with their little wet ledger-keeper eyes and I had pretended not to notice them noticing me. It was, I think now, the longest courtship of mutual suspicion in the history of either species.

Then a man died holding my hands in the salt alley, and my rules stopped mattering, because rules are for people who expect to have a future.

* * *

The courier's body was gone by dawn, taken by the enforcers with a efficiency that told me Master Cyrus already knew. What Cyrus didn't know — couldn't know — was folded inside my shirt, against my skin, where it felt less like a map and more like a coal: scorched leather, faintly warm, faintly breathing. I'd checked twice in the night to make sure it wasn't actually burning through my ribs. It wasn't. Probably.

I got through the morning the way I got through every morning: by being aggressively boring. I reconciled the third-quarter accounts for the caged storm-petrels (twelve sold, two dead in transit, four escaped — the escapes always went into the same column, *attrition*, as though the birds had simply been worn away like shoe leather). I initialed the delivery manifest for three jars of sleeping-river silt. I drank tea that tasted like boiled regret.

Brannic found me at midday. He didn't sit so much as lower himself onto the stool across from my desk, which groaned its opinion of his armor.

"You look terrible," he said.

"I've been working."

"Since when does working make you look like something the tide rejected?" He leaned forward. "You heard what happened last night. Salt door. A courier bled out forty feet from your window."

"I heard," I said, which was true, and wrote *one (1) crate, moth-eaten, contents disputed* in the ledger, which was also technically true, because the crate was moth-eaten and even the moths disagreed about what was left in it.

"Theo." Brannic kept his voice down. Around us the counting house murmured along, clerks scratching, the great abacus clicking in the corner like a giant nervous insect. "They're saying the courier came from the east roads. They're saying he was carrying something for someone inside the Guild. And they're saying—" He paused. Brannic pauses are expensive; he doesn't spend them often. "—that whoever was supposed to meet him didn't come. Or did come and did something worse."

My pen made a small blot on the page. I looked at it. The blot looked like nothing, like all blots, and I envied it enormously.

"What do they say happened to him?"

"Throat. One cut, clean. But the strange thing—" and here Brannic dropped his voice further, which for a man built like a cathedral door was quite an achievement — "the strange thing is there weren't any footprints around him except his own. Snow was coming down all night. Fresh fall. You walk through fresh snow, you leave tracks, unless you don't weigh anything, or unless you were carried, or unless—"

"Unless nobody touched the ground," I said.

Brannic looked at me. "That's a grim thing to say so quickly."

"It's arithmetic," I said. "Someone walks up to a man in snow without leaving prints, that's not a person. Persons are heavy."

He laughed, because Brannic laughs at grim things; it's how he keeps them from sitting on his chest at night. Then his face settled again, and he said, quietly: "Master Cyrus is asking questions. Quiet ones. Whoever the courier met — whoever *didn't* meet the courier — the master wants them found." He stood, armor settling. "Be careful, Theo. Whatever you saw from your window, whatever you heard—"

"Nothing," I said. "I saw nothing. I slept like the dead."

"Poor choice of words," said Brannic, and left.

* * *

Here is what I hadn't told Brannic, because telling anyone would have meant becoming a different story than the one I'd been living: I hadn't been asleep. I'd been awake at the window when the courier fell, and I had *heard* the moment before it. Not footsteps. Not a voice. Something worse — the absence of sound, a hole shaped exactly like listening, moving up the alley toward the salt door. Beasts go silent when death passes near. Every animal I've ever spoken to agrees on this the way theologians agree on nothing: you can argue about gods, but no sparrow will ever argue about that particular quiet.

And then, after the courier died, in the seconds between his last breath and the enforcers' boots on the cobbles, I had heard something else. Small, many-footed, whispering.

The rats had seen everything.

* * *

It took me until well past dark to break rule three, and I broke it in the worst way, which is to say completely and with no plan whatsoever. I took a stub of candle and a heel of bread down to the cellar where the guild kept its older stock — things too dangerous or too dull to sell, depending on the decade — and I sat down on a barrel among the dust and the pickled impossibilities, and I said, aloud, feeling like an idiot:

"I know you're there."

Silence. The particular silence of small creatures deciding whether you're a threat, a meal, or merely tedious.

"I'm not going to hurt you. I'm going to give you bread. This is, historically, how friendships begin."

A shadow detached itself from behind a jar labeled *WINTER, MILD, 1 PINT* and flowed across the floor toward me, and then another, and then more, until there were perhaps a dozen of them arranged in a loose crescent in front of my boots, whiskers twitching, eyes catching the candlelight like wet ink.

Their leader was enormous, scarred along one flank, missing half an ear. She sat up and looked at me with the flat patience of a creditor.

*I know you,* she said. Her mind-voice was dry and precise, like paper being counted. *Eleven years you've walked past us thinking loudly about barley futures. We had a wager running.*

"A wager."

*Old Whisker said you were deaf. I said no beast-speaker is deaf, we'd smell it. Beast-speakers stink of guilt.* She sniffed. *You reek of it tonight.*

"I need to know what happened in the salt alley last night."

The crescent of rats rippled. Somewhere in the back, a young one made a sound that I can only describe as a rodent swallowing a laugh.

*Bread first,* said the leader. *Grief and gossip both cost. Yours costs double, because you've made us wait eleven years.*

I broke the heel into pieces and distributed it, and the cellar filled with the small industrious sounds of rats eating, which is somehow both comforting and deeply unsettling, like hearing your own heartbeat amplified.

The leader ate daintily, watching me the whole time. When she finished she cleaned her paws, and then she said: *We saw him die.*

"Tell me everything."

*He came down the alley fast. Wrong-fast. Men run fast when they're chasing; he ran fast like something was chewing at his heels, except nothing was. He fell at your door and beat on it and you didn't open—*

"I didn't hear him," I said, and the shame of it still sat in my chest like swallowed glass. "I swear it, I never heard—"

*We know. He didn't knock like a man. He knocked like a message.* She licked a crumb from her whiskers. *Then the quiet came. You felt it, we saw you at the window — don't gape, we see everything, it's our whole profession. The quiet came up the alley and the man turned around to face it, and do you know what he said?*

"No."

*He said, "Please. Not the children." * Her tail flicked once. *Whatever he carried, it mattered less to him than his children. Remember that. It's the only clean fact in this whole filthy night.*

My throat was tight. "What killed him?"

*Nothing you could put in a cage.* The leader's ears flattened. *It came up the alley like weather. No prints — you'd have loved that, ledger-keeper, a perfect blank entry. And it wore—* she paused, and the pause had weight, the way a coin has weight, *—it wore a shape.*

"What kind of shape?"

*A good one,* she said simply. *That's the horror of it. It wore a kind face.*

The candle flame bent, though there was no draft.

*It knelt beside him,* she went on, quieter now, the way beasts go quiet around wounds. *It looked sorry. It looked so sorry, ledger-keeper. It reached out gentle as a mother and it asked him — politely, we heard it ask — "Where is the map?" And he wouldn't say. So it thanked him. It *thanked him*. And then it took his throat, and it stood up, and it looked at your window—*

The cellar seemed to get colder by several degrees. Several rats near the back began, very softly, to groom themselves, which is what rats do instead of screaming.

"—at my window," I finished.

*At your window. And it smiled. And then it wasn't anything anymore, just the snow falling where it had been.* The old rat fixed me with her ink-drop eyes. *Now here is what you pay the second half of your bread for. That thing isn't done. It lost the map, and maps don't die, maps get *found*, and it knows who found it better than you do.*

"How could it possibly know that? I hid it before the enforcers—"

*It wears a kind face,* she repeated. *Kind faces get invited in. Kind faces get trusted with errands. Ask yourself, ledger-keeper: who has been kind to you lately? Who smiles at you across a desk? Who calls you discreet?*

Nobody had called me discreet. Nobody had spoken to me at all today except Brannic, and Brannic—

—and Brannic had spent ten minutes asking me, gently, carefully, exactly what I'd seen from my window.

No. No, that was Brannic. Brannic had pulled me out of the ash-barrel when we were six. Brannic had lied to the matron about the broken window. Brannic's conscience was the single most reliable object in the Vellum Guild, more reliable than the ledgers themselves.

But the old rat watched me think it, and her whiskers drooped, almost pitying.

*That's the trick of it,* she said. *The kind face doesn't have to be the killer's own. Sometimes it's just the face the killer borrows. Sometimes it's the face standing next to you saying be careful, little clerk, trust no one — while all the while the real question, the question you should be asking yourself, is much simpler.*

"Which is?"

*Who knew the courier was coming?*

I opened my mouth to say nobody, and closed it. Because somebody had known. Somebody had set the meeting at the salt door, beneath my window, of all the windows in the Vellum Guild — and either the courier had chosen that spot at random, which couriers carrying dragon-fire do not do, or someone had chosen it *for* him.

Chosen it for me.

*There it is,* said the old rat, almost gently. *There's the guilt-stink going strong. You've just understood that you weren't the witness, ledger-keeper. You were the delivery address.*

The bread in my hand had gone cold. The map against my ribs pulsed once, faintly warm, like something sleeping and dreaming.

"Why would anyone route a dying courier past my window?" I asked. "I'm nobody. I balance accounts. I'm furniture with a pen."

*Are you?* The old rat rose onto her haunches, and around her the crescent of rats rose too, a dozen small silhouettes against the candle-glow, and for a moment they looked less like vermin and more like a jury. *Then why does the map breathe? Why does it wake when you touch it? We could smell it through the floorboards last night, singing to you. Old thing. Hungry thing. It's been waiting a long time for someone who could hear it.*

"I can't hear it," I said. "It's a map. Maps don't sing."

*Everything sings,* said the old rat, turning away into the dark behind the jars. *You of all people know that. The only question is whether you'll listen before the kind face finds you — or after.*

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at my desk with the lamp turned low and the map hidden under a false bottom in my account chest, and I did sums, because sums are my prayers. And somewhere past the smallest hour, with the snow starting again outside, a thought arrived with the weight of a delivered parcel:

Tomorrow, Master Cyrus would summon me. I didn't know how I knew — call it the same sense that tells beasts when weather is coming, the sense I'd spent a lifetime pretending I didn't have. But I knew. The guild's machinery had started turning last night when the courier fell, and I was somewhere in its gears now, and the man in the mask would want to look at the piece that had appeared in his machine unbidden.

And I would have to stand in front of that masked face — that smooth, patient, courteous face — and decide whether it was the kind one.

The lamp guttered. In the walls above me, soft as falling snow, the rats were moving — dozens of them now, maybe hundreds, streaming through the bones of the building toward the master's wing, toward the offices, toward everywhere that secrets lived.

I had asked them to watch. I hadn't quite meant to ask them to spy. But the old rat had read the request correctly anyway, because rats always understand debt, and I owed them now, and debts get collected.

Below my window, at the mouth of the salt alley, the snow lay smooth and unbroken in the lamplight.

By morning, there would be footprints leading to the guild doors — a single line of them, light as apology, coming from nowhere and belonging to no one.

Something with a kind face was already on its way to call on me.
