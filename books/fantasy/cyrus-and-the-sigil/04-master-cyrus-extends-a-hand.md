## 4. Master Cyrus Extends a Hand

The summons came at half past nine in the morning, which was indecent. Nothing in the Vellum Guild was supposed to happen before noon except the griffins' feeding, and the griffins had opinions about that which I recorded faithfully in the ledger under *Livestock: Provisions, Complaints, Verbal*.

Perrin, the youngest of the under-clerks, delivered it on a card of heavy cream stock, his eyes wide as saucers.

"He wants to see you," Perrin whispered, as though the card might overhear. "Master Cyrus. Himself. In the Amber Room."

"I can read, Perrin."

"You're going to *die*."

"Thank you for that. Tell the ravens their invoice is still wrong."

He scurried off, and I stood in the counting house with the card in my hand and my heart doing something unprofessional against my ribs. The card read, in a hand so elegant it looked like it cost money per letter:

*Theo Crane. Eleven o'clock. Do not bring the ledgers.*

Do not bring the ledgers. That was the part that frightened me. I had not been anywhere in this building without a ledger in nine years. The ledgers were my armor. You put a book between yourself and the world, and the world has to negotiate with the book instead of with you.

A dead courier in the salt alley. A scorched map folded into my shirt, warm against my skin like something alive — no, no, like something *burned*, that's all, paper holds heat, don't be a fool — and now the masked master of the guild wanted me, alone, unarmed by arithmetic.

I spent the intervening hour doing what I always did when terrified: I worked. I reconciled the storm-bottling accounts, because fear is best drowned in columns. I told myself a dozen comforting stories. He heard about the courier and wants to offer condolences. He wants to discuss alley security. He wants to promote someone else and needs a witness.

He knows, said the small honest voice I keep in the bottom drawer of my skull, next to the gift itself.

At five to eleven I washed my hands twice, checked that the map was still hidden where I'd hidden it — inside the false spine of my mother's old songbook, which even I never opened, because opening it hurts — and climbed the stairs to the Amber Room.

* * *

Cyrus watched the girl climb the stairs through the eyes of the portrait on the third landing.

That was an imprecision. Portraits have no eyes; they have paint. But the guild's walls were old contracts, and old contracts remember what they were sworn to see, and so he could borrow any of them the way one borrows a coat. He used the third-landing portrait because it caught her face when she reached the turn — and there it was, the flicker he wanted. Fear, yes, everyone feared him, fear was cheap, fear came free with the mask. But under the fear, calculation. She was counting steps. Counting exits. Counting what she knew.

Beautiful. You cannot seduce someone who does not want anything. This one wanted safety, and thought she had lost it, and would therefore listen to anyone who offered it back.

He let the portrait go blind and returned to his own eyes, his own room. The Amber Room earned its name honestly: panels of fossilized resin, honey-gold, mined from a forest that predated the covenant, each slab holding its little museum of ancient insects. He liked the room because everything in it was perfectly preserved and perfectly dead. It was the most honest décor in the guild.

The courier was already accounted for. Two of Brannic's enforcers had found the body in the salt alley and reported, with the flat competence he paid them for, that the map was missing. Searched the corpse twice. Nothing. Which meant the courier had passed it in his last seconds of life to whoever was in that alley — and the guild's records showed precisely one clerk with reason to use the salt door: Crane, who took deliveries of river-silt samples for the Sleeping River account, who kept irregular hours, who had been seen by the night-watch returning through the back ways looking, in the watchman's words, "like someone had walked over her grave."

Or his grave. The records disagreed about Theo Crane, in the way records do when a person has spent a lifetime being deliberately unremarkable. He had read the file twice. Nine years of flawless ledgers. No vices on record, no debts, no lovers, no faction. Promotions declined three times, politely. A salary banked rather than spent. A person built entirely of discretion.

And one more thing, buried in the intake records from the year of the cullings, flagged and then unflagged by some supervisor with more mercy than sense: *mother processed, beast-speaker line*. The guild had taken the mother's voice and released the child, on the theory that the gift skips generations, or that children who watch their parents be emptied make wonderfully obedient adults.

Both theories, in his experience, contained truth. But obedience was only the first ingredient. Obedience plus grief plus hunger — that was the alloy you could forge into anything.

The knock came. Punctual to the minute. Good.

"Enter," he said, and arranged his hands on the arms of the chair like a man settling into comfort he genuinely felt.

* * *

The Amber Room smelled faintly of pine sap and old honey, which I hadn't expected. I don't know what I expected. Ash, maybe. Villains are supposed to smell of ash.

Cyrus sat behind a desk that was older than the guild and possibly than the city, and he was exactly as rumor painted him and nothing like it at all. Tall — or tall-seated, hard to say — gloved, and masked. The mask was porcelain-white and smooth as an egg, featureless except for two eyeholes, and somehow it conveyed expressions anyway. Right now it conveyed *warmth*, which was the most alarming thing in the room besides everything else.

"Theo Crane," he said, and his voice was a pleasant baritone, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it because rooms raised themselves for him. "Sit. Please."

I sat. There was a chair placed exactly opposite him and it had clearly been placed there on purpose, at the exact distance that reads as *colleague* rather than *supplicant*. Everything about this room had been arranged on purpose. That was almost comforting. Seduction respects its victim; terror doesn't bother.

"Do you know why you're here?"

"Audit?" I offered. "My storm invoices have been immaculate for six years, sir, so if it's the storms, someone's forged—"

"It's not the storms." Something moved behind the mask that I chose to interpret as a smile. "Your ledgers are the finest in the guild's history. Did you know that? I have compared. Forty years of accounts, and none of your predecessors come close. Not in accuracy." A pause, measured out like medicine. "In honesty."

"I—" My mouth was dry. "Sir, I'm not sure what—"

"There was a man in the salt alley yesterday morning," Cyrus said. "A courier. He died ten feet from our door. And you were seen nearby, shortly after, pale as milk." He tilted the mask slightly. "You needn't confess anything. I am not accusing you of anything. I am telling you what I know, so you understand that I am a man who knows things. It saves time later."

Every instinct I had was screaming at me to lie, and every other instinct was screaming that lying to Cyrus was how you ended up in the ledger yourself, under *Assets, Human, Depleted*. So I did the thing that felt least like either: I told the truth sideways.

"I saw him die," I said quietly. "It shook me. I didn't report it because — what was there to report? He was past reporting to."

"And you touched nothing of his?"

The map burned against my sternum like a coal sewn into my shirt. "Nothing," I said, and hated the word as it left me, and hated more how easily it left me. First lie to the guild, yesterday, to the enforcers in the alley. Second lie today. They say it gets easier. It was getting easier. That was the horrifying part.

"Mm," said Cyrus, and let the silence sit just long enough to be felt. Then he rose, crossed to the amber panels, and touched one — the one with the moth inside, wings spread, forty million years from home. "Do you know what I value most in this guild, Crane? Not the vaults. Not the contracts. Discretion. Everyone in this city has talent; talent is common as rats. What is rare is a person who can carry something heavy and never once set it down in the wrong room." He turned. "You have carried things for nine years. Quietly. Beautifully."

*I have carried things,* I thought, with a lurch, *you will never guess at.* And then, immediately behind it, the colder thought: *unless you have guessed.*

"The guild wishes to promote you," he went on, returning to his chair. "Properly, this time — not one of the promotions you declined. Special acquisitions. You would keep the true ledger. The accounts we do not show the charter-holders. You would travel, occasionally, with Brannic's teams. You would see where our inventory comes from." The mask leaned forward. "Triple salary. Rooms in the east wing. And my personal protection, which in this city is worth more than the salary."

"That's generous," I managed.

"It is fair," he corrected, gently, as if generosity were a vulgarity. "Fairness is rarer. Now. I will ask you directly, once, and you may answer however you wish: did the dying courier give you anything?"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a griffin complained about breakfast.

"No, sir," I said. Third lie. Three lies in one day, from a woman whose entire life had been built on the principle that numbers do not lie and neither should I. My mother's voice floated up out of the drawer where I keep it: *a tally with one false entry isn't a tally, little bird.*

"Good," said Cyrus warmly. "Then there was nothing to conceal, and nothing to find, and we may proceed as colleagues." He extended one gloved hand across the desk. "Special acquisitions, Crane. Do we have an accord?"

And here is the thing I will have to answer for eventually, to whatever keeps the true ledger of me: in that moment I *wanted* it. Not the money, particularly. The protection. To stand inside the fire instead of hiding from it. To stop being invisible. To walk beside Brannic again, the way we did when we were orphans together, before the guild grew up around us and assigned us different cages. And underneath all of that, sly as sin: if I said yes, I could learn where the inventory came from. I could find out what the guild *is*, from the inside, with the map safe in my shirt and the rats' warning ringing in my ears. *Something that wears a kind face.*

Loyalty and ambition, I told myself, shaking his cold glove. Same muscle. Different exercises.

"We have an accord," I said.

"Wonderful." He rose, and I understood that I was dismissed, but at the door his voice followed me, light as a leaf falling: "One housekeeping matter. The east wing rooms are ready tonight. We'll want your current quarters cleared by dusk — standard practice for a promotion. Fresh start."

Outside the door, in the corridor, I stopped walking.

My quarters. Where my mother's songbook lived on my shelf, with its false spine, with its scorched cargo. Where I had slept every night for nine years, believing — oh, I had actually believed it — that invisibility was the same thing as safety.

"By dusk, Miss Crane," said the footman at the end of the hall, pleasantly, helpfully, horribly. "Shall I send porters up?"

* * *

Alone in the Amber Room, Cyrus permitted himself the small luxury of removing his gloves, finger by finger, and flexing his hands in the amber light.

She had lied to him three times. He had counted, the way she counted steps. The *no* at the end had been the interesting one — too smooth, rehearsed-smooth, the lie of a person who had decided in advance. Which meant the courier had given her something, and she had kept it, and she had accepted promotion anyway, which meant she intended to hide it inside the guild rather than run with it.

Even better. A runner you must chase. A keeper you need only wait upon. Whatever she carried would come to him eventually, drawn to the hearth like every warm thing in this world. And meanwhile she would open the true ledger for him, and read it, and understand what the guild *was*, and discover — this was the delicate part, the part requiring patience — that understanding could be survived. Had been survived. By him, for centuries.

He looked down at his bare hands. Under the amber light they looked almost young.

"Porters for the east wing," he said mildly, to the room, and somewhere in the walls an old contract woke up and went to do his errand.
