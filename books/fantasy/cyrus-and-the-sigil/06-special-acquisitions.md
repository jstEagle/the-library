## 6. Special Acquisitions

The promotion came with a coat.

That should have been my first warning. The Vellum Guild did not give its clerks coats; it gave them ink-stained aprons and a quiet expectation of early graves. But on the morning after Master Cyrus's summons, a package arrived at my desk wrapped in grey paper and tied with the guild's sigil in wax — the open hand holding the quill — and inside was a long coat of oiled leather, dark as a closed ledger, cut to fit me almost too well.

Almost. As if someone had measured me while I slept.

I stood there holding it like it might bite, which, given the guild's inventory, was not an unreasonable assumption.

"You're supposed to put it *on*," Brannic said from the doorway. He'd been promoted too, apparently — or rather, he had been assigned to me, which in guild arithmetic amounted to the same thing. He looked exactly as he always did: broad as a door, jaw like a dropped anvil, with that permanent expression of a man doing sums he doesn't like. "Special acquisitions. You're special acquisitions now."

"I'm an accountant," I said.

"Not anymore." He tossed something at me; I caught it by reflex, which was how Brannic got most people to do things — he simply began them fast enough that courtesy finished the job. It was a ledger. New, bound in green calfskin, the guild stamp still sharp on the cover. "Acquisition records. You'll keep them. That's why they want you."

"Why *me*?"

Brannic shrugged in the way that meant he knew perfectly well and wasn't going to say. "We leave within the hour. Wear the coat."

I wore the coat. Of course I wore the coat. This is the thing you must understand about me, before you judge what came after: I have always worn the coat. The right clothes, the right silence, the right lowered eyes. Twenty years in the counting house taught me that survival is mostly tailoring. The guild raised me from nothing, fed me, taught me my letters and my figures and the thousand small invisibilities that kept a beast-speaker alive in a city where such people were culled like weeds. I owed it everything.

I was twenty-six years old and I had never once asked what the everything cost, or who paid it.

* * *

The mission, as Brannic explained it on the river barge heading south, was simple. A collector in Saltmere wanted a certain item. The guild had located it. We would retrieve it, deliver it, collect payment, and come home richer. Standard business. The word *retrieval* sat oddly in his mouth, though — too smooth, like a stone he'd swallowed years ago and learned to talk around.

"Retrieve from whom?" I asked.

"Doesn't matter," said Brannic, watching the water. "It's not ours. Nothing we take is ever really anyone's. You know that. Half the vault is things nobody owns — storms, rivers, the last word of a dead king." He glanced at me then, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes, some old calculation finally refusing to balance. Then it was gone, filed away, and he was just Brannic again, my oldest friend, the boy who'd shared his bread with me in the orphan dormitory because I cried at night and he couldn't stand the sound. "You'll write it down clean. That's all. Write it down clean and don't ask."

"Don't ask," I repeated.

"It's better," he said, and turned back to the water, and I understood that this was not an order. It was a plea. I didn't understand yet what it was a plea *for*.

The river took us south through country I had never seen, having spent my whole life between the counting house and the dormitory and the alley where I bought eel pies from a woman who thought I was mute. I watched the banks slide past — willows, herons, a mill wheel turning — and felt the strange vertigo of a creature leaving its tank. Somewhere under the reeds, I could hear the faint gossip of water-rats complaining about the spring floods, and I kept my face blank and my mouth shut, as I had done every day of my life, and told myself the tightness in my chest was only the new coat.

* * *

Saltmere was a town built on the belief that money can buy its way out of consequence. The houses leaned over the harbor like fat men peering into their own reflections, and every third window displayed something impossible: a jar of bottled thundercloud, a clockwork nightingale, a painting whose subjects aged in real time. Wealth here was measured in captivity. The more marvelous the cage, the greater the man.

Our client lived at the top of the tallest house, in rooms that smelled of lilies laid over something older and worse. His name was Corvane Aldous, and he received us in a study lined with glass cases, each one lit by a small warm lamp, each one containing something alive.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was my trade. Not stuffed things. Not curios. *Alive.* A moth the size of my two hands, wings patterned with actual stars — or something so close to stars that the distinction stopped mattering. A salamander sleeping in a bed of coals that never cooled. A small grey bird that sang the same four notes over and over, and when I listened — I shouldn't have, but I listened — the notes were a name, repeated, a name in a language I didn't know, sung by a bird that had forgotten everything except that someone, somewhere, needed to hear it.

I made myself stop listening. I made myself look at the ledgers instead. There were ledgers everywhere; collectors love ledgers, they are proof that madness has been organized.

"Master Crane," said Aldous, shaking my hand with fingers soft as slug-belly. "Cyrus speaks highly of you. Discreet, accurate, no questions. My three favorite qualities in a clerk." He smiled. His teeth were very even. "And Enforcer Brannic. Good. Now — the item. Down at the docks. Your people have already secured it. You need only verify condition, complete the paperwork, and escort it to my house."

"What is it?" I asked, because I had promised myself I wouldn't, and breaking promises to yourself early sets a useful precedent for later.

Aldous's smile widened by one tooth. "A selkie," he said, in the tone other men used for discussing vintage wine. "A genuine pelt-bound selkie, female, young. Taken off the northern shoals last month. Do you know, she sang for three days straight when they caught her? Broke two of the crew's hearts clean in half. They sold her cheap rather than listen to any more of it." He sighed happily. "People have no appreciation. Her voice alone is worth what I'm paying. In fifty years, when the seal-roads close forever, she'll be priceless."

Something cold went through me, head to heel. "*Taken*," I said. "Off the northern shoals. From—"

"From the sea, clerk. Where else?" He waved a ringed hand. "Come. Verify your item. I do hate when the paperwork drags."

* * *

The crate was in a warehouse at the end of the pier, and I heard it before I saw it.

You have to understand what my gift actually is. People imagine talking to beasts is like talking to people — words, sentences, conversation. It isn't. Beasts speak in weather. In pressure. A dog's greeting arrives like sunlight on your face; a rat's warning is the smell of rain coming. And grief — beast-grief is a tide. It comes in slow, and it does not stop coming, and it fills up whatever contains it until either the container breaks or the grief finds somewhere else to go.

The crate contained grief enough to drown the harbor.

It was oak, iron-banded, big as a coffin for a large man, with air holes drilled along one side in a pattern someone had thought decorative. Two guild porters stood beside it looking anywhere else. Brannic checked the manifest with the warehouse keeper while I stood in front of the crate with my new ledger open and my pen dry in my hand, and inside, something shifted — a soft, heavy movement, skin against wood — and a voice reached me.

Not words. Selkies sing true words when they choose to, I would learn that later; but this was before words. This was the sound beneath them. It said: *the water is that way. The water is that way. The water is that way.* Over and over, the way a child says a prayer, the way a prisoner counts days. The sea was maybe forty feet from where I stood. I could smell it. So could she.

"Condition?" Brannic asked me quietly.

I opened my mouth and found I had no voice, which was its own private joke, the beast-speaker struck dumb by a beast.

"Theo." Brannic's voice, low. "Verify condition. That's the job."

Through the nearest air hole, I saw an eye. Dark, wet, human-shaped — because that is the cruelty of a selkie out of her pelt, that she looks like us, that you cannot pretend she is a cargo category. The eye found mine through the hole, and held it.

And here is what I did, and I want you to witness it clearly, because I have witnessed it every night since: I looked away first.

I bent to the crate, ran my fingers along the bands as if checking them — the wood was warm, impossibly warm, the warmth of a living body — and called out, "Pelt intact, subject breathing, minor abrasions to the wrists consistent with transport." Words from the manifest. Words from the world of receipts. My pen moved across the green calfskin ledger: *One (1) selkie, female, juvenile. Condition fair. Delivered per contract.*

Fair. I wrote *fair*, the way the ledger wanted, the way the guild wanted, the way Cyrus's warm voice in my memory wanted, and the pen scratched like a small animal digging its own grave.

"Sign," said the warehouse keeper, and I signed. Theo Crane, acquisition clerk, special division. My name in wet ink above a coffin with air holes.

Brannic watched me sign. I felt him watching the way you feel weather changing. He said nothing. The porters loaded the crate onto a cart. And the selkie, feeling the cart begin to move — away from the water, always away from the water — began to sing again, quietly now, the way you sing when you've understood that singing changes nothing, and the song followed our cart through the streets of Saltmere like a debt neither of us could pay.

* * *

Delivery took an hour. Unloading took another. Aldous's servants carried the crate up six flights to a room already prepared — I saw it, I saw the room, with its padded walls and its salt-water bath and its little barred window facing deliberately away from the harbor — and then it was done, and Aldous pressed a heavy purse into Brannic's hands and a lighter one into mine, "for the clerk's discretion," and shook my hand again with his soft terrible fingers, and thanked me.

Thanked me. For the paperwork. As if the paperwork were the thing.

We walked back down toward the docks in the late light, and the harbor smelled of fish and tar and freedom I had helped to cancel, and I got perhaps two hundred paces before my legs simply declined to continue, and I sat down on a bollard like a puppet with its strings cut.

"Theo," Brannic said.

"Don't." My voice came out cracked. "Don't say it's the job. Don't say I'll get used to it. Just— don't. Give me a minute where nobody says anything."

He sat down next to me on the next bollard, which groaned in protest, and said nothing. Gulls argued overhead about a fish head. Somewhere behind us, muffled by six floors and padded walls, a selkie was learning the dimensions of her new life.

"I wrote 'fair,'" I said finally. "In the ledger. She's in a box, and I wrote *fair*."

"That's what the box is for," Brannic said. "So the writing stays easy." He was looking at his hands. Big hands, scarred knuckles, hands that had enforced many retrievals before this one. "You think I don't hear it? At night. You think the ones we've moved don't follow a man home?" He shook his head slowly. "They do. They follow you home and they sit outside your sleep and they wait to see what you'll do about it. And most nights, the answer is: nothing. You get up, you eat, you go to work. Because the alternative—" He stopped. Started again. "Because the guild fed us, Theo. Both of us. Nobody else wanted us. You remember."

"I remember."

"And the guild is Cyrus, and Cyrus is the roof over everything, and the roof holds because nobody asks what it's nailed to." He looked at me then, and his face was the loneliest thing I had seen in twenty-six years, because it was the face of a good man describing his own cage with perfect accuracy and no intention whatsoever of leaving it. "So write it down clean. Keep the ledgers honest, even if the trade isn't. Somebody has to keep the true record. Maybe someday somebody reads it."

"Is that what you tell yourself?"

"No," Brannic said. "What I tell myself is worse. What I tell myself is that I'd need a whole army of men like me to change it, and I've only got me, and me has a sister in the guild infirmary whose care costs more than I'll ever earn." He stood, joints cracking, and offered me his hand. "Come on. Barge leaves on the morning tide. And Theo — tonight, when it comes to sit outside your sleep? Let it. That part you don't get used to, and you shouldn't. It's the last piece of you that's any use."

* * *

We sailed north through the dark, and I did not sleep, and the grief sat outside my sleep exactly as promised, patient as tide.

Somewhere past midnight I gave up on the bunk and went up on deck with the green ledger, because I couldn't bear it lying below among the cargo like one more item in transit. I read back through my own neat entries — *condition fair, delivered per contract, payment received* — and hated the handwriting, hated how steady it was, hated the version of myself that had produced it without a tremor.

Then I turned to a fresh page, and in the dark, by feel more than sight, I wrote a second entry. Not for the guild. For whoever Brannic hoped might someday read.

*Item: one selkie. Name unknown — names taken with the pelt. Voice: singing. Song's meaning: the water is that way. Location of water: forty feet east of the crate, entire and free and indifferent to contracts. Condition:* 

My pen hovered over the last word for a long time.

*Condition: stolen,* I wrote. *By the Vellum Guild. Verified by T. Crane, who signed anyway.*

The gulls had gone quiet. The river ran black and endless under the keel, going where it liked, answering to no ledger. And far off in the dark water, faint as memory, something that lived in that free water called out once — a long, rising note — and every hair on my arms stood up, because I understood it, the way I understand all of them, the gift I hide like a knife against my ribs:

It wasn't a call. It was a question, thrown out across the blackness to whatever might hear.

*Anyone? Is anyone there?*

And I sat on a merchant barge in a stolen coat with a stolen purse in my pocket and did not answer, because answering is the beginning of everything, and I was not yet brave enough to begin.

But I wrote the date in the margin. Under it, one more line, small enough that only a careful reader would find it:

*There is a map in my coat lining. I am starting to suspect it is worth more than every crate this guild has ever shipped.*

The tide carried us home. The guild towers rose out of the dawn fog like the fingers of a hand about to close.
