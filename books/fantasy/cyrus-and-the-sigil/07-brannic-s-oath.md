## 7. Brannic's Oath

I have broken three doors in my career as an enforcer of the Vellum Guild, and I regret all of them. Not because the doors didn't deserve it — one belonged to a counterfeiter who kept a bear in his cellar and fed it failed forgeries — but because a door is an honest thing. It stands where it stands. It does what you ask or it splinters. Doors have never once lied to me, which is more than I can say for anyone else in this building, including myself.

Theo Crane's door was not one of the broken ones. Theo's door opened before I could knock.

That should have told me something right there. You don't open your door to an enforcer's knock unless you've been standing behind it, listening to his boots come up the stairs, doing arithmetic about how much trouble you're in. And Theo — quiet Theo, ledger-inked Theo, the person I have known since we were both guild brats stealing sugar from the infirmary stores — Theo had been doing that arithmetic for six days now. I'd watched them do it across the mess hall, across the counting house, across the courtyard where they fed the messenger pigeons with hands that shook only when they thought no one was looking.

Six days since the courier died in the salt alley with his blood freezing black on the cobbles. Six days since Master Cyrus asked me, in that voice like warm oil poured over cold stone, whether anything had been recovered from the body.

"Nothing," I had said. "Papers burned. Pouch empty."

And Cyrus had looked at me for exactly one heartbeat too long, and smiled, and said, "How tidy," and I had spent every night since lying awake wondering whether my face had done what faces do when they lie, which is everything wrong at once.

Here is what nobody tells you about being raised by the Vellum Guild: they teach you locks, ledgers, blades, and silence, but they do not teach you how to look at your oldest friend and see a stranger wearing their posture. That part you learn on your own, badly, at two in the morning, standing in a doorway while the person you'd take a knife for looks back at you like a cornered animal trying very hard to be furniture.

"Theo," I said. "You're going to invite me in, because the hallway smells like the tannery, and because if we do this in the hall, Marla from the third floor will hear every word and sell it to the kitchens by dawn."

"Would she?" Theo said. "Or would she sell it to Cyrus directly? She strikes me as ambitious."

"That's the spirit." I stepped inside.

* * *

Their room above the counting house was exactly what I expected: narrow, clean, a bed made with corners sharp enough to draw blood, shelves of ledgers organized by some system only Theo understood and possibly only Theo was meant to survive knowing. A candle burned low. A cup of tea sat untouched long enough to have gone cold and bitter, which for Theo was roughly equivalent to another man screaming.

I sat on the stool. They stayed standing by the window, arms crossed, silhouetted against the gray light of the city below. Somewhere out there a cart rattled, and a dog barked at it, and the dog lost, as dogs always do against carts.

"You've been different," I said. "Since the alley."

"Have I."

"Don't do the ledger voice at me, Theo. I've known you since you were nine years old and cried over a dead sparrow for two days. I know all your voices. The flat one is the one you use when you're terrified."

Silence. The candle guttered. Theo's jaw worked once, twice, like someone chewing on a sentence that had too many bones in it.

"I'm fine, Brannic."

"There it is," I said. "The second-worst lie in the building."

"What's the worst?"

"'The Guild has your best interests at heart.'" I leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Come on. Out with it. Whatever you found in that alley, whatever the courier gave you — and he gave you something, Theo, because I saw your face when we turned him over, and I've seen corpses before, and you looked at that one like it was handing you a live coal —"

"Brannic."

"— whatever it is, you've been carrying it alone for six days, and it's eating you, and I am asking you, as the person who taught you how to throw a punch properly after you broke your thumb on Doss Merrick's teeth —"

"Stop."

The word came out small. That frightened me more than shouting would have. Theo Crane did not do small. Theo did precise, and dry, and quietly stubborn, and occasionally so funny it took me a full minute to realize I'd been laughed at. Small was new. Small was a crack in a wall I'd trusted my whole life to hold.

They turned from the window. In the candlelight their eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted, and older than twenty-six years had any right to make them.

"Why?" they said. "Why are you really here, Brannic? Because we both know you're not clever enough to have figured this out on your own."

That stung, mostly because it was true. "Cyrus asked me to ask you."

The stillness that went through Theo then — I will remember it until I die. Not a flinch. A total cessation, like a river hitting ice. "Asked you to *ask* me."

"He noticed you were rattled. He notices everything, you know that. So he sent me up here with instructions to be friendly about it." I spread my hands. "Which is a problem, Theo, because I've been friendly at you for ten minutes and you haven't given me a single thing, and I need you to understand what happens next in this story."

"Tell me what happens next."

"I go downstairs," I said, "and I tell Cyrus that you knew the courier, that you were shaken by his death, and that there's nothing in it. And then either he believes me, or he doesn't, and if he doesn't, he sends someone less friendly than me, and that someone doesn't sit on your stool and drink your terrible cold tea. That someone goes through this room board by board."

"And you came up here first," Theo said slowly, "to give me the chance to hand it over. To protect me."

"No," I said.

And here is the part I had rehearsed on the stairs, and botched anyway, because rehearsal is useless when the truth is involved. The truth comes out sideways or not at all.

"I came up here," I said, "to beg you to lie to me."

Theo blinked. "What?"

"I don't want the map. Or the papers, or the coal, or whatever it is. I don't want to know. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am standing here, enforcer of the Vellum Guild, sworn blade of Master Cyrus, and I am asking my oldest friend to look me in the eye and tell me a lie, and I am promising — I am *swearing* — to believe it."

* * *

You have to understand what my conscience costs, and what it's worth, before you can understand why I asked.

I was four when the Guild took me in off a step somewhere — I never learned whose step, and eventually I stopped inventing mothers for myself, because the Guild disapproves of fiction except when it's profitable. Theo arrived two years later, smaller and angrier, and we grew up in the same drafty dormitory learning the same lessons: the Guild feeds you, the Guild shelters you, the Guild asks nothing you can't afford to give. It's a good creed. It holds up beautifully right up until the day you're sixteen and they send you on your first retrieval and you stand in a warehouse holding a lantern while men crate up something alive, something that looks at you with eyes that understand everything and forgive nothing, and the foreman hands you your pay and says, "Good work, lad," and you take it, because you're hungry, and because everyone around you takes it, and because the alternative is a question you cannot afford to start asking.

I took the pay. I took it for ten years. I built a whole life on top of those crates, the way you build a house on a floor you've agreed never to look down at.

That's my conscience: a floor I don't look down at. It works. It has always worked. Some nights it groans under me, and some nights I hear things moving underneath it, but the house stands, and the people I love live in it, and I keep them safe, and that has to be enough. It has to be. Because the alternative is opening the floorboards, and I have seen what happens to people who open the floorboards. They stop sleeping. They stop laughing. They start walking into Master Cyrus's office with resignation letters written in their own blood.

So when I say I begged Theo to lie to me, understand: I wasn't asking for ignorance. I was asking to keep the house standing. I was asking my oldest friend to help me not look down.

"Theo," I said, "tell me the courier was a smuggler. Tell me he owed the Guild money and you were ashamed you knew him. Tell me anything. Give me one clean lie and I'll carry it downstairs and set it in front of Cyrus like a plate, and we'll both walk away from this, and in twenty years we'll be two old guildsfolk arguing about pension rates, and neither of us will ever have to know what was in that alley."

They looked at me for a long time. And I swear on every door I've ever broken, there was a moment — a real one — where they almost did it. I watched the lie assemble itself behind their eyes. I watched them reach for it the way a drowning man reaches for a rope.

Then their shoulders dropped, and the moment passed, and I knew I'd lost before they said a word.

"Oh, Brannic," they said, and their voice cracked right down the middle. "I can't. You know I can't. You just stood in my doorway and described exactly what they'll do to me if I get caught, and you think I can hand you a lie and trust you to carry it? You're the worst liar in the Guild. You sweat when you order soup."

"That's not — I don't sweat at soup —"

"You perspire *visibly*. At soup." They laughed, and it came out wet and awful, and then they pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes and stood there shaking, and I got up off the stool because there are moments when sitting feels like a crime.

I put my hand on their shoulder. They didn't shrug it off. That was something.

"Theo," I said quietly. "Whatever it is. However bad it is. I'm not going to give you to him. You have to know that. Whatever else I am, whatever oaths I've signed in whatever ink — I'm not going to be the hand that puts you in a crate."

"You don't even know what I'm carrying."

"No. But I know you. And I know that in thirty years I have never once seen you hurt anything that didn't deserve it, and I know that whatever's eating you alive right now, it isn't guilt over smuggling. It's something bigger. And I know—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I know that if I open that floor, Theo, I don't come back from it. So I'm asking you, one last time, as the person who loves you most in this rotten building: don't make me look down."

* * *

We stood there a while. The candle died. Neither of us moved to light another, and the gray morning crept up the walls between us like it was embarrassed to interrupt.

Finally Theo spoke, and their voice was steady again, which somehow hurt worse than the cracking had.

"If I told you," they said, "you'd have to choose. Right then. No thinking, no going home to sleep on it. And Brannic — I've seen you sleep on things. You once took four days to pick a boot."

"It was a good boot."

"It was an ugly boot." They turned and faced me fully, and in the half-dark their expression was one I'd never seen on them before: not fear, not guilt. Resolve. Terrible, lonely resolve, the kind you usually only see on people who've already decided to die for something and are waiting politely for the world to catch up. "I can't give you the lie you want. I'm sorry. I know what it costs you to ask, and I'm sorry, but I won't hand you a comfortable blindness, because comfortable blindness is exactly what he's counting on. From all of us. From everyone."

"*He.*" My stomach dropped through all ten floors of the Guild. "Theo. Whose name is that?"

But they were already shaking their head, stepping back, putting the dead candle between us like a border. "Don't ask me that. Please. Because you'll make me answer, and then you'll have to choose, and I refuse — do you hear me, Brannic, I *refuse* — to be the reason you break. Not you. Anyone but you."

"Theo—"

"Tell Cyrus I was upset about the courier because I knew him from the market. Tell him I'm fine now. Tell him whatever gets you through tonight." They managed something that was almost a smile, and it was the loneliest thing I have ever seen a human face do. "And Brannic — if someday soon I'm gone, if I run, if they tell you I betrayed the Guild — remember the sparrow. Remember that I cried over a sparrow for two days. Whatever they say I did, I did it for something that couldn't speak for itself. That's all I can give you. Take it or leave it."

I left. What else was there? I walked down the stairs past Marla's door, out into the courtyard where the pigeons were making their idiot morning noises, and I stood there in the cold with my heart hammering and my oath ringing in my ears like a cracked bell.

I went to Cyrus's office. I told him Theo had known the courier from the market, and was shaken, and was fine now.

He listened with his masked face tilted, firelight sliding along the silver filigree, and thanked me warmly, and dismissed me kindly, and believed me exactly as far as I deserved.

And that night, when I checked the duty roster as I do every night, I found a new assignment posted in fresh ink. Watch rotation: the counting house roof. Subject: the windows of one T. Crane, ledger-keeper. Effective immediately. Signed in the master's own hand.

They hadn't given the job to me.

They'd given it to Hesk, who breaks doors for pleasure, and who has never once in eleven years been known to report anything except exactly what he sees.

I stood in the corridor a long time, reading that roster until the letters swam. Then I tore the page down, folded it small, and put it in my pocket next to the clean conscience I was no longer sure I owned — and started, for the first time in my life, to plan a lie big enough to hide a person inside.
