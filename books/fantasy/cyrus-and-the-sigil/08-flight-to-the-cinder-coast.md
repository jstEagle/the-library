## 8. Flight to the Cinder Coast

I had planned my escape the way I planned everything: in a ledger, in pencil, in a column I could erase.

*Contingency — if discovered.* It sat at the back of the third ledger, the one nobody ever audited because nobody suspected the quiet bookkeeper of having contingencies. The column listed, in my neat clerk's hand: one cloak (guild-issue, grey, forgettable), eleven silver marks (hidden inside a hollowed-out *Treatise on Estuarine Tithes*), a knife I'd never used, and — under *assets, uncertain* — a fox.

The fox was not, strictly speaking, an asset. The fox was a liability with opinions.

"You're really doing it," Sorrel said, perched on the windowsill of my garret while I stuffed bread into a satchel with the calm of a woman having a nervous collapse. "You're actually doing it. The map, the running, the whole tragic-heroine production. May I say, for the record, that I advised against this?"

"You advised against waking up. You advised against eating anything that wasn't pigeon. You advised against my entire existence."

"And you ignored me, and look how that's gone." Sorrel was a half-tame fox, which is to say a fox who had decided, for reasons never disclosed, to tolerate my company, and who spoke to me in the dry, put-upon voice of a creature who had seen every mistake the world had to offer and found most of them derivative. "For the record, the record being me, I think the masked one already knows. I think he's known since the night you stood in the courtyard listening to his hounds like they were reciting poetry."

"They *were* reciting poetry. Bad poetry. They're hounds."

"They're *his* hounds, and hounds talk, and you talk to hounds, and somewhere in that arithmetic is a reason we're leaving tonight instead of never." He licked a paw with tremendous indifference. "Also, you're packing the wrong bread. The rye keeps. The white goes to mold by tomorrow, and you'll be weeping over it on the road like it's a dead relative."

I swapped the bread. This was our whole relationship: he insulted me, I obeyed, and neither of us acknowledged that we were, in whatever broken grammar foxes use, friends.

Below, the Vellum Guild slept the way it always slept — not peacefully, but *guardedly*, with lamps burning in the counting house and boots on the stairs at odd hours, an institution that had learned to be afraid of the things it kept. Somewhere in its vaults were bottled storms and caged griffins and a river that dreamed, and somewhere in its ledgers, in my handwriting, were the numbers that made all of it respectable. I had spent eleven years making theft look like bookkeeping. Tonight the bookkeeping looked back.

I slung the satchel over my shoulder and touched the map through my shirt, where it lay against my skin like a second heartbeat. It was warm. It was always warm. Dragon-skin, drawn in dragon-blood, breathing its slow route toward the Cinder Coast — and visible, the hawk had said, only to someone who could hear beasts. *The masked one eats fire.* I had turned that sentence over so many nights it had worn smooth as a river stone.

"Stairs or window?" Sorrel asked.

"Window. Brannic sleeps light, and if he sees me go, he'll have to choose. I won't make him choose."

"Sentimental. It'll get you killed."

"It's kept me alive so far."

"Has it?" He hopped to my shoulder, and his weight there — warm, alive, faintly smelling of ash and stolen chickens — was the last thing that felt like home. "Fine. Window. But when the hounds come, remember I suggested the sewers."

"The sewers flood at spring tide."

"The sewers are *character-building*."

I went out the window and down the drainpipe, and the city took me.

* * *

You would think a city at night would be quiet. It isn't. It's a different kind of loud — cats arguing over territory, gulls quarreling with the tide, rats in the walls conducting their small grim commerce. I had spent my whole life hearing it and pretending I didn't, the way a person pretends not to hear a neighbor weeping. Tonight, for the first time, I listened on purpose, and the city told me things.

*They've taken the dogs out,* a cat said from a rooftop, in the bored tone of a creature reporting weather. *The pale ones. The ones that don't smell like dogs.*

Pale ones. Cyrus's hounds were bred in the guild kennels, and there was something wrong with them, something the other animals wouldn't name but avoided. They didn't bark. They made a sound like a page tearing.

"They've got our scent from the alley," I told Sorrel. "The courier's blood. It'll be on the drainpipe, on my sleeve—"

"On your *soul*, more like." He flicked his ears. "Left here. The fish market. Smell will cover us, and I know a cat who owes me a favor, which is a sentence I never thought I'd say with a straight face."

The fish market was a canyon of ice and brine and old blood, and we went through it bent double between the stalls, and it was there — crouched under a table of silver herring — that I heard the hounds for the first time.

They came down the market's far mouth in a loose line, six of them, pale as unbleached vellum, and they were not sniffing the ground. They were sniffing the *air*, heads tilted, listening for something. For me. And when the nearest one passed close enough that I could see the stitched seams around its jaw — seams, as if it had been *made* — it spoke, in a voice like dry paper:

*The bearer. The bearer. The master wants the bearer whole.*

Sorrel's claws pricked my shoulder. "Don't," he breathed. "Don't answer it. I can feel you wanting to answer it."

"It's in pain," I whispered. "Under the — whatever they did to it. There's something under there, and it's *screaming*."

"Everything in that guild is screaming under something. That's rather the business model." But his voice had gone quiet, the mockery burned off it, and I understood that he was frightened — really frightened, for the first time since I'd known him. "Theo. Listen to me, because I'll only say this once and then I'll deny it forever. Whatever you think you owe that fox-shaped burden you've been carrying — whatever you think you owe *me* — you put it down and you run when I say run. Because those things aren't hunting a map. They're hunting the *hearing*. And if they catch you, the masked one won't kill that part of you. He'll *harvest* it."

We ran.

* * *

We ran through the fish market and the tannery rows and out the Salt Gate before the watch changed, and then the city was behind us and there was nothing under my feet but the world.

I want to describe it properly, because I had never done it before — never once, in twenty-six years of careful, penciled, erasable life, stood in open country under open sky and simply *been there*. The road west was a pale scar through gorse and heath, and above it the sky went on forever, no ceiling of rafters, no guild roof, no counting-house eaves. Just sky, and stars in numbers that made the ledgers look like a child's first attempt at counting, and a wind that smelled of nothing anyone had ever sold.

I stopped in the middle of the road and I laughed. It came out of me like something escaping a cage, ugly and loud and completely beyond my control, and Sorrel looked at me with an expression of profound fox-like embarrassment.

"Yes, yes, the sky is big, the stars are numerous, you've discovered nature. The hounds are—"

"The sky is *big*," I said, like an idiot, like a child, like someone hearing their own voice for the first time. "Sorrel, there's no roof. There's no *roof* anywhere."

"There's also no walls, which means no doors to lock, which means anything can walk up to you from any direction at any time. I'm so glad you're enjoying it." But he pressed against my leg, briefly, the way he did instead of the things other creatures said, and then he trotted ahead with his tail up like a banner. "Cinder Coast is nine days if we're clever. Six if we're lucky. Four if we're willing to be neither and go through the Weepwood."

"What's in the Weepwood?"

"Badgers with grudges. A river that holds conversations. And the guild's western waystation, which we will be walking directly past, because the direct roads all have pale things on them by now." He glanced back, and his eyes caught the starlight. "You did hear me say the hounds don't tire, didn't you? They don't tire, Theo. They'll walk all night and all day and all night, and they'll be *polite* about it."

* * *

Three days west, the land began to feel wrong, and it took me another day to understand why: it was *quieting*.

The first thing to go was the larks. A heath that should have been loud with them sang with maybe one, and its song had gaps in it, like a book with pages torn out. Then the bees — I passed an orchard where the hives stood silent, and when I put my ear to one, there was nothing. Not dead. *Nothing*, the way a room is nothing when everyone has left it. Then, on the fourth night, the crickets.

"They're not dying," I said, crouched by a hedgerow, listening to a silence that had texture and weight. "Sorrel, they're *forgetting*. The crickets aren't singing because they've forgotten the song."

Sorrel sat down in the road. He did that sometimes when something needed saying and he wanted to be sure I'd take it seriously — sat, and wrapped his tail over his feet, and became briefly, terribly lucid.

"Then the map is true," he said. "The fire is failing. The covenant is failing. And the beasts are losing the — call it the words. The old exchange. Human realm and beast realm, held together by dragon-fire, and the fire's been bled, and now the edges are going quiet." He looked at the empty hedgerow. "You understand what that means for you. For the hearing. If the beasts go silent—"

"Then I go silent."

"You go silent, and worse — you go silent *last*, watching it happen, the way I'm watching it happen." His voice did something I'd never heard it do: it cracked, just slightly, on the last word. He covered it with a sneeze. "Anyway. The Weepwood's a day ahead. Try to sleep. One of us should be well-rested when the badgers start in."

I didn't sleep. I lay under a gorse bush with the map warm against my chest and listened to a world forgetting itself, and somewhere past midnight I heard, very far behind us, a sound like a page tearing.

Then another. Closer.

* * *

We reached the Weepwood at dawn, and the hounds reached the Weepwood's edge at dusk, and between those two facts we had one day of thin, running grace.

The Weepwood was old the way mountains are old, all black trunks and hanging moss that dripped even without rain, and the beasts in it still had their voices — the wood was too old, too rooted, to forget — but they had heard us coming and they were not glad. *Runner,* said the trees, in the rustling way trees have. *Runner with the fire on her back. The pale ones follow. The pale ones are wrong.*

"We know," Sorrel muttered. "Everyone's a critic."

It was near the wood's heart, beside a stream that talked to itself in a language of small stones, that the badgers found us — three of them, low and grey and formidable, with the air of a committee that had already decided against us.

"Toll," said the largest.

"We're fleeing for our lives," I said.

"Everyone's fleeing for their lives. It's the whole road, these days. Toll."

"We have bread," Sorrel said, with the weariness of a creature negotiating with his own relatives. "Rye. It keeps."

The badgers conferred. The toll was set at one loaf, revised upward to one loaf and a true answer to a true question, and the question, delivered while the badger looked at me with small black eyes that had seen four generations of my kind come and go, was this:

*"The fire on your back, runner. When it's rekindled — what do you think it will cost you?"*

And I opened my mouth, and found I didn't know, and the not-knowing sat in my chest like a stone.

"I don't know," I said.

"Paid in full," said the badger, and took the loaf, and the three of them melted into the dark like a debt forgiven.

Sorrel didn't speak for a long time after that. We walked through the dripping wood, and the stream talked, and the moss dripped, and finally, without looking at me, he said:

"They ask everyone that, you know. Every runner, every pilgrim, every fool with a cause. *What will it cost you?* Most of them have an answer. *Nothing I'll miss. Nothing that matters.* Liars, all of them." He trotted ahead, then stopped, his back to me. "You said you don't know. That's either the most honest thing I've heard on this road or the most frightening."

"Which do you think?"

"I think," said Sorrel, "that the hounds are at the tree line, and I think we should discuss it while running."

* * *

They came through the Weepwood at moonrise, six pale shapes moving between the black trunks without a sound, and the wood itself rose up around us — the stream shouting, the owls screaming warnings, the badgers' sett going quiet as a held breath — and still the hounds came on, because they had been made not to hear warnings, only to follow.

We ran. The map against my chest burned like a coal, and the route it breathed into me turned off the path, off every path, toward a place where the trees grew so close the moon was cut to ribbons, and behind us the tearing-page sound came and came and came.

"Left!" Sorrel screamed. "The stream — in the stream, the water kills scent — *in the stream*—"

I went into the water up to my thighs and the cold closed over me like a hand, and I waded upstream with the fox on my shoulder and the hounds' voices tearing through the dark behind us — *the bearer, the bearer* — and then, ahead, through the trees, a light that was not moonlight. A clearing. A stone. A shape standing by the stone, tall and grey, wrapped in a cloak that moved like smoke.

The stone was carved with two hands clasped — one human, one not — and the standing shape turned, and she was a wolf the way a queen is a woman, ancient and upright, and her eyes caught me like a snare.

"You're late," said the wolf-woman. "The fire is nearly out, and you're *late*."

Behind us, at the tree line, the hounds stopped as one, and would not come into the clearing, and began, very softly, all six of them, to whimper.
