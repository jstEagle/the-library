## 9. The Beast-Realm's Edge

The fox had been lying to me about the distance for two days.

"Not far now," he'd said that morning, in the tone of a creature who has said "not far now" since dawn. "Just over this hill." The hill had been followed by a valley, the valley by a ridge, and the ridge by a stretch of moor so flat and gray it seemed the world had simply given up on having features. I began to suspect the fox considered "not far" a philosophical position rather than a measurement.

"You're doing this on purpose," I said, when we paused at a stream to drink.

"Doing what?" He lapped water with the delicate insolence of a cat, which is impressive for an animal built entirely out of mischief and mange.

"Stretching the road. You've been saying 'not far' since we left the pinewood."

He sat back on his haunches and regarded me with one amber eye. "Boy," he said — he called me *boy* though I was twenty-six and he was, by his own account, older than several rivers — "the road isn't stretched. It's *reluctant*. There's a difference. Roads feel what walks them."

I had learned not to argue with him about things like this. In eight days of flight I had learned a great many things not to argue about: which berries were lies, why you never thank a magpie aloud, how to sleep in a tree with your boots tied to a branch so the hounds below couldn't tell which end of you was which. The fox taught me all of it without ever once being asked, which I suspect is his idea of generosity.

His name — the name he permitted me to use — was Sorrel. His real name, he said, was nobody's business, least of all mine.

We crossed the last of the moor as the sun went down behind us, dragging our shadows out long and thin ahead like threads being pulled toward something. And then the land changed.

I don't know how else to say it. The land *changed*. Not gradually, not the way a field becomes a forest. It changed the way a sentence changes when someone says something true in the middle of it. The grass grew greener and then too green, green the way emeralds are green, and the wind coming off it smelled of rain and honey and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed room. The air itself felt thicker, as though the world here were less diluted.

"The edge," Sorrel said, and for once there was no joke under it.

Ahead, on a rise of dark stone, stood the covenant-stone.

I had expected — I don't know what. A wall? A gate? Something with hinges. What stood there instead was a single standing stone perhaps twice my height, weathered into softness, its surface covered in script that moved. That's the part I still can't make tidy in my memory: the letters *moved*, crawling over the stone like light through water, rearranging themselves, and every arrangement was in a different language, and every language was one I could somehow almost read.

And around it — around the stone, in a wide ring — the world was quiet. Not silent. Quiet the way a house is quiet when someone is sleeping in it. The grass bent. The wind held its breath between gusts. Even Sorrel stopped complaining, which frightened me more than anything else that day.

"There's writing on it," I said, because my mouth needed to do something ordinary or I was going to be sick with awe.

"There's always writing on it," Sorrel said. "It's been negotiating for nine hundred years. Stones are terrible at finishing a thought."

I walked toward it. My legs felt borrowed.

Up close, the covenant-stone hummed. Not a sound exactly — a feeling in the teeth, in the breastbone, like standing near a struck bell after the ringing stops. The moving script passed under my eyes and I caught fragments: *...in exchange... ...freely given... ...neither realm shall take...* The words kept dissolving before I could hold them whole.

"It's dying," I said. "Isn't it. I can feel it. It's like — like reading a ledger where the ink keeps fading."

Sorrel looked up at me. Fox faces aren't built for sorrow; they're built for cleverness and hunger, and sorrow just has to squeeze in wherever it fits. "You felt that from the writing?"

"I feel everything on this map. Since the coast road. It's like carrying a hearth that keeps going cold."

"That's the fire," he said quietly. "You're attuned to it now. The map did that. Or the courier did, when he put it in your hands. Either way—" he flicked an ear toward the stone, "—that's what dying sounds like to something that can hear. Fading ink. Cold hearth. Pick your metaphor. They all end the same."

"How long?"

"How long what?"

"How long does the covenant have?"

Sorrel didn't answer right away. He looked at the stone for a long time, and I realized he was listening to it the way I listened to rats in the guild walls — head cocked, whole body gone still, giving the small voices room to speak.

"Ask her yourself," he said finally. "She's been behind you for a quarter hour."

I turned around fast enough to embarrass both of us.

She was tall. That was the first thing — taller than any woman I'd seen, taller than Brannic even, and thin in the way winter trees are thin, all structure and no spareness. She wore a cloak the color of wet slate, hood back, and her hair was iron-gray going white at the temples and loose past her shoulders, and her face —

Her face was a wolf's and a woman's at once, and neither version was a mask. Amber eyes set deep. A jaw that could have been elegant or could have been a muzzle depending on how the light hit it, and the light couldn't decide, and neither could I. She stood the way mountains stand: without effort, and with the understanding that everything nearby was temporary.

"Master Crane," she said. Her voice was low and roughened, like a page that had been turned ten thousand times. "Or should I say *keeper of ledgers*. I'm told you keep excellent accounts."

"I'm told a lot of things," I managed. "Most of them lately have been lies."

"Yes," she said, and something in the amber eyes shifted — not warmth, exactly. Recognition. "They usually are. I am called Seraquin. Once I was ambassador plenipotentiary of the Eastern Packs to the Court of Both Realms. Now I am an exile who talks to stones." She inclined her head, very slightly, very precisely, the way you'd bow if you wanted the bow to be an insult to everyone except the person receiving it. "You carry the courier's map."

It wasn't a question. I put my hand over my coat anyway, which answered it better than words.

"He reached the salt door," Seraquin said. "That's more than I hoped. Less than he deserved." For a moment the stillness in her cracked, just at the edges, the way ice cracks — silently, structurally. "He was young. He ran three hundred miles with a hole in him because he believed a piece of skin mattered more than his life. I would like to know if he was right."

"I think he was," I said. "I've read it. It shows me things."

"Then show me."

I took the map out.

In daylight it was only ever scorched leather with faded lines. But here, at the stone, at dusk, with the humming in my teeth — it breathed. The lines stirred. The route I'd been following, the faint silver thread only I could see, brightened until it burned, running west and south, off the map's edge entirely, toward a coastline drawn in strokes that looked less like ink than like scar tissue.

Seraquin watched it with an expression I couldn't read, and then she closed her eyes and said something in a language that made the covenant-stone flare briefly brighter, as if answering.

"What did you say?"

"I asked the border's pardon for bringing a human across it uninvited." Her mouth curved, and the curve showed teeth that settled the question of her jaw. "It says it doesn't mind. It says it's lonely."

* * *

We made camp within sight of the stone, because Seraquin said the border's ground kept worse things away than any wall, and because she clearly intended to talk whether I was ready or not.

Sorrel flopped down near the fire with the groan of a much older animal than he pretended to be. "I'll sleep," he announced. "You two are going to talk about the covenant, and I've heard the covenant explained four times in my life, and each explanation cost me something I didn't get back. Wake me when it's over or when something tries to eat the boy. Preferably the second."

"Sorrel," I started.

"Sleeping," said Sorrel, and slept, instantly and completely, the way only creatures with clear consciences can.

Seraquin sat across the fire from me with her knees drawn up and her hands loose between them, and studied me for a while without speaking. Being studied by her was like being audited. I knew how that felt from the other side of the desk, and I'll admit I respected the technique.

"The guild sent you," she said at last. It wasn't quite an accusation. It was the setting-out of terms.

"No one sent me. I ran."

"You carried their map out of their house. You fled their enforcers. And you are wearing guild-issue boots with the maker's stamp still on the sole." She nodded at my feet. "Nothing about you is free of them yet. That matters, for what I have to tell you. Because the last man who came to me wearing guild boots went back to them, and I spent forty years wondering if I chose wrongly in trusting him."

"I'm not going back."

"They all say that. Usually sincerely." She fed a stick to the fire. "So. Ask your questions, keeper of ledgers. You'll have them anyway; better they come out of my mouth than fester in yours."

I had dozens. I asked the one that mattered.

"What is the fire? Really. Cyrus — the guildmaster — he called it the ember that binds the covenant. But that's a ledger-entry phrase. What *is* it?"

Seraquin was quiet long enough that the fire had opinions.

"When the world was younger and stupider," she said, "there were two realms occupying the same space, the way a song and the silence between notes occupy the same air. The beast-realm and the human realm. We touched everywhere and mingled constantly, and mostly that was fine, and occasionally it was war. Nine hundred years ago both sides were tired enough to try something else. So we made a contract. Not a treaty of borders — a covenant of exchange. Your realm would give ours *belief* — stories, names, fear, wonder, the attention of your kind, which is the most nourishing substance in existence. Our realm would give yours *wildness*. Rain that remembers how to fall. Rivers with opinions. Beasts worth the naming. Each side feeding the other what it cannot make alone."

"And the fire?"

"The fire is the signature." Her voice dropped. "Every covenant needs one. Something that belongs wholly to neither side, held in trust, burning at the place where the realms touch. Ours was given by dragons — dragon-fire, the oldest flame there is, fire that burns on meaning rather than wood. One flame, at the Cinder Coast, tended by the dragons in perpetuity. While it burns, the exchange holds. While the exchange holds, your rivers run and our forests dream, and neither realm starves the other."

"And now?"

"Now," she said, "the fire gutters. Has been guttering for longer than the guild admits. The dragons are nearly gone — hunted, chained, diminished. The last flame is kept by Vaelith, who is the last of the tenders, and she is old, and she is *bound*, boy, bound with sigils your masters carved, and the fire she should tend freely is being drawn off in sips. Decades of sips." Her amber eyes came up and fixed on me. "Your masked master drinks it. That is what keeps him alive past his season. Every year he takes a little, and every year the exchange runs thinner, and every year your realm forgets a little more — forgets how to believe in us — and our realm grows quieter, hungrier, smaller. The covenant isn't broken. Broken would be clean. It's being *bled*."

I sat with that. The fire popped. Somewhere beyond the border-stone, in the too-green dark, something sang three notes and stopped, as if it had forgotten the rest.

"The courier," I said. "He died to bring me a map to it. Why me? Why anyone? If the fire's failing, what does a map do?"

"A map finds the hearth. Finding it is the first thing. Someone must reach Vaelith. Someone must see the chains and the sigils and understand them." Seraquin leaned forward, and the firelight ran along her face, wolf and woman by turns. "But understand me, Theo Crane. Reaching the hearth is nothing. Anyone with legs and luck can reach a hearth. The question — the only question — is whether anyone can *reforge* the covenant. And that has a price written into its original terms, and I am going to name it to you now, plainly, because you deserve plainness and because I am too old to seduce anyone into heroism."

My mouth had gone dry. "Name it."

"The covenant renews only through a willing bearer. Fire trusts nothing it is forced into. So the renewal requires a person — mortal, of either realm — to carry the sigil into the flame itself. To stand inside the last dragon-fire and let it write the new terms on them." She paused. "And the fire takes its fee. It always has. It will take from the bearer the one thing that bearer loves most. Not their life — life is cheap, the fire has plenty of lives. It takes the dearest thing. For one bearer it was his sight. For another, her children's memory of her. For another..." she glanced, very briefly, at the sleeping fox, and said nothing more about it.

I heard myself ask, "And for me?"

"I don't know," Seraquin said. "Neither do you. That is the cruelty of it. You would walk into the flame carrying your whole heart, and the fire would choose what to keep."

The wind moved through the too-green grass. Beyond the stone, in the dark of the beast-realm, something vast exhaled — I felt it through the ground, slow as tide.

I thought of the counting-house. Of the rats whose names I knew and never used. Of Brannic's face the last time I saw him, asking me to lie to him. Of my mother, gone eleven years, whose last word to me had been a bird's call, and who I had never told about the gift we shared because telling felt like spending it.

I thought: *what do I love most?* And the terrible, honest answer rose immediately, and I looked at it, and I looked away.

"All right," I said.

Seraquin's ears — I hadn't let myself notice them before, but they were there, high-set and still — tilted toward me. "'All right' is not an answer to what I named."

"It's the only honest one I have. If I say yes properly, I'll be deciding now what the fire should take, and that's not my choice to make, and we both know it. So — all right. Take me to the Cinder Coast. Show me Vaelith and the chains. Let me see the hearth before I promise it anything." I pulled my coat tighter. "I'm a ledger-keeper, ambassador. I don't sign for goods I haven't counted."

For a long moment Seraquin said nothing. Then she laughed — a low, rough sound, startlingly warm, gone as soon as it arrived.

"Nine hundred years," she said, "and the covenant sends me a bookkeeper." She rose, unfolding to her full impossible height against the stars. "Very well, Theo Crane. Rest. Tomorrow we cross, and I warn you — the beast-realm has been starving for belief a long time, and it will know what you are the moment you step over the stone. Everything there will look at you. Everything there will *want* things from you."

"Want what?"

She looked down at me, and the firelight made her eyes banked coals.

"Everything," she said, "that your master has been drinking."
