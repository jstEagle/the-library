## 13. The Guild Comes Calling

There are three things a Vellum Guild enforcer is taught before he's taught anything else. One: the ledger is the law. Two: the guild is the family. Three: never read what you're sent to collect — carry it, don't open it.

I learned a fourth thing on my own, somewhere around my fourteenth year, watching Theo Crane balance a column of numbers for a bottled thunderstorm: some people are worth more than all three rules put together. I never told anyone that either.

So when Master Cyrus himself summoned me to the counting house at dusk and said, in that voice like honey poured over a blade, "Bring me back the map, Brannic. Or bring me back the bearer. I find I'm not particular which," I did what any good enforcer would do.

I said yes. And then I spent three days figuring out how to say no without dying for it.

* * *

You should understand something about tracking Theo Crane, because it will explain why I was in such a foul mood by the second week. Theo does not want to be found. Theo has spent their entire life wanting not to be found — I've known them since we were two orphans sharing a straw pallet in the guild dormitory, and even then, even asleep, Theo arranged themselves small. Folded up like a letter nobody was meant to read. It's a habit you can't train out of a person, and it makes them a nightmare to follow.

But here's what Cyrus doesn't know, because Cyrus has never slept four feet from anyone in his masked life: Theo Crane leaves tracks everywhere except the ground. A chandler who suddenly can't remember selling tallow. A farrier who swears a fox watched him work and *commented*. A woman in a market town who sold "a quiet person with kind eyes" half her winter stores and wouldn't stop grinning about it afterward. You don't track Theo. You track the wake they leave behind — the small, strange kindnesses that follow them like gulls after a fishing boat.

The fox was the giveaway. Half-tame, one ear chewed to lace, an animal that should have bolted from every human settlement on the road but instead sat on fence posts like a paid escort. I heard about it in three villages before I ever saw it. In the fourth village, I saw it — sitting on a milestone outside a tavern, looking at me with the flat, insulted expression of a creature who had expected better.

"Don't," the fox said.

I didn't scream. I want that noted somewhere, in some ledger. A talking fox addressed me on the Cinder Road and I did not scream, I merely sat down heavily on the milestone beside it, because my knees had opinions.

"You're the enforcer," it said. "The big one. The one who broke the slaver's jaw in Marrowgate."

"That was business."

"It was decent business." The fox licked its ruined ear. "They're three days ahead. Heading for the coast. And before you ask — yes, I'll tell them you're coming. Not out of loyalty. Out of spite. I've decided I like spite; it's the only thing keeping me upright these days."

"Why tell me where they are, then?"

The fox looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in that look that made my skin crawl — something old and tired wearing a young animal's face, like a grandfather's grief fitted into a kit's eyes.

"Because you're not going to do what he sent you to do," the fox said. "I can smell it on you. Guild-raised boys always smell like two things: the rulebook and the person who wrote it. You smell like the rulebook. He smells like the man." It hopped off the milestone. "Three days. Don't waste them being honorable. Honorable men walk straight into traps."

It limped into the hedgerow, muttering something about hounds and the general quality of my species, and I sat on that milestone until the sun went down, doing arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the counting house.

Cyrus wanted the map. Cyrus wanted the bearer. Cyrus had said *I'm not particular*, in that warm voice, and every enforcer in the guild knows what *not particular* means. It means bring back whatever's easier to explain.

And the thing was — the thing I kept turning over like a coin with two bad sides — I had already lied to myself about this job once. When the courier died in the alley behind the salt door, and the enforcers swept the street, and I stood over that scorched map-shaped absence in the dead man's grip, I had looked Theo Crane in the eye the next morning and seen a lie sitting behind their face like a bird behind glass. And I had said nothing. I had gone to breakfast and eaten my porridge and told myself: *whatever Theo is carrying, it isn't mine to know.*

That was the lie. That was the comfortable blindness I'd been begging for my whole life. And now the guild had handed me a writ that said, in effect: *open your eyes, or put them out.*

* * *

I caught up with them at the covenant-stone.

I knew it would be there, because everyone knows the stories — the border stone between realms, older than the guild, older than the kingdoms it divides, a grey fang of rock in a valley where the grass grows wrong. What I didn't know was that someone would be waiting there besides Theo. Someone who made the hair on my arms stand up and take notice.

She was standing in the shadow of the stone when I came down the slope — a wolf the size of a pony, or a woman the shape of a wolf, depending on how you squinted at her, with amber eyes that had clearly watched empires be born and die and hadn't enjoyed most of them. She didn't growl. She just looked at me the way a magistrate looks at a defendant who has already been convicted and is merely awaiting sentencing.

"You're the hound," she said. Her voice was like gravel rolled in honey.

"I'm an enforcer."

"You were going to say that eventually. I saved us both time." She turned her great head. "Theo. Your guild has come calling."

Theo stepped out from behind the stone, and I got my first look at them in six weeks, and gods, they looked *awful*. Thin as a ledger line. Shadows under their eyes deep enough to lose coins in. But they stood straighter than I'd ever seen them stand, and there was a map case strapped across their chest like a shield, and when they saw me their face did three things at once — gladness, guilt, and fear — so fast that none of them got to finish.

"Brannic." Their voice cracked on the second syllable. "How did you—"

"The fox sold you out. Well. Gave you away free, actually, which is worse."

"That absolute—" Theo pinched the bridge of their nose. "Of course. Of course he did. Did he at least charge you for the mileage?"

"He charged me with a prophecy, if that counts." I stopped ten paces off. Close enough to talk. Far enough that the wolf-woman could reach me in one bound if she felt like it, which I suspected she did feel like, constantly, as a hobby. "Theo. I have a writ. Signed by Cyrus himself. Retrieve the map or kill the bearer. Those were my options. He was very clear. He was almost cheerful about it, which for him is practically weeping."

Theo's hand drifted toward the map case. The wolf-woman's lips peeled back from teeth the length of my fingers.

"But I'm not going to do that," I said, "so you can put your hand down before you hurt yourself."

Silence. The wind moved through the wrong-growing grass. Theo stared at me the way people stare at ledgers that won't balance.

"You're not?"

"No."

"Why not?" And here's the thing that cracked something in my chest: they asked it honestly. Not suspiciously. Honestly. Like the possibility that I might choose them had genuinely never occurred to them, after twenty-odd years of me choosing them daily in small invisible ways — extra bread at supper, the blame for a broken window, the watch rotation swapped so they'd sleep through the worst of winter. Twenty years of choosing, and it had apparently all been conducted in a language Theo couldn't hear.

"Because I'm tired," I said. "Because I ran your wake down three hundred miles of road and every village had the same story — the quiet guild clerk who fixed a farmer's account book for free, who talked a mob out of killing a 'witch' who turned out to be a woman with a birthmark, who left food out for strays in towns that couldn't feed themselves. And I thought: I have spent my whole career collecting impossible things for men who lock them in boxes. And here's Theo Crane, leaving impossible things lying around loose everywhere they go, fixing things, freeing things, and getting thinner and more terrified every mile." I swallowed. "I did the math, Theo. I'm better at math than anyone gives me credit for. The sums don't favor Cyrus."

"They'll kill you," Theo whispered. "Brannic, if you defect — there's no coming back from that. He'll—"

"I know what he'll do. I've done it to other people. That's rather the point."

The wolf-woman made a sound that might have been a laugh, filtered through centuries of disapproval. "He speaks plainly. I begin to tolerate him."

"High praise," I said. "Don't spend it all at once."

* * *

We sat by a fire that night — a small one, because Theo flinched whenever flames got tall, which I filed away with everything else I didn't yet understand. The wolf-woman, whose name was Seraquin and who tolerated questions the way a mountain tolerates weather, had melted into the dark to walk the border. Which left the two of us, and twenty years of unsaid things, and one object I'd carried against my skin since the counting house.

I took off my glove first. Then the ring.

Every guild member gets one at induction. Iron, plain, stamped with the sigil of the scales-and-quill. We're told it marks belonging. What they don't tell you — what you learn only if you rise high enough, and I had risen higher than I ever wanted — is that the rings are keys. Every door in the guild answers them. The archive doors. The holding cells. And the vault beneath the counting house, the one that isn't on any floor plan, the one where Cyrus keeps the acquisitions too valuable or too terrible for the open ledgers.

I'd opened that vault exactly twice. Both times I'd told myself I saw only what the manifest described. Bottled specimens. Curiosities. *Carry it, don't open it.* Rule three, wrapped around rule one, buried under rule two.

"Brannic." Theo was staring at the ring in my palm like it was a live coal. "What are you doing."

"What it looks like." I held it out. "This opens his vault. The real one, under the east wing. If whatever you're carrying leads where I think it leads — and I think it leads to the coast, and the coast leads to fire, and fire leads to whatever Cyrus has been bleeding out of the world for thirty years while I signed receipts — then you're going to need to get inside that vault. Because I've seen the manifests, Theo. Even the ones I pretended not to read. There are bottles down there labeled in a way that will make you sick. And one of them—" I stopped. Made myself go on. "One of them is catalogued under a name I recognized. Crane. First initial M."

Theo went very still. The kind of still that isn't calm — it's the stillness of a bowstring.

"Your mother," I said quietly, "was listed as an acquisition. Eleven years ago. I was on gate duty that week. I remember the cart."

For a long moment Theo didn't breathe. When they finally did, it came out shaking, and they pressed the heel of their hand against their mouth, and I looked away to give them the privacy of the dark, because some griefs shouldn't have witnesses even when they're witnessed by everyone.

When Theo spoke again, their voice was scraped raw but level. "Why give me this? Why now? You could walk back tomorrow, hand Cyrus my location, keep everything. Your rank. Your pension. Your clean conscience."

"My conscience hasn't been clean in years, Theo. I've just kept it dusted." I closed their fingers around the ring. Their hands were cold. They were always cold; I used to steal them blankets in the dormitory and pretend I hadn't. "Here's the truth of it, since we're apparently trading truths tonight. Cyrus gave me an order, and for the first time in my life, I heard it clearly. Not the words — the words were simple enough. I heard what was underneath. He wasn't asking me to retrieve a map. He was asking me to bring him a friend so he could burn them slowly, and he assumed I'd do it, because he assumes everyone can be priced. And the worst part—" My voice caught. I pushed through it. "The worst part is he was nearly right. I walked three hundred miles trying to find a way to obey and stay decent. There isn't one. There never was. I just hadn't let myself finish the sum."

Theo's hand closed tight around the ring. They were crying, silently, the way they'd cried as a child — no sound, no drama, just tears like a leaking roof.

"You idiot," they said. "You beautiful, stubborn idiot. He'll hunt you."

"Let him. I've been hunted by debt collectors and one very persistent goose. I'll manage." I managed a grin, because somebody had to, and Theo laughed wetly, and for a moment we were just two guild orphans again on a pallet by a drafty window, before the world grew masks and maps and vaults full of stolen voices.

Then Theo's face changed. Went sharp. They looked past me, down the valley, toward the distant smudge of the Cinder Coast, and when they turned back there was something in their eyes that scared me more than Cyrus ever had — resolve, the pure and terrible kind, the kind that pays retail for wholesale damage.

"Brannic," they said. "There's something I have to do at the coast. Something that can't be undone, and probably can't be survived. And I need you to hear me out before you argue, because Seraquin will argue, and the fox will argue, and I cannot survive three arguments."

"Tell me," I said.

And they told me. About dragon-fire. About the covenant. About chains carved with guild sigils and a price written in the only currency Theo had left. And I sat there in the wrong-grass dark, listening to my oldest friend volunteer for a death dressed up as a duty, and I understood — with a clarity that felt like a wound opening — exactly what it cost to finally choose truth over loyalty.

It cost everything. That was the price. It had always been the price.

"All right," I said, when they finished, and their faces lit with hope, and I hated to snuff it, but I owed them honesty now if I owed them nothing else. "All right. But I'm coming with you. And Theo — when we get there, whatever the hearth wants, whatever the terms are — we renegotiate. I've read enough contracts to know a bad one when it's read aloud to me. Nobody volunteers alone. Not you. Not anymore."

Down the valley, far off, something howled — long and low, a sound with old grief folded into it, and beneath it, fainter, another voice answered, thin and bitter and unmistakably vulpine, complaining about the noise.

Theo stood. Held out the ring-hand, and I clasped it, iron to iron, oath enough.

"To the coast, then," I said. "Try to keep up. One of us has to look competent when the dragons arrive."

Behind us, in the dark, the fox muttered: "Neither of you does. But it's sweet that you try."

* * *

We walked through the night, and the land got quieter with every mile.

I didn't understand it at first — kept thinking my ears were blocked, that the road dust had gotten into them. But by dawn I knew it was the world itself. No birdsong. No insect-hum in the hedgerows. Rivers running silent as spilled glass, as if they'd forgotten water was supposed to speak. Once, passing a fallow field, I saw a hare sitting motionless in the furrows, staring at nothing, its mouth moving silently — chewing, maybe, or saying something that no longer had anywhere to go.

"The fire's failing," Theo said quietly, watching the hare. "Seraquin says the beasts are going silent first. Then the rivers forget to flow. Then—" They didn't finish.

I walked faster.

Because here's what I know, and what I'd learned too late and too well: you can serve an institution your whole life and never once look at what it's built on. I had guarded the doors of a house that was eating the world, and collected its wages, and polished its floors, and called the eating *business*. Well. The business was coming due. The ledger was unbalancing on a scale no counting house could hold.

Somewhere ahead, past the last hills, the sea waited, and a dying dragon, and a master with a smooth-burned face and a vault full of voices. Somewhere behind us, the guild would already have noticed my silence, and riders would be saddling, and Cyrus would be pouring honey over a new blade.

Let him come. I'd made my choice on a milestone, talking to a fox, and I found — to my considerable surprise — that I could breathe with it. Heavy, but breathable. Like carrying a pack you finally, finally set down properly on both shoulders instead of dragging it one-handed.

"Hold on, Theo," I murmured, mostly to myself, as the first grey light found the road. "Hold on. The cavalry's just late. It's always late. That's the trouble with cavalry."

Ahead, the sea-smell reached us — salt and ash and something older, something burning low and patient, like a hearth waiting for its keeper.

We hurried toward it, the three of us and our ghosts, while behind us the world went quiet as a held breath.
