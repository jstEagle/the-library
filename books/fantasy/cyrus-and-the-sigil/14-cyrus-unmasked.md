## 14. Cyrus Unmasked

There is a particular ritual to the end of a day, and I have kept it longer than most kingdoms have existed.

The counting house quiets by degrees. First the clerks go, their quills laid down like sleeping birds. Then the enforcers, Brannic's boots heavy on the stairs, the others lighter. Then the lamps are trimmed, one by one, until only the sanctum's light remains — and then, at last, I lock the door, draw the shutters, and perform the one act of honesty available to a man in my position.

I take off the mask.

People imagine the mask is the disguise. They imagine beneath it there is a face, and that the face is the truth. They are half right, which is more credit than most of them deserve. Beneath the mask there is a face, yes. It is simply not a face anyone could call mine. It is not anyone's face at all anymore. It is a face that fire finished a very long time ago — burned smooth, featureless as an egg, the skin pulled taut over the architecture of a skull that once belonged to a boy with a crooked nose and a cowlick his mother despaired of.

I keep the mask on in public because a smooth face frightens people, and frightened people ask questions. I keep the mask beautiful because beauty is a kind of lock. No one looks behind a beautiful door.

The mirror in the sanctum is old silver, spotted like a thrush's egg. I do not need it. I know what I will see. But ritual is ritual, and a man who has lived as long as I have learns that the rituals are the only things holding the shape of a life together.

There I am. Smooth as river stone. Eyes still mine — the fire took everything except the eyes, and I have never decided whether that was mercy or cruelty. The eyes are grey, the grey of ash that has not yet decided to be something else. They were blue once. My mother said they were the color of the sky over the Cinder Coast in summer, and I believed her, because I was seven, and because mothers do not lie about the sky.

* * *

You will want the story now. Everyone wants the story, eventually. They think it will explain me. It will not explain me — nothing explains me, least of all to myself — but you shall have it anyway, because I am alone, and the sanctum is warm, and an old man's memory is the one vault even I cannot lock.

I was born in a village that does not exist anymore. This is not a figure of speech. I have checked. I have walked the ground where it stood, on three separate occasions across four centuries, and each time there was less of it: first the char, then the grass, then the grass forgotten, until at last there was only a valley with a certain quality of silence that only I could hear and grieve.

The dragons came over the mountains in the autumn I was seven. Not to burn us. That is the joke of it, the joke I have carried like a stone in my shoe for four hundred years. They were dying, and they came to the valley to die, the way beasts do — away from the eyes of their own kind, in a place that meant nothing to anyone.

Except that it meant something to us. It was our valley. Our barley, our goats, our church with its bell that rang flat. And a dozen dying dragons, each of them large as a cathedral, each of them carrying enough fire to light a city for a century, lay down among our fields and began to go out.

Do you know what a dying dragon looks like? I do. I can describe it to you in the kind of detail that gets a man called mad. Their scales go grey from the edges inward, like paper burning in reverse. Their breath, which should be a furnace, comes out as a warm wind that smells of hot metal and, strangely, of cinnamon. And their eyes — their eyes stay bright almost to the very end. That is the worst of it. The fire leaves the body first and lingers in the eyes, as if it is looking for a way out, as if it knows it is about to end and would like, just once, to see the sky.

I watched them for nine days. The village elders said stay indoors. The priest said they were a plague. My mother said nothing at all, because she was already dead by then — the winter had taken her, along with two of my sisters, and it was this, I think, that made the dragons so unbearable to me. I had watched fire's opposite take everyone I loved. I was not going to stand by and watch fire itself die too.

I went out to them on the fourth day. I was a child; I did not know I should have been afraid. The nearest dragon was an old female, her flank rising and falling like a hill breathing. I put my hand on her scale, and the scale was warm, and I said — I remember saying it, I remember the exact foolishness of it — "Don't go."

She opened one eye. That bright, desperate, guttering eye. And she looked at me, a seven-year-old boy with a cowlick and no mother, and she said — dragons speak, did you know that? Not in words. In something older than words, something you feel in the marrow rather than hear in the ear. She said: *Little one. The fire does not die. It only goes where it is carried.*

I did not understand her. Of course I did not understand her. I was seven. I thought she was promising to stay.

On the ninth day the last dragon died, and the valley went cold in a way that cold had never gone before — a cold with a hole in the middle of it, a cold shaped exactly like the thing that was missing. And I stood in the ash of the fields — for their bodies had burned down to ash as they died, all that fire going somewhere, though where, I did not ask — and I made my oath.

I swore I would never be powerless again. I swore I would never watch something precious gutter out while I stood with empty hands. I was seven years old, and I swore it with the whole furious, grieving, bottomless seriousness of a child, and the universe — which has a cruel sense of humor and an excellent memory — was listening.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about oaths made by children: they are binding. Not magically — or not only magically. They bind because the child grows around them, the way a tree grows around a fence post, until you cannot tell where the boy ends and the vow begins. I am not a man who kept an oath. I am an oath that learned to walk and wear a beautiful mask.

It took me sixty years to learn how fire could be carried. Sixty years of alchemists' hovels and hermits' caves and one very instructive decade among the beast-speakers, before they were culled, before they became the thing the Guild pretends it never did. I learned that dragon-fire is not a substance. It is a *covenant* — a living agreement between flame and world, and agreements, unlike substances, can be amended. Renegotiated. Siphoned, if one is careful, and I was very careful. I have had centuries to be careful.

The first time I took fire from a dying thing, I wept. I want that on the record, in whatever ledger the universe keeps. I knelt beside a griffin with a broken wing, in a wood that is now a quarry, and I held its head, and I took the last of its warmth into myself, and I wept like the child I had been. I told myself it was grief. It was not grief. It was relief. That is the difference between me and the monsters in the stories, and it is also, if I am honest, the thing that makes me worse than them: the monsters do not weep, but they also do not know what they are taking. I have always known.

The face went first. Fire taken secondhand is not clean fire; it comes with a price written in the fine print, and my fine print was my skin. It did not hurt, particularly. It was like watching frost form on a window in reverse — the features softening, smoothing, going under. I was thirty-one. I remember looking in a innkeeper's mirror and seeing the boy from the valley disappear, and I remember thinking: *good.* Because a face is a debt. A face is every person who ever looked at you and expected something. My mother expected a son who would grow old. The fire and I came to a better arrangement.

The centuries after that were — what? I will not pretend they were torment. They were interesting. I built the Vellum Guild out of a bankrupt map-seller's shop and a talent for being the most reasonable voice in any room. I collected impossible things, which is a respectable way of saying I collected the world's orphans: the griffins, the storms, the sleeping rivers, the selkies, the voices. Every one of them a small fire, and every one of them, in my keeping, kept from going out. That is how I sleep at night, when I sleep at all. I am not a thief. I am an *archive.* The world forgets; I do not. The world lets things gutter; I do not. Somewhere in the vaults below this very floor there are voices that would have been silence forever, if not for me.

And if the keeping serves me too — if the fire I have banked for four hundred years has kept my heart beating and my mind sharp and my hand steady on the tiller — well. An archive must be maintained. Even the archivist is an artifact.

* * *

Tonight, though. Tonight the ritual goes wrong, the way rituals have begun to go wrong all season, and I stand before the spotted mirror with the mask in my hands and I admit — to the mirror, to the ash-grey eyes, to the boy who is still in there somewhere, furious and seven — that something has changed.

The map.

The courier died well; I will grant him that. He led my hounds through half the kingdom and died with the word *dragon-fire* in his mouth like a sacrament. But he was carrying it *to* someone, and my hounds lost the trail at the salt door, and ever since I have had the feeling — the old feeling, the valley feeling, the cold-with-a-hole-in-the-middle feeling — that the map is in my house.

And that the person holding it is the little clerk with the careful ledgers and the eyes that never quite meet mine. Crane. Theo Crane. Discreet, punctual, invisible — the perfect clerk, so perfectly invisible that I promoted them into my reach, because I have learned that the best place to watch a suspicious thing is from very close, with a warm hand on its shoulder.

I have been telling myself it is nothing. I have been telling myself that for a month. But tonight, standing maskless before the mirror, I finally let myself think the thought I have been not-thinking:

What if the map breathes? What if it shows its route only to ears that can hear the beasts? And what if the quiet little clerk, the one who signs the receipts without flinching and feeds the rats in the cellar when they think no one is watching —

Ah. There it is. There is the thought, out in the open at last, smooth as my face.

I am not angry. I want to be clear about that, because everyone assumes I will be angry. Anger is for men with short ledgers. I have been robbed before — by empires, by popes, by a pirate queen whose name is now only a wine label — and I have learned that theft is simply the world's way of introducing a negotiation.

But I confess the mirror shows me something I did not expect. Under the ash-grey eyes, in the smooth face that cannot frown, there is — hope. That is the obscenity of it. That is the thing I will never say aloud in daylight. If the clerk can hear the beasts, then the beasts are still speaking. And if the beasts are still speaking, then the fire at the Cinder Coast is not yet out, and the covenant can still be — amended. Renegotiated. *Siphoned*, one last time, one great final draught, enough to carry me through another four centuries of keeping the world's precious things from guttering out.

The boy in the valley swore he would never watch something precious die with empty hands.

I look at my hands. They are very steady. They have always been steady. That is what the fire bought, and it was not a bad price, and I have never once regretted it, and I am lying, I have regretted it every single night for four hundred years, and I do it anyway, and that — that is the whole of me, the entire ledger, the sum and balance of Cyrus.

I set the mask back on. It fits the way grief fits: perfectly, and forever.

Tomorrow I will summon the clerk. I will be warm. I will be reasonable — I am always reasonable; it is my best disguise, better than the mask. And I will set before them a bargain so gentle, so generous, so *kind* — a mother's voice, perhaps, restored from the vaults; I keep everything, I told you — that only a monster would refuse it.

And if they refuse — well.

I have watched many precious things gutter out. I have made my peace with being the last light in the valley.

But I confess, as I turn down the sanctum lamp and the smooth face in the dark mirror goes to ash and shadow: I find, this once, that I am hoping to be refused.

The boy in me would like, just once, to be proven wrong.

* * *

In the vaults below, in a bottle of green glass on the third shelf, a voice that has been silent for eleven years stirs in its sleep and whispers the opening line of a covenant older than my oath, older than my face, older than my name —

and I do not hear it.

But the rats do.
