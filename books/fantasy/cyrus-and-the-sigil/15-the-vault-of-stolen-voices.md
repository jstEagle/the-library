## 15. The Vault of Stolen Voices

The Vellum Guild had taught me arithmetic before it taught me kindness, and I had always assumed that was because arithmetic came first in the day. Standing now at the mouth of the Undercroft stairs with Brannic's guild-ring warm against my palm, I understood the truth: they taught us numbers first because numbers are how you hide a body.

Every impossible thing the guild ever traded passed through a ledger. Every ledger balanced. And beneath the counting house, beneath even the salt cellars where the caged griffins sulked and the bottled storms muttered in their sleep, there was a room that appeared in no ledger at all — a room whose whole purpose was to make certain that everything else did.

"The Vault of Stolen Voices," Brannic said quietly, and then winced at his own words. "Sorry. I shouldn't have said it like that."

"How else would you say it?"

"Vault of... acquisitions. Special holdings." He rubbed the back of his neck. "The way Master Cyrus says it."

"Master Cyrus also says 'please' when he has people flogged," I said. "Words are just coats. You should see what's underneath."

He looked at me for a long moment in the dark of the stairwell, and I realized I'd said something I hadn't meant to say aloud — something that belonged to a person who had already seen what was underneath. Which I had. The selkie in her crate. The sigils carved into a dragon's chains. The map drawn on skin.

"Yara," he started, using my old name, my guild name, the one that fit me like a borrowed coat—

"Theo," I corrected him, gently. "If we're doing this, do it right."

"Theo." He tried it out like a key in a stiff lock. "Theo. Right. Are you sure about this? Because once we're down those stairs, there's no version of the story where you're still the quiet ledger-keeper who never made trouble."

I thought about the fox, bleeding somewhere on the Cinder Coast road with Seraquin's hands pressed to his wound. I thought about a hawk's warning — *the masked one eats fire* — and about my mother, who had gone into the guild's "care" eleven years ago and never come out, and whose name I had found in exactly one ledger entry, filed under *Processed*, which is a word that sounds administrative until you understand it means *emptied*.

"I was never the quiet ledger-keeper," I said. "That was the coat."

* * *

Brannic's ring opened doors that keys could not, because the ring wasn't a key at all. It was an oath, cast in iron and old magic, and the vault recognized oaths the way a dog recognizes its master's step. He pressed it to the black door at the bottom of the stairs and the metal drank the light from it, and then the door simply wasn't there anymore — just a doorway breathing cold air at us, air that smelled of wax and dust and, faintly, impossibly, of sea-salt and pine and fur. A hundred small weathers. A hundred small wilds, sealed in glass.

We went in.

The vault was not what I expected, though I couldn't have told you what I'd expected. Not a dungeon. Something more like a library, if libraries were built by someone who loved books only for their spines. Shelves rose into darkness, and on every shelf stood bottles — hundreds of them, thousands, each no bigger than a fist, each glowing faintly from within with a light that moved. Not steady like candlelight. Moving like breath. Like speech.

"Oh," Brannic said, very softly, and stopped walking.

I knew what he was hearing, because I was hearing it too — not with my ears but with the thing under my ribs I had spent my whole life pretending wasn't there. The gift, waking up in the presence of all this, straining toward the shelves like a dog toward home.

They weren't empty bottles. They were voices. Bottled voices. Every beast the guild had ever taken apart for parts — every selkie stripped of her song, every raven emptied of its words, every creature "processed" so that its magic could be decanted and sold while the animal itself went on living, hollowed, docile, tradeable.

A bottle near my elbow glowed green-gold, and inside it something turned over in its sleep, and I heard — felt — a voice like river-water over stones saying, over and over, patient as tide: *the north bank, the north bank, the willows remember the north bank—*

A sleeping river. We had one in inventory. I had balanced its account myself. I had never wondered what it sounded like when it talked in its sleep.

"They're alive in there," Brannic whispered. His face had gone the color of tallow. "All of them. They're not recordings, Theo, they're — it's *them*, still in there—"

"I know."

"How many?"

I looked down the endless shelves, row after row after row, vanishing into a dark that the little lights didn't so much illuminate as punctuate.

"I used to think I was good at counting," I said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about horror: it is patient. It does not grab you by the throat. It lets you walk. It lets you read labels.

And the bottles were labeled. Of course they were. Cyrus was a merchant before he was anything else, and a merchant labels his stock. Little brass plaques, engraved in the guild's tidy hand: species, acquisition date, provenance, yield. I walked the aisles reading them the way I'd read ten thousand ledgers, because reading was the only thing I knew how to do with my hands, and somewhere in the middle of the third aisle my feet stopped moving without asking my permission.

Because the plaque in front of me didn't list a species.

It listed a name.

**MAREN CRANE. Beast-speaker (human). Processed [date]. Yield: exceptional.**

The bottle on the shelf above it was larger than most. It glowed the color of lamplight through honey, and inside it something shifted — not a beast, not a shape, just a warmth, a presence, a *listening*. And then, faintly, so faintly I might have invented it, a voice. A woman's voice, humming. Off-key. Always off-key. She used to hum while she hung the washing, and our neighbor swore the sparrows would line up on the line to listen, and she'd laugh and say they were mocking her, and I'd known — even at six, even before I knew what I was — that they weren't mocking her at all.

"Maren Crane," Brannic read over my shoulder, and then he went silent, because everyone in the guild knew the name Maren Crane, or rather knew the absence of it, the cautionary tale of the beast-speaker who'd been caught and "processed" and whose child had been taken in afterward, raised in-house, kept close. Kept *watched*. Me. I was the cautionary tale's epilogue.

"That's her," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "That's my mother."

"Theo—"

"She's in a *bottle*, Brannic." I reached up. My hand was shaking. The glass was warm. Warm, like a living thing, like a cheek. "Eleven years. Eleven years I told myself she was dead, because dead was terrible but dead was *over*, and instead she's been down here the whole time, three floors below my desk, singing to herself in the dark—"

The warmth inside the glass stirred. Pressed toward my palm. And the humming stopped, and the voice came, clearer now, threaded through the glass like light through water:

*—someone's there. Someone's there. Hello? Hello, little one, is someone—*

She couldn't see me. Whatever was left of her in the bottle, it had no eyes, only ears, only the listening that had been the best part of her. But she had heard my heartbeat, maybe. Or my breath. Or whatever it is a mother hears that no one else can.

"Yes," I said, and my careful ledger-keeper's voice finally broke clean in half. "It's me. Mam. It's Theo."

The light in the bottle flared — flared like a struck match, like a window thrown open, like every hearth in a cold house lit at once — and the voice poured out in a flood, tumbling, laughing, sobbing, all of it at once:

*Oh — oh, my love, my love, you grew, I can tell, your heart's too big for your chest the way it always was, I can hear it, I can hear you — you can HEAR me — oh, sweetheart, you kept it, you kept the gift, I told them nothing, I told them nothing, do you hear me, whatever they asked, whatever they—*

"I know," I said. "I know you did. I know."

*You have to go.* The joy in her voice collapsed into urgency so fast it was like watching weather turn. *You have to go NOW, little one. He comes down here. He comes and he listens to us, he holds us up to his ear like — like a sweetmeat, and when he leaves there's less of us, do you understand, there's LESS — he's been drinking from mine for years, that's why I lose the days, that's why I forget the washing and the sparrows — he'll know you've been here, the bottles TELL him things, they tell him everything—*

"Mam—"

*The map.* Her voice dropped to a whisper that somehow filled the whole vault, that hushed even the sleeping river and the muttering storms. *You have it. I can feel it on you, dragon-skin, it sings against your pocket like a struck bell. He's been looking for it for thirty years, Theo. Thirty years he's bled us to keep himself alive long enough to find it. Don't let him bargain with you. He'll offer you things. He offered ME things, at the end, and I was strong, but he'll find the shape of YOUR want, he always finds it—*

"He already has," I said quietly. "He offered me a promotion."

*And you took it.*

"And I took it."

*Good,* she said, and I nearly dropped the bottle. *Good girl. Walk in the front door. It's the last place he'll look for a thief.*

For a moment I just breathed. The vault breathed around me — a thousand small captives, inhaling, exhaling, waiting.

"Mam," I said. "I'm going to get you out. All of you. I don't know how yet, but—"

*No.* Gentle. Terrible. *Listen to me. Some bottles break, little one. That's not the same as being saved. If it comes to it — if it comes to choosing between us and the fire—*

"Don't." 

*—choose the fire.* Her voice softened into the old humming again, the off-key washing-day tune, and I understood she was giving me the only lullaby she had left. *You were always mine, Theo Crane. But the world was mine too. Both are true. Go be true.*

* * *

Brannic gave me the walk down the aisle and back, and the minute at the shelf, and the minute after that where I stood with my forehead nearly touching the glass and my mother's warmth soaking into my skin like sun through a window. Then he put his hand on my shoulder, and it was steady, and I hated him a little for it and loved him completely.

"We need to move," he said.

"I know."

"Theo. The bottles talk to him. Your mother said—"

"I heard her."

"So if he doesn't already know we're down here, he knows the *moment* we leave, because the moment we leave is the moment the whispering starts. Which means—" He swallowed. "Which means we don't leave the way we came. We need the map-route out. The one you found. The delivery tunnel."

"The salt door," I said. "Where the courier died."

"It's poetic," Brannic said. "I hate it."

"You hate everything."

"I hate everything *accurately*."

But he was already moving, ring in hand, checking the dark corners of the vault with the economy of a man who had been an enforcer since he was twelve and had never once been allowed to wonder what he was enforcing. At the far wall he found what I'd hoped was there: a service hatch, guild-stamped, leading toward the salt cellars and the alley beyond. He pressed the ring to it. The iron drank the light. The hatch sighed open.

I looked back once. Just once. My mother's bottle glowed on its shelf among thousands, a single warm lamp in a cold congregation, and I felt her attention rest on me like a hand on my hair.

*I'll come back,* I said to her, with the gift, with everything I had. *I swear it on the covenant itself.*

And the whole vault — the river, the storms, the ravens, the selkie-song, all thousand stolen voices — answered at once, a soundless chord that ran through my bones like the first note of something enormous:

*Then hurry, bearer. The fire is almost out.*

* * *

We made the salt door. Of course we made the salt door; the guild built it for smuggling and smugglers are punctual. What we did not make was distance.

We were three steps into the alley, the night air sharp and honest on our faces, when every bell in the Vellum Guild began to ring at once — not the alarm pattern, not the fire pattern, but a third pattern I had never heard in fifteen years of counting-house life. Slow. Deliberate. Almost courteous.

Brannic froze. "What is that?"

"I don't know."

"I've been guild-raised since I could walk, Theo, and I don't know."

And then the salt door behind us opened — silently, without oath or ring, which should have been impossible — and the smell of beeswax and cold spice drifted out into the alley, and a voice followed it, warm as a hearth, gentle as a hand on a child's head.

"There you are," said Master Cyrus, stepping into the moonlight with his silver mask catching the stars. "I do wish you'd knocked. I so dislike shouting in my own house."

Behind the mask, I could feel him smiling.
