## 17. Refusal

The bottle sat on the table between us like a small amber sun.

Inside it, something moved. Not light — I want to be clear about that, because for the rest of my life people will ask me what was in the bottle, and the honest answer is *my mother*, and the dishonest answer everyone prefers is *a pretty glow*. It moved the way a voice moves when it has nowhere to go. A curl of sound, coiled tight, pressing against the glass the way a moth presses against a window. Pressing the way she must have pressed against whatever room they put her in, all those years ago, before the guild "processed" her and told the neighborhood she'd gone to relatives in the south.

I had never known my relatives in the south. I had known a counting house and a cot and a rule I'd made myself at age nine: *never let them hear you talking to the rats.*

Cyrus watched me look at the bottle. He had removed his mask — that alone should have terrified me more than it did — and beneath it his face was smooth as river stone, burned featureless by centuries of borrowed fire, so that his expressions happened entirely in his hands, his shoulders, the tilt of his head. Right now his hands were folded on the table like a man at prayer, or a man at an auction.

"Her name was Maren Crane," he said. "You were four."

"I know her name," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Do you remember her voice?"

"No." And there it was, the knife under the offer, turned gently so I could see the whole length of it. "That's rather the point, isn't it."

"Then consider what I'm offering." He turned the bottle a quarter-turn with one finger, and the coiled voice inside shifted, catching what little light the sanctum allowed, and for half a heartbeat I heard — or imagined I heard, which is worse — a hum. The kind of hum a woman makes over a cradle. The kind of hum you can't invent from nothing because you were four when it stopped.

"The map," Cyrus said. "And your silence about what you've seen. In exchange: this bottle unsealed, her voice returned to the air where it belongs. Your gift stays secret. Your position in special acquisitions stands. A life of comfort, Theo. You've seen the alternative. You signed receipts for it."

He meant the selkie. He meant the crate. He meant every ledger line I'd ever balanced without asking what the entries weighed.

Here is the thing I am not proud of, and I will write it down anyway because honest ledgers are the only kind worth keeping anymore: for one terrible moment, I wanted to say yes.

Not wanted the way you want a thing. Wanted the way a drowning man wants air — total, thoughtless, cellular. My hand actually moved. I saw it move, saw my own fingers drift toward the glass, and some detached part of me — the bookkeeper part, the part that had spent twenty years watching numbers instead of feeling them — noted the movement with interest, as though auditing someone else's greed.

Four years old. That's how old I'd been when they took her. I had spent my whole life keeping accounts for stolen things without once asking who had stolen *mine*.

"You're hesitating," Cyrus said, and there was something almost tender in it, which was the most frightening thing in the room. "That's not weakness. That's sense. Everyone sells eventually, Theo. They just differ on the price. I sold a long time ago, and look how well I've kept."

I looked at his face. His smooth, patient, well-kept face.

"You bought fire," I said. "She bought a cradle-song. I don't think we're shopping in the same market."

* * *

Let me tell you what the fox taught me, because I didn't understand it until that moment, standing in the vault of stolen voices with my hand halfway to a bottle of my mother.

Reynard — the half-tame fox who'd guided me to the coast, who'd complained about every mile of it, who'd taken a hound's bite for me and called it *an inconvenience, not a favor, don't get sentimental* — Reynard had been a man once. A sigil-bearer who refused the fire. He'd told me on his deathbed, in that dry rasp that was already more ash than voice: *the sigil doesn't kill you, boy. It takes the one thing you love most.*

I had thought that was a warning against the flame.

It wasn't. It was a warning against *this* — against the small warm bargains, the ones offered kindly, at the exact moment of your worst hunger. Cyrus had been refusing the fire his whole long life too. Refusing it and refusing it, one fair trade at a time, and every trade took the thing he loved most, until what he loved most was staying alive, and that was all he had left.

My hand came back from the bottle.

I made myself look at it — really look — while I said no, because I owed her that much. Because if I was going to leave my mother on a shelf in a burning building, I was going to do it with my eyes open.

"A voice bought with a realm's silence," I said, "is just another cage. You'd have me trade every beast in two worlds for one song. She was a beast-speaker, Master Cyrus. Do you know what she'd say to that trade?"

His stillness changed quality. It's hard to describe, the way a statue's stillness changes when it decides to become a different statue.

"She'd say yes," he said quietly. "They always did."

"Not her." And I found, saying it, that I believed it — believed it the way you believe a load-bearing wall, suddenly, completely, everything above it resting on the fact. "She talked to beasts in a city that culls beast-speakers. She knew exactly what it cost. She paid it. And she never once — " my voice cracked, and I let it, because cracking was honest — "she never once taught me to hide it out of shame. Only out of sense. There's a difference. You wouldn't know it. Everything you've ever hidden, you hid out of fear, and then you built a guild on top of the hiding place and called it civilization."

The coiled voice in the bottle pressed against the glass.

Cyrus rose. Smoothly. Without hurry. A man who had all the time that stolen fire could buy.

"That's a fine speech," he said. "Ledger-men don't usually make speeches. It's the maps that make radicals of them." He glanced toward the door, and though I heard nothing, I understood we were no longer alone in the conversation. "Brannic is outside. He'll escort you back to your quarters while I consider whether your refusal was principled or merely slow. Take the night. People often find their principles soften by morning."

I stood. My legs worked, which surprised me.

"One question," I said, at the door. "When the archives burn — and they will burn, won't they? Once I'm safely locked away and the evidence is inconvenient — does she burn with them? Or do bottles survive fire?"

For the first time since I'd met him, Cyrus looked genuinely interested in me. The way a scholar is interested in a specimen.

"You've been reading the ledgers too closely," he said. "That's my fault. I promoted you for discretion and got an auditor." He folded his hands again. "Fire is how we keep things safe, Theo. From everyone. Including themselves."

* * *

Brannic walked me down the corridor in silence.

He was good at silence, Brannic. We'd been raised in the same drafty orphan wing of the guild, two kids counting other people's fortunes, and I could read his silences the way I read ledgers — by what was missing from them. This silence was missing relief. It was missing agreement. It was the silence of a man carrying something heavy and deciding where to set it down.

We passed the archive door. Third landing, iron-banded oak, the door I'd walked past ten thousand times without wondering what was behind it. Now I knew. Bottles. Rows and rows of them, each one a voice, a song, a scream, a lullaby — the guild's true inventory, the real ledger, the one written in glass.

Brannic stopped walking.

"Don't," I said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're about to ask me to lie to you. Again. So you can keep sleeping at night." I turned to face him. Up close, in the lamplight, he looked older than twenty-six, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "I won't do it, Brann. Not this time. Ask me anything else."

His jaw worked. "Theo —"

"You begged me once," I said. "On the road. You said, *tell me the map isn't what I think it is.* And I lied, because I loved you, and because lying seemed kinder than making you choose. And do you know what happened? You chose anyway. You gave me your ring. You're standing in a corridor right now instead of reporting me. You already chose, Brannic. All that's left is admitting it."

The lamp between us guttered. Somewhere below us — deep below, in the counting house proper — a bell rang once. Then twice. Then in the quick triple-strike that every guild orphan learns before they learn their letters, because it means *lock your doors and count your people*, and it means nothing good.

Brannic went very still. "That's not the intruder call."

"No," I agreed. "Intruders come from outside."

We looked at each other, and the arithmetic finished itself in both our heads at the same time. Archives. Evidence. A bearer who'd refused a bargain and a witness who'd handed over his ring. Fire is how we keep things safe.

"They're going to burn it now," Brannic said. "Tonight. With us inside the decision, so we're either complicit or ash."

"Or ash *and* complicit. He's efficient."

Something in Brannic broke loose then — not his composure; something underneath it, older, the thing that had made him give me his ring on a cold beach while calling himself a coward. He grabbed my shoulder with one big enforcer's hand.

"The bottles," he said. "The vault seals are warded against theft, not rescue. The ward reads intent. If you go in there meaning to *steal*, it'll take your hand off. But if you go in meaning to free — "

"You've been reading the enforcement manuals' appendices."

"I get bored on stakeouts." He was already moving, pulling me down the corridor away from my quarters, away from the comfortable cage Cyrus had offered, toward the smoke that hadn't started yet but would. "Theo. Listen. Whatever happens down there — whatever you hear — "

"I know what I'll hear."

"No." He stopped at the top of the archive stair and gripped both my arms, and his eyes were wet and furious and certain. "You don't. Twenty years of voices, Theo. Hundreds of bottles. And one of them is hers, and you're going to hear her, and there won't be time, and you have to promise me you'll run when I say run even if she's — "

"Even if she's mid-word," I said. "Yes. I promise."

It was the second-worst promise I've ever made.

* * *

Smoke moves strangely in a warded building. It doesn't billow; it *pours*, obedient, following channels cut into the stone by architects who designed the guild to burn cleanly centuries before anyone decided it would need to. We came down the archive stair into a room already filling with it — not chaos, order, the fire moving along its appointed gutters like ink along a pen stroke — and the heat hit me like a hand on the chest.

The shelves stretched up into the dark. Thousands of bottles. Thousands of small amber suns, and every one of them beginning, very softly, to sing.

That's the thing no one tells you about burning voices. Heat loosens the seal-spells. The bottles don't shatter all at once — they *leak*. And so the archive, as it died, filled with everything it had ever swallowed. A wolf's grief-howl from somewhere overhead. A river's slow complaint. Children's laughter — whose children? whose laughter? — threading through a hawk's hunting cry through something vast and low and old that might have been a whale or might have been a mountain, and underneath it all, rising, a thousand small human sounds that the guild had decided were contraband.

Brannic shoved a shelf aside with his shoulder. "Which row?"

"West wall. He kept it apart." Of course he had. Special acquisitions. "Third shelf, iron bracket, the small one."

We ran. The fire ran better. It was everywhere now, sliding down the gutters, and the singing was getting louder, voices escaping their glass in fragments — half a note here, a word there — and I heard, absurdly, clearly, a fox's voice snarl *don't get sentimental* in a dead animal's cadence, and I heard my own name in a voice I didn't recognize, and I heard —

There. Iron bracket. Small bottle. Her.

The coil inside had come loose with the heat. It filled the whole glass now, pressing, and through the amber I could see it shaping itself — not words yet, not quite, but the *shape* of words, the shape of a woman leaning over a cradle in a room I couldn't remember, in a life I'd been too young to keep.

"Theo." Brannic's hand on my arm. "Thirty seconds. Maybe less."

I wrapped both hands around the bottle. The glass was hot but not unbearable — the wards held the fire off their contents, even now, even uselessly, faithful little spells guarding a library with no readers left.

*I can carry it,* I thought. *I can run with it. One bottle. I carried a map across half the realm, I can carry —*

And then the ceiling gutter directly overhead split, and a sheet of flame came down the shelf like a waterfall, and the ward-sigils on the brackets flared white and failed all together, all at once, everywhere, a whole archive's worth of protection letting go in a single breath.

The bottles began to break.

Not all of them. Not hers first. But near her — beside her, around her — glass burst in bright small detonations, and every breaking bottle released its voice into the fire, and the fire *took* the voices, drank them, burned brighter for them, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling: this was the design. Not destruction. Consumption. Even the evidence would serve him. Even the stolen songs would feed the flames that erased them.

Maren Crane's bottle glowed white at its seams.

"No," I said. Stupidly. As if the glass cared. As if the fire negotiated. "No — wait —"

The bottle broke.

Sound came out of it the way light comes out of a struck match — total, instantaneous, filling every corner of the burning dark. And it was her. It was *her*, twenty years compressed into one impossible instant, her voice whole and warm and furious and alive, and it was not a lullaby after all, it was not a hum over a cradle, it was speech, it had always been speech, she had saved her words the way I saved mine, carefully, for the thing that mattered:

*"All covenant begins in exchange: what is freely given, freely returns."*

The covenant's opening line. The first sentence Seraquin had spoken to me at the border-stone, the words older than the guild, older than Cyrus, older than the fire itself — and my mother had whispered them into a bottle twenty years ago, and I had never known why, and now I knew, oh, I knew, she had known what they'd do to her, and she had left me the terms of the war.

The voice faded. Not died — *faded*, the way a bell fades, still present in the walls, in my ribs, in the ringing air.

Brannic had me by the collar. I hadn't felt him grab me. "Now," he was shouting, "Theo, NOW —"

I ran. I left her. I left her in the fire, in the exchange, freely given, and behind me the archive sang itself out in a hundred tongues, and ahead of us the stairwell breathed smoke, and somewhere above, boots — enforcers, dozens, sealing the exits, sealing us in with the proof.

Brannic slammed his shoulder into the east window — the old loading window, the one every orphan in the guild knew was painted shut and every enforcer knew was only *painted*. Glass went out into the night. Cold air poured in, tasting of rain and river.

"After you," he gasped.

"It's three stories."

"And the fire's faster. Go."

I went. The drop knocked the air out of me and the mud broke most of the fall and Brannic landed beside me with a grunt that would have been funny any other night. Behind and above us, the Vellum Guild's archive windows glowed like a lantern full of ghosts, and the last audible voice rising from them was small and high and clear — a child's voice, or something pretending to be one — and I did not let myself wonder whose it was. Some audits you close unfinished.

We ran for the river gate, two shadows among smoke, and the rain came down at last like the sky washing its hands.

Behind us the guild burned its own memory. Ahead of us lay the Cinder Coast, the dying fire, the last dragon, and the words my mother had spent her final freedom teaching me:

*What is freely given, freely returns.*

I had given everything. It was time to go collect.
