## 19. Vaelith's Choice

I have been asked many things in my long life. I have been asked to burn cities, to spare them, to carry kings on my back and pretend not to notice when they wept into my neck-scales. I have been asked for blessings, for curses, for a single scale to hang above a doorway against ill luck — that one always amused me, since I was the ill luck.

Never, until the small human with the ledger-ink stains and the fox's blood still under her fingernails, had anyone asked me what *I* wanted.

Brannic cut the last chain at dawn, or what passed for dawn on the Cinder Coast, where the sky has been the color of old ash for three years running. The guild-smiths had made those chains well; I will grant them that much, grudgingly, the way one grants a worthy enemy a clean death. Sigil-iron, cold-forged, each link inscribed with a covenant-word twisted backward. They did not hold my body. My body could have snapped them in the first century. They held my fire.

For sixty years I lay in this caldera and burned anyway, because that is the one thing dragons cannot choose not to do. Fire is breath is thought is name. So I burned, and the chains drank it, and somewhere far away a masked man sipped from my flame like wine from a decanter and called it *stewardship*.

The links fell away one by one, ringing on the black glass, and I will confess something no dragon admits aloud: when the last of them dropped, I did not rise. I stayed as I was — coiled, diminished, my wings folded like letters no one would ever read again — because rising felt like a thing one should do with more dignity than I currently possessed. I was, by dragon measure, a guttering candle. By human measure, I suppose, I was still a mountain that breathed. Perspective is everything.

"You're free," Brannic said. He stood there holding the bolt-cutters like a man who has just realized he's helped a wolf out of a trap and isn't sure whether gratitude or teeth come next.

"I am unchained," I corrected him. "Freedom is a larger property."

He flinched. Good. A man who flinches at the truth is a man still capable of learning it.

Then the small one climbed up onto the hearth-stone — she climbs everything, that one, ledgers and fences and finally, I suspect, the whole architecture of her own fear — and looked at me the way no one had looked at me in sixty years. Not with hunger. Not with terror. With the particular, terrible attention of an accountant.

She had heard my heart, I think. Beast-speakers do that; they listen past the skin to the arithmetic underneath. And what she heard in mine frightened her, because what she heard was a sum that didn't balance.

"Vaelith," she said. "Seraquin says you know how the fire can be reignited."

"I know," I said, "several ways. That is the trouble with knowing things. You become responsible for all of them."

* * *

Let me tell you about the covenant, since the humans who broke it never understood it, and the humans who might mend it deserve better than riddles.

When the world was younger and less embarrassed about its magic, the two realms were not two realms. They were one house with two wings, and the door between them was fire. Dragon-fire, specifically. Not the vulgar burning kind — any hatchling can make that — but the first kind, the kind that remembers. Every flame carries memory the way smoke carries scent. The oldest fire remembers everything: every beast that ever lived, every song, every name spoken in the dark before names were common. We are not merely animals who happen to be enormous. We are the archive. The beast-realm keeps its past in us the way your realm keeps its past in paper and stone and grudges.

The covenant bound the two realms through that remembering fire. As long as it burned at the border, each realm could see the other truly — beasts could cross without becoming monsters, humans could cross without becoming conquerors, and exchange remained exchange rather than theft. It was, in the old tongue, called simply the Hearth. No poetry. Poetry came later, when people began forgetting what they were being poetic *about*.

Dragons kept the Hearth. There were nine of us once, then four, then two. We did not guard it out of duty. Duty is a human invention for making servitude sound voluntary. We kept it because our fire *was* it — there was no separation between the flame and ourselves, any more than there is separation between a singer and the song while the singing lasts.

My brother died forty years ago. Killed cleanly, they tell me, by guild enforcers with sigil-iron harpoons and a contract signed by a man wearing a kind face. Since then, I alone have fed the Hearth, and the Hearth has eaten me, and the masked man has eaten the difference.

Do you begin to see the shape of my problem? A single dragon's fire, drained nightly through chains, cannot remember enough. A covenant runs on memory the way a lamp runs on oil. When the memory runs low, the realms stop seeing each other. Rivers forget their courses — your small fox told me that, dying, in a voice like dry leaves, and I have thought of it every hour since. Beasts fall silent mid-song. Humans wake unable to recall their mothers' faces and blame grief, which is a merciful mistake.

And so I have lain here doing arithmetic for sixty years, and I have arrived, over and over, at the same ugly answer. Which brings us to why the little accountant was looking at me as if I were a ledger about to lie to her.

* * *

"There is a way," I said, "in which I could reignite the Hearth alone. Tonight. Without the sigil, without a bearer, without further inconvenience to anyone."

Theo said nothing. She had learned already — I could see it in her, that hard-won wariness — that when someone begins a sentence with *there is a way*, the rest of the sentence is where the knife lives.

"The fire must be fed," I continued. "Fed with memory. Great gouts of it. And I am nearly empty, but—" and here I paused, because even now it shames me, "—the beast-realm is not. Its memories live in its creatures, its stones, its sleeping rivers and singing hills. A dragon of sufficient will could open herself wide and draw it all in. Every remembered thing in the beast-realm, poured through me into the Hearth in a single night of burning. The flame would blaze high enough to be seen from both worlds' ends. The covenant would stand for another age."

"That sounds like a solution," Theo said carefully.

"It is a solution the way a flood is a bath." I unfolded myself at last, joints cracking like green wood, and rose to my full height against the ash-colored sky, so she could see how much of me there was left to do such a thing with. Less than half. Less than a third. An archive reduced to a pamphlet. "If I burn the realm's memories to fuel the realm's protection, the realm survives and forgets itself entirely. The beasts would live. They would eat, mate, raise young, die. But nothing would be passed down. No songs. No histories. No names for the stars. Each generation would begin from nothing and end from nothing, animals in the plain sense, rich and blank as eggs. Your realm would keep its past. Mine would become a place with no yesterdays at all."

The silence after that had texture. Seraquin, down by the covenant-stone, had gone very still in the way only the very old can go still. Even the wind seemed embarrassed.

"And you've been lying here sixty years," Theo said slowly, "knowing that was the only way you could fix it yourself."

"Knowing it was the only way *I* could fix it myself, yes. There is a distinction, and it is the entire reason you are standing on my caldera instead of watching the world quietly end from a counting house." I lowered my great head until one eye — gold going grey at the rim, don't remark upon it — was level with her. "Tell me, keeper of impossible accounts. If a debt can be paid, but only out of an account that belongs to everyone — whose hand signs the receipt?"

* * *

I want it understood that asking was the hardest thing I have done since my brother died. Harder than the chains. Chains only require endurance, and endurance is pride wearing work clothes. Asking requires you to stand in front of someone smaller than you and admit that smallness is not the same as insufficiency.

I am the last dragon. Everything my kind was — the nine flames of the first morning, the songs older than continents, Vaelith-who-remembers, they called me, Vaelith-the-archive — all of it lives or dies with whatever I decide next. A creature in my position is expected to be magnificent. To seize the choice, make the sacrifice, burn grandly and let history sort out the ethics. Dragons in human stories always get the grand gesture. Humans write stories about the things they wish we'd do instead of eating their sheep.

But I had done my arithmetic honestly for sixty years, and honest arithmetic does not care about magnificence.

If I took the realm's memories, I saved the world and murdered its soul, and I would be the last dragon and the last witness both, alone forever in a paradise of amnesiacs. If I did nothing, the fire guttered out within a season, and the realms drifted apart like rafts, and the beasts faded into true animals while the humans forgot magic had ever been real, and everything ended not with a scream but with a shrug. Both answers balanced. Neither was right.

There was a third column in the ledger. It required a bearer. A living creature, willing, who carried the sigil into the fire's heart — not as fuel, but as *witness*. That was the covenant's original design, the part the masked man had spent decades ensuring no one remembered: the Hearth renews itself through exchange, never extraction. The bearer gives what they love most, freely, and the fire gives back renewal, freely, and the two gifts cancel into permanence. No theft. No forgetting. Both realms keep their pasts, because someone chose to pay from their own account instead of the commons'.

The price, of course, is the cruelty in the design. The sigil takes the one thing the bearer loves most. Not their life — life is cheap, the fire has plenty of lives. The beloved thing. For a king it might be his crown. For a mother, her child. For a beast-speaker—

Well. You can do that sum yourselves. I watched her do it, standing there on the hearth-stone with the wind pulling at her coat, and I hated the mathematics that put the equation in front of her.

"I won't order it," I said. "I want that recorded somewhere, in one of your precious ledgers. The last dragon does not command the small creature. The last dragon *asks*."

"Ask, then," Theo said. Her voice shook. Her spine didn't.

So I, Vaelith, who watched the first morning cool into the world, bent my neck to a human covered in ink stains and fox blood, and asked.

"Carry the sigil into my fire. Pay from your own account, so neither realm pays from theirs. I cannot promise it will not break you. I can only promise that what breaks will be yours alone to give, and that I will spend it as carefully as anything has ever been spent." I drew a breath that stirred ash off the caldera floor in grey ghosts. "You may refuse. If you refuse, I will do the ugly arithmetic myself tonight, and the beast-realm will keep its body and lose its memory, and I will bear that crime so you needn't. Either way, the fault stops with me. That, too, I ask you to record."

* * *

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Brannic shifted his weight, and long enough that Seraquin limped closer across the black glass, silver-maned and grave, carrying the covenant's original terms in a head older than most nations.

I did not press her. Pressing is what the kind-faced do.

But I will tell you what I saw, because a dragon sees more than she says, usually. I saw her look at her hands — ink-stained, fox-blooded, the hands of someone who had signed receipts for stolen selkies and hated herself precisely as much as was useful. I saw her mouth move around the word *voice*, silently, the way you test whether a tooth still hurts. I saw her glance at Brannic and then away, quickly, which told me the sigil's question had already found its answer inside her, and the answer frightened her more than the fire did.

And I saw — this is the part I will keep until my ashes cool — I saw her straighten. Not the way soldiers straighten, pushed upright by discipline. The way scales tip. The way a decision settles into a body and becomes load-bearing.

"The fox," she said at last. Her voice was rough. "It told me the sigil takes the thing you love most. It knew because it refused, once. It was a man, and it refused, and it spent three hundred years as a fox instead." She swallowed. "It also told me the realms were worth it. It just couldn't be the one to pay. And then it apologized for that with its last breath, and I told it there was nothing to forgive, and I meant it, and I still mean it. But Vaelith—"

"Yes?"

"I've spent my whole life hiding what I love. First the gift, then the map, then every person I cared about, filed away where no one could take them from me." She laughed, short and terrible. "Hiding turns out to be a terrible vault. Everything got taken anyway. My mother. The fox. Almost Brannic. Almost me." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and absolutely clear. "So I'm done keeping accounts I'm afraid to open. Ask me properly. One more time. I want to say yes out loud."

The old wolf reached us then and said nothing, because Seraquin understands ceremony better than any creature alive. Brannic pulled the guild-ring from his finger and held it out flat on his palm, offering it as a key or a witness, whichever the moment required.

And I — the last dragon, the archive, the guttering hearth of two worlds — opened my jaws and asked a human for help, humbly, in the sight of the sky and the stone and the failing fire.

She opened her mouth to answer.

That is when we heard it: wingbeats, wrong-winged, mechanical, coming out of the ash-dark from the direction of the guild. Many of them. And beneath the drum of engines, thin and clear across the dead air, a voice that no longer bothered to wear a mask, calling my name in the old tongue — the tongue of the boy who had watched dragons die, and learned their language, and never once used it kindly.

"He knows," Seraquin murmured. "The fire told him you're free."

Theo closed her mouth. Opened it again. Whatever she had been about to say, she folded it away, carefully, like a page marked to be returned to.

"Hold that yes," she said. "We'll finish this conversation after he's gone."

"After," I agreed, and turned to face the sky, and wished — for the first time in sixty years — that I had more fire left in me than memories.

The first of Cyrus's machines crested the ridge, and behind it, small and unhurried, walked the man himself, smiling the smile of someone who has come to collect a debt and believes, sincerely, that he is owed.
