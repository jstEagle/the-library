## 22. A Voice in the Ash

The trouble with honest ledgers, I have learned, is that they are boring.

This is not a complaint. I want to be very clear about that, because there are people — Brannic chiefly, and Seraquin when she bothers to correspond, which is to say when she wants something — who would take it as one. But after a lifetime of balancing accounts for caged griffins and bottled storms, after columns of numbers that were really confessions, there is a particular kind of peace in writing *one hundredweight of roofing slate* and knowing it is exactly that: slate, for the roof, because the roof leaks. No beasts were harmed in the procurement of this slate. No realms were mortgaged against it. It is the dullest line item in the history of accounting, and I love it the way you love an old coat that finally fits.

The rebuilt Vellum Guild stands on the same bones as the old one, because stone remembers even when we'd rather it didn't. The salt door still opens onto the same back alley where a courier died with my name half-formed on his lips — or someone's name; I never learned if he was reaching for me or for help or for home. We left the scorch marks on the threshold. Brannic wanted to sand them out. I told him a guild that trades in impossible things should keep at least one mark of what impossibility costs.

He grumbled. He always grumbles. Then he bought the slate anyway, because that is Brannic's whole philosophy of love compressed into a single transaction: complain first, carry second.

We run things differently now. There are no more special acquisitions, no retrievals, no crates that weep. The vault — Brannic's ring still opens it, though neither of us says so out loud — has been emptied of its bottled voices. Most went home. Some had no homes left to go to, and those we buried in the garden behind the counting house, each bottle wrapped in linen like a small sleeping thing, each marked with a stone. Seraquin came for the burials. Even exiled ambassadors, it turns out, know how to dig a hole and stand quietly over it.

I write the ledgers myself now, in my own hand, and every page bears a line at the bottom in letters I make deliberately too large to overlook: **All entries true. All debts named. Nothing caged.**

It took me four months to stop flinching whenever someone said the word *guild*. It took me six to stop expecting Cyrus's mask in every shadowed doorway. I am told grief does that — leaves a shape behind, the way a hand leaves a shape in wax long after the hand withdraws. I do not know if what I felt for him was grief. He burned himself alive rather than live in a world where fire could not be stolen, and some nights I dream of his smooth, ruined face looking almost relieved, and some nights I dream of the boy he must have been, watching dragons die and swearing he'd never be powerless again, and I wake up angry at myself for mourning him.

Then other mornings I wake up and hear nothing from the garden, from the gulls, from the cart-horses in the lane, and I remember what I paid, and the anger goes out of me like heat from a stone.

Because here is the part nobody warns you about, the fine print of miracles: the sigil didn't kill me. It took the one thing I loved most. And the thing I loved most was not my life, not my safety, not even Brannic, though God knows I love him with the quiet stubbornness of a well-kept ledger. The thing I loved most was the listening. The whisper under the world. Twenty years of hiding it, resenting it, starving it of use — and the moment it was gone I understood it had been the only true voice I ever owned.

I am deaf now to everything with feathers or fur or scales. The rats in the walls gossip about me in languages I can no longer parse. The fox — Ash, we called him, because he arrived out of the fire and because he was bitter as cinders — died before the reforging, a man again at the end, or near enough. His last words to me were about loss, about how the sigil takes what you love most, and I held his small body and thought, *then let it take the gift, because I cannot lose anything else today.* I thought I understood the bargain.

I did not understand that the bargain doesn't end. Every morning since, I wake into silence, and every morning the silence asks me if I meant it, and every morning I answer yes, and every morning the yes costs exactly as much as it cost the first time.

That is the price done properly, I think. Not paid once. Paid daily.

* * *

Brannic found me on the roof at dawn, which is where he always finds me, because apparently I've become predictable in my old age, and I'm twenty-six, which means either I aged fast or the last year counted double. He climbed the ladder with two cups of chicory and the particular expression he wears when he has bad news he's decided to disguise as logistics.

"The south wall's settled," he said. "Mason says another season before we repoint it."

"Mm."

"And the tanner's guild sent a petition."

"Mm."

"And there's a dragon on our roof."

"Mm." I stopped. "I'm sorry. What?"

"Dragon," Brannic repeated, with the patience of a man who has learned that repeating things to me is more efficient than watching me drop things. "On the roof. Our roof. Small one. Been sitting there since before light, according to the night watch, who would like me to inform you that they have resigned."

"They can't resign. They're the only watch we have."

"They resigned *conditionally*, Theo. They want hazard pay."

I set down my ledger — I had brought it up with me, because old habits, because the dawn light through the counting-house skylight is the best in the city for checking figures — and I crossed to the parapet with the careful, deliberate calm of a person whose heart has begun doing something unseemly.

And there it was.

Small, I'll grant Brannic that. Dragonet, properly speaking, no bigger than a barge horse, its scales still carrying the soft matte sheen of something newly hatched, its wings folded along its back like a closed book. It was charcoal-dark shot through with veins of ember-red, and it sat in the exact center of our new slate roof — our expensive, honestly-purchased, boringly accounted-for roof — and it looked at me across forty feet of morning air with eyes the color of the covenant-fire the day it came back.

Vaelith's firstborn. She'd written to us in autumn, in a letter carried by wind and delivered by a gust that smelled of ozone, telling us an egg had quickened. Telling us, in her slow, mountain-moving way, that new life at the edge of a renewed covenant was a thing without precedent, a sentence the world had not written before, and that she did not know what it would grow up to be. She asked nothing. Dragons, I have learned, rarely ask. They inform.

"You're trespassing," I told the dragonet, because I am a guildmaster now and decorum matters. "That's guild property. There will be paperwork."

The dragonet tilted its head. Opened its mouth.

And sang.

I don't know how else to put it. Not the furnace-roar of Vaelith, not the thunder that cracked the sky above the Cinder Coast the night the flame came back. A song — high and wavering and unmistakably young, notes tumbling over each other like water finding its way downhill, like something learning what its own voice could do. It sang of warmth, I think, though I couldn't have translated a word of it. It sang of a mother's side and a shell's dark and the long fall of light through water when you first open your eyes.

And I heard it.

Not felt. Not sensed. Not the ghost-pressure of beast-speech I've spent months pretending I don't miss. *Heard* — the way you hear a bell, clean and clear and outside yourself, arriving through the air like sound is supposed to arrive.

My knees went. Brannic caught my elbow, because he is always catching my elbow, and I heard him say my name twice, distantly, the way you hear rain on a window when you're reading.

"I hear it," I said. My voice cracked in the middle, undignified, ledger-worthy in its honesty. "Brannic. I hear it."

"You hear the — Theo, everyone can probably hear it, it's very loud—"

"No." I was laughing, or crying, or both, doing whatever it is that happens when a debt you've paid daily for a year is suddenly, impossibly, refunded. "No, they can't. This isn't sound, this is — oh, Ash, you miserable fox, what did you know?"

The dragonet cocked its head at my outburst, unbothered, and sang a little louder, a little bolder, a hatchling showing off. And underneath the music — not in words, nothing so simple, but in meaning the way sunlight means morning — I understood it was asking me a question.

*You are the one,* it said, in the way that a fire says *warm* and a river says *down*. *You are the one who gave. Why does your listening come back?*

And I opened my mouth to say I don't know, which was the truth, and then — standing there on my own ridiculous slate roof with my ledger fluttering and Brannic's grip tight on my arm — I found I did know. Or knew enough. Knew what the sigil had taken and what it hadn't. It had taken the gift, the hidden channel, the secret wire I'd used all those years to eavesdrop on the world. It had not touched whatever lived deeper than that. The part of me that had spoken with beasts was spent, yes — but the part that had *listened*, the part that had loved them enough to hide them, to lie for them, to burn down a life for them —

Maybe grace isn't the debt forgiven. Maybe grace is the debt transmuted. Given up by force, returned by choice. The dragonet had chosen to sing to me. That was the difference, maybe the whole difference: everything I'd lost had been taken, and this was being given, freely, by a creature the size of a pony sitting on my roof tiles like a cat that had decided the household was acceptable.

"I don't know," I told it anyway, because honesty is the house style now. "But I'm not going to waste it asking why."

The dragonet made a sound that I swear, sworn on the ledger, was laughter.

* * *

By midmorning we had a crowd. By noon we had a delegation. Seraquin arrived by evening — of course she did; I suspect the wolves have standing orders regarding anything interesting that happens within three days' walk of me — and stood in the courtyard looking up at the dragonet, who had declined to move and had instead eaten our entire woodpile, which I have added to the ledger under *provisions, diplomatic.*

"It shouldn't be able to do this," Seraquin said. Her amber eyes were wide, and Seraquin's eyes are never wide; they are ancient and narrow and see everything coming a century in advance. "A dragonet born under the new covenant, hearing-reaching toward a sigil-bearer. It's not in the old terms. It's not in any terms."

"Terms can be rewritten," I said. "We proved that."

"Yes." She looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her face that might have been hope, and hope on Seraquin's face is a frightening and wonderful thing, like sunrise in a country used to dusk. "Yes, we did, didn't we."

Brannic appeared at my shoulder with the evening's chicory, looked up at the dragonet, looked at me, and sighed the sigh of a man reconciling himself to a future full of impossible line items.

"We're going to need a bigger ledger," he said.

And high above us, on the slate, the last light of the day catching its ember-veins until it glowed like a coal refusing the dark, Vaelith's firstborn threw back its head and began to sing again — and this time, clear beneath the music, in words it should not have known and I should not have heard, it sang a single line:

*The covenant is not finished. Come and see what grows.*
