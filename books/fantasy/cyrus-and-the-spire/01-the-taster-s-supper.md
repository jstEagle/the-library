## 1. The Taster's Supper

The poison arrived in the third course, as poisons of quality always did.

Ulyana Crane knew this the way she knew the weight of her own tongue—intimately, without needing to think about it. The first course was never poisoned; a court that killed its king before the soup had no patience for subtlety. The second was possible but rare, for the second course was when the tasters were most awake, jaws working like clockwork, every nerve strung toward detection. But the third—the third was when hunger had dulled into satisfaction, when the wine had begun its soft work on the vigilance, when even the best taster's attention drifted to the conversation at the far end of the table and the pretty serving girl and the thought of bed.

The third course tonight was quail in a glaze of dark cherries and something sharper underneath, a vinegar so old it had gone almost sweet. Ulyana took her portion from the same serving dish that fed His Majesty Aldric III, let it sit on her tongue for the count of five the way she'd been taught—or rather the way she'd taught herself, because the crown's tasters were trained by rote and she had learned by instinct—and felt it.

Not taste. Taste would have been too easy. This was a texture beneath the texture, a faint gritting sensation against the back of her teeth, like biting foil, like licking a key. Arsenic, or arsenic's fashionable cousin. Not enough to kill a king outright—that would be crude—but enough, over weeks of small doses, to make a monarch gray and querulous and finally dead in a way that could be blamed on his heart, his melancholy, his stars.

Someone at this table wanted the king worn down like a coin passed through too many hands.

She swallowed anyway.

This was the part no one understood about her work, the part that made other tasters cross themselves when they thought she wasn't looking. A taster's job was not merely to detect poison. Detection was useless if you spat it out dramatically; then the poisoner simply tried again tomorrow, and the day after, until luck or lapse did the work. No—a taster of real skill *managed* the poison. She took it in, weighed it, decided how much the body could carry, and carried it. She was not a bell that rang. She was a ledger, and every dose was an entry, and the sum total of all those entries was written somewhere no physician could read: in the slow thickening of her blood, in the way her body had learned, over six years, to treat murder as a seasoning.

Ulyana had been poisoning herself since she was nineteen years old. Small doses, deliberate doses, doses chosen and catalogued the way a miser counts coins. Belladonna on Tuesdays. Mercury salts in the winter, when the court favored silvered fish. Arsenic whenever it appeared, because arsenic was the honest poison, the one that announced itself politely if you knew how to listen. Her immunity was not a gift. It was a balance sheet, and she kept it balanced the way she kept everything: precisely, secretly, alone.

"Crane." Lord Chamberlain Vosk's voice came down the table like a dropped fork. "You're smiling. I don't pay you to smile."

"You pay me to stay alive, my lord," she said, "and I find staying alive agreeable tonight."

A ripple of laughter from the lesser courtiers. Vosk's mouth thinned. He was a man who resented being made to laugh by someone he considered furniture, and Ulyana noted his resentment the way she noted everything—inwardly, permanently, filed under accounts receivable.

The king ate his quail. Ulyana watched him do it and felt the familiar double sensation: relief and contempt braided together. He was a decent man, King Aldric, decent the way weather is decent—unremarkable, occasionally cruel without meaning to be, entirely unaware of what held him up. He did not know her name. He knew her function. *The Crane girl*, the servants called her, though Crane was not the name she'd been born with, if she'd been born with one at all. Names at the guild were issued like tools, and hers had come to her already used, slightly bent, perfectly serviceable.

The fourth course passed clean. The fifth. The wine at the end she tasted twice, once for poison and once for pleasure, because a taster who forgot that wine could be enjoyed was a taster who would eventually forget why any of it mattered.

It was only afterward, in the cold corridor behind the kitchens where the tasters waited between services, that she allowed herself the small ritual: pressing her thumbnail hard into the pad of her forefinger, counting the seconds until the pain sharpened into clarity. Five seconds. Six. The arsenic sat in her like a guest who believed himself invisible. She catalogued him. *One measure, cherry glaze, administered via reduction—amateur work, but patient.* Someone in the palace kitchens had been bought. Someone with access to the reductions. She would need to find out who, and whether they were paid in coin or in fear, because those were two very different problems and required two very different solutions.

"Taster Crane." A kitchen boy, fourteen maybe, with flour to his elbows and terror in his eyes. "There's—a message. For you. Left at the scullery door."

The message was a slip of paper folded into a shape she recognized: the guild's calling fold, three creases, sealed with nothing because the guild sealed nothing. Wax was evidence. The guild dealt in deniability the way other merchants dealt in gold.

*Balance night*, it said. *Full set. Come by the coal gate.*

She read it twice, burned it in the tallow lamp with fingers that did not tremble—six years of practice—and told the boy there had been no message, and watched him decide, gratefully, to believe her.

Above the palace, the sky was doing what it always did in the city of Corvane: turning the color of old pewter, smudged at the edges with chimney smoke, holding its rain like a secret. Ulyana pulled her plain gray cloak around her plain gray dress and became, in four steps, nobody. It was the other half of her art. By day she was visible precisely as much as her function required—a taster must be seen eating, must be seen surviving—and by night she was a woman the world declined to notice, which was, when you thought about it, the more valuable condition.

She walked north through streets that smelled of wet stone and frying onions, past the shuttered apothecaries and the temple of the Twin Saints with its eternal candle, and did not once look over her shoulder, because looking over your shoulder was a habit and habits got people followed.

At the mouth of Coalwork Lane she stopped, as always, and listened to the city breathe.

Somewhere behind her, in the lit windows of the palace, the king slept under guards who did not know how close he'd come tonight to dying slowly. Somewhere ahead of her, down the lane that officially led nowhere, the Guild of Impossible Things was lighting its lamps.

And somewhere between the two—between the crown she served and the guild that owned her—there was a rooftop where a young man was probably waiting, and thinking of her, and drawing the Spire in charcoal on the backs of his master's discarded plans.

That thought did more for her heartbeat than the arsenic ever had.

* * *

The coal gate opened before she knocked. It always did. Ovett said the guildhall liked her, which was his way of saying the lock had been spelled to know her step, which was his way of saying *you belong here*, which was a lie wrapped in a kindness wrapped in a lie, and Ulyana had long ago stopped trying to unwrap it.

Inside, the guildhall was warm and dim and smelled of ink, tallow, and old paper—the smell she privately thought of as home, though she'd have cut out her own tongue before saying so. The Hall of Counting stretched away in front of her, long tables lit by green-shaded lamps, and at them sat the guild's clerks, balancing the impossible.

That was the trade, when you stripped away the romance. Everyone imagined the Guild of Impossible Things dealt in dragons' teeth and doors to elsewhere, and yes, there was some of that, locked in the deep archive where Ulyana was not permitted. But the daily business was bookkeeping. A stolen shadow, returned to its owner with interest. A borrowed year—someone's 1743, lent out and now repaid with a decade of good weather attached. A name that had never existed, purchased by a woman whose husband had died owing debts under it, so that the creditors could pursue a ghost while the widow started over. Every entry needed recording. Every record needed balancing. Fate, Ovett liked to say, was just arithmetic that hadn't been checked lately.

"Crane." Guildmaster Ovett stood at the head table, a narrow man assembled entirely of angles and wool, his spectacles perpetually sliding down a nose that seemed designed to reject them. "You're late."

"The king wanted a fifth course."

"The king," said Ovett, "wants many things. Sit. Full set tonight—we've a shadow coming back at midnight and it'll want witnessing."

She sat. She uncapped her pen. And for two hours she did the work she loved more than she loved anything except one person she could not name even here, especially here.

The shadow arrived at midnight exactly, slithering across the floorboards like spilled ink given ambition, and Ulyana witnessed its return with the proper forms: three clerks, one guild seal (a stamp, not wax—an iron stamp that left no mark but made the transaction true), and a ledger line. *One shadow, male aspect, duration of loan eleven months, condition upon return: stretched at the edges but sound.* The man who owned it wept when it slid back under his feet. They always wept. People thought shadows were nothing. Then they lived without theirs for a year and learned what nothing weighed.

Ulyana wrote it all down in her small, exact hand, and felt the particular peace that only came to her between these walls—the peace of a system that worked, of numbers that added, of a place where strange things were not feared but *filed*.

Which was, of course, when Ovett ruined it.

He came to stand beside her chair, close enough that she could smell the pipe smoke in his waistcoat, and looked down at the Prophecy Ledger where it lay chained to its lectern across the room—the oldest book in the building, older than the guild itself by some accounts, its pages the color of drowned parchment, its chain black iron forged from something that was not quite metal.

"You were looking at it again," Ovett said.

"I was looking at the wall it happens to hang near."

"Ulyana." He almost never used her name. When he did, it meant *stop*. "I know what you are. I've known since you were nine years old and caught a fever that should have killed you, and instead you dreamed the next week's weather. Tasters see patterns. It's why we took you in. But hear me, girl, and hear me plainly: the Prophecy Ledger is not for your eyes. Not for any taster's eyes. That book is read by three people in this world, and none of them pour wine for kings."

"Why?" It was the question she'd asked at twelve, at fifteen, at twenty. *Why?*

"Because prophecy is the one trade we don't make," Ovett said. "Everything else in this hall is negotiable. Years, names, shadows, luck—all of it can be bought, sold, amended. But a prophecy, once written, is a debt the universe has already signed for. We keep that book the way a bank keeps its charter. Not to use. To remember what we may not touch." He straightened his spectacles. "Touch it, and you don't lose your position. You lose your *self*. Do you understand me?"

She understood him perfectly. She understood that he was frightened, which was rarer than prophecy, and she filed that away too, in the drawer where she kept the things about the guild that didn't add up.

"Yes, Guildmaster," she said, and smiled her taster's smile, the one that survived arsenic and interrogation and Ovett both.

But it was nearly three in the morning before she climbed the stairs to the guildhall roof, and her hands were not entirely steady, and it had nothing to do with the poison.

* * *

Cyrus was already there, of course. He was always already there, sitting on the low parapet above the coal yard with his knees drawn up and his sketchbook open, rendering the city skyline in strokes so confident they looked less like drawing than like remembering. He didn't look up when she came through the roof door. He never did. He just said, in that voice like a struck bell, "You're late," and patted the stone beside him, and Ulyana Crane—who had faced down poisoners and guildmasters and the entire machinery of her own carefully constructed solitude—felt her chest do something unsanctioned.

"You're supposed to be asleep," she said, sitting. Their shoulders touched. Neither of them moved away. Neither of them ever moved away, and neither of them ever mentioned it, and this conspiracy of stillness was the closest thing to a vow either of them had.

"Apprentices sleep when the mortar dries." He turned the sketchbook so she could see. The Spire. It was always the Spire lately—that impossible needle rising from the dead center of the old war-field, white stone gone bone-gray with age, taller than anything men had built before or since. His master's repairs had consumed him for months now. In the sketch, the Spire rose out of the page with its base ringed by tiny figures: the ash-statues, the armies of the Sundering, ten thousand soldiers caught mid-charge a thousand years ago and standing there still, petrified mid-scream, waiting.

"They say the statues shifted last week," Cyrus said quietly. "Vess won't talk about it. He just measures the foundations twice as often." He glanced at her. "Bad night at the palace?"

"Cherries."

"Ah." He knew what that meant. He was perhaps the only person alive who did. "And?"

"And I'm fine." She held out her hand, palm up, an old gesture between them. He took it without asking why, pressed his thumb into her pulse point the way she'd once shown him, and counted.

"Fast," he said.

"It's always fast up here."

"That's not the arsenic talking, then." He said it lightly, but his thumb stayed on her wrist a moment longer than the count required, and Ulyana looked out at the smudged skyline and thought, with the particular despair of the well-defended, *this is the flaw in the design. I built myself a fortress and then I gave him the key by accident.*

"Cyrus." She heard her own voice go careful, which was the opposite of what she intended. "Why do you come here? Really. The roof. Me. You could sleep like a normal apprentice."

He was quiet for long enough that she risked looking at him. In the lamplight spilling up from the coal yard his face was all planes and shadow, twenty-two years old and serious as architecture. "Because everyone else sees what I'm going to be," he said. "Vess sees the architect I'll become. The guild masters see the apprentice. My mother—" he stopped, corrected himself, the way he always did, "—the people who raised me see the boy who got lucky. You're the only one who looks at me like I might turn out to be *nothing*." He smiled. "It's restful."

"That's the strangest compliment anyone has ever paid me."

"It's the truest one I know how to give." He closed the sketchbook. Below them, the city ticked and settled, and far off to the east, where the war-field lay under its permanent haze, something that might have been wind moved through ten thousand petrified throats. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Same time tomorrow," she said, and hated how much the words sounded like a promise, and loved it more.

He went down the stair first, because he always did—he claimed it was courtesy; she suspected he liked watching her watch him go—and Ulyana stayed on the parapet a moment longer, alone with the cold and the city and the small catastrophic warmth in her chest.

That was when she saw it. Through the roof door, left ajar, down the stairwell: the green glow of the Hall of Counting, and against that glow, the silhouette of the lectern where the Prophecy Ledger hung chained.

Ovett's voice floated up, speaking to someone she couldn't see, low and urgent: "—no. She doesn't suspect. And she never will. That book stays shut, do you hear me? Especially from *her*."

Ulyana stood very still on the cold roof, a woman who had spent her whole life learning what poisons tasted like, and realized she had just been handed the first taste of one far older than arsenic.

She memorized the moment. She filed it. And she began, silently, to plan.
