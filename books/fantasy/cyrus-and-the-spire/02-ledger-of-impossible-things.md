## 2. Ledger of Impossible Things

The guildhall did not exist, which was why it was so difficult to find and so impossible to lose.

Ulyana had learned its address the way she learned everything—by tasting. On her first night as a novice taster, Ovett had pressed a sugarplum to her lips and told her to name what was in it besides sugar. *Hemlock*, she'd said, *and something older*. And when the sweetness of the poison's antidote bloomed on her tongue a heartbeat later, the walls around her had rearranged themselves: a shuttered chandler's shop on Candlewick Row became a corridor, the corridor became stairs, and the stairs went down into a hall built before the street above it had been named.

Now she came down those stairs the way other women came home to husbands—with relief, with the loosening of a held breath, with the small vanity of knowing herself expected. The guildhall smelled of ink and cold stone and, faintly, of beeswax candles that never burned down. It smelled like safety, if safety had a price tag.

She hung her court cloak—the gray one, the invisible-servant one—on a hook that had no business being there, in a wall that was solid an hour ago, and descended into the Counting Room.

The Counting Room of the Guild of Impossible Things looked, at first glance, like any merchant's back office: long oak tables, brass lamps, quills in ranks like soldiers. The second glance was where merchants wept or prayed. For along every shelf stood ledgers bound in leather the color of stormclouds, each spine lettered in silver leaf with a title no honest book could bear.

*Ledger of Borrowed Years.* *Ledger of Names Never Spoken.* *Ledger of Doors That Open Inward.* *Ledger of Debts Owed by the Dead.* *Ledger of Rain That Fell Twice.*

And on its own lectern, apart from all the others, under a cloth of undyed wool, the oldest of them: the Prophecy Ledger. She could not see it from here, but she knew it the way one knows a scar—one is always aware of where it lies.

"Late," said Master Quillon, without looking up from his column of figures. He was the guild's auditor of shadows, a narrow man made narrower by forty years of hunching over entries that did not want to be written straight. "The king dined at seven. It is now half past ten."

"The king dawdled over the fish course," Ulyana said. "And someone put arsenic in the wine, which required a certain delicacy. One does not simply spit into the royal goblet and shout. There are protocols."

"There are deadlines," said Quillon.

"And I met them." She took her seat at the junior table, third from the door—a position of honor among novices, since it was nearest the tea—and drew toward her the night's ledger: a fresh folio, its pages blank in the way that guild paper was blank, which was to say hungry. "What came in tonight?"

Quillon read from his slate. "Two trades requiring entry. The first—" he hesitated, and Ulyana saw, with the particular pleasure of the clever, that he was embarrassed, "—the first is unusual even for us."

"I live for unusual."

"A shadow," Quillon said. "Stolen. A lady of the Marrow Court woke this morning without hers and has paid us handsomely for its recovery. We recovered it." He nodded toward the far corner of the room, where a lantern sat burning. Its flame cast light in every direction except one: behind the lantern lay a patch of darkness so complete it seemed cut from the world with scissors. Within that darkness, if one tilted one's head just wrong, something stretched and folded like a black silk banner drying on a line.

"It bit the retrieval agent," Quillon added, with dignity.

"Do shadows often bite?"

"When they've been stolen, they learn tricks from their thieves." He turned the slate. "Second trade: a returned name. A man named Aldous Ferrow sold his name to a moneylender eleven years ago—you'll find the original sale in the Ledger of Names Never Spoken, entry ninety-one—and tonight his granddaughter bought it back. Full payment. Witnessed. Clean."

"Clean trades," Ulyana murmured, dipping her quill. "How refreshing. Shall I enter them both?"

"You shall enter them both," said a voice from the stair, warm as brandy, "and you shall do it properly, because I intend to stand behind you and breathe on your neck until your letters are beautiful."

Guildmaster Ovett came down into the lamplight like a ship coming into harbor—broad, unhurried, inevitable. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he trimmed himself and therefore trimmed badly, and eyes the color of ledger-ink: black, patient, capable of recording anything. Ulyana had known him since she was nine years old and coughing blood into a rag, and he had knelt beside her pallet and said, *You have a talent, little one. Most people die of poison. You will make a career of it.* He had never once lied to her. She sometimes suspected that was because lying was beneath him; he preferred more elegant instruments.

"Master Ovett." She inclined her head. "I survived dinner. You may applaud."

"I never applaud survival," he said, settling onto the bench beside her with a groan of joints and bench alike. "Survival is the minimum standard of this house. Show me the entry."

She wrote. This was the work she loved best—not the tasting, not the careful theater of the court, but this: the making of the record. The guild's whole law rested on its ledgers. An impossible thing, once entered correctly, became real; entered wrongly, it curdled into something worse than unreal. She wrote the stolen shadow first, in the trade-hand the guild used for living things:

*Item: one shadow, adult-sized, property of Lady Vessamine of the Marrow Court, recovered intact and mildly enraged. Condition upon return: feral but settling. Fee paid in full. Notation: advise the Lady to keep her windows latched and her grudges small, for shadows remember who let the thief in.*

Ovett read over her shoulder. His breath smelled of the peppermints he chewed to cover the taste of whatever he'd swallowed last. "Feral but settling," he repeated. "Good phrase. Precise. You have a notary's soul, Ulyana Crane. It's a terrible thing to say to a young woman and I mean it as a compliment."

"Then the second," she said, turning the page. "The returned name."

*Item: one name, 'Aldous Ferrow,' redeemed from pledge by his granddaughter, Mira Ferrow, after eleven years' captivity in the books of the moneylender Gask. Name restored to bearer whole, unblemished, and pronounced aloud by three witnesses. Notation: the bearer wept. Notation: the granddaughter did not. Notation: names, like shadows, should be kept close, for the world is full of people willing to hold them for you—at interest.*

She sanded the page and looked up. Ovett was watching her with that expression she had never learned to parse entirely—pride with a cellar underneath it, warmth with a locked door somewhere behind it.

"Well?" she asked.

"Well," he said. "You're ready."

Her heart performed a small, traitorous leap. "Ready for what?"

"For the next table." He rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and beckoned. "Come. Walk with me. You've balanced the impossible ledgers for two years now—shadows, years, names, debts, doors. You've never once falsified an entry, never once peeked where peeking was forbidden, never once asked why the guild pays a taster to keep accounts." He smiled down at her. "Though I notice you've begun wondering. You wonder quietly. You think I don't hear it. I hear everything in this house; the stones repeat gossip to me."

Ulyana walked beside him down the aisle of shelves, past titles that whispered as they passed—some ledgers were vain and liked to be read; others flinched. Her fingers itched near the *Ledger of Doors That Open Inward*, as they always did.

"You're going to show me the Prophecy Ledger," she said. It was not a question. Her pulse ticked up despite herself.

Ovett stopped walking.

They had reached the lectern. The undyed wool cloth covered the book beneath it the way a sheet covers furniture in a dead man's house. Up close, Ulyana could feel it—or thought she could—a pressure, like standing too near a struck bell that had not yet finished ringing.

"No," Ovett said quietly. "That is precisely what I am not going to do. And I'm going to tell you why, because you've earned the truth, and because if I don't tell you, you'll find out some other way, and some other way would break my heart."

He lifted the edge of the cloth—only the edge, only enough to show a corner of cover, dark leather gone soft as skin with age, worked with a symbol she had seen nowhere else in the guildhall: a spire, thin as a needle, piercing a closed eye.

"This book is older than the guild," he said. "We did not write it. We found it. Or it found us—it has never been clear which, and the distinction may not matter. Every prophecy ever traded through these halls traces back to these pages. Every fate we have ever bought, sold, borrowed, or brokered began here, in ink no one has matched and no one has ever successfully forged." He released the cloth. It fell back into place with the softness of snowfall. "It is the one ledger we do not keep. It keeps us."

"But you balance it," Ulyana said. "Someone must. Every ledger requires a keeper."

"Three keepers. Myself, Mother Heshel, and the Warden's office, when the Warden chooses to visit—which is rare, and which we do not discuss." Ovett's hand settled on her shoulder, heavy and kind and, tonight, feeling suddenly like a weight placed rather than a comfort given. "And none of them will ever be a taster. That is not an insult, child. Hear me. It is the opposite of an insult. A taster's tongue is trained to detect intent—to find the poison in the sweet, the lie in the vow, the trap in the gift. Do you understand what such a tongue would find, reading prophecy?"

She considered. "Inconsistencies."

"Intent," Ovett said. "Prophecy is written in someone's interest, Ulyana. It always has been. Every fate is a forgery waiting to be suspected. A taster who read this book would taste the author's hand in every line—and then she would be faced with a question no one in this house wants asked: *who benefits?*" His grip tightened, gentle as a manacle. "Some questions, once tasted, cannot be spat out. So we keep our tasters magnificent and our tasters blind. You are trusted with everything in this guild except its oldest secret and your own history, and I am sorry for it, and I would do it again."

The words landed one by one, like coins dropped into a deep well—she heard each strike and waited for the splash that didn't come.

"My history," she repeated.

Ovett's face did something complicated and settled on kindness. "Another night. Tonight you've earned a different gift: the rest of the evening off, and the key to the roof garden, which I note you already possess, having copied it last spring." 

"I did not copy it."

"You did. Entry forty-four, *Ledger of Small Crimes by Persons Otherwise Exemplary*. You're listed twice."

Despite everything, she laughed. "Then I'll take my evening, Master Ovett. But I have a question first. Only one."

"Only one," he agreed warily. "From you that's a mercy."

"The Prophecy Ledger." She looked back at the lectern, at the cloth, at the shape of the book beneath it like a sleeping animal. "If it keeps the guild—if every fate we trade comes from its pages—then somewhere in it, there is an entry for tonight. For tomorrow. For everyone."

Ovett was silent for long enough that the candle flames leaned in.

"There is an entry for everyone," he said at last. "Go home, Ulyana. Or go up to the roof. Whichever roof you were planning on."

* * *

She went up to the roof.

The guildhall's roof garden was a fiction maintained by the same stubborn magic as the hall itself: wisteria that flowered out of season, a stone bench worn smooth by sitters who had perhaps never existed, and beyond the low parapet, the city laid out in tiers of lamplight—Candlewick Row, the palace hill with its watch-fires, and far to the east, past the war-field where no lamps burned, the Spire.

Even at this distance she could see it: a needle of pale stone against the stars, taller than anything human reason should permit, holding up nothing visible and supporting everything. They said the Sundering War stood sealed beneath it—two armies turned to ash mid-battle, kings and footmen alike frozen at the moment of killing, waiting. Children were told the Spire was a monument. Tasters were told it was a landmark. Nobody told anybody anything true about it, and Ulyana had long ago stopped minding, because the world ran on polite ignorance the way mills ran on water.

"You're late," said Cyrus, from the bench.

She hadn't heard him come up—she never did; he moved like someone accustomed to not being noticed, which was absurd, because he was the sort of person rooms reorganized themselves around. He sat with his elbows on his knees, sleeves rolled, forearms still dusted with the pale grit of the Spire-stone he spent his days measuring and mortaring. An architect's apprentice, third year, brilliant and broke, with ink on his collar and a smudge of chalk at his jaw like a dueling scar.

"I'm never late," she said. "I'm dramatically punctual. There's a difference and it's flattering to me." She sat beside him. Their shoulders touched, then arranged themselves into touching more deliberately.

"Rough night?"

"Someone tried to kill the king with arsenic in a vintage worth more than my life. I caught it. Nobody thanked me, because nobody knows I exist—I'm the girl who tastes the food and remembers the weather. Then I came to work and catalogued a biting shadow and a man's redeemed name." She tipped her head against his shoulder. "An ordinary Tuesday."

"Sounds extraordinary to me." He said it plainly, without irony, which was Cyrus's way. He meant it. He found her life astonishing, and she had never managed to tell him that his life was the astonishing one—that he spent his days climbing the one structure in the world that mattered, touching stones older than language, while she spent hers pretending soup was safe. "Aldous Ferrow," he said. "Wasn't that the man who—"

"Sold his name to pay a debt. Eleven years as a nobody. People forgot him mid-conversation. His wife left him for a man with a name of his own." Ulyana watched the far-off Spire. "Tonight his granddaughter bought it back. He cried when they gave it to him. Said it fit like a coat he'd outgrown and missed anyway."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. "Is it strange," he said, "doing what you do? Handling things like that. Names. Shadows. Knowing how much of a person can be… filed."

"Is it strange," she countered, "climbing a tower that hums?"

His head turned. In the dark she felt rather than saw the sharpening of his attention. "Who told you it hums?"

"No one. You did. Last month, after your shift, you said the north face sings on cold nights and Vess told you it was wind and you didn't believe him." She smiled against his sleeve. "I keep ledgers, love. Including of you."

"That's either very romantic or slightly alarming."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly alarming ones."

But he didn't laugh, and after a moment neither did she, because he had gone still in a new way—the stillness of a man deciding something. Below them the city murmured. Above them the wisteria shed one blossom, out of season, perfectly timed, like a cue.

"The humming's worse lately," he said. "Stronger. And it's not random anymore. It's…" He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture she knew meant he was about to say something his master would disapprove of. "Patterned. Almost like—"

"Language?"

His silence confirmed it better than words. "Vess says if I mention it again, he'll transfer me to bridge surveying. Bridges don't sing." A pause. "Sometimes I think the Spire knows me, Ulyana. I know how that sounds."

"How it sounds," she said softly, "is like an entry I haven't written yet."

He laughed then, finally, and pulled her closer, and for a while they said nothing at all, which was its own kind of ledger entry—*item: one hour, unrecorded, belonging to no one*—and she let herself believe, as she always let herself believe on the roof, that the world was large enough for the two of them exactly as they were.

Below, behind them, down in the Counting Room, under undyed wool, the Prophecy Ledger lay open on its lectern—though no hand had opened it—to a page bearing tonight's date, and a century-old ink, and a description of a rooftop, a wisteria blossom, and every word that had just been spoken.

Neither of them knew. Not yet.
