## 4. The Apprentice of the Spire

The stairs went down farther than the Spire went up.

Cyrus counted as he descended, because counting was how he kept the fear in its place — one hundred and forty steps, then a landing, then ninety more cut so precisely that his boot found each tread without his eyes. The lamplight of Architect Vess's hand-lamp slid along walls of pale stone that had no business existing this deep beneath the war-field. No mortar. No seams. As if the whole shaft had been grown rather than built, the way a tooth grows out of a jaw.

"You've never brought me down here," Cyrus said.

"I've never needed to." Vess walked ahead, unhurried, the lamp swinging a slow pendulum of shadow around him. He was a tall man made taller by habit — spine straight as a plumb line, hands folded behind him like a man perpetually inspecting something only he could see. Six years Cyrus had followed that back up scaffolds and across rooftops and along the Spire's ribs, and in all that time Vess had never once seemed to hurry, as though the world were obliged to wait for him.

"Needed to for what?"

Vess didn't answer. At the bottom of the last flight the stair opened into a chamber, and Cyrus stopped counting, because numbers suddenly seemed beside the point.

It was round, maybe forty paces across, and the ceiling rose into a shallow dome carved with figures — hundreds of them, thousands, ranks upon ranks of human forms spiraling inward toward a single stone at the apex. And every figure was a portrait. Not stylized, not symbolic. Portraits. Faces with crooked noses and missing teeth and laugh-lines and scars, rendered with such fidelity that Cyrus felt he had interrupted a crowd mid-conversation rather than entered a room.

He knew some of the faces.

Not from life. From dreams.

His mouth went dry. "What is this place?"

"The reason there is a Spire at all," Vess said mildly. He raised the lamp, and the light climbed the dome like water filling a bowl. "Everyone believes the Spire was raised to crown the victory of the Sundering War. A monument. A marker over a mass grave. That's what the histories say, and the histories were written by people who were not invited down these stairs." He turned, and the long smile appeared — the one that never reached his eyes. "The Spire is not a monument, apprentice. It is an instrument. And instruments have keys."

Cyrus looked back up at the dome. The spiral of faces tightened toward the keystone, and at its center — worn smooth by some touch repeated over years, over decades, over a century — was a blank space roughly the size of a man's chest.

"Where's its face?" he heard himself ask.

Vess's smile widened by a degree. "Ah," he said softly, "you noticed."

* * *

Afterward Cyrus could not say how long they stayed below. Time behaved strangely in that chamber, the way it behaved strangely in his dreams — stretching, pooling, refusing to move in straight lines. Vess showed him channels in the floor where nothing flowed but air, and air that moved against the draft of the stairs, breathing in when the chamber should have been exhaling. He showed him a wall where the stone, when struck with a knuckle, did not sound like stone but like a struck bell with the clapper muffled — a note felt in the teeth more than the ears. He asked questions Cyrus couldn't answer, and wrote down Cyrus's wrong answers in a small black book with the diligence of a man collecting specimens.

"What do you dream?" Vess asked, near the end.

"Ordinary things," Cyrus said. "Work. Weather."

"Nothing else?"

"There's a voice sometimes," Cyrus admitted, because lying to Vess had always felt like lying to a mirror. "In the dreams. It says my name."

"And is it your name?"

Cyrus's hands went still on the rail of the stair. Above them, faint through a hundred feet of earth and stone, he could feel the Spire — that hum, that patient sub-audible tone he had lived inside for six years until he'd stopped hearing it the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat.

"It's a name," he said.

Vess wrote that down too.

* * *

The war-field began at the northern edge of the city and ran to the horizon, four miles of gray grass growing out of gray dust, and standing in it — everywhere, in their thousands — the armies of the Sundering.

They were called ash-statues, though everyone knew they had not been statues first. They stood in formation still: ranks of infantry with spears half-raised, cavalry frozen mid-charge with the horses' manes streaming back like smoke, officers on hillocks with swords drawn and mouths open around commands no one would ever hear again. A century of weather had softened them, rounded them, given them the gentle blur of things remembered rather than seen. Rain had carved tears down their cheeks. Moss grew green in the folds of their cloaks, so that in spring the dead armies flowered, which was the cruelest thing about them, Cyrus thought. Even ruin insisted on blooming here.

The Spire stood at the field's heart, white as a bone pulled from the earth's throat, rising six hundred feet into a sky it seemed to hold up by consent.

Cyrus came out onto the third gallery at midmorning with his tool-belt riding low on his hips and the day's work list folded in his pocket. Repairs. Always repairs. For all its impossible age the Spire was mortal in small ways — a cracked voussoir here, a spalled course there, weeping joints, a finial gone green with rot where lightning had kissed it. The guild of architects maintained it the way priests maintain relics: reverently, continuously, without ever quite admitting what it was they were maintaining.

Today's work was the western buttress, third tier. Simple enough. He tied off, swung out over the drop, and let his hands remember what they knew.

This was the part of his life that made sense. Stone under his palms told him true things. A crack spoke honestly about its causes; a loose joint confessed; load and counterload kept their promises. Buildings didn't lie, didn't flatter, didn't watch him with long smiles and little books. By noon he'd re-seated two stones and was chasing a hairline fracture up into the buttress's shoulder, wedged in the scaffold's angle with the whole gray world spread below him — the city behind, the armies before, rank on rank on rank, the wind coming off them carrying dust and old rain and, if you were foolish enough to breathe deeply, something like breath —

The Spire hummed.

Cyrus froze, trowel halfway to the fracture. He knew the Spire's hum the way he knew his own pulse: constant, ignorable, structural. But this was different. This was the hum changing key — brightening, quickening, the way a tuning fork answers its twin across a room. It moved through the stone into his knees, his sternum, his back teeth.

And then he saw her.

She came up the pilgrim road that crossed the war-field from the east — a narrow track worn by a century of mourners and gawkers and the occasional fool who thought the ash-statues were safe to touch. She walked alone, hooded against the wind, and she moved like no mourner he'd ever seen: quick, purposeful, head turning to read the field the way other people read documents. When she reached the foot of the Spire she stopped, put one gloved hand flat against the white stone —

— and the hum swelled like a chord resolving.

Cyrus's mouth opened. No sound came out. Below, the woman took her hand away, tilted her head as though listening to something the rest of the world couldn't hear, and then did the strangest thing of all: she glanced directly up, directly at the third gallery, directly — impossibly, through wind and height and sixty feet of empty air — at him.

Even at that distance he could see her eyes were dark and level and entirely unafraid. Then she turned and continued around the Spire's base, walking the circumference slowly, tracing it, and the hum faded back to its ordinary patience, and Cyrus discovered he had been holding his breath long enough to see sparks.

He finished the buttress badly. His hands knew the work, but the rest of him was down on the pilgrim road, walking beside a stranger who touched stone like she was greeting an animal she'd tamed.

* * *

"You met someone on the field today."

Vess said it that evening, in the workshop, without looking up from his drafting table. Lamplight lay across the plans in yellow bars. Outside, the autumn dusk was coming down over the city, and somewhere below them the streets were lighting themselves window by window.

"A pilgrim," Cyrus said. "On the road."

"Mm." Vess inked a line. His pen made no sound, somehow. It never did. "Describe her."

"Why?"

"Because I asked." Still not looking up. Still pleasant. Still, in some way Cyrus could not name, the most frightening thing that had happened to him all day, which was saying a great deal.

"She was young. Dark hair. She put her hand on the stone at the base and — " He hesitated. Lying to Vess was lying to a mirror, but some mirrors you covered. "Nothing. She walked the circuit and left."

Vess set down the pen. He turned on his stool, folding his hands over one knee, and gave Cyrus the full weight of his attention, which was like being looked at by an instrument designed for the purpose.

"The Spire sang while she touched it," Vess said. Not a question.

Cyrus's stomach dropped. "You felt that? From here?"

"I feel everything that happens in my building." A pause. "That is what an architect is, Cyrus. The rest is ceremony." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood with his hands clasped behind him, watching the dusk. "There are people who come to the Spire and feel grief, and people who come and feel awe, and people who come and feel nothing, which is the commonest response and the healthiest. And once in a very great while — once a generation, perhaps twice — there is someone the Spire responds to. The stone knows them. The hum changes. The dreams get louder." He said the last part lightly, almost carelessly, the way a man mentions weather, and Cyrus felt the hair rise on his arms, because he had never told anyone about the dreams except Vess, this evening, underground. "Do you know what those people are called?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone else," Vess said. "That is rather the point."

He turned back from the window, and for a moment — a single unguarded moment, quickly shuttered — Cyrus saw something in his master's face that was not the long smile. It was older than the smile. Wearier. Almost tender, in the way that a cage-maker might look fondly at a nearly finished lock.

"Go home, apprentice," Vess said gently. "Sleep well. Mind the dreams. And tomorrow — " the smile returned, warm as lamplight, false as painted gold — "tomorrow we begin work on the sealing chamber."

"There isn't a sealing chamber," Cyrus said. "I've been through every plan in the archive. There's the bell chamber, the record vaults, the observation galleries — "

"There is a chamber," Vess said, "that appears on no plan ever drawn. It's been waiting a hundred years for a reason to be drawn." He picked up his silent pen again. "Good night, Cyrus."

Cyrus went. He took the outside stair, because he wanted the cold, and stood a while on the workshop landing looking north across the rooftops at the distant white finger of the Spire against the last violet light. It hummed. It always hummed. Tonight he could swear it hummed in the key it had used on the pilgrim road — the answering chord, the resolved note — as though somewhere in all that ancient stone, a question had been asked again and was waiting, patiently, for him to answer it.

A name that wasn't his. A chamber on no map. A master who collected his wrong answers in a black book. A stranger whose touch made a building sing.

He pressed his palm flat against the landing rail — ordinary oak, honest grain, wood that lied about nothing — and held it there until his hands stopped trembling.

Below, in the darkening street, a figure detached itself from a doorway and fell into step behind him, keeping a careful fifty yards, and did not turn off when he turned off, all the way home.

* * *

He dreamed, that night, of the round chamber with its thousand carved faces.

In the dream the spiral of portraits was moving — slowly, so slowly, the way the stars move if you watch long enough — each face turning inward toward the blank space at the dome's center, and the blank space was not blank anymore. Something was being written there. Letters surfacing through the stone like drowned bodies rising through water, one by one, in an alphabet he almost recognized.

And the voice said his name. The other name. The true one, or the borrowed one, or whichever it was, and for the first time in six years of dreaming, Cyrus answered aloud:

"Who am I?"

The spiral of faces stopped.

Every carved eye in the dome turned to him at once — and they were not stone anymore, they were wet, they were alive, they were grieving — and ten thousand voices answered him with a single word, spoken so softly and so certainly that he woke with it still ringing in the room, in his bones, in the white stone of a building three miles north that he could feel humming through his pillow like a second heartbeat.

*"Soon."*

Outside his window, dawn had not yet come. But far to the north, faint above the rooftops, the Spire's silhouette stood black against a sky that was already, impossibly, beginning to lighten — as if the stone itself were waking early, impatient, counting the days.

Cyrus sat up in the dark, wrapped his arms around his knees, and did not sleep again.

Tomorrow, Vess would take him below ground again. Tomorrow there would be a chamber that appeared on no plan, and a master with a black book full of his wrong answers, and somewhere in the city a dark-haired woman who touched stone like a friend — and Cyrus would begin, though he did not yet know it, to read the first line of the ledger that had been written about him a hundred years before his birth.

The stone knew his footsteps. The dreams knew his name.

And now, at last, the stone had started writing back.
