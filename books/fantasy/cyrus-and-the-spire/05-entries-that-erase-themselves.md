## 5. Entries That Erase Themselves

The Prophecy Ledger slept in the vault beneath the guildhall, and Ulyana had been raised—like every foundling in the Guild of Impossible Things—to believe it dreamed better left alone.

She had known the shape of that rule before she had known her letters. *Tasters do not touch the Book.* It was said in the same breath as the guild's catechisms, worn smooth by a century of small mouths: tasters did not touch the Book, did not ask after their own entries, did not wonder why the guild kept ledgers of names that never existed but no ledger at all of the names they themselves had lost. Ovett had taught her the rule with sugar on his tongue, the way one teaches a child not to play with fire—not because fire is evil, but because fire does not care.

For eleven years she had obeyed. She had balanced stolen shadows against returned names, logged borrowed years to the hour, recorded prophecies bought and sold like sacks of grain without ever once reading past the entry she was filing. She had told herself it was discipline. She had told herself it was loyalty. She had told herself, most nights on the roof with Cyrus's shoulder warm against hers, that there were things she simply did not want to know, because knowing was a poison too, and even her immunity had limits.

But she had seen tonight's date written in ink a century old, and she had heard Cyrus say the words before he said them, and obedience had died somewhere between the second line of that prophecy and the sound of his voice making it true.

So now, past the hour when even the guild's night-clerks went home to their narrow beds, Ulyana stood in the deep vault with a lamp turned low and the Prophecy Ledger open on the reading table before her, and her hands—the steadiest hands in the guild, hands that did not tremble for kings or arsenic—were shaking.

* * *

The vault was older than the guildhall above it, older than the city, if you believed the way the stairs spiraled wrong—each flight turning against the last, so that descending felt like climbing and you arrived below ground slightly out of breath, as though the architecture resented your visit. The walls were shelved floor to ceiling with the guild's dead ledgers: closed trades, settled accounts, names retired into leather and dust. But the Ledger itself sat alone on a table of black stone in the center of the room, unchained, uncovered, unguarded.

It needed no guard. That was the joke the senior clerks thought was funny. A book that wrote the future did not require a lock; it required only the certainty, instilled young and often, that some knowledge costs more than it pays.

Ulyana set her palms flat on either side of the book, not touching the pages yet, and let herself feel the thing she had never let herself feel in its presence: its attention.

Every ledger in the guild had a weight to it, a gravity. The shadow-ledgers felt cold. The year-ledgers felt heavy, as though time pooled in their spines. But the Prophecy Ledger felt *aware*—not alive, exactly, nothing so kind—but attentive, the way a trap is attentive, the way an open eye in a dark room is attentive. She had stood near it a hundred times and felt it like weather. Now she looked at it directly, and it looked back through the medium of paper.

"All right," she whispered, to the book or to herself. "Let's see what you've done to him."

She opened it where she had marked it—she had not dared mark it with anything so crude as a ribbon; she had simply remembered, with a taster's precision, the exact position of the page in the block, down to the thickness of what lay before and after—and read again the prophecy she had fled from two nights ago.

*In the hundredth winter of the holding, the Seal-Bearer shall walk upon the war-field with his love, and under the eaves of the Spire he shall speak three sentences, and the third shall be a promise made against all sense.*

Below it, in the same antique hand, the continuation she had not been able to make herself read that first night:

*And she shall hear it, and answer nothing, because she has already read it here.*

Ulyana closed her eyes. That was the shape of it, then. That was the shape of her. She had answered nothing on the roof because she had already read her silence in this book, and the book had known it would be so, had counted on it, had *written her silence as part of the machinery*—her stillness a cog, her love a gear-tooth, Cyrus's whole life a clock assembled a century before his birth and ticking toward one purpose.

Her stomach did what her stomach always did when she swallowed something bitter: it simply held the bitterness, patiently, without complaint. Eleven years of poisons had taught her body that protest was useless. But her body had never been poisoned like this.

She turned the page.

*The Seal-Bearer shall be called thrice by a name that is not his name,* read the next entry, in the same century-old script, *and the third calling shall come from the stone itself.*

She frowned. That hadn't happened yet. Or—it hadn't happened yet as far as she knew. Cyrus told her everything. Didn't he?

She turned another page. Another. The prophecies marched forward through the hundredth winter of the holding, each entry a small dense paragraph in that patient antique hand, and she read them the way she read a suspect dish: not for meaning first, but for threat. *The Warden shall come to court.* Yes—that had happened; the Warden's visit was scheduled within the month. *A faction shall rise within the guild.* Vague. Unverifiable. She noted it anyway, the way she noted an unfamiliar spice. *The ash-statues shall stir in their ten thousand ranks.* Her skin prickled. She filed it under unverifiable and moved on.

Then, near the middle of the winter's entries, she stopped moving at all.

There was a blank space.

Not an unwritten page—she knew what those looked like; the future was not blank, that was rather the entire point of the book, it brimmed with futures the way a riverbed brimmed with water. This was different. This was a paragraph that had been written and then *unwritten*. She could see the ghost of it: the faint impression of pen-strokes pressed through from the writing, the shallow trenches where ink had once lain, scraped away or—or *withdrawn*, sucked back into the nib of history like a word taken back mid-sentence. Around the absence, the neighboring entries stood intact, indifferent. Only this one had been hollowed out.

Ulyana leaned closer until the lamplight raked sideways across the page, and in that slanted light she could just retrieve the shapes of the strokes, the way a taster can retrieve an ingredient from a finished dish by the shadow it leaves on the tongue.

Three words survived the erasure entirely, too deeply impressed to fade: *the Seal-Bearer*—

And a date. Tonight's date.

She straightened up very slowly. Her heart was doing something unfamiliar—not fear exactly, though fear was in it. It was the sensation she got when a flavor in a dish refused to resolve, when her palate insisted something was present that should not be possible. A contradiction in a book that did not permit contradictions.

She began turning pages quickly now, forward through the winter, and the blanks came faster.

One erased entry. Then three. Then a whole run of pages where the ghost-impressions crowded together like graves in a battlefield trench, dozens of paragraphs unwritten, unwriting, the ink withdrawn from all of them at once or still withdrawing—she could not tell whether she was looking at erasures completed or an erasure in progress, whether she was reading the record of events undone or watching events being undone *right now*, under her lamp, in real time.

And every ghost, every scraped hollow, every surviving fragment in every single blank bore the same three words.

*The Seal-Bearer.*

*The Seal-Bearer shall—*

*—the Seal-Bearer, who was to have—*

*—before the Seal-Bearer—*

She flipped backward instead, toward the present, toward the entries she knew to be true because she had lived them. Here: the meeting on the war-field, intact. Here: the three sentences under the Spire's eaves, intact—she had watched Cyrus fulfill them word for word four nights ago, and the memory of how *seen* she had felt, how *authored*, rose in her throat like bile. Here: an entry dated tomorrow, still solid, its ink black and wet-looking, promising something about the Warden and a choice.

Between the solid past and the dissolving future lay a frontier, and the frontier was moving. Entries nearest to the present day stood firm. Entries a week out were scarred but legible. Entries a month out were ghosts. Entries beyond midwinter were—

She turned to them. Beyond midwinter the pages were not ghosted. They were *gone*. Not blank, not scraped—simply absent, the parchment smooth and pale as an eggshell, as though nothing had ever been destined there at all. She riffled forward with her thumb, faster and faster, and the book made a sound she had never heard a ledger make, a dry whispering rush like wind through dead grass, a hundred blank pages shivering past, and then the whisper stopped and her thumb stopped, because the book ended.

The Prophecy Ledger, which contained the next hundred years of the holding, the next ten thousand maneuvers of fate, the whole long authored life of Cyrus and everyone his life touched—ended. Midwinter. Nothing after. Not blank pages. No pages.

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

"It's unwriting him," Ulyana said aloud, and her voice in the vault sounded very small and very clear, the way a voice sounds over open grave-soil. "Something's unwriting him."

"No," said a voice behind her, old and tired and gentle in the way that only truly terrible things are gentle. "Something is unwriting *around* him. There is a difference, child, and the difference is the whole of the trouble."

* * *

Ovett came down the wrong-spiraled stairs slowly, one hand on the wall, and Ulyana realized in the lamplight that she had never actually seen him enter the vault before. He was always *of* it—the way he was of the guildhall, of the ledgers, of the whole dim economy of impossible trade—but she had somehow believed the vault went down to meet him rather than him coming down to it. Seeing him descend like any ordinary old man, breathing hard at the bottom step, was somehow more frightening than being caught.

He wore his nightclothes under his clerk's coat. He had come in a hurry.

"You followed me," she said.

"I didn't need to follow you." He lowered himself into the reader's chair across the table, wincing, arranging his knees. "I've been waiting eleven years for you to come down those stairs. I'd have been a poor steward indeed not to notice the night it finally happened." His eyes went to the open book, to the frontier of ghosts, and something in his face folded inward, like a man looking at a wound he has been dressing for a very long time. "You've seen it, then."

"The future is disappearing."

"The future is being *renegotiated*," Ovett said. "Which is worse. Sit down, Ulyana. If we're finally having this conversation, we're having all of it, and my legs are eighty-two years old."

She did not sit. She stood with her hands flat on the table on either side of the book, and she heard herself ask the question in the flattest voice she owned, her court voice, her *there-is-no-arsenic-in-this-dish* voice:

"What is the Seal-Bearer?"

Ovett closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked, for the first time since she had known him, genuinely old—not venerable, not dignified, just worn through, a garment used past its years.

"Stewardship," he said. "That's the word we use. I want you to hear it properly, because it is a beautiful word and it has been used to bury a great many people. The guild stewards the seal. The seal requires a bearer. The bearer must be prepared—shaped, sheltered, positioned, pruned—across generations, so that when the hundredth winter comes, he walks onto the war-field precisely as the book says he will, and says precisely what the book says he'll say, and does precisely what—" His voice caught, or he let it seem to catch; with Ovett one could never be sure, and she hated herself for still making excuses for him even now. "What must be done."

"Which is what," Ulyana said, "exactly. Say the thing you keep not saying."

"He holds the door," Ovett said quietly. "From the inside. The Sundering never ended, child. It was *suspended*—pinned, sealed, held in the throat of the world like a swallowed shout—and the Spire is the swallowing. And a seal built on a living consent must be renewed each hundredth year by a living bearer. The book writes his whole path so that he arrives without hesitation. Hesitation is the one thing the seal cannot survive."

"And Cyrus."

"Is the current vessel." Ovett said it steadily, and she gave him this: he did not look away. "As the mason Aldric was the vessel before him, a hundred years ago. As the shepherd-boy Emrys was before Aldric. We do not create them, Ulyana. We find the bloodline. We tend it. We protect it, we place it, we marry it well or not at all as the book requires, and when the winter comes, we deliver it." He spread his thin hands. "Stewardship."

Ulyana looked down at the book. At the ghosts. At the three words surfacing from every hollow like drowned faces.

"The entries are erasing themselves," she said. "His futures are disappearing. If he's your precious vessel, if everything about him has been arranged for a century, why is the book eating its own prophecies?"

"Because he's *offering resistance*." For the first time Ovett sounded not tired but afraid, and the fear in him frightened her more than anything else in the vault. "The book writes the path of least resistance, girl. Fate is not a tyrant; it's a current. And for a hundred years, every Seal-Bearer swam with the current, because we built their lives so carefully that swimming with it was the easiest thing they could do. Every door opened the way the book wanted. Every refusal was made impossible before it was ever imagined." He gestured at the ghosted pages, and his hand shook. "This one imagines them. Do you understand? The blanks are *refusals*. Futures where he does not comply are being proposed and discarded, proposed and discarded, hundreds of them, faster than the book can hold them—the current is hitting a rock it has never hit before. He is saying no to things before the book even finishes offering them. He doesn't know he's doing it. But the book knows."

The vault was very quiet. Ulyana became aware that she had stopped shaking.

"You put me at court," she said.

"Yes."

"You fed me poisons in measured doses for eleven years. You built my body into a fortress, dose by dose. Why?"

"Because the book required a taster who could survive the hundredth winter's table." Ovett's voice was very soft now. "Because the prophecies in the margins—you wouldn't have seen them, no one sees the margins but the guildmaster—they require her. The Taster. She appears three times in the outer entries, and every appearance depends on an immunity that cannot be bought or borrowed. It must be *grown*. From childhood. In one specific body." He looked at her then with an expression she would spend the rest of her life trying to forget: pride and pity in equal parts, the expression of a gardener for the tree he has raised for felling. "We chose you before you could walk, Ulyana. Your parents sold you to us with your cradle-board. We did not arrange your life around Cyrus's. We arranged both of your lives together, the way one arranges two gears."

The words went into her the way poison goes in: no pain at first, just a spreading cold correctness, a recognition. *She shall hear it, and answer nothing, because she has already read it here.* Even her silence had been specified. Even her stillness was a component. She had spent eleven years proud of a discipline that had been installed in her like a rivet.

"I trusted you," she said. Her voice did not break. She noted, distantly, that she wished it would. "I gave you every night of my life."

"I know." And horribly, terribly, Ovett smiled—a small, exhausted, genuine smile, the smile of a man confessing to the one sin he cannot regret. "That, too, is in the margins. Your trust. Page one, if you must know, in the founding hand. *The guild shall raise a daughter who loves its purposes more than her own.* We have never once had to lie to you, child. We only had to choose you."

Ulyana looked at the book. The frontier of ghosts had moved while they talked—she was certain of it now, certain the way a taster is certain. One of the entries dated tomorrow had gone hollow. In its scraped shallows, three words remained, deeper than all the rest, pressed hard into the parchment as though the pen had borne down with all its strength before the ink withdrew:

*the Taster chooses—*

Ovett saw it too. She watched the color leave his face, watched the old man understand, one heartbeat ahead of her and no more, that whatever the book was renegotiating, it was no longer only about Cyrus.

"Ulyana," he said, rising painfully from the chair, reaching across the black table toward her, "close the book. Child. Whatever you're thinking—"

She was thinking about a boy on a roof who said promises against all sense, whose whole life was a line in someone else's ledger, who was swimming against a current built by people who called drowning *stewardship*.

She was thinking that a book which could erase the future could, presumably, be made to write it.

"Get away from the table, Guildmaster," said Ulyana Crane, taster, foundling, gear, and reached for the page dated tomorrow.
