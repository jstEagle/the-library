## 6. What the Guild Bought

The deep archive lay beneath the guildhall like a secret kept from the building itself—a vault of black stone that predated the guild's founding, older than the city above it, older perhaps than the river that turned the mills on the far shore. To reach it, Ulyana descended three staircases that did not appear on any of the guild's own floor-plans, past a door that asked her a question in a language she did not know but somehow answered correctly, and into a cold that had nothing to do with temperature. It was the cold of things that had been left alone for a very long time.

She carried a single lamp and a lie.

The lie was for Ovett: that she needed the deep archive to verify a trade of borrowed years, a routine reconciliation. Guildmaster Ovett had looked at her for a long moment across his desk, his face a landscape of patient hills, and had said, "Take the green lamp. The white one wakes the shelves." And then, as she reached the door: "Ulyana. The deep archive remembers who opens it. Be sure you want to be remembered."

She had said she was sure. She had not been sure. She was not sure now, standing in the dark among the oldest ledgers in the world, her breath ghosting in the lamplight, listening to the vault settle around her like a sleeping animal turning over.

The green lamp's flame burned a color flame should not burn—a green like pond ice, like the patina on old copper—and under its light the shelves revealed themselves in reluctant layers. The guild's public ledgers, the ones she balanced nightly in the scriptorium, were beautiful things: vellum, gilt, ink mixed with silver. The ledgers here were uglier. Bound in hide she chose not to identify. Written in hands that shook, or hurried, or pressed so hard the nib had torn through page after page, as if the writer had been trying to stab the truth into the world rather than merely record it.

She had come for one thing. She told herself that. One thing, and then she would climb back into her ordinary double life and never speak of it.

But her hands, when she set them on the first shelf, were not steady, and she knew the reason, and the reason had a name, and the name was Cyrus.

* * *

It took her four hours to find the right ledger, and she would remember, afterward, that she had not searched for it so much as it had permitted itself to be found. The spine was unmarked. The cover was the color of dried blood, which she told herself was simply old leather, which she told herself was simply old.

The first page bore a date a hundred and two years past, and a heading in a clerk's careful hand:

**ACQUISITIONS — BLOODLINES, FATES, AND OTHER INTANGIBLES**

Ulyana read the heading three times. The guild traded in prophecies, borrowed years, names that never existed—she had known this since she was nine years old, when Ovett had first put a pen in her hand and taught her that an entry made in good faith was a kind of mercy and an entry made in bad faith was a kind of theft, and that the guild's whole art lay in knowing which was which. But *acquisitions*. As though a fate were a shipment of saffron. As though a bloodline were a horse.

She turned the pages with the tips of her fingers, the way Ovett had taught her to handle anything that might still be alive.

Most of the entries were small. A farmer's third son, purchased to satisfy a debt of luck. A widow's grief, bought and resold twice. A city's founding date, altered by a single day, and the consequences of that alteration running down the margin in red ink like a wound that would not close. She read them all. She could not help it. Each one was a life reduced to a line, and she was the guild's ledger-keeper, and every line was a small mirror.

Then, near the middle of the book, an entry longer than the rest, written in a hand she almost recognized—almost, the way one almost recognizes a face in a crowd, in a dream, in a generation removed:

*Item: one (1) bloodline, the Vess-line of the eastern terraces, being the descendants of Marla Vess, seamstress, together with all futures appertaining thereunto, in perpetuity.*

*Consideration paid to the said Marla Vess: protection in perpetuity from the Guild's own collections; remission of her late husband's debt of years; and one (1) unrecorded kindness, nature unspecified, delivered at a time of the Guild's choosing.*

*Condition of acquisition: the bloodline to be held in stewardship against the coming Century, the bearer thereof to be raised, positioned, and prepared as the Ledger requires. The bearer is not to be told. The bearer's consent is not required. Consent, per the terms of the original Sundering settlement, is to be manufactured by circumstance.*

Ulyana sat down on the cold floor without deciding to. The lamp's green light pooled around her like water.

Marla Vess. She knew the name. Every apprentice of the Spire knew it—Marla Vess was carved into the third landing of the great stair, one of the honored dead of the last Century, the seamstress who had, according to the official histories, died in the plague winter. Cyrus's great-grandmother. Cyrus had told her once, on the roof, in the voice he used for things that hurt: *my family's only distinction is a dead woman on a staircase.*

*The bearer is not to be told. The bearer's consent is not required.*

Cyrus. It had been Cyrus for a hundred years. Not a man who had happened to fall in love with her, not an apprentice who had happened to be assigned to the Spire's repairs, not a life that had happened to intersect with hers on a roof at midnight. A holding. An asset in stewardship. A boy purchased before his grandfather was born, raised like a candle kept lit for a ceremony no one had invited him to.

She thought of his hands, ink-stained and mortar-cracked, the hands she had held on the roof. She thought of the way he laughed, head tipped back, as though laughter were a thing he was slightly surprised to be allowed. She thought of his dreams, which he had never described to her, and which she had never asked about, because some privacy she had always honored even when she honored nothing else.

*Consent is to be manufactured by circumstance.*

She was the circumstance. Or she was one of them. The thought arrived without permission and sat down in her chest like a stone.

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that even as she turned the page. There is a kind of reading that is a descent, and there is a depth past which a reader does not come back up unchanged, and she felt the depth approaching the way divers feel it, in the ears, in the blood. She turned the page.

The next entry was dated ninety-eight years past, and it was not about the Vess-line.

*Item: one (1) position, being the office of Taster to the Crown of this city, to be filled in the current Century by a candidate of the Guild's selection.*

*Requirement of the candidate, per marginal annotation of the Prophecy Ledger (see folio 214, verso, sealed): the Taster must possess, or be made to possess, an immunity sufficient to survive seven poisons, being the number named in the Century's terms.*

*Consideration to the Guild: the Crown's debt of one (1) unrecorded favor, held in reserve.*

*Note: candidate to be drawn from the foundling rolls. A foundling has no family to object and no history to contradict. The candidate's own history is to be supplied by the Guild and is not to be accurate.*

Ulyana read the word *foundling* and felt the vault tilt.

She was a foundling. She had always known this, in the way one knows the color of one's own eyes—constantly, and never thinking of it. Left at the guildhall's door in the famine winter, Ovett had told her, wrapped in a blanket that the guild still kept in a cedar box, as though the blanket were evidence of something. She had built herself on that foundation: the accident of her arrival, the luck of it, the one unauthored thing in a life otherwise so thoroughly authored that she had made a profession of authorship. She was Ulyana Crane, who had no past, and therefore a past that belonged entirely to her.

*The candidate's own history is to be supplied by the Guild and is not to be accurate.*

Seven poisons. She counted them without wanting to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. The wolfsbane in the ambassador's wine, her sixteenth winter, three days of fever she had been told was a cold. The arsenic dusted through the sugar at the Duke's table, her nineteenth, subtle enough that she had only caught it by the way the candle flame had leaned. The nightshade, the hemlock, the thing in the oysters two years ago that the guild's physicians had never named. The slow drip of the taster-master Sef's training tonics, bitter as iron, which she had drunk every morning for a decade because Sef said a taster's body was an instrument and instruments must be tuned.

Seven. She had survived seven. She had thought of it, in her private and unspoken accounting, as talent. As her one inheritance, since she had no bloodline to inherit anything else.

It was not talent. It was a specification. She had been purchased as a foundling and poisoned on a schedule, and every survival had been a milestone in a project plan written before her birth, and the project plan lived in a sealed folio of the Prophecy Ledger, and the Prophecy Ledger was the one book in the guild she was forbidden to touch.

She was trusted with everything except her own history. Ovett had said that to her once, or nearly—she had thought it was a kindness when he said it, a confession of his own limitations. *I can give you the guild, child, but I cannot give you your beginning.* She had believed he meant it as grief.

He had meant it as disclosure. He had been telling her, in the only way his oaths permitted, that her beginning was a line item.

* * *

The lamp was burning low when she found the last of it. She had stopped reading entries and started reading margins, because margins were where the guild hid the things it could not bear to enter formally, and the margin of the acquisitions ledger, near the Vess entry, held a note in a hand she did know—Ovett's, but younger, sharper, written perhaps fifty years before she was found on the guildhall steps:

*Query from the junior clerk Ovett: if the bloodline is held in stewardship, who audits the stewardship? Who audits the audit? The Prophecy Ledger names a Seal-Bearer, and names also a second figure, unnumbered, in the margins. I have asked the Guildmaster what the second figure is for. He has told me the margins are not my concern. I note here, for whoever keeps this book after me: the margins are always someone's concern. That is what margins are for.*

Ulyana pressed her palm flat against the page. The paper was cool and slightly damp, the way living things are cool and slightly damp, and she hated it, and she did not pull her hand away.

The second figure. Unnumbered. In the margins.

She thought of every ledger she had ever kept, every trade of shadows and names and borrowed years, and how she had prided herself on reading not just the entries but the spaces between them—the hesitation in a seller's hand, the ink that was blotted as though the writer had flinched. She had thought herself the guild's best reader of what was not written. She had never once thought to read herself that way.

A taster who survives seven poisons. A foundling with no history. A ledger-keeper trusted with everything except the book of fate. If the guild had built Cyrus to be a key, what had it built her to be? A lock? A hinge? A hand to turn something?

Or simply the other line item. The second figure, unnumbered, purchased at the same time, from the same nothing, for the same purpose—and her whole life, the tonics and the fevers and the lonely precision of her craft, the roof and the lamp and the man she loved in defiance of every rule of her profession—her whole life was a cost entered in a column she had never been allowed to see.

She laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, one dry syllable that the vault swallowed whole, and the sound of it frightened her more than anything on the shelves. Grief, she thought. This is what grief sounds like when it has nowhere to go. Not weeping. Accounting.

She closed the acquisitions ledger and did not put it back where she had found it. She put it one shelf to the left, a small vandalism, the first unauthorized entry of her life: *this book has been read by someone it was written about.*

* * *

She climbed the three hidden staircases with the green lamp guttering, and the door asked her its question again, and this time she answered it in the same unknowing language and heard, or imagined she heard, a note of pity in the way it unbarred.

The guildhall above was in its night rhythm—the scratch of pens in the scriptorium, the soft commerce of clerks trading small impossibilities, the bell that did not ring but was nevertheless heard at the change of each hour. She moved through it unseen, as she had moved through the palace at dinner, as she moved through everything, a woman trained to be unremarkable, and for the first time in her life the training felt like a cage rather than a craft.

Ovett's door was closed. Light showed beneath it. She stood before it with her hand raised to knock, and stood, and did not knock. What would she say? *Master, I have read the deep archive, and I have learned that I am a purchase order*? He had warned her at the vault door. *Be sure you want to be remembered.* He had known what she would find. He had known for fifty years—had known since he was a junior clerk asking his own questions in the margins, and he had spent those fifty years feeding her, training her, positioning her, loving her perhaps, in the way one loves a thing one is stewarding.

*Consent is to be manufactured by circumstance.*

She lowered her hand.

She went instead to the window at the end of the east corridor, the one that looked toward the Spire, and she stood in the dark and looked at the distant pale needle of it rising over the war-field, lit by the moon, humming—she could almost hear it from here, or imagined she could, a note below hearing, the way one feels thunder in the ribs. Somewhere between here and there, in the apprentices' quarters at its foot, Cyrus was sleeping, or not sleeping, dreaming whatever the Spire dreamed into him, a purchased boy being prepared by circumstance for a consent he had never given.

She had gone into the deep archive to learn what the guild had bought. She had learned it. The guild had bought a bloodline and a foundling, a key and a lock, a century of manufactured coincidence, and it had paid for all of it with protection and favors and one unrecorded kindness, and the whole transaction balanced to the copper, because she had checked, because balancing was the one thing she knew how to do, and the guild's books, as always, were perfect.

Perfect books. She had spent her life keeping them. She had never before understood what they were for.

Tomorrow was a court day. The king's table, the Warden's visit, the long ceremony of the Spire's Century—she had the seating memorized, the menu drafted, the risk assessment drafted beneath it in her own private shorthand. She had thought it would be an ordinary poisoner's vigil, the hundredth of its kind, her body tuned and waiting.

She thought of the sealed folio. Folio 214, verso. *The Taster must possess an immunity sufficient to survive seven poisons, being the number named in the Century's terms.*

The Century's terms. The Spire's Century was now. And if the prophecy required her to survive seven poisons, then somewhere in the terms—written before her birth, sealed before her birth—something was waiting that intended to test her with an eighth.

She turned from the window and went to prepare for court, and her hands, which had survived everything the world had ever put in them, were not steady at all.
