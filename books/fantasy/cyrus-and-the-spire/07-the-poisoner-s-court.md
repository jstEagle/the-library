## 7. The Poisoner's Court

The Hall of Amber Light was built to make people feel small in a comfortable way—small the way pilgrims feel small before a relic, small in the manner that keeps them obedient and warm. Its vaulted ceiling had been gilded during a reign nobody alive remembered, and the gold had gone the color of old honey, so that the thousand candles below it seemed to burn inside a jar of preserved sunlight. Ulyana Crane stood at her station behind the king's high table with her hands folded and her face arranged into the pleasant blankness of furniture, and she counted.

Not the guests, though there were four hundred of them, glittering like fish caught in a net of candlelight. She counted exits: seven, including the servants' stair behind the arras that only someone who had spent three years memorizing the palace's service routes would know about. She counted courses: nine, which meant nine chances, each one a small door a poisoner could walk through. And she counted the people whose deaths would matter tonight, because at a court dinner the arithmetic of murder was always done in advance, and her job was to find where someone else had already done the sums.

King Aldous sat at the center of the high table, gray and good-natured, a man who had reigned long enough to be bored by his own importance. To his right, the visiting dignitary: the Warden of the Spire.

Ulyana had seen engravings of Wardens before, but the engravings were wrong in every particular that mattered. They made the office look ceremonial—a robed figure with a chain of office and a distant expression. The woman seated two places from the king was not distant at all. She was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered, with the sun-browned hands of someone who worked outdoors, and she ate the way soldiers eat, steadily and without pleasure, as though food were a report she was filing. Her eyes never stopped moving across the hall—not admiring it, *surveying* it, the way one surveys a fortification for weaknesses. Around her throat she wore a collar of black iron, unornamented, and Ulyana thought: *that is not jewelry. That is a key. Or a lock.*

The Warden had come down from the war-field to consult with the crown on matters of the Spire's upkeep—the official dispatches said *repairs*, the way all official dispatches said repairs—and the court had turned the visit into an occasion, because the court turned everything into an occasion. Nine courses. Four hundred guests. The Sundering sealed a hundred years this midwinter, and everyone wanted to be seen celebrating a war they had never fought.

*Everyone wants to stand near the seal,* Ulyana thought. *No one wants to ask how much longer it holds.*

She was thinking about folio 214 when the third course arrived.

It came up from the kitchens on silver salvers carried by pages who moved in the practiced choreography of the palace service—six dishes to the high table first, then the tiers below, each dish preceded by the tasting ceremony that was Ulyana's entire public existence. For the king's table she performed it herself; that was the honor and the burden of her post. She took the small forked spoon from her belt, touched it to each of the six dishes in sequence, held each morsel on her tongue for the count of ten, and swallowed or did not swallow according to what her tongue told her.

Her tongue was better than any assay office in the kingdom. That was not vanity; it was chemistry paid for in years. Arsenic tasted of garlic gone wrong. Antimony was metallic, sweetish, a lie of a flavor. Wolfsbane numbed the tip of the tongue before it ever reached the throat. She had catalogued forty-one poisons in her body's memory, and her body had learned, poison by deliberate poison, to refuse each one entry—or to survive its entry with nothing worse than a headache and a night of cold sweat. Seven poisons, said the sealed folio. She had taken far more than seven in her life, but the prophecy had not specified *varieties*. It had specified *survivals*. She kept her own count anyway, scratched small and private in the back of her guild ledger: seven, eight, eleven, thirteen. She had stopped believing in coincidence around the ninth.

The third course was quail in a dark sauce of wine and blood-orange, and Ulyana took her measure of it, and for the space of ten heartbeats nothing happened at all.

Then her tongue went cold.

Not numb—cold, distinctly, precisely cold, along the left edge where the sauce had pooled, a chill like a sliver of ice laid against the muscle. There was no taste to it. That was the first thing that frightened her, though her face did not change; she smiled at the page, inclined her head as if the quail met with her approval, and let him carry the dish onward while her mind ran through the catalogue at speed. Forty-one poisons, and none of them cold. None of them *silent*. Every poison she knew announced itself somehow—bitterness, metal, numbness, heat—because most poisoners were amateurs working from recipes, and recipes were legible.

This was not legible. This was something designed to pass a taster.

Which meant whoever had made it knew there would be a taster. Knew the ceremony, knew the timing, knew that a taster's verdict came within ten heartbeats and built accordingly—a poison slow enough to hide behind her approval, patient enough to kill hours later, when no plate could be blamed.

And it was already moving through her.

Ulyana did not stagger. Staggering was a confession, and confessions at court were fatal in ways that had nothing to do with poison. She lifted her goblet instead—water, untouched, hers alone—and drank half of it, and set it down, and began to breathe in the slow measured rhythm that Ovett had taught her in her first year: *four counts in, seven counts out; the body believes whatever the breath tells it.* The cold was spreading now, sliding down her throat and settling behind her sternum like a coin of ice, and her heartbeat was—she counted—four beats too slow and losing.

*What kills a slow poison is motion,* she thought, absurdly, clearly, as though the thought belonged to someone wiser standing just behind her shoulder. *Blood carries. Blood also cleanses.* She needed her heart faster, not slower. She needed—

She looked up, and across the width of the high table, the Warden of the Spire was looking at her.

Not idly. Not with the polite incurious glance a guest gives the palace's decorative staff. The Warden was watching Ulyana Crane the way a physician watches a wound, and her iron-collared throat moved as she spoke quietly to the king without once taking her eyes away.

The quail reached the Warden's place.

Ulyana saw the moment of it: the Warden's serving-woman setting the dish down, the Warden drawing her knife, the whole enormous machine of the evening rolling forward on its rails toward one more ordinary mouthful. And Ulyana understood, all at once and completely, the way one understands a falling thing only after it has struck—the direction of the sum. Someone had built a poison that could defeat a taster, and they had not wasted it on King Aldous, whom three separate factions would pay handsomely to lose and whose death would change nothing that mattered. They had spent it on the woman with the iron collar. On the keeper of the seal. On the one person whose death, at this precise moment in the world's accounting, would leave the Spire unattended at midwinter.

*Someone wants the door open,* Ulyana thought, and the ice behind her sternum seemed to answer the thought with a slow expanding ache.

There were perhaps thirty heartbeats between the Warden's knife and the Warden's fork. Ulyana had no antidote for a poison she could not name. What she had was a body trained for eleven years to be a fortress against exactly this class of weapon, and a suspicion—rising in her now with the certainty of things one has always known—that the cold spreading through her chest was not trying to kill her quickly because it had never been calibrated to kill her at all. It had been calibrated to kill a normal heart. Hers was not a normal heart. Her heart had been educated.

She came around the table.

Later, she would never be able to reconstruct how she had decided, only that there had been no decision, the way there is no decision in catching a dropped glass. She heard the nearest chamberlain begin a protest—"Madam, the tasting is concluded"—and she heard herself answer without turning her head, in the flat courteous register of palace furniture speaking: "The dish requires a second measure." And then she was beside the Warden's chair, and she put out her hand, palm open, over the plate.

"Stop," she said.

The hall did not stop. Halls never do; four hundred conversations have too much momentum. But the Warden stopped. The knife paused above the quail, and the grey surveying eyes rose from Ulyana's hand to her face, and something passed between them that Ulyana would spend many nights afterward trying to translate: recognition, maybe. Or arithmetic. The Warden looked at the taster's open palm the way one looks at a drawn map of a country one is preparing to invade.

"You've eaten it," the Warden said. Her voice was low, gravelled, pitched for one listener. "The sauce. You took your measure and you swallowed."

"Yes."

"And you're still standing."

"For the moment." Ulyana's voice was steady. Her heart was not; it had slowed further, six beats adrift now, each one arriving late like a messenger carrying bad news across a long distance. "There is something in it that shouldn't be. I can't name it. I can name forty-one other things, but not this. Whoever made it built it to pass a taster, which means they built it for tonight, which means they built it for you." She turned her open hand slightly, a small formal gesture, the only flourish permitted to her rank. "I am asking you not to eat, my lady, and I am aware of what it costs me to ask it. If I'm wrong, the crown can have my post and my name. If I'm right—"

"If you're right," the Warden said, setting down her knife with a click that somehow silenced the nearer quarter of the hall, "then you are currently dying in front of four hundred witnesses, and the interesting question is why your body hasn't finished the work."

"I was wondering the same thing," Ulyana said, and the edge of her vision went silver, and the floor tilted by a degree that no one else could see.

The Warden stood. It was done casually, unhurried, a guest rising to admire the ceiling—but her hand closed briefly around Ulyana's wrist as she rose, two fingers pressed to the pulse point, and Ulyana watched her count. Watched the Warden's lips move faintly with numbers. Watched the grey eyes narrow.

"Your heart is failing," the Warden said quietly.

"It does that when people try to poison me." Ulyana heard the strangeness of her own voice, how far away it had gone, how the silver at the edges of the world was creeping inward like frost across a window. "I need—I need to walk. Fast. Corridor behind the arras, there's a stair, it goes down to the buttery—"

"That's a servant's route."

"I'm a servant."

Something that was almost a smile crossed the Warden's face, and then the Warden did a thing that four hundred courtiers would still be discussing at season's end: she took the taster's arm, in front of the king, in front of the ambassadors, in front of everyone whose business was noticing, and she walked Ulyana Crane off the dais and through the arras into the dark.

* *

The servants' stair was lit by one lamp per landing and smelled of tallow and old bread, and Ulyana learned, descending it at the fastest pace her failing body allowed, that a poisoned heart and a climbing staircase negotiate their differences very badly. By the second turn her legs were answering her orders a full second late, and the silver frost had closed until the world was a lamp-lit tunnel with the Warden's iron collar glinting at the end of it. By the third turn she was being carried—she never felt the transition, only the change in altitude, the Warden hauling her arm across those broad sun-browned shoulders with a strength that no engraver had ever thought to record.

"Bitter almond?" the Warden asked, still counting her pulse with two fingers.

"No."

"Metallic?"

"No. Nothing. That's the whole horror of it. Cold, and nothing."

"Hm." The Warden sounded less alarmed than interested, and some stubborn professional corner of Ulyana's mind noted that interest, in this woman, was probably what mercy looked like. "There are compounds that read as nothing. Most of them come out of the old war-formularies. Most of them died with the Sundering."

"Then whoever brewed this," Ulyana managed, between stairs, "has been reading a hundred-year-old book."

"Or living next to a hundred-year-old door." They reached the bottom landing; the buttery corridor stretched ahead, empty, lamp-hung, smelling of spilled beer and beeswax. The Warden did not stop walking. "Where are we going?"

"Cellars run under the east wing. There's a cold room where they keep the river fish packed in ice." Ulyana's speech had begun to cost her; each word arrived like a coin counted out of a nearly empty purse. "Ice slows blood. Slows everything. If your poison is slow, maybe cold is slower. It's not medicine. It's arithmetic."

"It's good arithmetic." A pause, footsteps, the creak of a door on leather hinges. Then cold struck her face like a handful of winter, real cold, cellar cold, and the Warden lowered her onto a packing crate among the iced fish, and pressed both of Ulyana's bare forearms into a trough of cracked river ice, and held them there. "Tell me if you stop being able to talk. Talk while you can. Start with your name."

"You know my name. You watched me at the table. You watched me before the table—you'd been watching since the doors opened."

"I watch everyone since the doors opened." The Warden crouched in front of her, unbothered by the melt-water soaking her sleeves, and her grey eyes in the lamplight were the exact color of the Spire in the engravings, stone-colored, weather-colored. "But I'll hear it from you. Names freely given tell more than names looked up."

"Ulyana Crane." It came out half a gasp. "Taster to His Majesty. Third of the name to hold the post."

"Third." The Warden's eyes flickered, and Ulyana, drowning slowly in cold, saw the flicker and understood it, and wished she didn't. *Third of the name.* Three generations of Cranes placed at the king's left hand like chessmen, and a century-old book that required a taster with an immunity sufficient to survive seven poisons. The Warden knew something about threes. "Your grandmother held this post?"

"My grandmother. My mother before me, briefly." The ice burned. The burning was better than the cold behind her sternum; the burning was on the outside, where burning belongs. "Why do you ask?"

"Because posts like yours aren't given," the Warden said. "They're planted. I've stood a hundred years' watch on a door, Mistress Crane, and I know planted things when I lean on them." She checked the pulse again, and her brows drew together—not with alarm, Ulyana realized, but with something closer to puzzlement, almost offense. "Your heart is slowing further. Considerably. And yet you're speaking clearly, and your color is good, and by every law I know you should be unconscious on my floor." She released Ulyana's wrist and sat back on her heels. "What are you, taster?"

"Tired," Ulyana said. "Mostly tired."

The Warden laughed—one short bark, surprised out of her—and the sound echoed strangely off the ice-troughs, and for a moment the two of them were simply two women in a cold room full of dead fish, laughing at the absurdity of surviving. Then the Warden's face settled back into its surveying gravity, and she said, "It will pass. Whatever it is, it's passing. Your pulse has found its feet again. Which tells me the poisoner calibrated for an ordinary constitution—which tells me they don't know what you are." She rose, wrung melt-water from her sleeve, and looked down at Ulyana with an expression that Ulyana recognized, with a lurch, from her own guildhall ledgers: the look of an entry being written. "Someone at this court wants the Warden of the Spire dead in the weeks before midwinter. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the Spire needs its Warden."

"That the Spire needs its Warden *alive and present*, at the one season when the seal runs thin, or the Sundering wakes." The Warden pulled a key from beneath the iron collar—an actual key, black iron, worn smooth—and turned it over in her fingers without seeming to notice she was doing it, the way other people worry a ring. "A hundred years ago, two armies killed each other so thoroughly that the killing refused to stop even after the men were dead, and the builders of the Spire caught the war mid-stride and shut it in. Every hundred years the terms come due, and every hundred years the seal is renewed, and every hundred years, somewhere, someone decides the world would be better off with the war back out of its bottle. Because a waking war, taster, is a useful war. For conquest. For crowns. For profit." She put the key away. "Tonight someone tried to leave that door unattended. And the only reason they failed is that a palace servant tasted a quail and declined to die."

* *

Ulyana returned to her chambers past midnight, walking carefully, holding the walls of corridors as one holds a railing on a ship.

The ice had done its arithmetic; the cold behind her sternum had retreated to a dull bruised ache, like the memory of a fall, and her pulse had climbed back to its familiar disciplined march. She would live. She recorded the fact without drama, the way she recorded everything—*poison the forty-second, unnamed, survived*—and then she sat at her little desk with the single shuttered candle and did not write it anywhere at all, because there was no ledger in the world she could safely write it in.

Fourteen heartbeats. She counted again, to be sure, pressing two fingers to her own wrist in the dark. Fourteen, steady, alive. Somewhere in the palace above her, four hundred guests were sleeping off the third course, and not one of them knew that the evening had contained a murder, or that the murder had been interrupted by a woman whose whole art was to be invisible at tables. She had saved the Warden of the Spire. She had done it publicly, unmistakably, in front of the king and the ambassadors and the entire apparatus of the court's attention, and the Warden had taken her arm and walked her into the dark, and tomorrow the story would be everywhere, dressed in a dozen costumes, and in every costume she would be the woman at the center of it.

Visible. For the first time in her life, catastrophically visible.

*They don't know what you are,* the Warden had said, meaning her body's stubborn refusal of the formula. And it was true—the poisoner did not know. But the poisoner would want to know now. Whoever had brewed a silent cold death out of a century-old formulary would spend tomorrow asking questions, and the questions would lead, inevitably, backward: to the tasting records, to the post, to the Crane name, to three generations of women planted at the king's table like seeds in a furrow. And further back than that, if they dug deep enough—to a guildhall with no address, and a ledger with a sealed folio, and a prophecy written a hundred years before her birth that named a Taster who must survive seven poisons.

She had survived fourteen. She was, by any honest count, twice the woman the prophecy had ordered.

So why, sitting alone in the dark with her candle shuttered and her heart keeping its borrowed time, did she feel not triumphant but *exposed*—as though surviving the eighth test had torn something open rather than proven anything? Folio 214 had promised her seven survivals. It had not promised that the survivals would keep her safe. It had promised only that she would be alive, and sufficient, and *present*—the way one is present at a signing, or an execution, or a door.

There was one person in the world she could tell. One roof, one pair of shoulders against the chimney stack, one voice that asked her nothing except whether she was hungry.

She gathered her cloak and took the back stairs down and the alleyways across, moving the way she had moved for years, unseen, unremarked, one more shadow among the city's shadows. Only at the foot of the guildhall wall did she let herself think the thought she had been refusing all night, the thought that waited under everything like the cold under the sternum:

*If they had known what I was—if the poisoner had calibrated for me—it would have worked.*

Somewhere out there was a mind that read the same old books she read, that understood planted things and prepared bodies and the mathematics of immunity. And that mind had made a mistake tonight. Minds that made mistakes corrected them. Next time there would be no ordinary constitution assumed; next time the dose would be doubled, tripled, fitted to her specifically, the way a key is cut to its one lock.

She climbed the drainpipe with hands that had survived forty-two poisons, and pulled herself onto the roof, and saw him waiting against the chimney stack with his coat collar turned up against the wind—and for the length of one breath she almost turned around and went back down. Because she was carrying too much truth tonight, and the roof was where they kept nothing true, and she did not know if she could sit beside Cyrus and say *nothing* while her pockets burned.

He turned at the sound of her boots. Smiled. Held out, absurdly, solemnly, the heel of a loaf he had stolen from his master's kitchen, as he always did, as though this were simply what nights were for.

"Cyrus," she said, and her voice came out strange, stripped of all its usual architecture, "something happened at court tonight. And I think—I think it's already happening to you too, whatever it is, and I don't know how I know that, but I do."

The wind moved along the rooftops. He looked at her for a long moment, and then he said, in a voice she had never heard him use, "The Spire's been talking to me in my sleep, Lena. And I think it's time we talked about what it's saying."

She sat down beside him, and the city lay below them full of sleeping strangers, and above them the stars kept their own ledger, and neither of them, yet, knew how much of the night's honesty they were about to spend.
