## 8. Confessions on the Roof

*Ulyana*

The guildhall had a way of swallowing confessions whole. She had watched it happen for years—secrets carried in through the green door and never carried out again, filed away in ledgers with dates and prices until they stopped being secrets at all and became inventory. Tonight she had carried her own secret up three flights of stairs to the roof where the pigeons nested among the gutters, and she held it behind her teeth like a coin she was afraid to spend.

Cyrus was already there. He always was, on the nights she could come; he had learned her rhythms the way she had learned poison doses, by patient attention, by small experiments that never quite killed him. He sat on the low parapet with his boots dangling over the drop, his sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms still dusted white with Spire-chalk even now, even here, as though the building followed him home and refused to be washed off.

"You're late," he said, without turning.

"The crown ate slowly tonight." She hoisted herself onto the parapet beside him. Below them, Vell Street unspooled in lamplight, and beyond it the rooftops stepped down toward the river, and beyond the river—she did not look at it, but she felt it the way you feel a bruise—the Spire stood against the stars, thin and pale and patient.

"Poison again?"

"Worse. Parsnips." She let him laugh before she added, quietly: "And arsenic in the wine. The third time this month. Someone at court has decided I'm inconvenient."

He went still beside her. She felt it, the way she felt everything about him now—had been feeling it for weeks, since the ledger, since the date written a hundred years ago in a dead man's hand. *On such a night the taster and the apprentice will sit upon the roof of the counting-house.* Every word he said next would be a word someone else had written. She had spent six days deciding whether to tell him, and six days losing the argument every time he smiled.

"Cyrus." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "I have to tell you something, and I need you not to speak until I'm finished. Because if you speak first—if you say what I think you're going to say—I'll lose my nerve entirely."

He turned to look at her. In the lamplight leaking up from the street below, half his face was gold and half was shadow, and she thought, absurdly, *even your face is divided between what they made you and what you are.*

"I keep the ledgers," she said. "You know that. All the ledgers. Prophecies, borrowed years, names that never existed. And six days ago I found an entry in the oldest book we have—a prophecy written before either of us was born, before our grandparents were born. It describes tonight. This roof. You and me. It describes—" her throat tightened— "it describes things you've said to me before. Word for word."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, a cart rattled past, and a dog barked twice and gave up.

"It's called the Ledger of the Sundering Seal," she went on, because stopping now would kill her. "It's about the Spire. About the war that's held inside it—the armies that are standing out there on the field right now, frozen as ash, waiting. And it names a figure. The Seal-Bearer. The one who will either close the door forever or open it." She finally turned to face him fully. "It names you, Cyrus. Not by name. By bloodline. By a birthmark under your left arm that the entry describes in detail I wish it didn't. It has been describing you for a hundred years."

She had expected many things. Anger. Disbelief. The particular blankness people wore when their lives rearranged themselves without permission. What she had not expected was for him to exhale, long and slow, like a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying alone for a very long time—and to say:

"Then it's real. The dreams are real."

Her heart stopped and restarted wrong. "What dreams?"

He looked back out at the city, at the Spire, and when he spoke his voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice and testing each step.

"I didn't want to tell you because it sounds mad, and because—" he laughed, without any humor in it— "because I was afraid you'd look at me differently. Ulyana, the Spire talks to me. In my sleep. It has since I was fourteen, since my first day on the scaffolding. At first I thought it was just the work getting into my head—you spend enough hours listening to stone settle, you start hearing voices in it. But it knows things. It told me where a crack was hidden inside the third gallery wall that no survey had ever found. It told me which of Master Vess's drawings were wrong." He rubbed the back of his neck, hard, the way he did when he was frightened and refusing to show it. "And lately it's been calling me something. A name. It isn't mine. I've never heard it anywhere except in the dream, but it fits me the way a key fits a lock, and I hate it, and I answer to it anyway, every time, because some part of me thinks if I don't answer, something terrible will happen."

"What name?" she asked, though her mouth had gone dry, because she already knew. She had read it in the margin of the prophecy, in Ovett's careful hand: *the vessel answers to the old name.*

He told her. The syllables hung in the cold air between them, ancient and wrong, and Ulyana closed her eyes.

"That's him," she whispered. "That's the first Seal-Bearer. The one from a century ago. Cyrus—that isn't a dream. That's a memory that doesn't belong to you. Or—" and the thought arrived like a blade sliding between ribs— "or it's a function. Something they built into you. Into your family. Before you were born."

* * *

*Cyrus*

The word *built* landed in him like a chisel-strike, and he felt the crack run all the way down through everything he believed about himself.

He had grown up poor and lucky: poor in Copperlane, lucky in being chosen at twelve for the architect's apprenticeship, plucked off a street where his probable futures were the docks or the debt-prisons. He had spent ten years believing his life was the story of a boy who worked harder than his circumstances. Every promotion, every trust Vess placed in him, every hour alone with drawings other apprentices weren't allowed to see—he had earned them. He had *earned* himself.

And now Ulyana sat beside him saying *before you were born*, and the Spire hummed its stranger's name into his sleeping ear, and somewhere in a book older than his bloodline there was a description of the mole beneath his arm, and none of it—not one hour of it—had been his.

"How much?" he asked. His voice sounded far away to himself. "How much of me is theirs?"

"I don't know yet." Her hand found his on the parapet, cold fingers lacing through chalk-dusted ones. "I know the guild keeps records of everything. If they arranged this—if they stewarded your family the way I think they did—there will be entries. Prices. Dates. There's always a price, Cyrus. That's the whole trade. Nothing impossible happens without one."

"Then find it." He heard the hardness in his own voice and didn't recognize it. "Find the entry. Find what they paid for me, and who collected, and—" He stopped. Breathed. The anger was useful but it wasn't all of it, and underneath it was something worse: a small, cold question he couldn't stop asking. *If they wrote me, am I real? Is anything I feel real? Is this—* he squeezed her hand— "*is this real, or is it scheduled?*"

Ulyana went very still. He watched her arrive at the same question from the other direction, watched it hit her, watched her decide—as she always decided—to walk straight into it rather than around.

"There's an entry," she said slowly. "In the prophecy. About tonight. It gives dialogue, Cyrus. Words spoken on this roof. I read them six days ago and I've been terrified ever since that everything you say to me is just... fulfillment. That you're a puppet and the book holds the strings." Her grip tightened. "So I've been waiting all night for you to say the words. Waiting to hear the string pull."

"And?"

"And you haven't said them." She turned to him, and in the dark her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "You were supposed to tell me about the dreams *after* I named the prophecy. That's the order in the book. But you said it first. You went off-script, Cyrus. Ten seconds after I started talking, you went off-script." A breath shuddered out of her, almost a laugh. "Do you understand what that means? It means the book can be defied. It means you're not only what they wrote. Whatever they built into you, whatever name they buried in your sleep—there's a *you* underneath it that just proved it exists. I watched it happen."

He stared at her. The relief that broke over him was so violent it was nearly grief, and he had to look away, out at the black line of the war-field on the horizon, until his eyes stopped burning.

"Then we go off-script," he said. "All the way. Both of us."

"Yes."

"They wrote a fate for me. So we refuse it. They arranged your whole life too, didn't they? The tasting, the immunity—" he caught her flinch and pressed on gently— "I've known something was wrong for months, Ulyana. The way Ovett watches you. The way the guild needs you poisoned and perfect and exactly calibrated. Whatever they bought, whatever they built—we burn the design. We write our own entry."

"It's not that simple." But she was leaning into him now, shoulder to shoulder, and her voice had lost its dread and found something steadier underneath. "The Spire is real. The seal is real. If the prophecy is true about anything, it's true about the war waking up. We can't just refuse our way out of that."

"Then we find another way to hold the door. One that doesn't require me to be a key." He said it simply, the way he'd say a measurement or a load-bearing calculation, because that was how he knew how to promise things: structurally. "I build things, Ulyana. My whole life is finding a second way to carry the weight when the first drawing is wrong. Vess says the Spire is the finest lock ever made. Fine. Then locks have keys, and keys can be changed, and I swear to you—" he took her face in both hands, chalk and all, and made himself look at her— "I will redraw this. However long it takes. Whatever it costs. I will not be poured into a mold somebody cast a hundred years ago. Not even to save the world. *Especially* not to save the world."

For a moment she didn't answer, and he saw her doing the thing she did—weighing, pricing, tasting the future for arsenic—and then she laughed, a real one, cracked and startled out of her, and pulled his forehead down against hers.

"Do you know," she murmured, "that in eleven years of knowing you, that is the first thing you've ever said that wasn't in the book?"

"Then the book and I are done."

"Not done." She pulled back just far enough to see him. "Hunted. There's a difference. If we defy the prophecy, we defy the guild, and the Warden, and possibly the Spire itself. We'll need to move carefully. I'll need time in the archives. You'll need to keep working at the Spire like nothing's changed—can you do that? Can you stand under it, listen to it call you that name, and smile at Vess?"

He thought of the humming. Of the dream-voice, patient as masonry. Of Vess's eyes on him across the drafting table, warm and watchful in a way he had always read as pride and now suspected was something closer to a shepherd counting sheep.

"I can," he said. "As long as I know this roof is at the end of the day. As long as I know there's one person in the world who sees the difference between me and the mold."

"Then that's the vow." She raised her hand between them, formal as a guild oath, and he pressed his palm to hers. "No more scripts. No more ledgers between us. Whatever they wrote, we unsay it. Whatever they built, we rebuild."

"Together," he said, and the word settled into place with the clean certainty of a keystone.

They stayed a while longer, hands joined, watching the Spire stand pale against the stars. And neither of them noticed—how could they, wrapped in the first honest night either had ever had—that far out on the dark plain, along the silent ranks of ash-statues, something had begun, very faintly, to glow. A thread of light running rank by rank through the dead army's eyes, like a fuse lit at one end, burning slow and steady toward the Spire.

Or perhaps Ulyana noticed. Perhaps she filed it away, as she filed everything, in the ledger behind her ribs, under a date she had not yet dared to check.

But that was tomorrow's reading. Tonight there was only the roof, and the cold, and his hand in hers, and the wild, unlawful feeling—new to them both, and worth every danger coming for them—

of being unwritten.
