## 9. The Warden's Offer

The summons came the way poison came: dressed as something harmless.

A footman in palace livery appeared at the guildhall door at dusk with a card of heavy cream stock, and on it, in a hand too fine for any clerk's, a single line: *The Warden requests the taster's expertise before tomorrow's inspection.* No seal. No crest. The Warden, it seemed, did not need one; his name was authority enough, the way arsenic was authority, working silently and recognized only when it was far too late.

Ulyana read the card twice, then burned it in the hall's green lamp and went to change her gloves.

"You've been noticed," Ovett said from the doorway of her room, and there was something in his voice she had never heard there before—something that, in any other man, she would have called fear.

"Saving a man's life tends to do that."

"Saving that man's life." He came into the room without invitation, which he never did, and stood looking down at her hands as though they belonged to someone else. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone at court saw a taster act? You're supposed to be furniture, girl. Beautiful, invisible furniture. Instead you crossed a ballroom in front of forty witnesses and put your fingers down a Warden's throat."

"He was dying."

"He was *noticed*," Ovett said. "And now so are you." He paused at the door on his way out, and did not turn around when he spoke again. "Wear the grey. And whatever he asks you—and he will ask you something, because men like him never summon without wanting—remember that the guild's answer is not always your answer. But it should be, this once. It very much should be, this once."

Then he was gone, leaving the smell of pipe smoke and old paper behind him, and Ulyana sat on the edge of her narrow bed and thought about Cyrus's hand in hers on the roof, and about all the things she had not told him, and about how the word *should*, in Ovett's mouth, had sounded like an apology made in advance.

* * *

The Warden received her in the east wing of the palace, in a room she had never seen despite four years of tasting in that building—a long chamber walled in smoked glass, so that the sunset came through it broken into amber panels and the whole world outside looked bruised. A table stood in the center bearing wine, bread, fruit. Of course. Everything with this man would be a test of some kind.

He was younger than she expected. That was the first wrongness. Wardens of the Spire were supposed to be old, gray, half-ghosts already, their lives poured out in decades of vigil. This one had black hair going silver only at the temples and hands like a fencer's, and when he gestured her to a chair he did it with the easy courtesy of a host who has never once doubted his welcome.

"Taster Sandoval," he said. Though of course she had given no name, and none had been offered. "Or shall I use the other one? The guild name? I'm told it's rather lovely, in the old tongue. Something about ash, isn't it?"

"You have me at a disadvantage," Ulyana said, and sat, and did not touch the wine.

"I have everyone at a disadvantage. It's the whole of my profession." He poured for himself, unhurried. "You saved my life three nights ago. Hemlock in the honeyed pears—not aimed at me, as I'm sure you worked out within seconds, but I was between the cup and its intended destination, and fate is rarely graceful." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "You could have let it find whoever it found. A taster's duty is the king. The king was across the room. By every rule you live by, I should be dead."

"By every rule I live by," Ulyana said, "poison is poison. I don't ask it whom it serves."

"Mm." He set down the glass. "And yet you did ask. My people watched you work afterward—you were very thorough, most wouldn't have noticed—and you traced the source back to the kitchens, and then you did nothing. You didn't report it. You didn't warn anyone. You simply... filed it away." His dark eyes were very steady. "Which tells me that either you don't know who wants me dead, or you do, and it frightened you enough to keep quiet. Both possibilities interest me."

The glass wall threw amber light across the table between them, and Ulyana kept her face the way she kept her hands at a tasting: still, pleasant, giving away nothing. She had traced the hemlock, yes. She had traced it to the guild's own supply chain, through three cutouts and a dead carter, and she had spent two sleepless nights deciding what that meant, and she was nowhere near finished deciding.

"Perhaps I was simply tired," she said.

"Perhaps." He rose, and crossed to the smoked glass, and stood with his back to her, a silhouette stitched out of failing light. "I didn't bring you here to discuss pears, Taster. I brought you here because you are the only person in this city who has touched both halves of the problem and doesn't yet know it. Your guildmaster knows. Your architect Vess knows. Even, I suspect, your young man knows, in the wordless way that vessels always know. But you—" he turned, and there was no warmth in his face anymore, only a kind of terrible gentleness— "you are the last innocent in this affair, and I find I need one of those. Innocence sees things the instructed eye has learned to skip."

"My young man," Ulyana repeated, very evenly.

"Yes. Let's not pretend. I've read the ledgers, Taster—all of them, including the one your masters tell you doesn't exist. I know what the apprentice is. I know what you are, though I suspect even the margins haven't shown you that yet." Something flickered across his face at that—an error, quickly smoothed—and Ulyana marked it the way she marked an unfamiliar bitterness under honey: filed, dated, to be investigated. "Come. There's something you need to see before we speak further. It can't be described. It can barely be survived."

"Where?"

"The war-field," said the Warden. "Where else does one go to watch the end of peace?"

* * *

They rode out in a closed carriage with black curtains, and the Warden spoke little on the way, and what he said was worse than silence: small domesticities, the weather, the quality of the roads, as though courtesy itself were a drug he was measuring out. Ulyana answered in kind and watched the light through the curtain seams slide from amber to iron to nothing, and felt the ground beneath the wheels change from cobbles to packed earth to the soft ash-silt of the old battlefields, where nothing had grown in a hundred years and nothing was meant to.

The Spire rose against the last violet of the sky like a needle drawn from the world's flesh. She had seen it a hundred times from a distance, every citizen had—it was on the coins—but she had never come this close, and up close it was wrong in ways distance forgave. It did not reflect the fading light. It drank it. And it hummed, low and constant, a note she felt in her teeth more than her ears, and she thought, unbidden, of Cyrus saying on the roof: *sometimes when you're near it sings, and I've never told anyone that, please don't laugh.*

She did not laugh. She wanted, very suddenly and very badly, to be on that roof instead of here.

"This way," the Warden said, and led her past the first lines of statues.

Everyone knew about the ash-statues. They were history made solid: the armies of the Sundering, two hosts locked in mutual slaughter at the moment the First Architects sealed the war mid-motion, turned where they stood into gray figures that rain wore down but never wore away. Schoolchildren were taught they were monuments. Priests called them warnings. The guild's oldest ledgers, the ones Ulyana balanced every night, called them *collateral*, a word she had always assumed was accounting poetry.

Up close, in the Warden's lantern light, they were neither monuments nor warnings nor collateral. They were people. A boy no older than fifteen, mouth open on a shout a century old. A standard-bearer, her banner dissolved to a brittle fan in her fist. Two soldiers of opposing armies fallen together, one man's hand still closed on the other's collar, frozen in the exact instant of becoming each other's death.

"They were sealed," the Warden said quietly, walking, "not killed. That distinction is the entire architecture of the Spire, and it is why we are in danger. Death ends. Sealing suspends. Everything suspended waits to resume—that is what suspension means. For a hundred years the Spire held them in the moment before the blow landed, and the war stayed a held breath, and my order kept the bellows full." He stopped. Raised the lantern. "Look at the ground, Taster."

Ulyana looked. The ash around the statue's feet—around the boy's feet—was disturbed. Not scattered by wind; wind wrote in long strokes, and this was short, repeated, rhythmic. It was the mark of something that had shifted its weight.

"When?" she asked, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"Three months ago, the first of them. One statue, shifting, once a night. A month ago it was a dozen. Now—" He walked her along the sealed line, and the lantern showed her what the dusk had hidden: hundreds of scuffed halos in the ash, thousands, the entire sleeping army restless in its century-long dream, pressing against the inside of the moment that held it. As she watched, in the deepening dark, the nearest statue moved.

It was not dramatic. That was what made it unbearable. The head turned perhaps the width of a coin, slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound, and gray grit sifted from the neck-joint with a sound like sand through an hourglass, and Ulyana—who had held a king's poisoned cup in her bare hands without flinching—took a step backward before she could stop herself.

"You see the arithmetic," the Warden said. His voice was gentle, and that gentleness was the cruelest thing she had heard in years, because she recognized it; it was the voice she used herself, serving bad news to the crown with sugar on the rim. "The seal is failing. Not tomorrow, perhaps not this year. But the bellows run dry soon, and when they do, twenty thousand soldiers finish the blow they began a century ago, and everything built since—the city, the court, the guild, your roof and your ledgers and your apprentice bending over his drawings—all of it stands on a battlefield that will remember what it was for."

"And the Spire," Ulyana said. "It can be refilled. Refueled. Whatever your order does."

"It can be resealed." He turned to face her fully, and the lantern threw his shadow long across the restless ash, so that it seemed to reach toward the stirring statues, or to be pulled by them. "But resealing requires what it required the first time. A Seal-Bearer. A living consent, freely given, poured into the lock. The prophecy names him precisely—I have read the naming a thousand times, Taster, and it does not soften with familiarity. *The apprentice of the spire, born under the third frost, whose blood the guild holds in trust: he shall close the door, or the door shall close upon the world.*"

The wind moved along the statue lines and the ash sighed, and somewhere in the dark a joint creaked, slow as a ship's rigging.

"You're asking me to deliver him," Ulyana said.

"I am asking you to save the world," said the Warden, with that same terrible mildness. "You keep calling it by uglier names, but let us be precise about what is being asked. Yes. The sealing consumes the bearer. Every time, in every age your guild's books record and mine records longer, the Spire takes a life to hold the war, and the life must be willing or the lock rejects it—that is why we cannot simply take him, though I'll not pretend the thought hasn't visited less patient men than me. He must choose. And he will not choose while he has you." The Warden's voice dropped, became almost intimate. "He loves you. I've watched them build him for this his whole apprenticeship—he is dutiful, he is brilliant, he would have gone to the lock like a bride if left alone. Then you. And now every night I feel the Spire hum differently, and I know it is humming *him*, and I know why he hesitates. You are the question mark in a sentence that was meant to end cleanly."

"So remove the question mark." Ulyana's hands were perfectly steady. She had learned that trick young, swallowing small deaths until death grew bored of her; apparently terror obeyed the same curriculum. "That's your offer, isn't it? Beneath the mercy. Deliver him—persuade him, arrange him, break whatever holds him to this life—or stand aside and watch the armies wake. Duty or the sword at the world's throat. You've dressed it beautifully, Warden, I'll grant you. Mercy wears duty well this season."

For a moment—one breath, no more—the mask slipped, and underneath it she glimpsed something that surprised her more than cruelty would have: exhaustion. Real exhaustion, a century deep, the fatigue of a man who had counted statues in the dark for years and memorized every one of their faces.

"Yes," he said simply. "That is the offer. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise, because you'd taste the lie the way you taste everything else. Deliver the Seal-Bearer to the Spire by midwinter, and the war sleeps another hundred years, and the world keeps its roofs and its ledgers and its children learn their monuments again. Fail, and..." He gestured, one open-handed motion at the vast dark field, at the sighing gray thousands, at the needle drinking the sky.

"And I lose him either way," Ulyana said.

The words were out before she could weigh them, and she hated them, and knew they were true. That was the elegance of the trap, and she gave the Warden credit for craftsmanship even as she wanted to put her thumbs through it: there was no path here that did not run through her heart. Give Cyrus to the Spire and lose him to the lock. Refuse, and lose him and everyone else to the waking. The Warden had not built the trap—the guild had built it, a century ago, buying bloodlines and arranging posts and dosing a girl with poisons until her body fit the prophecy's requirements—but he stood at its door holding it open with perfect courtesy, and asked her, gently, to walk in.

"Not necessarily," the Warden said.

Ulyana went very still. It was the stillness of the tasting-table, the stillness she wore when the first sip sat wrong on the tongue and the whole room waited to see if she would name it.

"What did you say?"

"There are older books than your guild's, Taster. Books with amendments in them. Margins with teeth." He was smiling again, faintly, and the lantern light made the smile unreadable—which, she understood suddenly, was exactly how he intended it. "The prophecy as written demands your apprentice. But prophecies, as your guild knows better than anyone alive, are written things. And written things can be..."

He let the sentence hang. He was too good to finish it. He would make her finish it herself, later, alone, in the guildhall at night with the forbidden book open under the lamp—she saw that now, saw that this whole night was a seed planted in ledger-paper soil—and he turned back toward the carriage as though the matter were settled, brushing ash from his sleeve.

"Midwinter," he said. "Bring me your answer by midwinter, and bring the boy sooner if you can persuade him. My carriage will take you home." At the carriage door he paused, half-turned, the smoked-glass amber of the far-off city glinting somewhere behind his shoulder. "For what it is worth, Taster—and it is worth very little—I am sorry it is you. I read your file. The immunity, the post, the whole architecture of your life. Someone arranged you very carefully a long time ago, and I have often wondered whether they knew they were building the one person who might refuse to stay arranged."

The carriage door shut. The wheels turned in the ash. And behind them, in the dark, the sealed army shifted in its sleep—one statue, then another, heads turning by coin-widths toward the sound of departing hope—and Ulyana pressed her hand flat against her ribs, over the ledger she kept there, and opened it in memory to the only entry that mattered, and read it again and again:

*Cyrus. Born under the third frost. Sealed.*

Over the roar of the wheels she was already composing the forgery.
