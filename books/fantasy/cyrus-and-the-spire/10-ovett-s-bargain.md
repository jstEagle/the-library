## 10. Ovett's Bargain

The guildhall had never felt small to her before.

Ulyana had walked its corridors since she was nine years old, when Guildmaster Ovett had found her on the steps of a debtor's prison, tasting gruel for coins, and said, *You have a talent for not dying, child. Let us make it a profession.* In all the years since, the hall had seemed to her limitless — its archive stairs descending farther than any building had a right to descend, its ceilings lost in pipe-smoke and shadow, its shelves holding more impossible things than the world outside held possible ones. Tonight it felt like a box. She could feel the walls of it, close and deliberate, the way a rat feels the sides of a cage it has only just noticed.

She climbed the narrow stair to Ovett's study without knocking. She had never knocked in her life; that was what belonging meant in the Guild of Impossible Things. You did not knock. You did not ask permission to enter the rooms where your own history was kept.

Tonight she knocked. It was a declaration, and Ovett heard it as one. His voice came through the door, older and flatter than she remembered it: "Come in, Ulyana. I wondered how long you'd stand out there deciding."

The study was exactly as it always was — low-ceilinged, cluttered with instruments whose purposes she knew and three or four whose purposes she suspected no one living knew. A brass orrery turned slowly above the hearth, modeling a solar system with two extra planets. The smell was ink, tallow, and the faint mineral tang of the Prophecy Ledger's vellum, which Ulyana now realized she could identify the way other people identified their mothers' perfumes.

Ovett sat behind his desk, and he had not prepared tea, and he had not prepared excuses, and Ulyana understood from both omissions that he had known this conversation was coming for a very long time. Perhaps longer than she had been alive.

"Say it plainly," she said. "I've had enough of margins."

"So have I," Ovett said, and something in his face — exhaustion, mostly, but underneath it a genuine and terrible tenderness — made her hate him a little less before she hated him more. "Sit down, girl."

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit." He folded his hands. "Because what I have to tell you takes longer standing up."

She stayed standing. Stubbornness was a currency she had always had in surplus, and she intended to spend it all tonight if necessary.

"Tell me about the Seal-Bearers," she said. "All of them. Not just Cyrus."

* * *

He told her.

He told it plainly, as she had asked, which was somehow worse than if he had dressed it up in guild cant and euphemism. Every hundred years, at midwinter, the seal on the Spire weakened — the great war of the Sundering, held in suspension inside that pale tower on the old battlefield, strained against its binding like a sleeping man against a nightmare. And every hundred years, the Spire required a bearer. A living person, drawn by bloodline and by prophecy, who would walk into the sealing chamber and consent to become the lock.

"They don't die," Ovett said. "I want that understood. Whatever else you think of us after tonight, understand that they do not die. They dream. They hold the door from inside the dream, and they hold it until the next weakening, and then—" he paused, and his hands tightened on one another— "and then the Spire takes the dream deeper. The first bearers lasted decades. Some say the first one is still in there, at the very bottom of it, holding the oldest hinge."

"And Cyrus is the current one."

"Cyrus is the ninth."

"Ninth." Ulyana's voice came out very steady, which surprised her. She had expected it to shake. Perhaps the immunity worked on more than poisons. "Nine people. Nine lives, arranged. Groomed. Led to a tower and put inside it like a cork in a bottle. And the guild did this."

"The guild *does* this," Ovett said. "It is the reason the guild exists, Ulyana. Everything else — the shadows, the names, the borrowed years, your ledgers — all of it is scaffolding. Revenue. Cover. The trade funds the stewardship. We keep the war asleep. That is the whole of it. That has been the whole of it for nine hundred years."

"By feeding it a person every century."

"Yes."

She turned away from him, to the window, because looking at his face while he said *yes* so simply was going to make her do something she could not take back. Below, the guildyard lay in lamplight, apprentices crossing between buildings, a cart of blank ledgers being unloaded as though paper were innocent.

"My post at court," she said. "The tastership. Was that mine?"

"It was arranged before you were born. Your mother was a taster; her mother before her. The poison tolerance builds in the blood across generations — small doses, mother to daughter, each generation carrying more than the last. The prophecy requires a Taster who can survive the sealing chamber unshielded. The air in there is not kind to ordinary constitutions." He said it gently, as if gentleness were the problem rather than the content. "You were raised to it the way Cyrus was raised to his spires. I will not pretend otherwise. But Ulyana — I chose you. Among all the candidates, when your grandmother died, I chose to bring you here myself. That part was mine. Whatever else was arranged, my fondness was not."

"Your fondness." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, too sharp, a blade with a bad edge. "Is that what you call it? Keeping me in the dark? Warning me off the Prophecy Ledger with that pious little speech about tasters touching what they shouldn't?"

"That warning saved your life more than once."

"Whose life does it save when you put Cyrus in the tower?"

Silence. The orrery turned. Somewhere below, an apprentice laughed at something, and the sound came up through the floor like a voice from another world — a world where the guild was still a family and the worst thing in it was a stolen shadow priced fairly.

"The Warden came to me," Ulyana said, still facing the window. "Before I came to you. He offered me a choice: deliver Cyrus, or watch the armies wake. I came here hoping you'd tell me the Warden lies. Hoping you'd say the guild has a third way. You're telling me the Warden was offering me the guild's own mercy with better manners."

"The Warden offers what the Spire requires. We offer what history requires. They have converged on the same door for nine hundred years." Ovett rose, joints cracking, and crossed to a cabinet she had never seen opened. From it he drew a ledger — not the Prophecy Ledger, but one of its grey, unremarkable cousins, bound in scuffed calfskin, the kind of book that looked like it contained grain tariffs. Except that when he set it on the desk between them, the candle flames all leaned toward it at once, every one, like grass bending under wind.

Ulyana stared at it. "What is that?"

"The Erasure Ledger." He said it the way a surgeon says the name of the knife. "The guild's oldest contingency. There are entries in it from before the Sundering. Do you know what erasure is, Ulyana? Truly? Not the street version — not a returned name, not a stolen shadow. I mean true erasure. The removal of a thing from record. All records. Every ledger, every register, every parish book, every letter, every memory. The guild wrote it once, long ago, as a weapon against fates too entrenched to fight any other way. It has been used twice. Both times, the results were—" he stopped. Started again. "There is no record of the results. That is rather the point."

"Why are you showing me this?"

"Because you asked for a third way." Ovett laid both hands flat on the grey ledger, and Ulyana saw that they were shaking — old Ovett, who had once held a live curse in his bare hands for six hours while they negotiated it into a jar, whose hands she had never seen shake. "And because there may be one. Not a good one. Not a clean one. But listen to me, girl, and listen carefully, because I am about to commit treason against nine hundred years of my office."

She sat down. Her legs decided it without consulting her.

* * *

"The prophecy binds by name and by blood," Ovett said. "That is its strength and its weakness. Fate, as recorded, is a chain of entries — this begets this, this leads to this, the ninth bearer consents at midwinter. The Spire reads the ledger of the world the way we read ours. If a name is struck from the record — truly struck, everywhere, root and branch — then the chain has nothing to grip. An unrecorded man cannot fulfill an entry that no longer refers to anyone. Fate slides off him like water off wax."

"You're saying erase Cyrus."

"I'm saying erase the name the prophecy uses. Whether that name and the man are the same thing afterward—" Ovett spread his hands. "That is the question no one alive can answer, because no one alive has watched it done."

Ulyana's heart was doing something strange, half hope and half horror, hope and horror shaking hands like old conspirators. "Then do it. Erase him. Tonight. Before midwinter."

"There is a cost." Ovett's voice dropped, and it was the drop that frightened her, the way his voice dropped when he priced the truly impossible things, the trades where someone always wept. "Erasure is total, Ulyana. It does not strike a name from books. It strikes a name from *minds*. Every memory that contains him. Every record that mentions him. Every mind that ever held him — parent, friend, the milkwoman who sold him his breakfast, the architect who taught him his first arch. All of it, gone, smooth, as if he had never stood in the world at all. The prophecy cannot find him because no thread leading to him survives. He becomes a gap wearing a coat."

"All right," she said. "All right. People forget him. He runs. He lives. Isn't that better than a tower?"

"He would not remember himself." Ovett said it softly, and the softness was the cruelty of it. "His own memory is a record, girl. His name in his own head is an entry. To erase him completely — to leave nothing fate can follow — is to erase him from himself. He would walk out of that erasure a stranger with no past, and the world would close around the gap so neatly he'd never know to look for it. Or—" and here Ovett looked at her, directly, with an expression she had never seen on him before and hoped never to see again, the expression of a man about to hand over the bill— "*or*, the erasure can be anchored. Bound to a single surviving record. One memory, held by one keeper, outside the guild's books, outside the world's books, a private ledger of one entry. The erasure takes everything except what the keeper holds. The man keeps his self, his past, his name, because one person in all the world remembers him faithfully enough to anchor him."

Ulyana understood then. She understood it several seconds before Ovett finished speaking, the way one understands a fall has already begun while still at the top of the stairs.

"And the keeper?" she asked, though she knew. Of course she knew. She had spent her whole life knowing things a moment before they became unbearable. "What happens to the keeper?"

"The anchor holds because the keeper holds it. Continually. Consciously. For as long as the erasure lasts." Ovett closed his eyes. "If the keeper ever lets the memory go — if she forgets him for even a moment, if she sleeps too deeply, if she dies — the anchor fails, and the erasure completes itself, and everything she was holding goes with it. She would spend the rest of her life remembering him, Ulyana. Every hour. Deliberately. While everyone around her forgot, while the world forgot, while *he* perhaps forgot her in turn if the erasure reaches asymmetrically, which it sometimes does — and she could never speak of him, because speech is a record, and speech would unravel the work. She would carry him alone, in silence, in a world with no room for him anywhere but inside her. That is the price. That is the third way you asked for, and I am ashamed to be the one handing it to you, and I would rather cut off my own hand than show you that book, and I showed it to you anyway, because you are the best thing I ever stole and I will not watch them cork you into that tower next."

The candle flames leaned toward the grey ledger. The orrery turned through its impossible heavens.

Ulyana thought about the roof. About Cyrus's laugh, the specific pitch of it, the way he said her name with the stress on the second syllable as though it were a word in a language he alone spoke properly. She thought about the taste of arsenic, faintly sweet, and how she had learned to smile while her blood catalogued the damage. She thought about a century-old page with tonight written on it, and a boy walking toward a tower because a book told him to, and herself standing aside because a guild told her to, and she found — with something like relief, actually, relief like a blade coming out of a wound — that there was no decision in her at all. There was only arithmetic, and she had always been good with numbers.

"Me," she said. "I'm the keeper."

"If anyone is. The anchor must be love, or it doesn't hold. That's not sentiment; that's mechanics. The binding feeds on attention, and only love generates attention of the right... density. I've seen the old entries." He pushed the grey ledger an inch toward her. "The offer stands until midnight. After midnight I seal the cabinet, and the Warden gets his choice instead: the boy in the tower, or the war awake. Those are the three futures, Ulyana. The tower. The war. Or a lifetime of remembering a man alone, in silence, while the world forgets he existed — and maybe, maybe, a fate with no place to grab him, and a road that leads somewhere none of the books predict."

She reached for the ledger.

Her hand stopped above the cover. Not fear — she had trained fear out of herself dose by dose since childhood, poison by poison, until fear itself had developed a tolerance to her. What stopped her was simpler and worse. She was trying to imagine forgetting Cyrus, and she couldn't. Even hypothetically. Her mind refused to model it, the way a healthy body refuses poison, and she understood that this refusal — this stubborn, bodily inability to let him go — was precisely what would make her a good anchor, and precisely what would make the rest of her life a sentence served in full.

"One question," she said. "If I hold him — if I'm the keeper — does the prophecy still find him? Does the Spire still come for its ninth bearer?"

Ovett was quiet a long moment. Outside, somewhere over the rooftops toward the war-field, a bell began to ring the hour, and Ulyana counted the strokes automatically, the taster's habit, marking time by anything that might be the last safe thing.

"I don't know," Ovett said finally. "No one knows. That is what makes it a gamble instead of a plan. But Ulyana — there is one more thing, and I need you to hear it before you touch that book." He drew a breath that seemed to cost him years. "The Prophecy Ledger changed yesterday. New writing appeared in the margin of the midwinter page. Ink no one can date, in a hand the archivists swear belongs to no living scribe. It says the ninth bearer will refuse." His eyes came up to hers, bright and wet and afraid. "It says *the bearer refuses, and the key is given freely*. Ulyana — whatever you're about to do, do it knowing the book itself seems to already know."

* * *

Below the study window, far off across the dark city, the Spire stood pale against the stars, and Ulyana would have sworn — then and ever after — that she felt it hum, once, like a struck glass, like a throat clearing before it speaks.
