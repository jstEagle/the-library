## 12. Ledger-Burning

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old paper, as it always did, but that night the smell had a wrongness in it, like meat beginning to turn. Ulyana noticed it the moment she came down the back stair from the roof—Cyrus's warmth still on her mouth, his promise still in her chest like a swallowed coal—and she stood a long moment in the corridor of ledgers with her hand flat against the shelf, listening.

The guildhall was never silent. Even at the dead hour before dawn there were sounds: the scratch of a scribe's pen in the counting room, the soft argument of clerks over a disputed year, the breathing of the building itself, all that timber and stone settling around its cargo of impossible things. Tonight there was only one sound, and it was footsteps. Many of them. Moving together, in step, toward the Vault of First Entries.

She knew those steps. She had spent ten years learning to read people by their walk—the way a poisoner reads a pulse—and so she knew Sef's tread, heavy on the heel, patient as dripping water; and she knew the quick dry shuffle of the taster-masters who had trained her; and beneath them, worse, the lighter feet of apprentices. Children she had shared a bench with. Children who had been taught, as she had been taught, that the guild took no sides.

*The neutral guild takes sides at last,* Ovett had said to her once, laughing, when she'd asked why they traded for both armies in a border war. *We are the space between the knives, little crane. That is our whole country.* Now the knives were walking through the halls of the space between them, and they were carrying lamps, and lamp-oil was not what lamps were for.

She should have gone out the way she came in. She was a taster; she knew how to be small, how to be nothing, how to stand in a doorway and be furniture. But the Vault of First Entries lay at the end of that corridor, and behind its iron door, on its lectern of black oak, under three locks and a blessing, lay the Prophecy Ledger—the book she had touched against every law, whose blanking pages she had seen, whose margins held her great-grandmother's hidden hand. The book that had authored Cyrus down to the words he said on a rooftop.

*If you burn fate,* Sef had argued in the counting room, weeks ago, in the mild reasonable voice he used for teaching apprentices to hold poison under the tongue without swallowing, *you free him. You free everyone. No written word, no written end.*

*And everything the guild has ever bought or sold becomes ash with it,* Ovett had answered. *Every returned name. Every borrowed year. Every debt we hold in trust for people who will come asking.*

*Then let them ask an empty sky,* Sef had said, and smiled, and Ulyana had understood, with the cold clarity of a woman tasting arsenic in honeyed wine, that he had already decided, and that the counting room debate had been not a debate but a rehearsal.

Now the rehearsal was over.

She moved along the shelves instead of through them, into the narrow dark between the ledger-cases where the guild kept the trades too dangerous to display—a stolen shadow coiled in a jar like a sleeping eel, a name that had never existed folded into a lead box. The oil smell grew stronger. Light flickered at the corridor's far end, orange and jolly, the color of a festival.

They had already begun in the Annex of Borrowed Years. She heard the wet crackle of flame taking paper, heard someone—a girl's voice, young, frightened or exalted, impossible to tell—say, "It doesn't scream, look, it doesn't even scream," and heard Sef answer, calm as a man confirming a delivery: "Books don't scream. That is rather the point."

Ulyana pressed herself into the dark and did the arithmetic she had been doing since the roof, since Cyrus's mouth, since the Warden showed her the ash-statues stirring on the war-field like a field of sleepers turning over. If the Ledger burned, Cyrus would be unauthored—but the seal was failing now, tonight, this season, and the Spire needed a Seal-Bearer whether or not any book said so. Burning the prophecy would not stop the war from waking. It would only stop anyone from knowing how to close it again. Sef wanted to burn the map because he could not bear what the map said. And the fire would take everything else with it—every record, every name, every small mercy the guild had ever brokered—because Sef's mercy had always been the large, clean kind, the kind that burned villages to save them.

The iron door of the Vault stood open.

That was the thing that made her blood go cold and quiet, colder than any poison had ever made it. Three locks and a blessing, and the door stood open, and Ovett stood inside beside the lectern, alone, his old hands flat on the black oak cover of the Prophecy Ledger, his back to the door, facing the book the way a man faces a judge.

"Guildmaster." Sef's voice, from the doorway, gentle. "You opened it yourself. I want you to know I'll record that in your favor, if there's anything left to record it in."

"There is always something left to record it in," Ovett said. His voice was steady, and tired, and Ulyana heard in it the voice of every night he had sat with her over the impossible columns and taught her that numbers could be kind if you carried them carefully. "That is the entire trade, Sef. Go home. Take your children home. Whatever the Warden promised you for looking away, it isn't worth—"

"The Warden?" A laugh, dry as struck flint. "The Warden wants the book whole. That's why it must burn. Let him keep his seal and his sacrifice and his hundred-year arithmetic. Let him find his next vessel without a map." A pause, and then, softer, almost tender, which was somehow the worst thing yet: "You've grown fond of the Crane. Fondness makes archivists of us all—we start wanting to edit. Step aside, Guildmaster. I'll write you a good ending."

"I have written enough endings," Ovett said. "I am keeping this one open."

Ulyana saw it then, in the lamplight spilling through the door: Sef raising the lamp, not throwing it, holding it up like a verdict, and the oil slopping golden over his wrist, and Ovett turning—not aside, but forward, arms opening around the Ledger the way a father turns to shield a child from a thrown glass—and she understood, in the half-second the world gave her, exactly what she was going to do, and hated it, and did it anyway.

She came out of the dark between the cases and threw the jar.

The stolen shadow broke across the floorboards and woke up.

It had been in the jar for sixty years and it had opinions. It went through the taster-masters like a rumor through a court, and the lamplight went out with it, and the screaming started—not the books' screaming, which was silence, but the men's, which was human and satisfying and brief. In the sudden absolute dark Ulyana ran on memory, ten years of nights in these corridors, past the third case, past the smell of burning years, to the lectern, where Ovett still stood wrapped around the great book with firelight blooming on his shoulders from the Annex beyond.

"Little crane," he said, and she felt him smile in the dark more than saw it. "I told you never to touch it."

"You told me never to get caught touching it," she said, and got her arms under the Ledger—it weighed like a sleeping animal, like guilt, like a century—and pulled. He let it go. That was the terrible thing, she thought later; he let it go so easily, as if he had only been holding it until she arrived.

"Go," he said. "Down the record-stair. The fire will eat the upper floors first; paper draws flame like a wound draws pus. I'll follow."

"You'll follow," she repeated, because she was a taster and could hear a lie in a sentence the way she heard arsenic in wine, and this sentence was full of it, brimming, sweetened.

"Ulyana." Her guild name, in his mouth, for perhaps the last time. "I bought your post at court. I built your immunity dose by dose. I have spent twenty years arranging a girl into a weapon, and I am not proud of it, but I will not watch the instrument break itself on the anvil. The book goes. You go. Those are the two entries that matter tonight."

"And you?"

"I," said Ovett, "am the guildmaster. The guildhall is my entry." And he turned from her, deliberately, the way he turned from a finished column, and walked back toward the light and the shouting and the fire, and began, in his dry carrying voice, the voice that had quelled a thousand disputes over disputed years, to call the roll of the guild—one name after another after another, the living and the dead and the sold, calling them as though the burning building were a counting room and every soul in it must be accounted for before the books closed.

Ulyana went down the record-stair with the Ledger against her chest and the heat climbing after her like a tide, and she did not cry, because tasters do not swallow their own poisons, and grief, she had learned, was only love with nowhere left to go, and hers had somewhere to go: it had a war-field at the end of it, and a Spire, and a boy the size of a fate.

Behind her, above her, the guildhall burned. The Annex of Borrowed Years went up in a fountain of sparks—decades of purchased time igniting all at once, and she swore she felt it, a shudder through the stones, hours being given back to the air. The Hall of Returned Names followed, and somewhere in that roar she heard Sef's voice raised high and cracked, no longer calm, cursing the shadow, cursing the smoke, cursing a girl, and she allowed herself one thin smile in the dark of the stair.

At the bottom, in the delivery yard among the carts, she stopped and looked back. The guildhall wore fire like a crown. Against it, in a high window, a small straight figure stood with his arms at his sides, still calling the roll, and she raised her hand, and could not tell if he saw.

Then the window filled with light, and the figure was gone, and the Prophecy Ledger grew heavy and warm against her ribs like a second heart, and Ulyana Crane turned east, toward the war-field, toward the Spire, toward the dawn and the boy who was prophesied to die in it.

She did not see the rider detach from the shadows of the gate—did not see the Warden's gray livery, or the sealed letter in the rider's glove bearing her description and a single instruction: *the Taster, alive; the book, intact.*

But the Spire, thirty miles away, hummed—and in his tower room Cyrus woke with her name in his mouth, and the taste of smoke in the dream.
