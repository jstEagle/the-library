## 14. The Reading of the Book

They bound her hands with ledger-ribbon.

Ulyana noticed that first, before the cold of the cellars, before the smell of wet stone and tallow and old ink—the ribbon itself. It was guild stock, the heavy cream-colored kind reserved for entries of the first magnitude, for trades so impossible they required two witnesses and a wax seal cooled in river water. Sef's people had cut it into cords, and they had tied her wrists behind her back with it as though the material itself would vouch for them. As though fate approved.

The cellar beneath the burned guildhall still stood, which was more than could be said for anything above it. Three days had passed since the fire—or three nights; she had lost count in the dark—and the ceiling above her head wept fine grey ash through every crack, sifting down like patient snow. Somewhere overhead, the guildhall she had grown up in was a black ribcage open to the sky. Ovett was somewhere in it too, or under it. She did not let herself finish that thought. She carried it instead the way she carried all her poisons: sealed, labeled, and never opened indoors.

"Sit her up," said Sef.

Hands took her by the shoulders—gentle hands, which was somehow worse than rough ones—and propped her against a pillar. The cellar had been the deep archive once, before Ovett moved the oldest volumes upstairs to better light. Now its shelves were half-empty, half-burned, and in the center of the floor, on a table that must have been carried down through smoke and falling beams, sat the Prophecy Ledger.

It was unmarked. Of course it was. Fire had taken the roof, the staircases, the great reading room with its forty lamps and its rule of silence, and the Ledger sat among the ruins untouched, its covers dark as pond ice, its pages—she knew without opening them—still faintly warm, the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire dies. Books that recorded fate did not burn. She had suspected this for years. She had hoped, these last three days, to be wrong about something.

Sef stood over the table. He was not a large man; he had spent thirty years tasting for the crown alongside her mother's generation, and his body had been pared down by caution to something lean and bloodless, like a candle burned in a draftless room. His eyes were the pale yellow of old varnish. When he looked at Ulyana, he looked at her the way a man looks at an entry he has already decided how to balance.

"You're wondering why you're alive," he said.

"I'm wondering why you are," Ulyana said. "You burned the only building in this city that was ever honest."

"Honest." He turned a page of the Ledger with one finger, delicately, the way she herself turned pages when she feared what they might say. "Honesty is a word tasters use for poison they've already survived. The guild was never honest, Crane. It was *neutral*. And neutrality died three days ago when Ovett chose a boy over a hundred years of stewardship. When the Warden's agents came through our doors with fire in their hands." He closed the cover softly. "We didn't burn the hall to free Cyrus Vale from his fate, whatever you believe. We burned it because the Ledger cannot be allowed to fall into the hands of men who read it as instruction."

"And your plan is to read it yourself."

"My plan," Sef said, "is to destroy the page."

The cellar went very quiet. Even the ash seemed to stop falling. Two of Sef's people stood by the door—a young woman Ulyana recognized from the counting room, Maren, who had once shown her a trick for balancing borrowed years across a fiscal divide; and a broad silent man whose name she had never learned, who had the look of someone who had done his grieving already and found there wasn't much left afterward.

"You can't destroy a page of the Ledger," Ulyana said. "Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the guild *says*," Sef interrupted, mild as milk. "That the book is older than the guild. That it writes itself. That a forged entry, read aloud at the Spire, becomes true, and therefore the book must never be altered, because whoever alters it becomes fate's author, and no one should hold that pen." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has tasted something bitter for so long he has begun to mistake the bitterness for flavor. "I have believed those rules my whole life, Crane. I kept them. And do you know what I learned? The rules are real. Every word of them. The book *is* older than us. It *does* write itself. A forged prophecy read aloud *does* become true. All of that is true, and all of it is exactly why the page must burn."

He leaned over the Ledger again, and this time he did not turn pages gently. He tore.

The sound was wrong—not paper tearing but something deeper, a sound like ice cracking on a winter river, like a bone giving way inside a body. Ulyana flinched though the bindings held her. Where Sef gripped the corner of a page, the paper resisted and then yielded all at once, and a smell rose off it that she knew intimately: ozone and copper and the faint sweetness of arsenic, the smell of her own trade, the smell of small deaths carefully administered. Fate bled when you cut it. Who knew.

"The Sundering page," Sef said, holding the torn leaf up to the lamplight. "One hundred years of stewardship, four Seal-Bearers fed into the Spire like coins into a lock, and here is the accounting." He let the page hang. "Burn the page, and the prophecy dies unwritten. Burn the page, and Cyrus Vale is only a boy again—an apprentice with ink-stained fingers and a girl on a roof. No seal demanded. No sacrifice scheduled. The war wakes, yes. The armies wake, and the city burns, and perhaps the world. But it will burn *free*, not according to schedule, not according to a book written by dead men who never asked anyone's consent." His voice did not rise at all. That was the frightening thing about Sef; he had the flat delivery of a man reading figures aloud. "I have tasted for three kings, Crane. I know poison when I see it. This book is poison, and I am done administering it in small doses."

"It won't work," Ulyana said quietly.

"No?"

"The Ledger isn't paper with writing on it. You know that too—you just don't want to say it. The paper is just where it happens to be legible. Burn the page and the prophecy doesn't die. It goes somewhere else. Into dreams. Into the Spire's humming. Into the mouths of people who wake up speaking sentences they don't understand." She held his gaze. "You can't unread fate, Sef. You can only choose who pays for it."

Something moved behind his eyes—hurt, maybe, or its twin, certainty. "Then we'll test your theory," he said. "Because there is a step before burning. There is a reason I haven't burned it yet, three days, while the ash falls." He set the torn page down, smoothing it against the table with both palms, almost tenderly. "The book accepts amendments read aloud. Ovett told you that, I think. Or you found it yourself—you were always in the archives when you shouldn't be. So: we read it. All of it. Aloud, in this room, witnessed. If the prophecy is truly fixed, the reading changes nothing and we burn the page. But if the book is listening—" his yellow eyes came up to hers "—then perhaps fate can be negotiated with before it is executed. Perhaps there are terms we haven't seen. The guild taught me that every contract has margins, Crane. Even this one."

"That's not why you want me to read it."

"No," Sef agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They untied her hands because, Sef explained, the Ledger required it—an unbound reader was a coerced reader, and coerced readings did not take. Ulyana flexed her fingers and felt the ribbon-marks fading from her wrists and thought: *they need me willing. Everything in this story keeps needing me willing.* She filed the thought away the way she filed everything, in the locked interior room where she kept the poisons and the plans, and she walked to the table on legs that did not shake, because shaking was a luxury and she had never once been able to afford it.

Maren lit two more lamps. The broad man went to stand by the stairs, arms folded, watching the ceiling as if he expected the fire to return for a second course. The Prophecy Ledger lay open under the light, and up close Ulyana could see the wound Sef had made: a ragged absence at the lower edge of the great spread, the Sundering page maimed but not killed, its remaining lines still legible, still patient. Ink on that page did not sit *on* the paper. It sat slightly *above* it, the way frost sits on a window, and if you tilted your head the words shifted a hair's breadth, as though deciding, even now, whether to mean what they said.

"From the beginning," Sef said. "The Sundering entry. Read it exactly as written. No interpretation. Interpretation is how tasters lie."

"I've never lied with interpretation in my life," Ulyana said. "Lying is a craft of precision. Interpretation is for priests."

But she bent to the page, and the smell of it rose to meet her—that copper-and-ozone sweetness, stronger now, the smell of the torn edge still bleeding—and she began to read.

She had expected the old language, the high ceremonial diction the guild used for its public face, all *in the year of the second silence* and *when the towers remember their names*. What she got was plainer and far worse. The prophecy wrote like a ledger. It wrote the way she wrote.

"*In the century of the standing armies,*" she read, and her own voice sounded strange to her, flattened, professional, "*the war that was stopped shall be kept stopped by a door, and the door shall be kept by a bearer.*"

The lamplight guttered though there was no draft.

"*The bearer shall be born of the purchased line, stewarded seven generations, marked by the hum.*" Ulyana's throat tightened around the words *purchased line*, but she did not stop; Sef was watching her lips like a man checking a count. "*He shall be named twice: once by his mother, and once by the stone. The stone's name is older. In the hour of the seal's failing, the stone's name shall be the true one, and the boy shall go into the Spire consenting, and the door shall close upon him, and the war shall sleep another hundred years.*"

There it was. The whole architecture of it, laid out in ink that hovered a hair above the page: Cyrus's life, itemized. His birth, his apprenticeship, his dreams, his name, his death dressed up as duty. She had known it since the night on the roof when she found today's date written a hundred years in advance. Knowing it and hearing it were different animals. Hearing it, in her own voice, in a cellar full of ash, she felt the horror of it enter her body the way poison entered a body—first nothing, then a slow spreading heat, then the beginning of damage.

"Continue," Sef said.

"There's more," she said. Because there was. Her eyes had reached the bottom third of the page, and the bottom third was not empty.

It was crowded.

The margins of the Prophecy Ledger—which she had examined a dozen times in her life, which every apprentice examined, which were famous for being blank, famously, reassuringly blank, *the margins stay empty until the book needs them*—were full. They were full of a second hand. Smaller script, pressed hard, slanting downhill as though written in haste or grief or both, in ink that had gone brown with age: annotations, emendations, marginal prophecies nested inside the main prophecy like worms inside fruit. And she knew the hand. She had spent years of her childhood tracing it in the ledgers of the deep archive, learning her letters from it, learning her numbers from it. She knew it the way you know your own reflection.

Her great-grandmother's hand.

"*And the door requires a key,*" Ulyana read, and her voice dropped without her choosing it, dropped into the smaller register of the smaller script, "*and the key shall be drawn from the second line, the line that serves, the line that tastes.*"

The cellar tilted. Or she did. Maren caught the lamp before it fell; nobody caught Ulyana, because she did not fall, because she had trained herself out of falling the way she had trained herself out of everything else, but for a moment the world was at a bad angle and the ash hung suspended in the lamplight like a held breath.

"*The Taster,*" she read. Her mouth was dry. Somewhere very far away, Sef had gone utterly still. "*Who will be raised immune to every poison but the one that matters, and posted at the crown's table not to save the king but to season the bearer's path, and who will choose, in the failing hour, between the seal and the sword.*"

"Stop," Sef said.

She didn't. She couldn't have stopped now any more than she could have stopped mid-swallow. "*The Taster will love the bearer,*" said the margin, in her great-grandmother's brown ink, in her great-grandmother's downhill slant, "*and this is not a flaw in the design. This is the design. For a bearer who does not consent closes nothing, and a bearer who is loved may be persuaded, and a Taster who loves may be spent.*"

"*Choose the seal,*" the margin said, "*and she gives her name, her immunity, and her recorded self, and lives unrecorded forever, the key turned in the lock.*"

"*Choose the sword,*" the margin said, "*and she cuts the bearer free, and the door stands open, and the waking world learns why the war was stopped.*"

"*Either way, she chooses. Either way, she pays. This amendment is not prophecy. This amendment is a bill.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. The ash resumed falling, one grey fleck settling on the open page, and the page absorbed it—she watched it happen, watched the ash sink into the hovering ink and vanish, as if the book were eating even that.

"She was always in the book," Maren whispered, from somewhere near the wall. "The Taster. It was always—there was always supposed to be a—"

"Yes," Ulyana said. Her voice came out level. She was distantly proud of that. "Me. Or someone shaped like me. Someone bred like me." She looked down at the marginal script, at the great-grandmother she had never met, whose notes she had followed through the archive like breadcrumbs, who had forged a prophecy once herself and paid some price Ulyana was only beginning to guess at. *A bill.* Her whole life—the taster's post, the small doses, the arranged meeting on a roof with a boy whose bloodline had been bought a century ago—even her love had been a line item. She had spent chapters of her life believing she was invisible, unrecorded, a servant moving through the margins of other people's fates. And here were the margins, and she filled them.

And underneath the horror—underneath it, tangled in it, inseparable from it—was the other thing. The strange relief, warm and shameful, rising in her chest like brandy. *She was never invisible either.* The book had seen her. A hundred years ago, a dead woman's hand had reached across the century and written *her* into existence, had promised her a choice, had guaranteed—at the cost of everything—that she would not be furniture. She had a role. She had a terrible, ruinous, non-negotiable role. Some starved part of her that had lived her whole life on scraps of belonging received this monstrous inheritance and called it, treacherously, *being seen.*

"I said stop," Sef repeated. He had not moved, but his stillness had changed quality; it was the stillness of a man recalculating everything. "Read me the last line again. The sword."

"*Choose the sword, and she cuts the bearer free, and the door stands open.*" Ulyana looked up at him. "You understand what this means. Your fire changes nothing. Burning the Sundering page doesn't free Cyrus—it just removes the seal from the menu. The margin remains. *She* remains. The choice remains. The book doesn't need the page, Sef. The book needs *me*."

"That's not possible," Sef said. "The margins are meant to be blank. The margins have always been—"

"They're blank," Ulyana said, "until the book needs them. Your own catechism. You taught it to me when I was nine." She turned her wrists toward him, showing the fading marks of the ledger-ribbon. "The book needed a Taster a hundred years ago, and it wrote one. My great-grandmother wrote one. And now the seal is failing, and the armies are stirring in their ash, and the Warden is circling, and Ovett is dead or buried, and everyone in this cellar thinks they're holding the pen." She straightened. Behind her back, out of habit rather than hope, her fingers found the seam of her sleeve where three years ago she had learned to hide a thin glass vial against the skin of her forearm—empty now, they'd searched her, but the gesture steadied her anyway, the way touching a familiar tool steadies any craftsman. "So here is my reading, Sef. Not interpretation. Arithmetic. You wanted to negotiate with fate before executing it? Fine. Fate has named its terms, and the terms are me. Which means the negotiation is mine."

Sef studied her for a long moment. Around them the ruined cellar breathed its ash, and overhead, faint through the broken floors of the dead guildhall, came a sound none of them had heard in this neighborhood before tonight—a low, resonant hum, rising and falling, patient as tide. The Spire, two miles distant, singing through the stones of the city. Through the bones of the building. Through the page.

Through her.

Sef reached out and, with two fingers, turned the Ledger toward himself. "There's one more thing the margin says," he said quietly. "At the very bottom. Under the bill. Read it."

Ulyana bent. The last line of her great-grandmother's hand was smaller than the rest, cramped against the page's edge as if the writer had run out of room, or courage, or time:

"*If you are reading this, granddaughter: the sword is a lie. There is no third option written here—but I forged one once, and so can you. Look for my notes beneath the Vess carvings. Hurry. The Spire is already singing to you.*"

The hum swelled. The lamps flared green for half a heartbeat, the color of arsenic, the color of her whole life.

And Ulyana understood that the book had not brought her here to be read.

It had brought her here to answer.
