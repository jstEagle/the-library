## 1. Salt in the Wind

The wind came off the Thornfells in the hour before dawn, and every morning it told Sebastian Reyes the same lie: that she was no one.

She had learned to hear it as a kind of blessing. *No one*, it said, rattling the shutters of the tannery where she rented a room above the curing vats. *No one*, it said, dragging woodsmoke and brine through the crooked lanes of Saltmere, the border town that smelled of fish guts and old grievances. No one worth a bounty. No one worth a rope. In nine years, she had made the lie so seamless that even she sometimes believed it — and on those mornings when she almost did, she pressed her palm flat against the floorboards and felt, faintly, like a second heartbeat buried under her own, the thing she was not allowed to be.

The storm-gift. The Reyes inheritance. The reason her grandmother's name was still spoken in the capital the way people spoke of plagues.

In Saltmere they knew her as Bren. Bren the pass-runner, Bren who could carry two hundred pounds of contraband silk over scree that would break a mule's leg, Bren who never talked about where she'd come from because nobody in a smuggling town ever did. It was a good name. It fit her like a coat cut for someone slightly larger, slightly blunter, someone whose hands were only scarred from rope and rock instead of from gripping lightning out of the air to keep it from killing everyone around her.

She dressed in the dark, wound her hair flat beneath a wool cap, and went down to the harbor to meet the tide.

* * *

Saltmere lived by two economies: the herring that came in with the spring shoals, and the goods that came over the mountains from the free cities beyond Thornfell. The crown taxed the first and forbade the second, which meant half the town was honest and the other half was fed. Sebastian ran for Marta Okonkwo, a widow with a ledger sharper than any customs blade, and tonight's cargo was good: forty bolts of Vellorine silk rolled into oilskin, plus a small sealed case Marta had handled herself, saying only, "Don't open it, don't drop it, don't ask."

The pass road left Saltmere through a notch in the cliffs called the Shepherd's Throat, and from there it climbed for two days through country that had never decided whether it belonged to the sea or the sky. Sebastian knew every switchback, every shelf of loose stone, every hollow where the wind funneled hard enough to knock a loaded pack sideways. She knew them the way you know your own scars — not because you studied them, but because you made them.

The first day was clean work. Cold sun, firm trail, the silk riding easy against her spine. She passed the shrine at Halfway Cairn where travelers left ribbons for the mountain saints, and she did not leave one, because the saints of Thornfell had been dead a long time, and besides, what would she ask for? Forgiveness was for people who regretted their choices. She had chosen exile the way you choose amputation: badly, quickly, and in order to live.

It was on the second afternoon, coming down through the high meadows above the town, that the weather turned against her.

She felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the pressure, a taste like copper coins at the back of her throat. Her gift woke without permission, the way an old dog lifts its head at a sound no one else hears yet. *Storm*, something in her said. *Coming fast, off the ridge, wrong season, wrong shape.* A squall in high summer, black-bellied, moving down the mountainside like spilled ink.

Below her, strung along the last mile of trail between the tree line and the sea cliffs, were the herring crews walking home. And ahead of them, small and quick and utterly oblivious, a child.

A boy, maybe six, chasing a gull along the cliff path with his arms out like wings. Behind him a woman — his mother, presumably, burdened with baskets, calling his name in the tone mothers used when they'd called it too many times already. The squall would reach the cliff path within minutes. Sebastian had seen what a storm like that did to an exposed coast road. It would peel the boy off the rocks like a leaf off a branch.

Keep walking. That was the discipline. Nine years of discipline. You did not touch the weather; you did not look at the weather too long; you certainly did not *calm* the weather, because calming a storm left fingerprints the way strangling did, and there were people paid to read those prints. The crown's gift-hunters had noses for it. One act of mercy, one moment of weakness, and the careful architecture of her life came down like a felled tree.

She kept walking. She counted her own footsteps, which was what she did instead of praying.

Then the wind hit the meadow grass ahead of her, flattening it in a running wave, and the boy on the cliff path stumbled, and the gull screamed, and the mother dropped her baskets, and Sebastian stopped counting.

There was no decision in it. That was the truth she would chew on for weeks afterward — that all her discipline amounted to nothing, that the choice had been made long ago by whatever stubborn thing in her blood refused to watch children die when it had hands. She set down the pack. She opened herself.

Opening was the closest word. It was like unclenching a fist she had held shut for nine years, and it hurt, the way blood hurts returning to a numb limb. The storm poured into her senses — its weight, its spin, its childish rage — and she reached into that rage and took hold.

Not fighting it. Fighting a storm was how you died. You *soothed* it. You became the eye it didn't know it needed. She pulled the charge out of the clouds the way you draw a splinter, slow and steady, letting the lightning ground itself through her bones in thin, singing threads that should have cooked her and only left her shaking. The wind laid itself down. The black belly of the squall tore open into harmless rain, then less than rain, a gray drizzle sulking out to sea.

On the cliff path, the boy sat down hard in the wet grass and started laughing, because to him it had been nothing — a gust, a stumble, a summer shower. His mother gathered him up and hurried on, glancing once at the sky with the vague unease of coastal folk who trusted the weather the way you trust a dog that has bitten before.

Sebastian stood in the emptying meadow with her arms at her sides and lightning still whispering under her skin, and she understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what she had done.

* * *

She was three steps back to her pack when she saw the man.

He stood up-slope among the boulders, half-sheltered under an overhang where a sensible traveler would wait out rain. He wasn't dressed like a traveler. Travelers in Thornfell wore layers and looked apologetic about existing. This man wore a good oilcloth coat, dry despite the downpour, and boots too fine for the trail, and he was watching the cliff path below with the fixed attention of a man counting heads.

He turned. Looked straight at her.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Rain dripped off the overhang between them. Sebastian's mind ran cold and fast through everything she must look like right now — a woman standing alone in a meadow where a freak storm had just died politely, her cap soaked, her face doing gods-knew-what. She arranged it into blankness, the smuggler's mask, the one that said *I'm just tired and I want my dinner.*

"You got caught in it," the man said. Not a question. His voice had the rounded vowels of the capital, the accent of people who gave orders rather than took them. "Bad luck. These summer squalls come off the ridge without warning."

"They do," Sebastian agreed. Her voice came out low and bored. Bren's voice. "You'll want to get down before dark. Trail's treacherous when it's wet."

"Mm." He looked at her a moment longer — at her hands, specifically, which she realized too late were still trembling, faintly, with spent charge. She curled them into fists. "You're one of Okonkwo's runners, aren't you? Bren."

"I don't discuss my employers with strangers on mountainsides."

"Smart." He smiled, and it was a pleasant smile, which somehow made it worse. "I'm a surveyor. Crown charter. Mapping the passes." He tapped the leather tube slung at his shoulder, the kind that carried maps, or papers, or other things. "Strange weather we've been having. Strange everywhere, my colleagues say. Storms behaving oddly all along the range."

"Coastal weather," Sebastian said. "It does what it likes."

"It does," he said softly. "It always seems to do exactly what someone likes, doesn't it, when you're near."

He touched two fingers to his hat brim — courteous, almost mocking — and walked on down toward Saltmere, his stride unhurried, a man with nowhere in particular to be and all the time in the world to be there.

Sebastian stood in the rain until he was a speck on the trail, and then until he was nothing, and then a while longer.

*Surveyor*. Nobody surveyed these passes. Every smuggler from here to the free cities had them mapped to the pace-count, and the crown had never cared where the trails went, only what came down them. A surveyor was a story men told when they were hunting something and wanted you to relax about it.

And he had seen. Maybe not everything — maybe he'd only seen a woman standing in a meadow where the impossible hadn't happened, and filed it away the way informants filed everything, against future use. But maybe he'd seen the storm bend. Maybe he'd watched the lightning go quiet like a scolded animal, and known what he was looking at, because knowing was his trade the way hiding was hers.

Nine years. Nine years of false names and flattened hair and weather left strictly alone, of hearing drownings happen in other people's stories and keeping her eyes on her own boots. Undone in four seconds, by a child, a gull, and the oldest reflex she owned.

She shouldered the pack. The sealed case rode against her spine, and Marta's voice echoed in her head — *don't open it, don't drop it, don't ask* — and for the first time in years, Sebastian found herself wondering if the widow's cargo was the least interesting secret currently traveling this road.

By the time she reached Saltmere, the lamps were lit and the herring fleet was in, and the town smelled of smoke and salt and the particular anxiety of a place that has just noticed a stranger walking its streets. At the door of the Gull and Anchor, Marta Okonkwo was waiting for her cargo, and behind Marta, at a corner table with his back to the wall, sat the surveyor, drinking slowly, watching the room the way a man watches a pond he intends to fish.

Sebastian handed over the oilskin rolls, took her pay, and did not look at the corner table. On her way out into the lane, she heard him order another cup, and heard him say, pleasantly, to no one in particular:

"I'll be in town a few days. Good herring season, I hear."

She walked home through the dark with her heart hammering and her gift lying awake under her skin like a banked fire, and began, methodically, to plan how a woman disappears from a town that knows her face — and how much she can carry over the passes before anyone thinks to stop her.
