## 2. The Hunt Proclamation

The proclamation went up on the market cross three days after the herring man ordered his second cup, and Sebastian heard about it before she saw it, which was how she knew it had been written with her kind of people in mind. News that travels fast is news meant to travel fast.

She was weighing out black pepper for Marta Ostell when the boy came pelting through the door, breathless, cheeks scarlet with cold and excitement.

"They've posted it! The bounty! On the *gifted* — there's a list —"

"Mind the scale," Sebastian said, and her voice came out flat and even, and only Marta's sharp glance told her she'd squeezed the brass weights hard enough to leave her palm striped red.

The list. She finished the sale with hands that did not shake, because she had spent nine years teaching them not to, and then she walked out into the grey morning with her basket on her arm like any woman going about any errand, and joined the crowd at the cross.

The paper was fresh, the ink still glossy, the crown's seal stamped fat and red at the bottom like a wax wound. She read it the way she read everything now — twice, once for the words and once for what the words were doing.

*By order of His Majesty Aldous the Third, let it be known that the practice of the old gifts is hereby declared treason against the realm. Any person found to bear such gifts, or to shelter, feed, employ, or conceal a bearer, shall be taken into crown custody. Rewards are offered for information leading to the identification and capture of gifted persons: fifty crowns for a minor gift, two hundred for a storm-gift, five hundred for any bearer of the old binding lines.*

Five hundred. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Five hundred crowns was a fishing boat, a house, a decade of a laborer's wages. Five hundred crowns was enough to turn a neighbor's love into a ledger.

And *storm-gift* had been named. Not lumped in with hedge-healers and fire-fingers and the women who could sour milk with a look. Named, and priced, and hunted.

The crowd murmured around her the way crowds do — a low, uneasy animal sound, fear and greed braided together so thoroughly you couldn't tell which thread was which. An old man near her spat. A woman with flour on her hands crossed herself with the wrong hand, the old way, and then looked around quickly to see who'd noticed.

Sebastian noticed. She noticed everything now, and hated it.

She walked home by the long way, past the tanner's, past the chapel, and stood in her kitchen and began, for the second time that week, to pack.

* * *

They burned the Hollis cottage on the fourth day.

Not the Hollises themselves — Tam Hollis and his wife had been taken in the night, quietly, a cart and six soldiers and no shouting, which was worse somehow, because shouting would have meant the village could pretend it was an outrage. The quiet was an invitation. The quiet said: *you saw nothing, and we know you saw nothing, and that is the arrangement.*

But the cottage they burned in daylight, at noon, in the market square's full view, with the whole town assembled on the lane to watch, because a lesson unwatched is a lesson wasted.

Sebastian stood at the back of the crowd with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she watched Tam Hollis's roof — the roof Tam had thatched himself two summers back, sweating and swearing and letting her drink his terrible cider while he complained about the price of straw — catch and curl and go up in a column of black smoke that stood against the pale sky like a finger pointing at all of them.

The soldiers had stacked the furniture in the middle of the room first. She could see the shapes of it through the windows as the fire took — the table, the chairs, the bed where Tam's wife had lain through three winters of coughing. They burned the *house*, not just the cottage. A house is a history. A cottage is only timber.

A sergeant in crown blue read the proclamation aloud again, his voice pitched to carry, and the crowd was silent in a way that had nothing to do with respect.

"Let it be understood," the sergeant said, and his eyes moved along the line of faces like a man counting coins, "that the crown does not punish the innocent. Only those who harbor treason under their roofs. Only those who *hide what they are*."

Sebastian's hands were fists inside her sleeves. Under her skin, the storm lay awake and listening, and for one dangerous, luminous moment she felt it rise toward the smoke like a dog hearing its name — felt the pressure in the air shift, felt the clouds overhead begin, very slightly, to *gather* —

She killed it. She strangled it the way she'd strangled it a thousand times, breathing through her teeth, folding the power down into the small cold place behind her breastbone where she kept it, and the clouds unraveled, and the smoke went on rising straight and undisturbed, and nobody saw, and nobody ever saw, that was the whole of her life, nobody ever *saw* —

An old woman beside her was crying without any sound at all. It was Tam Hollis's mother. Sebastian knew her. The woman had taught her to gut fish when she first came to the town, nine years ago, with a false name and a dead husband's story and nothing in her pack but a knife and a storm.

Sebastian did not touch her. Touching her would be a thread, and threads could be pulled, and five hundred crowns was five hundred crowns. She stood in the crowd and let the old woman weep beside her and did not put one arm around her shoulders, and she thought: *this is what exile costs. Not the mountains. This. This exact moment, repeated forever.*

That night she finished packing.

* * *

She went up through the high passes by the routes she knew best, which were the routes that did not exist.

The Thornfells above the treeline were a country of grey stone and white wind, and the smuggler's roads through them were not roads at all but a grammar of landmarks — a split boulder like a broken tooth, a cairn built wrong on purpose, a shelf of rock where a man could walk with a full pack and a loaded mule and leave no print that a tracker would swear to. She'd learned them from a dead man named Corvin who'd learned them from his mother, and she had added her own punctuation over nine years: the overhang where you could wait out a patrol, the scree run that would carry any pursuit into the river gorge, the cave behind the frozen waterfall where she'd cached oil, salt, dried meat, a second knife, and coin enough to buy a new life in a port town three kingdoms away.

She was two days up and feeling almost — not safe, never safe, but *possible* — when the weather turned.

That was the first wrong thing. The weather had been turning wrong all day, a pressure in her back teeth, a taste like copper on the wind, and she'd told herself it was the season, it was the mountains, it was anything but what it was. The second wrong thing was the cairn at the broken-tooth boulder. It had been moved. Restacked. Someone had used her landmarks to mark *their* passage, and they'd wanted her to know it.

She was turning, hand already at her knife, when the crossbows spoke from the rocks on three sides.

She didn't see the bolts. She heard them — the flat snap of cord, the hiss — and then the storm in her blood answered before her mind could, and the air *hardened*, and three crossbow bolts that should have punched through her chest stopped a hand's breadth from her skin and dropped into the snow like tired birds.

Silence. Wind. Her own breath, ragged, white, loud.

Then the rocks began to produce soldiers. They came up out of the stone the way the mountains themselves seemed to — grey cloaks, grey hoods, crown blue beneath, a dozen of them, two dozen, fanning out across the shelf with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and did not expect it to be difficult. And down the center of them, walking easily over ground that had made her own knees ache for nine years, came a young man in a plain officer's coat with no braid and a sword worn like it had been worn every day of his life.

He stopped six paces from her. Close enough to talk. Far enough to be respectful of the fact that she had just caught three crossbow bolts out of the air with nothing but her own bad blood.

"Sebastian Reyes," he said.

The name went through her like a blade through wet canvas. Not the false name. Not the nine years. The true one, the dead one, the one that belonged to a girl in a house that had been burned for exactly the reason Tam Hollis's roof was burning now.

"I don't know that name," she said.

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't." He looked at the fallen bolts, then at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't expected and didn't trust: not triumph, not fear. *Attention.* The attention of a man reading a difficult page. "I'm told the storm-gift runs in certain lines. I'm told those lines were supposed to have been ended." He tilted his head. "The crown pays five hundred crowns for one of you alive. It pays nothing for one of you dead, because a dead gift can't be studied. So before my men decide this is difficult, let me be plain."

"Be plain, then," Sebastian said. The storm was still awake under her skin, singing, and she could feel the whole grey sky leaning toward her like a crowd. "You have crossbows. I have the weather. Let's both be plain about that too."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "Captain Veyra would shoot you where you stand and call it a mercy," he said. "I'd rather talk. My name is Alaric."

She knew the name. Everyone in the borderlands knew the name, the way you know the name of a wolf that's been taking sheep in the valley — the king's second son, the one who hunted, the one they sent into the mountains when the crown wanted something found. She'd assumed the herring man was an informant. She had not assumed he was *this*.

"Your father's proclamation is on every cross from here to the sea," she said. "Two hundred for a storm-gift. You could retire rich off my head."

"I could," Alaric said. "Instead I'm going to offer you a bargain, and you're going to hate it, and then you're going to take it, because the alternative is a rope in Veyra's hands and she doesn't do bargains." He glanced up at the sky she was holding, the low grey weight of it, and said, without any fear at all, "There's a dragon in the Thornfells. The last one. My father wants it found, and the men he's sent before me came back as rumors. I need a guide who knows these roads — *your* roads — and it appears I need one who can talk to weather besides. You'll take my expedition to the lair. You'll get me there alive, and my men with me. When it's done, you walk. A pardon, signed and sealed, and five hundred crowns to go with it, and no one hunts the name Sebastian Reyes again."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I give you to Captain Veyra," he said, "and I won't insult us both by describing what that looks like."

The wind moved through the pass, and Sebastian stood in it and did her arithmetic the way she always did — twice, once for the words and once for what the words were doing. A pardon was a lie told with a seal on it. A guide who led an expedition to a dragon's lair was a guide who could lead it anywhere, off any cliff, into any gorge, and the Thornfells were full of places where a column of soldiers could simply *stop existing*. He was handing her a knife and calling it a leash.

And he had said *my father wants it found*, not *my father wants it dead*, and he had looked at the three dropped bolts with something that was not fear, and he had offered to talk where Veyra would have shot.

"Your expedition," she said slowly. "How many men?"

"Forty. Sixty with the supply train."

Sixty. Sixty men to find a lair that nine years of smuggler's roads had never once brushed the edge of — and yet the crown kept sending, kept losing, kept sending again, as if it were not a lair they were hunting but something the lair *held*. She thought of her grandmother's voice, low, in a kitchen that had been ash for twenty years: *They bred us to bind her, girl. Then they feared what they'd bred, and burned us, and left the binding to rot.*

"Where exactly," Sebastian said, "does your father think this dragon's fire is going?"

Alaric looked at her for a long moment. The wind pulled at both their cloaks. Behind him, Captain Veyra — and she knew it was Veyra, she knew it the way you know a blade by its edge — sat her horse with her crossbow across her knees and her eyes on Sebastian like a woman counting the seconds to a kill.

"That," the prince said, "is the second bargain. And you haven't earned that one yet."

Sebastian looked at the soldiers, at the crossbows, at the sky she could break open like a jug if she was willing to die doing it. Then she looked at the prince, who stood in the weather of her making as if he'd been invited.

"Lead on, then, Your Highness," she said. "I know a road."
