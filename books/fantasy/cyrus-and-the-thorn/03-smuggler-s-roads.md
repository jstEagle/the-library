## 3. Smuggler's Roads

The road I chose for them was not a road at all.

It began where the king's highway gave out into scree and old pine root, a track so narrow the column had to ride single file, horses' hooves striking sparks off stone worn smooth by centuries of smuggled salt and smuggled souls. I'd run this route since I was sixteen, first with my father's packs on my back, later with brandy and untaxed iron and once—though I never asked what was in the crate—a coffin that rattled wrong when it should have been silent. I knew every switchback the way you know the lines of your own palm.

I also knew exactly which of those switchbacks could kill a man who didn't respect them.

That was not an accident. If they meant to hang me at the end of this journey, then the least I owed myself was the chance to make the hanging unnecessary. Every fork I chose, I chose twice: once to keep us alive, and once to keep the exits open. The Goat's Ladder, where the path thinned to the width of a coffin lid above a drop that would scatter a rider like spilled grain—I could take a mule down it blind. They couldn't. There, if it came to it, I could simply walk off the edge of their world and let gravity settle our accounts.

So I led them, and I watched them, and I counted.

Forty-one soldiers. Captain Veyra, who rode like she was punishing the horse. Prince Alaric, who rode like he was listening to it. And me, bound at the wrists with soft cord that would part under my knife if I ever got three unwatched seconds—which I had not yet gotten, because Veyra posted a man at my back day and night, a broad-faced corporal named Doss who chewed something bitter and smelled of woodsmoke and watched me the way a farmer watches weather.

The first week, I tested them the way water tests stone.

I took them up the dry bed of the Serpent's Tooth creek, where the footing rolled underfoot like marbles and two packhorses went down screaming. I let them believe it was the only way. It wasn't; there was a better crossing half a mile south. But I wanted to see what broke first—their nerve, or their discipline. What broke was a lieutenant's composure, briefly, and nothing else. They hauled the horses up by main strength, cursed in the economical way of men who curse often, and followed me onward without one word of mutiny.

*They're better than I assumed,* I wrote in my head, in the ledger I kept behind my eyes. *Dangerous assumption corrected.*

At night they built fires small enough to cup in two hands, per Veyra's orders—no light to draw mountain clans or worse—and I sat apart, as a prisoner sits, close enough to feel the warmth and not be warmed by it. Alaric came to me the third evening. He came alone, without his guard, which told me either he trusted me or he trusted no one else to hear what he intended to say. Both possibilities interested me.

He sat on a stone across the fire, and for a long moment he said nothing at all, just turned his hands toward the heat like any other cold man on a cold mountain.

"You knew my grandfather," he said finally. It was not a question. It was a trap laid gently, baited with history.

"I knew *of* him," I said. "Every soul in Thornfell knows of him. He burned the Hollow."

"The Hollow," Alaric repeated. "The village in the western pass." His voice did something careful around the name. "The histories say dragon-fire took it. That's why we're here—that's why the crown is here. To end the last of the line that ended those villages."

"Then the histories are wrong," I said, "and you already know it, or you wouldn't be sitting here asking me instead of telling me."

He looked at me for a long time across the small fire, and whatever moved behind his eyes, he kept it leashed. But he didn't deny it. And that, more than anything he could have said, changed the shape of the days that followed.

* * *

*From the field journal of Prince Alaric, fourth night out of Highmere:*

*Veyra sleeps armed. I have begun to understand this is not caution but creed. She believes the gifted are a rot in the body of the realm, and a captain who believes herself a surgeon does not question where she cuts.*

*I sat with the prisoner tonight. Reyes. She speaks like someone rationing her words, spending them only where they'll earn interest. I asked about my grandfather. I have read every account of the Border Wars in the royal archive—three chronicles, two epics, a merchant's diary nobody thought worth burning. None of them agree on anything except that fire fell from the sky and the crown emerged victorious.*

*She looked at me across the fire and said the histories were wrong, and I found I wanted her to be right, and I do not know yet what that wanting makes me.*

*My father sent me to find a dragon. I am beginning to suspect I will find something else entirely.*

* * *

By the sixth day we climbed into country where the air itself seemed thinner of mercy—grey stone, grey sky, wind that came off the high snowfields with a sound like a whetted blade drawn slowly across the world. The trees gave out. The birds gave out. Only lichen clung to the rock, patient as old grief, and the column strung itself along the trail like beads on a fraying cord.

This was the country I loved, and I hated leading enemies through it. Every campsite I chose felt like a small betrayal of the ground itself. But the mountains don't take sides; that's the whole of their virtue. They kill whoever missteps, royal blood or smuggler's, and they do it impartially, and there is a strange comfort in that even now.

On the seventh morning we crossed the Shattered Stair, an old landslide field where house-sized boulders had come down the mountainside in some ancestral catastrophe and settled into a chaos of gaps and hollows. The horses hated it. The soldiers hated it. Veyra rode through it with her jaw set like a door barred against winter.

Halfway across, the prince's horse put a foot wrong.

I saw it happen the way you see a fall beginning—in the ears first, then the bunching of the shoulder—and I saw Alaric do the correct thing, which almost no one does: he gave the animal its head and leaned forward instead of back. The horse scrambled, found stone, held. But a slab beneath its hind hoof shifted, groaned, and began to move, slow as a closing door, taking the edge of the trail with it.

Alaric jumped clear. The horse did not follow. It went down the slide in a rattle of tack and terror, and stopped, impossibly, wedged in a cleft forty feet below, alive, screaming.

What happened next happened fast, and I have replayed it since until the memory is worn smooth as river glass.

Veyra said, "Leave it," in the flat tone of a woman conserving resources for the mission. Two days' hard climbing lost to a horse, in her arithmetic, was theft from the crown.

Alaric was already climbing down.

I stood at the top of the slide with my hands still bound and watched the prince of the realm pick his way down loose stone toward a dying animal, and I felt something shift in my chest that I resented immediately and could not undo. Because I knew that slope. I knew which stones were seated and which were liars. And I knew that he did not, and that the same slide that had caught his horse could catch him, and that Veyra would write a fine report about the tragedy of it.

So I went down after him. Bound hands or no.

I won't pretend it was strategy, though I dressed it up as strategy afterward in my own head—goodwill purchased cheaply, a debt created, debts are levers. Some of that was true. But the truth underneath was simpler: he was going to die on my mountain, and my mountain and I had an understanding about that sort of thing.

I reached him at the cleft. The horse had a foreleg jammed to the hock and was thrashing itself toward a broken neck. I showed Alaric where to stand, where not to, and together—we worked well together, which annoyed me more than I can cleanly say—we levered the leg free with a length of broken branch and a rope from his saddle roll. When the horse came up out of the cleft, shaking, bleeding at one knee but whole, Alaric stood with his hand flat against its neck and his breath smoking in the cold, and he looked at me.

"Thank you," he said. Then, quieter: "You didn't have to."

"No," I said. "I didn't."

And I walked back up the slide ahead of him, and neither of us said anything else, and somehow that silence said more than the thanks had.

* * *

*From the field journal of Prince Alaric, eighth night:*

*She saved my horse today. Or perhaps she saved me, and the horse was simply the instrument at hand. Veyra says a prisoner's kindness is a tactic. Of course it is. Everything is a tactic; that is what survival means out here. But knowing a thing is tactics does not make it untrue.*

*She moves through this country like it answers to her. Not the way the gifted stories say—no lightning from her fingertips, no storm called down. More like the way a good huntsman reads a forest. Except deeper. As if the stone remembers her family.*

*I asked her tonight, over the fire, why her people were exiled—if the histories are wrong, if the storm-callers did not burn the Hollow, what did the crown fear enough to scatter them?*

*She said: "You don't exile a sword because it was used badly. You exile it because you can't pick it up yourself."*

*Then she looked at the fire and would not say more, and I understood I had been given more than she intended, and less than she knows.*

*I begin to think my father's hunt is not a hunt at all. A hunt ends a threat. This has the shape of a harvest.*

* * *

We made the high col below the Wyrm's Teeth on the eleventh day, the last easy ground before the routes turned serious, and camped in the lee of an overhang older than any kingdom. From there, in the clear cold of late afternoon, you could see the caldera country far to the north—a dark seam in the white peaks, a stain of ancient heat, where the last dragon slept out her long dying.

The soldiers looked at it the way men look at a battlefield before the battle: measuring, quiet, some of them praying. Veyra looked at it like a woman looking at a locked door she intends to open whether it consents or not.

Alaric stood apart at the overlook, and I stood apart from him, and for a while we both just watched the distant dark.

"My tutor made me memorize the Border Wars when I was nine," he said, without preamble, the way he sometimes spoke when he'd been carrying a thought too long. "Every battle, every date. The Storming of the Hollow. The Fall of Callowmere. I recited them at my father's table and he was pleased with me." He turned a stone over under his boot. "Nobody ever told me what started it. I used to think that was because I was a child. Now I think it's because the answer doesn't flatter anyone."

"It doesn't flatter anyone," I agreed.

"And you know it."

"I know pieces of it. Pieces the crown burned the rest of." I kept my eyes on the caldera, because looking at him while saying such things felt like handing over a hostage. "Your archive has three versions of the war. My grandmother had one. They agree on almost nothing except that at the end of it, the storm-callers were scattered and the dragons were nearly gone, and the crown wrote the ending."

"What did your grandmother's version say?"

I was quiet long enough that the wind filled the pause. Somewhere below us, a soldier's laugh carried up thin and strange, laughter in a place like this, defiance or nerves or both.

"It said," I said finally, "that my bloodline was made for the dragon. Bred to her, bound to her, generation after generation—to hold her fire, to keep it from spilling. That we weren't her masters. We were her lock. And when the crown decided locks were dangerous things to leave lying about, it broke us and scattered us and called the breaking justice." I shrugged, as much as the cord at my wrists allowed. "That's the version they burned people for repeating."

Alaric absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain—slowly, deeply, showing nothing on the surface while everything beneath it changed weight.

"If that's true," he said, "then this expedition isn't a hunt. It's—" He stopped himself. I watched him stop himself, watch the sentence die between his teeth, because finishing it aloud in front of his own camp would be treason, and he knew it, and I watched him choose, consciously, to carry the unfinished sentence instead.

"It's late," he said instead. "Rest. Tomorrow the real climb begins."

He walked back down toward the fires. I stayed at the overlook a while longer, watching the dark seam in the northern peaks, feeling it watch me back.

Tomorrow the real climb begins. And beyond it, the caldera. And beyond the caldera—

Below me, the camp settled into ember-light and low voices. Above me, clouds were gathering along the Teeth, fast, fat, wrong for the season, stacking themselves up like a wall being built by impatient hands. I felt it in my teeth before I saw it, that old pressure behind the breastbone, the storm reaching for me across the miles the way it always had, patient and enormous and certain of its claim.

I pressed my palms flat against the stone and breathed the pressure down, down, down into the mountain, the way I'd done ten thousand times, the way I'd sworn on my father's grave I'd always do.

But the storm was bigger than it had been in years. I could feel that too. Something north of us was feeding it—something vast and failing, venting the last of itself into the sky.

The clouds over the Wyrm's Teeth flashed, once, silently, from within.

Behind me, Corporal Doss spat and muttered something about weather coming. Ahead of me, in the dark seam of the caldera country, something old opened one eye.

Three days, maybe four, and we would be there. I had planned my escape a dozen ways down to the width of a knife-cut cord.

None of those plans accounted for what was waiting at the end of the road—or for the fact that, somewhere between the Shattered Stair and this cold stone under my hands, I had stopped wanting to take it.
