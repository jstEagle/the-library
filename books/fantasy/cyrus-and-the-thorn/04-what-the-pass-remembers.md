## 4. What the Pass Remembers

The mountain spoke first.

Alaric had grown up in a palace where every sound was curated—halls built to carry music, courtyards designed to swallow footsteps—and so he had never learned, until this expedition, to listen to a place that did not care whether he heard it. The Thornfell passes had taught him in three weeks what his tutors could not in twenty years: that stone has a voice, and wind is its grammar, and snow is the silence between words that means everything.

He was listening now, on the second morning past the Shattered Stair, when the mountain stopped speaking altogether.

They had made camp in a bowl of grey scree beneath a face of ice that rose like a held breath. The column was slow waking—Veyra's soldiers moving with the mechanical discipline he had drilled into them himself, mules stamping, breath rising in white columns. Sebastian crouched at the edge of camp, alone as always, chipping ice from her bootlaces with a knife she kept sharper than any soldier's blade. She had not looked at him since yesterday. He had not decided whether that was mercy or strategy.

Then the wind died.

Not gentled—died, cut off mid-gust like a door slammed in a house. The silence that followed was total, absolute, wrong. Even the mules went still, ears swiveling toward the ice-face above them.

Alaric's blood turned to cold water. He knew this from the old campaign journals he'd read by candlelight as a boy, the ones about the northern wars his father preferred forgotten: *when the wind stops on a high pass, it is gathering itself somewhere else.*

"Veyra!" His voice cracked across the camp. "Get them moving. Off the bowl—now, everyone, up the eastern spur—"

She was already turning, already barking orders, and for one heartbeat he thought they would make it. The soldiers moved. The mules lurched. Sebastian stood, and looked up—not at the ice-face, but at something above it, something Alaric could not see—and her face did what no face should do.

It went calm.

Then the mountain roared.

It was not a sound. It was the absence of every other sound, a white wall detaching from the sky with a grace so terrible it seemed almost gentle, and Alaric had time for exactly one thought—*Sebastian*—before the world came down.

* * *

He woke to pressure and dark and the taste of blood.

Snow, packed to the density of mortar, filled his mouth and nose; he clawed upward through a body-length of it before his hands broke into air, and even then the air was only a blue-lit seam between two slabs of avalanche debris. Around him, faintly, voices. Doss, swearing with impressive creativity. Someone weeping. A mule screaming somewhere under the white, a sound he would hear for the rest of his life.

"Alaric." Veyra's voice, close, iron-steady. Her gauntleted hand broke through beside him and hauled until he stood on a shelf of compressed snow, chest heaving. "Count. We're missing six. And the pass is closed behind us and ahead."

He counted anyway, because she needed him to, because a prince who panics kills more men than an avalanche does. Fourteen of twenty accounted for. Six buried. The bowl they'd slept in was now a tomb of blue ice, and the route forward—the only route Sebastian had charted through the Teeth—was a wall of snow forty feet high and settling deeper by the hour.

"The spade crews," Veyra said. "We dig for the six, then we dig for the road."

"We'll dig for three days and lose four men to cold doing it," Alaric said. "You know it. I know it."

Her jaw tightened. She knew it. That was the thing about Veyra—he had chosen her precisely because she saw battles the way he did, cleanly, without flinching, and he had never imagined that the same clarity would one day be aimed at something he loved.

Because that was the thought arriving now, unwelcome as a knife between ribs: he did not want to say the next part, because the next part was treason.

"There's another way," he said.

"Through that?" She gestured at the wall with something close to contempt. "No. Through nothing. We go back or we dig."

And then, from the far side of the shelf, where the blue light fell coldest: "Both of you are wrong."

Sebastian picked her way toward them over the debris, and Alaric saw immediately that something in her had changed. Not her face—that was still the guarded, weathered mask she wore like armor—but her hands. They were open. Loose at her sides. In three weeks he had never once seen her hands at rest; they were always holding something, ready for something, a smuggler's hands, a survivor's hands.

Now they were empty, and still, and it frightened him more than the avalanche had.

"The six you're missing," she said to Veyra, "are alive. Air pockets under the crust, along the old streambed—the mountain folded it, didn't crush it. You've got maybe an hour before the cold takes them." Her eyes flicked to Alaric, and there it was again, that calm, that terrible settled quality, like a woman standing at the edge of a decision she had already made. "And the road forward isn't buried. It's *under*. There's a chimney through the rock below the snowfall. I know it because my people cut it."

"Your people," Veyra said flatly, "are smugglers."

"My people," Sebastian said, "have crossed these mountains for nine generations, Captain, which is eight more than your king's maps can claim. But none of that matters right now." She turned fully to Alaric, and her voice dropped, and though she addressed him, the words were meant for some judgment higher than either of them. "I can get your men out. All twenty. But you have to let me do it my way, and you have to keep your captain back while I do it. Both of those things. Or I walk away and you bury your dead at your leisure."

"That sounds like a threat," Veyra said.

"It's a price," Sebastian said. "There's a difference. Princes learn it late, if ever."

Alaric looked at her—at this woman he had taken in chains, whose name he still wasn't sure was real, who had led them through country that killed a man on their third day out and hadn't lost one yet—and understood, with the strange clarity of extreme cold, that she was offering him something enormous and asking him to pretend it was small.

"What's your way?" he asked quietly.

"You won't believe me if I tell you."

"Try."

She held his gaze. And in the blue half-light of the trapped pass, with six men dying under the snow and his captain's hand drifting toward her sword, Sebastian Reyes—or whatever her true name was—said:

"I'm going to call the storm down off that ridge before it buries us all. And then I'm going to move the snow that's already fallen, because snow remembers being wind, and I remember how to ask it."

The silence after was longer than the silence before the avalanche.

"She's mad," Veyra said at last, but her voice had changed too; she had spent enough years around gifted prisoners to recognize the particular stillness of someone telling the truth. "Highness, she's confessing. Storm-calling is first-tier. That's the pyre, no trial, no—" 

"I heard her," Alaric said.

And here it was, then. The moment his whole life had been walking toward without his knowledge. Every lesson his father had ever given him about duty assembled itself behind his eyes like an honor guard: *the hunt is just, the gifted are dangerous, the law is the law.* And against all of it, one memory, small and human: three nights ago, Sebastian sitting apart from the fire, teaching Corporal Doss's young soldier how to read the stars because the boy had confessed he'd never learned, her voice patient, her chains long since traded for trust she had never been owed.

*The hunt may be built on lies,* whispered a voice in him that sounded horribly like his own. *You have suspected it since the Ash Village. Look at her. Look at her and tell me this is what dangerous looks like.*

"Captain," he said. "Take the men back to the tree line. All of them. Now."

"Highness—"

"*Now*, Veyra. That's not a request. It's an order from your prince, and if you doubt it, remember that I answer to the crown for every man in this column, and you answer to me." He made his voice into the thing his father had forged it to be, cold and final. "If she tries anything, you may kill her. If she saves our men, you will say nothing of what you see. Those are my terms, and they are not negotiable."

Something moved behind Veyra's eyes—calculation, suspicion, obedience grinding against each other like millstones. He watched her choose, for now, the millstone of obedience.

"As you command, Highness." She turned and strode away, shouting the column into motion, and Alaric felt the cost of the order settle onto his shoulders like a second mantle. He had just made himself complicit. Whatever happened in the next hour, he was no longer merely leading the hunt.

He was hiding from it.

* * *

Sebastian walked out onto the open shelf alone, and Alaric—disobeying his own order, because apparently that was the kind of prince he was becoming—stayed at the tree line where he could see everything.

What he saw, he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe.

She raised her arms, and the world *leaned*. There was no flash, no thunderclap, none of the theater the ballads gave storm-witches. There was only a change in pressure, a feeling like the moment before tears, and then the clouds that had hung over the ridge since dawn began to move—backward, sideways, unravelling like wool pulled thread by thread from a loom. The wind returned all at once, but gentle, combed, directed, flowing around the camp instead of through it.

And the snow.

The great wall blocking the pass shuddered, sighed, and *moved*—not melting, not collapsing, but flowing, sliding aside in vast slow sheets as if the mountain itself were opening a curtain. Beneath it, revealed in stages, lay the six missing soldiers, curled in their pockets of air, blinking up at the impossible sky. Alive. All six. Doss was laughing and crying at once, hauling them out, and no one, not one man, looked afraid.

That was the detail that broke something open in Alaric's chest. Twenty hardened soldiers of the gift-hunt, watching forbidden magic performed ten feet away—and not one of them reaching for a weapon. One of them, the boy Sebastian had taught the stars to, actually *applauded* before Veyra's glare cut him silent.

The power rolled off her in waves he could almost feel from the tree line, and she stood in the middle of it like a woman standing in rain she had been denied for years, head tipped back, eyes closed, drinking.

Then it stopped. All at once, the wind settled, the clouds reformed into ordinary grey, and Sebastian staggered, caught herself on a boulder, and vomited into the snow. When she straightened, wiping her mouth, she looked older. Drawn thin, like a candle burned at both ends.

She caught him watching. Held his gaze across the distance. And shrugged—one shoulder, wry, exhausted, utterly unrepentant—as if to say: *well. Now you know.*

* * *

They rode out through the opened pass within the hour, and Veyra said nothing, which frightened Alaric more than accusation would have. Silence, in Veyra, was a weapon being sharpened.

He found Sebastian that evening at the fire's edge, wrapped in a blanket, hands once again restless, holding a cup of tea she wasn't drinking.

"You lied to me," he said quietly, sitting beside her. "Three weeks. You let me put chains on you."

"They weren't real chains."

"You know what I mean."

She was quiet a moment. "I lied to everyone my whole life, Highness. It's how my family stayed alive after your crown exiled us. Lying isn't the sin you think it is. It's just the coat you wear when the weather's bad." She finally sipped the tea. "Besides. You suspected. Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. You've known since the Stair, when I talked that squall off the ridge-line. You just didn't want to know what knowing would cost you."

The truth of it landed like a blow. He *had* known. Had watched her, had catalogued the signs, had told himself each one had an innocent explanation, because the alternative—the alternative meant his father's hunt was hunting the innocent, meant the pyres and proclamations and burned cottages were all built on a foundation of fear dressed up as justice.

"Six men are alive tonight because of what you did," he said.

"And your father would burn me for it tomorrow, in front of a crowd, and call it mercy." She looked at him then, really looked, and her voice softened into something almost gentle. "So the question isn't what I am, Prince. You've answered that. The question is what *you* are. And you've got maybe four days of road left to decide."

She rose, folded the blanket over her arm, and walked toward the tents—and paused, just briefly, her shoulder nearly brushing his.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking back, "you ordered your captain away before you knew I'd succeed. That wasn't calculation. I've watched calculators my whole life. That was something else."

Then she was gone, and Alaric sat alone by the dying fire, staring east toward the caldera country, where the clouds flashed silently from within, once, twice, like something ancient counting the days.

Four days. One dragon. One choice.

And somewhere behind him, in the dark, the soft deliberate sound of Veyra writing in her report-book by candlelight.
