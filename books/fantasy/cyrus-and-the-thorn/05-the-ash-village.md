## 5. The Ash Village

They smelled it before they saw it.

The wind shifted in the night, and by morning the column marched through air that tasted of old ruin — not smoke, for nothing had burned here in three generations, but something older and stranger, a mineral bitterness like lightning-struck sand. Sebastian rode at the front beside Alaric with her hood up and her hands hidden in her sleeves, and she knew what lay ahead before the scout's horn sounded.

She had dreamed of this place as a child.

"Emberhollow," Alaric said, reading the name from a leather-bound survey as they crested the ridge. "Burned by dragon-fire during the last war. Every history agrees on that much." He glanced sideways at her. "You've gone pale."

"The mountain's thin here," she said, which was true, though not the truth. "The air does that."

Below them, cradled in a fold of the high valley, lay the village — or what the crown's maps still politely called one. Roofs gone. Walls standing to shoulder height in places, blackened to a color that was not the brown of ordinary fire but a deep, glassy blue-black, as if the stone itself had been scorched through rather than merely touched. The main road ran straight through its heart, and there was no way around; the pass narrowed on either side into cliffs no mule could climb. Whoever built the king's road had built it through a graveyard, and expected no one to mind.

Sebastian minded.

* * *

The column picked its way down into the valley in silence. Even Veyra, who filled silence the way other men filled wine cups, said little. Soldiers crossed themselves or touched iron, according to their homelands' customs. The horses hated it — ears flat, nostrils flaring, hooves striking sparks off stones that should have been cold.

Sebastian dismounted where the road entered the village square and stood very still.

There was a pattern here. She had known there would be, but knowing did nothing to blunt the shock of seeing it laid out beneath her boots, vast and deliberate as a signature. The scorch-marks were not random. They radiated outward from a single point near the old well in sweeping, interlocking arcs — spirals within spirals, each one finer than the last, the way frost feathers across a windowpane. Ordinary fire devoured. This had been *written*. The ground itself had been used as a page, and whatever hand had held the pen had written in a language of heat and pressure that only a handful of bloodlines in all the world could read.

She could read it.

*Convergent discharge,* her grandmother would have called it, tapping the hearthstones with her cane while the fire painted shadows on the rafters. *Three points of origin, drawn inward. That is not a dragon's work, child. A dragon burns what stands before it. This burned everything, everywhere, at once — and left the well untouched.* Her grandmother had always left the well untouched, in every story, and young Sebastian had never thought to ask why until she was old enough to understand: because whoever cast the working had needed water to survive the aftermath. Because the caster had been standing right there.

Because the caster had been family.

"Reyes."

Veyra's voice, behind her, flat as a blade laid on a table. Sebastian turned. The captain sat her horse at the edge of the square, helm under one arm, studying the scorch-patterns with the narrow attention of a woman who had spent her life hunting gifted things and learning how they left marks.

"Strange fire," Veyra said. "Don't you think?"

"All fire's strange when it's old," Sebastian said. "It stops being fire and starts being geology."

Something flickered in the captain's eyes — not belief, but the patience of a hunter who could wait. She wheeled her horse away to supervise the camp, and Sebastian let out a breath she hadn't realized she'd been holding.

Alaric appeared at her elbow. He'd been doing that lately — arriving quietly, without escort, as if his own soldiers made him nervous. He looked out over the ruined square, and his face held none of the piety she'd expected. No grief performed for the dead. Something more troubled than that.

"My tutors brought me here," he said. "I was fourteen. My father wanted me to see what dragons do." He crouched, ran a gloved finger along one of the glassy arcs in the stone. "Even then I remember thinking the burn was wrong. Dragon-fire leaves a crater, they told us. I found no crater. I asked my tutor about the spiral patterns and he told me I was imagining things, and he reported the question to my father, and I was given a new tutor." He looked up at her, and there it was again — that terrible, inconvenient honesty he kept producing at the worst possible moments. "You see it too. Don't you."

Sebastian weighed her answer against the rope that waited for her at the end of this road, and found, to her own disgust, that she was tired of lying to him.

"It isn't dragon-fire," she said quietly. "Look at the convergence. Three origins, drawn inward, centered on the well. A dragon attacks along a line. This was cast — shaped, aimed, held. This was storm-work, Your Highness. Someone with the gift stood in this square and unmade a village, and your histories wrote a dragon's name on it because a dragon was easier to hate than the truth."

"And what is the truth?"

"That your grandfather's army couldn't take Thornfell from the gift-born clans that held it. So someone found another way." She heard her own voice go strange and cold, heard her grandmother's cadence rising through it like groundwater. "The histories say the village was loyalist. Say the dragon burned it in reprisal, and the crown rode to war in righteous grief. But loyalists don't get erased. Traitors do. Someone burned Emberhollow to give the crown its war, and then burned the people who could say so."

Alaric stared at her. Around them, soldiers were pitching tents among the ruins, driving stakes into soil that had once been gardens, and Sebastian felt each hammer-blow like a knock on a door that should stay shut.

"How do you know all this?" he asked. Not accusing. Worse — genuinely asking, trusting her to answer, and she felt the trap of it close gently around her heart.

"Smugglers' roads carry more than cargo," she said. "They carry stories. Old ones." It wasn't even a lie. It just wasn't hers to tell.

* * *

That night she could not sleep, and slipped out past the sentries' firelit circle — they let her, now; somewhere in the passes the prisoner had become something else, a guide whose judgment they trusted with their lives, and the change frightened her more than chains would have — and walked alone into the heart of the ruin.

Moonlight turned the scorch-arcs silver. She followed them to the well.

It still stood. Of course it did. Her grandmother had described it so many times that Sebastian knew its chipped rim, the lightning-fork crack running down its eastern side, before she ever laid eyes on it. She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the stone, and the memory rose in her like floodwater breaking a dam.

*We were the binders,* her grandmother had said, the night before she died, gripping Sebastian's wrist hard enough to bruise. *Before the crowns, before the hunts. Our line was bred for it, girl — bred across seven generations to hold what the dragon holds. Storm and fire, bound together, so neither could devour the world. We were the lock. And locks are only loved until someone decides the door should open.*

*What happened?*

*The king we bound it for decided a weapon was worth more than a lock. When our grandmothers refused to unmake the binding, he called it treason. Called us dragon-lovers. Burned the villages that sheltered us and wrote dragons into the ashes.* The old woman's eyes had been white as winter stars. *Emberhollow was ours, child. The last place our whole line gathered. And the man who unmade it wore a crown, and his grandson wears it now, and his grandson's son is hunting the last dragon with a rope around the neck of the only binder left alive.*

Sebastian knelt by the well in the moonlight and wept for people whose names had been scraped out of every book in the kingdom, whose bones lay under this road, whose crime had been refusing to make a weapon of themselves.

Her inheritance sat in her chest like a coal wrapped in wet cloth. It had always been there. She had spent thirty years pretending otherwise, smothering it, starving it, calling it exile instead of amputation. But kneeling here, in the place her blood had been erased, she understood with terrible clarity that the suppression had never been survival. It had been surrender, renewed daily, to men long dead.

And the dragon waited ahead of them, guttering, dying, hoarding a storm that would shatter the world when its keeper's heart stopped — unless a binder stood between.

Unless *she* did.

Behind her, gravel shifted. She wiped her face fast and turned, hand half-raised before she remembered she was supposed to be giftless, and found Alaric standing ten paces off in the dark, having clearly seen everything — the well, the tears, the way she'd turned with power already gathering in her fingers like a reflex she'd never truly buried.

He didn't call for Veyra. He didn't call for anyone.

"You knew this place," he said softly. "Not from smugglers' stories."

The wind moved through the ruined square, lifting ash off stones that had held their secret for sixty years, and Sebastian understood that the next words she spoke would decide everything — the expedition, the dragon, the war her grandmother had died trying to prevent.

"No," she said. "Not from stories."
