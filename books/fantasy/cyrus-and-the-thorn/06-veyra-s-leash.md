## 6. Veyra's Leash

The rope was the first thing Alaric noticed wrong.

They had made camp in a hollow below the Greymaw saddle, where the wind combed down off the icefields and died against the stone, and Sebastian Reyes sat apart from the soldiers as she always did—apart, but never far, her back to a boulder, her wrists bound loosely before her. Loose. That was the word that snagged him. He had tied those knots himself on the second night out of Thornfell's shadow, and he had tied them tight, because she was a storm-caller and a smuggler and a prisoner whose life hung on his signature alone. The knots now were loose enough to slip. Loose enough that she could have freed herself a dozen times over and simply chosen not to.

He was still staring at them when Veyra dropped the coil of charred cord at his feet.

"Captain," he said carefully.

"Found it in her pack." Veyra's voice carried across the hollow without effort; she had never learned to speak quietly, or perhaps had simply decided long ago that quiet was for people with something to hide. "Wrapped in oilcloth, under the dried fish. A storm-charm. Braided hair, silver wire, salt-iron core. You know what this is, Highness?"

Alaric looked down at the thing. He knew exactly what it was. His grandmother had worn one until the day she died—a thin braid of grey hair wound with wire, tucked beneath her collarbone, and when he was small enough to sit at her feet she had told him it was a relic of the old wars, a keepsake from a friend whose name she would not say. He had thought it sentiment. He had been taught to think it sentiment.

"A charm," he said. "Half the mountain folk wear them."

"Mountain folk wear *dead* charms." Veyra crouched, picked up the coil, and held it so the firelight caught the wire. "This one's live. Feel the air around it. It hums. Salt-iron only holds a charge if there's a caller feeding it—and there's only one caller in this camp."

Around the nearest fires, soldiers had gone still. They pretended not to listen, the way soldiers always did, turning their cups and mending their straps with exaggerated care, but Alaric felt their attention gather like weather. This was how mutinies began—not with drawn steel but with listening.

"The woman saved your life on the pass," he said. "She pulled four men out of the snowslide. She's kept us alive on roads no crown map has ever charted."

"She did." Veyra rose, and her shadow fell long across him in the firelight. "And every day she doesn't, I wonder what she's waiting for. Your father gave you a hunt, Highness. Not a nursemaid. The edict is clear: the gifted are to be taken alive if convenient, executed if they resist. She resisted at Thornfell. She's resisted every mile since, in ways too clever to hang her for. And now we find a live charm in her kit." She let the silence stretch. "I'm asking for the order."

"You're asking me to execute my guide in the middle of the Greymaw passes," Alaric said, "with no one else who knows the way, three weeks from the caldera, in country where the last two expeditions vanished without a bone left behind."

"I'm asking you to do your duty."

The words landed like a slap, and he let them, because they were true, and because the truth of them was the whole shape of his trap. Duty said hang her. Duty said bind her hands properly, post a double watch, march her to the dragon's lair and then to a scaffold in the capital with her name read out as a warning to every gifted child in the realm. Duty was his father's voice, patient and cold: *A prince who cannot give the order is a prince who cannot be given a crown.*

"Duty," Alaric repeated, "also says I bring back the guide who can find the lair. Dead guides find nothing. If I hang her here, Captain, you can explain to the king why his hunt starved in the snow."

Veyra's eyes narrowed. She was not a stupid woman—that was the worst of it. She had served his father since before Alaric could walk, and she loved the crown with the simple, unbreakable devotion of a hound that has known only one hand. She would not be bought with logic. She could only be delayed.

"One night," she said at last. "She sleeps bound to the stake tonight. Double watch. And tomorrow, Highness, I want your answer in writing. If you won't give the order, I'll send a rider back with my report, and let the king decide which of us failed his edict."

"A rider can't cross the Greymaw alone and you know it. But write your report. I'll countersign whatever you put in it."

That surprised her—he saw it flicker across her face. She had expected him to fight the paper. Fighting the paper would have told her everything. Signing it bought him time, and time was the only currency he had left.

* * *

He came to the stake after midnight, when the watch had changed and the new sentries had settled into the drowsy rhythm of the coldest hours. Sebastian was awake. Of course she was awake. She sat with her wrists lashed behind her to the ironwood post, her head tipped back against it, watching the stars through the ragged tear in the clouds that her mountains allowed.

"They searched my pack," she said, without looking at him.

"Veyra did."

"And found the charm." Now she turned her head, and the firelight moved across her face, catching the bruise the avalanche had left along her jaw. "My mother braided that. Before the hunters took her. I've carried it since I was nine years old. It's the only thing I own that knows my real name."

Alaric crouched in front of her, close enough that the sentries would see a prince questioning his prisoner and not a man kneeling in the snow. "Why keep a live charm? You must have known what it would cost you if it was found."

"Because dead charms are for people mourning the gift." Her voice was low, stripped of all its smuggler's swagger. "Mine isn't dead. Burying it wouldn't make it true. I told myself the same lie for eleven years, Prince Alaric, and it nearly got a child killed under a squall I could have turned with a breath. I'm done lying about what I am. Even to myself." A pause, and something sharper entered her eyes. "Even to you."

"What do you mean by that?"

"I mean you already knew." She leaned forward as far as the rope allowed, and her whisper barely stirred the air between them. "On the pass. When the snow came down. Nobody else saw what I did—nobody else *could* see—but you'd stopped riding before the first crack of ice. You were looking right at me. You've known since the avalanche, and you've spent six days hiding it from your own captain, lying to her face about reports and riders, signing papers you have no intention of honoring." Her mouth curved, and there was no kindness in it. "So tell me, Highness. What is the crown prince of Veloria doing committing treason for a storm-caller?"

The word *treason* went through him like cold water. He had not named it, even in the privacy of his own skull. He had called it prudence, strategy, the careful arithmetic of a commander preserving his guide. But she said it plainly, kneeling in the snow with her hands bound, and he found he could not refute it.

"If Veyra sends her report—"

"She can't. No rider crosses the Greymaw alone, and you know it, and she knows you know it. So instead she'll wait for the caldera, where there are other officers, other ears. Every day you stall is a day closer to the moment you can't stall anymore." Sebastian held his gaze. "You're not protecting your hunt anymore, Alaric. You're protecting me. And you don't even know why."

"Don't presume to—"

"Why?" she pressed, soft and relentless. "Say it. Say it out loud, once, where the sentries can't hear, and I'll stop asking."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the icefields with a sound like distant surf. Alaric knelt in the snow before a bound prisoner and understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever he answered would be the first stone of a road he could not walk back down. His father's voice said *duty*. His grandmother's grey braid said something older. And Sebastian Reyes watched him with storm-grey eyes that had already survived the loss of everything, and asked him only for honesty—the one coin he had never been permitted to spend.

"Because I watched you hold up a mountain," he said quietly, "and the histories call that kind of power the reason for the wars. And all I could think, standing in the snow, was that the histories are wrong. That everything I was raised to hunt might be built on a lie. And that if it is—" His breath fogged between them. "If it is, then the man giving the orders is the monster, and I'd rather learn that now than after I've hanged everyone who could have told me."

Sebastian stared at him. Whatever she had braced herself to hear, it was not that.

"That's not treason," she said finally, and her voice had gone strange. "That's worse. Treason you can survive. Believing the hunt is a lie means everything you are is built on sand—and you'll burn your whole life down to stand on rock."

"Yes," Alaric said. "Probably."

Above the camp, on the ridge line, a figure detached itself from the rocks—there and gone, a silhouette against the stars—but Alaric did not see it, and neither did the sentries. Only Sebastian did, over his shoulder, and her blood went cold as she recognized the straight-backed stride, the sword at the hip, the officer's cloak pulled close.

Veyra had not gone to her tent. Veyra had heard everything.

And in the morning, when the column broke camp and started up toward the high pass, the captain rode at Alaric's side with her face composed into perfect obedience, and said, pleasantly, "Fine weather for the caldera, Highness. We should make good time now"—and Sebastian, marching in the ropes behind them, understood that the leash had changed hands, and that from this day forward, every step toward the dragon's lair was a step toward a blade neither of them could see coming.
