## 7. The Guttering Hearth

The last mile of the pass was a throat of black glass.

Sebastian felt it before she saw it—the way the air changed, the way the storm-gift under her skin went quiet as a held breath. For nineteen years she had lived with that power like a second heartbeat, always there, always needing to be pressed down and silenced. Now, walking into the caldera's approach, she realized the silence wasn't hers at all. Something vast was listening back.

"Stop." She raised a hand, and the column behind her stilled—thirty soldiers, two mules, one prince, all of them watching her now with something that had shifted since the avalanche from suspicion to awe. Alaric reined his horse up beside her. Frost clung to his cloak; three days above the snowline had carved hollows beneath his eyes, but his gaze was steady.

"What is it?"

"You feel nothing?" she asked. "No heat? No pressure in your ears?"

Alaric frowned, tilted his head. "Cold. That's all."

"That's because you're not *meant* to feel it." She nudged her mare forward. "The lair doesn't want to be found by anyone but—"

She didn't finish. Couldn't. The word *anyone but me* sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

The road narrowed along a ridge of obsidian, ancient lava cooled into blades and waves, and below them the mountain opened its mouth: a caldera two miles across, ringed in cliffs the color of old blood, floored with ash so fine it moved like water when the wind touched it. Sebastian had heard smugglers' tales of the Thornfell wyrm since she was a girl—tales told in whispers, half prayer, half warning. *Old Mother Cinder. The Last Flame. She who burned Vaskarran off the map.* Every story agreed on her size, her wrath, her endless fire.

None of the stories said anything about her dying.

They came down into the caldera at dusk, and the light itself was wrong. It should have been red in there, ember-lit, hellish. Instead the ash plain glowed faintly gold in patches, dimming even as they crossed it, like coals raked over and left to starve. Heat shimmered off the ground in weak, stuttering waves. Sebastian dismounted where the footing grew treacherous and walked, leading her horse, and behind her the whole expedition did the same, boots crunching on cinders that crumbled to powder with every step.

"There," Veyra said.

The captain had taken point since the avalanche, since the day Sebastian had torn open the sky to save them and Veyra had watched with an expression Sebastian couldn't read—hunger or hatred or both wearing the same face. Now she pointed her sword toward the far wall of the caldera, where a cave-mouth gaped like a wound.

And before it, folded among the rocks like a mountain that had lain down to sleep, was the dragon.

Even guttering, even diminished, she was too large for Sebastian's eyes to hold whole. Her scales were the grey of storm-clouds shot through with fading veins of copper and gold, and each vein pulsed with light—but the pulses were slow. Slower than a sleeping man's heart. Slower, Sebastian thought with a lurch, than a dying one. Her wings lay slack against her flanks, membranes translucent as smoke. One vast eye was open, amber gone dull as honey left out too long, and it watched them come without moving.

"Hold here," Alaric ordered, and for once no one argued. Even Veyra's soldiers hung back, superstitious dread winning over discipline.

Sebastian kept walking.

She didn't decide to. Her feet simply carried her forward across the ash, past Alaric's sharp intake of breath, past Veyra's barked *Reyes!*, past the mule that screamed and bolted. The dragon's eye tracked her, and with every step Sebastian felt the pressure in her skull build—not pain, exactly. Recognition. Like standing outside a house you'd forgotten you once lived in, hearing someone move inside.

*Little storm-child.*

The voice arrived inside her bones rather than her ears, vast and slow as continental drift, frayed at the edges with exhaustion. Sebastian stopped ten paces from the great head, close enough to see that the dragon's nostrils were crusted with grey scale, that her breath came in shallow, labored pulls that stirred the ash in feeble gusts.

"I know what you are," Sebastian whispered aloud, because speaking felt safer than answering in her mind. "You're why the king sent us. Not to kill you."

*Kill me.* A sound rolled through the caldera that might once have been laughter—the dry scrape of a landslide. *Child, if I wished to be killed, I would have died centuries ago. Your king does not send hunters to kill a dying hearth. He sends them to carry home the embers.*

The words landed like ice water. Sebastian thought of the proclamation nailed to every post in Saltmere: *the crown requires the service of any soul bearing the old gifts, for the safety of the realm*. She'd assumed it meant pyres and prisons. But Veyra's zealotry had always had an odd flavor to it—not fear of the gifted, but *assessment* of them, cataloguing, measuring. And the expedition's supply train carried more chains and iron cages than rope.

"They mean to harvest you," she said. "Your fire. Whatever's still hoarded inside you."

*I am the last vault,* the dragon said simply. *When my siblings fell—in the south, in the sea, under your mountains—I took their hoards into myself so that men could not dig them out and burn each other with them. Storm-magic enough to unmake a continent. I have held it for nine hundred years, and I am tired, little storm-child. So tired.*

The eye closed, then dragged open again, effort visible in the shudder that ran down the enormous neck. And in that amber depths Sebastian saw reflections that had no business being there: lightning over a green country, towers of ice, rivers running backward, a sky torn open like fabric.

"If you die," Sebastian breathed, "it all comes out."

*All at once. All unbound. Yes.* The dragon's breath rattled. *Or—*

"Or what?"

The great head lifted, an inch, two—a movement that cost the wyrm visibly, scales grinding against stone—and turned until that ancient gaze held only Sebastian, filled only Sebastian, and the world outside it went thin as paper.

*Or you take it.*

The caldera vanished. Sebastian stood nowhere, everywhere, in a dark full of falling light, and the voice spoke around her, within her:

*Your line was made for this. Do not look surprised—you have always known, somewhere under the hiding and the false names. The kings of old bred your bloodline as binders, vessels, keepers. When we gave men fire, we demanded a price: that some of them would carry what could not be safely held. Your grandmothers bound the first hoards. Then the crown looked at its servants and saw weapons it did not control, and it exiled you, hunted you, burned your histories and called it holy war. But the binding gift did not die. It slept. It waited. It walked down nine hundred years of mothers to arrive, at last, in a smuggler with a false name in a border town, suppressing a gift she was born to use.*

Sebastian's knees hit the ash. She barely felt them land. Her grandmother's voice surfaced from twenty years down—stories told by firelight in a dialect the crown had outlawed, songs about *keepers* and *the debt of flame*, which Sebastian had dismissed as exile-folklore, comforting inventions of a scattered people.

Every story she'd ever been told about this place was a lie. Every history book. The scorch-marks in the Ash Village that hadn't matched any account of dragon-fire. The way her own power had always felt less like weather and more like a *key looking for its lock*.

"Why me," she managed. "Why now."

*Because I chose to endure long enough to find you, and no longer. I can hold the hoard perhaps another season. Perhaps a month. My heart is failing, child, and when it stops, everything I contain goes out unbound—and your world ends in fire, and your prince's precious wars will seem a kindness.* The eye dimmed, brightened. *Bind it, and it lives in your line again. Quietly. As it was always meant to. You would bear the weight of nine hundred years of hoarded storm until your bloodline carries it forward—or until some future age learns wisdom. You would never be free of it. You would never be only yourself again. And the crown would hunt you until your last day, for you would become the single greatest weapon this world has ever known.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then you walk away and live whatever years the fire allows you, and the world burns after.* A pause, long as a winter. *I will not pretend this is a choice between goods, little storm-child. It is a choice between burdens. I only ask that you choose knowing what you are.*

Sebastian knelt in the ash, and the pressure of everything—the dying god before her, the continent-killing storm coiled in its chest, the lie of every history she'd been taught, the memory of Alaric's face when he'd watched her call down the avalanche, awed and afraid and *believing*—pressed down on her like the whole sky.

She had spent nineteen years learning to be small. To be Mara the mule-trader, Mara the quiet one, Mara who kept her hands in her pockets when storms came. She had buried her grandmother's songs, her mother's warnings, her own name. She had done it to survive.

And survival had been the whole of her ambition, and it had cost everything she was.

*There is no shame in refusing,* the dragon said gently. *I did not choose this burden either. I only carried it.*

That undid her more than anything else. Sebastian bowed her head, and tears cut tracks through the ash on her cheeks, and when she finally spoke, her voice came out rough and low and entirely her own.

"What's my name?" she asked. "My real one. The one my line carried."

The dragon exhaled—a long, slow tide of warm air smelling of cinders and rain—and something in that breath was almost tender.

*Villalobos,* she said. *Wolf-blooded. Keeper-line of the eastern holds. Daughter of Sera, granddaughter of Ilse, last of the binding gift.* The amber eye brightened, just slightly, like a lamp turned up against the dark. *Now. Choose.*

Behind her, distant but closing fast, Sebastian heard boots on ash and Veyra's voice cutting cold across the caldera: *"Captain's orders—no one speaks to the beast! Reyes, step away from it!"*

And another voice, nearer, furious, Alaric's: *"Stand down, Captain—that's a direct—"*

Sebastian rose to her feet.

She turned her back on the shouting, on the drawn swords, on nineteen years of hiding, and laid her bare palm flat against the dragon's scaled jaw, and felt beneath the cooling armor of her a heartbeat like a storm-front on the horizon—fading, fading, waiting for an answer.

"Yes," Sebastian Villalobos said. "I'll carry it."
