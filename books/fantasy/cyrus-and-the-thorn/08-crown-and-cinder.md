## 8. Crown and Cinder

Alaric had stood vigil at deathbeds before—his mother's, his old tutor's—but he had never watched a god die.

The dragon filled the caldera like a fallen mountain given breath. Scales the color of storm-slate lay dull along her vast flanks, and where fire should have pooled in the hollow of her throat there was only a faint ember-glow, guttering like a lamp out of oil. Her breathing came slow and vast, each exhale stirring the ash on the caldera floor into ghostly spirals. She did not raise her head when the expedition picked its way down the switchback trail. She did not need to. Alaric felt her attention settle over him like a hand, ancient and heavy, and knew that she had been aware of them since they crested the rim.

Sebastian had gone ahead alone.

She walked down the ash slope with her hood back and her hands open at her sides, small against the enormity of the dying creature, and the dragon lowered her head—a movement like a cliff deciding to kneel—until one enormous eye hovered before her, gold-shot with fading light. They regarded each other in silence. Alaric could not hear what passed between them. He only saw Sebastian's shoulders stiffen, then tremble, then go very still, the stillness of a woman hearing a sentence she had dreaded all her life finally spoken aloud.

*Blood of the binding,* she'd said once, in the dark of a smuggler's campfire, half-asleep and unaware she was speaking at all. *Bred for the leash, then leashed ourselves.*

He understood now. Gods. He understood everything now—the erased villages, the burned genealogies, his father's fevered urgency to reach the lair before winter sealed the passes. It had never been about ending the old wars. It had never been about mercy or closure or any of the noble words stitched into the hunt's proclamation.

It was about harvest.

The realization arrived fully formed, cold as the wind off the rim, and Alaric's stomach turned over. He thought of the king's letter, carried in Veyra's locked dispatch case, which he had never been permitted to read. *Secure the hoard intact. The gift-takers await at Greywatch.* He had assumed it meant the dragon's scales, her bones, her blood—trophy and medicine both. He had been a fool. You did not send the crown's gift-hunters to harvest bone.

You sent them to harvest power.

Behind him, boots crunched on ash. Veyra descended from the ridge with twelve soldiers in file, crossbows slung, and something in her gait made the hair rise along Alaric's neck. He had known Veyra for nine years. He had knighted her himself, three winters past, with a sword that had belonged to his grandfather. He knew the set of her jaw when she smelled victory, and he knew—had always known, if he were honest—that beneath her devotion ran a vein of zealotry as pure and pitiless as glacier water.

But he was not prepared for what she said next.

"Your Highness." Her voice carried flat across the caldera, pitched to carry. "Give the order."

Alaric turned. "The order?"

Veyra's eyes were fixed on Sebastian, standing dwarfed before the dragon's eye. "On the witch. The beast is dying regardless—it cannot defend itself. But the witch is the key. The dispatches name it plainly: the storm-line is the vessel. Kill the dragon, take the woman alive if possible, dead if necessary. The gift-takers can draw what remains from her body either way." She said it without relish and without remorse, the way she reported weather. "The king's orders are explicit. I have waited until we reached the objective so that nothing could compromise the operation. Give the order, and this ends today."

The words dropped into the silence of the caldera like stones into a well.

Twelve crossbows. Twelve soldiers who had followed him through avalanche country and ash, whose loyalty he had believed—had *chosen* to believe—ran to him first. Several of them would not meet his eye. Two shifted their weight toward Veyra, subtle as water finding its level.

And Sebastian stood below, her head turning slowly toward the sound of voices, her face draining white in the ember-light as she heard every word.

Something in Alaric went quiet. Not calm—he had known calm, and this was not it. This was the silence at the center of a collapsing tower, the instant between the crack and the fall, when everything that had held a life together revealed itself to be already broken.

His whole life, he had served the crown believing it served something larger. His father's wars had sickened him; his father's hunt had shamed him; but he had told himself the rot stopped somewhere short of the throne itself, that if he climbed high enough and stayed true enough, he could be the lever that bent the kingdom back toward honor. He had let Sebastian be bound and threatened rather than freed. He had concealed her gift instead of declaring it, because concealment was survivable and declaration was treason. Every step, he had told himself, was merely the next survivable step.

There were no more steps. There was only this ledge, and the choice.

"Captain," he said, and was surprised how steady his voice came out. "Read me the dispatches. Verbatim. Now."

"You are not cleared for—"

"I am the prince of this realm and commander of this expedition. Read them, or I will take them from your case myself, and you can explain to the king why his son pried the lock."

A muscle worked in Veyra's jaw. For a moment he thought she might refuse outright—and then, perhaps judging that obedience would wound him less than refusal, she unslung the case, broke the wax, and read.

Alaric listened to his father's voice in another man's mouth: *the hoarded storm-magic of the last drake, drawn through the bound line and distilled at Greywatch; sufficient yield to arm three legions with gift-fire; the rebellion in the east provinces to be answered with exemplary force.* Exemplary force. His father had used those words before, after the burning of Harrow Fen, and Alaric had spent a week afterward unable to look at mirrors.

Three legions armed with a stolen god. And the vessel—the conduit, the *key*—was the woman he loved, to be emptied like a cask and discarded.

"No," Alaric said.

Veyra looked up from the parchment. Around her, the soldiers had gone rigid, hands hovering near weapons, men caught between two oaths and knowing only that whichever they chose, they would be damned by someone.

"Your Highness," Veyra said quietly, "the orders are the king's."

"The orders are murder." His sword cleared the scabbard before he had consciously decided to draw it, the steel singing in the thin mountain air, and the sound rolled down the caldera and came back changed. "I renounce this hunt. I renounce these orders. Captain Veyra, you will stand down yourself and your company, and we will all walk out of these mountains alive, and I will answer for it before the court with my own mouth. That is the last order I will give you as your prince."

Veyra's face did not change. That was the worst of it—the terrible patience of a woman who had already done this arithmetic and found no column in which Alaric survived.

"I am sorry, Highness," she said. "I swore to the crown, not to you. Crossbows."

* * *

The world broke apart in bolts and shouting.

Alaric moved without thinking—three steps, shoulder down, into the nearest soldier, carrying him off the firing line as the first volley hissed past overhead. A quarrel tore through his cloak sleeve and kissed the flesh beneath; he barely felt it. He came up swinging the flat of his blade into a wrist, an elbow, anything to break the formation, buying seconds, only seconds—

Below, Sebastian screamed his name, and the sky answered.

Lightning split the cloudless air above the caldera rim, a white spear thrown from nothing, striking the rock face beside Veyra's position and shearing away a curtain of stone. The thunderclap slammed through Alaric's chest like a physical blow. Ash leapt in a blinding wall. Through it he glimpsed Sebastian on the slope, arms flung wide, hair lifting in a wind that had no source, storm-light crawling under her skin like veins of lightning trapped in glass. She was not hiding anymore. Whatever bargain she had struck with the dying dragon, whatever she had learned kneeling before that ancient eye, she was spending it now, all of it, for him.

*Do not die here,* her voice cracked across the chaos—not shouted, but arriving inside his skull nonetheless, intimate as a whisper against his ear. *Not now. Not for me.*

"Then fight beside me!" he roared aloud, laughing despite everything, wild and terrified and more alive than he had ever been. "Because I'm done choosing!"

The caldera became a battlefield of shifting smoke and sudden light. Veyra fought the way she did everything—economically, precisely, without hatred and without hesitation. She cut down the two soldiers who tried to stand with Alaric, and Alaric carried their faces with him into every exchange of steel afterward: Corporal Dennet, who had sung bawdy songs on the night march; young Ferro, seventeen, who had asked him once whether dragons dreamed. Both dead on the ash because Alaric had led them here believing in a lie.

*Then make it mean something.*

Sebastian came down the slope through the smoke like a figure out of the oldest stories, storm crowning her, and met Veyra's charge head-on. Steel rang against a shield of hard air. Veyra pressed her, relentless, reading the storm-witch's movements the way she read everyone's, hunting the pattern, hunting the opening—and Alaric saw her find it, saw her feint low and drive high toward Sebastian's throat, and he threw himself into the space between.

The blade took him under the collarbone instead. Fire and ice together, a bright obscene bloom, and he heard Sebastian scream from somewhere very far away, heard it echo strangely off the caldera walls, heard the dragon—for the first time in centuries, he was certain—lift her dying head and *roar*, and even guttering, even hollowed, that roar was the sound of mountains arguing with the sky.

Veyra froze, sword red, staring at what she had done. At the prince she had knelt to. At the line she had crossed that could never be uncrossed.

"Highness—" she began.

"Don't." Blood filled Alaric's mouth; he spat it and grinned at her, because the pain was clarifying and rage was cleaner than grief. "You don't get my forgiveness. Get my justice."

Above them, Sebastian raised both hands, and the storm that had been building behind her eyes since the border town where she'd saved a child from a squall—since her whole hidden life—finally broke.

* * *

Afterward, Alaric lay on the warm ash with Sebastian's hands pressed hard against his wound and her tears falling onto his face, and he watched the dragon's ember-eye watching them both.

Veyra was dead. The soldiers who remained had thrown down their arms—one of them, Sergeant Hale, weeping openly, swearing himself to Sebastian with the desperate fervor of a man who has seen too much to ever unsee it. The dispatches lay scattered across the caldera floor like black feathers, the king's own handwriting exposed to the wind and the witnesses.

"You fool," Sebastian whispered, pressing her forehead to his. "You absolute fool. I was supposed to protect you from this."

"You were," Alaric managed, and tasted blood, and laughed anyway. "Terrible job. I've decided to do it myself from now on."

Her laugh broke in the middle. Above her shoulder, the great eye dimmed, and dimmed again—and yet Alaric felt no dread, only a strange gathering pressure in the air, as though the whole hoarded weight of a shattered continent's magic had paused, poised, waiting on a word.

Sebastian lifted her head. Looked at the dragon. Looked at him.

"It wants an answer," she said softly. "Now. Tonight."

"Then answer," Alaric breathed, gripping her hand with the last strength he had. "Whatever it costs—I'll pay it with you."

The ember-eye kindled. The storm within Sebastian rose to meet it.
