## 9. Stormbound

The world had narrowed to pain and iron.

Sebastian lay where Veyra's blade had thrown her, half across the dragon's threshold, blood pooling beneath her ribs in a warmth that felt almost gentle against the cold of the caldera stone. Above her, the battle still roared—steel on steel, the crack of Alaric's shield, the bellowing of men who no longer knew what they fought for. But all of it came to her muffled, as if heard through deep water. Through storm-water.

*You are dying,* said the voice that was not a sound but a pressure behind her eyes, older than any language she knew. *The crown would call it a mercy. I do not.*

"Then you're the first," she whispered, or meant to. Her lips barely moved.

The great wyrm lay coiled at the heart of the caldera, and even dying she was vast—a mountain that had learned to breathe, scales gone dull as old pewter, the furnace-glow beneath them banked to embers. Her head rested an arm's length away, one eye like a golden eclipse turned upon Sebastian with something that was not pity. Dragons did not pity. It was older than pity: recognition.

*Your grandmother bound me once,* the dragon said. *Her mother before her. Your line was woven for this, girl of the storm-line. Woven to hold what I hold, so it would not burn the world again.*

"I don't want it." The words tore loose from somewhere deeper than her throat. "I never wanted any of it. That's why I hid. That's why I ran."

*Yes.* Something moved in the golden eye—not quite amusement, not quite sorrow. *And yet when the avalanche came, you did not hide. When the child fell into the squall, you did not hide. You have spent ten years practicing smallness, and every day of it, you were lying.*

The fighting crested nearer. Sebastian heard Veyra's voice cutting through the din like a whipcrack—*the prince is compromised, cut him down, cut them both down*—and then Alaric's answer, wordless, the ring of his sword taken up against his own captain's men. She tried to rise. Her body refused. Blood kept leaving her, patient as sand through an hourglass.

*I can close this wound,* the dragon said. *I can give you my fire before it gutters out—and with it, everything I have hoarded since your ancestors sealed it in me. A century of storms. The weight of the shattered continent, held in one small chest.* A pause, vast and deliberate. *Or you can die here clean, unbound, and let it scatter when I go. The mountain will not survive the scattering. Neither will the valley. Neither will he.*

Sebastian found Alaric with her eyes. He stood three paces from Veyra, blades locked, his face stripped of everything princely—just a man bleeding for a truth he'd only learned today. He had turned his sword on his own blood-oath for her. For a lie his father had built a kingdom's fear upon.

"Don't dress it up as my choice," she rasped. "If I die, everyone dies. That's not a choice. That's arithmetic."

*Everything is arithmetic until it is done,* the dragon said. *Then it is identity. Listen well, Sebastian Villalobos, last of the binding line. This fire will not make you a queen or a weapon. It will make you a vessel, and vessels are judged by what they carry and how. Some who took less than this became tyrants. Some became hearth-fires. The magic does not choose. It only pours.*

"And if I pour wrong?"

*Then we learn why the old ones feared us.* The great eye closed, opened. *Do you accept?*

Somewhere beyond the ringing in her ears, Veyra screamed—wounded now, Sebastian thought distantly, or furious, there was little difference in that woman—and Alaric shouted her name. *Sebastian.* Not "the prisoner." Not "the guide." Just her name, torn raw out of him.

She thought of her grandmother's hands, knotted like tree roots, teaching her to quiet a thunderhead with her palms. She thought of ten years of flinching from her own skin. She thought of a border-town child laughing in rain she should have died in.

"No more hiding," Sebastian said.

She put her hand flat against the dragon's brow.

The fire came in like a tide meeting a river.

There was no other way to describe it, and later she would not try. The hoarded century poured through the point of contact and she felt herself open to receive it—every storm the dragon had swallowed, every lightning-bolt banked and stored, the whole terrible surplus of a broken age flowing into channels her blood had been carved to hold. It hurt the way birth hurts. It remade her the way rivers remake coastlines. She felt her wound knit closed around a heat that was not fever; she felt the storm-gift she'd spent a decade strangling unfurl at last to its full wingspan; and above all, beneath all, she felt the weight settle—not crushing, not corrupting, just *present*, like a second heartbeat, like a passenger who had finally come home.

When she could see again, she was standing.

She did not remember rising. The caldera was silent except for the crackle of small fires and the groan of wounded men. The dragon's body had gone still—not dead, Sebastian realized with a jolt, but emptied, sleeping at last after a century of holding, her scales catching the light like cooled ash. And around Sebastian, in a wide circle, the surviving soldiers had fallen back, weapons shaking in their grips, faces gray with terror and awe.

Veyra lay on her back ten feet away, Alaric's sword-point at her throat, her own blade skittered out of reach. Her eyes found Sebastian and went wide—not with fear of death, Sebastian understood, but with fear of being right. Everything Veyra had hunted, everything she'd burned cottages for, everything she'd called abomination was standing in front of her wearing lightning under its skin.

"The hunt," Alaric said, and his voice carried across the caldera with the flat clarity of a man past all hesitation. He straightened. He did not lower his blade. "Is ended."

"You don't have that authority," Veyra spat. Blood threaded from her hairline. "The king—"

"My father sent us to slay a dying dragon and harvest her corpse for a weapon." Alaric's jaw was tight, but his voice did not waver. "I have seen the ash villages. I have read what the histories buried. There is no enemy left to destroy, Captain. There is only what we choose to become." He looked at the soldiers, one by one. "I renounce the hunt. I renounce the bounty on the gifted. Any man who follows those orders follows them without me—and answers to me if I live long enough to object."

Silence. Then, from the back of the column, a soldier named Brandt—the one whose frostbitten feet she'd warmed in the pass, Sebastian remembered—dropped his sword deliberately into the scree. It rang once, bright as a bell.

One by one, others followed. Not all. But enough.

Sebastian stepped forward, and the ground hummed faintly under her boots, and no one ran. She stopped beside Alaric and looked down at Veyra.

"Kill me, then," Veyra said. "Prove me right."

The old answer rose in Sebastian—the cornered fury, the calculation, the cold arithmetic of survival. She let it pass through her like weather. The new fire did not push it aside; it simply gave her room enough to hold it without drowning.

"No," she said. "You'll go back and tell the king what happened here. All of it. That's heavier than a grave."

* * *

They made camp at the caldera's rim because no one could face the descent in the dark.

Sebastian sat apart on a shelf of black stone, watching her own hands. Lightning lived in them now, coiled and patient, answering her pulse the way her storm-gift once answered only fear. She reached inward and touched the hoard—carefully, the way you touch a sleeping animal—and felt it stir and settle, vast but willing to be still. The dragon's ember pulsed at its center, dim but alive. *Not a prisoner*, she told it silently, testing the words. *Not a weapon.* It gave nothing back but warmth, which was somehow answer enough.

Alaric climbed up and sat beside her without asking, the way he'd learned to on the smuggler's roads. His arm was bound; her blood was on his sleeve. For a while they said nothing, because they had earned their silence honestly.

"It's still in you," he said at last. Not a question.

"All of it." She flexed her fingers. Sparks crawled between them like curious insects, and she let them die. "A hundred years of storm. Enough to crack the continent twice over." She laughed, low and unsteady. "I keep waiting to feel like the monster in the histories. I feel like myself. Just—more floor under me than I used to have."

"That's how I'd describe you anyway," Alaric said, and when she looked at him he wasn't smiling, exactly, but nearly. "Even before. Like someone standing in a house bigger than anyone guessed."

Below them, the camp murmured—men binding wounds, sharing water, casting glances at her that were no longer simple fear. Brandt was arguing quietly with another soldier about whether the histories could be trusted at all. Somewhere a horse complained about the altitude. Ordinary sounds, in a place where nothing would ever be ordinary again.

"He'll send armies," Sebastian said. "Your father."

"He will." Alaric watched the stars coming out over Thornfell, sharp and indifferent. "And some of these men will stand with us, and the mountain clans owe you their passes, and the dragon's fire sleeps in you instead of in his armories. He's lost the weapon he wanted. What's left to him is only rage." He paused. "Rage makes poor strategy. I learned that watching him my whole life."

Sebastian leaned her shoulder against his, careful of the wound, careless of everything else. Far below, deep in the caldera, she felt the old wyrm breathing in her sleep—slow, empty, finally at rest—and felt her own heartbeat echo it, two rhythms learning to share one chest.

"They'll call us traitors," she said.

"They'll call us worse by spring." He turned his head, and his voice dropped to something meant only for her. "Let them. I've been the crown's son for twenty-eight years. I'd like to find out what else I am."

The wind came up off the glacier fields and moved through her hair, and for the first time in ten years Sebastian did not flinch from it. She let it speak to her. It spoke of the passes, and the clans in the high valleys lighting signal fires, and a road winding north toward a border town where a child was alive who should not be—and south, toward a capital where messengers were already saddling horses.

The storm in her chest listened to all of it, and held, and waited to be asked.
