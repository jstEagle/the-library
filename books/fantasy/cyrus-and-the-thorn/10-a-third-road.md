## 10. A Third Road

The first snows came early that year, and with them the first riders.

Sebastian felt them before she saw them—six heartbeats of iron and purpose climbing the switchbacks below Highvale, their horses' breath pluming white against the grey morning. The ember in her chest stirred at the tremor of hooves on stone, a low warmth spreading beneath her ribs like sunlight through amber. She had learned, over these months, to read the dragon's fire the way she had once read storms: not as a weapon waiting for her hand, but as a language. And it was telling her that men were coming up her mountain with murder in their saddlebags.

She stood on the watchtower's broken parapet, wind pulling at her hair, and let herself feel it all—the fear, yes, but also something steadier underneath. Six months ago she would have run. Six months ago running was the only thing she knew how to do.

"Riders?" Alaric's voice came from the stairwell, and then Alaric himself, cloak half-fastened, sword already at his hip out of habit rather than expectation. He had stopped wearing it at his side every day; today he'd remembered.

"Six," she said. "Crown livery under plain cloaks. They're being careful about it."

"Scouts, then. Not soldiers." He joined her at the parapet, close enough that his shoulder brushed hers, and she leaned into the contact without thinking about it anymore. That was new too—how little she thought about it now. How the touching had become as ordinary as breathing, after everything it had cost them both.

"Scouts mean the king has stopped pretending," Sebastian said. "He knows where we are."

"He's known since midsummer." Alaric's jaw tightened. "He was deciding what we were worth."

"And?"

"And I don't think he's decided yet. Which means we still have time." He said it lightly, but she heard the weight beneath—time was a currency his father spent freely and never replenished. "Maren's clan will want to know. And the garrison at Coldbrook—half those men swore to me before they swore to him."

"Then we tell them tonight." She turned from the parapet. Below, the refuge spread through the valley like something grown rather than built: the longhouse with its turf roof, the smithy where a storm-touched boy named Elio hammered out hinges because shaping metal calmed the lightning in his hands, the terraced gardens where a woman who could whisper seeds awake coaxed green from thin mountain soil. Forty-one souls now. She counted them some mornings the way other people counted coins—not because she feared losing them, but because she could hardly believe she'd found them.

Forty-one people who had spent their lives folding themselves smaller. Who had hidden gifts the way she'd once hidden hers, swallowed down behind false names and flat eyes and the exhausting performance of being nothing at all. Now they grew things. Built things. Sang things. The valley hummed with small magics, unhidden, and the sky above Thornfell had not cracked open. The world had not ended. It had, if anything, grown quieter—as though the mountains themselves had been waiting generations for someone to stop apologizing for existing here.

* * *

Alaric wrote letters that afternoon, and Sebastian read them over his shoulder, which was its own quiet revolution. A year ago she would sooner have handed a knife to a stranger than her secrets to a prince. Now she sat beside him while ink dried, correcting his geography of the passes and arguing about which clan elders should be addressed by title and which by name.

"To Maren," he dictated, "you'll want to lead with the scouts. She respects directness. To Captain Ossa at Coldbrook—" he paused, pen hovering, "—I don't know. Loyalty is a strange thing to ask for when you're asking a man to consider treason."

"You're not asking for loyalty," Sebastian said. "You're asking them to look at what they'd be loyal *to*. There's a difference."

"Is there? From where they stand?"

"From where they stand, you're the prince who drew his sword on his own captain to protect a storm-caller. You're either a traitor or the only honest man in the kingdom, depending on what they believe about the hunt." She took the pen from him, an old habit now, and added a line to Ossa's letter: *You marched with us to the caldera. You saw what Veyra carried in the king's name. Decide with your eyes, not your oath.*

"That's a dangerous sentence," Alaric said mildly.

"It's a true one. Those seem to be the same thing lately."

He laughed—really laughed, head back, the sound bouncing off the rafters—and she caught herself memorizing it again, the way she had in the first weeks, when laughter between them had been contraband rarer than anything she'd ever smuggled over the passes. Some part of her still braced for it to be taken away. Some part probably always would. But the bracing had grown quieter too, worn smooth by repetition, the way river stones wear smooth: not gone, just gentled.

The letters went out with two of Maren's runners at dusk. Then they climbed to the ridge above the valley, because Alaric had made a habit of it and because Sebastian had discovered she liked standing beside him under open sky, where there was nowhere to hide and no need to.

The ember pulsed warm in her chest as the stars came out. Sometimes, on nights like this, she could feel the whole shape of what slept inside her—not the hoarded storm, not anymore, that had settled into her line like sediment into stone—but the *ember*, the living seed of the last dragon, breathing its slow fire in time with her heartbeat. She had been afraid of it once. She was not afraid of it now. It was not a weapon. It was not a prisoner. It was simply hers, the way her gift had always been hers, only larger, older, and far more patient.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "The palace. Being certain of what you were."

"No." He said it without hesitation, and then, because he was Alaric and honesty with her had become a discipline he practiced like swordwork: "I miss my mother's garden. I miss knowing which fork to use. I don't miss certainty. Certainty is what let me march forty men toward killing something ancient because a proclamation told me it was evil." His hand found hers on the cold rock between them. "You taught me that doubt isn't weakness. I'm still learning it."

"You taught me that strength doesn't have to be solitary," she said. "We're even."

Below them, lights kindled one by one in the longhouse windows. Someone was singing—Elio, probably, whose voice cracked in the middle register and who sang anyway, loudly, joyfully, in a way none of them had dared sing when hiding meant silence. The song drifted up the ridge, wordless and wandering, and the ember in Sebastian's chest answered it with a faint golden warmth, as though the last dragon, sleeping in her blood, had opinions about music.

* * *

The answer came faster than any of them expected.

It was Maren herself who brought the news, riding hard through a wet snowfall three days later, her grey mare lathered and her face grim beneath the hood. She strode into the longhouse trailing cold air and shook the snow from her cloak like a wolf shedding rain.

"Ossa's garrison has declared for you," she said, without preamble. "Coldbrook stands with the prince. But the king has raised a force in the south—two thousand, maybe more, marching under General Corvane." She spat the name as though it tasted of ash. "And he's not calling it a punitive expedition. He's calling it a *cleansing*."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Around the great hearth, the refugees of a dozen purges went quiet in the particular way of people who had heard that word before and survived it anyway. Old Sefa, who had watched her village burn when she was nine, closed her eyes and began, very softly, to braid and unbraid the fringe of her shawl.

"When?" Alaric asked.

"Spring, at the latest. Winter roads will slow them, but Corvane is not a patient man, and the king wants this finished before the gifted of Thornfell become a symbol instead of a rumor." Maren's eyes swept the room, counting faces, measuring. "You have until the thaw. Maybe less."

Sebastian felt the ember stir, and made herself breathe past it. Fear fed fire; she had learned that at the caldera, with Veyra's blade at her throat. She would not feed it now.

"Then we have winter," she said, and her voice came out steady, and she was proud of that. "Winter is ours. These are our passes, our snows, our roads—and half the kingdom thinks we're dead already. Let them come in spring and find out how wrong they were."

"It won't be enough to hide," Alaric said quietly. He was looking into the fire, and she recognized the expression—he was doing arithmetic with lives, the terrible princely mathematics he hated and was good at. "Two thousand soldiers, Sebastian. If Corvane reaches this valley, no amount of mountain craft saves forty-one people. We need the clans united. We need Coldbrook's garrison. We need—" he hesitated, "—we need to be more than a refuge. We need to be a reason."

"A reason for what?"

"For the rest of them. Every gifted soul in the lowlands hiding under a false name, every soldier who saw what Veyra carried and couldn't sleep after. They're watching us. Right now, tonight, they're wondering if Thornfell is a grave or a door." He looked up, and the firelight caught the scar along his jaw—the one he'd earned at the caldera, standing between her and his own captain. "We get to choose what they see."

Silence held the longhouse. Snow ticked against the shutters. And then old Sefa opened her eyes and said, in her cracked voice that had outlived two purges: "A door. I've hidden in enough graves."

One by one, the others nodded. Elio stopped hammering. The woman who whispered to seeds pressed her soil-dark hands flat on the table, as if taking root.

Sebastian looked around the hearth—at the frightened, stubborn, extraordinary people the crown had taught to disappear, gathered now in the open under one roof—and felt the ember turn over in her chest like a sleeper settling deeper, warm and unhurried and utterly without fear. The last dragon had waited centuries in the dark of a caldera for someone worth binding to. Whatever came up those passes in spring would find what patience built.

"Then we build a door," she said. "Wide enough for everyone."

Outside, the snow fell thick and silent on the high passes, erasing the road south, and somewhere beyond it an army gathered in the dark like a held breath. Spring was coming. Let it come.

They would be ready—or they would be free trying. Either way, no one in Thornfell would ever fold themselves small again.
