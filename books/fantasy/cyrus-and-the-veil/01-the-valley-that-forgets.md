## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats woke him before the sun did, as they always did, and this was the first mercy of the valley: that it gave him small, stupid things to answer to.

Gideon Dunmore rose from his narrow bed in the grey dark, pulled on the wool shirt that smelled of woodsmoke and lanolin and nothing else, and went out into the cold with the lantern swinging from one hand. The mist lay along the river like a sleeping animal. Somewhere up-slope, a nightjar was still calling its two-note question into the silence, getting no answer, asking again. He had learned to love sounds that expected nothing of him.

"Coming," he told the goats, because he had taken to talking to them in his third year here, and they had never once judged him for it. "I'm coming. You'd think a man hadn't fed you in a week."

The byre door stuck in damp weather, as it always stuck, and he shouldered it open with the same hip, at the same angle, in the same way he had done three thousand mornings running. This was the discipline of the valley. Not penance exactly — he had tried penance in the first years and found it fed the thing inside him like coals fed a fire — but rhythm. Repetition. The same motions worn smooth as river stones until there was no groove left in them deep enough for lightning to hide.

He measured out the barley. He checked Bramble's hoof, which had gone soft again, and wrapped it. He let the herd out onto the east pasture where the grass came up silvered and dripping, and stood a while with his hands on the gate rail watching them scatter into the fog like ideas dispersing from a tired mind.

Ten years. Ten years since he had come down out of the pass with frostbitten ears and an empty pack and a story he'd rehearsed the whole way — a shepherd looking for work, no family, no history, nothing worth asking after. The valley had believed him. That was its nature. The valley forgot. It forgot names the way other places forgot weather; it forgot faces within a season; and it forgot, most mercifully and most completely, anything that had ever burned or broken or screamed. There were folk down in Hollowbrook who could not have told you why they flinched when thunder rolled over the ridge, only that they did, and that afterward they went back to their bread and their quarrels and their ordinary lives.

That forgetting was the whole reason the place existed. He understood that now, though no one had ever said it aloud. Magic could not follow a man past the ridgeline. Whatever he had been before — whatever had lived in his blood like a second heartbeat — could not cross. It waited outside the valley the way a wolf waits at the edge of firelight: patient, certain, and unable to take one step closer.

Most mornings, that was enough.

He filled the kettle. He ate the same breakfast he always ate — oats, honey from Marta's hives, black tea brewed strong enough to strip paint, because taste was one of the few appetites the storm had never touched and he meant to keep it sharp. Then he took up his staff and his sling and walked the fence line, which was the day's first real labor and the day's first real prayer.

* * *

The fence needed mending near the alder stand, where the river had eaten at the bank through the spring melt. Gideon knelt in the wet grass and drove posts with a wooden mallet, and the work was good, the way simple destruction-and-repair is always good: the post goes in crooked, you pull it out, you set it true. Cause and consequence laid out clean as scripture. Nothing ambiguous. Nothing that would grow teeth later.

It was while he was driving the fourth post that his hands betrayed him.

Not badly. Not dramatically. A tremor, just — a fine shiver running through his fingers as the mallet came down, so that the post rang off-center and the sound came out wrong, a bright discordant note instead of the dull thud of honest wood. He stopped. He looked at his hands lying open against the mallet's shaft, and watched them shake.

Small tremors. Like an old man's. Like a man holding something very heavy very still, and losing.

"It's the cold," he said to the alders, in the flat voice he used for lies he'd stopped bothering to make convincing even to himself.

It was not the cold. It was never the cold. The valley suppressed what he carried; it did not erase it. Ten years of suppression and the storm still sat in him like water behind a dam, and dams, he knew — he had been raised in hill country where every stream was argued with, diverted, contained — dams do not weaken what they hold. They only postpone it. And some part of him, some traitorous animal part below thought and below grief, felt that pressure building year over year, grain by grain, and was not afraid of it.

That was the worst of it. Not the shaking. The wanting. On nights when thunder walked the ridgeline beyond the Veil — real thunder, wild thunder, thunder that belonged to no one — some cellar-deep chamber of him turned toward it like a man in prison turning his face to a barred window, drinking light.

He set down the mallet. He pressed his palms flat against the earth, against the wet cold loam, the way he'd taught himself in the early years, grounding, discharging, borrowing the valley's own deep indifference. The soil of this place had never once been asked to remember anything. It held no charge. He let it hold his, for a moment, the way you'd press a hot iron into snow.

His hands stilled.

He sat back on his heels and looked at the river, and for a long time he let himself do the thing he did maybe once a season, the indulgence he hated himself for: he remembered.

Not everything. Never everything. But pieces.

He remembered rain that obeyed him — not metaphorically, not the way farmers pray at clouds, but actually obeyed, bending around him in a wall while he walked dry through the heart of the storm. He remembered the taste of ozone on his tongue and how it had tasted, God help him, *good*. He remembered standing on the tower at Caer Duloc with wind tearing at his coat and feeling, for one incandescent hour, like the answer to a question the sky had been asking for a thousand years.

And underneath all of it, as it was underneath everything now, laid like a foundation stone: his brother's face.

He did not go further than the face. That was the treaty he had made with himself somewhere in the fifth winter, alone with a fever in this same cottage: he could look at the face, but he could not walk past it into the rest of the room. The room stayed shut. The room would stay shut until he died, and probably after.

The face smiled at him across ten years and a mountain range, and Gideon got up, and picked up the mallet, and drove the post home straight and true, and did not think about anything at all for a very long time.

* * *

Midday he walked down to Hollowbrook for salt and lamp oil, because the walk itself was part of the discipline. An hour along the river path with the water talking to itself in its shallow voice. The village sat in the crook of the valley like a handful of grey pebbles someone had dropped and not bothered to pick up: twelve houses, a mill, a shrine so old its god's name had been forgotten along with everything else, and Marta's shop, which sold everything else besides.

"Gideon." Marta looked up from her ledgers, and her face did the thing it always did — the small pause, the flicker of almost-recognition, the shrug. "You need salt? You bought salt three weeks ago."

"I use a lot of salt."

"No one uses a lot of salt." She rang it up anyway. She was sixty and built like a root vegetable and she had forgotten his name perhaps forty times and relearned it each time from the ledger, and he loved her for it with the uncomplicated love of a man who has been forgiven without knowing the crime. "You hear about the traveler? Went through last week asking about the high passes."

"No," Gideon said. His hands were very still around the paper sack of salt. "What about him?"

"Asking odd questions. Which roads are closed. Whether anyone comes down from the ridge." Marta sniffed. "Told him nobody does. Nobody does."

"Right," Gideon said. "Nobody does."

He carried the salt home and did not run, because running was not something a shepherd did on a warm afternoon with no wolves about, and if he had begun to run then some watching part of him might have asked why, and he did not want to know the answer yet.

A traveler. Asking about the passes. It was nothing. It was spring, and spring brought traders, pilgrims, lost hunters, all the small traffic of the world that washed against the valley's skirts and drained away again. In ten years there had been a dozen such. None of them had mattered.

But his hands, when he noticed them, were trembling again, and this time he could not pretend it was the cold.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like slow blue water. He milked, he banked the fire, he ate, and then he sat in the doorway with the last light going amber on the ridge and did the final exercise of the day, the one that mattered more than all the fence-mending and goat-tending put together.

He counted what he was not.

He was not a storm-caller. Storm-calling required a name, and names required a world that would carry them, and the valley forgot words the way it forgot faces; here, in the deep grammar of the place, there was no word for what he was, and so he could not be it. He was not the heir to anything. He was not anyone's brother anymore — the word had worn thin as an old coin, its face rubbed smooth, spendable nowhere. He was not guilty, because guilt required memory and the valley had eaten the edges of his until only the shape remained, a hole in the middle of him shaped exactly like a person.

He was Gideon Dunmore, shepherd, thirty-four years old or thereabouts, owner of eleven goats, one bad roof, and a kettle that whistled flat.

That was all. That was enough. Some nights it was almost enough.

The stars came out hard and close, the way they did here where no magic bled into the air to blur them. He named none of them. Naming was a door he kept shut.

He was still sitting there when he heard the dog.

There was no dog in the valley — he'd have known; he knew every animal within a day's walk the way shepherds do — but the sound came up the river path anyway, distant and ragged, a big dog barking itself hoarse at something, or a dog being worried by something, or —

Or a man crying out in a voice so torn and wrong that it took Gideon's mind a full three seconds to file it under *human*, and another three to understand what human distress at the ford at dusk, ten miles from anywhere, could possibly mean.

He was on his feet before he decided to be. The old reflexes woke in him like lamps coming on floor by floor: the weight shifting to the balls of his feet, the senses opening, the strange cold clarity that had once preceded every storm he'd ever called. He caught himself doing it — catching the tension, gathering it, the way his body had gathered lightning once upon a time — and he strangled the impulse with both hands, the way he'd practiced, the way he'd gotten good at.

Down by the ford, faint on the evening air, he heard splashing. Heavy, stumbling, the sound of a body fighting the current.

Nobody crossed the ford at dusk. Nobody crossed the ford at all, hardly. The high passes were closed nine months of the year, and the low road swung wide around the valley as if the mapmakers themselves had agreed to look away.

Gideon stood in his doorway with the last light dying and the first stars sharpening overhead, and felt the tremor rise in his hands again, unbidden, eager, like a dog hearing its name.

Whatever was coming up the river path, it was coming from outside the valley.

Nothing from outside had reached him in ten years. That had been the covenant, unwritten and unspoken and paid for in everything he had lost: stay small, stay quiet, stay forgettable, and the world will forget you back. He had kept his side of it for a decade. He had buried his name, his craft, his grief, his brother — buried them all under barley and fence-posts and flat-whistling kettles, and the grave had held.

The splashing came again, closer now, and with it a smell the wind brought him that made the hair rise on his arms: blood, yes, but under the blood something scorched, something burnt from within, the smell of a wound that smoke had come out of rather than into.

Gideon took down the lantern, and the heavy crook-handled staff, and stepped out into the dark, and went down toward the water to see what the world had finally sent him.
