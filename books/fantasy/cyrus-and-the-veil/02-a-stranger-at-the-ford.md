## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river was the first thing Gideon learned to love in the valley, and the last thing he trusted.

It ran shallow and quick over pale stones, cold enough in every season to numb the fingers, and it made a sound like someone whispering the same word over and over — *stay, stay, stay* — until you stopped hearing it as language and started hearing it as law. Ten years of mornings had taught him its moods: the green glass of high summer, the brown muscle of spring melt, the thin lace of ice that formed at the edges in winter and never spread to the middle, because the middle never froze here. Nothing in the valley froze entirely. Nothing in the valley burned entirely, either. Everything was held at a temperature just short of change, like a breath held so long it stopped feeling like holding and started feeling like being.

Gideon crouched at the ford with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, washing the smell of the goat pen off his hands. The water bit at his knuckles. He let it. Cold was honest. Cold asked nothing of him except endurance, and endurance was the one skill exile had perfected in him the way a river perfects a stone — not by striking it, but by refusing, year after year, to stop.

His hands were steady this morning. He noted that the way a farmer notes the sky: without hope, without relief, only inventory. Some days they shook. Not all day, not dramatically — just a fine tremor in the right hand, a flutter like a bird trapped under cloth, there when he reached for the latch or lifted the ladle or tried to write his own name, which he no longer did, because there was no one left in the valley who needed reminding of it and no one outside the valley he wished to remind. On those days he would sit on his doorstep and press his palm flat against the doorframe until the wood took the shaking into itself, until the tremor passed through him and out, into something older and more patient than he was. The valley drank it. The valley drank everything. That was its purpose, and its mercy, and its slow cruelty: magic could not follow him here, but neither could it quite release him. It pooled in him instead, tideless, waiting for a moon that would never rise.

He had learned to call that waiting *peace*. It had taken him six years to learn the word and four more to almost believe it.

The goats were loose in the high pasture above the ford, their bells a scattered music in the fog. Mist lay along the water in long white ropes, and the birches on the far bank stood in it like the ribs of some drowned and patient animal. It was, by every measure Gideon permitted himself, an ordinary morning. He had bread rising under a cloth at home. There was a fence post leaning at the top of the east field that he had been meaning to straighten for three weeks and would likely mean to straighten for three more. His boots were by the door, patched at the left toe. His life fit him the way an old coat fits — loosely, worn soft at the shoulders, smelling faintly of another time — and he had stopped noticing the fit, which was the whole art of it.

Then the bells went wrong.

Not silent — nothing in the valley ever went fully silent — but *scattered*, the way they scattered when something moved through the herd that wasn't a shepherd. A clatter of hooves. A single sharp bleat, cut off. Gideon rose slowly, water sheeting from his hands, and turned upriver, toward the tree line where the mist thinned into shadow.

At first he thought it was a deer. Then he thought it was a drunk. Then he saw the way the figure ran — lurching, arms wide, correcting itself against falls that had already happened — and understood that no drunk ever ran like that. Drunks fell forward. This man fell *away*, as though the air behind him were shoving.

"Hey!" Gideon's voice came out rougher than he intended, rusted from disuse in anything louder than conversation with animals. "Hey — the bank's soft past the willows—"

The man did not hear him, or did not care. He hit the shallows at a dead run and the water exploded white around his knees, and in the sudden clearing of the spray Gideon saw what ten years of valley quiet had made him unready to see.

Blood. A great deal of it, from wounds that had no business still bleeding — a gash across the brow, a tear through the shoulder of a grey riding coat, a dark bloom spreading low across the abdomen. And the blood was *wrong*. It steamed. In the cool of the morning, in air that couldn't have been warmer than the man's own skin, each wound gave off a thin grey smoke, delicate as incense, curling upward and dissolving before it reached the height of Gideon's eyes. It smelled of hot iron and struck flint and something else beneath, something scorched and sweet, like sugar burnt black in a pan.

Magic-wounds. Gideon's body knew it before his mind consented to. His hands began, very quietly, to shake.

*No.* He closed them into fists. *Not here. Not in the valley. It can't reach here.*

But the man was already down.

He went under at the center of the ford, where the water ran fastest, and for one absurd heartbeat Gideon simply stood and watched — watched the grey coat billow and darken, watched one hand slap the surface, watched the mist close over the place where a person had been — and then his body moved without consulting him, the way it always had, the way that had once been called courage and was now merely habit. He was in the river before he remembered deciding to be. The cold seized him to the waist. He caught a fistful of collar, hauled, braced against the current's shove, and dragged the man onto the near bank with a strength he kept for emergencies and lambing season and never otherwise examined.

They ended up on the gravel together, Gideon kneeling in the shallows, the stranger coughing river water and blood onto the stones between them. Up close the man was young — younger than Gideon had first taken him for, twenty-five at most, with a courier's lean hardness and a face currently the color of the mist. His eyes found Gideon's and fixed there with the terrible clarity of the nearly gone.

"You're real," the man said. His voice was shredded. "You're — the Veil didn't take you. You're standing *in* it."

"I'm standing in a river," Gideon said. "You're bleeding on my morning."

The man laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough brought up more of that smoking darkness. Gideon looked around — at the empty banks, the blind white trees, the indifferent hills — and felt the valley's silence press against him from all sides like deep water. No help was coming. Help, in the valley, was a neighbor two miles off and a surgeon three days away, and neither would arrive in time, and neither, he suspected, would know what to do about wounds that smoked.

"Press there," he heard himself say, catching the man's hand and dragging it to the abdominal wound. "Hard. Both hands if you've got them."

"I haven't got them." But he pressed anyway, teeth bared, and the smoke leaked out between his fingers like breath in winter. "It doesn't matter. It doesn't bleed like blood. It burns like — like a coal wrapped in paper. She said—" His eyes rolled, hunted the treeline, came back. "She said the valley would slow it. She said the Veil starves it. Is that true? Tell me that's true."

"She," Gideon repeated. The word arrived in him like a stone dropped down a well, falling a long way before it landed. "Who's *she*?"

The man didn't answer. He was looking past Gideon now, up the slope, at the mist among the birches — and his face did something Gideon had only seen once before, years ago, on a night he did not permit himself to visit. It emptied. It became the face of a man watching something arrive that his mind refused to name because naming it would make the next moment real.

"It followed me," the courier whispered. "I crossed at dusk yesterday. I ran all night. It shouldn't be able to — the Veil eats it, it *starves* out here, she promised me—" His hand shot out and clamped Gideon's wrist with a strength that had terror in it rather than muscle. "Is it here? Look at the water. Look at the water and tell me if it's here."

Gideon looked.

The river ran as it always ran, quick and clear over pale stone. The mist hung as mist hangs, doing nothing, meaning nothing. A heron stood in the shallows thirty yards downstream, gray and patient, one foot lifted, regarding them both with its flat yellow eye.

And yet.

There was a place upstream — Gideon could not have said how he knew, only that he knew, the way animals know weather — where the current bent oddly. Where the water seemed to flow *around* something rather than past it, parting in a smooth lens-shaped seam, the way a stream parts around a submerged boulder. Except there was no boulder. There was nothing there at all, a stretch of ordinary water perhaps eight feet long, moving in a pattern that was almost right and therefore entirely wrong, and the heron, Gideon noticed, had begun very quietly to edge away from it, one slow step at a time, its neck low, its eye fixed.

"The ford," Gideon said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Can you stand? We need to be off this bank."

"It's in the water." The courier was shaking now, a full-body rattle that had nothing to do with cold. "Oh gods, it's in the *water*, it learned the water, it used me to learn the water—" He tried to rise, failed, tried again, and Gideon got a shoulder under his arm and hauled him upright, and for a moment they stood swaying together in the shallows like two drunks, two strangers, two men bound by nothing except the fact that one of them was dying and the other had forgotten how to run.

Because that was the truth of it, and Gideon knew it with a clarity that felt like frost forming: he could run. Right now, this minute, he could put the man down and walk away up the hill and bar his door and tell himself, as he had told himself for ten years, that the valley kept things out, that the Veil was a wall and not a membrane, that what happened at the ford happened to other people. He had built his whole life on that sentence. He heard it now in his own skull, steady as the river's whisper — *stay, stay, stay* — and heard, beneath it, for the first time, the question the whisper had always been drowning:

*A wall keeps things out. What does it keep in?*

The seam in the current slid closer. Without hurry. Without any motion at all, really — one moment it was upstream, and then it was opposite them, eight feet of water flowing wrongly, and the air above it had begun to dim, subtly, the way light dims behind smoked glass. The heron lifted off, unhurried, offended, and beat away downstream.

"What is it?" Gideon asked. He hated how level his voice came out. Level was old training; level belonged to a life he'd buried. "What's your name? Who sent you? What is that thing?"

"Cassian," the man breathed. "Cassian Vey. Courier of the — of the third road, sworn under seal—" A wet laugh. "Seals don't matter now. Listen. *Listen.* I wasn't supposed to stop. I was supposed to run the length of the valley and out the far side and never look back, and I would have, I swear it, but the wound—" He sagged; Gideon took his weight. "It rides the hurt. Whatever did this, it rides the hurt like a rider rides a horse, and every mile it grew heavier, and by the ford I couldn't — my legs just—" 

"And *she*?" Gideon said. "You said *she said*. Who is she?"

Something moved through Cassian's face then — grief, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes. "Sera," he whispered. "Sera Voss. My partner. She took the map and went ahead. She said if I fell, she'd circle back for me, and I told her not to, I told her the math was simple, one courier or two, and she looked at me the way she looks at—" His voice cracked clean through. "She'll come back. She never listens. She'll come back and it will be waiting, it learns, do you understand, it *learns*, and she doesn't know it learned the water—"

The seam in the current was directly across from them now. Close enough that Gideon could see, in the bending water, a reflection that should not have existed: not his own face, not Cassian's, but a darkness with structure to it, a suggestion of robes, of a hood, of a patience so vast it made the valley's ten years feel like a sneeze. It did not move. It did not need to. It regarded them the way a ledger regards a debt.

Every instinct Gideon owned — the old ones, the buried ones, the storm-trained ones — screamed at him in a language he hadn't spoken in a decade. *Power,* it said. *Here, now, use it. Call it up. You are standing in a river and you were born knowing what rivers are for.* His right hand trembled against Cassian's back, and the tremor was not fear. That was the horror of it. The tremor was *hunger*, answering across the Veil like a dog hearing a whistle it had been told no longer existed.

Gideon drove the hunger down. Drove it down the way he had driven it down ten thousand times, with routine and cold water and the memory of why he had buried it — and for the first time in ten years, the burial did not hold cleanly. Something came back up with him. Not power. Just a question, small and terrible:

*How long does a wall hold, when something on the other side has learned to knock?*

"It can't act here," he said aloud, to Cassian, to himself, to the dark reflection in the water. "Whatever it is — the Veil starves it. You said so yourself. It's blind and starving in this valley. So we walk. Slowly, away from the water, up to my house, and we—"

Cassian's knees folded.

Gideon caught him, lowered him, laid him on the gravel with a gentleness that surprised them both. The courier's lips were gray. The smoke from his wounds had thickened, curling now in ropes instead of threads, and where it touched the wet stones it left marks — faint, charred outlines, like the shadows burned into walls at Hiroshima, like the valley itself was being *noted*, recorded, claimed in advance.

"No," Cassian said. It was barely sound. "No, listen, there's no time for walking, there's no time — it waits because it knows I'm done, it's waiting for the — for the *transfer*—" His hand crawled across the gravel, found Gideon's sleeve, gripped it with the last of everything he had. "She said you'd understand. She said the man in the valley would understand better than anyone alive. She said — she said to tell you your name isn't the one you're using."

The words went through Gideon like a blade finding the gap in armor. He did not move. He did not breathe. Somewhere behind his ribs, behind ten years of careful, deliberate, load-bearing silence, something shifted its weight.

"My name," Gideon said, "is Dunmore. I keep goats."

"Gideon Dunmore." Cassian smiled, and it was the worst thing Gideon had seen in ten years, that smile — tender and apologetic and utterly certain. "Storm-caller. Sworn shield of the western marches. The man who walked out of the war and buried his name under a hill. She knew you'd deny it. She said deny it all you like, the river knows, the river's been calling you *stay* for ten years and you're still here, and men only stay where they're called if some part of them hears it."

The seam in the water drifted a foot closer. The light above it dimmed another shade. And Gideon, kneeling in the shallows of the only safe place left to him, holding a dying stranger whose blood smoked like a snuffed candle, felt the valley's great silence change key — felt it stop being peace and start being *held breath*.

"Tell me what she wants," he said.

"To live," Cassian whispered. His eyes were losing focus, wandering up toward the birches, the mist, something beyond both. "We all just — wanted to live. And the king—" A shudder took him. "The king his father—"

He stopped.

Not trailed off. *Stopped* — mid-word, mid-breath, the way a clock stops, the way a heart stops, with a finality that has no argument in it. His grip on Gideon's sleeve did not loosen. It simply ceased to be a grip, becoming instead only weight.

For a long moment Gideon knelt there in the cold water, holding a dead man's arm, listening to the river whisper its one word — *stay, stay, stay* — and watching the seam in the current, eight feet of wrongness, drift closer with the unhurried certainty of something that had all the time in the world and knew exactly what it was waiting for.

Upstream, faint through the mist, from the direction of the far bank, came the sound of footsteps. Light ones. Quick ones. Running.

And a woman's voice, raw with a night's flight, calling a dead man's name.

* * *

"Sera," Gideon said — not to her, not yet; she could not have heard him over the water. He said it to himself, testing the shape of what was coming, the way a man tests ice before putting his weight on it.

The footsteps grew louder. The seam in the river slid, smooth as oil, to intercept the far bank.

Gideon got one arm under Cassian's body and began, without any plan at all, to drag him out of the water — because whatever else was true, the thing in the river had come for a courier, and there was one lying in the shallows, and there was a second one about to step into reach.

"Don't," he breathed, to the water, to the dark, to the ten years of silence he could feel cracking like river ice. "*Don't you touch her.*"

The mist on the far bank parted, and she came out of it at a dead sprint — and the thing in the water rose to meet her.
