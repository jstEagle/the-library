## 3. The Map and the Oath

The man died at dusk, which Gideon would later think was fitting, because everything that had ever come to him from beyond the Veil seemed to arrive at the hour when the light gave out and the shapes of things grew uncertain.

He had carried him up from the ford as the afternoon thinned, one arm under the stranger's shoulders, boots slipping on the wet stones. The man was lighter than he should have been, the way birds are light, the way things are light when a great deal of them has already gone. His blood came away on Gideon's hands in slow dark smears, and where it touched skin it did not cool. It *itched*. By the time he laid the courier down on his own hearthstones — he could not have said why the cottage rather than the barn; some instinct older than thought — Gideon's palms were flushed red to the wrist, and beneath the flush, faint as veins in marble, thin lines of silver crawled and faded.

Magic did not work in the valley. That was the whole of the valley's purpose, the reason it existed at all, tucked into a fold of mountains that the maps forgot on purpose. Whatever the courier was bleeding from, it had ridden him across the border like a hound on a scent and was dying with him now, stubbornly, refusing to be left behind.

Gideon built up the fire because fire was something he knew how to do. He boiled water. He found the clean rags, the needle, the jar of goose fat and yarrow that he kept for the goats and had never once needed for a person. Ten years of ordinary competence, gathered like stones in his pockets, and none of it any use against whatever was eating this man from the inside.

Because that was what the wounds were. He saw it clearly when he cut away the ruined tunic: no blade had made these. The tears in the courier's flesh were cauterized at their edges, grey and crisp as old parchment, and from them rose a smell Gideon knew from his former life — ozone and burnt hair, the smell of power discharged wrongly, power that had passed through a body not built to hold it. And in the deepest of the wounds, something *moved*, a shadow under the surface, turning over slowly like a fish under ice.

Gideon sat back on his heels and made himself breathe.

"You know what I am," the courier said. His voice was a ruin, but underneath the ruin there was discipline, a soldier's or an official's, someone accustomed to being obeyed. "You looked at the wounds first and the face second. Ordinary men do it the other way around."

"Ordinary men are all there are here." Gideon wrung out a rag in the hot water. "Hold still."

"Hold still." A ghost of a laugh, which cost him visibly. "Yes. Let's pretend that will help."

Gideon cleaned what he could reach and bound what he could bind and did both badly, because his hands were not shaking from the difficulty of the work but from something rising in him like water in a well — the old awful *attention* of magic noticing magic, the way one struck bell answers another across a room. The valley was supposed to be silent. It had been silent for ten years, silent as a held breath. And now silence itself was leaking out of this dying man onto Gideon's floor.

"What's your name?" he asked, mostly to have something to ask.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters if you die here. Someone should know who died here."

The courier considered that with eyes that were very clear and very tired, grey eyes going glassy at the edges, the pupils uneven. "Sera," he said, and then coughed, wetly, terribly. "Sera Voss. Courier of the Ninth Road. Which you'll not have heard of, and that's the point of the Ninth Road." His gaze drifted to the fire. "You have a good house. Goats. That's a life, isn't it. Someone decided you'd earned a quiet life."

"Nobody decided anything for me." The lie came out flat and immediate, and Sera Voss heard it, because dying men hear everything true.

"Mm," he said. "Well. Nobody decided anything for me either. Here."

* * *

It took him a long time to get the pack off his shoulder, and Gideon should have refused to help, and did not. The satchel was oilskin, double-sealed, stamped with a sigil Gideon did not recognize and yet felt in his teeth — a tower split by a line of lightning, half-worn away as if someone had tried to scrub it off with sand. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone soft with handling, was a map.

Not printed. Drawn. Ink on hide, by the look of it, rolled tight and tied with wire, and when Gideon held it without unrolling it he felt the way he used to feel standing on a ridge before a storm: that pressure behind the sternum, the sense of a vast attention leaning toward him through distance. The map wanted to be opened. The map had been *sent*.

"Burn that after," Sera said. "Or don't. You won't. She said you wouldn't."

The pronoun landed in the small room like a stone in a well. "*She* said. Who said? Who told you about me? Nobody knows—"

"Sera Voss knew." He smiled, and it cracked the grey skin of his lips. "Different woman. Same name, near enough. Our order does that. Names pass down like coats." His breath caught, held, released. "I'm sorry. I'd planned to say all this properly. There was a whole speech. Dying is very undignified work, Dunmore. Nothing like the songs."

Gideon went cold from the scalp down.

That name. Not spoken aloud in ten years. Not written. He had shed it at the border like a coat himself — *Dunmore* left folded on a rock somewhere north of the Veil, and taken up *Merrick* instead, a plain name for a plain man, a goatherd's name, a name with no history and no weight. No one alive beyond the mountains had reason to know the old one. No one alive beyond the mountains had reason to *care*.

"How—" His voice failed and restarted. "Who sent you?"

"The same person who's been sending us for years. Letters that never find you. Nine couriers before me." He said it simply, factually, the way a man reports weather. "Turned back at the ford, every time. The valley keeps its promises. We can't cross carrying anything living — anything *magical*, I mean. So she sent me with nothing. Paper and words. Paper and words can walk anywhere."

"Letters," Gideon repeated stupidly. "For *years*?"

"She never gave up on you. Stubborn woman." Sera's eyes closed. Opened again, slower each time. "I don't understand it myself, if you want honesty. What you did—"

"Don't."

"—it doesn't matter now," he finished, gently, as if Gideon hadn't spoken. "None of the old griefs matter. Only the new ones. Listen. Listen to me, storm-caller, because I am going to run out."

And there it was, the title, the word he had spent ten years starving out of himself, and it fit him still, perfectly, horribly, like a key fitting a lock that had been bricked over.

"I can't," Gideon said, and heard how young he sounded. "Whatever this is — the king, the crown, the wars, all of it — I buried that. I *earned* the right to bury it. Find someone else. There must be others. There's always someone else."

"There isn't," Sera Voss said. "That's rather the entire problem."

He moved then — a terrible, deliberate movement, dragging himself up on one elbow despite the wounds, despite Gideon's hands trying to press him down — and gripped Gideon's wrist with fingers that had almost no strength left and somehow would not be shaken off. His skin burned. Not fever-hot. *Lit*-hot, a coal's heat, and through the contact Gideon felt the last of whatever carried him flicker and gutter like a lamp running dry.

"Heir to an unfinished oath," Sera said, and each word now cost him visibly, coins paid out one at a time from an emptying purse. "That's what you are. Not by blood. By witness. The old king bound something into his crown to save his son, and the binding was meant to end, and it hasn't ended, and the thing wearing the crown now — it eats. Do you understand? It eats what makes a man a man. Memory first. Then love. Then the ability to refuse. The boy king Aldric has maybe a season left before there's nothing inside the boy at all except the Regent, wearing him, ruling with his hands, signing with his name—"

"Then take it to someone with an army."

"Armies serve the crown." A thin smile. "You see the difficulty."

His grip tightened. With his free hand he pressed the map against Gideon's chest, hard, as if he could push it through the ribs into the space behind the heart.

"Three keystones," he said. "The binding was made with three, and only three can unmake it. One in the drowned bell-tower at Callowmere, where the water remembers the ringing. One in the quarry the stone-cutters abandoned, the Weeping Quarry — it will test you, so be ready to be tested. The third..." His brow furrowed, and for a moment the clarity went out of his eyes entirely, and Gideon thought he was gone. "...the third is kept. Hollowmere. Ask for the keeper. Ask kindly. She has waited longer than anyone should wait for anything."

"Wait. Slower. Say it again—"

"No time." It was true; Gideon could hear the truth of it in the wet whistle of the man's breathing, shorter and shorter intervals, a tide going out. "The renewal rite. Solstice. After solstice, nothing can be done — the possession becomes permanent, root and branch, and every year after that the Regent grows stronger, spreads further, sends its inquisitors further along the roads. Your valley included. You understand that, don't you? There is no *outside* forever. There is only later."

The fire popped. Outside, the goats shifted and muttered in their pen, ordinary animals making ordinary sounds, and Gideon hated them a little for it, hated the whole gentle machinery of his days, feed and mend and sleep, that had seemed like salvation this morning and now sounded like a man humming loudly in a burning house.

"Why me?" It came out raw. "Ten years. You don't understand what I — the last time I called the storm, someone—" He stopped. Swallowed the rest of it, the way he had swallowed it a thousand times, the way the valley had been built to help him swallow it. "Someone died. I don't carry oaths well. Ask anyone."

"I did," Sera said. "She said that's exactly why. A man who knows what his power costs holds it carefully. The Regent's whole strength is carelessness. Appetite without consequence." His hand slid down Gideon's arm, strength leaving it by degrees. "She also said — and I quote her exactly, because I promised — she said: *tell him the valley was never meant to be a punishment. It was meant to be a seed bank.*"

"A what?"

"I don't know." The courier's voice was failing now, fraying at the edges of every syllable. "Old woman. Riddles. Drives me mad. But she's been right about everything else, so write it down—"

And then his whole body seized, and the silver light that had been guttering under his skin flared once, bright, bright enough to throw shadows of the rafters across the walls, and in the flare Gideon saw the shadow-thing in the wound rear up, thrash, and dissolve — the last of the foreign magic dying with its carrier, refusing to be left behind even now.

Sera Voss slumped. Gideon caught him. Held him. For a moment the courier's eyes focused one final time, and they were calm, almost amused, a man reading the last line of a letter he'd been carrying too long.

"Tell the king his father—"

Nothing.

The sentence hung in the room, unfinished, an arrow loosed at a target that had ceased to exist mid-flight. Gideon knelt on his own hearthstones holding a dead stranger, waiting, absurdly, for the rest of it, for the *his father loved him*, or *his father foresaw this*, or *his father left instructions*, any ending at all. The fire ticked. The wind worried the thatch. The man's weight settled into his arms with the particular absolute heaviness of the dead, heavier than the living ever are, and no ending came.

Gideon laid him out with more care than he had shown anything in years. Closed his eyes. Folded the hands. It seemed important that the hands be folded. Then he sat back on the floor beside the body and looked at the map lying on the stones where it had slipped, coiled and wired and patient, radiating its low insistent want like a coal that would not cool.

He should burn it.

That was the sane arithmetic. A dying stranger's ravings, a name spoken that should have stayed dead, three impossible stones in places out of fable — none of it was his. He had paid for his quiet. He had paid *dearly*; there was a grave behind the north pasture that stood as the receipt, visited once a year, weeded, never spoken to. The valley had accepted him on the condition that he bring nothing in, and he had brought in a corpse and a mission before supper.

Burn it. Burn the map, bury the man, scrub the blood from the stones, and tomorrow the goats would need feeding just as they always had, and the tremor in his hands was only age or cold or nothing.

Gideon built the fire up. He took the map to the flames. He stood there holding it over the heat long enough for the wire to grow warm in his fingers.

He could not open his hand.

It was not that the map gripped him — he examined the feeling with a craftsman's suspicion, and found nothing so dramatic. It was simpler and far worse than that. Somewhere under ten years of ash and routine, in the sealed cellar of himself where he had walled up the storm and the grief and the name, something had heard the words *heir to an unfinished oath* and turned over in its sleep. His hand would not open because some part of him had already accepted delivery, the way a scar accepts rain, the way an exiled bell answers a struck bell across a room whether it wants to or not.

He stood there until his arm shook. Then he swore — a foul, heartfelt oath in a language the valley had never heard — and carried the map to the table instead, and untied the wire, and unrolled it.

Hide, tanned thin, covered edge to edge in ink. Mountains he recognized, and ranges he didn't. The valley itself rendered precisely, lovingly, with a single word at its center in a hand that was careful and feminine and slightly hurried, as if written while walking: *remember*. And leading out of the valley, drawn in a different ink — red-brown, and Gideon did not let himself think about what kind — a route. Through the Veil. Toward the drowned tower. Three marks like small crowns, two named and one blank, waiting at Hollowmere.

At the bottom corner, in the same careful hand, a line of script so small he had to hold it to the firelight:

*You were always going to say yes. I've known you forty years. — S.V.*

Forty years. Gideon read it three times. He had lived thirty-six of them.

He was still standing there, the map in his hands and the dead man on his floor and the sentence *tell the king his father* turning over and over in him like the fish-shadow in the wound, when he heard it.

Hooves. More than one horse, coming fast along the valley track, in the last blue light, moving with the particular disciplined rhythm he had hoped never to hear again in his life.

Riders. At dusk. In the valley that forgot.

Gideon rolled the map, hid it under a floorboard with hands that had stopped shaking and gone very steady, and reached for his cloak.
