## 4. What Follows the Dying

The goats knew before Gideon did.

He was in the yard at first light, hands buried to the wrist in the churned mud of the pen, when the whole herd went still at once — twenty-three heads lifting in unison toward the western ridge, ears pricked, breath steaming in the cold. Gideon had learned to read animals the way other men read weather, and this was a sentence he did not like: *something is coming up the valley road.*

Not wolves. Wolves made them press together, a knot of shared warmth and fear. This was different. This was the stillness of prey deciding whether it had already been seen.

Gideon straightened slowly, wiping his hands on his apron, and looked west. The morning mist hung low over the river, thick as spilled milk, and through it came two figures on foot, grey as the fog that birthed them. They walked without lanterns, though dawn had barely crested the eastern fells, and they walked in step — not deliberately matched, he thought, but matched the way two bells of the same casting ring at the same pitch.

His stomach dropped through the mud beneath his boots.

*Grey robes.* He had heard of them only in fragments, in the years before his exile — whispered warnings from his brother's mouth, from the mouths of men who traded beyond the Veil. The Inquisition of the Crown Regent. Seekers of unbound magic. They did not come often to places like the valley, because the valley was the one place in all the world their craft was useless; magic could not live here, could not follow, could not be found.

But Sera Voss had died with magic smoking in her wounds, three paces from his door.

"Stay," he told the goats, absurdly, and crossed the yard to the cottage. He paused at the threshold only long enough to look at the patch of turned earth behind the woodshed, where he had buried her body two nights past, deep, under a slab of fieldstone he'd had to drag with a rope and his own aching back. He had said no words over her. He hadn't known any that would fit.

Now he wished he'd said something. Anything that might have marked the grave as belonging to grief rather than guilt.

* * *

They knocked the way rain falls — steadily, without malice, as though the sound were simply what happened to doors when such men existed in the world.

Gideon opened it with a bowl of porridge in one hand, having decided in the four minutes since he'd seen them cross the ford that playing startled farmer was better than playing prepared one. He had rehearsed nothing. That was the trouble. Ten years of routine had stripped the liar out of him almost entirely; the valley asked nothing of him but honesty — honest labor, honest silence, honest forgetting.

The taller of the two stood forward into his doorway. A woman, past middle age, with a face like a winter apple — dried, seamed, still holding its shape. Her eyes were pale to the point of near-colorlessness, and they moved over Gideon the way an appraiser's fingers move over silver, testing for flaws. Behind her, the shorter one — young, soft-jawed, wrong for the robe he wore — drifted sideways, unhurried, and Gideon understood with a cold twist in his gut that the boy was angling to see past his shoulder into the single room of the house.

"Sister Hale," the woman said. "This is Brother Emon. We travel under the authority of the Crown Regent." She produced no papers. Apparently she believed her robes were letters of introduction enough, and the terrible thing was that she was right. "A courier passed through this district some days ago. A woman. Dark hair, grey eyes, a scar through her left brow. You will have seen her."

"No," Gideon said.

The word came out too fast and too flat, a coin slapped down on a table instead of counted into a palm. Sister Hale's expression did not change, which was somehow worse than if it had. She simply waited, the way stone waits, while somewhere behind Gideon's ribs his heart began to hammer out a confession on its own.

"Seen no one," he tried again, and made himself slow down, made himself put the porridge bowl down on the shelf by the door as if his hands needed freeing for chores, as if this interruption were merely inconvenient. "Roads've been quiet all month. Just me and the goats."

"The goats," Sister Hale repeated.

"Aye." He gestured vaguely at the pen. It was the truest thing he'd said yet, and it showed.

She stepped past him into the cottage without asking. Her robe brushed his arm and the wool smelled of nothing at all — no sweat, no road dust, no incense, none of the accumulated human stink that clung to every traveler Gideon had ever met. These people smelled laundered and empty, like cloth kept in a closed chest. The young one, Emon, lingered at the threshold, and Gideon saw him tilt his head slightly, nostrils flaring, tasting the air the way a hound tastes wind.

Inside, Hale's pale eyes went over everything with that same appraising touch. The hearth, banked but warm. The table, scarred and clean. The bed, one bed, slept in by one man. And on the table — Gideon saw her see it half a heartbeat too late to do anything — a coil of bandage linen, bloodstained, folded with a soldier's neatness rather than a farmer's.

He watched her stop beside it. Watched her lay two fingers on the stained fold, gently, almost tenderly.

"You keep sheep," she said. Not a question.

"Goats."

"Do your goats bleed much?"

"Lambs do. Kids. When they're birthing." His voice sounded distant to him, someone else's voice, a man speaking from the far side of running water. "Spring's hard on them."

"It is autumn."

"Late kids happen."

Sister Hale lifted her fingers from the linen and turned to face him fully, and now her expression changed at last — not to suspicion, which he had braced for, but to something more patient and more dreadful. Kindness, or its flawless counterfeit.

"Gideon Dunmore," she said.

Every hair on his arms rose. The porridge bowl sat on the shelf where he'd left it, and he thought, absurdly and vividly, that it would fall, that he would hear it break, and that the sound would be the last ordinary thing in the world.

"You know my name," he managed.

"We know many names." She clasped her hands before her, sleeves folding like grey water. "We knew the courier carried something of great value, and we know she reached this valley, and we know that no one reaches this valley by accident. The Veil does not open for wanderers, Master Dunmore. It opens for those it is made to open for. Someone drew her here. Perhaps someone drew her *here*—" and one pale eye flicked, briefly, around his mean little room, and back. "If you found her. If she spoke to you. If she gave you anything — a word, an object, a charge — you should understand what you are carrying."

"I don't carry anything," Gideon said.

It was even true, in the letter of it. The map lay wrapped in oilcloth inside the false bottom of his grain chest, and the oath — the oath wasn't a thing you could confiscate. It lived in him now like a swallowed hook.

"She was wounded when she crossed," Hale went on, softly, relentlessly. "Burned from within. Our order calls it *the Regent's kiss*. It means she fled something the crown wanted very badly, and it means the thing she fled was very close behind her." She paused. "You will have noticed, perhaps, that she did not survive. Things the crown wants do not tend to survive long once the crown notices them." Another pause, longer, and her colorless eyes settled on him with the weight of laid stones. "Nor do things the crown wants that have been *given away*."

Outside, the young one, Emon, had begun to walk the perimeter of the cottage. Slowly. His lips were moving.

*Counting,* Gideon realized. Or reciting. Some litany, some cantrip — except no cantrip could work here, nothing magical could work here, that was the whole covenant of the valley, ten years of proof—

But Emon stopped at the corner of the woodshed. Stopped dead, head bowed, and stood there so long that Gideon's skin crawled off his bones in fine cold sheets.

He was standing six feet from Sera Voss's grave.

* * *

They made him walk them down to the ford afterward. He understood why: it was not about the ford. It was about watching him. Hale fell into step beside him on the rutted track and talked the whole way, pleasantly, about weather and wool prices and the hardness of the winter coming, and every question was a small hook trailing through the current of the conversation, and Gideon answered what he could answer honestly and lied where he had to, and both felt identical in his mouth, which frightened him more than either alone.

Emon walked behind them. Off the path, mostly. Drifting along the treeline with his head tilted like a man listening to music no one else could hear.

At the ford, where the shallows ran loud and white over the stones, Hale stopped and regarded the water with something like distaste.

"Dead water," she said. "Do you know how it feels to us, Master Dunmore? To stand in a place where the world has been... muted? Like walking through a house where every mirror has been covered." She looked at him. "Some of my brothers find it unbearable. I find it restful. I imagine you do too."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" Almost gentle. "Ten years in a valley with no name on any crown charter. Ten years, and no visitors, and no letters, and a man who flinches when thunder rolls though there's never been a storm here worth the name." She let that sit in the roar of the water between them. "The valley forgets, they say. But the crown does not. The crown keeps excellent records, Master Dunmore. Yours makes melancholy reading."

The river went on shouting over its stones, indifferent. Gideon stood very still and felt the fear in him change state — the way water changes to ice, motionless and hard and holding a new shape. All his life, fear had been the storm in him: sudden, vast, destructive, and always, always moving. This was not that. This was something stiller and colder, and it surprised him by feeling almost like clarity.

*They will not leave. They will come back, or others will come after them, and next time they will bring the tools to dig.*

"You'll stay for a meal?" he heard himself say, and marveled at the steadiness of it. "Bread's rough, but—"

"That's kind." Hale's smile was thin and correct. "But we're expected south. There was a third traveler, after all, and the trail has grown cold in this..." a small gesture at the mist-wrapped fells, "...quiet. We'll want to widen our search. North, I think. Over the pass." Her colorless eyes rested on him one moment longer than comfort allowed. "Keep well, Gideon Dunmore. Mind your goats. And if anything should wash downriver to you — anything the crown has lost — remember that the crown has a long memory and longer reach, and that mercy is a matter of what is returned, not what is hidden."

Then they went, the two of them, back across the ford and up the western track, grey into grey, until the mist took them and the goats relaxed all at once like a held breath released.

Gideon stood in the shallows' spray until his sleeves were soaked, doing sums he did not want to finish.

* * *

That night he dug the map out of the grain chest and spread it on the table by candlelight, and for the first time really looked at it — not at the terror of it, but at the thing itself.

It was drawn on skin. He had known that since the second night, when the candle had passed behind it and shown the fine tracery of pores; he had chosen not to think about whose skin, and he chose not to now. What he looked at instead was the route. It began here — a small inked mark at the valley's edge, the crossing-point, the place where the Veil wore thin — and it ran north, over the pass.

North. Over the pass. Exactly the direction Sister Hale had said they would search.

*Because they know,* he thought. *They didn't miss the trail. They're following it. They were never looking for her body — they were confirming where she went, and who received her. And when they confirm it, they'll come back, and there won't be two of them. There'll be twelve. And they won't stand in my kitchen being patient.*

He turned the map. In the lower corner, in a cramped hand quite unlike the confident cartography, someone had written four words:

*Three stones. One key.*

And beneath that, smaller still, pressed so hard the skin had nearly split:

*Before the solstice.*

Gideon sat back. The candle guttered, and his shadow swung huge across the low ceiling, and outside the wind worried at the shutters with its small persistent valley voice — the tame wind, the safe wind, the wind that had never in ten years once answered him when he called.

He thought about staying. He turned it over honestly, the way he tried to turn over everything these days, since honesty was the only penance he trusted. He could burn the map. He could bury it deeper than Sera Voss, deeper than memory, deeper than the frost line. He could go back to the goats and the porridge and the turned earth, and maybe — *maybe* — Hale would report that the courier had died alone in the valley, cargo unfound, and the trail would die, and the crown would turn its attention to some other wound in the world.

Maybe.

But Emon had stopped at the woodshed corner. Emon had stood over the grave for a long time, lips moving, and whatever the boy had tasted there, he had walked away with it folded into his grey sleeve. Residual magic, Hale might call it — the Regent's kiss lingering in dead flesh. Proof enough that the courier had come here. Proof enough to bring them back with spades.

And when they dug, and found her, and found that her hands were empty — they would come to the cottage. And they would not be patient twice.

And beyond that, quieter and worse, in the cellar of him where he kept the truths he rationed: *she said my true name.* A dying stranger, a stranger of the outer world, and she had known Gideon Dunmore — not the goat-keeper of the nameless valley, but the man he had been before, the name he had buried with everything else. Which meant the world beyond the Veil remembered him too. Which meant exile had never been erasure. It had only been waiting.

He looked at the map a long time. The candle burned down another inch. The wind worried the shutters.

"All right," he said aloud, to the room, to the map, to the dead woman under the fieldstone behind the woodshed. "All right."

He spent the rest of the night working, because work was the one prayer he still knew. He salted meat and packed it. He filled the waterskins, oiled his boots, sharpened the short-handled billhook he meant to carry in place of a sword, since he'd never owned a sword and wouldn't trust his hands with one. Near dawn he wrote — with difficulty, his lettering rusty as a gate hinge — instructions for the care of twenty-three goats, and addressed them to no one, and left the paper weighted under the salt box where any neighbor passing might find it, knowing full well that no neighbor would, that the nearest farm was two days' walk and that he had spoken to another human being, before Sera Voss, perhaps forty times in ten years.

The valley had made him ordinary. It had also made him alone, and he was only now, packing bread into a sack by grey morning light, admitting how thoroughly those two things had been the same.

At sunrise he stood in the yard. The goats crowded the fence, sensing departure the way they'd sensed arrival, and Mottle — the old white-faced nanny who had been his shadow since her first winter — pushed her blunt wet nose against his palm and breathed at him.

"I'll come back," he told her, and heard the lie in it, and scratched her ears anyway. "Or I won't. Either way you'll eat better than I ever did."

Then he shouldered the pack, with the map against his spine like a second set of shoulder blades, and walked out of the yard without looking at the woodshed, because he knew if he looked he would stop.

The valley road ran north. Mist lay on the fells like a sheet drawn up to the chin. And somewhere ahead of him, over the pass, two grey figures were widening their search along the very corridor he would have to walk — and somewhere beyond them, past the Veil's edge where the map promised the world would come flooding back into him like blood into a numb limb, the storm waited. Ten years caged. Ten years quiet.

He could feel it already, faint as a struck bell heard through a wall. Not ringing yet. Listening.

Gideon pulled his collar up against the tame wind, and started north, and did not let himself run, because running was the beginning of the other thing, and he was not ready to be that man again.

Not yet. But soon. Whether he wanted it or not.
