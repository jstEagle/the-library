## 5. Crossing the Veil

Gideon buried Sera Voss at dawn, in the thin stony soil above the goat pen where nothing grew well enough to be disturbed. He dug the grave himself with a spade whose handle he had worn smooth over ten years, and he did not use magic to soften the ground, though his hands ached to try and the earth fought him like something with teeth. Some things deserved the cost of effort. He could not have said why this was one of them, only that it felt true in the same wordless way the tremor in his fingers felt true — a knowledge that lived below language.

He marked the grave with a river stone because there was no wood to spare and no carver within a hundred miles, and he scratched no name into it. Names were dangerous now. The grey-robed men had asked for names yesterday, standing at his gate with their faces like folded paper, and he had lied so badly that the lie still rang in his ears like a cracked bell. *Never seen her. No one comes here.* While behind his ribs her bloodstained map lay folded against his heart like a second heartbeat, and every one of them must have heard it. They had looked at him too long before turning away. That long look was a debt, and debts of that kind were always collected.

So: no name on the stone. But he spoke her name once into the turned earth, quietly, the way you'd set down something fragile.

"Sera Voss."

The wind took it. The wind here took everything eventually — words, footprints, grief. That was the whole design of the valley. It forgot so its inhabitants could too.

He went back inside the cottage and packed.

It was not much of a ritual, because he owned almost nothing: two shirts, a wool coat gone threadbare at the elbows, a knife, flint, three days of hard cheese and bread wrapped in cloth, a clay jar of goat's milk that would sour by tomorrow and which he carried anyway out of some stubborn habit of provision. The map he kept separate, wrapped in oilcloth, tucked into his shirt. He had spent the night — after burying her, after lying to the grey robes, after sitting until the candle drowned itself in its own wax — studying it by memory rather than firelight, because firelight through parchment might show a glow to anyone watching from the dark.

He knew it now the way he knew the path to the river. The valley's edge lay half a day's walk northeast, past the high summer pasture, up through the scree field where the goats wouldn't go, to a saddle between two bald shoulders of mountain. The map called it nothing at all. There was simply a line drawn across the pass in ink that had dried brown, like old blood, like hers, and beneath the line four words in a hand that had pressed hard enough to tear the page:

*Step through. Don't stop.*

Don't stop. As if whoever drew it knew what waited on the other side. As if they knew what stopping would cost.

* * *

He left the gate open.

That was the only farewell he allowed himself, and even that felt like vanity. Ten years of closing that gate against the wind, latching it each night with numb fingers while the valley hummed its blank song around him, and now he walked away and left it swinging, and the goats watched him go with their flat yellow patience as if to say: we always knew you were borrowed.

The high pasture took him by midmorning. The grass up here grew silver-headed and knee-deep, and it moved in long slow waves without any wind he could feel, as though the valley were breathing in its sleep. He had shepherded these slopes for a decade and never once thought of them as beautiful. Now, knowing he would not see them again — or worse, knowing he might, having become the kind of man who came back changed — he found he couldn't look away. Beauty was easier to bear at a distance. Up close it demanded things.

His hands were shaking again.

Not the small tremor of age or cold. This was the other thing, the thing he'd spent ten years teaching his body to disguise as a farmer's palsy, the fine rapid flutter in his fingertips that meant the storm was dreaming inside him. In the valley it stayed asleep. Magic could not follow him here; that was the mercy of his exile, the whole point of the place, a country with a hole cut out of it exactly his shape. Whatever power had been sewn into his blood at birth — whatever had answered him when he called, too eagerly, too often, until the answering became the calling became the ruin — could not reach him across the valley's threshold. Here he was only flesh. Only tired. Only ordinary.

But the map was pulling him toward the edge of the hole, and already, half a day out, the thing inside him had begun to stir like a dog hearing footsteps at a door.

*You're not going back,* he told it. *I'm going. You stay.*

It didn't answer. It never answered. It only waited, patient as weather.

He ate at noon on a flat stone overlooking the scree field, and found he had no appetite, and ate anyway, chewing mechanically while he stared at the mountains ahead. Two peaks, grey and bald, with a saddle of broken rock between them. From here it looked like nothing. A low place in a high wall. He had grazed animals near it in his first summer, before he learned the flock refused to go there, before he noticed that the birds circled but never landed, before he understood that everyone in the valley gave the northeast pass the wide, unthinking deference people give a grave.

Nobody in the valley spoke about what lay beyond. Not because it was forbidden. Because it was uninteresting. That was the deepest strangeness of the place, the one that had taken him years to articulate even to himself: the valley did not forbid the outside world. It made the outside world *dull*. Ask a neighbor about the cities beyond the mountains and watch their eyes slide off the question like rain off slate. They knew there was more, the way a caged bird knows there is sky, and the knowledge sat in them without weight, without longing. The Veil did not just keep magic out. It kept want out.

Which meant, Gideon realized, setting down the cheese he couldn't finish, that once he crossed, everything he had failed to want for ten years would come due at once. All of it. Every grief he'd set down at the border would be handed back to him with interest.

*Step through. Don't stop.*

He stood, brushed the crumbs from his coat, and started up the scree.

* * *

The climb took the rest of the day. The scree field was a thousand feet of loose grey stone that shifted and sighed under his boots, each step forward buying an inch and surrendering most of it back, so that climbing it felt less like ascent than argument. Twice he dislodged slides that roared down behind him and echoed strangely — the sound falling flat, cut short, as if the mountain swallowed its own voice. By late afternoon the light had gone the color of weak tea, and the wind had died completely, and the silence up there was so complete he could hear his own pulse in his ears, slow and stubborn.

At the top, the saddle.

It was narrower than it had looked from below — maybe thirty paces of broken flagstone between the two peaks, walled on either side by the sheer drop into cloud. And along its middle ran the line.

He saw it before he understood what he was seeing: a seam in the world, faint as a scratch on glass, running across the pass from wall to wall. Not painted. Not carved. Simply *there*, visible only when the light caught it, the way a spider's silk is invisible until the sun finds it. On the near side of the seam, the air smelled of stone and dry grass and goat. On the far side — he could smell it from ten paces, could feel it on his face like warmth from an open oven — the air smelled of rain.

Rain. Real rain, storm rain, ozone and wet iron and green things growing fast. Ten years since he had smelled it. His body recognized it before his mind did; his mouth flooded, his knees went soft, and he had to stand very still with his hand flat against the cold flagstone until the shaking stopped.

Because it wasn't going to stop being shaking anymore. He understood that now. Everything past that line was shaking.

"Tell the king his father—"

Sera Voss's last sentence hung in the air of the saddle with him, unfinished, and he knew — he had known since the moment she died with her eyes still fixed on his — that the rest of that sentence existed only on the other side of this line. Whatever she had carried, whatever dying king's business had sent a courier bleeding smoke through the one gap in the world where no one should have been able to find her, all of it waited beyond the seam, along with the storm, and his brother's face, and every other thing he had buried at this altitude in his heart if not in the ground.

He almost turned around.

Let it be honest: he almost turned around twice. Once at the first step, when his boot hovered over the seam and every animal instinct in him screamed the way the goats screamed at the scree field's edge. And once halfway, when he had crossed and the world had not yet changed and he thought, wildly, *it's a trick, it's nothing, I can go back and be ordinary forever, ordinary is not so bad, ordinary kept me alive—*

But that was the lie, wasn't it. Ordinary hadn't kept him alive. Ordinary had been the slowest form of the same death. Ten years of numbness was not survival; it was a sentence, commuted daily.

He stepped through.

* * *

Later he would try to describe it, and fail, because there were no words arranged in any order that held. The closest he ever managed — to Wren, much later, when she asked with the particular hunger of someone who collects what others remember — was this: *"It was like being underwater for ten years and then surfacing all at once."*

Sound came first. The valley had been silent in a way he'd stopped hearing, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and beyond the Veil the world roared — wind in the high crags, water somewhere far below, the vast slow conversation of a living mountain range — and the roar hit him like a wave and knocked him to one knee on the far side of the line, gasping.

Then color. The clouds overhead were not the pale washed wool of valley skies but bruised and layered, violet under grey under gold, stacked and moving, *going somewhere*. The rock under his hands was banded red and black. Even the air had color, a shimmer at the edges of things, as if the world had been holding its breath for a decade and had finally exhaled.

Then the wind reached him properly, and with it, everything else.

Power moved through the wind the way current moves through water. He felt it find him the instant he crossed — felt it *recognize* him — a great blind attention turning toward the gap in the world's skin where he stood, and then pouring through. Into him. It entered through his palms where they pressed the stone, through his open mouth, through the thin skin of his scars, everywhere at once, and it was not gentle and it was not cruel, it was simply *vast*, and it fit him the way a key fits a lock that has been rusting shut for years: painfully, and then all at once, and then perfectly.

The storm answered his pulse.

That was the truth of it, the truth he had run from, the truth the valley had mercifully muffled: his heartbeat and the sky's electricity were the same rhythm. He felt the pressure building in the clouds above the peaks sync itself to the blood in his wrists. Felt the static lift the hair on his arms. Felt, deep in his chest, the first far-off grumble of thunder that was not thunder yet but *invitation* — the sky leaning close, the way it always had, saying what it had always said:

*Call us. Call us. You know you want to.*

And gods forgive him, he wanted to. Ten years of drought and the first taste of water nearly drowned him with wanting. His hands rose without his permission. Lightning crawled white behind the western peaks like veins in a leaf, and every vein was a nerve ending, and every nerve ending was *his*, and he stood in the saddle of the world with his arms half-raised and tears freezing on his face, terror and homecoming fused into one unbearable emotion with no name in any language —

—and he stopped.

He wrenched his arms down. He clenched his fists against his thighs and bent double, breathing like a man surfacing, and he forced the words out through his teeth, to the sky, to the thing inside him, to himself:

"No. Not today. Today we walk."

The storm sulked. There was no other word for it. The pressure eased by degrees, grudging as a fist unclenching, and the lightning behind the peaks dimmed to a rumor, and Gideon knelt on the far side of the Veil with his heart slamming and laughed — a cracked, awful sound, half sob — because he had forgotten. He had genuinely forgotten how good it felt. And remembering was worse than never having known, because now he understood that he hadn't escaped the storm at all. He'd only been keeping it fed on absence.

He got up. He checked the map with hands that would not fully steady. The route ran north and west from the pass, down out of the mountains toward — according to the faded annotations in Sera's teacherly script — a border town called Ashmarket, three days' walk, where a certain innkeeper owed her a favor and discretion could be bought with good wine. Beyond that, the capital. The king. The unfinished sentence.

Three days. He could do three days. He had survived ten years; he could survive anything, provided it moved.

He shouldered his pack and took the first step down the far slope, and that was when he saw them: boot prints in the soft volcanic ash near the seam, hours old at most, half-erased by wind. Someone had crossed before him. Recently. Someone small and light, judging by the stride — someone who had stood exactly where he was standing, looking back the way he'd been looking, before walking on.

And further down the slope, half-hidden in the shadow of a boulder, glinting dully in the storm-light: a scrap of metal. He crouched, wary, and picked it up.

A lockpick. Well-used, oiled, dropped or lost or *left*.

Gideon stood a long moment, the little tool cold in his palm, scanning the grey slopes below until his eyes ached. The wind moved through the grass. Nothing else moved.

Someone was following him into the world he'd sworn he'd never return to — and whoever they were, they had crossed the Veil first.
