## 6. The Thief on the Road

Wren had stolen her first purse at six years old, and in the eleven years since she had learned one truth above all others: the world tells you everything you need to know about a mark before he ever opens his mouth. The way a man carries his coin says whether he's rich or wants to seem rich. The way he wears his sword says whether he's killed or merely bought the blade. The way his eyes move through a crowd says whether he belongs to it.

The tall grey-eyed man who came up the pilgrim road out of the eastern hills belonged nowhere at all.

She marked him at the tollbridge below Millhollow, where the road narrowed between a drystone wall and the drop to the river, because that was where she did most of her best work — choke points made honest men careless. He walked like a man carrying something heavier than his pack, shoulders set against an invisible wind, and he flinched when the bridge timbers groaned under his boots. Not the flinch of a coward. The flinch of a man who had heard things groan before and knew what came next.

*Storm-blood*, she thought idly. Or mad. Out here they often looked the same.

He was not dressed like a pilgrim — no votive ribbons, no saint's medallion worn shiny from thumbing — but he carried himself with that particular hollow piety pilgrims wore, the look of men walking toward something because standing still had become unbearable. His coat was good wool gone grey with road dust, mended at the elbow with neat, careful stitches. Mended by his own hand, then. No wife. No servant. He paid the toll with exact copper and thanked the collector, which was either genuine manners or excellent camouflage, and Wren had learned not to bet against camouflage in anyone.

But it was his hands that decided her.

They were wrong for a farmer, wrong for a clerk, wrong for anything. They stayed half-curled at his sides like talons, and every so often — when thunder muttered somewhere over the western hills, though the sky was clean blue above them — the fingers would twitch, all five at once, as if plucking strings only he could feel. She had seen hands do that once before, in Ashmarket, on a weather-worker who drank herself blind trying to still them. Magic caged too long didn't leave a person. It just went underground and gnawed.

*Not your business,* said the sensible voice in her head, Sera's voice, really, because everything wise in her was borrowed from the dead woman now. *Take his purse, take his pride if he's carrying anything interesting, and be three fields away by dusk.*

That was the plan. That had always been the plan. Eleven years of thieving had taught her that curiosity was the one vice that got thieves hanged faster than greed, because greed took what it came for and left, while curiosity lingered, asked questions, followed strangers down dark roads.

She lifted his pack anyway.

It was almost embarrassingly easy. He stopped at the shrine by the crossroads past the bridge — a leaning post hung with ribbons and tiny carved birds, offerings for safe travel — and something in his face cracked open, some grief too large for a roadside shrine, and while he stood there staring at a wooden wren of all things with an expression like a man reading his own name on a gravestone, she drifted past in the little knot of pilgrims, her knife-edge slipping the buckle, her fingers finding the strap, and his pack rode off her shoulder light as a stolen thought.

She was a quarter mile gone before she let herself look inside.

Purse first: modest coin, a tin whistle, a whetstone. A spare shirt smelling of woodsmoke and goat. A man who kept goats, then, or had kept them until very recently, which meant he'd come out of somewhere quiet and small and far, which meant whatever put him on this road was recent and enormous. Her fingers found the oilcloth packet sewn flat against the back panel — the hiding place of a man who expected to be robbed, which was amusing, except she'd already robbed him — and unwrapped it with the care of someone defusing a trap.

The map unfolded into her hands, and the world went narrow and ringing.

She knew that hand.

She knew the way the *f*s looped too high, like the writer was always reaching for something overhead. She knew the little chevron marks used for hills instead of the proper cartographer's symbols, a habit Sera Voss had never broken from her surveying days. She knew the ink-blot at the margin shaped like a lopsided heart where the pen had rested too long while its owner thought about something else. Wren had learned her letters tracing that hand, sitting cross-legged on warehouse rooftops and tavern benches while Sera tapped the page and said, *Again, little bird, slower this time, a map is a promise the land makes you, learn to read promises.*

Sera Voss, who had left her in Ashmarket eight months ago with a kiss on the forehead and instructions to lie low and a joke about being back before the frost.

Who had never come back.

Wren stood in the middle of the pilgrim road with the map open in her hands and the crowd parting around her like water around a stone, and she read it the way Sera had taught her, promises first. It showed the eastern hill country, the borderlands, and there — drawn in that looping hand — a valley with no name, marked with a symbol she had never seen Sera use before: a circle bisected by a line. A door, closed. And around the valley's edges, in tiny cramped script she had to hold the page to the sun to read, annotations in a cipher she recognized because she'd watched Sera invent it, laughing, saying *this is for the thing I'm doing next, little bird, and if I do it right you'll never need to know.*

The frost had come. The frost had gone. Sera hadn't come back, and here was her cipher, her secret map, riding in the pack of a goatherd who flinched at thunder.

Wren did not cry. She had trained the crying out of herself years ago, on the nights when crying would have been fatal, and besides — the feeling in her chest wasn't grief exactly, or not only. It was guilt, and guilt was worse, because grief you could only carry but guilt you could *do something about*. Eight months ago Sera had kissed her forehead and said lie low, and Wren had lied low, gloriously low, profitably low, while her mentor walked into the eastern hills alone toward a closed door, and now Sera's last work was in the hands of a stranger who didn't even know what he was carrying.

Or maybe he did. Maybe he was the reason she hadn't come back.

The thought arrived cold and stayed. Wren folded the map along Sera's creases, wrapped the oilcloth with reverent precision, and made the second-worst decision of her life, right after the first time she'd ever followed Sera anywhere.

She was going to follow him.

* * *

Shadowing a man across open country should have been beneath her. Wren could ghost a market at noon, could lift a signet ring off a sleeping magistrate's finger, could vanish from a room that had only one door and her in it. One slow marksman on an empty road was nothing.

Except he kept *stopping*.

Twice before midday he turned full circle without warning, eyes sweeping the hedgerows, and both times Wren flattened herself behind a wall with her heart hammering and wondered how he'd marked her. He shouldn't have. She moved when the wind moved, breathed when the birds sang, wore browns and dust-grey and the patient stillness of her trade. But the third time it happened, near a ford where he knelt to drink, she realized he wasn't looking for a tail at all.

He was listening.

He stood knee-deep in the shallows, cupped water in his wrong hands, and tilted his head westward the way a deer does, and far off — so far it might have been imagination — thunder answered. Not storm-thunder. There wasn't a cloud within ten miles. This was a single low roll, directionless, like something vast turning over in its sleep, and the man at the ford closed his eyes and shuddered, and Wren, pressed into the reeds thirty feet away, felt the hair rise on her arms and understood two things at once.

The first was that the twitching hands and the thunder-listening were the same thing. The storm called to him, or he to it, across distances that made no sense, and the call hurt him, and he was enduring it the way men endured a wound they couldn't reach.

The second was that Sera had gone looking for this man. Of all the people in all the world, her mentor's last map led to a storm-caller walking out of a nameless valley, and Sera Voss had never in her life done anything without seven reasons stacked like coins.

*I need to know what you are,* Wren thought at his broad, dusty back as he climbed the far bank. *And I need to know what you cost her.*

So she followed, and the following became a strange education, because the more she watched him the less the picture cohered. Thieves, assassins, inquisitors — the dangerous ones moved through the world like knives through cloth, economical, leaving no snags. This man snagged on everything. He gave his bread crust to a beggar child and then looked stricken, as if he'd remembered too late that beggars reported to beggar-networks and kindness left tracks. He argued with a carter over a fare, won, and then paid the difference anyway out of visible self-disgust. When a rider clattered past too fast and spooked the pilgrims, his hand went to a sword that wasn't there — old soldier, then, or something worse — and Wren revised her ledger of him again: dangerous, kind, haunted, and completely unpracticed at being any of those things in public anymore. Ten years out of the world, at least, worn into him like a groove.

Like exile.

By late afternoon she had worked within twenty feet of him, close enough to hear him muttering to himself in a voice pitched for goats rather than people, and that was when the bells started.

Ashmarket rang its dusk bell every evening — she'd grown up inside that sound, the great cracked bronze voice of the Thieves' Bell in the old customs tower, rung once at dusk since before anyone remembered why, a sound so constant it became silence. Except tonight the bell came early, and wrong, and it wasn't one bell.

Every bell in the town ahead was ringing at once.

Gideon — she'd pried the name from the toll-collector's gossip, *the grey-eyed fellow, said he came from the east valleys* — stopped dead in the road. Ahead, the smoke of Ashmarket rose thin and ordinary against the reddening sky, but the bells kept on, all of them, no pattern to it, no fire-alarm rhythm, no festival joy. Just clamor. And threading through the clamor, faint on the evening air, came a sound Wren knew better than she knew her own heartbeat: the flat, nasal sing-song of proclamation-criers, the kind the Crown's men used when they wanted a name known in every ear by morning.

Gideon turned around. Full circle, slowly, the way he'd done all day, but this time his eyes were working properly, sharp and afraid, and Wren dropped behind the wayside wall and lay flat among the nettles with her pulse in her ears.

When she dared look again, he had taken something from his coat. A scrap of paper, unfolded with hands that had begun, very slightly, to shake. Even at twenty feet she recognized the shape of the gesture — she'd seen fugitives do it in dockside alleys, checking the one thing that proved they existed — and she felt her guilt twist another ratchet tighter, because the scrap was a corner torn from a larger sheet, and even at twenty feet she'd have sworn the looping *f*s on it belonged to Sera.

He read it, folded it away, and looked east — back the way he'd come, back toward the nameless valley, the closed door — for a long, long moment. Then he looked at Ashmarket, smoking and bell-clamorous in the dusk, the only town of any size between him and wherever he was going, and Wren watched a man lose an argument with himself and start walking toward trouble because there was no other road.

The criers' song resolved as she ghosted after him, close now, closer than was safe, because the words mattered:

*"—by order of the Regency, sought for questioning in the death of a royal courier: a man answering to no registered name, travelling east from the borderlands, tall, grey-eyed, bearing wounds of unnatural origin. All loyal subjects—"*

A courier. *The* courier. Dead, and announced dead, and the announcement had beaten Gideon to Ashmarket by hours, which meant whoever ran the hunt wasn't chasing rumors. They were running ahead of him, closing doors before he reached them.

*Sera.* The word wasn't a thought anymore; it was a wound reopening. Sera Voss, royal courier, dead by unnatural means, and her final map stitched into the pack of the man they wanted for it.

Wren lay in the nettles at the roadside and let herself feel all of it at once — the grief, hot and useless; the fear, cold and useful; and underneath, banked like a coal, the guilt, which was neither, because guilt was fuel. She had stayed low when Sera told her to. She would never stay low again.

Ahead, unnoticed, the grey-eyed man walked into the gathering dark toward a city full of bells, carrying a dead woman's map and a dead woman's secrets and no idea at all that the Regency's inquisitors had already seeded Ashmarket's gates and gutters with grey robes and watching eyes.

Wren rose out of the nettles, shook them from her hem, and followed him in.

Somebody had to keep Sera's promise alive, and it clearly wasn't going to be him.
