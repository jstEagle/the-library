## 7. Ashmarket

Wren had been following the storm-caller for three days, and in three days he had done nothing to deserve her.

He walked like a man who had never once looked over his shoulder, which either meant courage or a lifetime of not being hunted, and she suspected the latter. He stopped at shrines out of habit rather than devotion. He paid for bread with coins counted twice. In the evenings he sat with his back to walls that were too low and doors that were too thin, and if he noticed any of this, he never corrected it. Sera's map rode against his ribs in an oilcloth wrap, and he touched it the way a man touches a wound he has decided not to look at — absently, often, without understanding.

*You're going to get us both killed,* she thought, watching him from the shadow of a grain-wain as he crossed into Ashmarket at dusk. *And worse, you're going to get her map killed.*

The town straddled the border like a drunk leaning on a fence. On one side of the high street, the slate roofs and grey wool of the northern provinces; on the other, the red tile and loud colors of the marches. The river ran through it shallow and slow, carrying leaves from two kingdoms at once, and the market square between them smelled of tallow, wet stone, frying onions, and — beneath it all, faint but unmistakable to Wren's trained nose — burnt sugar and cold iron.

Ozone and binding-metal. The smell of the Veil's other side doing its work.

Inquisitors were in town.

She'd known since morning, when a grey-robed man had paused outside the ferryman's hut and run one white finger along the doorframe, tasting whatever lingered there the way a sommelier tastes a cork. There were four of them, maybe five, moving through Ashmarket with the unhurried patience of men setting a snare rather than springing one. They weren't searching for anything. They were waiting for something to walk into their net.

And here came Gideon Dunmore, walking straight toward the center of it, because the map said the road south went through the square and the storm-caller had apparently never heard of taking another road.

Wren closed her eyes for half a breath. *Sera.* Her mentor's voice lived in her head still, dry as old paper: *We don't save people because they deserve it, little thief. We save them because someone will grieve otherwise, and grief is heavy, and we are already carrying enough.*

*I'm not saving him,* she told the ghost. *I'm saving the map.*

It was a lie. She was getting good at telling lies to Sera's memory; it was the only conversation they had left.

* * *

She worked the crowd the way water works a crack — patient, finding every seam. Three years on the roads after Sera died had taught her the grammar of markets: which stalls had sightlines, which beggars sold information by the whisper, which children ran errands for coppers and remembered faces. She bought a fish pie from a woman with red hands and asked, idly, whether the grey brothers had eaten yet.

"Two of 'em took rooms at the Salt Cellar," the pie-seller said, wrapping the pastry in cloth. "Third one's been standing by the well since noon. Doesn't drink. Doesn't talk. Just stands there like he's listening for something under the ground."

Listening. Yes. That was how they hunted — not with eyes but with that pale, hungry attention that tasted the residue of magic the way her own fingers tasted memories. And what was there in Ashmarket for them to taste? A courier's blood spilled ten days' ride north. A trail of borrowed horses. And now, walking into the square with his hood down like an honest farmer: a man whose blood was a lightning rod, whose skin still hummed with ten years of caged storm, no matter how far he'd buried it.

Gideon reached the well.

The grey-robed man turned his head — slowly, slowly, like a weathervane finding wind — and even across forty feet of crowded market, Wren saw the moment he caught the flavor. His nostrils flared. His hand rose, not to point, just to rest against his own chest, over the sigil stitched there: a circle bisected by a line, the mark of the Regent's questioners.

*Oh, you absolute fool.*

She was already moving. Not toward Gideon — toward the third inquisitor, the one standing at the mouth of the cooper's lane, the one Gideon hadn't seen because Gideon didn't know how to look. Two of them, then. One by the well, one in the lane behind, and somewhere a fourth she hadn't found yet. A closing net, and the storm-caller strolling into its center with pie crumbs on his coat.

Wren did what she did best. She stole something.

Not from Gideon. From the crowd. She slipped past a carter arguing about a broken axle and lifted — gently, delicately, the way you lift a hairpin — the edge of his attention. A touch of her gift against the surface of his mind, nothing taken, everything redirected: suddenly the carter's ox *was* loose, or would be in three heartbeats, and the shout went up, and the market square dissolved into exactly the kind of chaos Wren needed. Cattle don't care about borders or kings. People scattered before six hundred pounds of indignant beef.

Gideon didn't scatter. Of course he didn't. He stood there like a post while the crowd broke around him, and the inquisitor by the well began to move, robes whispering over cobblestones, and the one in the lane stepped out behind him with something long and pale already in hand — not a blade, worse than a blade, a hollow needle of grey glass that drank light instead of reflecting it.

A memory-needle. Wren had seen what those left behind. Empty houses where people used to live.

There was no clever way out. She'd spent three days building clever ways and none of them fit this shape of trouble. So she spent the only coin she carried that mattered.

Her gift had rules, and the first rule was that stolen things could be traded. A memory held in a vial of glass-and-wax, pressed to the temple, given willingly — that was currency. Merchants of the hidden archive took them. Smugglers took them. And inquisitors — inquisitors took them most eagerly of all, because a questioner's greatest hunger wasn't answers. It was *material*. Memories of the Veil, of the old binding, of the time before the crown grew teeth — those were worth more than gold in the grey cloisters, and Wren had spent three years collecting them precisely so she would never have to do what she was about to do.

She drew the vial from the lining of her sleeve. Small as a fingerbone, stoppered with black wax. Inside it swam a sliver of amber light, and inside the light: the harbor at Vell, the summer she was nine, her mother's laugh rising off the water, the taste of salt and honey cake.

One of the first things she'd ever stolen. Stolen from herself, kept all these years because some things you steal so no one else can take them.

*Sera,* she thought, uncorking the wax with her thumb. *I hope wherever you are, you're not watching.*

Then she shouted, "Fire!" at the top of her lungs, pointed at the tannery row, and threw the vial at the inquisitor's feet.

* * *

Here is what the merchants of memory never tell you: a released memory doesn't simply vanish. It *blooms*. All that stored living, all that salt and honey and a mother's voice, bursts outward in a sphere of pure experience, and anyone caught inside it lives the memory whole — drowning in it, believing it, lost in one bright second of someone else's life.

The sphere caught both inquisitors and half the fleeing market.

For eleven seconds, Ashmarket stood utterly still. The carter's ox wandered free. A child mid-scream forgot to finish. The inquisitor by the well dropped to his knees with tears running down his face, laughing, laughing, laughing — because it was Wren's mother's laugh, and it was a beautiful sound, and he would never in his life hear anything so beautiful again. The one with the needle swayed like a man in high grass, honey cake dissolving sweet on a tongue that wasn't his.

Eleven seconds. It cost her nine years of hoarded sunlight, and it bought her eleven seconds, and she made them count.

She crossed the square at a dead sprint, hit Gideon at the shoulder — he grunted, staggered, nearly went down — hauled him around by the collar, and got them both into the cooper's lane before the bloom collapsed and the world remembered itself.

"What—" Gideon started.

"Run first," Wren gasped. "Questions after. It's a rule."

They ran. Behind them the square woke up screaming in three different registers — the crowd confused, the ox triumphant, and the inquisitors rising from their knees with the terrible calm of men who had just felt joy for the first time in decades and knew exactly whom to thank for it.

* * *

They didn't stop until the rooftops of Ashmarket were a smudge of slate behind them and the road had narrowed to a footpath along the river. Then Gideon stopped, planted himself like a fencepost, and turned on her with the expression of a man who had rehearsed being angry and couldn't quite find the script.

"You've been following me," he said.

"Three days." She bent, hands on knees, catching her breath. "You walk very loudly for such a quiet man."

"Those men — the grey ones—"

"Inquisitors. The Regent's hounds. They hunt magic the way you hunt escaped goats, except they never stop, and they take pieces of you home in jars." She straightened. "You walked into their trap with your hood down."

"I didn't see them."

"No. You really didn't." Something between pity and exasperation climbed up her throat. "That's the problem, storm-caller. You don't see anything. You've spent so long in your valley learning to be nobody that you've forgotten how to be hunted. Well. Welcome back to the world. It missed you."

His jaw tightened. Up close, in the last copper light, she could see the tremor in his right hand — fine as a plucked string, constant as a pulse. He saw her seeing it and curled the hand shut.

"Who are you?" he asked. "Really."

"Wren."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer you've earned." She held up the empty vial, letting the dying light pass through the glass. "But here's what you should know, since you seem determined to die stupid: I knew Sera Voss. She taught me my trade. That map in your shirt — I'd know her handwriting anywhere, and I have been following you since the ford because I intend to finish what she started. Whether you're competent enough to come along is, frankly, negotiable."

Something moved behind his eyes — grief, maybe, or its numb cousin. "You knew her."

"She pulled me out of a gutter in Vell when I was thirteen and taught me that theft could be a moral act. She talked about a valley sometimes. A place magic couldn't reach. Said there was a man there who'd buried something too big for the grave." Wren tilted her head. "I used to think she was talking about a treasure."

"And instead?"

"Instead it's you, and you can't even spot an ambush in an open market."

For a moment she thought he'd be angry. Instead he laughed — short, startled, rusty, like a hinge unused for years — and rubbed a hand over his face. "Fair," he said. "Gods. Fair enough." He looked at the empty vial again, and his voice changed, gentler. "What did it cost you? Back there. Whatever you threw — that wasn't alchemy. That was you."

Clever man. Wren looked away, down the darkening river. "A memory. Mine. Given freely, which is the only way they trade." She shrugged, making it small. "Harbor town. Summer. Not important."

"You're lying."

"Of course I'm lying." She smiled, and let him see that it was a lie, which was its own strange honesty. "But it's a good lie. Well-built. Load-bearing. Leave it alone."

He studied her for a long moment — and she watched him decide something, watched the wariness settle into something harder and more useful. Recognition, maybe. Two people who dealt in concealment spotting each other's tools.

"You gave up something real," he said quietly, "to save a stranger who's been nothing but a liability since the ford. Why?"

"Because Sera died getting that map to you, and I don't waste her purchases." She tucked the empty vial away, and found her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when the truth got close. "And because inquisitors don't quit, storm-caller. They'll have our scent now, yours especially. Every town between here and wherever that map leads is going to be a version of tonight, and next time I might not have anything left to spend." She started walking, south, into the dark. "So here's the arrangement. I keep you alive. You carry the map. We find what Sera died pointing at. And neither of us asks the other questions we're not prepared to answer."

Gideon fell into step beside her. For a while there was only the river muttering alongside, and the first stars coming out over the border hills, sharp as needle-points.

"The well," he said finally. "The third one. He turned toward me before I'd said a word. How did he know?"

Wren's stomach tightened. She'd been turning that over herself for three miles, and she didn't like the answer.

"They weren't hunting a rumor," she said. "Traps like that take planning. Warrants. Someone told them a storm-caller was crossing the Veil, and told them where he'd surface." She glanced sideways at him. "Which means, Gideon Dunmore, that somebody knows exactly who you are. And they knew it before you ever set foot in Ashmarket."

Ahead, the road bent south toward the low country, and somewhere past the hills, a bell was ringing — one long, unbroken note, the kind towns rang for a death or a warning.

Neither of them said which it was.
