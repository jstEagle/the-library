## 8. The King Who Is Not Alone

Aldric woke to sunlight he did not trust.

It came through the east windows of his bedchamber in long amber bars, lying across the coverlet like bars of another kind, and it told him the hour was past mid-morning — which was wrong, because the last thing he remembered was midnight. He remembered the bell of Vespers. He remembered standing at the window with a cup of watered wine going cold in his hand, watching frost climb the glass, thinking about nothing in particular, or trying to. And then —

Nothing. A seam cut clean through the cloth of the night, and on the other side of it: morning, sunlight, and ink.

He held up his hands.

The right one was stained to the second knuckle, black drying in the creases of his fingers, in the whorls of his thumb, a crow's feather of it caught along the edge of his palm where a pen would have rested. The left bore only a single smudge, high on the wrist, as though he had pushed back a sleeve to keep it clean. Someone who had written for a long time. Someone who had written carefully.

Aldric did not remember writing anything.

He sat up slowly, the way one sits up in a boat that has begun to rock, and listened to the room. The fire had been fed and banked; someone had come and gone while he slept, or while whatever he had done instead of sleeping had happened. His boots stood by the hearth, cleaned. His ceremonial doublet hung on its stand, brushed. Everything in its place. Everything attended to. The orderliness of it should have comforted him, and instead it made the skin of his arms prickle, because he could not account for any of it, and there was no one in the world — no one he knew — who moved through these rooms without waking him.

Except the crown.

*Good morning,* said the voice that lived behind his eyes. It did not use his ears. It never used his ears. It arrived the way warmth arrives into a cold hand, or the way a memory arrives — already inside before he noticed the door opening. *You worked late.*

"I don't remember working."

*That is not the same as not having worked.* The voice was patient. It was always patient. In four years it had never once raised itself, never hurried, never begged. It spoke to him the way a tutor speaks to a promising student, or the way a father speaks to a son he is proud of — and that comparison, Aldric thought, was precisely the trap of it, because he had lost his real father at nine years old and the crown had been there the very next morning, warm against his brow at the coronation, whispering *I have you now. I will carry what you cannot.* *The realm required signatures. You provided them.*

"Signatures." Aldric swung his legs out of bed. The stone floor was cold through his stockings. "What did I sign?"

*Decrees. Ordinances. The ordinary maintenance of a kingdom.* A pause, brief as a blink. *There were petitions outstanding from the harvest courts. Delays are how kingdoms rot.*

"There were three petitions," Aldric said. "I remember reading them. I hadn't decided."

*I decided. That is what I am for.*

He crossed to the washstand and poured water from the ewer into the basin, and watched his own reflection come apart and reassemble in the disturbed surface. Twenty-three years old. His mother's dark hair, his father's long jaw. Eyes that had once been described — by whom? By someone, once, some nurse or courtier whose name slid away from him when he reached for it — as *honest*. He looked at those eyes now and could not find the word. They looked tired in a way sleep did not touch.

He scrubbed at the ink. It clung. He scrubbed again, harder, until his knuckles went pink, and the black came away in grey clouds that spiraled down the basin's throat, and still, when he spread his fingers under the light, faint shadows remained in the lines of his hands, like stains from handling an old book. Like something soaked in deeper than skin.

*You press too hard when you write,* the crown observed. *You always have. Your father did the same. I have told you this.*

"You've told me things about my father every day since I was nine years old," Aldric said quietly. "And yet I can't recall the sound of his voice anymore."

Silence. Not offended silence — the crown did not take offense; that was one of its most unnerving qualities — but the silence of a thing choosing which truth to offer next.

*Grief wears memory smooth,* it said at last, gently. *Time does the rest. I keep for you what time would take. Would you rather I did not?*

He would have answered — he did not know what he would have answered, yes or no or the screaming he kept folded up small beneath his breastbone — but the door opened then, and Chamberlain Oves entered with the day's schedule, and Aldric became, in the space of a breath, the king.

* * *

Being the king was easier than being Aldric. He had learned that early. The king had a body that performed: it rose when heralds announced petitioners, inclined at the correct angle toward the correct rank, ate sparingly at state dinners, laughed at Lord Cassel's jokes with exactly the duration Lord Cassel expected. The king had a face. Aldric sometimes felt he watched that face from somewhere slightly behind it, the way one watches an actor from the shadowed wings — close enough to see the sweat, too far to feel the heat of the lamps.

Oves walked beside him through the gallery toward the council chamber, reading from a leather folio in his dry, unhurried voice. "The renewal rite preparations, majesty — the Patriarch requests confirmation of the solstice date. The masons have finished the scaffold for the eastern tribune. And—" the chamberlain hesitated, a hesitation so slight most people would have missed it, "—there is the matter of the Warden of the Western Marches."

"What matter?"

"He submitted his resignation yesterday evening. By letter." Oves handed the folio over. "It is unusual."

It was more than unusual. Wardens did not resign. Wardens died at their posts or were dismissed with scandal, but resignation was a door the office did not have. Aldric unfolded the letter as he walked, and read it twice, and felt the corridor tilt very slightly around him.

The handwriting was his own.

Not similar. Not a fair imitation. His own — the same forward lean, the same cramped ascenders, the same habit of pressing too hard that left grooves he could feel with a fingertip, ridges in the paper like a scar. Dated yesterday evening. Signed *Aldric Rennan, fourth of that name, King of Caelmor*, with the signature he had practiced a thousand times as a bored boy during interminable lessons.

*I do not accept this resignation,* he began to say, and stopped, because Oves was looking at him with polite expectation, waiting for the decision that kings make and chamberlains record, and because a colder thought had arrived underneath the first:

Had he accepted it already?

Was that what the ink was? Was that what the missing hours were? Had some part of him — the part that wrote at night, the part that pressed too hard — already handled this matter, sealed it, filed it, sent it down the chain of clerks and couriers, while the part of him that woke to sunlight lay dreaming of nothing?

*Majesty?* Oves prompted.

"The Warden has served faithfully for nineteen years," Aldric said. His voice came out level. He was distantly proud of that. "Summon him to court. If he wishes to resign, he will say so to my face, and I will hear his reasons myself."

Something flickered across the chamberlain's face — relief? fear? both braided together? — and was smoothed away by professionalism. "As you wish, majesty. Though I should note the letter was countersigned."

"By whom?"

Oves named the Grand Inquisitor.

The word went through Aldric like a needle finding the gap between two ribs. *Vessia.* Ser Vessia of the Grey Robes, who smelled of cold ash and spoke in questions she already knew the answers to, who knelt to no one but the crown — and who, Aldric realized with a slow, sinking clarity, had never once needed to kneel, because the crown leaned toward her on its own.

*The Marches have been restive,* the crown murmured, in the tone it reserved for explanations that were not explanations. *Inquisitorial oversight is prudent.*

"The Marches have been quiet for six years," Aldric said, under his breath, behind the shield of his own thoughts, where even Oves could not follow.

*Quiet is not loyal. There is a difference. You will understand when you are older.*

"I am twenty-three."

*Your father understood at thirty-one.* The voice softened, wrapped itself around him like a blanket pulled up by careful hands. *You are tired. You slept badly. Let me carry the arithmetic of the morning; you need only walk and be seen. That is half of ruling, Aldric. To be seen. I have never lied to you about that.*

No, he thought. That was the horror of it, the thing he could explain to no one, the confession he had never spoken aloud even to himself in the dark privacy of his own skull: the crown had never lied to him. Every word it had ever whispered had been true, or true-adjacent, or a truth bent just far enough to fit inside his ear. It explained. It justified. It showed him, when he doubted, memories of his father — his father gaunt-eyed and raving in the last winter, his father signing orders with a shaking hand, his father weeping over maps of borders that no longer existed — and it said *this is what a king becomes without me, and look at you, look how steady you are, look how well you sleep.*

He slept wonderfully. That was the worst of it. He slept like a stone, dreamless and deep, and woke rested, and could not account for the hours, and the ink was always there, and the kingdom ran smoothly, smoothly, smoothly, like a millwheel turning over water that grew darker every year.

They reached the council chamber doors. The guards bowed. Somewhere beyond them, lords and ministers arranged themselves around the long table like pieces on a board, each with their grievances and their loyalties, their little fires of ambition and duty. Aldric straightened his shoulders. He put on the king the way one puts on a cloak against cold.

*Ready?*

"I'm always ready," he said aloud, softly, and heard how much the words had stopped meaning, and hated that most of all.

* * *

The council session passed in a golden blur of procedure. Grain tariffs. Bridge tolls on the Ilse Road. A dispute between two river barons that had been disputed, Aldric suspected, since before his grandfather's reign, and would go on disputing after them all were dust. He gave the answers the crown fed him, or perhaps he gave his own answers and simply no longer knew the difference — that was the other thing, the thing he circled at three in the morning when the candles burned low: he could no longer locate the seam. Where did Aldric end and the Regent begin? Which opinions were his? Which mercies were his? When he felt love for his people — and he did feel it, warm and specific, love for the orchard country and the fishing villages and the crooked streets of the capital below his windows — was that feeling his, or was it installed, maintained, *provided*, like heat through great unseen pipes?

Lord Marenne was speaking — the old Chancellor, white-bearded, deliberate, a man who had served his father and frightened him a little, the way honest men frighten kings. "—and so I must counsel delay on the western garrison levy, majesty. The roads are ice. Half the conscripts would arrive frostbitten, if they arrive at all. Whatever threat the Inquisition perceives in the west can wait for thaw."

*He doubts,* the crown said, and there was nothing cruel in its voice, which somehow made it worse. It said *he doubts* the way a physician says *the wound is infected* — clinical, regretful, already reaching for the knife. *Listen to how he frames it. Delay. Thaw. He is a good man saying a dangerous thing, and dangerous things spread from good men faster than from bad ones.*

"He is giving sound military advice," Aldric answered inwardly.

*I know,* said the Regent, almost tenderly. *That is what makes him dangerous. Do nothing. I will handle it.*

"Do nothing," Aldric repeated aloud, into the pause Marenne had left, and the old chancellor bowed, satisfied, mistaking the words for agreement. Around the table, heads dipped. Quills scratched. The machinery of the realm turned one more notch, oiled and gleaming, and Aldric sat at the head of it smiling his father's smile — he knew it was his father's smile; he had seen it in portraits — and behind the smile, in the small locked room where he kept himself, a boy of nine stood with his ear to the wall, listening to the house change owners.

* * *

Evening came early in winter. By the time the session ended the windows were blue-black, and torches had been lit in the corridors, and Aldric walked back to his apartments alone, having refused his escort at the turning stair — a small rebellion, meaningless, his only one.

He did not go to his bedchamber. He went instead to the old library at the heart of the royal wing, the one with his grandfather's globe and the map of the world as it had been drawn before the Veil treaties, and he shut the door, and stood in the dark among the smell of vellum and cold stone, and let himself shake.

Just for a moment. Just the once. Hands braced against the reading table, head bowed, breath coming fast and shallow, shaking like a man stepping out of a freezing river. Then he mastered it, because he always mastered it, because there was no one to show it to and no one to hide it from except the one listener who could never unsee anything he did.

*This is unnecessary,* the crown said, when he had gone still. *You know I dislike seeing you distressed.*

"I know," Aldric said. "You dislike many things."

*I dislike few things. Waste. Cruelty without purpose. And your suffering, above all.* A warmth spread behind his eyes, artificial and perfect, the sensation of being forgiven. *Come. Sit. Let me give you something pleasant. Your mother's garden in summer — you barely remember it. I kept it. I can return it to you whenever you wish. Lavender. Bees. Her laugh. All of it intact, better than memory, because I do not fade.*

And the terrible thing — the thing he would have knelt and confessed to a priest if priests were permitted within a mile of the crown — was that he wanted it. He wanted his mother's garden. He wanted her laugh. He wanted the lavender and the bees and the version of his childhood that did not end in a sickroom smelling of fever and lilies. Four years ago he had wanted it so badly he had stopped fighting the gift entirely, and the gifts had become nightly, and the nights had become seamless, and somewhere in that softness the seams had started appearing — hours gone, ink on his hands, letters signed, wardens resigned, ministers' names spoken at dinner with a new careful hollowness that meant they had been discussed in rooms he did not attend.

His mother's garden. He closed his eyes and let it bloom around him, lavender and heat and the drone of bees, and his mother turning, laughing, her sleeves full of cut stems — and it was perfect, it was flawless, and that was how he knew, finally, absolutely, the way one knows one is drowning by the sweetness of the water: no real memory was ever flawless. The real garden had had weeds. The real laugh had cracked once, on a cold day, when she slipped on the path. This one never cracked. This one played the same every time, note-perfect, endless, a song on a music box winding down and down.

He opened his eyes. The library was dark. The garden was gone.

"What happens," he said to the empty room, to the thing behind his eyes, "on the solstice?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. In four years, the crown had never taken three heartbeats to answer him.

*The renewal rite,* it said. *A ceremony. Old words, old bells. You will stand before your people and be crowned again, and the realm will be steadied for another age. It is what your father did. And his father. It is duty, Aldric. It is the whole of what I ask of you.*

"And what changes? Afterward?"

Another pause. Longer. When the voice came again it was softer than he had ever heard it, and that softness frightened him more than any threat could have, because it sounded like love, and everything in this palace that sounded like love belonged to it.

*Afterward,* the Regent said, *you will never have to ask that question again.*

Far off, beyond the walls, beyond the city, beyond the mountains where the world ended at a border no map honestly drew, thunder rolled once across a clear winter sky — a single dry crack, impossible weather, gone before the guards at the gate could remark on it.

Aldric stood alone in the dark library, ink-shadowed hands hanging at his sides, and felt, for the first time in four years, something answer in his blood that was neither his voice nor the crown's: a distant, storm-shaped call, like a name being spoken in a language he had never learned but somehow, deep down, already knew.

The crown heard it too. For one instant, the presence behind his eyes went utterly, perfectly still — the stillness of a spider feeling the first tremor in the web.

*Curious,* it said. And said nothing more.
