## 9. The Regent Explains

Aldric woke with the taste of ink in his mouth.

That was how it always began now — not with dreams, which the crown had long since sanded smooth, but with the small wrongnesses that survived the smoothing. The taste of ink. A cramp in his writing hand. A candle burned to a stub he did not remember lighting, and pages of correspondence in his own handwriting that he had no memory of writing.

*You were tired,* said the Regent, in the voice that was not a voice. *You worked late. You are a king. Kings work late.*

He lay in the dark of his chamber and listened to his own breathing and did not answer, because answering was the first step toward arguing, and he had learned — or been taught, or been made to learn, the distinction had worn away like a stair tread — that the Regent did not lose arguments. It simply rearranged the room until the argument had never existed.

He sat up. Dawn was a grey seam under the curtains. Somewhere below, the city was waking: cart wheels on cobbles, the fish-cries rising faint and silver from the river district, the great bell of the cathedral tolling the hour with a crack in its voice that no one had ever found the money or the courage to mend. He knew all these sounds the way he knew his own hands. That was the trouble. He knew them the way he knew his own hands, and lately he was no longer certain about his hands.

There was a letter on his bedside table. His seal, already broken. His handwriting, flowing and confident, thanking Lord Chancellor Veym for his *diligence in the matter of the northern levies*.

He could not remember the northern levies.

*You will,* said the Regent, gently, the way a mother reminds a child of a promise made at a fair. *You are tired now, but you will remember by noon. You always do.*

He always did. That was the worst of it. By noon there would be a memory sitting in his mind where the gap had been — the council chamber, the maps, the weight of the decision, his own voice saying the words — and it would fit so perfectly, so seamlessly, that he would wonder why he had ever doubted. The memories the Regent gave him were better than memories. They were *tidier* than memories. His real childhood had smells in it that didn't make sense, and conversations that trailed off, and whole afternoons lost to nothing. The Regent's memories were like illuminated pages: everything where it belonged, gold leaf on every margin.

He had stopped trusting the beautiful ones.

He did not remember deciding that. But it was there in him, a splinter under the skin of his mind, and he had learned to keep it very still.

* * *

The crown weighed four pounds and eleven ounces, or so the master of the treasury had once told him, in the dry tone of a man who weighed everything. Aldric had been crowned at fourteen. He was twenty now. Six years, and in all that time he could not recall a single morning when he had woken without it — not on the pillow beside him, where tradition said a king's crown might rest, but *on* him, or near him, always within arm's reach, always warm.

Crowns should be cold metal. He was nearly sure of that. Crowns should be cold when you put them on.

*It is warm because I am in it,* the Regent said. It did not use sound, exactly. It used the same channels his own thoughts used, so that at first — in the early years, the years he could no longer properly see — he had mistaken it for his own conscience, his own intelligence, his own better self. *And you are in me. That is what guardianship is, Aldric. Warmth, shared.*

He dressed. His valet, a soft-footed man named Pell who had served his father before him, helped him into the ceremonial grey — not a court day, but the Regent liked him visible, liked him *circulating*. Pell's hands were steady on the buckles. Pell never looked him in the eye anymore. Aldric had noticed that perhaps a year ago, or perhaps the noticing had been planted too; he had given up on forensics of his own mind. But Pell's eyes went to the crown, always, and away, quickly, like a man averting his gaze from a fire.

"Pell," Aldric said, "how long have you served my father?"

"Your father, Your Majesty?" Pell's hands paused on the clasp at his throat. "You've asked me that before."

"Have I?"

"Three times this month." The valet's voice was careful, kind, the voice of a man handling a wounded bird. "King Osric. God keep him. You served — he served — " Pell's brow creased, genuinely, painfully. "I find I've lost the thread of it, Your Majesty. Forgive me. The years."

"The years," Aldric agreed.

*You see?* the Regent murmured, warm as a hand on the back of his neck. *Grief does this. Grief and time. The whole court is a little threadbare with it. You lost your father young, and the realm lost a great king, and everyone's memory has gone soft with mourning. It is not strange. It is only sad.*

And it was sad, and it made sense, and Aldric felt the splinter shift under his skin and go still again.

* * *

They walked in the long gallery after breaking fast, because the Regent liked the gallery. The portraits of the kings of Cairnlow hung there in their gilded frames, back and back into the dark, and the Regent liked to walk him down the line of them like a tutor walking a student down a row of specimens. Here, the founder, sword on knee. Here, the Lawgiver. Here, Osric the Wise — his father — painted at forty, grey at the temples, a falcon on his wrist and an expression Aldric had spent six years trying to read and had finally, with the Regent's patient help, settled on: *pride*. Pride in the realm. Pride in the dynasty.

His father had died when Aldric was thirteen. A wasting sickness of the mind, the physicians called it. The crown had passed early, and the Regent had come with it, and the realm had been spared the shame of a mad king by the vigilance of — of —

*Of me,* said the Regent. *Say it. It costs you nothing to say it.*

"Of you," Aldric said aloud, to the empty gallery, because the Regent liked him to say it aloud, and because the gallery was empty, and because a boy of twenty talking to himself in a corridor was merely eccentric, and eccentric kings were permitted much.

*Good.* The warmth deepened. *Now. You have been troubled again. I felt it in the night, in the taste of ink. Shall we look at it together?*

This was the ritual he both dreaded and, in some chamber of himself he was ashamed of, craved. The Regent called it *review*. It would show him things — memories, true ones, it claimed, salvaged and preserved — and each time, the showing explained a little more of the wrongness in him, and each time, the explanation fit so well that the wrongness closed over like water.

They stopped before his father's portrait.

*Look at him,* the Regent said, and the portrait *moved*.

Not the paint. Something behind his eyes moved — the memory, unspooling. He was small again, six years old, in this same gallery, and his father was kneeling before him, and his father's eyes were wrong. Not angry-wrong. *Absent*-wrong, like windows with the shutters closed from inside. There was a smell of smoke where no smoke was, and his father's hands — the falconer's steady hands, the hands that had taught him to whistle a hawk down from the sky — were trembling, and his father was saying, in a voice of terrible gentle reason: "The birds are telling me the harvest will fail. The birds in the walls, little one. They count the grain at night. We must open the granaries before the birds grow angry."

And little Aldric, six years old, had said, "Papa, there are no birds in the walls," and his father had looked at him for a long, long moment, and then smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because it was *sane*, it was warm, it was his father's true smile laid over the closed shutters like a painted curtain. "No," his father had agreed. "Of course not. What a silly fancy. Come, let's find your mother."

The memory folded away. Aldric stood in the gallery with his heart going hard and his eyes stinging.

*That was the beginning,* the Regent said. *You were six. You saw it before anyone — before the physicians, before the chancellor, before your mother, God rest her, who chose not to see. You saw it because you were perceptive, and because you loved him, and love is the sharpest instrument of grief there is.* A pause, tender as a held breath. *Do you remember what came after?*

He did. He did not want to, and he did.

His father, at the winter court, standing at the high table, announcing to two hundred guests that the dead king — his own father, Aldric's grandfather, eleven years in the tomb — had been meeting him in the orchard at night to give him instructions for the spring planting. His father, signing edicts in a language that did not exist and growing furious — *furious*, spittle-furious, guards-called furious — when told no one could read them. His father, in the last spring, found in the nursery at midnight, standing over the cradle of the chancellor's visiting granddaughter with a pillow in his hands, saying, "It's for the best, it's for the best, the birds said it's for the best."

The physicians had named the sickness. The council had named the regency. And the crown — the old crown, the crown of Cairnlow, forged in the founder's age and worn by every king since — had been brought out of the vault a year early, because the law said a king must be crowned to reign, and the realm could not wait.

*And when the crown touched your father's head,* the Regent said, *the madness did not stop. Do you know why?*

"Because he was too far gone," Aldric said. He had said it before. The words came out smooth as river stones, worn by repetition. "Because nothing could have saved him by then."

*Because he fought me,* said the Regent, and for the first time there was something under the warmth — not coldness exactly, but the memory of coldness, the way a hearthstone holds the ghost of the night's frost at noon. *I offered him what I offer you, Aldric. Order. Clarity. A mind swept and kept. He refused. He raged against me for eleven months. He burned his own memories like a man burning his furniture to heat his house. In the end there was so little of him left that the crown sat on a ruin, and the ruin gave the realm eleven months of terror — edicts unwritten, alliances unmade, the treasury looted by men who smelled weakness — before God or chance took him.*

The gallery was very quiet. The morning light lay along the floor in long gold bars, and the painted kings looked down their painted noses, and Aldric stood in the middle of it all feeling the way he always felt after a review: scoured. Hollowed. *Clean*, the way a wound is clean after the surgeon has done his work.

*I found you at fourteen,* the Regent went on, softer now. *A boy, crowned too soon, in a court full of wolves who had spent a year fattening on your father's ruin. Do you remember the first council you sat?*

He did. He remembered — or he was given — the long table, and the faces, and Chancellor Veym's smile that showed all his teeth, and the way the word *regency* hung in the air like a drawn blade, and how every man in the room was already dividing the boy-king's authority among themselves like men carving a carcass.

*You were drowning,* the Regent said. *And I held your head above the water. I have held it for six years. I have swept the wolves from your council — you signed the orders yourself, you were right to sign them. I have kept the borders. I have kept the treasury. I have kept* you. *And in all that time, Aldric, I have asked for one thing in return.*

"Order," Aldric said.

*Cooperation,* the Regent said. *Order is what we make together. Cooperation is what I ask. When I set work before you at night — the levies, the correspondence, the judgments — it is because the realm cannot wait for your sleeping hours, and because I can carry the burden while you rest, if you let me. When I smooth a memory that would only hurt you — your father's last year, the nursery, the orchard — it is because a king who flinches at shadows is a king the wolves will smell. I do not take from you, Aldric. I* keep *for you. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.*

And here was the trap, the beautiful trap, the trap he walked into fresh every time because it was not built like a trap at all but like a home: it was *true*. Every word of it was true, or true enough, true in the way that mattered at three in the morning when a young king lay awake under the weight of a realm and could not tell which of his thoughts were his own. The court *had* been full of wolves. The wolves *had* been swept. The borders held. The treasury filled. The realm was fed and at peace, and if the price was that he could not remember his father's face without the Regent's hand arranging the light —

— without the Regent's hand arranging the light —

The splinter turned under his skin.

*You are troubled again,* the Regent observed. Not unkindly. Never unkindly. That was the deepest horror of it, the thing he could never make anyone understand, if there had ever been anyone to tell: the Regent was not cruel. It was *devoted*. It loved him the way a gardener loves an orchard, the way a warden loves his prison, the way —

"Father," Aldric said. "In the portrait. The falcon on his wrist." His voice came out strange, distant, a borrowed voice. "What was the falcon's name?"

Silence. A long silence, the first silence the Regent had ever left unfilled, and in that silence Aldric felt something move in the deep of his own mind, something quick and small and *his*, turning over in its sleep.

*You were very young when the falcon died,* the Regent said at last, warm again, seamless again. *It is no failing. Come. The council gathers at noon, and there is the matter of a courier — a woman, found dead at the edge of the realm, in one of the forgotten valleys. The inquisitors believe she was carrying something. We will discuss it after you have rested.*

The warmth withdrew, the way a hand withdraws from a shoulder, and left the ghost of it behind.

Aldric stood alone in the gallery, in the gold light, under the painted eyes of nine dead kings.

*A courier,* the Regent had said. *Found dead.* And it had shown him nothing of it, had given him no tidy illuminated memory, had offered the fact and then — for the first time in six years — *closed a door*.

He turned back to his father's portrait. Osric the Wise, grey at the temples, falcon on his wrist, pride in his painted eyes. And now, looking with the splinter awake and turning, Aldric saw what six years of reviews had trained him not to see: that the pride in the painted eyes was painted *over* something. That behind the shutters of his father's gaze, in the portrait as in the memory, there had been a man watching. A man who had raged against the crown for eleven months. A man who had, somewhere in that eleven months of ruin, in the gaps between the burning, found time and cunning enough to hide something — letters, a warning, a *name* — for a son he could not trust himself to remember.

The falcon's name had been Sera. He was almost sure. He was almost sure, and almost was the only country he had left, and he meant to go there.

At noon, he would sit in council and listen to the inquisitors describe a dead courier and a forgotten valley, and he would watch Chancellor Veym's teeth, and he would keep the splinter very, very still.

And at night, when the Regent set its work before him and the ink taste came, Aldric would begin — carefully, in the margin of a tax schedule, in a hand so small it might be nothing — to write down everything he could not afford to forget.
