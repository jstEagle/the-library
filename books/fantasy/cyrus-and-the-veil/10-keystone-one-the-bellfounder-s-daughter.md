## 10. Keystone One: The Bellfounder's Daughter

**Gideon**

The road to Vellamere was drowned on purpose.

Wren said it the way she said most things — sideways, watching his face in her peripheral vision as if honesty were a purse someone might cut from him. *They flooded the valley to hide it,* she'd told him three days ago, standing at the edge of a brown lake that had swallowed an entire town: rooftops like drowned teeth, a church spire leaning out of the water at a drunkard's angle. *The bellfounders cast the first keystone into their great bell, and when the Regent came looking for it, they sank everything rather than give it up.*

Now Gideon stood at the lake's edge with his boots off and his trousers rolled to the knee, and the water was so cold it burned.

"You're sure it's down there," he said. Not a question. A hope that she'd say no.

"The map says the bell rings when it wants to be found." Wren crouched beside him, testing the water with two fingers, her dark hair tied back with a strip of red cloth that made her look younger and more dangerous at once. "Which is either poetry or a trap."

"Could be both."

"Everything worth finding is both." She stood, brushed grit from her knees, and looked at him with those quick grey eyes that never seemed to land anywhere long enough to be caught. "You first or me?"

"Into the water? Why would either of us go first?"

"Because one of us has to." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only craft. "And I'd rather not watch you drown from the shore. It's bad for business."

*What business,* he wanted to ask, but he was learning her rhythms. Every question he asked, she answered with another question; every truth he offered, she banked away like coin. Two days out of Ashmarket, where she'd traded a stolen memory — some poor man's wedding day, sold back to the inquisitors' agent like a jewel — to buy them their lives, and still he didn't know what she kept and what she gave away. He only knew that she'd seen Sera Voss's handwriting on that map and gone pale as milk, and that grief, in Wren, looked exactly like purpose.

He waded in.

The cold climbed him like something climbing a tree — ankles, knees, hips, chest — and by the time it reached his shoulders his breath came in short white gasps. The lakebed shelved away beneath him, silt and old stone, and through the green murk he could see streets. An actual street, cobbles furred with weed, running down into the dark toward the tower.

"It's breathing," Wren said behind him. She'd followed without being asked, which was its own kind of answer. "The water. Feel it?"

He did. The lake rose and fell against his chest in a slow rhythm, too slow for wind, too even for current. In and out. Like the whole drowned town was sleeping under glass, and they were two flies who had landed on the lid.

The bell rang.

Not sound — he felt it in his teeth, in the water itself, a shudder rolling up through the silt. Once. Then again, closer, though nothing moved. Then a third time, and the surface of the lake went perfectly, horribly still, and the reflection of the leaning spire stood straight up, whole and unburned, in water that had suddenly turned clear as air.

"Down," Wren said. "It wants us down. Don't breathe anything it shows you."

"What does that mean—"

She didn't answer. She took a breath and dove, and after one heartbeat of pure animal refusal, Gideon did the same.

---

**Wren**

The drowned town was beautiful the way grave goods are beautiful.

Wren swam down a street called — she could read the sign, which should have been impossible, but the water carried light strangely here, generously — Cooper's Row, past doorways hung with curtains that drifted like jellyfish. There were cups on tables inside the houses. There was bread on a board, gone to soft grey stone. Someone had left this place mid-morning, mid-life, and the water had kept the moment the way she kept memories: perfectly, and without permission.

*Don't breathe anything it shows you.* Her own warning, and harder to follow than she'd expected, because the town wanted to be breathed. It pushed sweetness at her. Lavender. Woodsmoke. Her mother's kitchen, which had not existed since Wren was nine years old, and which no flooded valley in any kingdom had any right to know about.

She fixed her eyes on Gideon's heels ahead of her and kicked.

The bell-tower rose out of the dark below — the church had sunk deepest of all, as if it had been weighted — and the bell hung in its frame with the keystone set into its crown like a jewel in a crown, a wedge of pale stone shot through with veins of gold that glowed faintly, patiently, the way embers glow an hour after the fire seems dead.

A girl sat on the sill of the belfry window.

That was the thing Wren noticed third, after the bell and the stone. First was the girl, because you notice people before objects when the people are dead. She sat with her legs dangling over the drop into the nave, and she wore the fashion of a hundred years ago, a high-collared dress gone translucent as smoke, and her hair floated around her head in the water as if she were still falling.

Her mouth was open. Wren realized, with a lurch, that the girl was ringing the bell — had always been ringing the bell — pulling the rope that no longer existed, over and over, for a hundred years, and the tolling Wren felt in her ribs was the ghost's hands.

Gideon touched the floor of the belfry and stood, and the water let him. That was the strangest part. They stood in the drowned tower in a bubble of held breath and impossible air, dry as pilgrims, while the lake leaned in around them like an audience.

The girl turned her head. Her eyes were white as the inside of a shell.

"*Who comes to Vellamere?*" Her voice arrived inside Wren's skull, wet and resonant, a struck bell wearing a child's shape. "*Answer true. The water keeps what is false.*"

"Wren," Wren said. "Just Wren."

The girl tilted her head. Somewhere above them, distantly, a cup in a drowned house cracked.

"*Half-true,*" the ghost said, almost gently. "*Half-true sinks slower. But it sinks.*"

Wren's lungs tightened. Not drowning — worse. Being *weighed*. She understood then what kind of guardian this was, and cursed Sera Voss with real feeling, because of course Sera would send them to a place that required the one currency Wren had spent her whole life learning never to spend.

"Gideon," he said beside her. "My name is Gideon Dunmore."

The ghost looked at him a long moment.

"*True,*" she said, and the pressure eased a fraction, and Wren hated him a little for how easy he made it look. "*But names are the cheapest coin. You've come for the heart of the bell. Truth buys passage. Whose?"*

Silence, except for the slow breathing of the lake.

Then Gideon said, "Mine," and Wren turned to stare at him, because he hadn't discussed this, hadn't signaled, had simply stepped forward into the space where payment was demanded the way other men stepped in front of carts.

---

**Gideon**

He didn't know what he was going to say until he opened his mouth.

Ten years of practice stood him in good stead and betrayed him at once. Ten years of *I tend goats, I mend fences, I'm no one, I'm from nowhere* — a whole architecture of small lies built brick by brick until he could live inside it, and now a drowned child with shell-white eyes waited for him to take the roof off his own house in front of a stranger.

"The valley I lived in," he said. His voice echoed wrong in the belfry, flat, as if the water were listening very closely. "People think exile means being thrown out. Mine wasn't. The Veil isn't a wall they put around me. It's a wall I asked for. I walked in and shut the gate myself, and I paid a warden everything I had to make sure magic couldn't follow me over."

"Why?" the ghost said. Not unkindly. The lake breathed.

"Because I couldn't trust my own hands." The words came easier once the first one tore loose — that was the terrible trick of truth, he remembered now, it was a dam, not a door. "Because there's a storm in me. Not weather. *Me*. When I was young it answered my anger like a dog answers a whistle, and I told myself I had it leashed, and I told myself—" He stopped. Swallowed. Wren was watching him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to. "There was a night I stopped telling myself stories. And I hurt someone. Badly. And instead of facing what I'd done, I ran somewhere the storm couldn't reach, and I called the running *punishment*, because punishment felt nobler than fear. That's the truth. I wasn't exiled. I hid."

The silence afterward had texture. The ghost drifted a little closer, and up close he could see she had been crying when she died — the tracks still on her cheeks, preserved like everything else in this patient, awful water.

"*A half-truth offered as a whole,*" she said slowly, "*is the heaviest lie of all. But yours rang true to its edges.*"

"I have a brother," Gideon said, quieter, and didn't know why he was still talking, except that the dam had broken and the water goes where it goes. "Had. The storm didn't kill him. *I* nearly did. That's why I really ran. Not the stranger I hurt. Him."

"*Enough,*" the ghost said softly. "*A truth given past its weight is a bribe.*"

The bell tolled once, and this time it was only sound, enormous and mournful, and the keystone in the bell's crown flared gold, and the veins of metal began to move, flowing like honey, gathering themselves, reaching—

And the water above them went dark.

Not deeper-dark. *Other*-dark. A shadow slid across the surface of the lake, high overhead, and through forty feet of water Gideon saw it clearly: grey robes, four of them, walking out onto the lake on a skin of ice that crackled into existence beneath each step.

"Inquisitors," Wren breathed. "On the water. On *frozen* water. How—"

"They don't need to swim," the ghost said, and her voice had changed — the gentleness gone out of it, the bell coming back. "*They came for the bell once before. That is why we sank. Girl — thief — whoever you are. Take the stone and run, or stay and help me keep my vow. But choose quickly. The ice is coming down.*"

The keystone tore free of the bell crown and dropped into Gideon's hands, warm as a living thing, humming against his palms — and the lake screamed. Every drowned window lit at once. The ice above groaned, thickening, descending in spears.

"How do we get out?" Gideon shouted.

Wren was already moving, kicking for the tunnel of the nave, and the last thing he saw before he followed was the bellfounder's daughter turning back to her ropeless rope, taking up her hundred-year watch, ringing her bell against the dark — alone, again, so that others wouldn't have to be.

---

**Wren**

They broke the surface two hundred yards downstream where the river fed the lake, gasping, and the keystone was still warm against Gideon's chest where he'd stuffed it inside his shirt, and behind them the frozen lake sang — a high, grinding note, inquisitors walking its length, probing the drowned town with instruments that glittered like needles.

"We can't outrun them on foot," Gideon panted.

"No," Wren agreed. She wrung out her hair, thinking fast, thinking the way she thought best — crooked. "But they're not hunting us. Look."

The inquisitors had stopped at the center of the lake. One of them knelt and pressed a palm flat to the ice, and the ice glowed beneath him, and far below, faint as a star through fog, the drowned bell lit gold in answer.

"They can feel it," Gideon said. "Even now. Even after it's gone."

"They can feel the *hole* it left." Wren pulled him down behind a fallen willow. "Which means every keystone we touch leaves a scar they can follow. Congratulations. We just made ourselves a trail."

Gideon was quiet a moment. Water dripped off his chin. When he spoke, his voice was rougher than she'd ever heard it, stripped down to joists. "What you heard down there. What I told the ghost. That stays between us."

Wren looked at him — this big, careful man who buried storms under goat-keeping, who'd handed a dead child his worst truth like coins across a table, and who was afraid now not of the inquisitors on the ice but of one thief knowing too much.

"Sera trusted you," she said finally. "I haven't worked out why yet. But she was the best judge of people I ever knew, and I include myself in the people she judged." She stood, offering him a hand up. "Your secrets are safe with me, storm-caller. I deal in them. I know the market rate."

He took her hand, and for a heartbeat neither of them let go, and the ice behind them sang its grinding song.

Then the keystone, against Gideon's chest, pulsed — once, hard, like a second heartbeat — and both of them felt it, and both of them knew what it meant without being told.

It was calling to the others.

And somewhere east, under a mountain, under a quarry, something ancient and patient had just heard it call back — and begun, very slowly, to wake.
