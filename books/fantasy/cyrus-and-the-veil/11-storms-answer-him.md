## 11. Storms Answer Him

The road out of Ashmarket climbed through orchards gone feral, apple trees strangled by ivy, their fruit small and dark as old blood. Gideon walked with his hood up and the first keystone wrapped in wool against his chest, and he counted his steps the way he had learned to count them in the valley — one, two, three, four — because counting was a kind of cage, and cages were the only prayer he trusted.

Wren walked ahead of him, light on her feet in the way of people who had spent their lives taking things that weren't theirs. She hadn't spoken since they'd left the town's western gate. That was unlike her. Silence, from Wren, was usually a weapon she was choosing where to set down.

"You keep looking at the sky," Gideon said.

"I keep looking at the sky," she agreed.

"What are you looking for?"

"Crows." She didn't turn her head. "Inquisitors travel with crows. Grey-winged ones, tame as pigeons. Where the birds go, the grey robes follow."

"Then we hope there are no crows."

"There are always crows." She stopped at the crest of the rise and shaded her eyes, and Gideon saw her whole body go still — that particular stillness of a small animal that has caught a scent. "Gideon."

He came up beside her. Below, the road descended into a shallow vale where a river made a lazy silver loop, and on the road, spaced with the casual precision of people who had never once been hurried, stood six figures in grey.

They hadn't seen them yet. The distance was kind that way. But the figures were spread across the road like a net already cast, and at the treeline to either side, Gideon could make out the pale shapes of horses hobbled and waiting, and above the nearest rider, circling on wings the color of wet slate, a single crow rode the wind without beating it.

"Told you," Wren said softly.

"How did they find us? We left Ashmarket before dawn."

"Because you left Ashmarket before dawn." Wren turned and started walking, fast, back down the slope away from the road, into the trees. "Nobody travels at that hour except fugitives and liars. You might as well have sent them an invitation with your name written in candle soot."

"They don't know my name."

"They know what you carry. That's better than a name." Her voice was flat, quick, professional, but Gideon had spent ten days learning her, and he could hear the hair-thin fracture underneath it. Fear. Real fear, from someone who did not frighten easily. "They can smell the keystone. Not literally — well, possibly literally, I've stopped ruling things out — but the Resonants among them feel it the way you'd feel a toothache. It pulls. And right now it is pulling every grey robe between here and the capital directly toward your chest."

"Then I'll leave it with you."

"You'll do no such thing." She said it sharply, almost angrily, and did not explain, and Gideon understood anyway: whatever the stones answered to, whatever old blood or old oath ran in Sera Voss's line and now, absurdly, in his, it did not run in hers. The map had chosen its courier's heir. Stones that fused only to willing hands would not sit quietly in a thief's pack.

The trees thickened around them. Gideon ducked a low bough and felt the world narrow to green shadow and the soft rot-smell of leaf mold, and behind them, faint as a thought, came the sound he had been dreading: the crow. Not a caw. A kind of dry clicking, rhythmic, deliberate — a signal, passed bird to bird like runners relaying a torch.

"They've marked us," Wren said. "We have maybe a quarter hour before the riders cut ahead and box the vale. Less if there's a Resonant with them, and there will be, because there always is."

"Then we run."

"We run, yes. But running just delays the question." She glanced back at him, and in the dimness under the canopy her face was very pale and very serious. "Sooner or later, storm-caller, you're going to have to decide what you are. Because they've decided what you're for."

"I'm not anything. I haven't been anything for ten years."

"Keep telling yourself that." She pointed, with her chin, at his hands.

Gideon looked down. His hands were trembling. Not the fine, cold shake of exhaustion — he knew that one intimately, had lived inside it for a decade, the body's ordinary complaint. This was deeper. This started somewhere behind his sternum and rolled outward in slow waves, and the hairs on his forearms were standing upright, and the air around him tasted the way a cellar tastes before rain finds it through the stone.

*Ozone*, he thought, with something like grief. *Even here. Even under the trees.*

* * *

They nearly made it.

The vale narrowed northward into a gorge where the river ran loud and white over broken rock, and Wren led them along a shepherd's path above the water, half-hidden, slick with spray. For twenty minutes Gideon let himself believe. The counting in his head steadied. His hands quieted. The gorge walls rose grey and dripping on either side, and the noise of the river swallowed their footfalls, and he thought — foolishly, wonderfully — that perhaps the crows would lose them, that the net would close on empty air.

Then the light changed.

It didn't dim so much as *flatten*, the way candlelight flattens when a hand comes near it, and Gideon looked up and saw them on the rocks above: three figures in grey, motionless, and between them a fourth whose robes were not grey at all but the color of ash after the fire has gone, and whose eyes were closed. The Resonant stood with both palms open at her sides, and her lips moved without sound, and Gideon felt it when she found him — a pressure, delicate and horrible, moving across his skin like fingers reading a letter written in braille.

*There you are,* the pressure said, without words.

"Gideon." Wren's voice, very low. She had gone statue-still, one hand drifting toward the knife at her hip in a movement so practiced it barely happened at all. "Don't move. Don't speak. They track intent as much as anything. If you—"

"Hold," said a voice from below.

Gideon turned. At either end of the shepherd's path, where it emerged onto the river stones, more grey robes had appeared — four of them below, three above, closing the gorge like a book. Their leader stepped forward, an older man with a face like a folded letter, seamed and unreadable, and he carried no weapon that Gideon could see. He didn't need one. He had the Resonant.

"Sera Voss," the man said. His voice was mild. That was the worst of it; it was the voice of a schoolmaster, a man describing arithmetic. "She crossed the Veil carrying property of the Crown and died on foreign ground. The property has been recovered—" he nodded, courteously, toward Gideon's chest, toward the wool-wrapped weight of the keystone, "—and now only the complication remains."

"I'm not a complication," Gideon said. "I'm nobody. A goatherd. She pressed this into my hands and died, and I'm bringing it back. That's all."

Something flickered across the folded face. Not belief. Amusement, perhaps, or the memory of amusement, worn thin by decades of use.

"A goatherd," the man repeated. "Who walks out of nowhere, carrying a binding-stone, accompanied by—" his eyes moved to Wren, and this time the flicker hardened into something colder, "—ah. The Voss girl's other student. Wren. The Archive has a price on your head that would buy this valley twice over."

"Flattering," Wren said. "Is it posted anywhere? I'd love a copy."

"Take her alive," the man said, to no one in particular. "The other one—" and then he paused, and tilted his head, and Gideon watched the mildness drain out of his face and leave something older and hungrier underneath. "The other one, the Resonant says, is *full*. Ten years dammed up and not one drop spilled. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know what it costs, to hold a thing like you holds a lake in a cup?" He took one step closer. The grey robes on the rocks shifted, patient as weather. "You could simply give us the stone. And then, I think, the Regent would very much like to meet you."

The pressure against Gideon's skin intensified. The Resonant on the rocks had opened her eyes now, and they were the pale washed-out blue of skimmed milk, and they were fixed on him with an expression he recognized, because he wore it himself in mirrors, some nights, before he learned to stop looking: *longing*.

Ten years dammed up. She could feel it. Of course she could feel it. He was a struck bell standing in a room full of the deaf, and she was the only one who could hear him humming.

"No," Gideon said.

"No to which part?"

"All of it."

The schoolmaster sighed. "Then we begin with the girl. It focuses the mind, watching—"

What happened next, Gideon would spend the rest of his life failing to describe.

Two of the grey robes took Wren by the arms, and she twisted like an eel and put her knife into the forearm of one and it didn't matter, there were too many, and she was shouting something — his name, probably, though the river was suddenly very loud, or rather the river was exactly as loud as it had always been and everything else had gone strangely quiet, quiet as the center of a held breath.

And Gideon felt the cup tip.

It wasn't a decision. That was the thing he could never forgive himself for, later, when he lay awake replaying it. It was not a decision. It was a *relief*. Ten years of holding, ten years of counting steps and biting his tongue and pressing his trembling hands flat against doorframes in the dark — and all of it, all at once, was simply *allowed*. The storm rose in him like water finding a breach, like a prisoner hearing keys, and it came up through the soles of his feet out of the wet stone of the gorge, out of the river's breath, out of the bruise-colored clouds that had been gathering all morning above the peaks without anyone noticing, and it answered him.

Not gently. Storms do not answer gently. It answered the way a dog answers a master who once beat it and once fed it and cannot tell the difference anymore: with its whole body, with joy and terror in the same sound.

"Get away from her," Gideon said, and his voice came out doubled, layered, his own voice and underneath it a vast low rolling thing like the underside of thunder heard across a valley, and every grey robe in the gorge froze, because they were trained to feel magic and they had never felt anything like being addressed by the weather itself.

The first bolt took the rocks above.

He aimed it high. He would tell himself that forever — *I aimed it high, I chose the stone and not the flesh* — and it would be true, mostly, in the way that a man falling aims his hands at a branch. Lightning came down out of the low clouds in a white column thicker than his waist, and the crack of it arrived inside his skull before his ears registered it, and the rock face shattered, and shards sang outward like thrown gravel, and two grey robes went down screaming with blood in their hair, alive, alive, he had chosen the stone and not the flesh—

—and it felt *magnificent*.

That was the sickness. Not the burning smell, not the screams, not Wren scrambling free of her stunned captors with her knife out and her face white as bone. The sickness was this: ten years of numbness cracked open in one instant, and underneath the numbness was not grief, was not guilt, was not the grey obedient goatherd he had built himself into. Underneath was *power*, clean and enormous and singing, and every nerve in his body lit up like the gorge itself, and he laughed — actually laughed, a wild cracked sound the river stole away — because he had forgotten. He had genuinely forgotten what it was like. It was like being forgiven. It was like being *right*. The storm poured through him and asked nothing, judged nothing, remembered nothing; it did not care about his brother's face or the duel or the valley or the oath; it only wanted to be let through, and letting it through felt like the first deep breath after a decade underwater.

*This*, whispered some small surviving corner of him, cold as a fish's belly. *This is what ruined you. And you love it. You love it right now, and you will love it again tomorrow.*

The second bolt he did choose. Deliberately, precisely, with an artistry that frightened him more than any inquisitor: he split it three ways and laid it across the path at either end of the gorge, herding, fencing the grey robes back from Wren, from the horses, from him, a corral of white fire. The Resonant on the rocks threw up her hands and the lightning bent around some invisible working, spending itself in the air in sheets of blue riot, and even that thrilled him — a worthy opponent, a real opponent, someone who could *take what he gave* — and he hated himself for the thrill even as he reached deeper.

Above the gorge the clouds had organized themselves. They were circling now, a slow black wheel centered on him, and he could feel every drop of moisture in them the way he could feel his own fingers, and somewhere far to the south a true front was answering, dragging itself over the mountains toward him like a loyal hound that had scented its master after ten years lost.

"Gideon!" Wren's voice, cutting through. She had hold of his sleeve. When had she gotten hold of his sleeve? "Gideon — look at me — you're going to bring the whole mountainside down — *look at me*—"

He looked. Her face was very close, and very pale, and there was a cut above her eyebrow leaking a thin red line, and she was not looking at the inquisitors or the lightning or the wheeling clouds. She was looking at him the way you look at someone standing on a ledge.

"It feels good," she said. "Doesn't it. I know. The good ones always feel good. That's how you know to watch them." Her hand tightened on his sleeve. "Let it down slowly. Slowly. Like a breath out. They're running. Look — they're running — you've won, so *stop winning*."

They were running. The schoolmaster had vanished first, of course; men who send others to die are always the swiftest to leave. The remaining robes were scrambling up the scree, dragging their wounded, and the Resonant alone held her ground on the rocks above, palms open, milk-pale eyes wide — not afraid. Grateful. She bowed to him, actually bowed, a small ironic courtesy, and then she was gone over the ridgeline, and the gorge held only rain.

Because it was raining now. Of course it was raining; he had unmade the sky for fifty miles in every direction, and the sky was coming home to roost. Warm heavy rain fell in ropes, drumming on the stones, filling the gorge with white noise, and Gideon stood in the middle of it with steam rising off his shoulders and his hands still raised and full of the storm, and he could not lower them.

"Wren," he said. His voice was only his own again, and it shook. "I liked it."

"I know."

"You don't understand. I didn't— it wasn't just that it worked. I *liked* it. Ten years I told myself I missed being normal. I told myself I'd give anything to be ordinary. And the second they touched you I—" His breath went ragged. "It was like coming home. It was like coming home and home was the thing that killed my—"

He couldn't say it. He had never, not once in ten days of roads and dangers, said it to her.

Wren waited. Rain ran off her chin. Then she did something he did not expect: she reached up, took his raised hands in both of hers, and pulled them down, gently, the way you gentle a horse, until his arms hung at his sides and the storm in him had nowhere left to look.

"Later," she said. "You can tell me later, when you're not steaming. Right now we walk. Fast. There'll be more of them, and next time they won't send a schoolmaster, they'll send soldiers, and soldiers don't ask you to hand anything over." She turned upstream, into the rain, and then paused, and looked back at him over her shoulder, and her expression was unreadable in the way of all her most honest moments. "For what it's worth — the way you split that third bolt. Three ways at once, clean as a jeweler. Sera talked about you sometimes. She said you were the finest storm-caller the Veil ever swallowed."

"She talked about me?"

"She talked about a lot of people." But her voice was softer than the words, and she held out her hand for him to take, there in the drowning rain, and Gideon — hollowed, terrified, still humming like a bell that would not finish ringing — took it.

They walked upstream until the light failed. Behind them, far to the south over the drowned hills, the storm he had summoned went on without him, rolling and flashing, answering no one, and Gideon did not look back at it, because he knew with perfect certainty that if he did, it would come when he called. It would always come when he called.

That night, in the lee of a fallen rowan, Wren slept badly and Gideon did not sleep at all. Near dawn, shivering, he took out the map to check their route, and his eye fell on Sera Voss's final notation — the one at the eastern edge, in cramped fading ink, beneath a symbol he did not recognize:

*The second stone remembers what hands have done. Bring him honestly, or not at all.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost an afterthought, in letters pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*If the storm answers him again — and it will — tell him this: what ruined him was never the calling. It was what he was willing to call it for.*
