## 12. The Archive of Stolen Things

The archive did not want to be found.

Wren had known that the first time she came here, three years ago, with Sera's hand on her shoulder and the smell of river-mud on her boots. The building sat in the crook of the Graywater where the city forgot to look — a drowned granary, half-sunk, its upper floors leaning together like conspirators sharing a secret. From the street it was nothing: a slumped roofline, a door that had rotted shut a generation ago, a wall furred with moss so thick it looked like the stone was growing hair. People walked past it the way they walked past beggars and graveyards, eyes sliding away before their minds could form a question.

That was the point. Memory was the most valuable commodity in Ashmarket, and the people who traded in it could not afford to be remembered.

"Down here," Wren said, and led Gideon off the cobbled lane into an alley so narrow their shoulders brushed both walls.

Gideon followed without argument. He had been quiet since the bell-tower — quieter than usual, which for a man who spoke perhaps forty words a day was saying something. The ghost's truth still hung between them, unspoken but present, like smoke after a candle goes out. He had told the tower something about himself. She had heard him pay it, though she hadn't caught the words; the ghost had wrapped them in silence the way a moneylender wraps coins in cloth. Whatever he'd confessed, he carried it now in the set of his jaw, in the way his eyes went distant when he thought she wasn't looking.

She didn't ask. Thieves understood locked doors. You didn't pick them out of curiosity; you picked them when you needed what was inside.

The alley ended at a wall of black brick. Wren pressed her palm flat against it, over a place where the mortar swelled into a knot no bigger than a plum, and whispered the counter-sign Sera had taught her — not words exactly, more a shape made of breath, *hnn-ah-tch*, like a sneeze strangled at birth. Somewhere behind the wall, a mechanism sighed. A section of brick swung inward on greased hinges, revealing a stairwell lit by lamps turned down to embers.

"You're sure this is safe?" Gideon asked. His voice was low, pitched under the sound of the river.

Wren almost laughed. "No. It's safe the way a knife is safe. It does what you need if you hold it right." She stepped through. "Stay close. Don't touch anything with your hands. And whatever anyone offers you — a drink, a bargain, a memory of your mother's face — don't say yes until I've counted the cost."

"And if someone offers me nothing?"

"Then be twice as careful. Generosity is the most expensive thing in this building."

* * *

The descent took longer than it should have. That was deliberate too — Wren knew the trick of it now, how the stairs coiled downward through more turns than the building's footprint could possibly contain, each landing marked with a sigil that wasn't quite a language. Sera had explained it once, in her dry way, while Wren pretended not to be impressed: *The way down is a sieve, girl. It shakes loose the ones who come here on impulse. Impulse is the enemy of trade.* Three years ago Wren had counted the landings out of spite. There were nine. There were always nine, no matter how many steps it felt like.

At the bottom, the air changed.

It was hard to describe to someone who hadn't felt it. The air below Ashmarket tasted of other people's lives — not metaphorically, not as poetry, but as a physical fact, faint and sweetish, like the smell of a room where someone has been crying and laughing in equal measure. Memories leaked. Even sealed in glass, even warded in lead, they sweated through the seams, and after decades of trade the whole archive had become saturated, a sponge soaked in ten thousand stolen afternoons.

Gideon stopped on the last step. His nostrils flared. "What is that smell?"

"Grief, mostly," Wren said. "Joy, if you're lucky. Come on."

The trading floor opened around them like the inside of a shell. It had been a granary vault once — ribbed ceiling, pillars thick as old trees — but the memory-brokers had spent a century carving it to their purposes. Alcoves lined the walls, each one curtained in dyed leather, each one holding a table, a scale, and a broker. The scales were the heart of the trade: brass pans balanced with exquisite cruelty, weighing vials of memory against coin, against favors, against other memories. In the center of the floor stood the racks — thousands upon thousands of small glass vials racked in wooden frames that rose toward the ceiling like the pipes of some mad organ. Each vial held a swallow of silver-grey liquid that moved when you looked at it, slow as smoke underwater.

Memories. Bottled, labeled, priced.

Wren had grown up hungry in the kind of streets where hunger was the least of what got taken from you. She had thought she understood theft. Then Sera had brought her here, put a vial in her hand, and said, *This belonged to a farmer. It's his wedding day. He sold it to pay a debt he never should have owed, and now he remembers getting married the way you remember a story someone told you.* Wren had held the little glass tube up to the lamplight and watched the silver turn, and something in her chest had closed like a fist. She had been a thief before that day. Afterward, she was something else — a thief with a doctrine, which is either a reformed criminal or a worse one, depending on whom you ask.

"Keep your hood up," she murmured to Gideon. "Storm-callers are rare enough that someone will try to buy the memory of meeting you."

They crossed the floor. The brokers watched them pass from behind their curtains — Wren counted seven pairs of eyes, catalogued seven degrees of interest — but nobody moved to stop them. That was the etiquette of the place. Everyone who descended the nine landings had already paid admission in risk.

The racks were organized by a taxonomy only the archivists fully understood. Wren knew its grammar well enough: childhood by the west wall, loves by the south, deaths nearest the cold corner where the damp crept in because even brokers found death-memory bad luck. Crimes had their own alcove, guarded by a man with a face like a closed ledger. And along the eastern racks, in rows sorted by date of sale, lay the recent acquisitions — the fresh stock, the memories taken within the season, still bright with the wet shine of new grief.

Sera had died eleven days ago. If her last words had been taken — if someone had cracked open her dying mind and ladled out the final minutes like soup from a pot — they would be here. Fresh stock. Eastern racks.

Wren's hands were steady. She noticed that about herself sometimes, in the worst moments: the steadiness, the professional calm, and underneath it the scream, all of it held apart like oil and water. Sera had taught her that too, though she'd never named it as a lesson. *A thief's hands lie so her face doesn't have to.*

She started at row one and worked east.

* * *

The labels told stories in miniature. *Woman, 60s. Her daughter's voice, saying goodbye at the harbor. Sold by the daughter.* *Man, 30s. The taste of his grandmother's bread. Sold by himself; debts.* *Child, approx. 8. The day the soldiers came. Seller unknown.* Wren read them the way other people read casualty lists, letting each one land and move through her and out, because if she stopped to feel them all she would stand in this aisle until she starved.

Beside her, Gideon had gone very still. He was looking at a rack of vials near his eye level, and his face had the particular blankness she'd learned to distrust — the look of a man reading something he wished he couldn't.

"What is it?" she asked.

He didn't answer at once. When he did, his voice came out sanded flat. "There's a whole shelf of these. All the same label. *The valley.*" His jaw worked. "Someone's been selling memories of my valley. Of home."

Of course they had, Wren thought, and hated the arithmetic of it. Exiles were the best customers and the best product both. A man who had lost a place would pay anything to taste it again — and a man desperate to forget a place would sell it for less than the price of the boat that carried him away. The archive fed on exile the way mold fed on damp.

"How many?" she asked.

"I stopped counting at thirty." He touched nothing — she'd told him not to touch, and he obeyed with the rigid care of a man holding himself back from something worse. "Do they know? The people who sold these. Do they know what they gave up?"

"Some do. Some sold on purpose and regret it every morning. Some were robbed and don't know yet — they just feel a hole shaped like somewhere green." Wren pulled her eyes off him and back to the labels. "That's the trade, Dunmore. Nobody walks out of here owning everything they carried in."

Row four. Row five. The lamplight caught the silver in the vials and threw it wavering onto the shelves, so that the whole aisle seemed to swim gently, a corridor built over water. Wren checked dates against the little bone tally-stick she kept in her sleeve, marking off the days since Sera's death, narrowing the field. Fresh stock, eleven days or newer. Eastern rows, third shelf and lower.

She found it on the sixth shelf of row seven.

She knew before she read the label. She knew from six feet away, the way you know your own name being called in a crowd — some recognition too deep for the ears. The vial sat between two others, ordinary as any of them, a swallow of grey light in glass no longer than her thumb. But the handwriting on the label stopped her heart mid-beat, because it was a hand she knew better than her own: the sharp downstrokes, the impatient crossbars, the letter *R* that always looked like it was trying to escape the line.

Sera's hand. Sera had labeled it herself.

*Voss, Sera. Final minutes. Taken post-mortem. Provenance: Questioner's office, third seal.*

Wren did not move. For a long moment she wasn't sure she was breathing. The archive went on around her — a broker's laugh behind a curtain, the soft clink of the scales, the endless silver turning in its thousand small prisons — and she stood in the middle of it, nailed to the floor by a two-inch strip of paper.

*Final minutes. Taken post-mortem.*

Someone had opened Sera Voss's skull — not with steel, worse than steel, with the extraction needle, the thin grey wand the inquisitors used — and drawn out the last thing she ever was. Her last sight. Her last thought. The words she'd spent her final strength pushing through a torn throat. *Tell the king his father—*

And then someone had priced it. Weighed it on the brass scales. Set it on a shelf between a dead woman's goodbye and a child's worst day, and waited for a buyer.

Hope curdled in Wren's chest, thick and sour. Because there was hope in it, that was the obscene part — hope first, before the horror. If the vial existed, then Sera's last words existed, and Sera's last words might hold the rest of the sentence that had died with her on the riverbank. *Tell the king his father—* what? What had the old king done, what had he known, what secret had he paid a courier to carry out of the capital in her own blood? The answer was six inches from Wren's fingertips, sealed in glass, trembling faintly with the pressure of a life folded into a swallow of silver.

And it was for sale.

"Wren." Gideon's voice, very quiet, very close. "Your face. Whatever you're looking at — breathe."

"I'm breathing." She was. She proved it. "It's her."

"Her?"

"Sera." Wren lifted the vial from the rack with both hands, the way you lift a wounded bird, the way you lift a live coal. The silver inside turned toward the lamplight and, for half a heartbeat, she would have sworn it turned toward *her*. "This is her. Or the last of her. Eleven days old. Extracted by the inquisitors after—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She clamped down on it. "After they killed her."

Gideon looked at the vial for a long time. When he spoke, his words came slow, each one tested before use. "Can we buy it?"

"With what?" Wren laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "You've seen our combined fortune. This shelf is premium stock. Dying words of a royal courier, provenance-sealed — the brokers will want fifty crowns minimum, and probably a favor on top. Favors are how they really feed."

"So we steal it."

Now she did look at him. Storm-caller, goat-herder, liar-by-necessity, standing in the deepest den of thieves in Ashmarket suggesting theft with the earnest gravity of a man proposing they attend church. Something in her chest that had been curdling all morning warmed by one degree.

"Dunmore," she said, "you continue to surprise me."

"It's hers." His jaw set in that way she was beginning to know, the way stone sets before lightning finds it. "She gave us everything else she had. She shouldn't be sitting on a shelf waiting for some fat broker to auction her last breath to a collector."

"Oh, it's worse than an auction." Wren turned the vial in her fingers, reading the fine print beneath the provenance mark, and the sourness came flooding back. "*Reserve: private treaty.* That means it's not on general sale. Someone has already claimed it — put money down, first refusal. Someone specific wants Sera's last words badly enough to reserve them before they hit the open racks." She met his eyes. "Question is who. An inquisitor tying off loose ends? A collector of royal secrets? Or whoever it was that wanted her dead in the first place?"

Behind the leather curtains, a broker coughed politely.

Wren slipped the vial into the hidden pocket inside her sleeve — the one Sera had sewn for her, years ago, with a thief's neat stitches — and felt the glass settle against her wrist like a pulse. Stealing it outright was madness; the archive's wards would sing the moment reserved stock crossed the threshold, and the brokers employed men whose job description began and ended with *retrieval*. No, this required craft. A substitution — a decoy vial, a switched label, three seconds of misdirection. She'd need to come back tonight, when the floor emptied and the night-brokers grew drowsy. She'd need Gideon creating a distraction worthy of the name.

She'd need, she realized with a lurch, to play back the memory itself — and playing back a dead woman's final minutes meant living them, tasting them, standing inside Sera Voss's skull while the blade came down. She had done extractions before, on small things, on safe things. Never on this.

"Tonight," she said. "We come back tonight. And tomorrow we'll know how the sentence ends — *tell the king his father* what."

Gideon nodded once. But as they turned back toward the stairs, Wren caught him glancing east, toward the racks of valley-memories, toward the thirty small vials of a home he could no longer enter — and the look on his face was one she recognized from mirrors.

Everyone left this place owing something. She had simply never expected to owe it to a dead woman, in the currency of her final words — or to leave carrying another person's whole life against her wrist, warm as a coal, patient as a fuse.

Above them, faint through nine landings of stone, the bells of Ashmarket rang the hour. Six hours until dark. Six hours until the archive slept, and the thief went back down to finish what the inquisitors had started — and to learn, at last, what a king's father had done that was worth killing for.
