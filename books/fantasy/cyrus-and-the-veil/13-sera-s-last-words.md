## 13. Sera's Last Words

The vial was warm.

Wren had held a hundred stolen memories in her hands—little glass eggs of other people's griefs and glories—and she had never once been afraid of one. They were merchandise. Inventory. The archive paid her by the drop and she'd learned early not to look too closely at what she carried, the same way a butcher learns not to name his animals.

But this vial was Sera's, and it was warm, and it trembled faintly against Wren's palm like something alive that had waited too long in the dark.

"Memory keeps heat," said Quill, the archivist, from behind his counter of blackened oak. He was an old man folded into himself like a letter never sent, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of pond water. "The strong ones do. A dying woman's last hours burn hotter than most. Whoever pulled this from her did it gently, at least. You can tell by the color." He nodded at the liquid inside: not the murky grey of a rough extraction but a deep amber, threaded with gold, turning slowly on itself even when Wren held it perfectly still. "That's craft. That's love, if you want my word for it."

"I don't," Wren said, because her voice would have broken otherwise.

Gideon stood at her shoulder, close enough that she could feel the strange weather of him—the way the air around the storm-caller always seemed to hold its breath, pressure gathering at some horizon only he could see. He hadn't spoken since they'd found the vial on its velvet cradle in the drawer labeled *V*, between *Vale, a wedding* and *Vane, the drowning of*. His hands were fists at his sides. She could hear his knuckles creak.

"The viewing room is through there," Quill said. "You have it until the candle burns out. Standard rate covers one playback. I don't sell repeats of the dead." He paused, and something moved behind those pond-water eyes—not pity, exactly; pity was a currency he'd stopped accepting decades ago. Something more like recognition. "Whoever she was, she trusted someone to carry that warmth this far. Be worth the carrying."

He slid a brass key across the counter and turned back to his ledgers, already done with them, already returning to the great catalog of stolen things where everything human could be bought by the dram.

* * *

The viewing room was small as a coffin and shaped like one, lined floor to ceiling in dark felt that drank every sound. A single chair sat before a shallow basin of smoked glass, and above it hung a candle stub burning with a flame that gave off light but no heat. Wren had seen these rooms in three cities. They were all built like confessionals, which, she supposed, was what they were. People came here to receive what other people could no longer confess.

She set the vial in the basin's cradle. Her hands were steady. She was proud of that, in a distant, hollow way—ten years in the trade had taught her hands to lie even when nothing else in her could.

"You should know what you're about to see might be—" Gideon started.

"She died in your arms. I know." Wren didn't look at him. "I taught her that trick, actually. How to pass off a wound as nothing. How to keep talking so the person holding you doesn't realize how bad it is until after." She laughed once, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I taught her everything useful about dying and almost nothing useful about living. So. If there's a lesson in here, I'd like to think I finally get to learn something from her instead."

"Wren."

"Sit down, Gideon."

He sat. The chair groaned under a man built like weather coming.

Wren pressed her thumb to the basin's rim and spoke the trader's word—a soft syllable, half prayer, half key—and the amber memory rose out of the vial like breath leaving a body.

It filled the basin first, then the room, then them.

* * *

Rain.

Not valley rain, not the gentle grey drizzle Gideon must have known for ten years, but hard mountain rain, the kind that came sideways and stung, hammering on slate roofs, filling the ruts of a road somewhere high and wild. Wren knew that road. The Widow's Stair, the smugglers called it—the switchback trail down from the Thornmark passes toward the capital, six days' ride if the weather held and you were lucky, nine if it wasn't and you weren't.

Sera had not been lucky.

The memory opened on her own face, reflected dimly in a puddle, and Wren's heart stopped and restarted wrong. Her mentor looked *awful*. Hollow-cheeked, grey-lipped, hair plastered to her skull, one eye swollen nearly shut. And yet she was grinning—that crooked, insufferable grin she used when she'd just stolen something impossible or was about to.

"*If anyone's watching this,*" Sera said to the puddle, to the rain, to whoever might someday stand in a dark room holding her death in their cupped hands, "*then I'm either rich or dead, and knowing my luck, both.*"

Beside Wren, Gideon made a sound low in his chest. In the memory, Sera Voss limped along the Stair with her arm bound against her ribs, talking to herself the whole way down, because that was what Sera did—she narrated, she joked, she filled silence like a woman pouring wine into a cracked cup, keeping the crack from echoing.

"*Day six,*" she told the rain. "*The inquisitors got me at the ford two nights back. Don't ask how they found me. Ask who sold me, because I intend to, assuming I live long enough to enjoy the answer.*" She coughed, and the cough had blood in it, and Wren saw her hide it the way Wren had taught her—turn away, wipe the mouth, keep talking. "*They took the map copy I made for the guild. Good. Let them chase paper. The real one's sewn in my coat, and the real one goes to the man it belongs to, whether he wants it or not.*"

The memory lurched—memories always lurched, cut by exhaustion, by pain, by the mind deciding what mattered—and suddenly it was night, and firelight, and Sera sitting in the mouth of a cave with her boots steaming, writing. Writing fast, on scraps, in code, her swollen eye making her squint like a suspicious cat.

"*I need to say this while I still remember it clearly,*" she murmured, and now her voice had changed. The performance dropped away. This was the voice Wren had heard maybe four times in seven years—the voice underneath, the true one, the one Sera used only when the thing she was saying was too heavy for jokes. "*Because the inquisitors take memories. Everyone knows they take memories from their prisoners. Nobody asks what they do with them after.*"

The fire popped. Rain drummed the slate of the world.

"*I was in the Questioner's cell for two days before I broke out. Two days of drops. They pour your head out a spoonful at a time and bottle it, and I watched them work, because watching is what I do. And the third day, when they thought I was empty, they brought in a new prisoner, and I heard the Questioner say something to his assistant that I will swear on anything you like, on any god you like, on my own grave:* 'Careful with this one's childhood. His Majesty has requested it preserved intact.'"

Sera looked directly into the memory—as directly as a dead woman can ever look at the living—and her voice went flat and cold.

"*His Majesty. Requested. Preserved. Intact.*

"*Do you understand what I'm telling you? The king drinks. We all whisper that the crown eats his memories, that he forgets his friends, that he signs things twice because he doesn't remember signing them once. But I sat in that cell and I understood: it isn't eating blind. It isn't a parasite gorging itself. It's being fed. Someone curates what the Regent takes and what the Regent keeps, and the Regent keeps the king's childhood whole, and do you know why? Because you don't erase a hostage's past. You preserve it, precisely, so that when you show him a false one, he can't tell the difference. You keep the foundation intact so the house you build on top of it stands up straight.*"

Wren's hands had gone cold on the basin's edge. Beside her, Gideon had stopped breathing entirely.

The memory lurched again. Dawn now, or something like dawn, grey light in the cave mouth. Sera stood, swaying, and pressed her hand flat against the cave wall as though steadying herself against the world.

"*And then I went looking,*" she said softly. "*Of course I went looking. I'm a thief. It's what we're for.*"

"*There are places in this kingdom older than the Regent's rule. Places the inquisition doesn't bother searching because the papers burned centuries ago and no one remembers why the doors are bricked up. I spent the last month in one of them. The old royal vault beneath the summer palace at Carrowmere—flooded to the second stair, sealed since the reign of King Maerin the Forgotten. That's what the histories call him. Maerin the Forgotten. They say it like an accident, like history simply misplaced him. History misplaced nothing.*

"*Maerin bound the Regent. Not crowned it—bound it. Sealed it into the iron of the crown itself with three keystones and a rite that required a host willing to be forgotten. And then, when it was done, when the thing was chained and harmless and the kingdom was safe—Maerin ordered the rite erased. Every page. Every stone inscription. Every mind that knew it.*"

She laughed, and there was nothing funny in it at all.

"*Including his own. He chose to forget how he'd saved everyone. Do you understand? He didn't lose the knowledge. He executed it. Because he'd read enough of the Regent's nature to know that a binding written down is a binding waiting to be undone, and the only safe place for the rite was inside a man who would die without ever knowing he'd carried it. He made himself the last keystone. He made forgetting the lock.*

"*And then his son inherited the crown. And his son's son. And the binding frayed, because bindings fray, because iron rusts, because nobody remembered there was anything to maintain. And forty years ago the Regent woke inside the iron and found a boy king wearing it who couldn't remember his father's face without help.*"

The memory shuddered. Sera gripped the cave wall. Blood showed bright between her fingers where her ribs had given way again, and still she talked, faster now, racing the leak inside her.

"*So here is what matters. Here is what you carry forward, whoever you are. The Regent isn't a demon. It's a wound that learned to feed. It was bound once by ordinary human hands using ordinary human courage, and it can be bound again—but not with force. Force is what it eats. You bind it the way Maerin bound it: willingly. Three keystones. One key. And a host who consents, because consent is the lock and always was, and the reason the Regent has ruled forty years unchallenged is that no one has ever asked the boy wearing it whether he'd like to be free.*"

She slid down the wall. The memory tilted crazily—sky, rain, rock, sky—and steadied as she dragged herself upright one more time, laughing at herself, at the absurdity, at everything.

"*Listen to me. Listen. I'm going to find the storm-caller in the valley. The old king wrote about him—the exile, the one they put beyond the Veil. The letters say the Veil doesn't stop magic. It stops memory. It's a forgetting-place. And a forgetting-place is the one ground in this world where the Regent cannot reach, cannot taste, cannot follow—which means it's the one place where a man can think clearly enough to plan its destruction. Maerin hid the rite in a man who forgot. Maybe the storm-caller can be the opposite. Maybe he can be the man who remembers how.*

"*Tell him—I never caught your name, stranger, whoever's wearing my last hours—but tell him his father—*"

The memory ended.

Not faded. Ended. Cut clean, mid-word, the way a candle ends when pinched, and the amber light collapsed back into the vial, and the coffin-room was dark except for the candle stub overhead, guttering, and Wren realized she was on her knees on the felt floor and did not remember falling.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke.

The candle burned down in silence. Somewhere beyond the felt walls, the Archive of Stolen Things murmured its endless commerce—clinks of glass, the scratch of Quill's pen, a buyer haggling over somebody's first kiss. The world went on buying and selling people's insides, and Wren knelt on the floor with her mentor's death cooling in a basin beside her.

"His father," Gideon said at last. His voice sounded scraped. "She died trying to tell me something about my father. And the words—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Ten years I've wondered if he was ashamed of me. If the last thing he thought of me was disappointment. And she knew. She *knew*, and she died one sentence short."

"No." Wren pushed herself up. Her knees ached. Everything ached. "No, Gideon, listen to what she actually said. She called you the man who remembers how. She went into a flooded vault and a Questioner's cell and halfway up a mountain to reach *you*. Whatever your father was to her, whatever he was to you—it wasn't shame. Nobody crosses the Veil for a disappointment." She picked up the vial. It had gone cold now, finally cold, an ordinary piece of glass holding an extraordinary absence. "She ran out of time. That's all. Time ran out on her, not on what she knew."

"And what she knew—" Gideon stood, and the little room seemed smaller with him upright in it, the air tighter, the candle flame leaning toward him as if drawn. "A king who chose to forget. Consent is the lock. The Regent was bound before and can be bound again." He looked at her, and she saw something kindling in his face that frightened her a little, because she recognized it. It was the look Sera got. Purpose arriving like weather. "We have one keystone. There are three. And somewhere there's a rite that hasn't existed in any living mind for four hundred years."

"Not any living mind," Wren said slowly.

Gideon frowned.

"Think like a thief, Gideon. Where do you put something you need to keep but can't afford to be seen holding?" She lifted the vial. "You don't write it down. You don't carve it. You *store it*. In the one vault in this kingdom where memories are kept safe from the Regent's appetite, catalogued, preserved, waiting for whoever comes asking the right questions." Her heart was beating very fast now, grief transmuting in her chest, refining itself down into something with an edge. "Sera learned the truth from stolen memories in a Questioner's cell. Where do you think the inquisitors *bottle* what they take?"

"The Archive," Gideon breathed.

"The Archive. Or one like it. Maerin's rite is sitting in a drawer somewhere with a label on it, Wren's Law of Locked Rooms says it has to be, because the Regent can't search what it can't feel, and the only things it can't feel are the ones we stole first." She was moving now, pulling open the door, striding back out into the lamplit stacks with the cold vial clenched in her fist like a talisman. "Quill! Quill, I need your oldest ledgers. Royal provenance. Pre-Regent acquisitions. Anything catalogued under M, and anything under R, and anything your grandfather's grandfather was too frightened to label properly."

From behind the counter of blackened oak, the old archivist looked up from his ledger with those pond-water eyes, and for the first time since they'd entered, he smiled.

"Took you long enough," said Quill. "Your mentor asked me the same question eleven years ago. I've kept a drawer warm for her ever since."

He reached beneath the counter and drew out a slim volume bound in cracked leather, unlabeled, its pages edged in gold gone brown with age—and set it on the counter between them like a card turned face-up.

The title, embossed in a hand Wren somehow already knew was a dead king's:

*What Was Done, and How — to be opened only by the willing.*
