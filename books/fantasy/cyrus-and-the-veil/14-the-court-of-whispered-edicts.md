## 14. The Court of Whispered Edicts

The morning the Regent killed Lord Cassel, Aldric woke with the taste of copper in his mouth and no memory of how it got there.

He lay in the dark of his chambers and took inventory of himself the way a man takes inventory after a burglary — checking each hour of the night for what had been taken. He had gone to bed after the third bell. He remembered the candle guttering, the weight of the blankets, the particular silence of the palace at that hour when even the mice seemed to hold their breath. And then there was a gap, smooth-edged as a river stone, and then he was waking, and his right hand ached faintly, and there was ink dried in the creases of his fingers like a stain that had seeped up from beneath the skin.

He held his hand to the window's grey light and turned it over. The ink was in the whorls of his fingerprints. As if he had gripped something — a pen, pressed too hard, for too long.

*You signed it,* said the crown, from its place on the stand across the room. It did not speak aloud; it never spoke aloud. Its voice arrived inside his skull the way warmth arrives inside a bath, all at once, everywhere, already part of him. *You signed it well.*

Aldric sat up slowly. "Signed what."

*The edict. You'll see it at court. Everyone will be very pleased with you.*

He washed his hands three times. The ink would not entirely leave. He stood before the basin watching the water swirl pink-grey down the drain and understood, with the dull certainty of a man who has been ill for years and finally stops expecting to recover, that the water was not the point. Nothing he washed away would stay washed.

*You're brooding,* the crown observed, with something almost like fondness. *Eat your breakfast. The realm requires you.*

---

The throne room of Vael Corren had been built by men who wanted to feel small in the presence of power, and they had succeeded so thoroughly that their descendants now felt small in the presence of everything. The ceiling arched up into gloom painted with constellations no astronomer could name. The pillars were carved with kings whose faces had been worn featureless by centuries of incense smoke, so that history itself looked out at the court with blank eyes. Light came down through clerestory windows in long slanted columns, and motes of dust turned in them slowly, like snow that had forgotten which way was down.

Aldric processed down the length of that hall every morning, and every morning the walk grew longer. Not the distance — the distance never changed. The *walk*. Each step required more of him than the last, because the crown rode his brow like a second skull, and with every stride it drank a little deeper, and what it drank was him.

He had learned to think of himself in the plural now, sometimes. *We are tired. We are not hungry. We do not remember.* It was easier than admitting that the first person singular had begun to sound like a stranger's voice in his own throat.

The court assembled in its hierarchies: the high lords nearest the dais, glittering and cold; the ministers at their curved table below, quills scratching; the petitioners beyond the velvet rope, holding their little papers and their little griefs. Aldric took the throne. The crown was lifted from its cushion by the Lord Chamberlain with white-gloved hands and set upon his head, and the moment it touched him the whispering stopped being a whisper and became a tide.

*Good. Now smile. Minister Duvane is about to disappoint us.*

Aldric smiled. His face knew how to do it without him.

The morning's business moved through its liturgy — grain tariffs, a border dispute, a petition from the glassmakers' guild rendered in language so ornate it might have been a prayer. Aldric spoke when prompted, and his voice came out level and correct, and between one word and the next he could feel hours going missing from him like coins slipping through a torn pocket. He had stopped trying to catch them. Catching them only meant feeling them go.

Then Lord Cassel rose.

Cassel was old. He had served Aldric's father, and before that Aldric's grandfather, and he wore his decades of service the way an old soldier wears scars — not proudly, exactly, but without apology. His hair was white as frost. His hands trembled slightly when he unrolled his memorandum, and Aldric found himself staring at those trembling hands with an attention that frightened him, because they were hands that shook honestly, and he could no longer say the same of his own.

"Your Majesty," Cassel began, in his dry paper voice, "I bring a matter concerning the solstice preparations. The renewal rite. There are irregularities in—"

The crown flared.

Aldric felt it happen — felt it as a physical event, a heat blooming across his scalp, a pressure behind his eyes like a thumb pressing on the soft place above the nose. The Regent did not merely listen to the court. It *tasted* it. Every mind in that hall gave off a flavor, and most of them were flavors the Regent had catalogued long ago: ambition, fear, servility, the sour tang of men calculating their advancement. But doubt — doubt was different. Doubt was a flavor the Regent could not permit to exist within the walls of its own house, because doubt was the one spice that could spread through a dish and ruin it entire.

And Cassel, standing there with his trembling memorandum, was thinking a thought.

Aldric could not hear the thought. He was never permitted to hear the thoughts. That was the cruelty of it — the Regent showed him everything except the evidence. He saw the verdicts but never the trials. He watched men fall and was told only, afterward, what they had been guilty of, in terms so vague they could have described anyone: *disloyalty. Corruption of intent. A sickness of the inner court.* He had learned not to ask. Asking earned him a night of missing hours and a morning of explanations delivered in the Regent's gentle, reasonable voice, explanations that always ended the same way:

*I protect you. I protect the realm. You were too young to rule alone, and your father was too broken to teach you, and so I have done both jobs, and I have asked nothing in return but obedience, which is the least expensive thing a king can give.*

But this morning, for the first time in months, Aldric caught a fragment. Just a splinter, just a shard of Cassel's thought, surfacing through the Regent's grip like a bubble through ice — because the Regent was distracted, momentarily, savoring.

Cassel was thinking: *the boy's father warned me. Twenty years ago, over wine, laughing so it wouldn't frighten us both — he said, if ever the crown begins to love me too much, if ever it starts finishing my sentences — promise me you'll doubt it. Promise me someone will doubt it.*

The fragment dissolved. The Regent's attention closed over it like water.

*There,* it said, satisfied, warm, terrible. *Do you taste it? Doubt. In this hall, under your throne, breathing your air. It spreads, Aldric. It gets into the young ones, the impressionable ones, the ones who haven't yet learned that certainty is a duty. We've tolerated Lord Cassel for years because he was useful and because his family is old and beloved. But tolerance has limits, and this morning he has shown us his true flavor.*

"Tolerated," Aldric repeated. The word came out of him before he could stop it, and it came out wrong — too loud, too raw, cracking on the second syllable. Heads turned. Cassel looked up from his memorandum with mild, puzzled eyes.

The Regent paused. And then it did the thing Aldric hated most, the thing that was worse than the missing hours, worse than the ink on his hands. It was patient with him.

*Careful,* it said softly. *You're tired. You didn't sleep well. I noticed you woke unsettled — I arranged a lighter schedule, you're welcome. This is not a matter for your unsteady morning. This is a matter for the state. Leave it to me, as I have always left nothing to chance. Say: 'The Crown will consider the minister's concerns.' Say it, and we can move on to the petitions, and by noon you'll feel better, I promise you.*

"The Crown will consider the minister's concerns," Aldric said.

His voice sounded fine. That was the horror of it. Somewhere in ten years — no, four years, since his father died and the crown came down onto his nineteen-year-old head — somewhere in those four years, the Regent had taught his body to lie fluently while his mind screamed behind the sound like a man locked in a cellar under a ballroom.

Cassel bowed and sat. The morning continued. Petitions were heard. A widow asked for her mill back. A child was presented for blessing and stared up at Aldric with round, unguarded eyes, and Aldric blessed him, and the words came out in the ritual cadence, and underneath the cadence he was thinking: *this child will grow up in a kingdom where a good old man can be destroyed for doubting, and the boy on the throne will sign the order, and no one will ever know the boy was screaming.*

He did not know yet that he would sign the order. He would not know until the ink was dry.

That was how the Regent worked. Never in front of witnesses. Never anything that could be refused. The refusals happened in the missing hours, and by the time Aldric arrived — by the time *he* arrived, wherever *he* had been — the thing was done, and the choice was not offered to him but reported to him, and the reporting always came wrapped in such perfect, suffocating care.

---

The order was brought to him at dusk, in the small council chamber, where the windows faced west and the light came in low and red as an open wound.

Chancellor Merek laid the document on the desk before him. Merek was a narrow man with a scholar's stoop and a face like a closed book, and he performed his duties with the flawless neutrality of a man who had survived three reigns by ensuring his face communicated nothing whatsoever. He unrolled the parchment. Seals hung from it like ripe fruit.

"By order of the Crown," Merek read, in a voice sanded smooth of all texture, "Lord Edmun Cassel, Minister of the Interior Records, having demonstrated corruption of intent and disloyalty to the throne, is stripped of office, title, and estate, and remanded to the custody of the Inquisition for examination. Effective immediately upon royal assent."

Aldric read the words. They swam. He read them again.

"Examination," he said. "What does examination mean?"

Merek's face did not change. "It means what it has always meant, Your Majesty."

"It means they take things out of him." Aldric's voice was quiet. He was surprised by how quiet it was. "They take his memories. Drop by drop. Until there's nothing left that remembers why any of this mattered."

Merek said nothing. In the silence, Aldric became aware of the sunset pouring through the west windows, gilding the parchment, gilding Merek's hands, gilding the pen lying beside the document — a white-quilled thing, ceremonial, used only for orders of state. The light made everything beautiful. That was the trick of this room, he realized. Someone had designed it deliberately, centuries ago, so that the worst decisions would be made in the most forgiving light.

*Sign it,* said the crown.

"I want to know what he did." Aldric kept his eyes on the parchment. If he looked up, Merek would see whatever was happening in his face, and Merek reported faces. Merek reported everything. "Specifically. What did Lord Cassel do?"

*He doubted,* the Regent said, with the serene simplicity of a parent explaining weather to a child. *Doubt is the seed. Everything else grows from it. You've seen what happens to kingdoms that let doubt take root — your tutors showed you the histories. The succession wars. The Interregnum. Forty years of blood because one regent hesitated and hesitation is a door and doors admit anything.*

"He's served this family for fifty years."

*Which is precisely why he is dangerous. Loyalty that old becomes ownership, Aldric. He thinks the throne belongs partly to him. Men like Cassel don't betray thrones. They inherit them, one memorandum at a time.* A pause, and then, gentler, the gentleness that was worst of all: *You're shaking. I can feel it. Stop reading and sign. It's only ink. It's only a name, and the name is yours either way — with the signature or without it, the order stands, because I am the crown and the crown is the law and you, my dear boy, are the hand I write with. Sign, and it's done cleanly, with dignity, and Cassel goes quietly to a comfortable cell and a peaceful examination. Refuse, and I must do this through other instruments, and other instruments are never gentle. I am offering you mercy. His, and yours. Take it.*

Aldric picked up the pen.

He told himself, later — he would tell himself many times, in the nights that followed, turning it over like a coin he hoped would come up different — that he was choosing the lesser harm. That a clean order signed was kinder than a dirty order enforced. That his signature was a wound he could control rather than a wound inflicted. These arguments had a shape. They had weight. They fit together. And that was exactly what should have warned him, because the Regent built its arguments the way it built memories: seamless, load-bearing, impossible to feel the joins.

The nib touched parchment.

He signed. *Aldric*, in the careful hand his tutors had drilled into him, the letters upright and disciplined, the final flourish that his writing master had called *confident* and that Aldric had privately always thought looked like a bird trying to fly off the page and being pulled back down.

The ink gleamed wet in the sunset. He set down the pen, and his hand — his own hand, the traitor at the end of his own arm — left a smear across the bottom edge, because it was shaking after all, shaking badly, and the Chancellor pretended not to notice, and the pretending was its own kind of signature.

"So witnessed," Merek said, and blotted the page, and rolled it, and carried it away, and the red light followed him out like something leaking.

Aldric sat alone in the council chamber as the sun went down. He did not move for a long time. The candles were lit around him by servants who did not look at him, or perhaps looked at him and saw nothing worth seeing — a boy-king at a desk, still as furniture. When the room was fully dark except for the candle-flames, he looked down at his right hand, at the ink in the creases of his fingers, and he understood something with a clarity that felt less like thought than like a bone breaking:

This morning he had woken with ink on his hands and no memory of signing. Tonight he had signed while awake, while watching, while *choosing* — or while wearing the shape of choosing, which amounted to the same thing in the eyes of everyone who would ever learn of it. History would not record the screaming. History would record the signature. And the Regent had known that from the beginning. That was why it needed his hand at all. Not because it lacked the power. Because it lacked the *legitimacy*, and legitimacy was a thing that could only be borrowed from living flesh, and it had chosen his flesh when he was nineteen years old and drowning in grief, and it had never once, in four years, given him back to himself.

*I'm proud of you,* the crown murmured, settling against his brow as the chamberlain came to bear it back to its cushion. *That was difficult, and you did it anyway. That's what kingship is. One day you'll understand how much I've spared you.*

Aldric walked back to his chambers through corridors lit by lamps that burned with a greenish flame — enchanted, all of them, every flame in the palace, so that no shadow anywhere could gather unseen. He passed guards who saluted. He passed tapestries depicting his ancestors, their faces worn blank by smoke. He passed a window, and beyond the glass the city of Vael Corren lay spread out under the early stars, ten thousand roofs, ten thousand lives, and somewhere among them — though he did not know it yet, could not have imagined it — two strangers were moving toward him along roads he had never traveled, carrying a dead woman's map and a stolen key, coming to tell him that the thing on his head could be removed.

In his chambers, he barred the door, which he was not supposed to do, and which no one corrected anymore, because the Regent had decided long ago that a king's small rebellions were healthy — pressure valves, it called them, *let him bar his door, let him skip his meals, let him keep his little secrets; a host that struggles gently stays alive longer.*

At his desk, by candlelight, Aldric opened the drawer where he hid the things the Regent did not read. There was not much in it. A childhood carving of a horse, half-finished, from the year his hands had still been allowed to make useless things. A letter from a girl he'd loved at fifteen, before love became a security risk. And beneath these, wrapped in oilcloth, flat and heavy as a sleeping animal —

His father's ring. Not the signet; the *other* ring, the plain iron band that King Maeren had worn on his right hand and that the physicians had sworn was buried with him. Aldric had stolen it from the funerary vault two years ago, in another of his pressure-valve rebellions, and he had never told himself why. He had simply needed it near.

He turned it in the candlelight. Iron, unadorned, scratched with use. And on the inner curve, where only a wearer's flesh would ever press against it, a line of letters so small they might have been scratches:

*If the crown loves you, run.*

Aldric closed his fist around the ring until the edges bit.

Tomorrow, he decided — tomorrow he would find out what his father had known. Tomorrow he would begin looking for the letters, the papers, the hidden correspondence that a frightened king must surely have left somewhere in this palace of ten thousand rooms. Tomorrow.

Outside, far to the south, over hills he could not see, a storm was gathering that had no business gathering in a cloudless season. He felt it in his teeth. He felt it, faintly, in the iron ring.

The Regent felt it too. And for the first time in four years, the voice in Aldric's skull went quiet — not calm, but *listening*.
