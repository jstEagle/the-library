## 15. Inquisitors at the Inn

The inn at Millharrow was called the Drowned Ox, and it was the kind of place that had drowned long ago and simply hadn't told anyone.

Its timbers were black with old smoke. Its floors creaked in dialects no map recorded. The ale tasted of the barrel it had been born in and would die in, and its keeper — a woman named Hesta with forearms like mooring ropes and a face that forgave nothing — watered it without apology because she knew her customers came for the roof more than the drink.

Gideon liked it immediately. It was honest about being a lie.

He sat in the corner with his back to the wall, as he had every night for six years in another life, and watched Wren work the room.

She was very good. That was the thing he kept forgetting and then remembering with a small cold shock: she wasn't performing the friendly traveler, she *was* one, seamless, a woman whose laughter fit her face like it had been fitted there by a smith. She'd charmed the story of the mill levy out of Hesta in under an hour. She'd learned that the toll bridge east of town washed out every spring, that the reeve's son owed gambling debts to men who collected with knives, that three peddlers had passed through this week heading south and only one of them had bothered to pay for his bed.

"And?" Gideon said when she slid back into the seat across from him, carrying two bowls of something brown and steaming.

"And nobody's asking after us." She set a bowl in front of him. "Nobody's looking sideways. Either we've outrun them or they're better than I think they are."

"Which do you believe?"

"Neither." She tore her bread in half. "I believe we got lucky, luck is a loan not a gift, and I'd like to be on the road before whoever holds our debt comes calling."

They had been in Millharrow two nights. The first keystone rode against Gideon's chest in a sling of oiled leather beneath his shirt — the bellfounder's daughter's drowned bronze, still faintly warm, still humming a note too low for ears, so that some part of him was always listening to himself. He had stopped sleeping properly days ago. The stone didn't keep him awake exactly; it just made sleep feel like leaving a door open.

The tinker arrived while the light was going out of the windows.

Gideon noticed him the way you notice weather — a shift in the pressure of the room, heads turning, Hesta's voice lifting into a welcome pitched half a tone brighter than her usual. A broad man, red-faced, with a pack that clinked like a chandelier in a windstorm: pots and pans and buckles and little tin whistles strung across his chest like medals. He filled the doorway the way cheerful men do, taking up all of it and most of the air.

"Evening, evening! Cold out there, eh? Cold enough to crack a brass monkey!" He laughed at his own joke before anyone else could decline the honor. "Pots mended, blades honed, kettles un-kettled! And a pint of whatever's darkest, landlady, for the road-worn hero of the highways!"

Wren's boot found Gideon's ankle under the table. A small pressure. *Watch.*

But there was nothing to watch, not really. The tinker — Tam, he said his name was, Tam Cobble — worked his way along the benches selling small wonders to people who needed none. A whistle for the reeve's boy. A buckle for a carter. He complimented the stew. He complained about the roads. He asked, idly, over the rim of his fourth pint, whether the southern road was flooded this year, and Hesta said what Hesta said to everyone: *ask the river.*

It was ordinary. It was so ordinary that Gideon let his shoulders come down from around his ears, and hated himself a little for how easily ten years of vigilance could be talked loose by a man with tin whistles.

Later — much later, when the fire had burned down to a red eye and most of the common room had staggered up to bed — Tam Cobble settled onto the bench beside them with the uninvited confidence of the very drunk.

"You two," he said, pointing at each of them with a finger that required both hands to aim, "are running."

"We're traveling," Wren said pleasantly.

"You're *running*." He leaned in, breath a wall of ale and honey-spirits. "I can always tell. I run too. Been running thirty years. From what, you ask? Debts. A wife. Two wives. A magistrate in Corrowdale who has my face drawn on a piece of paper he shows to horses." He giggled. "Running folk know running folk. It's a fellowship. Secret handshake is we don't ask questions."

"We appreciate the fellowship," Wren said, "and we'll take the secret part."

But the tinker had latched on, the way lonely drunk men do, and he talked. About the road south. About the ford at Two Rivers where the water ran fast enough to drown a horse. About the inquisition checkpoints on the king's highway since spring, new men in grey robes, asking travelers strange questions — *not the usual ones*, he said, leaning close as though sharing state secrets, *not where-from and where-to, but have-you-dreamed-anything-strange, and do-your-hands-ever-shake* —

Gideon's hands were still.

He made sure of it. He looked down at them lying flat on the scarred table, and they were perfectly, deliberately still, and Tam Cobble was watching them anyway with eyes that were not nearly as drunk as the rest of him.

"Well," the tinker said softly, straightening up. All the slur fell out of his voice between one sentence and the next, like a coin through a torn pocket. "Well now. Lovely talking with you both."

He gathered his clinking pack. He paid his tab with exact coins. At the door he turned back and raised his tankard in salute, grinning his broad red grin, and said:

"Safe travels, friends. Hope the road stays quiet behind you."

Then he was gone into the dark, and the night swallowed the jingle of his pots whole.

* * *

Wren didn't sleep either. They sat up in their separate beds in the narrow room above the stable, fully dressed, listening to the building settle.

"He was a scout," she said finally, in the dark. "Not an inquisitor. Wrong build. They recruit lookouts locally — people who owe them, or fear them, or hate their neighbors enough. He'll report to someone at dawn."

"Then we leave now."

"Now we leave *separately*," she said. "You go north through the orchards, cross the millrace above the wheel, pick up the drover's track on the far side. I go out the way we came, south, loud — I want any eyes on me, not you. We meet at the standing stones past Harrow Fell, tomorrow sundown. You remember the—"

"I remember."

"Gideon." Her voice changed. He heard her sit up, heard the straw tick sigh. "If they take me, you don't come back. You don't negotiate, you don't trade, you don't get clever. You take the stone and you go. That's not bravado, it's arithmetic. One of us is replaceable and it isn't you."

"That's not true."

"It's completely true and you know it. You're the storm-caller. Sera died putting that map in your hands. There are maybe four people alive who can finish what she started and I'm not sure I'm one of them, but I'm certain you are." A pause. In the darkness her voice came smaller. "So promise me. Promise me you'll run."

And here is the truth of Gideon Dunmore, which he carried like a second stone under his ribs: he had already run once, from the worst moment of his life, and he knew exactly what it cost, and he knew — even then, even in that dark room smelling of straw and horse — that if the moment came he would run again, because running was the one thing his body had ever learned better than the storm.

"I promise," he said, and the lie sat between them, patient as the keystone against his chest.

* * *

They went down separately, as planned, an hour before the grey hour. The stable yard was silvered with frost. His breath hung around him like gauze.

He was at the orchard wall, one hand on the cold stone, when the world went wrong.

It began with the dogs. There were no dogs in Millharrow worth the name — Hesta kept one ancient hound that slept twenty hours a day — but suddenly every farmyard within a mile was barking at once, a ragged chorus rising all around the sleeping town, and Gideon's skin understood before his mind did. Animals heard things coming. He had grown up among goats that screamed before earthquakes.

Then the lanterns. Not one. Not scattered. A ring of them, spaced with terrible neatness around the inn, around the orchard, around the lane — closing like a hand.

Grey robes. Six of them, eight, more, moving without hurry, because they did not need hurry. The one in front carried a censer that smoked a slow violet smoke, and where the smoke drifted, the frost on the ground *melted*, curling away in wisps, and Gideon felt the pull of it like a fishhook behind his sternum — it was drinking the residue of magic, tasting the air for traces, and it had already turned, questing, toward him.

Toward the stone.

*Run.*

His body made the decision the way it makes you blink. He went over the orchard wall low and fast and dropped into the black rows of bare apple trees, and behind him he heard Hesta's voice rise in outrage — gods bless her, she was arguing with them, actually shouting at armed inquisitors about paying for broken fences — and then a sound he would hear for the rest of his life however long that was: the crack of a staff against a door, Wren's window, the second-floor room, splinters, and her voice, sharp and clear and furious:

"—you could just knock, you absolute—"

Then rope, and the sound of her fighting, which was considerable, and then the sound of her losing, which was worse.

Gideon stopped.

That was the thing he would never forgive himself for, not the running itself but the stopping — three heartbeats in the frozen mud between apple trees, turned back toward the inn, the violet smoke rising, the ring of lanterns, eight men and a censer and iron nets and him with one hand full of lightning and no plan and Wren's voice in his head saying *arithmetic, arithmetic, one of us is replaceable* —

The censer swung toward the orchard.

He ran.

He ran the way he had run ten years ago out of a valley of ash, the way his body remembered without his permission, low and silent through the tree rows with the keystone hammering its buried note against his breastbone as if screaming. Behind him the inn door banged open. Someone shouted — not his name, they didn't know his name, they shouted *the orchard, the orchard* — and a whistle sounded, one of Tam Cobble's tin whistles, God help him, three sharp notes splitting the freezing night, and answering notes came back from the dark on both flanks.

They had posted listeners. The cheerful fool had sung them all into position over pints for two days.

A shape rose out of the tree rows ahead of him — grey robe, staff leveled, the violet light of the censer washing its hood into a skull's hollow — and Gideon did the last thing he wanted and the first thing that worked. He opened the door he had kept shut for ten years, just a crack, just a seam, and let a little of the storm see out.

Lightning is not a weapon you hold. It is a debt you call in. It came down the inside of his arm and out of his palm in a white sheet that missed the inquisitor entirely and struck the apple tree beside him, and the tree exploded — bark and burning wood and a century of rings blown outward — and the concussion took the robed man off his feet and took Gideon's hearing with it and took, for one endless instant, everything else too: thought, guilt, Wren's voice, all of it, wiped white.

When the world came back, it came back ringing.

He was running again. He didn't remember starting. The orchard was behind him, the millrace ahead, and his left hand was scorched pink and shaking, shaking, shaking — the tremor, the old betrayer, home at last. Behind him, dimly, voices. Someone saying *let him go, follow at distance, the stone will lead us to the rest.* Someone saying, in a voice like a key turning in a lock: *"The girl first. The Regent wants her memories read before they fade."*

*The girl.*

He crossed the millrace on the slick planking above the wheel with the spray icing his boots, and he did not look back, and the not-looking-back was the hardest work he had ever done, harder than the lightning, harder than ten years of burying the storm. Because some traitorous part of him wanted to look. Wanted to see her being dragged out in irons so that some final image could brand itself into him, as if witnessing it were a form of loyalty, as if watching her taken were anything other than the last and worst convenience of a man who needed a reason to keep moving instead of turning around.

He gave himself none. He ran.

* * *

Dawn found him on the drover's track above Harrow Fell, walking because running was finished, running had done its work, and now there was only the other thing — the thing that walked beside a man all day with its hand on the back of his neck.

The fells were brown and bare and indifferent. The standing stones stood where standing stones stand, patient as debts, and he reached them hours before sundown because he had run from Millharrow like a coward and cowardice is fast.

He sat down against the largest stone, the one shaped like a bent thumb, and took out the keystone and held it in his blistered hand.

Warm. Humming. Unbothered. Bronze doesn't judge. That was the cruelest thing about it — it simply sang its low drowned note against his palm, steady as a heartbeat, having been dredged up out of a ghost-guarded lake by two strangers who had promised each other, in whatever unspoken currency such promises are made, that neither would be left behind.

*I promise,* he said to the empty fells. To her. The wind took it and spent it.

He inventoried himself the way his father had taught him, back before, back when inventory was a comfort: one keystone. One map, Sera's bloodstained map, soft as cloth now from handling. Half a loaf, a knife, a waterskin, eleven coppers. One ally, captured. One promise, broken before the frost had melted off it. One talent, used again, and God forgive him, when the lightning had come down his arm there had been a single white instant — gone now, thank all mercies gone — when it had felt like *coming home.*

He put his head back against the stone and looked at the sky going orange over the ridge and did not cry, because crying would have meant he believed there was time for it.

Because here was the arithmetic Wren hadn't done, the sum she'd gotten wrong: she had assumed he was valuable and she was spare. But Wren knew the archive, knew the thieves' roads, knew Sera Voss, knew what the Regent was and how it fed. Wren was the map's living margin, all the notes written between the lines — and she was now in a questioner's cell with her memories being drawn out drop by drop, and every drop was a route, a name, a keystone, a death.

He couldn't rescue her. Eight inquisitors, iron nets, a censer that drank magic — he had seen the size of it. He was one burnt-handed fugitive with eleven coppers.

But he knew someone who owned smugglers' roads and grudges, someone whose name Sera had scrawled in the map's border in letters small enough to hide: a man who moved anything anywhere for a price, and whose price, the courier had noted in her tight dying hand, was always *negotiable, but never cheap.*

Brannoc.

Gideon looked at the standing stones a long time, at the space where sundown would come and she would not.

Then he stood up, tucked the singing stone back against his chest, and turned south — toward the coast, toward the salt-flats, toward the dirty coin that bought justice — and began, for the third time in his life, to run toward the thing instead of away from it.

Behind him, on the highest point of Harrow Fell, a crow lifted off a stone and wheeled once, twice, and flew the same direction he did.

He tried not to think about what it might be following.

He failed.
