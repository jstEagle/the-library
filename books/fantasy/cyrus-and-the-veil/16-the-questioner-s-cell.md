## 16. The Questioner's Cell

The cell smelled of cold iron and old prayers.

Wren knew this because smell was the first thing they let you keep, and the last thing they took. The inquisitors of the Regent worked in a careful order — she had watched them work on others, back when she was only a thief among thieves and the Grey Order was a story told to frighten apprentices away from certain rooftops. First they took your sight, or convinced you it had never been yours. Then your name. Then the small warm things: your mother's kitchen, the weight of a cat on your chest at dawn, the taste of rain on a summer street. They went slowly. They were paid to go slowly.

She had been in the cell for three days now, though three days had stopped meaning anything after the first night. There were no windows. A single lamp burned behind a grille in the wall, hooded so that its light fell in a thin grey seam across the floor, and by that seam Wren measured time the only way she could: by counting how many times her own heartbeat made her flinch.

That was the other thing about the Questioner's craft. It wasn't pain, not mostly. Pain was for soldiers and stubborn kings. For thieves, for rememberers, for people whose whole trade was what they carried behind their eyes, the inquisitors used thirst.

Her mouth had been dry since they brought her here. Not tortured-dry — simply dry, the way stone is dry, the way a page left in the sun is dry. Somewhere beneath the floor a machine hummed, patient as digestion, drawing the water out of her one memory at a time. She could feel which memories it wanted. It pulled at them like fingers plucking threads from a hem: *the quarry road, the quarry road, the quarry road.*

She gave it nothing. Or rather — she gave it something else.

* * *

They came for her on the fourth day, if her count held, which it probably didn't. The door opened without sound, which was somehow worse than a shriek of hinges would have been, and two grey-robed acolytes lifted her from the cot and set her in the chair.

The chair was the worst piece of craftsmanship she had ever sat in, and Wren had sat in thrones built as jokes and gallows built as art. It was plain pine, deliberately, insultingly plain, with straps that weren't needed because no one who reached this room ever struggled anymore. Above it, suspended on a brass arm like a lamp over a surgeon's table, hung the extraction apparatus: a funnel of dark glass narrowing to a needle-thin spout, and inside the glass, a single bead of liquid that caught the lamplight and held it, trembling, silver-grey, like a tear that had learned to think.

Memory, distilled. She'd stolen enough of it in her life to know the look.

The Questioner came in after the acolytes withdrew, and Wren studied him the way she studied locks: patiently, looking for the flaw.

He was younger than she expected. That unsettled her more than cruelty would have. He had soft hands, ink-stained at the nails, and the mild face of a man who did accounts. His robes were clean grey wool; his voice, when he spoke, was the voice of a tutor asking after homework.

"Good morning," he said. "Or good evening. I'm afraid I've lost track myself."

"Then we're even," Wren said. Her voice cracked on the second word, dry as the rest of her, but she kept it level. "I lost track around the time your colleagues put a bag over my head."

"Yes." He settled into the chair opposite hers — there was always a second chair; the theatre of conversation mattered to these people — and folded his ink-stained hands. "I'm sorry about that. My colleagues believe fear loosens the mind. In my experience it only sours the vintage." He smiled apologetically. "Memories taken under terror keep badly. They cloud. I prefer my subjects calm."

"I'm delighted to be preferred."

"You may call me Brother Liss." He said it lightly, as if offering a name at a wedding rather than in a cellar beneath a cathedral. "And you are Wren. Of Ashmarket, formerly of the Lowstairs, formerly of nowhere in particular. A thief. A *memory-thief* — which interests me greatly, I confess. You're the first of your trade I've had in this room." His eyes were pale hazel, almost colourless, and they rested on her with genuine, horrible warmth. "You understand better than most what's about to happen. That should make this easier for both of us."

"Should it?"

"You know that memories aren't sacred. You've sold them. Bought them. Cut them out of sleeping heads with a needle thinner than regret." He tilted his head. "So you know they're only things. And things can be collected."

Wren looked past him at the bead of silver trembling in the glass funnel, and thought of Sera Voss's vial sitting on a shelf in the Archive of Stolen Things, labelled in a clerk's neat hand, priced like a bolt of cloth. She thought of Gideon running into the dark with the bellfounder's keystone against his chest while she stayed behind to buy his escape with a fistful of her own past — the harbour at Corvess, the gull-cries, her sister's laugh coming off the water. She had traded those to the inquisitor-trap in Ashmarket like a purse of coin, and had told herself it was nothing, that memories were only things, that she was the one thing they couldn't take because she was the taking of them.

Now she sat in the chair, and the lie sat beside her, and neither of them fit comfortably.

"Ask your questions, Brother Liss," she said. "I'm very calm."

* * *

The machine began with a sound like a struck wineglass, felt more than heard, a high sweet tone that lived in the teeth. The bead in the funnel shivered, stretched, and a thread of silver ran down the needle-spicot and touched the skin below Wren's ear — light as a fly's foot.

And the thirst bloomed.

Not pain. Worse than pain: absence. It was the feeling of reaching for a word and finding the space where it had been swept clean, except the word was a day of her life, an hour, a colour. The thread drew taut. Somewhere in the glass above her, the bead grew fatter by a drop.

Brother Liss watched her with his tutor's patience and asked, softly:

"The man you travelled with. The storm-caller. Where is he going?"

"I don't know."

"Mm." The thread pulsed. The thirst deepened, and with it came a small mercy she hadn't anticipated: whatever the machine drew from her, it drew *blindly*, a random sip from the well. She felt it take — and felt herself lose — a morning. Just a morning. Rain on a tin roof in some city she'd slept in years ago, the particular green smell of wet slate, a boy selling roasted chestnuts calling out in a voice like a cracked bell. Gone. Not dimmed. *Unhappened.* There was a smooth-edged hole where it had been, and the hole ached like a missing tooth, and she understood with sudden perfect clarity why the old thieves called this machine the Tithe.

"They say you're good," Brother Liss continued. "Sera Voss trained you, before she turned apostate. She wrote of you, you know. In her reports, before her reports became treasons. 'My apprentice has quick hands and a quicker tongue. She will be trouble to someone someday.'" He smiled again, gently. "She was right. So let's not waste each other's time. The storm-caller carries a stone. Grey, faceted, warm to the touch. Where did he take it?"

"I don't know," Wren said. "He left me in Ashmarket. I woke up in your people's cart."

"That's true," Liss said, and she heard the small satisfaction in it. "That part's true. Which is useful, because it means I can tell when you lie. Truth tastes one way in the extract, Wren. Falsehood tastes another. Sweet and then bitter, like honey over ash." He leaned forward, and the lamplight through the grille caught the edge of his face. "You've been feeding my colleagues false memories since they put you in that bag, haven't you? Little gifts planted along your surface, like decoy coins for a cutpurse. The harbour at Corvess. Your sister. Very pretty work, incidentally. Almost professional."

Her heart stumbled. She kept her face still, because stillness was the last craft she had left in this room.

"But here is what your mentor never taught you," Liss went on, rising, walking a slow circle around the chair, his voice dropping into something almost kind. "The machine doesn't take what's offered. It takes what's *wanted*. And what it wants is the quarry road. Every hour you sit in that cell, it pulls a little harder toward it, and every decoy you plant costs you a real memory to feed it. You are spending yourself, drop by drop, to guard a secret." He stopped in front of her. "How many mornings do you have left, thief? How many sisters' faces?"

"I don't know," Wren said, and heard her voice shake, and hated it.

"No," he agreed softly. "But I do. Shall I tell you what happens to the ones who hold out? They leave this room whole-shaped but hollow-filled. We dress them, thank them, and send them home to families who weep at their door because their daughter looks at them kindly and knows them not at all. The body remembers the shape of love long after the content is drained. It's quite tragic. It's also entirely unnecessary." He crouched to her eye level, and his pale eyes were full of something that in any other face she might have called compassion. "Tell me where he's gone, and you walk out of here with everything you ever were. Every ugly beautiful ordinary morning. That's the whole bargain. One road, one stone, and all of yourself returned."

The thirst was a desert now. The bead above her hung heavy, fat with her spent hours. And Wren thought — she couldn't help thinking — of her sister's face, which was already fraying at the edges, the harbour gone, the gulls gone, the laugh reduced to a fact she knew rather than a sound she remembered. *Maren. Her name is Maren. She has — she had — brown hair. She laughs. Laughed. Like this—*

Like what?

The hole where the sound should have been ached worse than the thirst.

And in that ache, Wren found the thing she had been looking for since they threw the bag over her head. Not hope. Not courage. Something meaner and more durable than either.

Because Brother Liss was wrong about one thing, and it was the one thing that mattered, and Sera had taught her true after all.

Memories weren't sacred. Memories were *things*, and things could be copied, forged, counterfeited, planted — and a thief who understood that was never truly robbed. She had spent her whole life learning that a person was not what they remembered. A person was what they *chose* when the remembering was taken away. Sera, dying in the mud at the ford, had chosen a stranger. Gideon, exiled ten years, had chosen to walk back into the storm. And Wren, strapped in a pine chair beneath a glass teat dripping with her own spent self, chose the quarry road — chose it with nothing left but her will, which was the only possession the Tithe could never reach, because it wasn't stored anywhere. It *was* her.

"I don't know where he's gone," she said again. And then she smiled, dry-lipped, and gave him the gift she'd been saving. "But I'll tell you something true, free of charge, since you like truth. Your machine wants the quarry road. Fine. Let it drink the quarry road."

Liss went very still. "What?"

"It's a big country, Brother. There are hundreds of quarry roads. Stone comes out of the ground all over this realm — your crown builds cathedrals on it." She let her smile widen, tasting blood where her lips had split. "Drink deep. I'll even help. I have a wonderful memory of a quarry road. White dust, wheel-ruts, a shrine to some forgotten saint. Northwest of Ashmarket, three days' ride. Shall I show you?"

And she opened herself — actually opened, the way a lock opens, the way she'd once opened other people — and pushed forward the memory she had spent two sleepless nights in this cell building, stitching it from scraps the Tithe hadn't wanted: a real road she'd really walked, re-graded, re-signed, re-peopled, dressed in the specific gravity of truth. Real dust in it. Real blisters. A fake shrine she gave a real saint's name, culled from a chapel she'd burgled at fifteen. And threaded through it, buried where a dowsing rod would find it, the warm grey gleam of a keystone — drawn from memory, drawn beautifully, because she had held the real one in her own two hands and her hands never forgot anything they'd stolen.

Brother Liss stared at her for a long moment.

Then he laughed — short, startled, genuinely delighted — and it frightened her more than rage would have.

"Oh," he said, standing, smoothing his robes. "*Oh,* you're magnificent. Sera undersold you." He turned to the grille in the wall. "Bring me a map of the northwestern roads," he called, "and a rider. Fast horse."

* * *

They gave her water after that.

She hadn't expected them to. But Liss was a craftsman, and craftsmen don't sour their own vintage, and besides — she understood now, watching him make notes in a leather book with his ink-stained fingers, that he had entered a new phase of his work. She was no longer a problem. She was a specimen.

"You should know," he said conversationally, while an acolyte held a cup to her lips and she drank like the animal she'd become, "that I'll verify the road before I act on it. If it's false — if I find so much as a shrine that isn't where you put it — I'll come back down here, and I won't ask questions anymore. There are older brothers than me, Wren. They lack my patience." He capped his inkwell. "But if it's true — if your storm-caller is really holed up in the old white quarry northwest of Ashmarket—" he paused at the door, and gave her a look of such benign, scholarly pleasure that her stomach turned over, "—then you've done the Regent a great service. And the Regent rewards service. Ask anyone whose loyalty you can still remember."

The door closed. The lamp behind the grille guttered. The machine's wineglass tone faded to silence, and Wren sat alone in the dark with her cup of water and counted what remained of herself.

*Maren. Brown hair. Laughs like—*

Like nothing. Like a hole shaped like laughing.

She drank the water to the bottom. Then, because there was no one to see, because defiance needed no audience to be itself, she set the cup down on the floor with exaggerated care and said, clearly, to the empty cell:

"Northwest. Three days. Enjoy the ride."

It was false, all of it — the shrine, the saint, the gleam of stone in the dust. She had built the decoy out of her own bones and it had cost her a morning of rain and a harbour and half a sister, and it might cost her everything else when Liss verified it, and none of that mattered. What mattered was this: somewhere in this fortress, right now, an inquisitor was saddling a fast horse to ride three days in the wrong direction. Somewhere Gideon was moving, and every hour the Grey Order chased white dust and a forged shrine was an hour he kept.

She had given them a map. Let them follow it off the edge of the world.

Wren closed her eyes and began, methodically, to rebuild her sister from the facts that remained. Brown hair. Older by two years. Left-handed. Afraid of deep water, loved it anyway. She laid the facts down like stones in a wall, and where the mortar of memory was missing she pressed in invention, and told herself — knowing it for a lie, telling it anyway, because a thief's whole soul was the lies she refused to stop telling —

*told herself that it was nearly the same thing.*

Above, muffled through fathoms of stone, she heard the fortress wake. Boots in corridors. A gate grinding open. Hooves.

Going northwest.

* * *

She did not hear the second rider.

No one in the cell did. But an hour after the first courier clattered out through the north postern, a second horse left by the south gate, unremarked, carrying a rider in undyed wool who rode hard through the pre-dawn streets and out along the river road — southeast, toward the flooded quarry and the drowned bell-tower where a ghost guarded a truth.

Brother Liss had believed her. That had been the plan.

But Liss reported to someone, and that someone had read Sera Voss's final reports before they became treasons, and knew that the dead woman had written one line about her apprentice that the archives still kept: *She lies best in the direction she wants you to run.*

The rider rode southeast, and did not look back, and the fortress gates closed behind him without a sound.
