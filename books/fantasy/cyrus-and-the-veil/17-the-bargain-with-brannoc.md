## 17. The Bargain with Brannoc

The city of Saltcross smelled of tar and rot and money — the last one strongest, though it tried hardest to hide. Gideon had been in the city two days, sleeping in doorways and walking streets he did not know, asking questions he could not afford to ask, and every answer came back the same way: *You want Brannoc.*

He heard it from a fishwife gutting eels at dawn. He heard it from a beggar with no teeth who took his copper and gave him nothing else but that name. He heard it from a boy selling roasted chestnuts who looked at him as if he were simple. *You want Brannoc. Everyone wants Brannoc. That's why nobody gets him.*

So Gideon walked down to the harbor where the smugglers' quarter began — you could tell by the change in the light, the way the lanterns hung lower and burned redder, the way the shadows between buildings seemed to have been arranged on purpose — and he stood at the mouth of a crooked alley that three separate people had described to him, each description contradicting the others, and he waited.

Waiting was something the valley had taught him well. Ten years of watching weather come over the ridge, ten years of standing still while goats decided things, ten years of letting the world arrive at its own pace because rushing it would only wake the storm sleeping under his skin. He leaned against wet stone and watched gulls argue over a fish head and let the hours pass through him like water through a net.

It was full dark when the alley finally spoke.

"You're the one asking after Sera Voss."

The voice came from above. Gideon looked up and saw nothing at first — then a shape resolved out of the roofline, a man sitting on a windowsill with his boots dangling, eating an apple. He was broad without being heavy, gray at the temples, dressed in clothes that wanted to look poor and failed because the cloth was too fine to fool anyone. A knife lay across his knees with the casualness of a cat.

"I'm the one she died in front of," Gideon said.

The man stopped chewing.

"That's a strange way to put it." He turned the apple in his hand, considering it. "Most people would say she died helping them. Or died for them. Died *in front of* makes it sound like you stood there and watched."

"I did stand there and watch."

"Honest." The man dropped the apple core into the gutter and swung down from the sill, landing soft for a big man. Up close he had a face like a coastline — weathered, cut by channels, hard to read from any distance. "Honesty's rare enough in this quarter that I'll give it credit. Come inside before someone decides you're interesting."

* * *

The room behind the unmarked door was warm, lamplit, and full of things that should not have fit together: charts of coastlines next to a child's wooden horse, a rack of wine bottles beside a shelf of ledgers bound in green leather, a caged songbird that sang anyway, as if it had made its peace with captivity long ago. The man poured two cups of something dark and pushed one across the table.

"Brannoc," he said, though Gideon hadn't asked.

"Gideon Dunmore."

"I know." Brannoc drank. "Sera wrote about you. Not by name — she wasn't stupid — but there was a letter, two years back. *I've found the valley-man,* she said. *He tends goats. He pretends the sky doesn't scare him.*" Brannoc set the cup down. "She thought you were funny. She thought a lot of people were funny. It was how she loved them."

Gideon's throat tightened around nothing. He had known Sera Voss for less than an hour. She had bled out against his hands in the mud at the ford, her wounds smoking as if something inside her were burning outward, and her last act in the world had been to make him an heir he never asked to be. And now here was a stranger telling him she'd written letters about him, that she'd found him worth two years of remembering, and the grief that hit him was disproportionate and enormous — grief not just for her but for every version of knowing her he would never have.

"She talked about you too," Gideon managed. "At the end. She said—" He stopped. He couldn't remember exactly what she'd said; the memory had gone strange and bright-edged, the way memories do when they're the last thing a person gives you. "She trusted you."

"She shouldn't have." Brannoc smiled without any humor in it. "But yes. Twenty years. She ran cargo for me before she ever ran secrets for the crown. Best courier I ever had, because she understood that the fastest route is worthless if you can't remember why you're carrying the thing." He leaned forward. "So. You've got her map. I know you do — my people saw you flash it at the harbormaster's office like an idiot, trying to find a route inland. You want to get somewhere fast and quiet, and you don't know this country, and you've got no friends. Which means you need me."

"The map isn't mine to trade."

"Everything's yours to trade once the owner's dead. That's not cynicism, that's inheritance law." Brannoc spread his hands. "Here's what I think. I think Sera was carrying that map to someone, and whoever killed her didn't want it delivered. I think the grey-robes are crawling up and down the river road looking for it. And I think you're a valley-man with storm in his blood who hasn't the first idea how to spend what he's holding. Am I wrong?"

Gideon said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Good. Then we understand each other, and I can make you an offer." Brannoc pulled a rolled chart from beneath the ledgers and flattened it on the table — the same territory as Sera's map, drawn cruder, older. His finger traced a line west from Saltcross, up into hills marked only with a symbol like a broken crown. "Wren Halloway was taken four nights ago. Inquisitors. She's alive, or she was when my ears last worked, because they don't kill thieves quickly — a thief's head is a warehouse, and the Questioner likes to shop slow. If your friend is anywhere, she's at the Questioner's house outside Thornmere. Three days' ride, through country where every bridge has eyes."

"You know where she is." Gideon's voice came out flat and hard. "And you're going to sell me that information."

"I'm going to *trade* it. There's a difference, and the difference is what keeps men like me breathing." Brannoc tapped the chart. "My price is the map. Not the whole thing — I don't care about your keystones or your king or whatever oath is eating you. I want the route. Sera mapped a road through the borderlands that doesn't appear on any crown survey, and I've spent six years trying to buy it off her and she kept saying *when I'm done with it, Brannoc, when I'm done.*" His jaw tightened, briefly, the only crack in the merchant's calm. "Well. She's done. And I'll be damned if the Regent's hounds get it. So: the route comes to me, my guides take you to Thornmere, and my people pull Wren Halloway out of whatever cage they've built for her."

"Why?" Gideon asked. "Why help? What do you actually want?"

Brannoc looked at him for a long moment, and underneath the coastline face Gideon saw something tired and old surface, like a wreck showing at low tide.

"Sera Voss pulled me out of a burning warehouse when I was nineteen and too stupid to run. I've owed her twenty years. She never collected. Now she's dead, and the people who killed her are the same people who are going to eat that kingdom alive unless somebody stops them, and I am not somebody who stops things. I move them. But I can move *you*." He sat back. "That's the whole of it. Don't dress it up as loyalty. Loyalty's expensive and I'm a cheap man. Call it debt. Debts I understand."

* * *

They negotiated until the candle burned down to a stub. Brannoc drove a hard bargain the way water drives a millwheel — patiently, relentlessly, always finding the channel Gideon's resistance would naturally follow. Gideon surrendered the western half of Sera's map, tracing the route onto Brannoc's own chart with shaking hands while the smuggler-king watched every stroke like a hawk watching a field mouse cross open ground. He kept the eastern portion — the part that mattered, the part with the keystones and the king's city and the rite that was coming whether anyone was ready or not.

"There," Brannoc said at last, rolling up his copy. "Now you're poorer and I'm richer and we both feel worse. That's a fair trade in this quarter." He rose and fetched a leather satchel from a chest in the corner, dropping it on the table with a clink of coin and the smell of oiled steel. "Coin for the road. Two knives, since I'm guessing you carry yours like a farmer carries a hay hook. And a token — show this at the sign of the Black Eel in Thornmere, and the woman there will put you in touch with my crew. They'll be moving on the Questioner's house within the week."

"Within the week." Gideon's stomach dropped. "She's in their hands *now*. Every day—"

"Every day they take pieces of her. Yes. I know what the Questioner does; I've sold him enough of my enemies to know." Brannoc said it without flinching, and Gideon felt the moral floor tilt under him — this was the kind of man he was dealing with, a man who fed the machine and fought it in the same breath, and he was the best ally Gideon had. "Which is why you'll leave tonight, and why you'll go the way I tell you, and why you won't play the hero at any checkpoint along the road. Dead rescuers rescue no one. Sera taught me that too, in her way."

Gideon picked up the satchel. It was heavier than it should have been, or maybe everything was heavier now — the map, the knives, the name of a dead woman spoken aloud in a stranger's warm room. "One more question."

"Free of charge, then."

"At the ford. Whatever killed her — it wasn't men. Her wounds smoked from the inside. Do you know what does that?"

For the first time, Brannoc's composure flickered. He glanced at the caged bird, which had gone silent, its head cocked toward the window as if listening to something beyond the glass.

"I know what the grey-robes carry," he said quietly. "Regent-steel. Blades quenched in something that burns what it cuts — burns the *self*, not the flesh. It's how they take people apart without leaving marks anyone can prosecute." He met Gideon's eyes. "If Sera died of Regent-steel, then whatever she knew was worth killing a kingdom's best courier slowly and painfully. And if she gave it to you—" He stopped himself, shook his head. "No. Not my business. My business is routes and prices. Go save your thief, valley-man. Try not to die doing it; I hate writing refunds."

* * *

The guide Brannoc gave him was a girl of perhaps sixteen named Tally, all elbows and silence, who materialized at the Black Eel's back door with two mules and a face that suggested she'd rather be anywhere else. They rode out of Saltcross under a sky bruised purple at the edges, taking goat tracks that avoided every bridge and every toll and every pair of eyes the crown owned in the borderlands.

Gideon rode and did not sleep. The satchel bumped against his hip with every stride, and inside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the eastern half of Sera's map pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat. He kept thinking about the price. Not the coin — coin was easy, coin meant nothing, coin was just the shape that compromise took so you could hold it. He kept thinking about what he'd actually given away: a dead woman's work, handed to a man who moved contraband and sold enemies to the Questioner, purchased justice with dirty coin because clean coin wasn't on offer anywhere in this kingdom.

*Would Sera have minded?* He tried to imagine her — really imagine her, not the bloodied stranger at the ford but the woman who wrote letters about goat-tending valley-men and called them funny. He thought she would have laughed. He thought she would have said something like *the map was always meant to be spent, not kept,* and he thought that might be true and might be the lie he needed to sleep, except he wasn't sleeping.

Behind them, far to the east, the horizon pulsed once — faint, brief, like lightning trapped under the skin of the world. Tally saw it too. She reined in, squinted, and spat.

"Inquisitor signal-fire," she said. "Or a ward coming down. Either way, someone's hunting tonight." She clicked her tongue and the mules picked up their pace. "Ride faster, valley-man. Your thief doesn't have a week."

Gideon dug his heels in and rode into the dark, toward Thornmere, toward the Questioner's house, toward whatever was left of Wren Halloway to save — carrying a bought route, a borrowed crew, and the growing, sick certainty that everything he was about to do would be paid for by someone else.
