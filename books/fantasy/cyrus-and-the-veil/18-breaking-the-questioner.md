## 18. Breaking the Questioner

Gideon had never seen a man buy a city the way Brannoc bought this one.

It happened in the space of an afternoon, in a smoky back room above a dyer's shop where the wool hung in skeins of bruised purple and the air tasted of vinegar and iron. Brannoc sat with his boots on the table and his rings clicking against a cup of wine he never drank, and one by one the men came to him — gate wardens, a turnkey, a woman who kept the lamps along the prison wall lit at night. Each left heavier in the pocket and lighter by one oath.

"Questioner's house has three doors," Brannoc said, when the last of them had gone. He spread a scrap of leather across his knee and drew on it with a stub of charcoal: a long rectangle, a courtyard like a swallowed tooth, a narrow wing jutting east. "Front for show. River door for bodies. And this—" he tapped the east wing, "—the stair down to the cells. One guard on it, because no one's fool enough to come down here uninvited."

"Except us," Gideon said.

Brannoc looked up at him then, and there was something in the smuggler-king's face that wasn't entirely the flat amusement he wore like armor. "Except us," he agreed. "You understand what you're asking. I'm spending ten years of careful rot to crack one stone. If this goes wrong, every favor I've hoarded burns with it, and I'll be running cargo up the coast with a price on my head before the moon turns."

"You knew Sera Voss."

"I knew her." The words came out quieter than anything else Brannoc had said all day. "She ran the river route with me for two years, back when we were both young enough to think the water was ours. She was the only person I ever met who could lie to an inquisitor's face and make him thank her for it." He turned the charcoal over in his fingers. "When she stopped coming, I told myself she'd found better work. That's what I do, Dunmore. I tell myself stories so I don't have to look at the truth. It's cheaper than grief."

Gideon thought of the map pressed into his hands by a dying woman, of the sentence that had broken off mid-word. *Tell the king his father—* A life cut off in the middle of its own message.

"She died getting that map to me," he said. "The least I can do is spend it badly enough to honor her."

That startled a laugh out of Brannoc — a real one, rough as gravel sliding. "Spend it badly. Aye. That's the only way anything worth doing gets done." He rolled up the leather. "Midnight tomorrow. The lamp-woman douses the wall lights on my signal. You take the river door. I take the front, loud as a festival, and give them something to look at that isn't you."

"And if they catch you?"

Brannoc smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Then I'll lie beautifully. It's the family trade."

* * *

They came up the river at slack water, in a boat muffled with sacking, poling through fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like the world forgetting itself. Gideon crouched in the bow with four of Brannoc's men — quiet ones, scarred ones, men whose names were things like Ferro and Half-Mouth and nothing else — and watched the prison wall slide past overhead, black stone sweating in the river damp.

The river door was exactly what Brannoc had promised: a low arch of wet granite, half-drowned at high tide, guarded by a single sentry who had been paid to be somewhere else. They found him instead standing right where he should have been, and for one lurching moment Gideon thought the whole thing had rotted through — until the man looked at them, sighed like a laborer at the end of a long day, and stepped aside.

"Third landing," the sentry muttered. "There's a new one below. Questioner's been down there since dusk. He knows something's coming — he's been burning papers since supper."

Burning papers. Gideon's stomach dropped through the bottom of the boat. Not Wren. Please not Wren.

They went up through the undercroft in darkness, hand on shoulder, breath held, past storerooms where confiscated goods slept in rows like coffins — bolts of cloth, crates of books, jars whose contents Gideon chose not to examine. The stair to the cells was narrow and smelled of lye and older, worse things. On the third landing, Ferro put out a hand and stopped them all.

Voices. Below. One voice smooth as poured wax, asking questions. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, another voice answering — ragged, defiant, alive.

Wren.

Gideon's heart slammed so hard he could feel it in his teeth. Ten days. Ten days since the tinker's betrayal, since he'd run with the first keystone clutched to his chest while they dragged her away, and every hour of those ten days he'd heard the same sentence repeating in his skull like a bell that wouldn't stop: *I left her. I ran. Again.* He had run from the valley once, run from the duel, run from everything that ever asked him to stand still and be counted. Running was the oldest habit he owned, and he hated it more than he hated the Regent itself.

He went down the stairs first. Nobody asked him to. Nobody needed to.

* * *

The Questioner's chamber was round, which somehow made it worse — no corners for cruelty to hide in. A brazier burned at the center, and around it, arranged with the neatness of a surgeon's tray, stood a collection of instruments Gideon refused to look at closely: needles, vials, a small brass apparatus like an orrery gone wrong, its arms ending in cups of cloudy glass.

And in the middle of it, strapped upright in a chair of black iron, sat Wren.

She was thinner. Her left arm was wrapped where the straps had bitten. But her eyes — her eyes were open and fixed on the Questioner with an expression Gideon recognized from mirrors, from the worst nights of his exile: the look of someone who has decided that whatever is taken from her next, she will still be holding something they cannot reach.

The Questioner stood with his back to the stair, a grey-robed figure with a voice like silk drawn over a blade. He was holding a vial up to the brazier light, turning it, admiring it.

"—and this one," he was saying, in that patient, dreadful tone, "is interesting. Your sister's face. Do you know how rarely we get a memory this clean? Most people's recollections blur at the edges. Yours is sharp as glass. I could sell this ten times over and no buyer would ever know it was secondhand."

Gideon came off the last step without deciding to.

What happened next happened fast and ugly and not at all the way songs would later tell it, if any song were ever foolish enough to tell it. There was no duel. There was no clever exchange of quips. There was Ferro's knife opening the throat of the second guard before the man finished turning, and Half-Mouth's shoulder hitting the Questioner square in the spine, and the grey robe going down among his own neat instruments with a sound like a cart collapsing.

Gideon reached Wren's chair and worked the straps with shaking hands. The iron buckles were cold and wouldn't give, and for three endless seconds he fumbled like a boy at his first lock, and then Ferro was beside him with a blade and the straps parted and Wren slumped forward into his arms.

She weighed almost nothing. She smelled of lye and blood and underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of the rosemary oil she must have used on her hair a lifetime ago, before all this.

"Gideon," she said, and her voice was a ruin, scraped down to the boards. "You came back."

"I always—" He stopped, because the lie was too large even for this room. "No. I didn't. Last time I ran. This time I came back. That's not the same as always."

"It's the same as now," she said. "I'll take it."

* * *

Getting her out was the part Brannoc had planned best and understood least. He had imagined a prisoner who would need carrying. What came up the river stairs instead was a woman who walked, swaying, one arm over Gideon's shoulders, refusing the boat's bench with a shake of her head, sitting straight in the bow with the fog wrapping her like a bandage.

Brannoc met them at the downstream weir, and when he saw her face his whole performance fell off him at once, the way a mask falls when the string breaks. He took her hands in both of his ringed ones and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

It was Wren who spoke first.

"They took some of it," she said. Flatly. Like reading an inventory aloud. "He told me. He liked telling me — he thought it would break me faster, knowing what was going. He was right about most people, I suppose." She laughed, and the laugh had a crack down the middle of it. "I fed him false memories for six days. Whole lifetimes of them. I gave him a sister who never existed, a childhood in a fishing village I invented on the spot, a first love with a name I made up from a wine label. He drank it all down like it was vintage. He's going to report that I'm a fisherman's daughter from Callowmere with a brother named Ottavio." Her jaw tightened. "But he got greedy near the end. He stopped believing I'd run out. He went digging past the decoys."

"What did he find?" Gideon asked, though the question felt like pressing on a wound to see how deep it went.

Wren looked at the black water sliding past the hull, and her face did something Gideon had no word for — a kind of collapse inward, a shutter closing over a window that no longer had anything behind it.

"My sister's face," she said.

* * *

They made camp that night in a smuggler's hollow above the river, a fold in the hills where Brannoc kept stores and, apparently, a store of silences. A fire burned low. Wren sat closest to it, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of tar, and she had not spoken in an hour.

Gideon found her there and sat down across the flames, because he didn't trust himself to sit beside her. Some distances you keep on purpose, the way you keep a hand off a burn.

"Tell me," he said.

"She had a face." Wren's voice came out level, which was somehow more terrible than weeping. "Everyone does, I know that, it sounds mad when I say it. But I mean I *had* hers. Twenty years of it. The gap in her teeth when she laughed. The way her left eyebrow did that thing — higher than the right, always, like she was permanently mid-question. The freckle under her ear shaped like a comma." She stared into the fire. "I could have drawn her in the dark. I used to, actually, when the road was long. Little sketches on scraps, just to feel close to her. Now—" She lifted her hands, palms open, empty. "Now I know facts about her. Brown hair. Older than me by two years. Died of fever when I was nineteen. I know everything about her except the one thing that made her *her*. It's like knowing a song's lyrics without ever having heard the tune."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope an owl called, and the sound fell into the silence between them and lay there.

"The Questioner," Gideon said slowly, "kept the vial. We saw it. He was showing it off when we came down."

"He'll have moved it by now. Or drunk it, or sold it, or locked it in whatever vault they keep the rest of the stolen country in." Wren pulled the blanket tighter. "Do you understand what they're building, Gideon? It's not a prison. Prisons hold bodies. This is a *ledger*. Every memory they take goes into circulation. Somewhere in that archive is my sister's face, filed between a farmer's wedding day and a dead child's first steps, priced and catalogued, waiting for some grey-robed clerk to decide it's worth more as inventory than as mine."

Her voice hadn't risen. That was the frightening part. It had gotten quieter with every sentence, gathering density, like water compressing behind a dam.

"They didn't torture me to get information," she said. "I never gave them anything true. They tortured me because taking is what they *are*. Because the Regent eats, and the inquisitors are its teeth, and teeth don't ask permission." She looked up, and her eyes caught the firelight and held it, and Gideon saw that the guilt that had driven her since Ashmarket — guilt over Sera, guilt over the map, guilt over every theft she'd ever committed and enjoyed — had burned down to something harder underneath.

Rage. Clean, banked, structural rage, the kind that doesn't flare but *lasts*.

"I spent my whole life stealing," she said. "Memories, mostly. Little ones. A baker's memory of his mother's kitchen, sold to a homesick merchant. A soldier's memory of home, sold back to him at interest. I told myself it was a trade, that everyone pays for what they need, that the people I took from barely noticed the loss." Her hands closed slowly into fists inside the blanket. "I didn't notice what it cost because I'd never been on the other side of the tray. Now I know. Now I know exactly what it feels like to have a piece of yourself lifted out and priced." She smiled, and it was the coldest thing Gideon had ever seen her do. "So here's what happens next. We get the second keystone. Then the third. Then we go into that archive, and I am going to find every vial they've ever taken from every person in this kingdom, and I am going to give all of it back. All of it. Every face, every voice, every dead mother and lost sister. Let the whole realm wake up remembering what was taken. Let it *burn* remembering."

* * *

Brannoc found Gideon afterward, apart from the fire, at the lip of the hollow where the hills fell away toward the river plain. The smuggler-king had a bottle in one hand and a grief he was managing badly in his eyes.

"She'll do it, you know," he said. "The archive. She'll tear it open if it kills her, and it might."

"I know."

"And you'll help her."

"Yes."

Brannoc drank, wiped his mouth with the back of a ringed hand, and studied Gideon with the appraising eye of a man pricing cargo. "Ten years in that valley," he said. "Magicless. Ordinary. Learning to be a goatherd. And the first time someone hands you a reason, you walk out and start a war." He shook his head slowly. "I don't know whether that's courage or just a storm looking for an excuse."

"Neither do I," Gideon said honestly. "Some mornings I think the valley cured me. Most nights I know it just taught me to wait."

Below them, the firelight showed Wren still awake, still talking — softly now, to Ferro, drawing on the dirt with a stick. Drawing, Gideon realized, from memory that no longer held what it drew. Sketching a face she could describe but no longer see, testing each line against a blankness where the truth used to live, trying to rebuild her sister out of facts the way a man rebuilds a drowned village from a list of street names.

It was the bravest and saddest thing Gideon had ever watched a person do, and she was doing it casually, by firelight, as if it were nothing, as if losing a face were a wound like any other that time would close over.

It wouldn't. He knew that better than anyone. Some things the valley had taken from him had never grown back either.

"We move at dawn," Brannoc said, corking the bottle. "The quarry's two days north. Stone country. Bad roads, worse company." He paused, and something flickered behind his eyes — the look of a man about to say a thing he'd sworn not to say. "Dunmore. About the quarry. About what the stone does there, what it shows people." He set his jaw. "When it asks you questions — and it will, it asks everyone — answer it true. Whatever it costs. Because I've seen what happens to liars in that place, and I'd rather bury you than watch that."

Gideon looked at him. "What happens to liars?"

Brannoc turned away toward the dark, and his voice came back flattened, stripped of all its smuggler's polish.

"The stone remembers every hand that ever cut it," he said. "Every hand. And it makes you remember yours."
