## 19. Keystone Two: The Weeping Quarry

The road to the quarry was a scar.

Gideon felt it before he saw it — a wrongness under his boots, the way the earth hummed faintly where cartwheels had ground the same ruts for two hundred years. The mountain had been opened here like a wound, and though the cutting had stopped a generation ago, the land had never quite closed over it. Grass grew thin and grey. Trees leaned away from the pit as if flinching from an old blow.

Wren walked ahead of him, her hood up against the drizzle, her steps light in the way of someone who had learned early that weight drew attention. She hadn't spoken much since they'd left Brannoc's camp at dawn. Neither had he. The smuggler's parting words sat between them like a third traveler neither wanted to acknowledge.

*The stone remembers every hand that ever cut it.*

"You could still wait outside," Wren said, without turning. "If you wanted."

"I don't want."

"That's not what I asked."

He didn't answer, and she let it go, which was its own kind of mercy. He was learning that about her — that Wren's silences were not absences but rooms she kept furnished for other people's griefs. She gave him one now, and he walked through it, and was grateful.

The first keystone hung against his chest on a cord of braided leather, cold even through his shirt. The Bellfounder's Daughter. It had cost them a truth apiece to win it, and Gideon still sometimes woke at night with the ghost's voice in his ears: *You paid honestly. That is rarer than you think.* He wondered what this place would charge. Brannoc had refused to say. Brannoc, who haggled over everything, who put a price on breath and rope and loyalty with the same cheerful fluency — Brannoc had gone quiet when they asked what waited in the Weeping Quarry, and quiet, from him, was a kind of screaming.

The path bent around a shoulder of rock, and there it was.

The quarry opened below them like a bowl cut by a god's careless thumb. Terraces descended in great steps into the earth, each one the width of a market square, the walls scored with chisel-marks so regular they might have been scripture. Rain had gathered in the lowest terrace, a pool of water the color of pewter, perfectly still despite the drizzle falling into it. And everywhere — everywhere — the stone wept.

Not metaphor. Water seeped from the cut faces in slow beads, tracing channels down the terraced walls, gathering in runnels worn smooth by centuries of the same descent. The whole quarry glistened and dripped, a hollow mountain crying without pause, and the sound of it filled the pit: a thousand small trickles braided into one long sigh, like a crowd mourning very far away.

"Gods," Wren breathed beside him. "It really does weep."

"It's just groundwater," Gideon said, because saying anything else felt dangerous.

"Mm." She looked at him sidelong. "And the drowned bell-tower was just flooding."

They went down.

* * *

The terraces were older than any kingdom Wren knew how to steal from.

She'd grown up in cities where history was a commodity — sold in relic shops, worn as pendants, melted down and recast whenever fashion turned. But this place made her feel the way she'd felt the first time she'd slipped into the Archive of Stolen Things and understood, all at once, how much of the world had been taken from people who never got it back. The quarry was grief with geography. Someone had needed stone, and the need had been answered with a wound this deep, and the wound had never learned to stop bleeding.

She counted the chisel-marks as they descended, out of habit. Thieves counted exits; Wren counted everything. Four hundred marks to a wall-face, more or less, each one the length of a forearm. Which meant hands. Thousands of hands, tens of thousands of days, men and probably women too, swinging iron into the mountain until the mountain gave. Every mark a small violence. Every violence remembered.

"So," she said, to break the weeping's rhythm before it got into her blood. "Brannoc said the keystone's at the bottom. In the pool?"

"He said it's *kept* at the bottom." Gideon's voice had gone flat, careful. She'd heard that tone from him before — in Ashmarket, before the trap; on the road, whenever anyone asked about the valley. It was the voice of a man walking on ice he knew was thin. "He said the stone decides who leaves with it."

"The stone."

"The stone."

She let a few steps pass. "You know I've watched you lie to inquisitors with your pulse barely moving. You lied to a Questioner's face about your own name. So why is it every time someone mentions this quarry, you start sounding like a man rehearsing a eulogy?"

"I don't—"

"You do. You've been doing it for two days." She stopped on the fourth terrace, rain dripping off her hood, and turned to face him fully. Below them the pool waited, silver and patient. "I'm going down there with you regardless. I'd just like to know what I'm walking into. That's not suspicion, Gideon. That's craft. A thief always asks what the lock was built to keep out."

He looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away. And she saw him do the thing she'd seen him do once before, at the ford, over Sera Voss's body — the thing where he reached for a lie and found his hand wouldn't close around it.

"Not here," he said finally. "Whatever it is. Not on the stairs."

"Fair enough." She started down again. "But the stone's going to ask, isn't it? Whatever Brannoc meant. The stone's going to ask *you*."

"Yes," Gideon said. "I think it is."

* * *

The bottom of the quarry was a country of echoes.

Gideon's boots rang on wet stone with every step, and each ring came back to him doubled, tripled, layered with other footsteps that were not his own — the ghosts of ten thousand workdays pressed into the acoustics like fossils in shale. He could almost pick them apart if he listened: the heavy tread of the overseers, the quick scurry of the water-carriers, the dragging shuffle of men at the end of a shift. The quarry had swallowed their sounds and kept them, the way it kept everything.

The pool lay at the center of the lowest terrace, perhaps forty paces across, fed by a hundred weeping seams in the walls around it. Up close the water was clearer than it had looked from above — clear enough that he could see the bottom, and what waited there.

A slab of pale stone rested on the pool's floor, no larger than a coffin lid, and set into its surface was a shape that hurt to look at directly: a wedge of crystal the color of lightning-split sky, faceted like a cut gem but wrong somehow, its angles refusing to settle, as though the eye kept sliding off geometry itself. The second keystone. It glowed faintly, a light like distant weather, and the glow pulsed.

In time with his heartbeat.

"Oh," Wren said softly, at his shoulder. "Oh, that's not good."

"No."

"It knows you're here."

"It knew I was here before we crossed the ridge," Gideon said. "I think—" He stopped. Swallowed. His hands had begun their old tremor, the fine shiver that had betrayed him for ten years in the valley, the storm caged under his skin pressing at the bars. Except the cage felt thinner here. The whole quarry felt like a held breath. "I think it's been listening since we started down."

Wren crouched at the pool's edge and did not touch the water. Good instincts. He crouched beside her and studied the surface instead. It reflected the grey sky perfectly, perfectly still, and in the reflection he saw something that made his stomach drop: the terraces behind them, empty. No birds. No insects on the water. Even the weeping seemed to have drawn back, holding its thousand small voices in reserve.

"What do we do?" Wren asked. "Just reach in?"

"I don't think it's about reaching." He flexed his trembling fingers. "Sera's map said the keystones answer to truth. The ghost at the bell-tower wanted a truth as payment. This one—" He gestured at the walls, the chisel-marks, the patient weeping stone. "This one *is* truth. Cut stone doesn't lie about what it is. It can't pretend to be uncut. Maybe that's the test. Maybe you stand in front of it and it looks at what you actually are."

"And if what you actually are doesn't match what you've been telling everyone?" Wren's voice was light, but her eyes weren't. "Including me?"

The question landed exactly where she'd aimed it. He should have known. Two liars recognizing each other's craft — she'd seen the seams in him from the beginning, and she'd been courteous enough not to pick at them, and now the courtesy was spent.

"Wren—"

"Don't explain to me. Explain to the rock." She stood, brushed rain from her knees, and nodded at the pool. "Go on. I'll be right here. If it takes you, I'll... think of something. Probably involving theft."

Despite everything, something in his chest loosened. "That's the plan?"

"It's always the plan."

He stood. Walked to the pool's edge. The water was warm — warmer than it had any right to be, body-warm, blood-warm — and where his reflection should have been, the surface showed him something else: not his face but his hands, younger, unscarred, wrapped around a sword hilt in a training yard he hadn't seen in eleven years.

The quarry began to speak.

* * *

It didn't use words at first. It used memory.

The training yard rose around him out of the rain — sun-baked sand, the white walls of the academy at Cairn Hallow, banners snapping overhead. He was twenty-two again and invincible, and across the sand stood Tam, grinning, rolling his shoulders loose the way their father had taught them both, and the whole yard was watching because everyone always watched when the Dunmore brothers crossed practice swords, because it was the finest spectacle in the province, fire against storm, brother against brother—

"Gideon." Wren's voice, thin, as if through water. "Your eyes are doing something. Gideon—"

He couldn't answer. The stone had him.

*Show me,* the quarry said, in a voice like a chisel drawn slowly across granite. *Show me the exile.*

And he showed it, because there was no refusing — the story he had told for eleven years, polished smooth as river glass: the young storm-caller whose gift had raged past his control, whose lightning had struck indiscriminately during a trial of power, who had been exiled beyond the Veil not as punishment but as mercy, for everyone's protection, a tragedy, an accident, a sorrow.

The stone took the story in its hands the way a mason takes a block, turning it, testing it.

Then it found the flaw, and split it open.

*Liar.*

The word went through him like iron through slate. The training yard shattered, and the true memory stood bare underneath, and there was nowhere to look away to:

The duel had not been a trial. There had been no instructors, no wards, no sanctioned contest. There had been wine, and a feast, and Tam laughing at him across the table — *the great storm-caller, afraid of a little sibling rivalry* — and Gideon, twenty-two and drunk on pride and brandy both, unable to bear being laughed at, unable to bear that Tam's magic came easy where his came hard, that Tam was loved easily where he was loved carefully. They had gone out to the yard at midnight with no witnesses because Gideon had insisted on no witnesses. He had called the storm first. He had called it bigger than he could hold, deliberately, to win, to make Tam's eyes go wide, to make the whole family finally understand which brother the sky belonged to.

And the sky had answered him gladly, the way it always had, and he had lost the edge of it for three seconds — three seconds, he had counted them every night for eleven years — and the bolt that escaped had not been aimed at anything.

Tam had died with his name half-spoken. Not angry. Surprised. That was the worst of it, the seam the stone drove its chisel into now, merciless: *surprised*. His brother had died surprised, mid-grin, trusting him absolutely, and Gideon had knelt in the sand holding what was left and screamed loud enough to bring the household running, and the storm had come again to his grief like a dog to its master, eager, and he had had to fight his own gift while his brother cooled in his arms.

No trial. No accident. The council had offered him the gentlest lie available — madness, miscontrol, exile-as-mercy — and he had seized it with both hands because the truth would have broken their mother, and because exile was easier than facing a province that would have demanded justice for the heir of Cairn Hallow. He had not been banished. He had *fled*, wearing the respectable costume of the banished, and he had told the story so long he had almost made it true.

Almost.

The pool's surface churned. The keystone's light flared white, then settled into a hard, steady blue, and the quarry's voice came one final time, not cruel now, only vast and sad as the weeping walls:

*Every hand is remembered. Even the ones that cut themselves.*

The light dimmed. The vision released him.

* * *

Wren caught him before he hit the water.

She didn't know how long he'd been standing there — minutes, an hour; time had gone strange in the pit, stretched thin as stolen silk. What she knew was that he'd gone rigid, that his lips had moved through things she couldn't hear, that tears had cut tracks through the rain on his face without his expression changing at all, and then his knees simply quit, and she was moving before thought, arms around him, hauling him back from the edge.

He was heavier than he looked. Solid, the way grief makes people solid, all the softness compressed into weight. She got him three steps from the pool and lowered him onto the wet stone, and he knelt there shaking, head bowed, water dripping off his hair, and did not speak.

Behind him, the keystone rose from the pool.

It came up slowly, trailing silver water, and drifted through the air to hang before Gideon's chest — waiting, patient as the quarry itself. All he had to do was take it. She could see that. The stone had weighed him, found him wanting and honest in the same breath, and decided that wanting-and-honest was sufficient. The wedge of impossible crystal pulsed gently, in time with his ragged breathing now rather than his heart.

"Gideon." She crouched in front of him, careful, the way you approached a wounded animal or a loaded trap. "Hey. Storm-caller. Look at me."

He didn't. His voice came out scraped raw. "You heard."

"Some." She had heard enough — fragments bleeding out of the vision like light under a door. A brother. A yard at night. The word *liar* ringing off the terraces. "Not all."

"All." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever pulled out of him, worse than the sickened awe after the lightning at the ford country. "It made sure of all. That was the price, wasn't it? Not privacy. The whole ledger, read aloud, in front of—" He stopped. Finally lifted his head, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terrible and steady. "In front of you."

Wren held his gaze. Everything in her craft said: look away, give him the mercy of pretending, let the lie reassemble itself so you can both go on carrying it comfortably. Everything in her craft said a secret freely given was worth ten times one pried loose, and this one had been pried loose with a crowbar, and the kindest thing was to help him bury it again fast.

But the ghost at the bell-tower had asked her for a truth, and she had given one, and it had cost her, and it had also — she hated admitting this — freed something. And this man had carried her out of a burning building in Ashmarket without asking what she'd stolen to save them. Fair was fair. Liars kept accounts too.

"So it wasn't an accident of power," she said quietly. "You weren't exiled. You ran."

"I killed my brother." Flat. Final. A stone laid on a grave. "For pride. For a moment of pride at midnight with no one watching. The valley, the Veil, the ten years — none of it was a sentence, Wren. I sentenced myself and let everyone believe the court did it, because it was easier to be pitied than hated." His hands opened on his knees, palms up, the tremor visible, the storm caged and rattling. "There. Now you know what you're traveling with."

The keystone hovered between them, glowing its patient blue.

Wren looked at it, and at him, and did the hardest thing she'd done since Sera Voss died: she stayed.

"Good," she said.

His head came up. "*Good?*"

"Good. Because I've spent my whole life around people whose stories never cracked — beautiful seamless stories, and every one of them rotten at the core. Yours has a crack in it now. I trust cracked things." She reached out and closed his fingers around the cord, around the keystone, folding his hand shut. "Take it. And when you're ready to tell me the rest — the parts even the rock didn't get — I'll be somewhere nearby, pretending not to listen. It's what I do."

He stared at her. Something moved through his face — not relief, not yet, maybe not for years — but the shaking slowed, and his hand closed around the stone, and the keystone's light gentled to a warmth like banked coals.

That was when the weeping stopped.

All of it, at once — a hundred seeping seams falling silent together, the whole quarry holding its breath — and in the new silence Wren heard what had silenced it: voices above, on the rim of the pit. Many boots. The high cold note of an inquisitor's tuning-fork, singing the pitch that unraveled hidden things.

"They followed us from Brannoc's camp," she breathed. "The bastard sold us after all."

Below them, the pool had begun, very softly, to boil.
