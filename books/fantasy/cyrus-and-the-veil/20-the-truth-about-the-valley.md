## 20. The Truth About the Valley

The quarry did not let them leave.

That was how it felt, anyway, walking the switchback path down through terraces of pale stone that had been cut and abandoned a hundred years before either of them was born. The second keystone rode in Gideon's pack now, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a sleeping child. It had accepted him — *tested* him, the stone-voices had said, in their grinding chorus — and what it had found in him he did not want to name. Wren hadn't asked. She'd walked out of that quarry beside him with her jaw set and her eyes fixed on the middle distance, and she hadn't asked, and the not-asking had been worse than any question.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of an old cutting wall, where the stone rose sheer above them like the side of a cathedral built by no god worth worshiping. Gideon gathered dead scrub-brush for a fire he didn't light. His hands shook. They had been shaking since the keystone touched his blood, since the stone sang its slow verdict into his marrow: *there is a lie in this one.*

"You could just say it," Wren said from across the cold fire ring.

He looked up. She sat with her knees drawn up, her back against the stone, turning Sera's map over in her fingers though they both knew its route by heart by now. In the last light her face was all angles and shadow, and there was something in her expression he'd learned to read in the weeks since Ashmarket — the particular stillness of a thief deciding whether a lock was worth her time.

"Say what," he said, which was cowardice, and they both knew it.

"The thing the quarry tasted." She didn't look at him. "The lie you've been carrying like a stone in your boot since we met. You walk crooked around it. I steal things for a living, Gideon. I know what people carry, and I know when they're guarding something so hard they've forgotten they're doing it."

* * *

He should tell her. That was the thing that kept circling back, patient as water wearing at rock. He owed her — she'd traded pieces of herself to save him in Ashmarket; she'd lost her sister's face in a Questioner's cell and walked out of that ruin still standing beside him; she had every reason, ten times over, to take the map and go, and she hadn't gone. And here he sat, hoarding the truth like a miser while she starved politely three feet away.

But the telling — that was the problem. There were things a man could confess and remain himself after. And there was the valley, and the storm, and Tam.

"I wasn't exiled," Gideon said finally.

Wren's hands went still on the map.

"The valley doesn't exile anyone. It's not a punishment. It's where the Crown sends people whose magic is too dangerous to execute and too useful to kill — or it was, in my day. You're sent there to be *forgotten*. Politely. With a pension." He heard his own voice go flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man reading someone else's confession. "Storm-caller. That's what I was. Not a hedge-witch's trick, not weather-sense — the real thing. Lightning answered me like a hound answers its master. I could feel every storm within fifty miles the way you feel a hand on your shoulder."

"And?" Wren said quietly. "What did it cost?"

"That's the part everyone asks wrong." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Everyone assumes the magic costs something. Power for pain, tithe for gift. But the valley doesn't hurt you. That's the horror of it. You step past the Veil and the wind stops *knowing* your name, and it doesn't ache. It just... quiets. Ten years of quiet. You learn to tend goats. You learn to sleep through thunder without your pulse climbing. You learn ordinary until ordinary is all that's left of you, and then one morning you realize the quiet isn't peace. It's amputation."

Wren said nothing. She had the gift, he was learning, of silence used as a tool rather than a weapon.

"My brother came to find me," Gideon said. "Three years ago. Tam. Younger than me by two years, and better in every way that mattered — kinder, steadier, braver. He crossed half the kingdom because he couldn't bear the thought of me rotting in a place that smelled of goat and forgetting." Gideon's hands curled in his lap. "And I hated him for it. For finding me. Because every time I looked at him I remembered who I'd been before the storm ruined everything, and I couldn't stand the shape of my own life reflected in his face."

"Gideon—"

"He challenged me." The words came faster now, tumbling, as if speed could blunt them. "Not a real duel — a game, a brothers' quarrel, the kind we'd had a hundred times as boys. Who could call the storm first, who could hold it longer. Pride. Stupid, boyish pride, except we weren't boys anymore, and I'd spent three years starving for the storm, and when it finally answered—" His breath caught. "It answered *too well*. Like a starved dog. Like a fist unclenching. I called lightning down onto open ground between us and it forked, it split, and I was too drunk on it — too *glad* — to pull it back in time."

The fire ring stayed cold between them. Somewhere above, wind moved along the rim of the quarry, sighing through the old cuts, and the sound was almost like voices.

"It took him where he stood," Gideon said. "One heartbeat he was laughing at me, daring me, and the next he was on the ground and the rain was putting out the fire on his clothes, and I knelt there in the mud holding him and screaming at the sky to give him back, and the sky just... went on raining. Storms don't apologize. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing you learn."

Silence. Long enough that he counted his own heartbeats against it.

"So I ran," he said. "Back past the Veil, back to the goats, back to the quiet. I told myself it was penance. Ten years of ordinary as payment for one moment of pride. But it wasn't penance. Penance would have been staying to bury him properly, to face our parents, to let the kingdom see what a storm-caller does when he loses control. Running was just running. I've been running ever since — from Sera Voss bleeding out at the ford, from the inquisitors, from the crown, from this whole wretched quest. Every time it gets hard I look for a ford to cross and a valley to hide in." He looked up at Wren, and found he couldn't read her face at all now, and that terrified him more than any inquisitor. "So there's your lie. The quarry tasted it. I'm not a man unjustly exiled seeking justice for a stolen king. I'm a coward who killed his brother and calls his cowardice duty."

* * *

The silence afterward was not the silence of judgment. That was the strange cruelty of it.

Wren unfolded herself slowly and came around the cold fire ring, and sat down beside him — not opposite, not apart, but close enough that her shoulder nearly touched his. She laid the map down flat on the ground between them, smoothing its creases with careful fingers, the way she handled anything that mattered.

"Sera told me once," she said, "that guilt is just love with nowhere to go. She said thieves understand that better than priests do, because we spend our whole lives taking things we can't give back." Her voice was low, even, stripped of performance. "She never talked about her past much. But sometimes she'd drink too much and say things like that, and I'd pretend not to listen, because listening felt like stealing from her."

"Wren—"

"I'm not finished." No sharpness in it. Just weariness, and something underneath the weariness that sounded almost like tenderness. "You think what you did makes you monstrous. I watched a man in a Questioner's cell peel my sister's face out of my head and sell it drop by drop, and I'll tell you what monstrous actually looks like. Monstrous is the Regent eating a boy king alive and calling it protection. Monstrous is a court full of adults watching it happen and signing papers. You were twenty-something and grieving and full of a power nobody taught you to leash, and you made one terrible choice in one terrible second, and you've spent ten years paying for it in the only currency cowards understand, which is solitude." She turned to look at him then, fully, and her eyes caught the last grey light. "That's not absolution. That's just arithmetic."

"I killed him."

"Yes." No flinch, no softening. "And you can carry it honestly or dishonestly. Honestly means you use it — it means the next time the storm rises in you, you remember exactly what pride costs, and you choose differently. Dishonestly means you keep hiding in valleys pretending the quiet is penance." She picked up the map again, folded it with precise care, tucked it away inside her coat. "Which is it going to be? Because we've got two days' hard ride to Hollowmere, an oracle waiting who wants a price none of us wants to pay, and a solstice clock ticking. I don't have room in this for a man who's still running. But a man carrying his dead brother honestly—" She paused, and something flickered across her face, grief-shaped, quickly mastered. "A man like that might be worth marching beside."

Gideon's throat worked. He had prepared himself — God knew he'd prepared himself for years — for revulsion, for recoil, for the moment Wren looked at him and saw the thing he truly was. He had rehearsed the leaving. He had imagined her walking out of the camp without a backward glance, taking the map and the keystones and the mission, and he had imagined deserving it.

He had not imagined this.

"Why," he managed. "Why would you stay. After that."

"Because you told me." She said it simply, as if it were obvious, as if it explained everything. "Because you could have lied to me forever and I'd probably never have known, and you didn't. Because the quarry dragged your worst secret into the light and instead of breaking you, you handed it to me like it was yours to give." She shrugged, awkward suddenly, the thief's armor slipping enough to show the girl beneath. "I've known a lot of people, Gideon Dunmore. Liars mostly, myself included. Trustworthy ones are rare as honest magistrates. A man who tells the truth about the worst thing he's done—" She looked away, out at the darkening quarry. "That's rarer still."

The wind moved overhead. Far off, beyond the quarry rim, clouds were gathering — real ones, fat and dark with rain, the first he'd felt since crossing the Veil. He could sense them the way he'd once sensed nothing in the valley: pressure building, charge crawling along his nerves, the storm's attention brushing against him like a cat deciding whether to be friendly. Ten years ago that touch would have sent him spiraling toward panic or hunger. Tonight it felt almost like a greeting.

"I keep waiting for you to run," he said.

"I know."

"How do you bear it? Knowing the worst of someone and staying?"

Wren was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone very soft. "Sera stayed for me. After the first job went wrong and I got a boy hurt — a boy I loved, actually, though I was too young and stupid to call it that. I froze, and he took the blade meant for me, and he lived but his hand never worked right again. I expected Sera to wash her hands of me. Thieves' logic says you cut losses fast." Her thumb traced the edge of the folded map through her coat. "She didn't. She said, 'Now I know what you fear, and knowing that, I can trust you properly.' I didn't understand it then." She glanced sideways at him. "I think I'm starting to."

Above them, the first drops began to fall — fat, slow, unhurried, spotting the dust around the cold fire ring. Gideon lifted his face to it and let the rain strike him, and for the first time in ten years he did not flinch from the sky's attention. The storm hummed somewhere in his blood, patient, enormous, neither caged nor caging. He thought of Tam laughing in the rain, young and golden and daring him. He thought: *I will carry you honestly now, little brother. Whatever that costs.*

"We should sleep," Wren said. "Hollowmere's two days if the roads hold."

"Hollowmere," he agreed, and reached for the pack, and paused. "Wren. The oracle. The price she named — one of us carries the crown's burden when it's torn free. We haven't spoken of it since."

"No," Wren said carefully. "We haven't."

There was something in her tone — a smoothness, a deftness, the faintest seam of craft — that the old Gideon would never have noticed. But he had spent ten years learning to read silences, and this one had a lock in it. She was planning something. Some arrangement made behind his back, some sacrifice she intended to make without asking his leave, and the realization landed in his chest with a weight entirely different from the keystone's.

She met his eyes, and for one naked instant he saw it there: resolve, hidden poorly, worn like a mask over fear.

Then she smiled, thief-bright, and said, "Sleep, storm-caller. Tomorrow we buy a miracle."

And far to the south, unseen, unheard, the bells of the capital began to toll — the Regent announcing the renewal rite, the solstice clock now audible even here, even in the rain, even in the space between two liars who had finally stopped lying about everything except the one thing that mattered most.
