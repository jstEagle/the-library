## 21. The Crown Remembers

Aldric Vane woke before dawn with his hands aching.

He lay still beneath the weight of the winter quilts and studied them in the grey light that seeped through the shutters — his own hands, long-fingered, ink-stained at the cuticles, the right one calloused where the quill pressed. They looked like his hands. They had to be his hands. And yet they ached as though they had been working all night, gripping something, writing something, and he could not remember what.

*You were restless,* said the Regent, its voice arriving inside his skull the way warmth arrives inside a bath — gradual, total, impossible to locate the source of. *You dreamed badly. Sleep now. The petitions begin at nine.*

"I don't remember dreaming," Aldric said aloud, because he had learned that speaking made it harder for the thing to smooth him over. Words were stones in the current. Silence let it carry him.

*A dream of falling,* the Regent said, patient as a tutor. *You have them often. It means nothing.*

But it was lying. He knew it was lying the way a man knows the difference between a fire that warms him and a fire that is eating his house — not by evidence, at first, but by some animal arithmetic beneath the evidence. The Regent's lies had a texture. They arrived too smoothly, too complete, without the ragged edges that real memory carried: the smell of a thing, the irrelevant detail, the shame you didn't ask for. Real memories were badly made. The Regent's were seamless, and that seamlessness was itself the flaw.

Aldric lay in the dark and did not sleep, and did not tell the crown that he knew, and felt the first thin cold pleasure of a secret kept from a god.

* * *

The day proceeded as days proceeded. That was the Regent's genius, he had come to understand — not spectacle but routine, the slow substitution of its judgments for his own until he could no longer find the seam. He sat through petitions. He approved the grain tariffs for the river provinces (the Regent approved them; his hand moved; the distinction had become academic). He received Lord Casserly of the Marches, whose left eyelid twitched when he spoke of the northern garrisons, and heard himself say, in his own voice, words about loyalty and vigilance that made Casserly go pale and bow too deeply on his way out.

*He doubts,* the Regent murmured afterward, satisfied, the way a man is satisfied after a good meal. *Doubt ferments. Better drawn off early.*

"Better poured down the drain," Aldric said, before he could stop himself.

The silence that followed lasted three heartbeats. Then the Regent laughed — it had learned laughter from him, he realized, had sampled it from his childhood the way a magpie samples silver — and said, *You are tired. You are always tired in winter. Your father was the same.*

And there it was. The hook, baited, dropped into the water as it always was: *your father.* The Regent used King Osric the way a surgeon uses a mirror, angling it so Aldric saw only what the thing wanted him to see. His father's madness. His father's rages. His father, in the Regent's telling, wandering the palace corridors at night talking to people who were not there, signing decrees that shamed the throne, until the crown — merciful, dutiful, long-suffering — had taken up the burden of ruling while the king declined. His father had died mad. The Regent had saved the kingdom from him. The Regent had saved Aldric from him.

Aldric had believed this for six years. He had believed it the way you believe the floor will hold you: constantly, unconsciously, structurally.

Lately the floor had begun to creak.

* * *

It happened that evening, in the map room.

He had gone there because the Regent disliked the map room — no, that wasn't right either, the Regent didn't dislike anything, dislike was a human luxury — because the Regent *deprioritized* it, considered it an obsolete chamber full of old wars, and therefore it was one of the few rooms in the palace where the whispering quieted to a murmur. Aldric had begun collecting such rooms the way a drowning man collects air pockets. The chapel during the hour of silent prayer. The kennels, where the hounds' growling seemed to bother the crown. The old nursery, though he told no one he went there, least of all the thing wearing his mind like a glove.

In the map room there was a globe older than the dynasty, brass-ringed, its oceans painted the green of pond water, and a table where his father had once spread campaign maps, and a window that faced west over the lower city, where the evening lamps were coming alight one by one like a fever breaking out across the dark.

He stood at the window. The Regent drowsed — it drowsed sometimes, after heavy days of judgment, sated the way a leech is sated — and its attention thinned to a warm static at the base of his skull, and Aldric breathed, and watched the lamps, and let his mind drift.

That was when the floor of the world cracked open.

It came without warning, without shape, a fragment rising through the planted strata like a bone surfacing through soil. Not a thought. A *memory* — real, ragged, badly made, stinking of tallow smoke and wet wool —

— a corridor, torchlight guttering, his father's hand around his small wrist, gripping hard enough to bruise, and his father's face above him contorted, tears cutting channels through the grime of sleeplessness, and his father *screaming*, screaming so loudly that the torches seemed to shrink from it:

*"—it LIES, do you understand me, whatever it shows you, whatever it gives you, it is PAYING you with your own coin — Aldric — look at me — it eats the past and calls it wisdom, it eats the future and calls it providence — if you ever feel it loosen, if you ever feel it SLEEP, you run, boy, you run to the—"*

The corridor vanished.

Aldric came back to himself standing at the window with both hands pressed flat against the cold glass, gasping, his forehead nearly touching the pane, the lamps below swimming in a blur he understood after a moment to be his own tears. His wrist ached where no hand gripped it. His throat was raw, as if he had been the one screaming.

*What is this?* The Regent's voice snapped awake, sharp-edged, alarmed — and its alarm was the most honest thing it had ever shown him. *What did you see?*

"You tell me," Aldric whispered. "You keep my memories. Where was that one filed?"

Silence. A long, grinding silence, and beneath it he felt the thing *searching* — he could feel it now, unmistakably, riffling through him like fingers through a strongbox, checking its locks, counting its coins. The sensation was obscene. Six years he had mistaken that riffling for his own thoughts.

*I find nothing,* the Regent said at last, and its voice had reassembled itself into calm, but the calm was a bandage over a wound and they both knew it. *You dreamed again. Standing up, this time. The winter nights are long and your mind wanders. This is common in young rulers. I will prepare you a draught.*

"No."

*A mild one. Valerian and—*

"*No.*" Louder than he intended. His voice cracked against the map room's high ceiling and came back to him doubled, as if the room itself were testifying. "I'll sleep as I am. Unmedicated. Unhelped." He turned from the window, heart slamming, and said the thing he had never dared say, the sentence he had carried coiled in his chest for years like a swallowed fishhook: "Whatever you are keeping from me — whatever you took — I want it back."

The Regent regarded him from inside his own skull. He imagined — he could not help imagining — a vast pale intelligence turning its attention upon him fully for the first time, the way a lighthouse turns, the way a hawk does.

*You are tired,* it said finally, gently, terribly. *Go to bed, Aldric. We will speak in the morning, when you are yourself.*

And he understood, walking back through the freezing corridors with his escort of guards who were also jailers who were also, perhaps, men who loved him, that the Regent had just made its first mistake. It had called him *not himself*. It had admitted, in the grammar of its condescension, that there was a *himself* distinct from the thing it had built — a self it had buried, a self that screamed in corridors, a self that had a father whose voice could still reach up through six years of silt.

It was careless. For the first time, the god was careless.

Aldric climbed into bed, closed his eyes, and began, very quietly, inside the locked room of his own skull, to plan.

* * *

The planning was its own education in captivity.

He could not write things down — or rather, he could, but anything written became the Regent's property by morning; he had tested this once with a false note about a hunting trip and had watched, with sick fascination, as the thing produced the note's contents two days later as evidence of his own forgetfulness, gently correcting him, gently filing him. He could not speak of it aloud except in the few rooms where the whispering thinned, and even there he could not be sure. He could not trust his ministers — Lord Casserly's twitching eyelid haunted him; how many others had been drawn off early, their doubt fermented into compliance? He could not trust the priests, who anointed the crown each solstice and called the thing inside it divine guardianship. He could not trust his own recollections, because the Regent edited nightly, pruning him like a hedge, and he had no way of knowing which of his childhood summers were real and which were topiary.

But he knew this: his father had screamed a warning, and the warning had survived. Buried under six years of planted strata, compressed, corrupted, half-eaten — but alive. Which meant the Regent did not destroy everything. Which meant somewhere in the deep archive of his own plundered mind, or in the palace's older dark, other things survived. Letters. Records. A dead king's handwriting.

*Run to the—* his father had said, and the memory had torn away like wet paper. Run to the what? A place? A person? A vault?

He lay awake cataloguing what he knew of his father that the Regent had never bothered to plant over — the small unprofitable facts, the ones beneath its interest. His father had chewed mint leaves against headaches. His father had hummed when he read, tunelessly, a habit the courtiers had found charming and the Regent had presumably found irrelevant. His father had been afraid of the crypts and had never said why. And his father — this surfaced slowly, reluctantly, like a body turning in dark water — his father in his last year had developed a habit of standing for long minutes before certain walls of the old wing, running his fingers along the mortar, as if listening for something behind the stone.

The courtiers had called it madness. The Regent had called it madness. Everyone had agreed it was madness, and agreement, Aldric now understood, was the Regent's favorite weather.

His father had been hiding something in the walls.

Or finding something. Or reading something. A king who could not trust paper that moved through hands might well trust stone that didn't. A king being eaten alive, losing a little more of himself each night, might well write his testament in the one place his parasite never looked — because why would it? Walls held nothing. Walls were not minds.

Aldric stared at the canopy above his bed and felt something kindle in his chest that he had not felt since boyhood: not hope exactly, hope was too soft a word for it. It was closer to appetite. The cold, clean hunger of a man who has found the first loose stone in a prison wall.

*You're awake,* observed the Regent, drowsily suspicious.

"I'm sleeping," Aldric said. "I'm asleep right now. Isn't that what you wanted?"

Another silence. He smiled into the dark, and made sure the Regent felt the smile, and did not care what it cost.

* * *

Three nights later, the crack widened.

He had begun sleeping in the old wing — he had manufactured a reason, a fit of sentiment about his father's death-day approaching, and the Regent had permitted it with that particular indulgence it reserved for grief it believed it had authored. The old wing smelled of dust and cold ash and, faintly, of rosemary, which someone had burned here once, years ago, and the walls remembered. He walked the corridor from his dream — his father's corridor — counting doors, matching torch brackets to the guttering light behind his eyes, until he stood before the stretch of wall where the memory had lived and died.

Nothing marked it. Grey stone, grey mortar, a wainscot panel gone dark with age. But when he laid his palm flat against the panel, his heart was going like a siege drum, and the Regent — he checked, carefully, delicately, the way a thief checks a sleeper — the Regent was drowsing, sated on the day's judgments, its attention pooled somewhere in the warm lowlands of his brainstem.

Behind the panel, faint as a pulse under skin, he felt a draft. Cold air, moving. There was a space back there. There was a *hollow*.

"Aldric."

He spun. One of the night guards stood at the end of the corridor, lantern raised — Tamms, old, loyal, a man who had served his father before him. Aldric's hand dropped from the panel and he arranged his face into princely melancholy, the expression the Regent had taught him to wear for the old wing.

"I couldn't sleep," Aldric said. "I was near him. That's all."

Tamms looked at him for a long moment across the candle-dark. And then the old guard did something extraordinary: he glanced up the corridor, and down it, and stepped closer, and said, very quietly, in the voice of a man setting down a weight carried twenty years:

"Your father stood just there. The week before he died. Same panel." His eyes flicked to it and away. "I walked him back to bed after. He was laughing. First time in months." Tamms straightened, resumed his blankness, and added, at full volume, "Come, sire. The floors are cold. You'll catch your death."

*Catch your death.* The old proverb, worn smooth as a stair-stone. But Tamms's eyes, above the lantern, were not smooth at all.

Aldric followed him back through the freezing corridors, obedient, docile, a son being led to bed — and inside, behind the locked door of his skull, in the one country the Regent had never managed to annex, he was already back at the panel, already prying, already reading.

His father had laughed. The week before he died, having hidden whatever it was, having finished the work — he had *laughed*.

The Regent stirred, uneasy, unable to say why. Somewhere in the walls of the old wing, in a hollow behind dark wainscot, a dead king waited with his arms full of paper, and his son was coming for him, and neither of them intended to knock.
