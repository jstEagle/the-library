## 22. Letters in the Wall

The crack in the wall had been there since Aldric was six years old.

He remembered it because he had once asked his father about it — a thin seam running from the floor to the wainscoting in the old study's eastern corner, too straight to be accident, too small to be anything worth mending. His father had knelt beside him, laid one finger along the fissure as if it were the spine of a sleeping animal, and said, *Some walls keep secrets so well they forget they're keeping them. Best not to wake them until you need what they hold.*

Aldric had laughed. He'd been six. He had thought it was a joke.

Now he stood in that same corner at the hour when even the palace seemed to hold its breath — past midnight, past the last patrol of the guard, past the point where the Regent's attention thinned into something like drowsiness. The candles he had brought were stubs stolen from the chapel, three of them, burning low and guttering in air that smelled of dust and old vellum and, beneath it all, faintly of iron. The study had been sealed for four years. No servant entered. No king entered. His father's chair still sat behind the desk at an angle, as though the man had only just risen from it.

*You should not be here,* said the voice inside his skull, soft as silk drawn over a blade.

"I couldn't sleep," Aldric murmured aloud, because speaking aloud made the lie more solid somehow, gave it weight and shape. "I wanted my father's books."

*Your father is dead.*

"His books aren't."

The Regent was silent for a long moment. Aldric had learned to read its silences the way sailors read the sea — the flat calm before weather, the restless chop that meant it was thinking, calculating, weighing his words against whatever ledger it kept of his soul. This silence was the thinking kind. Then: *Grief is natural. Grief is permitted. But do not linger where grief can be harvested by others.*

It always spoke like that. Tender and terrible at once. A mother's voice wearing a gauntlet.

*I'll only be a moment,* Aldric said, and knelt down in the corner, and put his fingers into the crack.

---

The stone came away easier than it should have. That was the first sign — the first confirmation of the thing he had glimpsed three nights ago, the thing that had been growing in him like a splinter working toward the surface of the skin. Walls did not come away easily. Walls in this palace especially did not, walls that had been mortared by masons who owed their positions to the crown and their silence to something older than the crown. But this section shifted with a grinding whisper, and behind it was a cavity lined with oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth was a bundle wrapped in waxed leather and tied with cord gone brittle as autumn grass.

Letters. Dozens of them. And beneath the letters, a book bound in grey calfskin with no title on its spine.

Aldric's hands were shaking. He noticed this with a strange detachment, as if the shaking belonged to someone else — some other boy kneeling in another king's study. *You are trembling,* observed the Regent, and there was something in its tone he could not name. Curiosity, perhaps. Or hunger.

"Cold," he said. "The room is cold."

*The room is warm,* said the Regent. *You are lying to me.*

He had never heard it say that before. Not directly. It had always preferred softer instruments — suggestion, redirection, the gentle closing of doors inside his own mind. But now it said *you are lying to me* in a voice like frost forming, and Aldric understood with sudden, total clarity that he had crossed a line whose existence he had only guessed at. There were things the Regent would forgive. There were things it would permit. And there was this: a son kneeling at his dead father's hidden wall, holding letters he had no right to hold, lying about why his hands shook.

*Put them back,* said the Regent.

"No," said Aldric.

The word fell out of him before he knew he was going to say it. Small word. Enormous word. He felt it land somewhere in the deep places of his own skull, and he felt the Regent feel it land, and for one suspended heartbeat neither of them moved — the boy and the thing wearing the boy, both staring at the same open wall, both understanding that something had changed and neither knowing yet what.

Then the pressure receded. Not gone. Withdrawn, the way a hand withdraws from a chessboard while the player considers the board.

*Read, then,* it said at last, almost gently. *Dead men's paper cannot hurt you.* And Aldric heard what lived underneath the gentleness, which was: *but I will be watching every word you read, and I will know every thought you think about it, and there is nowhere in your own head you can hide from me.*

He almost believed it. That was the horror of the Regent — not its cruelty, which was real but rare, but its competence. It had eaten four years of his nights and half his days and whole conversations he'd had with people whose faces he sometimes struggled to summon. It had rewritten his grief into duty and his doubt into loyalty so smoothly that he had thanked it for the service.

But three nights ago he had seen his father screaming. Not in memory — in *memory*, planted, curated, polished by the Regent's careful hands — and beneath the polish, for half a heartbeat, the real thing had shown through like bone through torn flesh. His father had not been mad. His father had been *afraid*. And afraid men do not seal letters in walls unless they believe the walls may be the last honest thing left to them.

Aldric broke the cord and unfolded the first letter.

---

*To my son, if he ever finds these — and if he finds them, then I am dead or worse than dead, and you have already begun to suspect what I could never bring myself to tell you at supper.*

The handwriting was his father's. Not the formal hand of royal edicts, all vertical strokes and ceremonial flourishes, but the quick slanted script of the man himself, the hand that had written Aldric birthday notes and margin comments on history lessons and once, memorably, a mock treaty dividing the palace kitchens between the cooks and the cats. Seeing it hit Aldric harder than he expected. Four years of the Regent's smoothing, and he had nearly forgotten what his father's actual handwriting looked like — the way the *r*s leaned like runners, the way he pressed so hard on his final strokes that the ink pooled.

*They will tell you I went mad. Listen to me: madness is the story they built because the truth was worse. The truth is that the crown speaks. It has always spoken. Every king since Erevan the Binder has heard it, and every king has believed himself the one man strong enough to wear it without being worn.*

*I wore it eleven years before I understood. Eleven years of waking with hours missing. Of finding decisions made in my name that I did not remember making — good decisions, mostly, wise ones, and that is how it keeps you. It does not ruin the kingdom. It governs it well. It governs it better than any living man could, because it has governed longer than any living man has lived. You will look at the ledgers and the peace treaties and the granaries full against famine, and you will think: whatever else is true, the realm prospers. And you will be right. And that rightness is the trap.*

Aldric stopped reading. His breath had gone shallow. Outside the shuttered window, far off across the sleeping city, a dog barked twice and fell silent, and the sound seemed to come from another world — a world of dogs and night watchmen and ordinary sleepers, a world that still belonged to itself.

*It eats memories,* his father had written. *Not carelessly, not cruelly, in its way — it takes the ones that threaten it. Your mother's face went first. I woke one morning and could not recall her voice, and when I went to her portrait in the long gallery I found I remembered the painter's name but not the woman. I wrote to her sister in Vhale. Her sister's reply sits in this bundle. Read it and weep, as I wept, and then understand: what it took from me it will take from you, and it will take it kindly, and you will thank it.*

*But it can be bound. It was bound once. Erevan did it — the first king, the founder — and the binding holds still, barely, fraying at the edges like everything old. Three keystones anchor it. The Regent knows this and fears it, and it has spent two centuries hunting the stones down, scattering them, burying them, killing their keepers. Two are lost to common knowledge. The third is not lost. The third has a keeper, and the keeper has a name, and I paid a great deal — more gold than sense, perhaps — to learn it.*

Aldric turned the page. His father had written the name in cipher — a child's cipher, absurdly simple, the substitution code they had used for riddles when Aldric was small. *Hollowmere,* it decoded to. *The blind oracle of Hollowmere. She was the first host. They keep her alive as a warning, or she keeps herself alive as a reproach; no one is certain which. If you have read this far, go to her. Trust no one at court. Trust the storm-caller if he lives — I sent him away to save him, and I have regretted it every day since, and if you are reading this, find him. His name is Gideon Dunmore, and he is the only man I ever met whom the crown feared.*

Aldric read the name three times.

*Gideon Dunmore.*

It meant nothing to him. That was the thing — it meant nothing, and he could feel the nothing like a hole in a tooth, the absence with edges. Somewhere in the eaten years there had been a conversation about a storm-caller. He was sure of it the way you are sure of a word on the tip of your tongue. His father had spoken of him. Had perhaps spoken of him often. And the Regent had taken those memories so cleanly that all that remained was the shape of their absence, a Gideon-shaped silence in the archive of his mind.

*It is watching me write this,* his father had written, in a hand grown suddenly cramped and hurried. *I can feel it tasting my intent. So I will write the rest plainly and quickly and trust to wax and stone and a dead man's luck. The binding ritual is in the grey book. Erevan's own hand, copied true. It requires three keystones fused into a key, and it requires the host's consent — hear me, Aldric, it requires YOU to choose, freely, to let the thing go, and it will fight that choice with everything it has and everything it is, because consent is the one lock it cannot pick. It can forge your signature. It cannot forge your surrender.*

*One more thing. When you doubt yourself — and you will, it will make certain of it — remember that you found these letters. Remember that a dead man trusted you enough to leave them where only his son would look. I could not beat it. Perhaps no one beats it alone. But you will not be alone. That is the whole secret, boy. That was always the whole secret.*

*Your father,
Marek*

---

Aldric sat on the cold floor of the sealed study for a long time with the letter open in his lap, and the candle stubs burned down around him, and he did not move.

He was not crying. He noted this distantly, clinically, the way he had learned to note things — the Regent disliked tears, called them inefficiency, and had trained the response out of him over years with the patience of a gardener training a tree against a wall. But something in his chest was doing what crying would have done if crying had been permitted, some silent hydraulic grief pressing outward against the ribs, and he let it press, and he breathed through it, and he picked up the grey book.

Erevan's hand. Or a copy of it. The pages were dense with diagrams — circles within circles, keystones set at three points of a triangle around a central figure, and lines of script in Old Revalic that Aldric could only half read. But the shape of it was clear enough. The geometry of unbinding. And at the center of the diagram, where the host must stand, the copyist had drawn a small figure with its arms open. Not bound. Not resisting. Open.

*Consent is the lock it cannot pick.*

He thought about that for a long moment. Then, carefully, he began to fold the letters back into their waxed leather — and stopped.

No. Folding them back meant returning them to the wall, and returning them to the wall meant leaving them where the Regent might someday find them, where a careless mason or a curious servant might find them, where four years of the Regent's growing confidence might eventually lead it to look. His father had hidden them from a crown that watched less closely. Aldric would have to hide them from a crown that watched everything.

He took off his shirt, and tore the lining from inside it, and sewed — badly, with needle and thread from the mending basket he had brought for exactly this purpose, fingers clumsy in the candlelight — the flattened letters and the thinnest pages of the grey book into the false seam. It was not craftsmanship. It would not survive a determined search. But it would survive a casual one, and casual was all he could afford to plan for.

The rest of the grey book — the heavier leaves, the diagrams — he wrapped again in oilcloth and pushed deep into the cavity, and fitted the stone back into the wall, and pressed it home until the crack showed once more, thin and innocent, a seam that kept its secrets.

*What did you find?* asked the Regent.

It had been quiet so long that the voice startled him. He rose, knees aching, and gathered the candle stubs, and stood a moment in the doorway looking back at the study — at his father's chair angled as though the man had only just left it, at the desk, at the corner where the wall kept its silence.

"Dust," Aldric said. "Mice. A dead man's empty room."

*Liar,* said the Regent, without heat, almost idly, the way one notes a change in the wind.

*But lie well,* it added. *You will need the practice.*

He closed the door behind him and walked back through the dark corridors with his heart hammering against the letters sewn into his shirt, and with every step he repeated his father's last instruction to himself like a prayer, like a rope thrown across a chasm:

*Find the storm-caller. Find Gideon Dunmore. You will not be alone.*

Somewhere beyond the palace walls, beyond the city, beyond the mountains he could not see in the dark — if his father's faith was worth anything, if a dead man's handwriting could reach across four silent years and take hold of his son's hand — a man who did not yet know he was being sought was already walking toward him.

Aldric reached his chambers, barred the door, and lay down fully clothed in the dark.

For the first time in four years, he slept without dreaming of anything at all — and woke at dawn with the names written on the inside of his eyelids: *Hollowmere. The oracle. Gideon Dunmore.* And beneath them, in his father's leaning, urgent script, a sentence he had not consciously memorized and could not forget:

*The solstice comes. Whatever you mean to do, do it before the renewal rite — after it, there will be no you left to do it.*

Eleven days.

He counted them the way a condemned man counts steps, and began, quietly, to plan how a king slips his own chains.
