## 23. The Renewal Rite Announced

The heralds went out at dawn, and by noon every bell in the capital was ringing it: the renewal of the coronation would be held on the solstice, before the assembled realm.

Gideon heard it first as a rumor in a market square, carried on a hundred mouths like a coin passed hand to hand. *The rite. The rite is set. The king will renew his crowning.* An old woman selling plums said it with pride. A boy sweeping fish scales off the cobbles said it with fear he didn't understand. Gideon stood among them holding a bag of apples he had forgotten to pay for, and felt the words land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

The solstice. Nineteen days.

He walked back to the inn without buying anything else, sat on the edge of the bed with his boots still on, and did the arithmetic again because he could not stop doing it. Three keystones. They had two. The third belonged to a keeper whose name they had only just pried out of Sera's stolen memory — a name wrapped in warnings, in a place called Hollowmere that no map bothered to name twice. And after the third stone, the unbinding itself, which required a king's consent they did not yet have, from a king who might already be too far gone to give it.

Nineteen days. His hands were steady until he thought about it, and then they weren't.

Wren found him like that when she came back up the stairs, hood damp with river fog, a paper of roasted chestnuts in her fist that she'd clearly bought for herself and now shoved at him anyway, because feeding him had become one of her ways of talking.

"You've heard," she said.

"The whole city's heard."

"Then you know what it means." She sat across from him, knees nearly touching his in the narrow room, and pulled her hood back. Her hair was flat with rain. There was a new scratch along her jaw he hadn't seen this morning. "It's not ceremony, Gid. It's a lock being thrown. Whatever the Regent is now — a lodger, a squatter, a thing wearing the crown like a second skull — the rite makes it permanent. Owner, not tenant. After the solstice there is no Aldric to save. There's just the Regent with a face."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "Because you're sitting here counting days like a man watching sand, and I need you counting them like a man running."

He looked up at her. Ten years in the valley had taught him patience the way drowning teaches swimming — thoroughly, and against his will. But Wren was right, and he hated how easily she was right, the way she always was about urgency, about motion, about the exact speed at which a thing must be done before it curdles into a thing that cannot be done at all.

"Hollowmere," he said. "How far?"

"Four days' ride if the roads are kind. Six if the inquisitors are thorough." She smiled without humor. "They're thorough."

Outside, the bells were still going. He could hear them through the shuttered window, all the different towers slightly out of step with one another, so that the sound never quite resolved — a ringing that overlapped itself, doubled, tangled, like the whole city stuttering the same word. *Renewal. Renewal. Renewal.* Somewhere below, a drunk was singing along badly.

Gideon ate a chestnut. It burned his fingers and tasted of ash and sugar, and he thought: nineteen days, and everything we love is on the other side of them.

* * *

Wren left the inn before dark, because Wren always left before dark; staying in one place past nightfall was a habit she had never managed to forgive herself for, not since the archive, not since Sera's name on a little glass vial in a drawer full of other people's worst days.

She went first to the tannery district, where a boy named Pip kept watch on the inquisitors' barracks for a fee she paid in small kindnesses and smaller coins. He met her in the shadow of a drying shed that smelled of lime and old blood, and he was bouncing on his heels, which was never good. Pip bounced when he was frightened. He bounced when he was proud. Tonight it was both.

"They posted it," he said. "On the barracks door. Big as a door, practically. The rite proclamation." He lowered his voice to something reverent and terrified. "And underneath it — Wren, underneath it — a warrant list. New names added."

"How many?"

"Seven. But one of them—" He swallowed. "One of them's got a sketch. Not a description. A *sketch*. Ink on good paper. Someone drew your face from memory, and it's good, Wren. It's better than good. It's the mole. The scar through the eyebrow. The way your mouth does that thing when you're lying, sort of crooked, like it's apologizing in advance."

Wren stood very still in the reek of the tannery and let that settle over her like cold water. A sketch meant someone close. A description came from a stranger who glimpsed you once; a likeness came from someone who had studied you. Someone who knew her face well enough to betray it had sat down with paper and ink and given the inquisition her mouth.

"Pip. Who else is on the list?"

"The storm-caller. Dunmore. And—" he hesitated, hating himself for the next word, she could see it happen in his face, "—the smuggler. Brannoc."

Brannoc. Of course. The smuggler-king who had sold their route once already and been paid in a pardon that apparently hadn't covered enough. Or perhaps it had, and this was simply what pardons looked like in a kingdom where the crown remembered everything and forgave nothing. She turned the thought over and found it didn't matter. What mattered was the clock.

"Were there dates on the warrants?"

"Just the rite. Everything's dated to the rite." Pip scratched his ear. "Like the whole city's being wound toward it. My mum says the baker's already taking orders for the feast bread. Three weeks of ovens going day and night. Everyone's making ready for a festival." He grinned, gap-toothed, thirteen years old and already fluent in the grammar of a police state. "Nobody's making ready for what happens after."

Wren put a hand on top of his head, briefly, the way you'd touch something breakable you couldn't afford to keep. "Go home. Stay home until the solstice. If anyone asks, you've never seen me and you don't know my name and you especially don't know my mouth."

"My what?"

"Never mind. Go."

She watched him scamper off between the drying frames, small and quick and alive, and then she stood alone in the lime-smell and made herself think the thought she'd been avoiding since the archive: *nineteen days, and they know our faces.*

There was a version of her — an older instinct, a colder one — that said run. Take the two keystones, take the map, take Gideon's storm and her own clever hands and get out beyond the Veil again, back to some other valley that forgot, and let the kingdom keep its crowned parasite. She had survived this long by knowing which doors to walk away from.

But the vial was still in her pocket. Sera's last words, played back a dozen times now, worn smooth as a river stone from handling: *it was bound once before, by a king who chose to forget how.* Every time she heard it she heard Sera's voice fraying at the edges, the recording degrading the way memories did, and every time she thought: soon even this will be gone, and then it will be as if she never said it, and then it will be as if she never lived, and the Regent will have won the only war it ever actually fought — the war against being remembered.

Wren pulled her hood up over the face that was now inked on good paper in some clerk's careful hand, and went to find Gideon, and told herself the clock was a weapon too. Deadlines cut both ways. The Regent had given them a date, which meant the Regent had given them a shape: nineteen days, one keeper, one king, one chance. You could move inside a shape. You could hide in it. You could sharpen yourself against its edges.

She just had to be faster than a drawing of her own mouth.

* * *

In the palace, Aldric woke at the fourth bell with ink on his hands.

He was getting used to waking like this — used to it the way a man gets used to a limp, cataloguing the damage each morning, flexing what remained. This time the ink was black and formal, the good iron-gall they used for edicts, ground fine with the particular bitterness that meant the chancery stores. His right hand was stained to the second knuckle. His wrist ached with the specific fatigue of long writing.

He did not remember writing.

*You worked late,* said the crown, warm against his scalp, a voice like a hand smoothing his hair. *You were diligent. The realm requires diligence.*

"What did I sign?"

*Orders. Necessary ones. You would only fret over the details, and you fret so beautifully, but it wastes you.*

Aldric lay still beneath the linen and performed the exercise his father had taught him — though his father hadn't taught it to him, not exactly, not in any memory he could verify; the exercise had simply always been there, planted so deep it felt native, which was precisely what made him trust it now. *Name three things you know. Name three things you believe. Notice the difference.*

Three things he knew: his own name. The weight of the crown, four pounds and a little over, he had weighed it secretly once with a grain scale and the number had pleased him absurdly. And the taste of the ink, faintly, on his lips, as if he had touched his mouth while writing.

Three things he believed: that his father had gone mad. That the Regent had saved the realm from him. That the renewals were mercy.

He lay in the dark and noticed, as he did every morning, the seam between the two lists — the widening, the way the known things shrank and the believed things swelled to fill the space, like water finding a crack. Nineteen days. After the solstice, would there be a difference at all? Would there be a *he* left to do the noticing?

*You are troubled,* the crown observed, with tenderness. *This is the burden of kingship. I carry it beside you. I have always carried it beside you.*

"Whose hand signed the orders?" Aldric asked the ceiling. "Mine, or yours moving mine?"

*What a question,* the crown murmured, almost laughing. *As if there were a difference. As if there had ever been a difference.*

That was the sentence he took with him into the day, into the dressing, into the procession of ministers who bowed and spoke and were spoken through. *As if there had ever been a difference.* It should have been comforting. It was designed to be comforting — the dissolution of the terrible boundary between self and guardian, the promise that surrender was really union. And most mornings it worked. Most mornings the warmth of it poured over him like bathwater and he floated.

But last week he had seen his father screaming. One real memory, surfacing through the planted ones like a body rising through lake ice, hands pressed flat against the underside of the world. *Don't let it finish the rite. Don't let it—* And then the ice had closed, and the crown had soothed him, and he had slept, and woken with ink on his hands.

Now, standing in the throne room while a herald read the rite proclamation in a voice polished for exactly this purpose — *on the longest day, before the assembled realm, His Majesty shall renew his sacred crowning* — Aldric felt the real memory stirring under the floor of him again. Not rising. Just turning over in its sleep. And beneath the herald's golden cadences he heard the other sound, the one no one else in the hall could hear, the sound of a clock that was not measuring time but consuming it.

Nineteen days. His father had died — or vanished, or been erased; the records disagreed, and the disagreement itself was surely the Regent's needlework — rather than let the rite complete. His father, mad or not, had understood something.

Aldric lifted his head and smiled at the assembled court, because smiling was a skill the crown approved of, and said nothing at all with perfect fluency.

* * *

The Regent did not experience anticipation. Anticipation was a creature's emotion, wet and impatient. What the Regent experienced was *sequence*: the orderly approach of a state it had spent twenty-three years constructing, each element arriving on schedule, mortar curing, walls rising.

The proclamation had gone out. The realm responded as realms did — bells, feasts planned, roads filling with pilgrims who would kneel in the cathedral square and feel, collectively, the renewal pass through them like a blessing, because the rite had always been built to be felt that way. Twenty years ago it had been a formality. Forty years ago, under the old king, it had been a tether checked once a year, a knot inspected for fraying. Under the Regent it had become something more ambitious: a consummation. This solstice, before ten thousand witnesses, the binding would be made total. No more annual maintenance. No more drift. The host's consent, extracted and formalized, would become permanent consent — the difference between a lease and a deed.

The Regent reviewed the security arrangements with satisfaction. The inquisitors had doubled their patrols. The warrant list had flushed three informants and one traitor out of the woodwork within a day of posting — the smuggler Brannoc, whose value had always been his priceability, had already sent word offering the route to Hollowmere in exchange for an expansion of his pardon. The Regent did not trust Brannoc; trust was another creature's currency. But information was information, and a route was a route, and the third keystone's keeper was the last variable the sequence could not yet account for.

Two keystones recovered by the fugitives. The Regent had expected this, had in fact permitted it in the early months, when the stones scattered in the wild were safer lost than found — but that calculus had expired. Now the stones had to come home, or be destroyed, or be buried so deep beneath the cathedral that no thief's key could ever answer them again.

And the storm-caller. The Regent turned its attention to Gideon Dunmore the way a jeweler turns a loupe upon a flawed stone. Exile had blunted him, but exile had also hidden him, and hidden things kept their edges. The reports from Ashmarket described lightning called from a clear sky, precise, obedient, *willing*. Willingness was the one ingredient the inquisitors' arts could not manufacture. It made the storm-caller dangerous in a way the Regent respected and, in its cold architecture of a mind, almost appreciated: a kingdom was best tested by worthy opposition, and the solstice rite deserved worthy opposition, the way a lock deserved a burglar worth locking against.

Let them come to Hollowmere. Let them gather their three stones. Consent was the lock, and the host was the key, and the host was *here*, dressed in white and gold, smiling at courts, sleeping in a bed the Regent had warmed for twenty years.

The sequence held. The clock ran. On the longest day of the year, before the assembled realm, the Regent would stop borrowing the king's life and begin owning it outright — and the realm would applaud, because the realm had always applauded its own captivity when the chains were gilded finely enough.

* * *

They rode for Hollowmere two nights later, ahead of the sketch, ahead of the warrants, ahead of the news that Brannoc had gone to the inquisitors a second time — news that would reach them too late, in the way such news always did, folded into the moment it could do the most harm.

But on that second night, camped in a hollow of gorse with the horses hobbled and the solstice moon barely a fingernail of light, Gideon sat up suddenly out of a dream of bells.

"Bells," he said aloud, and Wren was awake instantly, knife already in her hand, because that was Wren.

"It's the city," she started to say. "We're thirty miles—"

"No." His voice came out strange to him, resonant, as if the hollow of the gorse had become the inside of a drum. "I can hear them from here. All of them. Out of step. Ringing *renewal*." He pressed his palm flat against the earth. "The whole road is humming. Like wire before a storm."

Wren stared at him in the dark, and then she did the thing she did instead of comforting people: she made a joke, dry and quick, about how he'd finally found a use for all that caged weather — a warning system, a storm-caller's ear, better than any scout. But her hand, when she reached out and closed it around his wrist, was not joking. It was shaking very slightly, and so was his, and the two tremors matched, beat for beat, like the bells.

Nineteen days. Seventeen now. The clock was audible in every scene of their lives — in the market gossip, in the posted warrants, in the ink on a young king's hands, in the hum of the road itself, singing toward the longest day.

Gideon lay back down and did not sleep. Above the gorse the sky was clear and enormous and waiting, and somewhere east of them, in a blind woman's keeping, the last keystone sat in the dark like a held breath.

Hollowmere. Four days. Then whatever the keeper knew, whatever price she asked, whatever waited in the water there — the clock would be ticking louder still.
