## 24. The Keeper of Hollowmere

The road to Hollowmere did not so much lead as descend.

Gideon felt it in his knees before he understood it with his eyes — a long, patient slope through country that grew older the farther they went. The gorse gave way to bracken, the bracken to birch, and the birch stood pale and silent as candles that had never been lit. No birdsong here. He noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth: by the tongue's constant return to the gap.

"Four days of this," Wren said behind him, "and I already miss being chased."

"You don't."

"No," she admitted. "I don't." She came up beside him, walking with her hands loose at her sides the way she always did when the ground was unfamiliar — ready to steal whatever needed stealing, even balance. "But I'd rather be running from something than toward something that knows we're coming."

"The oracle can't know we're coming. She's blind."

Wren gave him the look she reserved for his most confident stupidity. "Gideon. She was the Regent's first host. If half of what Sera's memory said is true, she doesn't need eyes to see us. She probably knew we were coming before we did."

They walked on. The meres began as they neared evening of the second day: black water lying flat between hummocks of peat, each pool holding its own private piece of sky. The light came down amber and thick, and the water took it and did not give it back. Gideon kept glancing at the pools and finding himself looking away again quickly. There was something wrong with the reflections — not distorted, exactly. *Delayed.* A heron lifted off one mere and its reflection rose a heartbeat after the bird, as if the water had to remember how.

"Storm-caller." Wren had stopped.

"I see it."

"Do you? Because you keep looking at the sky like it owes you money instead of at the water like it's about to bite."

He almost smiled. It was strange, how easily her voice could pull him back into his body. Ten years in the valley had taught him to live inside his own skull like a tenant who never unpacked; four months on the road with Wren had undone some of that, without asking permission.

They made camp that night on a rise above the largest mere, and Gideon dreamed of drowning without water — of sinking slowly through air while a voice counted his memories aloud, ticking them off like coins, keeping only the ones that weighed the most.

* * *

On the third day they found the first warning.

It was a stone pillar, waist-high, standing alone in the peat, and it was carved all over with writing that had been deliberately effaced. Not weathered — *effaced*. Someone had chiseled every character smooth, but not deeply enough; under the right slant of light, the ghosts of the letters still rose like bruises.

Wren crouched over it, trailing her fingers across the worn surface. "I know this script. Old court-hand. My mentor used to fence documents in it." She paused. "Sera used to."

"What does it say?"

"It says—" She frowned, tracing harder. "Most of it's gone. But there's a word repeated. Here, and here, and here." Her finger tapped three times. "*Remember.*"

Gideon looked out over the meres, at the still black water and the pale birches and the enormous waiting silence. "Someone wanted this place forgotten badly enough to erase it. And then someone else carved *remember* where they'd erased it."

"Or the same person did both," Wren said quietly. "People are rarely simple, storm-caller. Especially people who've been alive a very long time."

She didn't ask how old the oracle might be. Neither of them said the number that Sera's memory-vial had suggested — the number that made no sense, that no human body should survive — because saying it would make the meres feel colder than they already did.

That night the fog came up off the water, and in the fog Gideon heard singing. It was not beautiful. It was an old woman's voice, cracked and patient, repeating a single line in a language he didn't know, and though he couldn't understand the words, he understood their function perfectly. They were a counting. Somewhere in the dark, something was keeping time — and unlike the coronation clock that ticked in all their lives now, this count had been going for longer than kingdoms.

* * *

Hollowmere itself announced nothing. That was the horror of it, Gideon thought later. They crested the last ridge expecting a fortress, a shrine, *something*, and found only a village — or the bones of one. A dozen cottages with their roofs caved in, walls furred green. A chapel without a bell. And at the center, ringed by dead reeds, the mere itself: wide, round, unnaturally still, like an eye that had been open too long to blink.

A single cottage near the water's edge had kept its roof. Smoke rose from it, thin and grey and straight, undisturbed by any wind.

"She's home," Wren whispered.

"Then why do I feel like we're the ones being waited for?"

They crossed the village in silence. Gideon's hands trembled — not the storm-tremor, or not only that; something older, the shiver of a man approaching a grave he has been dreaming about. At the cottage door, raised on the lintel, was one carving the erasers had missed or dared not touch: a woman seated, hands open in her lap, and above her head a crown rendered not in gold but in chains.

Wren read it and went pale. "Gideon."

"I see it."

"That's not decoration. That's a *portrait*."

Before either of them could move, the door opened on its own, inward, unhurried, exhaling the smell of tallow smoke and dried herbs and something underneath both — something mineral and cold, like the air off deep water.

"Come in, then," said a voice like dry leaves settling. "You've let the cold in long enough. And mind the threshold. It remembers feet."

* * *

The interior was small and immaculate. One room. A hearth. Shelves of clay tablets instead of books, hundreds of them, each incised with careful lines. A spinning wheel, unused, its wool gone grey with dust. And in the chair beside the fire sat the keeper of Hollowmere.

She was old the way mountains are old — not decrepit, just beyond argument. Her skin was mapped with fine white scarring, radiating outward from her temples in patterns almost regular, almost deliberate, like frost-ferns or like the branching of lightning frozen mid-strike. Her eyes were closed and had been closed so long the lids had thinned to translucence, showing the pale shapes beneath. Her hands lay open in her lap exactly as the lintel-carving showed.

Exactly.

"Well," she said. "The storm-caller and the thief. Sit. There are two stools; I had them made forty years ago, which will tell you how slow the post is here."

Gideon did not sit. "You knew we were coming."

"Child, I have known you were coming since before your valley learned to forget you. That is not a boast. It is the least pleasant fact about me." She turned her face toward him — toward him precisely, though her eyes never opened — and he felt the weight of her attention like a hand on his sternum. "Sit down. Both of you. What I have to show you is better taken sitting, and worse taken standing."

Wren sat first. Gideon followed, because the alternative was swaying on his feet like a fool.

"I am Maren," the old woman said. "Though I haven't used the name since the crown wore me. You may call me keeper, if names frighten you. Most things here frighten visitors, and I've stopped apologizing for it."

"You were the Regent's host," Wren said. Not a question. Her thief's voice, the one that extracted information by offering her own like bait.

"For nine years." Maren said it simply, the way one reports a length of road. "From my seventeenth year to my twenty-sixth. Do you know what that means, girl? It means I was queen of everything and resident of nothing. It means I signed laws I cannot remember signing. It means there are people alive today — well. People who were alive then — who loved me, whom I loved back, whose faces I have never seen, because the Regent ate those years whole when it moved on to a younger head." She smiled, and it was the worst thing in the room, worse than the scarred temples: gentle, and utterly without self-pity. "It left me my arithmetic. It always leaves you something small, so you understand what was taken."

"Why show us?" Gideon asked. His throat was tight. "Why keep yourself alive like this — out here, carved into warnings?"

"Because I am the warning." She said it without drama, which made it land harder. "The old king bound the Regent once and chose to forget the how of it — you know this much, or you wouldn't have come. But binding is not the same as *ending*, and forgetting is not the same as *solving*. So he made one provision. The first host would not be permitted to die. She would sit by the third keystone, blind, scarred, remembering everything the parasite discarded, until someone came who needed to see what waits at the end of the road you're traveling." Her head tilted. "Nine years in the crown. Sixty years as the cautionary tale. I have been a warning far longer than I was ever a person, and I have made my peace with it, mostly, on the good days."

The fire popped. Outside, the mere lay silent as held breath.

Wren's voice, when it came, was smaller than Gideon had ever heard it. "The boy. Aldric. He's been under it for — how long?"

"Three years, and some months. Younger than I was when it took me. Cleverer, too — it says so itself, in his head, flattering him. That's how it keeps them quiet. It makes captivity feel like aptitude." Maren reached out and touched the nearest shelf of clay tablets, running one finger along the incised lines with terrible tenderness. "These are my memories. All the ones it dropped, and all the ones I've recovered since. I write them while they're fresh, because paper burns and minds leak, but clay — clay keeps. Nine years of a life, pressed into dirt and fired. That is what the king you're trying to save will be, if your rite fails or comes too late. Not a corpse. A shelf."

Gideon thought of Aldric — the young king he had met only in glimpses, in Sera's memories and Wren's stolen intelligence — waking with hours missing, ink on hands he didn't remember using. He thought of a boy slowly becoming a shelf, and something in his chest went hard and hot.

"We need the keystone," he said.

"Yes."

"And you're going to give it to us."

Maren laughed — a dry, real sound, startling in the close room. "Oh, storm-caller. You have spent ten years among goats. Straight to business, no flattery, no fear. Good. Fear is useless here; I banned it decades ago." Her smile faded. "Yes, I will give you the keystone. It sits at the bottom of the mere, where I can watch over it, in the manner available to a blind woman. And yes, I will tell you what you must do with it. But nothing in Hollowmere is given free — the ghost at the drowned tower taught you that, I think. The bellfounder's daughter. She and I corresponded, once. Through the water. We were both keepers of prices."

Wren shifted. "What's yours?"

Maren turned her face toward the window, toward the black circle of the mere, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — lower, slower, the voice of someone reading from a document she knows by heart and hates.

"One of you must carry the burden."

Gideon felt Wren go still beside him. He did not look at her. He was afraid of what his own face was already doing.

"When the key tears the Regent loose," Maren said, "it will not simply dissipate. It is old, and hungry, and it will seize the nearest vessel strong enough to hold it — unless a vessel *offers*. Willing hands, willing mind. Someone must take it in, hold it, burn with it, long enough for the unbinding to finish. Long enough for it to die in them." Her blind eyes seemed, impossibly, to fix on Gideon. "And it will take its price out of whoever carries it. Memories. The soft ones first. The ones that make a life worth the name."

The fire crackled. Somewhere out on the mere, the heron cried once, late and lonely.

"How long?" Gideon heard himself ask. "How long would the carrying take?"

"A minute," Maren said. "Perhaps less. Perhaps an age. Time behaves strangely inside that thing." She folded her hands in her lap, the open gesture from the carving, and added, almost gently: "You should also know — and I tell you this because no one told me — that the Regent does not merely eat memories. It offers trades. It will whisper to the carrier, in that minute, in that hour: *give me this grief and I'll spare that love; give me the valley and I'll leave you the brother's face.* Every carrier bargains. No carrier wins. But oh, the temptation is exquisite, and I would be lying if I said the choice of what to surrender isn't, in its awful way, a kind of power."

Wren stood up abruptly. "We'll discuss terms outside."

"There are no terms," Maren said placidly. "There is only the price and the payer. Take the night. The mere doesn't sleep, and neither, you'll find, will you."

* * *

Outside, the fog had risen again, and the village ruins loomed and dissolved in it like thoughts you try not to finish. Wren walked fast, arms crossed tight, and Gideon followed her to the lip of the mere before she stopped.

"He can't carry it," she said, without turning around. "Aldric. He's spent three years inside that thing. If he takes it back in, even for a minute—"

"He won't. He'll insist he should. He's that kind of stubborn — you've seen it."

"Of course he'll insist, everyone insists, that's the whole cursed design of it—" She spun on him. In the fog her face was sharp and pale and frightened, and she was letting him see it, which for Wren was nearly unprecedented. "So it's you or me. That's the arithmetic the old woman is too polite to do out loud. You or me, Gideon."

"Wren—"

"And I've been doing sums since we left the quarry." Her voice shook, barely, at the edges. "You've lost enough. You lost ten years, you lost your brother, you lost the whole valley, and you think I don't notice that you talk about all of it like it happened to a stranger? If anyone carries that thing, it should be someone who's good at giving things up. That's my entire profession, storm-caller. I am *very* good at letting go."

"That's the worst argument I've ever heard."

"It's the best argument I've ever made."

He stepped closer. The mere breathed its cold against their backs. "Then hear mine," he said. "When the Regent whispers its trades — when it offers to deal away the soft memories one by one — whose past is worth less? Mine is a graveyard, Wren. Yours is a sister's face you fought a questioner to keep. Mine is ten empty years. Let it eat the empty years."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Out on the water, the fog parted briefly, and Gideon saw — or thought he saw — a figure standing far out on the mere's surface, robed and still, watching them with no eyes at all. Then the fog closed and the figure was gone, and he decided not to say so.

"We're not deciding tonight," Wren said finally, hoarse.

"No."

"Because if we decide tonight, one of us lies to the other, and I don't want to find out which of us it is."

"No," Gideon agreed quietly. "Not tonight."

Behind them, the cottage door stood open, spilling firelight onto the threshold that remembered feet. And in the doorway, unmoving, Maren's blind face was turned toward them both, and though no wind stirred, her lips were moving — counting, counting, the patient tally of sixty years — and Gideon understood, with a chill that had nothing to do with the mere, that she was not counting days.

She was counting hosts.

Three, so far. Herself. The old king's forgotten bargain. And the boy.

The fourth slot in her tally was empty, and waiting, and whichever of them stepped forward to fill it, the other would spend the rest of their life remembering — which was, Gideon thought grimly as they walked back toward the fire, the one fate in Hollowmere crueler than being forgotten.
