## 25. What the Oracle Sees

I have been blind for longer than most of the living have been alive, and I will tell you what the sightless know that the seeing do not: light is a rumor. It is a story the eyes tell, and like all stories, it can be bought and sold and rewritten. I sold mine a long time ago — or it was taken; the two are often the same transaction wearing different clothes — and in exchange I was given the other thing, the true thing, the thing that does not lie.

I see people the way you see weather.

Not their faces — faces are just weather too, fronts and pressures moving across the small geography of a skull — but what they carry. The storms they were born with and the ones they made. The rooms inside them, lit and unlit. The doors they have nailed shut and the doors that were never doors at all but windows painted on stone.

For two hundred years I have sat in the house at the center of Hollowmere and watched the weather of the world come up the road toward me. Pilgrims. Thieves. Kings. Once, a man with a crown already eating him, who came to beg and left as a warning, and they propped him in the eastern chair where he sits still, breathing when they remember to feed him, and every traveler who sees him understands what the Regent does to the ones it loves.

They call me the Keeper of Hollowmere. I was the first host. I was the first to survive it, which is a cruelty they dressed as a mercy, and I have had two hundred years to decide whether I am grateful, and I still have not decided, and I suspect I never will.

But today — today the weather coming up my road was worth the waiting.

* * *

I felt them before Brannoc's man announced them, the way you feel a storm before the first drop: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like copper rain.

The first one was the storm-caller. Gideon Dunmore. I knew the name the way you know a word you learned as a child and never used — Sera Voss had carried it to me years ago, folded up inside her like a letter she was afraid to open, and I had read it in her without her knowing, because that is what I do, that is what I am for now. *The valley that forgets,* she had thought, walking my road. *The man who buried his own name under ten years of goat shit and prayer.* Sera thought in long sentences even when she was dying. It was one of the things I loved about her, in the way the blind love — wholly, without the distraction of faces.

And now here he was, the letter finally opened, walking up my road with his grief strapped to his back like a pack he'd forgotten how to put down.

I felt the storm in him first. Of course I did. It is not a subtle thing, a storm-caller; it is a cathedral bell trying to fit inside a wine cup. He had caged it for ten years in a valley where magic could not follow, and I want you to understand what that means, what ten years of caging does: it does not kill the thing. Nothing kills the thing. It grows around the bars. It grows *through* them, the way a tree grows through a fence, so that when you finally open the gate the tree and the fence are the same wood and you cannot free one without freeing the other, and the freeing looks, from the outside, exactly like destruction.

His storm was threaded through him now. Every fear, every tenderness, every memory of his brother's face — the storm had roots in all of it. That was why he feared it so much, I saw. Not because it was powerful. Because it was *intimate*. To call it was to hand it everything he was and trust it to give him back.

He had not trusted anything in ten years.

Behind him came the thief, and oh, the thief was a different weather entirely. Wren. I tasted her from a mile off and laughed aloud, which startled the crows off the eastern roof, because she was — she was a *hole*. Not an emptiness; I want to be precise, because the difference is the whole of her. She had stolen so many memories and carried them so carefully that she had become a kind of architecture, a vault built out of other people's kept things. Her own childhood was in there somewhere, shelved between a fisherman's last morning and a soldier's first kill, and she visited it so rarely she sometimes doubted it was hers. But she knew where everything was. Every vial. Every stolen hour. She was the most organized person I had ever tasted, and she had organized herself around a wound she refused to name.

The wound had a name. It was her sister's name, though she did not carry the memory of the face anymore — the Questioner's cell had seen to that — and so the name floated in her, unanchored, like a bell whose tower has fallen.

I sat in my chair in the dark of Hollowmere and felt them come, and beneath my joy — and it was joy, do not mistake me; I had waited years for this weather — beneath the joy there was the other thing, the thing I always felt when the ones I had waited for finally came up the road.

Because I knew what I would have to ask of them.

I have been the keeper of the third keystone for two hundred years, and a keystone is not a stone, whatever the stories say. It is a *sentence*. It is the part of the binding that only completes when someone consents to carry the cost, and the binding's makers were not fools — they knew that no cost is ever paid unless someone, in the moment, chooses to pay it. So the third keystone waits in me, and it waits for the question, and the question is the same question it has always been, and every time I have asked it in two hundred years the answer has been no, or flight, or death, or worse — *yes from someone who did not understand*, and I have had to refuse the yes, because a yes given in ignorance unbinds nothing and only makes room.

The Regent cannot be destroyed. I have watched people try for two centuries. It can only be *torn loose*, and a thing like that, torn loose, does not dissipate like smoke. It falls. It falls like a hawk falls, with intent, and it will take the nearest open mind, and the nearest open mind is always the one standing closest to the tearing.

Someone must catch it. Someone must hold it. Someone must let it root in them for the length of time it takes a thief to drive a key home through a screaming god.

I knew, before they reached my door, which of them it would have to be.

That is the cruelty of sight, the true sight: it does not show you futures, because futures are not real things. It shows you *shapes*. And the shape of Gideon Dunmore's mind — storm-rooted, gate-opened, a fence grown through with lightning — was the only shape in the world that could hold the Regent and not be eaten before the key came down. The thief was too full of kept things; the Regent would strip her vault like a locust field. The storm-caller was already half ruin. Ruin, it turns out, is a kind of armor. You cannot devour a man who has already been devoured once and rebuilt himself around the absence.

I did not want to be right. I have been right for two hundred years and I would trade every correct vision I have ever had for one hour of being wrong.

* * *

They came into my house the way all visitors do: loudly, then not at all.

The blind house does that to people. Hollowmere is built of stone that remembers every hand that ever cut it — no, that is the quarry, forgive me, my memories braid together after two centuries — Hollowmere is built of silence, deliberate silence, walls thick enough to drink sound, and visitors always fill the quiet with their voices for the first few minutes and then the house takes the voices away and they hear themselves for the first time in years, and most of them do not like what they hear.

The storm-caller heard a man who had run twice. I watched it land on him — the taste of it, the flinch — and then, and this is why I had waited for *him*, I watched him not look away. He stood in my dark with his ruined weather and he let himself hear it.

The thief heard something else. She heard the eastern chair, where my predecessor breathes his long warning, and she went to him — I felt her cross the room, felt her fingers find the two-hundred-year-old crust of wax on his wrist where the candles have dripped on him every solstice since they sat him there, and I felt the moment she understood what he was, and I felt her grief, which was not for him. It never is. The living grieve sideways. She grieved for a sister whose face she had paid away, and she stood in my dark holding a dead man's wrist and I thought: *yes. This one. This is the one who can drive the key.* A thief's hands do not shake at the moment of the taking. It is the one thing they are for.

"Keeper," the storm-caller said, and his voice was careful, the voice of a man who has learned that words are doors and some doors open onto weather. "They said you were blind."

"They said correctly." I let him hear me smile. It is one of the few weapons I have left, the smile; the blind learn to make it do the work of eyes. "They also said I was mad, and a saint, and a warning, and a fraud. The last is the nearest to true. A fraud keeps what is not hers and sells it back at a price. I keep the third keystone and I will sell it back at a price. You may decide for yourselves which of us the word fits."

"We've come for the stone," Wren said. Direct. Good. The indirect ones waste my hours, and I have fewer than they think, even now, even with the Regent's long patience wearing thin in the capital like a rope over a stone edge. I had felt the solstice coming for a year, the way you feel a storm coming — and I am an authority on that feeling. The renewal rite. The permanence. After the solstice there would be no tearing the Regent loose at all, only a king-shaped husk and a god wearing it comfortably forever.

"I know why you've come," I said. "I have known you were coming since before you knew. Sera Voss walked this road eleven years ago with your name folded inside her, Gideon Dunmore, and she sat where you are sitting and she asked me if the valley could hold you forever, and I told her the truth, which is that nothing holds forever, and she wept, and I have been waiting for her weather to come back up my road ever since. It came back in pieces. In you. In the thief who carries her last words in a vial against her heart."

I heard Wren's breath catch. Yes. The vial. Sera's memory, bought back from the archive of stolen things at a price Wren had not told the storm-caller — I saw that too, the small dishonesty shelved neatly between the others, the way she shelved everything. They had not yet learned to lie to each other well. They would. Lying to each other well is what love looks like in people who survive by craft.

"Then you know why we need it," Gideon said. "The third keystone. The binding. The king is — there's a thing in the crown, and it eats him, and in nine days it makes him permanent."

"Nine days," I said, and let the silence do its work. "And you have crossed a kingdom, survived a quarry that found the lie in your exile, broken a Questioner, lost a smuggler who died better than he lived, and come at last to a blind woman in a house of silence. And you believe I will simply *give* you the keystone, because your need is great and your cause is just."

"Isn't that how it works?" Wren said. "The stories say—"

"The stories," I said, "were written by people who never had to pay. Sit down. Both of you. What I have to tell you is not a story. It is a sentence, and I am about to pass it, and in two hundred years of passing it I have never once enjoyed the telling."

They sat. I heard them sit — the storm-caller like a man lowering himself into cold water, the thief like a cat arranging itself, ready to leave. I turned my true sight on them, both of them, full and open, and I let them feel it, because they deserved to know what was reading them. The thief flinched. The storm-caller did not. He leaned *into* it, the fool, the brave fool, the way a man leans into wind.

"Here is what I see," I said. "I see you, Gideon Dunmore, and I see that the valley did not cage your storm. It married you to it. Ten years of bars, and the bars grew through, and now there is no version of you without the lightning in it, and you have finally stopped pretending otherwise. I see that you called it over the cathedral district three nights ago and tore the sky open like cloth, and that it felt like coming home, and that the coming-home frightened you more than the inquisitors did. I see that you ran from the inn when they took the thief, and that you have not forgiven yourself, and that you will not run again, and that this resolution is the most dangerous thing about you, because a man who will not run will stand anywhere, even where he should kneel. I see your brother. I see the duel. I see that you have told one person the truth of it and that her staying undid you more than any judgment ever could, and I see that you do not yet know what to call the thing you feel for her, and I will not tell you, because it is more fun to watch you arrive at it badly."

"Keeper—" he began.

"I see *you*, Wren of no family name, because you sold your family name to a fence in Corvane when you were nine and you have regretted it every day since, not for the money but because it was the first thing you ever stole from yourself. I see a vault. I see a fisherman's last morning, a soldier's first kill, a queen's boredom, a hundred small kept hours, all shelved and dusted and *cared for*, because you are the only thief I have ever tasted who loves what she steals. I see the hole where your sister's face was, and I see that you have built the whole architecture of yourself around that hole, the way a tree builds around a lightning scar, and I see that you are planning, even now, even sitting in my dark, to take a price from Gideon that you mean to pay yourself. Do not bother denying it. I do not need your faces. I have your weather, and your weather is *guilt with a plan*."

The silence after that was the good silence, the honest one. Then Wren, flat and defiant, the defiance that is her identity, the thing she is instead of a self: "And if we are? Planning that? What's it to you?"

"Everything," I said. "It is everything to me. Because the price I am about to name is real, and one of you will pay it, and I have known which one for eleven years, and I have spent those eleven years hoping with everything left in me that I was wrong."

I rose from my chair. Two hundred years, and I rose, because some sentences must be stood for.

"The Regent cannot be killed," I said. "I was its first host. I know it the way you know a scar — from the inside, in the years before they cut it out of me and left me this, this *sight*, this payment. It can be torn loose. The three keystones make the key; the key makes the tearing possible; but the tearing itself — listen to me now, children, because this is the part the stories leave out — the tearing itself requires an open mind to fall into. The Regent will not dissipate. It will *fall*, like a hawk, like a hammer, and it will take the nearest vessel, and the nearest vessel will be the one doing the tearing, unless someone else stands in the way. Someone must catch it. Someone must hold it in their mind while the key is driven home. And it will not be gentle. It will eat as it falls. It will take the softest things first — the faces, the valleys, the ten lost years, everything a person is made of — and it will keep eating until the key comes down or there is nothing left to eat."

I heard Gideon breathing. Storm-slow. Already understanding. I hated him a little for understanding so fast.

"And you know," he said quietly, "which of us it has to be."

"I have known since Sera Voss sat in your chair and wept," I said. "The thief's mind is a vault — full, defended, *valuable*. The Regent would strip it in a heartbeat and wear her kept things like a coat. But you — you, storm-caller — you are already ruin and rebuild. You grew through your own cage. There is no clean place left in you for it to root, and a thing that cannot root can be held, and a thing that can be held can be keyed. It must be you. It can only be you. That is the price of the keystone: not a coin, not a memory, not a truth told to a ghost. A *body*. A mind. You, Gideon Dunmore, standing between a falling god and the woman you love, holding it, while she drives the key through both of you."

The silence went on so long I counted my own heartbeats. Forty. Fifty. The eastern chair breathed its long warning breath. Somewhere in the walls the house drank the sound of two people not speaking.

Then Wren, and her voice was the voice of a knife being drawn: "No. Find another way. There's always another—"

"There is not," I said, and I was gentle, because the truth deserves gentleness even when it is a hammer. "There has never been another way. I have had two hundred years to look for one. I asked the price of every pilgrim, every king, every clever thief who ever walked my road. The binding's makers built it this way on purpose, because they knew that a cost no one chooses is a cost no one pays. The keystone is yours, Wren. I give it freely, now, tonight — take it from the hollow beneath the eastern hearth, take it and go and save your king. But the price comes due at the tearing, and the price has a face, and the face is his."

I heard her stand. I heard her cross to the hearth. I heard the stone scrape and her breath change as her fingers found what two hundred years of pilgrims had died trying to find, and I heard her hold it, and I heard her not thank me.

And I heard Gideon Dunmore, the storm-caller, the man who ran twice and would not run again, say the words I had known for eleven years he would say, the words that were the whole reason the weather of my long blind life had been worth enduring:

"Then we'd better make sure she has time to drive it home."

* * *

They left before dawn. I stood in my doorway — I still stand in doorways; the body keeps its habits like a miser keeps coins — and I felt them go down my road, the storm and the vault, side by side, each carrying a price the other didn't know had already been accepted.

Because yes. I will confess it, here, in the last of my telling. He did not tell her. He took the keystone's condition into himself the way he takes everything — silently, like weather — and she walked beside him planning her own version of the same sacrifice, and neither of them said *yes* out loud, and neither of them said *no*, and they lied to each other with their silence the way only the very best liars can.

I have watched ten thousand weathers come up my road. I have never once seen two people so perfectly built to destroy themselves for each other.

The solstice is in eight days. The Regent has begun to hunt. And far to the east, in the capital, a young king is waking from a planted dream with his father's screaming caught between his teeth — I felt that too, this morning, a crack in the god's long patience, a hairline fracture running through a crown.

They are going to need every day of it.

And I will sit here, in the house of silence, with the breathing warning in the eastern chair, and I will do the one thing left to a keeper whose sentence has finally been passed: I will listen for the storm, and I will hope — for the first time in two hundred years — to be wrong.
