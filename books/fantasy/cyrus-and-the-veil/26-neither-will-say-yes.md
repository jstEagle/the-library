## 26. Neither Will Say Yes

Gideon woke before dawn in the guest cell of Hollowmere's sanctuary and lay still, listening to the oracle's breathing through the wall.

It was a strange sound to fall asleep to and stranger to wake by — slow, wet, deliberate, like water finding its way through stone. The old woman had not stopped breathing like that in forty years, Wren had told him. Not since the Regent left her. As if her lungs had learned its rhythm and never unlearned it, a parasite's cadence outliving the parasite itself.

He stared at the ceiling beams and turned the price over in his mind for the hundredth time since the oracle had spoken it.

*One of them must carry the crown's burden when it is torn free.*

She had said it plainly, without cruelty, the way a physician names a disease. The Regent would not simply die when the key came home. It would flee. It would seize whatever mind stood nearest, whatever will was weakest or strongest — she hadn't been clear on which, and Gideon suspected she didn't know — and for the span of the unbinding rite, someone would have to hold it. Hold it the way you hold a drowning animal: knowing it will claw you, knowing you cannot let go until the other hand drives the blade.

Wren thought he hadn't understood. He could see it in the way she talked around the subject, circling it like a bird over deep water, describing everything except the center. She believed that when the moment came, he would freeze. That the storm-caller who had run from his own power for ten years would stand at the altar with his hands full of god-rot and do nothing.

She wasn't wrong about the running. She was wrong about what he'd learned from it.

Because here was the arithmetic no one else in the room could do: Gideon Dunmore had already lost everything soft. Ten years of valley mornings. His brother's face — and gods, even now, saying the name *Tam* in his own head felt like pressing a bruise, but the shape of him, the crooked smile, the way he'd said *Gid* like an accusation — all of it lived behind glass now, remembered rather than felt. The storm had taken his brother and the exile had taken the rest, and what remained was a man built mostly of scar tissue and stubbornness.

What more could the Regent eat? A few goat-tending years? The smell of rain on dry stone?

But Wren — Wren was made entirely of things worth eating. Her sister's face, already half-stolen once by the Questioner's needles; the archive of small bright thefts she carried like coins sewn into her coat lining; Sera Voss's voice, which she alone still heard; the fierce ridiculous hope that had carried her across a kingdom after a dead woman's handwriting. If the Regent took Wren, it wouldn't be taking scraps. It would be taking a whole person, mid-becoming.

So the plan was simple, and he had built it the way he built everything now: quietly, load-bearing, without ornament. When the rite came, he would step between the king and the tearing. He would take Aldric's place at the center. And he would tell Wren afterward — or she would work it out, because she was cleverer than him, and hate him for it, and live.

That was the part he kept flinching from. Not the holding. The living-after, hers.

Through the wall, the oracle exhaled.

Gideon got up, dressed in the dark, and went down to the sanctuary kitchen to make bread, because his hands needed something honest to do while his mind did this other work.

* * *

Wren found him there an hour later, flour to the elbows, kneading dough with the grim concentration of a man throttling something.

"You're up early," she said.

"Couldn't sleep." He didn't look up. "Bread doesn't wait."

"Bread waits fine. Bread is mostly waiting." She hopped onto the scarred table, boots swinging, and stole a pinch of raw dough, and he let her, because letting her steal small things was one of the few languages they'd perfected. "The oracle's awake. She wants to teach us the rite properly today. All of it. The words, the order, the—" she paused, and he watched her decide not to say *the price*, "—the timing."

"The timing matters," Gideon agreed mildly.

"You've been thinking about the timing."

"I've been thinking about bread."

Wren looked at him. She had a thief's eyes — quick, cataloguing, always three moves ahead of your hands — and right now they were doing exactly what they did when she lifted a purse: reading him for the seam. For the place where the true thing was hidden under the performed thing.

"Gideon."

"Hm."

"What are you planning?"

He kept kneading. Fold, press, turn. "I'm planning to learn the rite. I'm planning to get the key to the solstice rite without getting us killed. I'm planning to sleep sometime before the end of the world. What are you planning?"

"I asked first."

"And I answered first. We're even."

She slid off the table and came around to his side, close enough that he could smell the road on her — dust, horse, the faint resin of the lockpick oil she was never without — and she put one finger flat on the dough, stopping his hands. "You do this thing," she said quietly. "When something frightens you, you get domestic. You make food. You mend things. You become so aggressively useful that nobody asks what's underneath."

"That's not a thing I do."

"It's absolutely a thing you do. I watched you re-hang a door in Ashmarket while inquisitors were searching the street outside. You were *oiling hinges*, Gideon. Nobody oils hinges during a manhunt unless they're hiding from their own head."

He wanted to laugh. It was too accurate to laugh at. "And what do you do," he said instead, "when something frightens you?"

Her jaw tightened. There it was — the same flicker he'd seen in the quarry, in the archive, every time the conversation bent toward the price. "I make jokes," she admitted. "Bad ones. Rapidly."

"Yes."

"So neither of us is going to say it."

"No," Gideon said. "Neither of us is going to say it."

They stood there, flour-dusted and silent, two liars holding the same secret in opposite pockets, each convinced the other couldn't feel its weight. Then Wren took her finger off the dough, kissed him — brief, hard, tasting of stolen bread — and went to wake the oracle, and Gideon stood alone in the kitchen understanding, with terrible clarity, that she knew. That she had known since Hollowmere. That her bad jokes were armor plate over the exact same plan he was building, and that she was going to try to beat him to it.

Fold, press, turn. He kneaded the bread and did not think about how good she was at going first.

* * *

The oracle taught them the rite in the sanctuary's inner room, where the walls were hung with forty years of a blind woman's weaving — tapestries that recorded nothing she had seen and everything she had witnessed, threads dyed in colors that meant grief, meant complicity, meant the particular gray of a mind being slowly rewritten.

"The words are old," she said. Her voice was dry as the loom-threads. "Older than the binding. The binding was written *over* them, the way ivy grows over a wall. You'll speak the wall away."

She made them repeat each phrase until it sat in their mouths like a stone. Wren learned fastest — of course she did; memory was her trade, and these were words designed to be remembered against forgetting — but Gideon noticed something the oracle's lessons kept circling and never landing on.

The rite required consent. They'd known that since chapter — since the beginning, since the fused key had shown them its nature. But listening to the full text now, Gideon heard how deep the requirement ran. It wasn't just Aldric who must consent to be freed. The words were shaped like a door with two sides. Someone had to open it from the outside. Someone had to *hold the frame* while the captive walked through — and the frame-holder's part of the liturgy was not a spell. It was an offering. *Take me instead, if it must have a throat. Take me willingly.*

The Regent fed on coercion. It always had — that was the whole architecture of it, a guardian grown from fear, ruling through erased grief. The only thing it could not digest, the oracle said, was a gift freely given. Which meant the frame-holder could not be forced, tricked, or sacrificed by another's decision.

It had to volunteer. Out loud. In front of everyone.

Which meant, Gideon realized as the oracle's voice droned through the closing phrases, that his quiet plan had a flaw the size of a cathedral. He couldn't simply step in. He would have to *say it*. Say it in front of Wren, in front of Aldric, in front of the assembled realm — say *take me* and mean it, and give Wren the length of one heartbeat to shout him down.

One heartbeat. He'd have to time it perfectly. He'd spent ten years learning patience; surely he could spend it here.

Across the room, Wren repeated the frame-holder's phrase with her eyes closed, and her voice did something he'd never heard it do. It went gentle. Not soft — Wren was never soft — but *gentle*, the way a picklock is gentle, precise and total and aimed at one small hidden thing.

"*If it must have a throat,*" she recited, "*let it be one that has already given itself away.*"

The oracle's blind face turned toward Gideon then, and held there, and he had the distinct uncomfortable sensation of being read like a page.

"You both intend to carry it," the old woman said. Not a question.

Silence. Wren opened her eyes.

"Don't," Gideon said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to argue that it should be you, and I was about to argue back, and we'd burn the whole day on it and neither of us would move." He wiped his hands on his apron, though they were clean; the flour had gone hours ago. "So let's not."

The oracle smiled — a small, terrible smile, the smile of someone who had been a host and knew precisely what they were volunteering for and was watching them volunteer anyway. "Love," she said, "arrives disguised as argument more often than any other way. I wove a tapestry about it once. It's on the north wall. It looks like two people shouting in a doorway during a fire."

"We're not—" Wren started.

"You are," said the oracle. "Now. Again, from the third phrase. Both of you. The solstice is four days off, and the Regent does not wait politely."

* * *

That night Gideon could not sleep, and gave up trying near moonset, and climbed the sanctuary's outer stair to the roof where Wren was sitting with her knees drawn up, watching the road below Hollowmere.

He sat beside her. Below them the land fell away in folds of dark, and far off — a day's ride, maybe less — the lights of the capital burned in a low golden smear, the city where a boy-king was being dressed for his own consumption. Four days.

"Wren."

"Hm."

"If it comes to it. At the rite. If something goes wrong and there's a choice—" He stopped. He had rehearsed this speech forty times in the dark and it fell apart the moment it met air, the way rehearsed things do. "I need you to promise me you won't step in front of me."

She didn't answer immediately. She picked at a loose thread on her coat cuff, the one she always picked at, the one he suspected she kept loose on purpose, a fidget with a decade of history in it.

"Promise you won't step in front of *me*," she said finally.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have." She turned to look at him, and the moonlight did her no favors and she didn't need any; her face was all edges and honesty in it, which was its own kind of beauty, the kind you had to earn. "You want me to promise to let you destroy yourself because you've decided your life is worth less than mine. And I can't. Because it isn't, and because I know why you think it is, and I love you too much to agree with the lie."

The word landed between them like a dropped coin.

Neither of them moved. Above the valley a nightjar called, twice, and somewhere below a dog answered, and the world went on being ordinary while the two of them sat very still around the thing she'd just said.

"You love me," Gideon said, stupidly, because someone had to.

"Don't make it weird."

"It's already weird. Everything about us is weird. You stole my map."

"I returned your map."

"You returned my map *after reading it*."

"After *memorizing* it. Reading implies effort." But her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, and she pressed her mouth shut, and Gideon understood that the joke was the only structure holding her upright, and reached over and took her hand — the pickpocket's hand, quick and scarred at the knuckles — and held it, and did not let go.

"Here's what I can promise," she said, quieter now, looking at the distant city rather than at him. "I promise that whatever happens at the solstice, I will not decide for you. I won't shove you aside. I won't trick you. Your choice will be yours." A breath. "And you'll extend me the same courtesy."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know what you asked."

"Wren—"

"And I know what you're going to do, Gideon. I've known since the oracle spoke. You've been walking around Hollowmere like a man settling his affairs — you mended the gate hinge, you wrote letters to people who won't remember receiving them, you made enough bread for a funeral." Her thumb moved over his knuckles, back and forth, an unconscious tenderness that undid him worse than any argument. "You think I don't recognize someone preparing to pay? I grew up in the archive of stolen things. I know what payment looks like."

He had nothing. Every deflection he owned — and he owned good ones, ten years' worth — lay between them in pieces.

"Then you know I'm right," he managed.

"I know you believe you're right. I know you've done the arithmetic — the valley's already gone from you, your brother's already glass, you think you're pre-spent coin." She finally turned to face him fully, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "You're wrong. You're wrong because the Regent doesn't eat memories, Gideon. It eats *having*. The having of a morning. The having of a person. And you have me, and I am not glass, and I am not pre-spent, and if you feed yourself to that thing to spare me, you will be spending the one future I actually want."

The nightjar called again. Gideon found that his eyes were burning and did not trust himself to speak.

"So," Wren said, and her voice steadied into something almost light, almost the old thieving lilt, "we're at an impasse. Two volunteers, one burden, neither willing to lose the argument."

"Neither willing to win it, either," Gideon said. "Winning means the other one pays."

"Gods." She laughed — a broken, real laugh, wiping her face with her free hand. "We're the worst conspirators in the kingdom. Brannoc would disown us."

"Brannoc would sell us."

"Brannoc *did* sell us, probably, he just hasn't gotten around to telling us yet." She said it lightly, tossing it away, and neither of them knew yet — could not yet know — how the words would come back to them wearing iron and torchlight, how the smuggler-king's pardon was already being signed in a tent two miles down the road, how the ambush was already moving toward Hollowmere through the dark like weather.

They sat together on the roof until the sky went gray at the edges, hands linked, each privately rehearsing the same single heartbeat, each determined to be faster than the other, and neither saying yes, and neither saying no, and the solstice coming on like a tide.

Below them, on the road, a lantern swung — early, wrong, moving fast toward the sanctuary gates.

Wren saw it first. She always saw it first.

"Gideon," she said, and all the tenderness went out of her voice and left only the thief, sharp and certain. "Someone's coming. And they're not slowing down."
