## 27. Brannoc's Betrayal

The lantern came on too fast for a pilgrim and too steady for a drunk, and Gideon was moving before Wren finished speaking — down the roof's shallow pitch, over the drain-spout worn smooth by a century of rain, dropping the last six feet into the herb garden where the oracle's sisters grew things that should not have been named aloud. The soil took his boots without sound. That was the first mercy of the night. He had learned, in ten years of listening to a valley that wanted nothing from him, to notice mercies early, because they were always followed by their opposite.

Wren landed beside him with the softness of something that had never once expected the ground to catch her.

"Gate," she breathed.

He saw it. The sanctuary's iron gate — older than the kingdom it guarded, worked with sigils that hummed when he passed them, sigils that had let him in three nights ago because the oracle had told her sisters *the storm-caller comes; open wide* — stood shut. And against it, lantern raised, stood a man in a grey robe, and behind him, spilling down the road like ink finding its level, were more.

Inquisitors. A dozen at least. Maybe more in the dark past the reach of the lantern-light.

"They can't have followed us," Wren said. "We burned the back-trail. We doubled twice through the fens—"

"We didn't burn anything," Gideon said, and heard his own voice go flat and cold, the way it did when his mind arrived at a place his heart refused to go. "Someone sold us."

He watched her arrive at the same name he had already reached. Watched her jaw tighten around it.

"Brannoc."

"He knew our route to Hollowmere. He's the only one who did." Gideon's hands were shaking, and not with storm. "I gave him the map's western leg as payment. I handed him the road we'd take, and the hour we'd take it, and the gate we'd come through."

"You bought Wren back with dirty coin," Wren said quietly. "You told me that. You said you knew what it cost."

"I said I knew what I paid," Gideon answered. "I didn't know what he'd sell."

Below them, at the gate, the lead inquisitor raised one hand — a small gesture, almost gentle, the way a man quiets a horse — and the sigils along the iron began to scream. Not loudly. Not in any sound the ear could hold. But Gideon felt them die, one by one, like candles pinched between wet fingers, the old protections of Hollowmere going dark ward by ward until the night itself seemed thinner, more porous, easier for grey robes to move through.

The gate opened from the outside.

They had keys. Of course they had keys. Somewhere between Brannoc's ledger and the Regent's chancery there existed a document, signed and sealed, that made this all legal — that rendered the desecration of a holy sanctuary a lawful act, performed under lawful writ, by lawful men who would sleep soundly afterward. Gideon understood that now with a clarity that felt like frost forming: the Regent did not send monsters. It sent clerks.

"Back door," Wren whispered. "Through the reliquary. There's a postern the sisters use for—"

"The oracle," Gideon said. "She's blind, not deaf. She'll have known they were coming since sundown."

* * *

They found her standing in the middle of the chapel of Hollowmere, which was not a chapel at all but a long low room where the ceiling had been left unfinished on purpose, so that the sky could look in. She stood with her face turned up toward the stars she could not see, and her hands were folded, and she was not afraid. That was the worst of it. Around her the blind sisters moved with terrible calm, banking fires, carrying the youngest novices toward the cellars, doing the ordinary arithmetic of a house about to be violated.

"They are here for the stone," the oracle said when they came close. Her voice was dry and even, like pages turning. "And for you. They do not know which they want more, and their confusion will buy you perhaps two hundred breaths."

"Then we run," Gideon said. "Postern. Now."

"No." She turned her ruined eyes toward him, and though they saw nothing, he felt seen anyway, thoroughly, the way a specimen is seen. "You will run, yes. But listen, storm-caller, because two hundred breaths is not enough time to be clever in. The key sleeps beneath the altar-stone in my keeping room. Take it. Take it and go north through the fells, where horses fail and grey robes walk badly. And take this—" she pressed something into his hand, small and cold, a ring of plain iron, "—to the keeper of the high pass. Tell her the last host sends her love, and her orders."

"What orders?"

"The ones I've carried thirty years," the oracle said, "waiting for someone worth giving them to."

Beyond the walls, iron rang. The inquisitors were inside the outer court.

Gideon ran. He would think about that later — how easily he ran, how the body remembered exile before the mind could argue — but there was no later yet. There was only Wren prying up the altar-stone with a knife-blade while the oracle stood guard at the door with a candle-snuffer held like a scepter, only the keystone coming free of its bed of salt and silk, warm as a living thing, heavier than its size, humming against his palm in a frequency that matched, horribly, the storm still caged behind his ribs.

Three stones now. One in his pack, two fused against his chest, and all of them singing quietly to each other like separated choir-voices finding their line again.

Then the chapel door burst inward, and the two hundred breaths were spent.

* * *

There were four of them in the doorway, then six, grey-robed, faces smooth as river stones, each holding a rod of black glass that drank the candlelight. And at their center, walking slowly, savoring it, came the questioner himself — the one whose cell Wren had left with pieces of herself missing. He looked rested. That was what Gideon noticed. The man looked like he had slept well since the last time they met, and Gideon's rage arrived so fast and so total that the candles guttered in their sconces, and somewhere overhead thunder answered across a sky that had been clear all night.

"Storm-caller." The questioner smiled. It was a kind smile. It was the worst thing in the room. "The crown has been generous with me since your thief escaped. Do you know what generosity buys, in my profession? Patience. Patience, and better tools."

"You're in a sanctuary," Gideon said. His voice came out low and strange to him. "This house predates your crown. Your writ means less than the dust on it here."

"The crown's writ," said the questioner, "means whatever the crown says it means. That is rather the point of crowns." He lifted his black-glass rod, and the nearest sister — old Mother Cassel, ninety years of service — went rigid, and from her, thin as thread pulled through cloth, something silvered out of her mouth and into the rod. A memory. Taken casually, the way a man takes an apple off another man's tree. "Now. The stones, if you please. And the thief. In whichever order causes less mess."

What happened next happened fast, and Gideon would spend the rest of his life assembling it from fragments.

Wren threw her knife — not at the questioner, at the candelabrum, plunging half the room into darkness. Gideon called the storm, or the storm called itself, lightning cracking down through the unfinished ceiling into the black-glass rods, shattering two, and the shriek of breaking enchantment was so loud that every sister in the cellar below would later swear the mountains themselves had cried out. The oracle's voice cut through it all, calm as ever, saying words in a language older than the valley, older than the Veil —

—and the doors at the far end of the chapel exploded.

Not opened. Exploded. Off their hinges, into the room, and through the smoke and splinters came men in oiled leather and no livery at all, and at their head, broad as a barn door, gray-bearded, bleeding already from a cut above his eye, came Brannoc.

Brannoc. Who had taken Gideon's coin and Gideon's route and sold both to the Regent's clerks for a pardon written on vellum. Brannoc, who stood now in the wreckage of the doorway with twenty of his smugglers at his back and a war-hammer in fists like anchor-stones, and who looked directly at Gideon across the burning chaos and shouted, above the storm-noise, above the screaming:

"DUNMORE! THE PARDON WAS FOR *THEM*, NOT ME!"

It made no sense. None of it made sense, and there was no time for sense. Because the questioner had recovered, and his remaining rods were rising, and Wren was down on one knee clutching her side where a shard of black glass had opened her, and Gideon had exactly one heartbeat to choose whom to fight.

Brannoc chose for all of them. The smuggler-king charged the questioner like a landslide given intent, and the two of them went over the altar-rail together in a crash of grey robe and oiled leather, and the last thing Gideon saw of that struggle, before Wren hauled him bodily toward the postern passage, was Brannoc's hammer coming down, and down, and the questioner's guards converging on the old man with their rods drawn, silver threads streaming out of him from a dozen wounds, his memories being eaten alive even as his body kept swinging.

* * *

They ran north through the fells with the keystone against Gideon's chest and Wren's blood soaking through the bandage he'd tied in the dark, and behind them Hollowmere burned against the sky like a second, smaller dawn. The sisters had gotten out — he had to believe that; the oracle had been herding them toward the cellars before the doors broke — but the sanctuary itself, thirty years of the oracle's quiet guardianship, was going up in fire and inquisitorial writ, and Gideon had done nothing, could do nothing, except run, again, always run—

"Don't," Wren said.

He hadn't realized he'd spoken. He didn't know what he'd said. His voice had gone somewhere past language.

"She got them out. She knew. She had *hours* of knowing." Wren winced as she climbed, one hand pressed to her ribs. "Blind women don't survive thirty years as a parasite's first host by being surprised. Stop rehearsing your failures. Save it for the ones that land."

They walked. The fells rose around them, black and trackless, and the keystone sang against his skin, and dawn came up gray and joyless over a world that had lost a sanctuary in a single night.

It was mid-morning when they found the first body.

One of Brannoc's men, laid out neatly in the heather with his arms crossed on his chest, in the old smuggler's fashion — the fashion for men dead among friends. Another fifty yards on, two more. Then a trail of them, spaced like milestones, marking the route the survivors had taken north, and Gideon's stomach turned colder with each one, because these men had fought a rearguard action for people who had paid them to betray it.

At noon they found Brannoc.

He sat propped against a boulder with his hammer across his knees, and he was dying, and he was grinning. The silver-thread wounds had stopped bleeding; that was what memory-theft did at the end, Gideon realized — it didn't kill the body, it emptied it, and the body just forgot how to bother. Brannoc's eyes found Gideon with effort, and there was still recognition in them, but it came slow, dredged up, like a man hauling buckets from a deep well.

"Whitlock's boy," he rasped. "No. No, that's not... Dunmore. Storm-caller. Knew I knew you. Knew it mattered. Can't recall why, now. They took the why."

Gideon knelt. He didn't trust himself to stand.

"You sold us," he said. It wasn't accusation. It was just the fact, set down between them like a coin on a table.

"Aye." Brannoc's grin widened, showing broken teeth. "Sold your route. Took their pardon. Read it careful, though, didn't I — read it like a man reads a contract, 'cause I've been reading contracts since before you were whelped. And the pardon, Dunmore. The pardon pardoned *me*." His breathing rattled. "Not my crew. My crew hangs regardless. Forty-one souls, boys and girls I've fed since they were pickpockets, and the parchment says nothing of them. Nothing. So I thought — right. If my name's clean either way, might as well spend the other coin I'm holding."

"The route," Gideon said slowly. "You sold them our route *twice*."

"Sold it to the grey robes for the pardon. Sold it to *my own captains* for a fighting crew." Brannoc laughed, and the laugh became coughing, and the coughing shook him. "Double-dealing's only a sin if you deal with one side honest. Asked Sera that once. She said—" He stopped. Frowned. Reached inside himself for something and found the shelf empty. "Can't recall. They took her too. Everything I had of her. Everything I..." His hand flailed, caught Gideon's wrist with grip that was still, even now, iron. "Listen. Listen, storm-caller. The questioner. Didn't kill him. Couldn't. But I broke his favorite tool, and I broke his *pride*, and pride's the only chain that holds that man. He'll come for you angry now. Angry men make errors. Remember that. Angry men make errors."

His grip loosened. His eyes drifted upward, to a sky he was no longer entirely sure how to read.

"Tell my crew," he said. "Forty-one. Tell them the old man died square. Tell them—" The frown again, deeper, frightened now. "Tell them anything. I can't remember what they look like. I can't remember what *I* look like. What's my name?"

And that — that was the moment Gideon's throat closed completely, because he understood what the questioner's rods had truly taken in that burning chapel: not Brannoc's life. Brannoc's *self*. Piece by piece, wound by wound, the man had paid out everything he was to buy forty-one children of the gutters another hour of retreat.

"Brannoc," Gideon said. "Your name is Brannoc. King of the Salt Roads. And you died square."

"Square," the old man repeated, tasting it. "Aye. That'll do."

He died with the sun on his face and his hammer in his lap, and the wind coming down off the fells smelled of heather and smoke, and Gideon stayed kneeling beside him long after there was any reason to, because there are debts you cannot repay and debts you cannot even properly carry, and this was somehow both.

Wren stood a little apart, arms crossed against her wound, watching the horizon north.

"He betrayed us," she said, when Gideon finally rose.

"Yes."

"And he saved us."

"Yes."

"And he can't remember doing either." She was quiet a moment. "That's what the crown does. That's what it *is*, Gideon. It doesn't kill you. It makes you a stranger at your own funeral."

They buried him in the heather, under a cairn of fell-stone, with his hammer. Wren scratched letters into the topmost rock with her knife — not a name, she said, because names get erased, but a mark: the smuggler's sign for *debts settled*, the one carved above every safehouse door on the Salt Roads. Forty-one people would pass that way someday, she said, and know exactly what it meant.

They walked north after that, into the high passes, toward the keeper the oracle had named, with the iron ring cold in Gideon's pocket and three keystones warming against his heart. Behind them the smoke of Hollowmere still stood up straight in the still air, a black pillar visible for miles, a message written in ruin:

*Angry men make errors.*

Ahead, in the pass, something glinted — a watcher's signal, or sunlight on armor, or only the mountain lying. The solstice was four days away. And somewhere south, an emptied-out king was being dressed for a ceremony he did not understand, while the thing wearing his smile counted the hours.

Gideon put his hand over the keystones and felt them pulse, once, like a second heartbeat that was not his own but had chosen him anyway.

"Hollowmere's gone," Wren said beside him. "The oracle's scattered. The questioner's wounded and furious and knows our heading. By every sensible measure, we've lost."

Gideon watched the glinting in the pass resolve — at last, unmistakably — into riders. Many riders. Moving fast.

"So why," he said, drawing the storm up through his arms like a blade from a sheath, "does it feel like we're winning?"
