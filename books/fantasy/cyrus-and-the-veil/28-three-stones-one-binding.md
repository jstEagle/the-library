## 28. Three Stones, One Binding

The oracle's chamber smelled of cold iron and old honey, and Wren decided she hated it before the ritual even began.

She hated it the way you hate any room where something true is about to happen — the way a lockpick hates a door with no keyhole. Hollowmere's depths were carved from the same weeping stone as the quarry, and the walls here sweated a slow, mineral grief that beaded on the candlesticks and ran in silver threads down the pillars. Somewhere above them, the blind woman who had once worn the crown's burden sat listening to three heartbeats the way other people listened to music.

Wren had stolen a great many things in her life. She had never once been asked to give one willingly. The distinction was turning out to matter.

"Set them down," the oracle said.

Her name was Maren. She had told them that much and nothing else, as if her name were a coin paid to keep them from asking for more. She stood with her palms turned upward at her sides, an old woman's hands, scarred across the knuckles in patterns too regular to be accident. Binding scars, Gideon had whispered. She'd been made to hold it once. Wren hadn't asked what *it* cost her to say that aloud.

Gideon laid the three keystones on the table of black stone between them. He did it carefully, the way he did everything now, like a man carrying water across ice. The bellfounder's keystone first — a disc of greenish bronze, pitted from its century underwater, still humming faintly when you held it near your ear, as though it remembered being rung. Then the quarry-stone, grey and dense as a fist, warm to the touch in defiance of every law of stone. And last, the shard from Hollowmere itself: white, translucent, shot through with veins of gold that moved when you weren't looking directly at them.

Three stones for three oaths, Sera's notes said. Three promises the old king had scattered so that no single traitor could gather them.

*No single traitor,* Wren thought, watching Gideon's hands. *But two thieves and a storm might manage.*

The stones did not move. They simply lay there, inert, while Maren tilted her head as though reading a page only she could see.

"They're waiting," she said. "They've waited ninety years. They will not wait much longer."

"For what?" Gideon asked. His voice was steady. Wren had learned to hear the effort underneath it, the way she'd once learned to hear the false bottom in a merchant's strongbox.

Maren turned her ruined eyes toward him — milky, unseeing, and yet somehow aimed with terrible precision. "For willing hands."

---

It took Wren an hour of watching before she understood what the oracle meant, and another hour before she stopped hoping she'd misunderstood.

The stones would not fuse by force. That was the first lesson, taught in failure: Gideon tried to hold them together and they slid apart like oiled glass; Wren tried wrapping them in cord and the cord aged fifty years in the space of a breath, crumbling to brown dust. The second lesson came when Maren guided Gideon's hands flat over them without pressing and said, simply, *ask.*

He asked. Nothing happened.

"You don't mean it," Maren said, without judgment, which somehow made it worse.

"I came across half a kingdom to do this."

"You came because a dying woman handed you a debt. Debts are not willingness. Willingness is—" the old woman paused, searching, and Wren realized with a lurch that this creature who read hearts like broadsheets was hunting for words, "—willingness is standing in front of a thing knowing exactly what it will take from you, and reaching out anyway."

The chamber went quiet except for the drip of the weeping stone. Wren found herself studying the ceiling, the floor, anywhere but Gideon's face, because she knew — she had known since Hollowmere, since the price named — that when he reached out next, he meant to pay it himself. He thought he was being subtle. Storms were many things; subtle was not among them. He'd started saying goodbye in small ways. He'd given her Sera's map without being asked. Last night he had looked at the fire for a long time and said, *the valley was probably nothing special,* which was the most dishonest sentence she had ever heard him speak.

Two liars recognizing each other's craft. She'd thought that once, in Ashmarket. She should have known then how it would end: two liars, each lying about the same lie, each planning to pay the other's bill.

But that was later. First, the stones.

It was Wren herself who broke the stalemate, in the end, because patience had never been her virtue and honesty apparently wasn't either, and so she did the one thing she knew how to do. She stole something.

Not from Gideon. From herself.

"Here," she said, and took his wrist, and pressed his palm down against the bronze keystone, and spoke fast before her nerve failed. "You want willingness? Fine. I'm willing. I'll carry it. Whatever the crown leaves behind when it's torn out — the burning, the hollow, whatever it does to a mind — I'll take it into mine, and I've got practice at holding things that aren't mine, so don't argue, just *touch the stone—*"

The keystone flared.

Green light, bell-toned, a sound like the drowned tower ringing after ninety years of silence, and under her hand Gideon's skin lit from within, and the second stone rolled toward the third on its own, and Maren made a sound that was half laughter and half grief.

"Oh, you clever thief," the oracle breathed. "That's not what I meant by willing. But it counts. It counts *enormously.*"

"What did—" Gideon started, staring at his own hand.

"It responds to sacrifice offered freely. Not sacrifice planned in secret." Her blind gaze swung to Wren, and Wren felt seen down to the ledger of her soul, every theft itemized. "Which means, child, that the two of you have spent days lying to each other about which of you will die, and the stones have known the whole time. They were waiting for the truth."

The three stones touched.

There was no explosion. There was something worse: harmony. A chord struck once, low and perfect, and where the stones met their edges softened like wax remembering a flame, and they flowed together — bronze into grey into white, gold veins threading through all of it — until what lay on the table was a key. Long as a forearm, heavy as guilt, its haft wound with the ghost of a bell's inscription and its teeth cut in shapes that hurt to look at directly.

Wren's ears rang. Her palm, where she'd gripped Gideon's wrist, burned with a heat that had nothing to do with fire.

"That's it?" she managed. "Ninety years of hiding, three guardians, an oracle, and it just — *fuses?*"

"The hiding was never to keep the key from being made." Maren lifted it — it should have been too heavy for her, and wasn't; it seemed to lighten in her grip, or perhaps she had long ago grown strong enough to carry anything the crown had touched. "It was made so that no one could forge it alone. So that whoever unbound the Regent would have to *trust.* The old king understood the parasite better than anyone since. It survives on isolated minds. Isolated kings. Isolated hosts. Everything it does, it does to make sure its victim stands alone." She set the key in Gideon's hands. "So the counter-rite requires the opposite. Hands that chose each other. Which brings us," and here her voice gentled into something terrible, "to the flaw."

* * *

The flaw had a name, and the name was consent.

Maren taught them the rite in the hours before dawn, drawing diagrams in spilled salt on the black table, her voice taking on the cadence of recitation — words worn smooth by repetition, though she could not have spoken them aloud in ninety years.

"The binding is a lock with three tumblers," she said. "The keystones are two of them. The third is the host. The Regent cannot be torn out; it must be *released*, and only the mouth it lives in can open to let it go. There is no trick. There is no override. Believe me" — and her scarred hands flexed, once — "I have searched for one across a very long life. The king must choose it. Must stand in the rite's circle, hear what it will cost, and open himself to the key *willingly.* If he fights it, the binding holds. If he is drugged, coerced, deceived, the binding holds — the rite tastes intention the way the stones tasted yours. And if the host does not consent before the solstice renewal—"

"Then the possession goes permanent," Gideon said quietly. "And no key ever opens that lock again."

"And Aldric has been raised inside that thing's care since he was nine years old." Wren heard her own voice come out flat and hard. "He doesn't know which of his memories are real. He signs orders with his own hand. You want us to walk into the heart of the inquisition, find the boy, and ask him to *volunteer*?"

"I want you to tell him the truth," Maren said. "All of it. Including the price."

The price. The part Wren had been circling for two days like a tongue probing a broken tooth. Because the rite did not simply evict the Regent — a thing that old, that hungry, did not leave quietly, did not leave *empty*. When the key drove it out, the parasite would seize whatever mind was nearest and strongest, the way a drowning man seizes a rope. Someone had to stand in the circle beside the host. Someone had to offer themselves as the rope.

Someone had to carry it, for as long as it took to burn it out. And the burning took everything soft on the way through.

"The oracle already told us the price," Gideon said. His voice was very controlled. Wren recognized the control; she'd spent a week learning the architecture of it. "One of us carries the burden."

"One of you *offers*. Whether the offer lands depends on the rite, on the Regent, on chance." Maren's blind eyes fixed on Wren. "But understand this, both of you. The host must consent. No one else can consent *for* him. And a king who has spent years being handled, steered, rewritten—" her mouth twisted around the word *handled* like it was a bone caught in her throat, "—a king like that may find it very hard to believe that anything offered to him freely is truly free."

Wren stared at the salt diagram. At the little circles representing the altar, the key, the four bodies. At the space marked *host*, which was also marked *consent*, which was also marked *cannot be forced*, and thought: *we came all this way, we buried friends and bought back memories and bled in quarries, and the whole plan ends at a locked door that only opens from the inside.*

"We have six days," she said. "The renewal rite is at solstice. After that, permanent."

"Five," Maren corrected. "And the Regent knows its host is slipping. It will smell the doubt in him now. It will tighten everything — guards, edicts, the boy's own hours. You will not get a gentle audience with him. You will get a hunted one."

Gideon was quiet for a long moment, the key across his knees, his thumb moving along the bell-inscription like a man reading braille. When he finally spoke, he didn't look up.

"Then we'd best decide who tells him. And how. And what we do," — his thumb stilled — "if he says no."

"He won't say no," Wren said.

"How do you know?"

Because, she almost said, I watched a man sign away his own sister's face rather than give up your location. Because I read Sera's letters and she believed in your family's oaths the way other people believe in sunrise. Because I saw the way you looked at the fire and lied about the valley, and liars know liars, and Aldric is a boy being lied to by something wearing his father's voice, and somewhere under all that planted tenderness there is a boy who *knows*, the way you always know, that the love feels wrong—

"Because everyone in this story keeps choosing to pay more than they owe," is what she actually said. "It's disgusting, honestly. It'll catch on."

Gideon huffed something that was almost a laugh. Maren smiled her terrible smile and began sweeping the salt diagram into her palm.

* * *

They left Hollowmere before full light, because the oracle insisted the Regent's sight was weakest at dawn and strongest at midnight, and because staying longer meant answering questions Wren couldn't afford to answer — questions like *which of you has decided*, asked in that voice that read heartbeats.

At the mouth of the caverns, Maren caught Wren's sleeve. Just Wren's.

"A word for the thief," she said, and Gideon, bless his deliberate soul, walked on ahead without pretending not to listen. "When you face the boy — and you will face him before anyone else does, because kings trust thieves sooner than they trust heroes, thieves have honest eyes — he will ask you why he should believe strangers bearing keys. And you will be tempted to tell him about the oath, and the map, and Sera Voss, and all the machinery of destiny." Her grip tightened, surprisingly strong. "Don't. Tell him instead that you know what it is to have your own mind edited. Tell him what you lost in the Questioner's cell. He needs to hear that the people offering him freedom have paid for theirs."

Wren's throat closed around the shape of her sister's name — the face she could no longer see, though she remembered loving it, remembered it the way you remember a room you grew up in after the house burns. She kept maps of it. She drew it sometimes, from descriptions in her own journals, and it was never quite right.

"And if he asks what it costs," Wren said, "when the key goes home?"

Maren released her sleeve. For a moment the old woman simply stood in the grey dawn light, listening to something — wind, maybe, or memory, or the far-off sound of the city waking toward its solstice.

"Then tell him the truth about that too," she said. "That someone must hold the door open while the thing walks through them. And that the person holding it will lose—" she paused, and Wren watched her choose honesty over comfort, "—will lose whatever the Regent judges softest. Its favorite food. Love, mostly. Home. The faces of the dead." A breath. "Tell him, and then watch his face, thief. Watch it very carefully. Because a boy who has been eaten alive from the inside will look at that price and understand it better than either of you ever could."

She turned back into the dark of her caverns, and her voice drifted after them, thin as the last of the candle-smoke:

"Sixty years I carried it, and what it took when they finally cut me free was my mother's voice singing. I have been deaf to music ever since. Now go. The clock in that city has been running since before you were born, and it does not wait for willing hands. It waits for none at all."

* * *

The road to the capital ran downhill through orchards gone wild, and Wren rode in silence for most of the morning, turning Maren's words over like a lockpick she didn't yet know how to hold.

By midday she'd worked out the shape of the problem, and it was worse than she'd told Gideon. The plan, such as it was, had a hole in the middle shaped exactly like a nineteen-year-old king: they needed Aldric to consent to a rite whose price neither of them had confessed to him — because each of them intended to pay that price secretly, without telling the other, without telling *him*, and the stones had already demonstrated what they thought of secrets. The key responded to truth. The whole rite ran on willingness. And their entire strategy rested on a foundation of merciful lies.

*We deserve to fail,* she thought grimly. *We've built a rescue out of the exact material the Regent uses.*

They made camp that night in the ruin of a wayside shrine, its patron saint long since decapitated by some pious iconoclast, its stone basin still holding rainwater. Gideon built a small fire and cooked nothing over it, because cooking was one of the domestic arts exile had left him, and sat turning the fused key in the firelight. Gold veins crawled through it like slow lightning. It hummed when he held it near his chest — she'd noticed that two nights ago and said nothing — a faint harmonic, as if it recognized something in him. Or was calling to something. Or counting down.

"Gideon." She said it before she could stop herself, which was how most of her honesty happened. "When we get to the city. When we find him. One of us is going to be standing in that circle."

He didn't look up. "Yes."

"And we both know which one of us has decided it's going to be him."

The key stopped turning. Firelight moved on his face, and for a moment Wren saw the storm-caller plainly — not the careful man, not the exile, but the thing underneath, the weather waiting for permission. Then he looked at her, and his eyes were steady, and he said:

"When we find the king, we tell him everything. All of it. Exactly as it is. No trades, no substitutions, no clever thieving our way around the price." A pause, and then, quieter, the closest thing to cruelty he'd ever aimed at her: "That's what willing hands means, Wren. It means we don't get to steal this one."

He went back to watching the fire, and she went back to watching him, and the silence between them filled up with everything neither of them would say — the offer she'd already made to the stones, the offer he'd already made to the dark, the two secret sacrifices sitting across the same small flame from each other like strangers sharing a bench.

Above the shrine's broken saint, the stars were wheeling toward solstice. Five days now. In the capital, candles were surely being counted, altars swept, a boy-king fitted for a ceremony that would weld his cage shut forever — unless he opened it himself.

*He has to choose,* Wren thought, pulling her cloak tight against the mountain cold. *We can bring the key. We can bring the truth. We can bleed for him, die for him, lie beautifully to inquisitors for him. But the lock only turns from the inside.*

Somewhere south, behind walls thick enough to muffle screaming, a young man was sleeping with ink on his hands he didn't remember using, and a voice in his ear was murmuring, tender as a nurse, *soon, now, soon* —

and Wren lay back against cold stone and made herself a promise she fully intended to break: that whatever it cost, however it ended, she would not be the one who lied to him first.
