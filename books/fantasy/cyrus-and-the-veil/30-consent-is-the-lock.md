## 30. Consent Is the Lock

The undercroft smelled of old stone and older secrets — cold air moving through it like water through a sunken ship, carrying the mineral breath of the foundations themselves. Aldric had known this place as a child. His father had called it the Root of the Palace, and once, only once, he had brought his son down here and shown him the seam in the wall where a king long dead had hidden letters that no one was meant to find.

Aldric had thought himself clever for finding them again.

Now he stood in the lamplight with two strangers who smelled of road dust and rain and something sharper beneath — ozone, he realized, when his gaze settled on the tall man with the storm-grey eyes. Ozone, like the air before lightning. Like the air after.

"The price," Gideon Dunmore said again, because Aldric had gone quiet too long. "You should hear all of it before you decide anything."

"I heard you the first time." Aldric's voice came out steadier than he felt. "The Regent can't simply be torn out like a splinter. It's rooted. When the key unbinds it from me, it will seize whatever host is nearest — unless someone is willing to hold it. Willingly. Openly." He swallowed. "And holding it means it takes things from you. Memories. Years. Everything soft, Wren called it."

"Everything soft," Wren echoed from the shadows by the wine racks. She sat on an upturned barrel with her boots braced against a cask, arms folded, watching him the way she watched everything: like she was memorizing it for later resale. "That's what the oracle said. And oracles don't lie, they just make you wish they did."

"Then it should be me," Aldric said.

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against his ears.

Gideon moved first — a slow shake of his head, the way a man refuses something he has already expected to be refused. "No."

"You haven't even—"

"No." The word landed flat as a dropped hammer. "You don't get to decide that. You've been wearing that thing since you were a boy. You've lost years to it. You've signed orders you didn't understand with hands you didn't control—" Gideon stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Aldric saw the effort it cost him, the way a man reins in a horse that wants to bolt. When he spoke again his voice was lower. "You've already paid more than anyone should. We're not going to charge you the rest."

"And you?" Aldric stepped closer, close enough to see the exhaustion carved into the exile's face, the sleeplessness bruising the skin beneath those grey eyes. "You're offering to pay it yourself. I saw your face just now, Dunmore. You'd already decided before we ever came down here. Both of you had." He turned toward Wren. "That's why neither of you would look at each other when the oracle spoke. That's why you argued about which inn to take last night, about nothing, loudly, so you wouldn't have to talk about this."

Wren's expression didn't change, but something behind her eyes did — a shutter closing over a window. "Kings shouldn't eavesdrop on their subjects' arguments."

"I'm not your king yet. Right now I'm barely my own." He laughed, and it came out bitter, tasting of copper and candle smoke. "Do you know what that thing does to me? Every morning I wake up and check my own hands. Last month there was ink on them — ink, at dawn — and no memory of writing. The Regent told me I'd composed a proclamation in my sleep. For the realm's good. I believed it for three days." His voice cracked on *three days*, and he hated it, and he kept talking anyway because if he stopped he would not start again. "It took my mother's face first. Did you know that? I was eleven. I woke one morning and could remember that I loved her but not why. Not her voice. Not the way she hummed when she braided her hair. The Regent said grief was a burden kings shouldn't carry, and it *took* her from me, gently, like lifting a coin out of a sleeping man's palm."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above them, through fathoms of stone, the palace went on breathing — guards shifting at their posts, servants hauling water, the whole vast organism of the court pulsing along unaware that its young king stood in its root-cellar bargaining for his own mind.

Gideon's hand had drifted to the satchel at his hip, where the three keystones rode together now in their wrapping of oiled leather, humming faintly against each other like struck tuning forks that hadn't quite finished ringing. Aldric felt that hum in his teeth. He suspected the crown felt it too, far above, coiled in its velvet case in the regalia chamber — felt it and was afraid. Good, he thought. Let it be afraid.

"You want to carry it," Aldric said quietly, studying Gideon. "Why?"

For a long moment he thought the man wouldn't answer. Then Gideon looked down at his own hands — big, scarred, steady in a way that seemed practiced rather than natural — and said, "Because I know what it is to lose everything soft. I've already paid that price once. Paying it again costs me less than it would cost you."

"That's not nobility," Aldric said. "That's despair wearing nobility's coat."

Wren made a sound — half laugh, half cough. "He's got you there, storm-caller."

"Don't encourage him," Gideon muttered.

* * *

They argued for an hour. Perhaps longer. Time moved strangely in the undercroft, pooled and sluggish, and Aldric lost track of it entirely somewhere around his fourth attempt to explain why the sacrifice had to be his.

"It's arithmetic," he insisted. "Simple, brutal arithmetic. You have a kingdom to deliver the key to. A rite to disrupt. An inquisition to survive. If either of you carries the Regent afterward, even briefly, even willingly, you'll lose the memories that let you finish the work. Wren, you're the only thief alive who knows the archive vaults. Gideon, you're the only one who can call the storm. Take either of you off the board and the realm falls with you. Take me off the board and—"

"And you're dead weight anyway," Wren finished, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "Is that what you think?"

"I think I'm a symbol in a velvet hat," Aldric said. "I think the Regent has spent nine years hollowing me out, and what's left of me fits in a thimble. I think a borrowed martyr is worth less than a whole king, and I would rather give my people a whole self than a rescued husk." He spread his hands. "Let me owe them that much. Let me pay my own debts."

Gideon had gone very still. It was Wren who answered, sliding off the barrel and crossing the lamplight until she stood near enough that Aldric could see the fine scars on her knuckles, the thief's calluses, the ink stain on her left thumb where she tested pens for forgery.

"You want to know what the oracle told us? Really told us, not the version we gave you?" Her voice was low, almost gentle, which from Wren was more alarming than shouting. "She said the crown's burden doesn't just take. It *keeps*. Whoever holds it feels nine years of a boy-king's terror, every erased minister's last doubt, every memory the Regent ever ate, all of it, all at once, forever, because the Regent doesn't die — it only changes pockets. And then we burn it out of you the way we burned Sera's killer out of the world, and what's left afterward—" She paused. "What's left afterward isn't guaranteed to be enough of you to appreciate the gesture."

"Then I'll gamble," Aldric said. "Kings gamble with other men's lives every day. Let me gamble with mine for once."

"That's exactly the kind of thinking that put the crown on your head in the first place," Gideon snapped, and then flinched, because it was crueler than he'd meant, and they all knew it.

The words hung between them like smoke.

Aldric breathed through it. In, and out, the way his fencing master had taught him — pain is information, Your Highness; breathe until it becomes useful. "You're right," he said at last. "It is. My father wore himself away making decisions for everyone, and the Regent slid into the space he left. So maybe I learned the wrong lesson. Maybe I learned that a king must spend himself, and the parasite has been living fat on that lesson ever since." He met Gideon's eyes. "But hear me on this, exile. I have been a passenger in my own skull for nine years. I have watched my hands sign death warrants and felt my mouth say gracious things to men I knew, in some buried part of myself, I was betraying. I have been *polite* while my kingdom was eaten. If there is one act in my life that will be entirely, unmistakably mine — one choice that the Regent cannot make for me, cannot soften, cannot rewrite — I want it to be this one."

The lamp flame leaned as though listening.

Wren looked at Gideon. Something passed between them — not words, Aldric realized, but the fluent shorthand of people who had nearly died together often enough to stop needing language. Her chin tilted a fraction. His shoulders dropped a fraction. A whole negotiation in two small movements, and Aldric understood suddenly that these two had been planning this without each other's knowledge, each intending to steal the sacrifice the way Wren stole purses, and that the argument they'd been having in inns and alleyways was really two liars trying to out-lie each other into martyrdom.

"You've both decided," Aldric said softly. "Neither of you will say it."

"We decided nothing," Wren said.

"You decided everything, and you lied about it, and now you're lying about the lie." He almost smiled. "Sera Voss died to bring Gideon here. Brannoc died buying us time. Half of Ashmarket remembers things it shouldn't because you emptied your own head to save a stranger. Do you imagine I can stand in front of people like you and let you spend yourselves on my behalf?" His voice roughened. "My father wrote letters in a wall for ten years believing I would find them. Believing I would be brave enough. This is what his faith purchased. Don't cheapen it by paying my ransom for me."

* * *

It was Gideon who broke first — not with surrender, Aldric noted, but with a soldier's pragmatism, the kind of pivot men make when they realize a position cannot be held and choose instead to fortify the ground behind it.

"If it comes to it," Gideon said slowly, "if the rite goes wrong, if the Regent seizes someone before the key turns — you don't volunteer. You *step back*. You let one of us take it, because we've trained our whole lives for losing things, and you haven't."

"That sounds like a compromise."

"It's a condition. There's a difference." Gideon's mouth twitched, the ghost of humor surfacing through the grimness. "Consent is the lock, the oracle said. The binding responds only to willing hands. Which means the Regent will fight hardest against the person who most wants to be free of it — and least against whoever offers itself gladly. If you offer yourself gladly, Aldric, you may be handing it exactly what it needs."

The cold that went through Aldric then was not the undercroft's chill. It was older, deeper, the specific frost of understanding arriving late. *Least against whoever offers itself gladly.*

"You think it wants me to want this," he whispered.

"I think it's spent nine years learning the shape of your wanting," Wren said. "And I think a creature that eats loyalty knows better than anyone how to manufacture a sacrifice."

The lamp hissed. Above them, faint as a heartbeat through stone, a bell began tolling the third hour of night, and Aldric counted the strokes the way he counted everything now — measuring time against the solstice, against the renewal rite, against the narrowing corridor of days left in which he was still, however imperfectly, himself.

Nine strokes. Nine days, if the heralds spoke true. Nine days and then the assembled realm would watch a crown settle onto his brow and make permanent what had merely been parasitic.

"So we do it carefully," he said, forcing his voice into the register his tutors had called *kingly*, though privately he suspected it was only fear wearing a better coat. "We plan the rite so that no one's willingness matters until the final instant. We rehearse. Wren, you'll map the cathedral's underways — every door, every grate. Gideon, your storm opens the roof. And I—"

"You stall," Gideon said. "You speak. You buy minutes with your father's forbidden words and pray the Regent's pride keeps it listening."

"Three conspirators," Aldric murmured. "One terrified, one stubborn, one a thief. My father would have adored you both. He collected impossible allies the way other men collect coins."

"He sounds like a man worth remembering," Wren said quietly.

"He is." Aldric touched the wall where the letters had lain hidden, feeling the cold stone against his palm like a handshake across the years. "And I intend to still be able to say that when this is over. All of it — him, my mother, the ministers the Regent purged, every name it stole. I want it *back*, gentlemen. Not avenged. Returned."

* * *

They climbed the narrow stair together, single file, the lamp held high, and at the top Wren doused the flame and pressed her ear to the trapdoor for the count of twenty before easing it open onto the dark of the linen stores. No guards. For now. But as they slipped back into the palace's sleeping corridors, hugging the shadows, Aldric noticed what he had been too preoccupied to notice before:

The torches in the great gallery had been replaced. New iron sconces, taller, hung with fresh pitch-soaked bundles. And posted beside each one, silent as furniture, stood men in the grey livery of the inquisition — not searching tonight, not questioning, simply *waiting*, their faces turned toward the regalia chamber at the corridor's end, where behind triple-locked doors the crown sat alone in the dark.

Waiting, Aldric thought, the way one waits outside a sickroom. Or a nursery.

Or a cage built for something that has begun, in its long cold patience, to suspect that its keeper is planning to leave.

One of the grey-robed figures turned its head as they passed — just slightly, just enough — and though the hood hid its face, Aldric felt its attention move over him like a hand checking a lock.

He kept walking. He did not run. But his heart had begun, quietly, to keep time with the bell above: nine strokes, nine days, and somewhere in the dark of the regalia chamber, something ancient turning his willingness over in its mind like a coin it had just discovered might be real.
