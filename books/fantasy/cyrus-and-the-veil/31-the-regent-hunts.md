## 31. The Regent Hunts

The crown knew before the boy did.

It had known for eleven days — since the moment a hairline fracture opened in the architecture of Aldric's sleep and something foreign moved through it. The crown experienced this not as alarm. Alarm was a creature's emotion, warm and wet and wasteful. The crown experienced it as a surveyor experiences a crack in a dam: noted, measured, weighed against the water behind it.

It sat, as it always sat, in the deep hour before dawn, when the palace was nothing but breathing. Four hundred and twelve breathers within its range tonight — guards on the walls with their numb hands folded, kitchen boys asleep among the ovens' last warmth, the chambermaids who no longer looked the young king in the eye because they could not remember why they once had. And at the center of them all, in the royal bed beneath the dust-sheet of banners taken down years ago because they showed the wrong heraldry — or the right heraldry, depending on which version of history one preferred, and the crown preferred only one — Aldric slept badly.

Good, the crown thought, in whatever language it is that thoughts wear when no mouth speaks them. Bad sleep is honest sleep.

The boy had begun to dream again.

This was the trouble. Dreams were leaks. For six years the crown had managed Aldric's nights as carefully as his days, sanding the rough edges off memory the way a river sands stone — patiently, without malice, because malice was inefficient. It removed the father's face first, then the father's voice, then the terrible last winter when King Edren had raved in the east tower about bindings and keystones and *it is in my head and it is drinking me*. It replaced these with gentler constructions: a father distant but dignified, an illness dignified too, a son who grieved cleanly and ascended cleanly and needed, as all young kings need, a wise counselor close at hand.

The crown was that counselor. The crown wore the boy's skull like a hand wears a glove, and it had done nothing these six years but what it was made to do.

That was the part the historians would never write correctly, if any historians survived to write anything: the crown did not hate Aldric. Hatred required a self to hate from, and the crown had long ago traded most of itself away for function. What it felt toward the boy was closer to what a bridge feels toward the river it spans. The river is not loved. The river is carried. And if the river rises — if it floods, if it strains the pilings — the bridge does not resent it. The bridge reinforces.

The crown began to reinforce.

* * *

In the morning it let him wake slowly, which it had not done in months, and fed him the memory of a comfortable evening: wine with Chancellor Ivel, talk of grain tariffs, an early bed. Aldric sat at breakfast and ate bread he could not taste — the crown had dimmed taste two years ago; it kept pulling attention sideways — and read dispatches, and the crown watched the crack widen by imperceptible degrees.

There. A pause of half a second over the seal of the Hollowmere province. The boy's eyes had rested on the wax a fraction longer than duty required. Hollowmere, where the oracle lived. Where the third keystone slept under a blind woman's keeping, if the old king's ravings had been ravings at all and not instructions.

The crown did not punish curiosity. Punishment taught the punished to hide. Instead it leaned along the boy's nerves the way a rider leans over a jump, and made the morning feel ordinary, and slipped a small suggestion into the space between his heartbeats: *the solstice is near, and you are tired, and tiredness before a coronation renewal is natural.*

The boy signed three documents. One of them, the crown noticed with its cold bookkeeping attention, bore handwriting that trembled at the descender of the final letter — a tremor that belonged to neither of them.

Something else was writing through his hands now. Or rather: something of his own that the crown had buried was clawing back up through the soil.

The mathematics were simple, and the crown trusted mathematics the way other things trust gods. In eleven days, at the solstice rite, before the assembled realm, the renewal would make the binding permanent. After the renewal there would be no more cracks, no more dreams, no more half-second pauses over provincial seals. There would be a king who was whole in the only sense that mattered: seamless. A kingdom with one will inside it instead of the fractious thousands that had bled it white for centuries before the crown came.

Until then, the crack was a door, and doors admit weather.

The crown sent for the Grand Inquisitor.

* * *

Vessia Marrow came at the fourth bell, grey-robed, grey-eyed, smelling faintly of the tanning-salts they used in the Questioner's cells to keep the extraction vials clean. She knelt. She always knelt, and the crown had long ago stopped finding the gesture satisfying, because kneeling freely and kneeling rewritten were different postures wearing the same shape, and Vessia Marrow was one of the few minds in the palace still entirely her own. It had left her intact deliberately. A blade needs a wrist behind it.

"You felt it too," she said. Not a question.

"I felt a change in pressure," the crown said, using the king's voice because the king was present, standing at the window with hands clasped behind his back in a posture of royal patience, his inner self pressed down flat and small and screaming, though the screaming was muffled to a murmur, the way a shell holds the sea. "Like a door opened somewhere far off. Tell me what your people have caught."

"Three things, majesty." She remained kneeling; she spoke to the floor, which was correct. "First: a storm-caller crossed the Veil nine days past. The valley watchers report the boundary shivered. Nothing has crossed outward from that place in ten years."

"Name."

"Gideon Dunmore. Exiled under the old king. The record of his sentence was among those Your Majesty's father sealed." A pause, thin as wire. "Among those the archives lost."

The crown registered the substitution and let it stand. Vessia believed the archives had lost the records. The archives had lost nothing; the crown had eaten them, page by page, in the first year of the regency, because Edren's sentencing ledgers contained names the realm was healthier not remembering. Apparently one name had leaked anyway, carried out by a dying courier with a map in her blood.

Sera Voss. The crown turned the name over. It remembered killing her — not personally, nothing was personal, but through instruments, through the pursuit that had chased her to the one place in the world the crown's reach thinned to nothing. It had assumed the Veil would finish her. It had not accounted for her spite. Spite was such a durable fuel. The crown had studied it for centuries and never once found where it ran out.

"And the other two things," it said.

"A thief. Wren, of the Ashmarket guilds, formerly apprentice to Voss. She broke into the Archive of Stolen Things eleven days ago. What she took we do not know. What she *left*—" Vessia's jaw tightened, the only uncontrolled movement she permitted herself, "—was the body of the archivist, alive, who can no longer remember his own childhood. She took memories from him to find what she wanted. And third: the oracle at Hollowmere sent no lamp to the window on the eve of the new moon. She has sent a lamp every new moon for forty years. It is how we know she still lives and still... consents."

"She has stopped consenting."

"She has stopped signalling. Whether those are the same thing, majesty, I cannot say from here."

The crown considered the window. Below, the inner court glittered in the pale light, ministers crossing the flagstones like well-fed crows. Among them, Chancellor Ivel, who had begun — the crown tasted it whenever he came near, doubt has a flavor like copper gone sweet — who had begun to *doubt*. Small doubts. Doubts about the grain tariffs, absurdly, of all things, as though tariffs were where the rot would show. But doubt was doubt. Doubt in a chancellor was a splinter in a finger; ignored, it walked to the heart.

Eleven days. Enough to reinforce the bridge.

"Close the city," the crown said.

Vessia's head lifted perhaps a finger's width. "Majesty. The solstice brings pilgrims. Merchants. The renewal rite requires—"

"The renewal rite requires order," said the crown, in a voice it had calibrated over six years to sound like grief wearing authority's coat. "You have all seen the histories. You know what happens when a coronation is troubled. When ambition sniffs weakness in a young king, it does not knock. My father—" and here it gave the boy's throat a small, practiced thickness, "—my father taught me that a realm dies not by the sword but by the rumor. By the whisper that the king is not himself." The irony was a flavor it had learned to enjoy the way one enjoys a bitter medicine: by association with cure. "I will not be whispered about. Gates shut. Roads watched. Every stranger questioned. Every memory that does not match its owner, extracted. Do you understand the shape of the order, Grand Inquisitor?"

"I understand it, majesty."

"Then it is yours."

Aldric's hand signed it. Aldric's hand, moving under the crown's guidance with the smooth fluency of long practice, wrote the lockdown decree into law while somewhere behind the crown's attention the true king beat against the inside of his own skull like a moth against glass — and the crown felt the beating and catalogued it, and noted with its surveyor's calm that the moth was stronger this week than it had been in months.

Someone had given the moth news of the outside. Someone was coming with tools.

Reinforce the bridge.

* * *

They closed Cindralon the way one closes a fist.

By noon the gates were barred and the pilgrim roads seeded with checkpoints, grey-robed inquisitors standing at crossroads with their extraction vials glinting like a rosary of tears. By dusk the harbor chain was up and the river barges turned back, and the city — which had spent six years learning to move smoothly around its own missing pieces, the erased ministers, the unfounded rumors, the neighbors who had gone quiet and pleasant in ways no one could quite explain — began, for the first time in six years, to chafe.

The crown monitored the chafe with interest. Chafe was data. Six thousand citizens sampled at random through their daily touchpoints — the guard patrols, the tax rolls, the confession-booths the inquisition kept in every parish — and the aggregate rose off the ledger like steam: unease, unease, unease, and underneath it in perhaps four hundred souls, something harder. Something that felt like remembering.

Four hundred. That number was wrong. Before the storm-caller crossed the Veil, the number had been sixty.

Whatever Gideon Dunmore carried in his bloodstained pack, it was contagious.

So the crown did what it had been built, ages ago, in a country whose name even it had surrendered, to do. It protected. That was the word. Whatever the thieves and the poets and the boy-king in his glass prison called it — parasite, leech, eater — the word the crown held for itself was older and kinder: *warden*. It had watched kingdoms eat themselves with faction and faith and vengeance for longer than any living thing's memory, and it had concluded, with the patience of geology, that the disease was choice. Too many wills. Too many griefs. A realm was a body, and a body with a thousand competing voices in it was not free, it was feverish.

One will. One seamlessness. Peace.

It had bound itself to Edren on those terms, and Edren had agreed — oh, how he had agreed, weeping with relief the night the crown settled onto his brow, *take it from me, take the deciding, I was never meant to carry so many lives* — and only later, only after the crown had sanded away the weak places and Edren found himself smooth and hollow and unable to remember why he wept, only then had he begun to rave about bindings and keystones and drinking.

Ingratitude. The crown had a file on ingratitude going back eight centuries.

And now the son was doing the same. Raving inward. Cracking. Reaching for the same old cure — keystones, unbinding, freedom — never understanding that freedom was the fever, that the crown was the poultice, that everything it took it took as a surgeon takes: the limb is bad, the limb must go, you will thank me when the fever breaks.

The crown did not require thanks. That was the difference between it and men. Men required thanks constantly, in coin and applause and monuments. The crown required only continuation.

It sat in the king's skull through the afternoon audiences and watched the locked-down city through the eyes of a hundred borrowed guardsmen, and it planned.

Dunmore would come to Hollowmere — the keystones drew each other the way lodestones draw, and the oracle's silence confirmed the third stone was stirring. Between the storm-caller and the oracle lay two hundred miles of watched road, one border town full of inquisitors already forewarned, and one smuggler-king named Brannoc whose greed the crown had purchased before, cheaply, with pardons.

Wren the thief would come too; the records said she had escaped the Ashmarket trap with the storm-caller, and thieves always came back for their mentors' unfinished business. Sentiment. Another durable fuel the crown had never located the bottom of.

Let them all come. Let them gather at Hollowmere like flies at a wound. Eleven days was enough time to lay grey robes across two hundred miles, enough to turn every innkeeper and toll-keeper and village priest into a listening instrument, enough to make the roads themselves a question asked over and over: *who are you, what do you carry, what do you remember?*

And if the flies reached the wound anyway — if the keystones fused, if the unbinding was attempted —

The crown touched, very lightly, the sealed door at the bottom of Aldric's mind, behind which it kept the one memory it had never dared eat: the memory of the binding ritual's final clause, the clause Edren had discovered too late and screamed about for a winter. *The host must consent.* The crown kept that clause locked not because it feared the boy would find it — it feared nothing so imprecise — but because the clause was load-bearing. Remove it from the structure of what the crown knew, and the whole edifice of its guardianship developed a flaw at the center, and flaws, the crown knew better than anything alive, propagate.

Consent. The old king had refused it at the end, and so the binding had frayed, and so the crown had been forced to hold the realm together by main strength for six years, alone, unthanked, eating memories just to keep the mortar wet.

The son would refuse too. They always refused at the end. That was the nature of hosts: they forgot what they were saved from and resented the saving.

Unless.

Unless the refusal was made impossible. Unless the renewal went forward not with a hesitating king but with a seamless one, the rite spoken over a will so unified that consent and compulsion were the same thing at last — and the crown had spent six years and four hundred stolen memories building toward exactly that, and it would not stop eleven days short because a moth beat against glass.

"Your Majesty," Vessia Marrow said from the doorway, returning with her orders sealed and her face carefully arranged around something new. "Report from the eastern checkpoint. A tinker came through yesterday asking questions about the palace undercrofts. We have him in custody. He says he was paid to ask by a woman with river-grey eyes."

The crown turned Aldric's face from the window, and smiled with the boy's mouth, gently, the way a bridge smiles at nothing, at the water rising beneath it.

"Then they are already inside the walls," it said. "Double the watch on the undercroft. And bring me the tinker — I should like to hear, in his own words, everything he can no longer remember."

Far below the smile, small and muffled and growing less muffled by the day, the true king heard the order given in his own beloved voice, and pressed his hands flat against the stone sill, and felt — for one unguarded half-second the crown did not catch — the first far-off flicker of storm-light on the horizon, answering something in his blood that the crown had never managed to kill.

Eleven days, the crown calculated.

The bridge would hold. It had held for six years against a river of griefs.

But that night, for the first time since it had taken this kingdom in its grey hands, the crown dreamed — if a thing without sleep may be said to dream — of lightning finding the crack.
