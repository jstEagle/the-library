## 32. Riot of the Remembered

The Archive of Stolen Things had a back door, because everything in Ashmarket did — a narrow stair behind a shelf of false ledgers, descending into a cellar where the vials were kept that no one was permitted to sell. Wren had spent three nights among those shelves, working by the light of a single shuttered lantern, reading labels written in six dead hands.

She had not come for Sera's memory. That grief was already spent, poured out and played back and turned into purpose. She had come for the rest of it. The crates. The unclaimed things. The memories confiscated by inquisitors over thirty years and never destroyed, because the Regent wasted nothing it had swallowed — it hoarded, the way a miser hoards, the way a cage hoards the shape of the bird that beat itself against it.

Thirty years of a city's forgotten grief, stacked in straw-packed crates with wax seals and inventory numbers.

She counted eleven thousand four hundred and six vials.

*Enough,* she thought, standing in the cold dark with her breath ghosting. *More than enough.*

* *

Gideon had argued against it. Of course he had. He argued the way storms argue — low at first, a pressure you felt in your teeth, and then all at once everywhere.

"You'll be tearing wounds open," he'd said, pacing the length of Brannoc's back room while Wren packed satchels with straw and glass. "You don't know what's in those vials. Some of these people lost children. Lost whole years. You can't hand that back to them on a street corner and expect—"

"Expect what?" Wren had snapped, sealing a satchel. "That they'll thank us? I'm not doing this to be thanked."

"Then why?"

She had stopped packing then, because the honest answer was sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she had learned — from a drowned bell-tower, from a quarry full of remembering stone, from a man who confessed his worst sin under an open sky — that lies cost more than truth ever did.

"Because forgetting is how they rule." Her voice came out quieter than she meant. "Not the inquisitors. Not the crown. The *forgetting*. Every purge, every vanished minister, every family that woke one morning missing someone and couldn't even say who — it only worked because no one remembered it happening. The Regent doesn't just eat memories, Gideon. It eats the evidence. And a city that can't remember what was done to it can't grieve, and a city that can't grieve—" she had gestured at the wall, beyond which the capital lay sleeping under its curfew bells, "—that city will kneel forever."

Gideon had looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight his face was all angles and shadow, and there was something in his expression she had learned to read by now: the particular stillness of a man deciding whether to be afraid of you or follow you.

"And if they tear the city apart?" he'd asked.

"Then they'll be tearing it apart *knowing*," she'd said. "That's all I can offer them. It's more than anyone offered me."

He hadn't said yes. He had picked up a satchel, tested the weight of it, and begun helping her pack — which in the language they had built between them amounted to the same thing.

* *

Now, two days before the solstice, the city was locked down so tight it creaked.

Wren moved through the pre-dawn streets wearing a courier's grey — ironic, she knew, but the inquisitors' own livery was the best disguise left in a capital where every other color had been questioned. Curfew held the streets empty except for patrols walking their lantern-lit circuits, pairs of grey robes moving through the fog like fish through deep water. The cathedral rose over the rooftops ahead of her, scaffolding crawling up its flanks as workmen prepared the renewal rite's great stage. Even from here she could see the altar platform being assembled in the square below its steps, and the long tables where the assembled realm would kneel, and the iron frame being bolted into place above the altar — a crown-shaped cradle, she realized, sized for a king's head.

They were going to nail it down permanently in front of everyone. Make it a sacrament. Make ten thousand witnesses into accomplices.

Her hands shook, and she let them. Fear was fuel; she'd learned that young. You didn't smother it. You fed it into the machinery.

The distribution points were simple: six squares across the city, each one chosen because it had been chosen before. She'd found them in the archive's own records — the locations of past purges, the places where the Regent had harvested most heavily. The Bellfounder's Square, where a guildmaster and his entire council had been erased from civic record in a single night. Weavers' Row, where three hundred families had woken to find their tenancy deeds rewritten and their grandmothers unmentioned in any register. The fountain square by the old law-court, where dissidents had been taken and where their neighbors now walked daily over ground they could not remember being bloodstained.

The Regent had a habit, Wren had realized. It harvested in the same places it had harvested before. Predators return to their kills.

So she would give the memories back exactly where they had been taken. Let the stones themselves witness the restitution.

* *

The first satchel went down a well.

It sounded absurd when Gideon said it that way — *you're going to throw eleven thousand memories down a well?* — but the mechanics were elegant. Each satchel was weighted, sealed in oiled canvas, rigged with a delayed dissolution charm that Wren had bought off a hedge-witch in Brannoc's employ for a price she didn't want to think about. When the canvas dissolved, the vials would break against each other on the well-bottom, and the released memories — memory-smoke, memory-fog, whatever you called the pale luminescence that leaked out of broken vials — would rise. Wells breathed. Every child in the capital knew that the wells exhaled mist at dawn.

Six wells. Six squares. One dawn.

She reached the first square as the sky went from black to bruise. The Bellfounder's Square was cobbled and silent, dominated by a dry fountain and a statue of some magistrate whose name had been re-carved twice — she could see the ghost of the older letters beneath, chiseled out and replaced, history palimpsested into obedience.

A patrol passed the far colonnade. Wren flattened herself behind the fountain, counted the patrol's rhythm — eight paces, pause, turn, eight paces back — and used the turn to cross the open ground and lower the satchel into the well on its cord.

The cord paid out. The satchel hit water far below with a sound like a swallowed cough.

Then she ran, because the charm gave her ninety seconds and the fog rising from a well-mouth was visible for half a mile.

* *

She watched the second release from a rooftop, and this was where she stopped being able to treat it as tactics.

Weavers' Row exhaled its dawn-breath, and the dawn-breath was *wrong* — too thick, too pale gold, threaded with light that moved with intention. Memory-smoke did not dissipate the way ordinary fog did. It drifted. It *sought*. Vials broken in a well-bottom released their contents upward through the shaft, and the shaft funneled them into the square, and the square was full of early risers — bakers firing ovens, carters loading, a woman sweeping her step — and the smoke touched them the way rain touches everything, without preference, without mercy.

The sweeper stopped mid-stroke.

Wren saw it from forty feet above: the woman going rigid, broom clattering, both hands coming up to her mouth. Then she sat down on her own doorstep, hard, and began to rock, and the sound that came out of her carried across the square and stopped two carter men in their tracks just as the smoke reached them.

One of the carter men began weeping without a sound, tears sheeting down his face while he stood perfectly still, holding a crate of cabbages he had suddenly remembered buying from a stall whose owner had been taken away. The other dropped the crate entirely and started running — not away, Wren realized. Toward the law-court square. Toward the place his returned memory was dragging him, some debt of witness twenty years overdue.

Windows lit up down the row. Doors opened. People stepped out into the golden fog and stood in it like people standing in shallow sea-water, letting it break over them, some of them falling, some of them catching the fallers, all of them *remembering*, and the sound of it — Wren pressed her fist against her mouth — the sound of a whole street grieving at once was not like anything else. It was not a riot-sound. It was the opposite. It was terribly quiet underneath, a vast hush with weeping in it, the silence of ten thousand private funerals happening simultaneously in doorways and gutters and kitchens.

Then somewhere down the row a voice started shouting the name of the taken. Just one name at first, hurled at the sky like a stone — *"MAREN! MAREN COLL!"* — and then another voice took up another name, and another, and within minutes the whole of Weavers' Row was ringing with names, hundreds of names, thousands, the erased spoken aloud in every register of grief and rage, until it wasn't a hush anymore but a roar, and the roar had a direction, and the direction was the cathedral.

* *

The bells saved the inquisitors from arriving blind, and damned them in every other respect.

Wren heard the alarm change across the city — the curfew bells shifting from their slow toll to the urgent triple-strike pattern she'd last heard during the harbor fire two winters ago. Grey-robed squads appeared at the ends of streets, then broke formation almost immediately, because you could not charge into a crowd of a thousand grieving citizens with drawn steel and expect the crowd to simply part. These were not the docile subjects the Regent had curated. These were mothers and sons and widowers with twenty-year-old wounds torn freshly open, and when the first inquisitor grabbed a weeping woman by the arm, the crowd closed around him like water around a dropped stone.

Wren did not stay to watch what the water did. She had three wells left and the sky was lightening fast.

The third square nearly killed her. A patrol had found the satchel already lowered — the cord still trailing from the well-mouth — and she came over the fountain-square wall directly into the standoff: four inquisitors, hands raised in extraction-gestures, facing a knot of law-clerks who had come out of the records hall at the commotion and were now standing between the greyrobes and the well with the bewildered defiance of people defending something they didn't yet understand.

"Step aside," the lead inquisitor was saying. "This is containment protocol. There is contamination in the water supply—"

"There's *names* in the water supply," said one of the clerks, and his voice cracked on it, because Wren guessed — from the way he kept looking at the well, then at his own hands — that he'd already been touched by the drift from a neighboring square, that something was scratching at the sealed doors inside him.

Wren did the only thing she knew how to do. She stole.

Slipping along the wall, she got within reach of the lead inquisitor's shadow and pulled — a light lift, a flicker-take, nothing crude: she drew out the man's memory of the morning's orders, three seconds of it, his superior's voice saying *the wells, seal the wells, the thief-girl is poisoning the wells.* Then she leaned around the corner and threw the stolen moment back into the crowd-borne air as a whisper, pitched to carry: *"The thief-girl is poisoning the wells."*

Chaos is a craft like any other. Within a minute the clerks had surged the well — not to destroy the satchel but to defend it, bodies interlocking around the wellhead — and the inquisitors were trying to arrest everyone at once, which meant arresting no one, and Wren crossed the roofline and was gone before anyone thought to look up.

But she heard the satchel dissolve as she ran. Heard the square inhale. Heard the names begin.

* *

By full sunrise it had stopped being six squares.

The memory-fog had drifted on the morning wind, and wind does not recognize containment protocols. It slid through market lanes and under doors and into barracks where grey-robed inquisitors stood their posts and received, unasked, the memories their own order had confiscated from others — confessions extracted, families separated, orders signed in the Regent's name by ministers whose signatures had been forged after their arrests. Wren watched a squad of six inquisitors outside the granary stop mid-march, stand in a row like struck bells, and then, one by one, pull back their own hoods, as if the air itself had become unbearable to wear.

Some of them knelt where they stood. Some of them vomited. One of them began walking toward the cathedral with his extraction-tools held above his head in both hands, carrying them like surrendered weapons, and others fell in behind him as he went.

The city was awake now, fully, loudly, irreversibly awake. Bells rang from three directions with nobody authorized to ring them. Someone had set fire to the curfew-post at Crossway. A column of citizens flowed toward the cathedral square from the east, and another from the north, converging on the altar where in two days the crown intended to make its parasitism holy and permanent — and Wren, perched on the granary roof with the last satchel strapped across her back, understood suddenly that she had not started an uprising. She had only handed the city back its own dead. What people did with their dead was never the giver's to command.

And that terrified her more than any inquisitor had.

* * 

She found Gideon at the fountain in the old law-court square, of all impossible places, standing hip-deep in a crowd that should have trampled him and somehow parted around him instead — the way crowds had begun, lately, to part around him, sensing the storm caged under his skin the way cattle sensed weather. He had a satchel-sling across his chest and soot on his jaw and he was holding a small boy up onto the fountain's rim so the child could see over the crowd, and the sight of it cracked something in Wren's chest that she did not have time to examine.

"You're supposed to be at the undercroft," she said, dropping beside him.

"The undercroft can wait." He nodded at the square around them. At the fog still threading gold between the cobbles, reluctant to leave. At the crowd — clerks and carters and lamplighters and at least a dozen deserting greyrobes — all of them speaking names to each other, comparing losses, building out loud the map of everything that had been done to them. "Look at it, Wren."

"I'm looking."

"No. Look at it properly." His voice was rough. "I spent ten years in a valley that forgets, and I told myself forgetting was peace. That it was *kind*." He shook his head slowly. "This — all this grief — it looks like the end of the world, and it's the kindest thing I've ever seen."

Above them the sky was stacking grey clouds over the cathedral spire, and Wren could not tell whether the weather was turning on its own or whether it was answering something in the man beside her. Around the square the crowd was thickening, consolidating, acquiring a gravity and a direction. From the cathedral steps, faintly, came the sound of the solstice scaffolds being hammered together — the Regent's people still building their permanent altar as though the world beneath it had not just changed its mind.

A woman near the fountain climbed onto the basin and raised her voice over the multitude: "They're renewing it in two days! They're going to do it to us *again*, forever, unless—"

The rest was lost to the roar, but Wren caught the direction of it. Everyone did.

Gideon looked at her. "The rite is day after tomorrow."

"I know."

"If the city breaks through the cathedral doors before we're ready—if Aldric doesn't know it's coming, if the Regent panics with ten thousand people at the gates—"

"Then everything we've done ends on those steps," Wren finished. "Tomorrow night. The undercroft. He has to meet us tomorrow night, Gideon, or all of *this*" — she swept her arm at the golden fog, the weeping, the vast and furious aliveness of the square — "becomes just the loudest purge the Regent ever conducted."

Overhead, the first drop of rain struck the fountain, then another, and the crowd looked up as one — thousands of faces turning skyward, into the gathering dark, into the storm that Gideon had not visibly summoned and could not visibly stop — and Wren felt the hair rise on her arms and thought, with something that was not quite fear:

*The sky knows whose side it's on. Now the king has to learn.*
