## 33. The Solstice Rite Begins

They came for him before dawn, when the candles in his chamber had burned down to pools of wax and the sky beyond the shutters was the grey of old pewter.

Aldric had not slept. He had spent the night on his knees before the loose stone in his chamber wall, reading his father's letters by candlelight until the words blurred, then reading them again — the binding ritual, the three keystones, the name of Hollowmere's keeper written in a hand that shook toward the end, as though King Edran had known he was writing against time. He had pressed the letters flat between two floorboards and replaced the stone and sat back on his heels with ink still ghosting his fingers, listening to the Regent turn over in its sleep behind his eyes like a great cat that dreamed of hunting.

*You are awake,* it said, when the boots began to ring in the corridor.

*I am awake.*

*Good. Today you become what you were born to be.* A warmth spread through his skull, honey-slow, suffocating. *I have waited so long for this day, Aldric. We both have.*

He thought of the undercroft, of Gideon Dunmore's storm-grey eyes and Wren's quick clever hands, of the key fused from three keystones hidden now beneath the third flagstone from the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth. He thought of consent — how the rite required it, how the Regent could drag his body anywhere but could not renew its hold without a yes from somewhere inside him. So he would give it nothing. Not today. Not ever.

The doors opened without a knock. Four inquisitors in grey, their hoods thrown back, their faces smooth as river stones. Behind them, Master Corvane — the Questioner himself, summoned from whatever dark work he did in the cells below the archive — inclined his head with the courtesy of a man greeting an equal.

"Your Grace. The realm awaits."

"Does it," Aldric said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "It has waited ten years for my father's rites. It can wait while I dress."

Corvane's smile did not reach his eyes, which were pale and lidless as a fish's. "Of course. Though I would remind Your Grace that the solstice sun will not wait, and neither will the people." He paused at the door. "Your chamber was searched last night, incidentally. Standard precaution before a rite of this magnitude."

Aldric's heart stopped, started again. *Did they find—*

*Nothing was found,* the Regent murmured, amused. *I made certain of it. You are welcome.*

That was worse somehow — that the thing in his crown protected him from discovery, kept his secrets the way a jailer keeps keys, all the while knowing exactly where they led.

*You think you are being clever,* it said, as the inquisitors walked him down stairs that smelled of stone and tallow. *You think your little friends below the city will come. I know they are coming, Aldric. I let them come. A trap is only a trap if the prey believes the field is open.*

He said nothing. He had learned that silence was the one country the Regent could not map entirely — that beneath its planted memories, beneath the warm honey of its approval, there remained a sliver of him it could not enter, thin as a knife's edge, sharp enough to cut. His father had found it. The letters said so. *It cannot read what you truly mean,* Edran had written, *only what you say. So learn to say one thing and mean another. Learn to lie with your whole surface self.*

His father had been a liar too, then, in the end. A liar and a dead man. Aldric intended to be neither.

* * *

The Cathedral of the Concord filled the eastern quarter of the city like a mountain of white stone, its spires stitched with banners for the occasion, gold thread catching the first true light of the longest day. The plaza before it was black with people. Ten years since the last renewal rite; most of them had never seen one. They stood shoulder to shoulder in the summer heat, and when the procession emerged from the cathedral's shadowed mouth, the sound they made was not cheering exactly — it was deeper than cheering, a low collective moan of awe, the sound a crowd makes when it witnesses something it does not fully understand but has decided to trust.

Aldric walked the white marble path alone. The inquisitors flanked him at a distance, and the High Cleric of the Concord went before him swinging a censer, but the path itself was empty, deliberately so, because the rite required the king to approach his god unaccompanied. That was what the liturgy called it. *Approaching your god.* Aldric had read the old texts in the weeks since the undercroft meeting, and the older texts beneath those, and he knew now what the liturgy had carefully forgotten: that the thing waiting on the altar dais was no god, only a prisoner wearing divinity like a stolen coat, and that every renewal rite for two hundred years had been less a coronation than a feeding.

The altar stood beneath the great oculus at the cathedral's heart, a disc of black glass through which the solstice sun would pour at midmorning. The crown itself rested there on a cushion of white silk, and seeing it apart from his head — seeing it as an object, small and golden and patient — made Aldric's stomach turn. It looked almost humble. It looked like something a child might find and wear in play.

*Do not fear me,* the Regent said softly. *Today is not a day for fear. Today we are joined forever, you and I, and the kingdom will never again tremble on the edge of madness or war. I have held this realm together for two centuries. Every harvest. Every peace. Every year the plague did not come. Ask them if they would trade that for your little freedom.*

And the crowd — the crowd was already answering, though no question had been spoken aloud. As Aldric climbed the steps to the dais, the sound swelled again, thousands of voices murmuring the litany of the Concord, words drilled into every schoolchild: *The crown remembers so we need not. The crown watches so we may sleep.* He heard it rise around him like weather, and he understood suddenly why the Regent had chosen this — not just the permanence of the binding, but the audience. It wanted the whole realm to watch its host kneel willingly. It wanted ten thousand witnesses to his surrender, so that no future rebellion could claim the king had ever resisted.

*Yes,* the Regent breathed, pleased at his understanding. *You see. Now, Aldric. Kneel.*

His knees bent. Not because he chose it — because the Regent chose it, walking his body down onto the marble like a puppeteer testing strings. His hands folded themselves. His head bowed. The crown rose from its cushion, borne up by nothing, turning slowly in the light, and the crowd gasped as one creature, and the High Cleric's voice rang out with the opening words of the Rite of Renewal:

*"As the sun stands still, so stands the covenant—"*

*No,* Aldric thought, with everything in him. *Not yet. Not like this.*

And then he did the thing his father's letters had taught him. He opened his mouth, and he lied with his whole surface self.

* * *

"Wait."

His voice cracked across the cathedral like a whip. The High Cleric faltered mid-word. The crown hung motionless in the air, three feet above the silk, and Aldric felt the Regent go very still behind his eyes — not angry, not yet, but attentive, the way a hunter goes still when the deer turns to face it.

"Speak," the Regent said aloud, through his own mouth, and the crowd rippled at the doubled voice, the boy-king and the something-else speaking in unison. Aldric fought his throat free. It was like wrestling his own body out of a current.

"The rite is sacred," he said, loud, letting the acoustics carry him. "The oldest rite in the kingdom. And the liturgy requires—" he paused, buying half a heartbeat, searching memory — "the Recitation of Origins. Before renewal, the covenant must be spoken whole. That is the law. My father performed it. His father before him."

It was not entirely a lie. There *was* such a recitation in the oldest versions of the liturgy — he'd found it in the restricted archives three weeks ago, a page so worn it had clearly been excised from every printed copy centuries ago. The Regent knew it existed. The Regent also knew that Aldric knew, and could not strike the knowledge from him without striking other things, recent things, things it needed intact. He had counted on that. He had spent sleepless nights counting on that.

*What are you doing?* The warmth in his skull cooled several degrees.

"Stalling," Aldric said silently. Aloud, he lifted his chin and addressed the cathedral, the ten thousand faces turned up toward him like flowers toward a sun that had begun, very quietly, to rot. "People of the realm. Before I take the crown anew, you deserve to hear where it came from. All of it. Not the litany they teach children — the truth the old rites preserved."

He began to recite. The words were in Old Revalan, the tongue of Edran's grandfather's grandfather, and he had practiced them until his mouth bled, and now they poured out of him in a rhythm like surf:

*"In the age of the Sundering, when the realm tore itself in three, there came a wisdom out of the east that offered to carry what kings could not. It asked only a seat. It asked only a head. And the king of that age, Meraval the Third, whose name is struck from every stone, gave it his crown and learned too late that a guest who carries your burdens begins by carrying your memories, and ends by carrying you."*

The High Cleric's face had gone the color of tallow. Somewhere among the inquisitors a hand moved toward a weapon, and Corvane's raised finger stopped it — Aldric saw the gesture, saw the calculation in it. Let him talk. Let him hang himself before the realm. Or perhaps Corvane simply wanted to hear how much the boy knew.

*Stop,* the Regent said, and it was not soft anymore. *Stop now.*

But Aldric had found the current, and he rode it.

*"Meraval's son hid his father's shame and renewed the binding each solstice, calling it covenant. But the first binding was never meant to last. The keystones that hold the Regent's true name were scattered by design — three locks for a door that should have been closed forever. And the ritual of closing was written down once, in a king's hand, and hidden where only a son who loved his father would ever think to look."*

He watched the crowd as he spoke. He had expected fear, or confusion, or the blank glazed obedience the Regent wove through the city's better districts. Instead he saw something that frightened him more and gladdened him more: recognition. An old woman near the front had gone rigid, her lips moving, repeating a word he'd spoken — *Regent* — as though tasting a name she'd been told to forget. A young clerk in the second rank had paled and gripped his neighbor's arm. Small fractures, spreading through ten thousand faces like frost across a window.

The Regent felt it too. He felt it flinch — actually flinch — behind his eyes, a spasm of something he realized with astonishment was *fear*, and the realization nearly undid him. It could be afraid. It had spent two centuries telling the realm it was eternal, and here on its own altar, in front of its own congregation, it was afraid.

*You are killing yourself,* it hissed. *Every word. Do you imagine your storm-caller and his thief can reach this hall? The gates are sealed. Corvane's people hold every street. Whatever you hope for, it dies outside these walls, and when it dies, I will peel the last ten years out of you like fruit, and you will kneel here smiling and remember none of why you knelt.*

"Then I will have said it," Aldric answered, aloud, in front of everyone, which was not an answer to the Regent at all but a sentence delivered to the realm. "Whatever happens after — I will have said it."

He kept going. His voice was raw now, and sweat ran down inside his ceremonial coat, and the sun through the oculus had climbed to the angle that meant midmorning was close, that meant the rite could not legally be delayed past noon, but he had minutes yet, maybe tens of minutes, and he spent them like a dying man spends gold.

He told them about the purge of Minister Halevy — not by name, the name was gone, erased even from Aldric's own memory, but by shape: *a good man who doubted, and doubt became treason overnight, and his family woke to find he had never existed.* He told them about the archive beneath the city where memories were bottled and sold, about the villages that paid their taxes twice because no one could remember paying once, about the queen his grandmother who had died begging her ladies to tell her the name of her own son. Each fact landed in the crowd like a stone dropped into still water. Some people wept without knowing why. One man began shouting that the king was mad, and the shouting spread, and Corvane's inquisitors moved through the ranks with quiet terrible efficiency, and still Aldric talked, because every second they spent managing the crowd was a second Gideon and Wren didn't have to spend bleeding in some street.

*Why?* the Regent demanded, and for the first time in all their years together, the question sounded genuine. *Why do this? You will lose. Look at what surrounds you. Why spend your last hour making it harder?*

And Aldric, hands braced on the altar's cold black glass, looking out at the roiling plaza, thought about his father's last letter, the one line he had not been able to forget: *"If you are reading this, I am sorry, and I am proud, and both are equally true. Truth spoken is never wasted, even when no one lives long enough to act on it. Say it anyway. Say it anyway."*

"Because someone has to," he said aloud. "Because a kingdom that lets a parasite speak for it deserves to hear, once, what its own silence costs."

The crown descended another inch. Time was running out. Through the high windows he could see the light changing, the shadows shortening, and far off — impossibly far off, at the edge of hearing, or perhaps only at the edge of hoping — a low grumble rolled across the sky, and the crowd's murmur stuttered, and faces turned eastward toward a horizon that had been cloudless all morning and was cloudless still, and yet—

Yet the air tasted of rain.

The Regent heard it too. He felt the thing behind his eyes go rigid with fury and dread in equal measure, and it spoke one word into his mind, quiet as a blade being drawn:

*Him.*

The cathedral doors, vast as cliffs, boomed once — from the outside.

Then again.

Then the screaming started.
