## 35. Unbinding

The solstice sun came through the shattered roof in a column of gold and falling glass, and inside that column, Aldric was dying by inches.

He stood on the altar because the thing in his crown wanted him there. His body obeyed with a smoothness that frightened him more than struggle would have — his feet finding the worn silver inlays, his hands rising into the prescribed attitude of offering, his spine straightening as though someone had reached down his throat and pulled him taut like a rope. The cathedral roared around him. Inquisitors in their grey ranks, courtiers pressed against the barricades, common folk jammed into every gallery and doorway — the whole realm gathered to watch a boy become a vessel forever.

*Speak*, the Regent said, and its voice was warm, was honey and hearth-smoke, was the voice of every kind night of his childhood. *Say the words as you were taught. The people need to hear them.*

Aldric opened his mouth.

And what came out was not the litany.

"The crown is not a kindness," he heard himself say, and his voice cracked across the nave like a whip, impossibly loud, carried on some current the Regent had not authorized. "My father learned that too late. My father screamed it through these very walls, and we were taught to call it madness."

The sound in the cathedral changed. Not silence — something worse than silence. A thousand held breaths. The Regent's grip tightened behind his eyes, a pressure like a thumb pressed to a ripening fruit, and Aldric felt his own tongue go heavy, felt the words being taken from him even as he clawed them back.

*Stop.* The warmth drained from the voice. What remained underneath was older. Colder. *You do not understand what you risk. I have kept this realm breathing for thirty years. I buried plague-towns before the sickness could walk. I unmade two wars and one famine, and no one remembers, because remembering hurts, and I have spared them the hurt—*

"You spared us the truth," Aldric said through his teeth. "It isn't the same thing."

High above, where the roof gaped open to the sky, the clouds had begun to move the wrong way.

* * *

Gideon came over the barricade with lightning still crawling on his knuckles and blood in his mouth that was mostly his own.

The storm had been his idea. It had seemed, crouched in the bell-tower's shadow while grey-robed figures sealed the square, like the only honest option left: if he could not sneak past the Regent's army, he would bring the sky down on it. And it had worked, in the way that drowning works as a cure for thirst. The wind answered him the way a dog answers a whistle it has waited ten years to hear. The rain came when he shaped his hands into claws. The thunder —

The thunder had felt like his brother's laugh, and that was the part he refused to think about while three inquisitors closed from the west stair and Wren shouted his name from somewhere under the rose window.

"GIDEON! The altar!"

He saw her then — small and quick in the churn of robes and panic, citizens pouring down the aisles as the storm ate the stained glass, and above her, above everything, the young king standing in the shaft of light with his arms raised like a man surrendering or ascending. Even at this distance Gideon could see the wrongness of him. The crown did not sit on Aldric's head so much as *root* into it, filaments of pale gold light threading back along the skull, pulsing in time with something that was not the king's heart.

The keystones hung against Gideon's chest in their sling of oiled leather. Three broken stones that had spent the morning humming against his sternum like trapped bees, and now, as he ran, they began to sing.

"Left!" Wren screamed, and he went left without thinking, which was itself a kind of miracle — ten years of trusting no one, and here he was turning on a thief's word with his life swinging loose behind him. An inquisitor stepped out of the smoke with a rod of white iron already burning; Gideon put his palm out and the storm obliged, a single crack of blue that turned the man's weapon to slag and dropped him smoking to his knees, alive, disarmed. Alive. He was keeping that rule today if it killed him.

Wren reached him at the foot of the altar steps. She was bleeding from a cut above her eye and grinning like a knife.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other—" He caught himself. "Wren. The rite. They've already begun it."

"I know." Her grin died. She looked up at Aldric, and Gideon watched her face do the thing it always did when she looked at something that hurt her — go smooth, go professional, go nowhere. "The oracle said the rite tears it loose. She didn't say what happens after."

"She said one of us carries it."

"She said," Wren said quietly, "that whoever holds the key must be holding the host when it comes free. That's all. Everything else was you two lying at each other all week, and yes, I heard both versions, and you're both appalling liars."

Above them, Aldric's voice broke off mid-word. The light around the crown flared white.

Then the singing of the stones became a shriek, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

The rite had been designed, three hundred years ago, to be spoken by a king who trusted his god. That was its flaw and its mercy both. The old words required consent — the oracle had been adamant, the plan had nearly drowned on that fact — and so when Aldric, standing in the light with his stolen body, refused in the deepest place of himself to say *yes*, the binding inverted.

He felt it happen. Felt the Regent's hold slip the way a hand slips on wet rope.

For one impossible second, Aldric was alone in his own skull.

It was the loneliest and sweetest silence of his life. Thirty years — no, twenty-two; he had been small once, he had been *small*, there had been a garden and a woman who smelled of rosemary — twenty-two years of sharing the space behind his own eyes, and now: quiet. His heartbeat sounded enormous. His own thoughts moved slow and astonished, like fish in a drained pond.

*Father,* he thought, wildly, uselessly. *Is this what you—*

The Regent came back for him.

Not into him — it tried that, and found the door barred by the broken rite, the old words still ringing in the stone, consent denied, consent denied, consent denied echoing in the marrow of the building. So instead it poured *out*. Off the crown in a flood of gold-white light, off the altar, a shape made of memory and hunger, vast and formless and screaming without a mouth — a parasite torn from its host, blind with rage, seeking any warmth at all.

It found Gideon Dunmore.

* * *

Later, he would try to describe it and fail. The nearest he ever came was this: imagine every lie you have ever told yourself about yourself, believed all at once, by something that can reach inside your chest and adjust your pulse like a lamp-flame.

The Regent hit him mid-stride, halfway up the altar steps, key half-drawn from its sling. There was no pain. That was the horror of it. There was only *arrival* — cold spreading through his shoulders, down his arms, a settling-in, a tenant taking possession of rooms long vacant. He felt it touch the storm first. Felt it recoil.

*What are you?* it asked, from inside his own mouth, in a voice that was almost his. *What did they do to you in that valley?*

"They didn't do anything," Gideon said, and his lips moved but the words were still his own, barely, because the storm was in there too, and the storm did not share. "That's rather the point."

The cold probed deeper. Looking for purchase. Finding the grief of his brother's face and sliding off it — the valley and its forgetting had scoured him smooth in places, and there was less of him to grip than the Regent expected, and that, Gideon realized with a wild dizzy clarity, was why the oracle's price had never frightened him. Ten years of exile had already hollowed him. He was the only man alive with practice at carrying an absence.

*You cannot hold me,* the Regent said. *I have worn kings. I have worn saints. You are a goatherd with weather in his blood.*

"Yes," Gideon agreed. His knees buckled. The cold went deeper, tasting the edges of him, and somewhere far away he heard Wren screaming his name, and he thought, with the last of his unclaimed attention: *she won't stop. She'll come anyway. God help me, she'll come anyway.* "But I'm holding the door open for you, aren't I? Every second you spend tearing at me is a second you aren't getting back into him. I know exactly what I'm worth. Turns out it's enough."

* * *

Wren had never run toward anything this hard in her life.

She had spent her whole career perfecting the opposite skill — the light feet, the backward glance, the exit measured in heartbeats. Sera had drilled it into her: *the thief who stops to fight has already lost the purse.* And here she was, sprinting up the altar stairs of a collapsing cathedral with a dead woman's map still folded against her ribs, toward a man being eaten alive by light.

Because she knew what was happening. She had seen the vial. She had heard Sera's last words a hundred times in her head: *the binding needs willing hands on both ends, Wren — a king who consents to lose it, and someone willing to catch what falls.* Sera had died before explaining what catching meant. Wren understood now. Catching meant this. Catching meant Gideon.

He was on his knees at the top of the steps, and the light was pouring into him through eyes and ears and the soft places under the jaw, and his face — gods, his face was calm. That calm terrified her more than screaming would have. She had seen men wear calm like that only twice: once on Sera, walking back into Ashmarket knowing the inquisitors were waiting, and once in her own mirror, the day she decided to stop running from the guild.

"Gideon." She dropped beside him, wrenched the sling open. The three keystones blazed against his chest, and where their broken edges faced each other they had begun, impossibly, to grow toward one another — hairline bridges of gold forming between the fractures, three stones knitting themselves into one key. It was warm as a living thing. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"Wren." His voice came out doubled, his own timbre with something vast underneath it, and his eyes when they found hers were half gold. "The key. Drive it — the rite needs to complete — drive it home and it takes me too, understand? It doesn't distinguish. If you wait for a better moment there won't be one. Do it now."

"No." Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't. "That's the version where you die."

"It's the version where everyone else doesn't." Gold crept further. His jaw locked. Beneath the doubling, the true voice, ragged: "Wren. Please. I have spent ten years being no one's anything. Let me be this. Let it mean something."

And there it was. The argument they'd been having in code for a week, laid bare on the altar steps with a god chewing through the man she—

Through the man. Through Gideon. Who drove goats and lied badly and called storms like he was apologizing to them. Whom she had known for six weeks. Whom she had planned, in the secret ledger of her heart where she kept everything worth stealing, to keep.

"You listen to me," Wren said, and took his face in both hands, thumbs on his cheekbones, forcing his half-gold eyes to hold hers. "Sera Voss died saying your name. The oracle traded three hundred years of suffering for your storm. Aldric refused the rite for you — for *you*, a stranger. Everyone who has ever met you has chosen you, you absolute fool, and you still think belonging is something that happens to other people. So no. I am not driving this key home to kill you. I am going to drive it home *while you hold*, because that is the rite, that is how Sera said it works — the host consents, and the catcher *trusts*, and trust is the instrument, not the hammer. Do you hear me? Trust me. That's all. That's the whole price. Just for one minute, trust somebody."

Something moved behind his eyes. The gold flickered.

"That's harder than the other thing," he whispered.

"I know," Wren said. "Do it anyway."

She fitted the fused key into the socket the oracle had shown them — a seam in the altar-stone beneath the king's abandoned crown, older than the kingdom, waiting three centuries for willing hands — and felt the moment Gideon stopped fighting her.

It was the smallest thing. A loosening of his shoulders. A breath let out instead of held. Ten years of clenched survival relaxing all at once, like a fist opening, like a knot slipping, and the key sank into the stone the way a word sinks into memory, and the world went white.

* * *

From the outside, those who survived to tell of it described the solstice unbinding as a sound: a single deep bell-note that passed through the cathedral, through the city, through every citizen in the realm, and in its passing, returned things. A baker in the Low Quarter suddenly remembered his first wife's face. A grey-robed inquisitor fell to her knees in the square, weeping, unable to recall why she had ever worn the color. Minister Corvane, imprisoned and forgotten in his own cells, remembered that he had once loved music.

Inside the light, there was no sound at all.

The Regent left Gideon the way a fever breaks — one vast shudder, and then the cold running out of him faster than breath, dragged down through the key, unraveled thread by golden thread by the three stones and the rite and the simple unbearable weight of being trusted. It fought. Of course it fought. It showed him his brother's face and offered it back to him like a bribe. It showed him the valley, the goats, the gray unremarkable peace, and whispered *I can give you all of it back, every year, every hour, just let me stay—*

"No," Gideon said, from somewhere beyond exhaustion, beyond self. "I'm done renting rooms to things that eat me."

And the Regent — thirty years a crown, three centuries a parasite, older than the kingdom it had ruled with such terrible tenderness — understood, finally, that it was not being destroyed but *refused*, and made one last lunge not for escape but for meaning, seizing at Gideon's memories as it went, grasping whatever it could carry into the dark—

The bell-note faded. The light guttered. The storm overhead sighed itself apart into ordinary rain, soft and warm, falling gently through the broken roof onto the ruined altar, onto the crown lying empty and dark as an old coin, onto a king kneeling free for the first time in twenty-two years, and onto a novelist's-hands-broad ex-thief named Wren, who was holding Gideon Dunmore's head in her lap and calling his name and getting, at first, nothing back.

His eyes opened. Grey. Clear. Hers to keep.

"Wren," he said slowly, and his voice was only his own again, but thinner somehow, lighter, a house with rooms emptied overnight. "Why am I lying in a church? And — forgive me — who in the hells are you?"

Behind her, Aldric made a sound like a wound. Wren did not turn around. She looked down into the face of the man who had held a god at bay with nothing but stubbornness and a goatherd's hands, and she smiled, because she had promised herself she would not cry until this was over, and it was not yet over, and she had one more theft to make right.

"You're Gideon," she told him gently, as the rain came down like absolution. "And I'm the woman who's about to give something back."
