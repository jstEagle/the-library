## 36. The Price Paid

The key went home with a sound like a bell breaking underwater.

Gideon felt it in his teeth first — a high, silver note that was not a sound at all but a pressure, a tuning of every bone in his body to a pitch just past bearing. Then it moved inward. Through the shattered roof of the cathedral, through the rain he had torn down from a sky he no longer recognized as his own, through the smoke and the screaming and the light of the solstice rite burning white along the altar stones, the note found the center of him and rang there.

And the Regent came for him.

It did not come like a beast. He had expected claws, had braced himself for teeth, for the cold grip of something ancient closing around his mind the way it must have closed around Aldric's, around the oracle's, around every host it had worn thin as a glove across the centuries. But it came like water finding a crack. It came like memory.

*Oh,* it said, and its voice was not cruel. That was the worst of it. It sounded almost grateful. *You are full of weather.*

He held on.

That was all the rite asked of him now — Wren had said it, kneeling over him with the fused keystones blazing in her hands, her face streaked with soot and blood and the particular terrible calm she wore when she was about to do something irreversible: *Hold it. Whatever it shows you, whatever it takes, hold.* So he held. He knelt on the altar steps with the storm roaring through the broken ceiling above him and the Regent pouring into the hollow the unbinding had opened, and he held, the way a man holds a door against a flood, the way he had once held his brother's shoulders in the river and could not —

No. Not that. Not yet. He would need everything he had, even the things that hurt.

The Regent settled into him like a tenant who had lived there before. And it began to read.

* * *

It started with the valley, because the valley was nearest to the surface, ten years of it laid down in careful layers like sediment. The Regent turned over each layer with a scholar's delicacy. The smell of wet wool and goat milk. The particular gray of dawn over the eastern ridge, the way fog pooled in the low pasture like spilled milk. The creak of the third stair in a house that was not his but had become his. The taste of well water with iron in it. Ten years of mornings, each one nearly identical, each one chosen, each one a small act of will — *I am ordinary, I am ordinary, I am a man who tends goats* — until the repetition itself had become a kind of prayer.

*You built this,* the Regent observed, sifting through them. *Stone by stone. A wall against yourself. It is admirable work. It is also a wall.*

*I know what it is,* Gideon said, or thought he said. His mouth might not have been moving. Inside the rite, inside the burning, speech and thought had collapsed into the same channel, and everything he said, the Regent heard.

*Then you will understand that walls come down.*

It took the valley the way frost takes a window — from the edges inward, silently, beautifully. He felt the fog lift off the low pasture and not return. He felt the third stair stop creaking. He reached for the smell of wet wool and found a shape where the smell had been, an outline, a word — *wool* — with nothing living inside it. He knew he had tended goats. He could see the fact of it written somewhere in him, legible, inert, like a line in a ledger belonging to a stranger. But the morning itself, the actual gray-gold weight of those ten thousand dawns, drained out of him and did not stop draining, and he understood with a clarity that felt like falling that this was what Aldric had lived with. This was the shape of the king's whole life. Every day eaten. Every morning a ledger entry.

*Hold,* he told himself, and held.

* * *

His hands were shaking. He noticed this from very far away, the way you notice weather through a window — distantly, without ownership. The shaking was old. Older than the valley. It had started the night the storm first answered him, at fourteen, and it had never entirely stopped, and he had spent twenty years learning to hide it, folding his fingers closed, sitting on his hands, telling everyone — telling himself — that it was nothing, a tremor, a weakness of nerves.

The Regent found the night the shaking began.

It did not have to dig. He had never buried it properly; he had only built the valley on top of it, and now the valley was gone and the thing beneath lay exposed, glistening, exactly as fresh as the hour it happened.

Rain. A summer storm rolling in over the hills of home, and two brothers in the yard behind the forge, and pride — his pride, sixteen years old and stupid with it, showing off, calling the lightning down between his palms because Tam had dared him, because Tam always dared him, because Tam laughed at everything and Gideon needed, desperately, to be the one who made *him* laugh —

The lightning had answered too well.

He remembered the light. He remembered that afterward the world was very quiet and smelled of ozone and copper, and that Tam was lying in the wet grass looking surprised, and that Gideon had knelt beside him and taken his shoulders and shaken them and said his name over and over, *Tam, Tam, wake up, I'm sorry, wake up* — and then their father's voice from the doorway, and then nothing for a long time.

*There it is,* the Regent said softly. *The wound under the wall. You fled from this. Not from justice — they never tried you, did they? Your father sent you away before the magistrates came. Exile by mercy. And you let them call it exile because exile was easier than guilt.*

"Stop," Gideon said. Rain ran down his face. Or maybe it wasn't rain. Maybe it had been rain for some time now and he hadn't noticed. "Take it. Take all of it. I don't want —"

*No,* the Regent said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was obscene. *This part stays. This is the load-bearing grief. Take this and the whole structure of you comes down, and I need you standing. I need you standing for perhaps another minute. Consider it professional courtesy.*

And it took his brother's face.

Not the memory. The memory stayed, whole and horrible, every detail intact except the one that mattered. He could remember the yard, the grass, the rain, the ozone, the weight of Tam in his arms. He could remember that Tam had freckles, because someone had told him so, because it was written in the ledger. But when he reached for the face itself — the crooked grin, the gap in the front teeth, the way one eyebrow sat higher than the other, the face he had loved before he had words for loving — there was a smooth blank place, like a portrait left out in the sun for twenty years. He knew his brother had a face. He could not make it appear.

He made a sound. He was aware, distantly, that it was a bad sound, the kind of sound that frightened people, and that Wren was still there at the edge of him, holding the key against his chest, her hands steady on his, and that she heard it and did not look away.

*Hold,* she had said. So he held.

* * *

The Regent worked outward from the wound, and everything soft went next.

He had not known how much softness he carried until he felt it leaving. It went in pieces small enough to slip through fingers: the name of the woman in the valley who sold him bread and always gave him the end piece without charging for it. The tune he used to hum to the goats because it calmed them, three notes, rising, that he'd learned from — from someone. From his mother? From Tam? The someone was gone before he could check. The specific comfort of rain on a roof when you are safe underneath it — that went, and with it went the knowledge of ever having been safe underneath anything.

Ten years of exile, gone in the space of a long breath. Not erased the way a page is erased — burned the way a letter is burned, leaving him certain a letter had existed, certain of its weight in his hand, unable to read a single word.

He grieved things he could no longer name. That was the strangest cruelty of it: the mourning arrived after its object had departed, like thunder after lightning, so that he knelt on the altar steps weeping for — for what? For the bread woman. For the three-note tune. For a gray-gold morning he could describe but no longer feel. The grief had nowhere to attach. It floated in him, orphaned, and he understood that this hollow ache, this shapeless loss, was going to be his permanent weather now, and that he had chosen it, and that choosing it had been right, and that both of these truths could live in him at once without canceling each other out.

*Why,* the Regent asked, and for the first time its voice had something in it that was not gratitude or gentleness. Something smaller. Almost puzzlement. *You could have refused. The oracle offered a choice. The thief would have taken your place if you had let her. The king begged for it. Why you?*

Because, Gideon thought, because I have already lost everything once and survived it, because I built a wall and called it a life and I know now that it was never a life, only a waiting room, because Wren looked at me in the quarry and stayed anyway, and I will not — I cannot — watch this thing eat another person I —

He stopped. The thought stood there in the open, in the rain, in front of the ancient parasitic intelligence wearing his mind like a coat.

The Regent made a sound that might have been laughter, in a being that had never laughed. *Ah,* it said. *There it is. The thing your kind builds kingdoms out of and burns kingdoms down over. How inefficient. How — *

And then Wren drove the key the last inch home, and the Regent stopped speaking forever.

* * *

What came out of him was not fire. He had expected fire. What came out was light — a slow, total, interior dawn, as if every window in a dark house had been thrown open at once — and through that light the Regent passed out of him the way a shadow passes off a wall when the sun finally clears the roofline. He felt it go. He felt the exact moment his mind was only his own again, one occupant, one voice, and the silence where the second voice had been was so enormous that for a moment he mistook it for emptiness, for death, for the void where the valley used to be.

Then he was on his back on the altar steps, and the rain was real rain, warm and clean, falling through the torn-open roof of the cathedral onto his upturned face, and somewhere above him the storm he had summoned was grumbling away eastward like a dog settling down after barking, and someone was saying his name.

"Gideon. Gideon. Look at me."

Wren. Kneeling over him, soot-streaked, one eye swelling shut where an inquisitor's gauntlet had caught her, her hands — her clever thief's hands, scarred at the knuckles — pressed flat against his chest as if checking that his heart still belonged to him. Which, he supposed, it did again. For the first time in minutes. Possibly for the first time in years.

"I'm here," he said. His voice came out scraped, rusted, a hinge unused. "I'm — it's gone. It's out."

"I know." Her thumb moved once across his sternum, unconscious, checking. "I felt it leave. The whole square felt it leave. Half the city is weeping in the streets and doesn't know why, and the other half is cheering, and Aldric is — " She stopped. Swallowed. "Aldric is standing up. On his own. He's asking for his father's letters. He's giving orders, real ones, his own." A breath that shuddered slightly on the way in. "You did it. We did it."

"We," he agreed, because it was true, and because the word felt new in his mouth, and because he wanted to say it while he still could.

But she was watching him too closely. She had spent her life reading faces for the flicker of a lie, and he saw her read his now — saw her catch the hollowness underneath, the way his eyes moved over her face and found it unfamiliar. Not unknown. Unfamiliar. He knew facts about her: brown hair, quick hands, a sister whose face she had lost, a mentor named Sera whom she had loved. He knew these the way he knew the ledger entries. The warmth that should have risen with the knowing rose instead as a kind of ache, grief with no address, love arriving as an echo of love.

"You paid it," she said quietly. "All of it."

"Some of it." He tried to smile. It felt like moving furniture. "It kept the worst parts. Said it needed me standing."

"Gideon — "

"Don't." He lifted a hand — it shook; it would always shake now, with nothing left to hide it behind — and touched her wrist. "Tell me later. Tell me everything later. Who I was to you. What I said in the quarry. Whether I was — " He stopped, restarted. "Whether I was worth staying for."

She looked at him for a long moment, rain running down her bruised face, and something moved behind her eyes that he could not read and hated, suddenly, violently, that he could not read.

"You were," she said. "You are. And yes. Later. All of it." She helped him sit up, and the cathedral swam around them — the shattered roof, the dead altar, the white scorch-mark where the rite had burned, the scattered figures of inquisitors kneeling in the wreckage with their grey robes soaked and their certainty visibly leaking out of them. Beyond the doors, beyond the square, a city was waking up to its own stolen memories, and a young king was standing in the rain giving orders in his own voice, and somewhere in all of it a kingdom was beginning the long, clumsy work of repair.

Hollow victory, he thought. He had saved all of it and kept none of it. He turned his pockets out and found them empty of everything except the present moment — this rain, this woman's hand under his arm, this ache.

It should have felt like ruin. Instead, resting against Wren's shoulder while the storm rolled away east, it felt almost like a floor. Bare. Swept. Ready.

"Wren," he said, as she pulled him to his feet. "Before the later. Before all the telling. There's one thing I need now."

She steadied him, frowning. "Name it."

"Sera." The name surfaced from the ledger, weightless, a fact. "I know she gave me the map. I know she died at the ford. I know I'm supposed to feel something about that, and I can't, and it's — " His jaw tightened. "You knew her. You loved her. And you keep looking at me like there's something I've lost that you can give back."

Wren went very still. Then, slowly, her hand went to the inner pocket of her coat — the thief's pocket, the one he had seen her guard through ambushes and cells and the burning of half a border town — and her expression did something complicated, tender and afraid at once.

"There is," she said. "One thing. I've been carrying it since Ashmarket, waiting for — " She exhaled. "Come on. Somewhere dry. There's something you need to see."

She drew out a small glass vial, no longer than a finger, and the last of the solstice light caught inside it and glowed — and Gideon felt the orphaned grief in his chest turn toward it like a compass needle finding north.
