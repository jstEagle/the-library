## 37. What Wren Keeps

The palace gave them rooms.

That was the strangest part, Wren thought, standing in the doorway of a guest chamber on the third floor of the east wing — that a building like this had *rooms*, ordinary ones, with beds turned down and water set out in pitchers and windows that looked out over gardens instead of walls. She had spent her whole life assuming palaces were made entirely of thrones and interrogation cells. It was disorienting to learn they also contained washbasins.

The room they'd given Gideon was across the corridor from hers. She knew this because she had checked. Not because she was worried about him — she told herself she wasn't, or at least not more than usual, not more than she'd been worried about him since Ashmarket — but because old habits were load-bearing. You learned where the exits were. You learned where *he* was. Those were the same instinct wearing different clothes.

He hadn't come out since Aldric's healers had finished with him. Three days now.

She knocked anyway. Twice, soft. The way you knocked on a door you weren't sure you wanted opened.

"Wren." His voice through the wood was flat and careful, like someone walking on river ice. "I'm fine."

"You said that yesterday," she said. "And the day before. In my experience, when a man says *fine* three days running, he's either dying or reorganizing his entire soul, and I'd like to know which so I can plan accordingly."

A long silence. Then, low: "I don't remember your name."

Her hand stayed on the door. She had known he would say something like this eventually. She had been bracing for it since the night the Regent burned out of him, since she'd knelt beside him on the cathedral floor while the storm still crackled in the broken roof tiles overhead and he'd looked up at her with eyes that held nothing of her at all.

"I know," she said.

"You're being very calm about it."

"I'm a thief, Gideon. Calm is what I do instead of crying where people can see."

Another silence, longer. Then the latch clicked, and the door swung open, and there he was: thinner than he'd been a month ago, hollowed around the eyes, dressed in borrowed court clothes that fit him wrong in the shoulders because everything about him was wrong in the shoulders now, too broad and too tired for silk. He stood aside to let her in without quite meeting her gaze.

* * *

The room was neat in the way rooms get when a man has nothing left to arrange. His pack sat squared against the wall. Sera's map lay rolled on the table, weighted at both ends with candlesticks, though it was useless now — every route it charted led back into a country that no longer needed saving. The first keystone sat beside it, dull as river stone, its light spent.

Gideon lowered himself onto the edge of the bed and watched her the way you watch weather.

"Tell me something true," he said. "Since apparently I have to be told everything now. Tell me one true thing about us."

Wren drifted along the wall, trailing her fingers over the wainscoting, buying time the way she'd bought time in a hundred rooms with a hundred strangers. But this wasn't a mark. This was the man who had run back *into* a burning cathedral for her. Who had bargained with Brannoc and lied to an oracle and let a god chew on his mind because the alternative was letting it keep a boy king.

"You hate the smell of goats," she said. "You tended them for ten years and never once got used to it. You complained about it in your sleep, our second week on the road. Said their eyes followed you like creditors."

Something moved behind his face — not memory, not exactly. More like the shape a memory leaves in plaster after the fire. "That sounds like me," he said slowly. "Goats are terrible."

"They are," she agreed. "Terrible animals. You loved them anyway. That's the whole shape of you, if you want to know. You love things that don't deserve it and can't return it."

"That doesn't sound like a compliment."

"It isn't. It's a diagnosis." She stopped by the window. Below, the gardeners were already pulling down the solstice scaffolding, the great altar dais broken back into planks, sawdust drifting gold in the morning light. The city had been singing for three days. She could hear it even here — bells, drunken choirs, the ragged joy of people who remembered, all at once, everything that had been taken from them. Wren had given them that. Thousands of stolen memories poured back into ten thousand skulls in a single afternoon, and the sound the city had made when it woke up was a sound she would carry to her grave, right alongside all the other things she carried.

"Gideon," she said. "There's a reason I came."

"There usually is, with you."

She reached into her coat — her real coat, the thief's coat she'd refused to trade for court finery — and drew out the vial.

It was small, no longer than her thumb, glass gone amber with age, sealed with wax stamped by hands thirty years dead. Inside, the memory swam like smoke in milk: pale silver threads turning over themselves, catching the window light. She had kept it against her sternum since Hollowmere. Through the raid, through the quarry, through the rite. Through the moment she'd pressed the key home into Gideon's chest while the Regent screamed through his mouth in three voices, and she had thought — briefly, honestly — that she was killing him, and had done it anyway, because he'd trusted her to.

Restitution, she thought now. That was the word the oracle had used. *Everything taken must find its way home.* The oracle had meant the crown. Wren had decided it applied more broadly.

"This is yours," she said. "Or — it was never mine. It's Sera's."

His breath caught. He knew the name. They'd told him the story twice — the courier, the map, the oath, the woman who had died mid-sentence in a valley that couldn't hold magic. He knew it the way you know a battle you slept through: accurately, and not at all.

"She knew you," Wren said. "Before the valley. Before any of it. She found you once, years ago — I pieced it together from what she kept, from the route she walked, from the way she said your name the night she died. She didn't deliver that map to a stranger, Gideon. She delivered it to the person she'd chosen. And before she chose you, she—" Wren stopped. Her throat had gone tight, which was inconvenient, which she did not permit. "This vial holds her memory of you. Of meeting you. Of whatever you were to each other, in the years when you still had a life outside that valley."

"I don't understand," he said quietly. "If it's her memory, why give it to me?"

"Because memories aren't owned. They're *kept*. That's the first thing my mentor ever taught me, before I stole her whole trade and half her heart. A memory lives wherever someone carries it. Sera's body died in your valley. Her memory of you has been sitting in my pocket ever since, and that's—" Wren laughed, short and unsteady. "That's obscene, actually. That's like keeping a funeral in a coat. She should be with you. Even if you can't feel what she felt. Even if it lands on you like a letter written in a language you've forgotten. You should know you were loved, Gideon Dunmore. Everyone deserves to know that much, even if the feeling's gone missing."

He was staring at the vial. His hands had come up partway — reaching, then stopping, then reaching again, as if the object might bite.

"Will it hurt?" he asked.

"Yes," Wren said. "Probably. Memory always does. That's how you know it's real — the fakes don't sting." She crossed the room and took his hand, turned it palm-up, and set the vial in it, closing his fingers over the glass with both of hers. His hand was warm and steady and completely unfamiliar to the touch, and she hated that, and she didn't let go until she was sure he wouldn't drop it.

"How do I—"

"Break the wax. Hold it under your tongue. Count to ten." She stepped back. "I'll wait outside if you want."

"No." He said it fast, almost sharp. Then, softer: "Stay. Please. If I'm going to meet myself, I'd rather there be one witness who knows the difference between us."

So she stayed. She leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, thief-fashion, ready to leave at speed, and watched him work the wax seal loose with his thumbnail. It crumbled like old bread. The smell that rose off the open vial was rain on hot stone, and underneath it something fainter — cedar, and ink, and the particular sweetness of a life that ended too soon.

He drank it down.

* * *

For a long moment, nothing. Gideon sat very still on the bed, the empty vial cupped in his palm, his eyes fixed on the middle distance. Wren counted his breaths the way she counted exits. Four. Eight. Twelve. On the fifteenth breath his shoulders began to shake.

Not sobbing. Something worse than sobbing — the silent, full-body tremor of a man feeling something enormous arrive in a house that had been swept empty. Tears slid down his face without his expression changing at all, as if they belonged to someone else, as if grief were passing *through* him rather than belonging to him.

"Sera," he whispered. "Oh. *Oh.*"

"What do you see?" Wren asked, though she knew. She'd played the vial once, alone, in the archive, before hope curdled and mission took over. She knew the young man in it — younger than Gideon was now, laughing, storm-eyed, standing in a market square in a city whose name she'd never learned, holding an umbrella he clearly didn't need over a woman caught in sudden rain while thunder walked the rooftops like a friendly dog. She knew what Sera had thought in that moment, because memory keeps thought as faithfully as image: *there he is. There's the one who'll finish it.*

"A market," Gideon said hoarsely. "Rain. I'm — I'm holding an umbrella over her and I'm *laughing*, I look so young, why do I look so young—" His voice cracked clean through. "She says something. She thinks something. I can hear it, it's her voice inside the memory — *there he is* — Wren, she was looking for me for years. She crossed the Veil to find me. She died in the mud holding my hand and I didn't know her. I buried her and I didn't know what I was burying."

"You buried her kindly," Wren said. "I saw the cairn. You stacked it straight. People notice how they're buried, Gideon. It's the last thing we steal from the living."

He laughed — wet, broken, real — and wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "This is cruel," he said. "You know that? You handed me a gift I can't unwrap. I can *see* her loving me and I can't feel it. It's like reading someone else's letters. It's like watching a stranger wear my life."

"I know."

"And you gave it to me anyway."

"Especially that," Wren said. "Tenderness is restitution, Gideon. My whole trade runs on taking things people need. I figured it was past time I gave one back." She pushed off the doorframe and crossed to him, and before her courage could file a formal objection she sat down on the bed beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. He didn't pull away. After a moment, cautiously, like a man testing ice, he leaned into the contact.

"I keep waiting," he said, "for it to feel like losing. Everyone keeps telling me what I sacrificed. The valley. My brother. Ten years. You especially — you keep looking at me like I'm a monument. But sitting here, with her rain in my head—" He turned the empty vial over in his fingers. "It just feels quiet. Is that terrible?"

"No," Wren said. "That's the mercy of it. The Regent ate everything soft. What's left is what's true. You ran back into a collapsing cathedral for a girl who pickpocketed you. That wasn't memory, Gideon. That was just you. Turns out the self survives the inventory."

He was quiet for a while. Outside, the bells went on ringing, and somewhere below a gardener began singing — badly, gloriously, some old solstice hymn about the returning of the light.

"The king wants to see us tomorrow," Gideon said at last. "Both of us. There's a council forming — ministers who survived the purges, the oracle's people, Brannoc's smugglers-turned-witnesses. Aldric means to tear the whole rotten court down to the studs. Justice without executions, he keeps saying, like it's easy, like it isn't the hardest thing anyone's ever tried." He glanced sideways at her. "He asked for you by name. Said the realm owes its remembering to the Thief of Ashmarket, and he wants you there when they decide what remembering costs."

Wren snorted. "Kings and their debts. Next he'll offer me a title."

"He might, actually. He's sincere like that. It's very irritating."

"Gideon Dunmore." She turned to face him fully. "Ten days ago you were a goatherd with a map. Now kings ask for you by name. Doesn't that frighten you?"

"Constantly." He smiled, small and tired and — she noticed with a jolt — *warm*, warmth reaching his eyes for the first time since the unbinding. "But I've decided fear and I are going to be colleagues rather than enemies. We worked well together on the roof of the cathedral."

Then his smile faded into something more careful. "There's one more thing. Before tomorrow." He held up the empty vial, turning it so the last thread of silver light caught the glass. "When Sera's memory settled in me, it brought — echoes. Fragments that were hers but touched mine. Places we'd both been. And there's one fragment I can't make sense of. She was writing a letter, late at night, and I heard her say a name over the ink. She said it like a warning. She said it like a prayer." He looked at Wren, and his storm-grey eyes were steady and troubled. "She said *the valley remembers what was buried there*. Wren — the map's routes all end at the Veil. But this fragment — it points *through*. Past the ford. Down into the deep valley floor, where I lived ten years and never dared dig. Whatever Sera found — whatever made her cross the Veil in the first place — it isn't done with us yet."

Wren felt the old familiar prickle walk up her spine, the one that meant a locked door had just appeared where a wall used to be.

"Well," she said, standing, retrieving the empty vial from his hand and slipping it into her coat, next to her heart, where the kept things live. "Good thing we're both such experts at digging things up."

Outside the window, far beyond the city, beyond the hills, the sky over the western mountains had begun, very quietly, to darken — and the first distant roll of thunder sounded less like a warning than like a summons.

*End of Chapter 37*
