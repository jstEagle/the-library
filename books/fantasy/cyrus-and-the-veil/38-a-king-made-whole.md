## 38. A King Made Whole

The crown came off Aldric's head at dawn on the first day of autumn, and the silence it left behind was so vast he wept.

Not the weeping of grief — though there would be grief enough in the weeks to come — but the weeping of a man who had lived for years inside a room with another occupant and only now understood how small the room had been. The Regent's voice had been a constant pressure behind his eyes since his twelfth year, a low tide of approval and correction that he had mistaken, with a child's terrible adaptability, for his own thoughts. Now the tide was out. What remained was just him: twenty-two years old, exhausted, hoarse from shouting forbidden words over an altar while lightning tore the cathedral roof apart above him, and utterly, terrifyingly alone inside his own skull.

He sat on the altar steps for a long time. Dust and broken slate drifted down through the torn roof like grey snow. Somewhere beyond the shattered doors, the city was roaring — not the roar of riot anymore, or not only that, but the strange, raw sound of ten thousand people remembering things they had paid to forget.

Wren found him there. She came limping up the aisle with Gideon's arm under her shoulder — both of them scorched, bloodied, hollow-eyed, carrying between them the weight of what they had done — and she looked at him sitting among the wreckage of his own coronation and said nothing at all. Just studied him, thief's eyes cataloguing, and then nodded once, as if confirming he was still the same person she'd conspired with in the undercroft. That the Regent hadn't taken even this last piece of him as it burned.

"Your Majesty," Gideon said, when Wren's silence had stretched long enough to need filling.

"Don't." Aldric's voice cracked on the word. "Please. Not yet. I don't know what I am yet. I'd rather no one decided for me."

He remembered, later, that Gideon smiled at that — a tired, crooked smile, the smile of a man who had recently lost ten years of himself and was learning to stand inside what remained. *I know exactly what you mean,* the smile said. Neither of them needed to speak it aloud.

* * *

The first week was the hardest, and Aldric spent most of it asleep, or trying to be.

Sleep without the Regent was its own country. For years he had dreamed in borrowed colors — the parasite curated his nights as carefully as his days, smoothing away anything jagged, feeding him restful images like a nurse spooning medicine into a child. Now his dreams were entirely his own, and they were savage. He dreamed of his father screaming warnings from beneath the planted memories. He dreamed of Minister Corvel, who had signed the edict of exile with steady hands and been purged three winters later because doubt tasted sweet to something that ate loyalty. He dreamed of the oracle at Hollowmere, her blind face turning toward him across decades, and woke with the certain knowledge that he was looking at his own future, the future he had escaped by the width of a stranger's sacrifice.

Gideon Dunmore. He thought about that name more than any other. A man who had carried the crown's burden in his storm-torn mind for eleven minutes — eleven minutes that felt, by every account Wren gave afterward, like years — so that a boy king could be made whole. And the price had been everything soft in him: a valley, a brother's face, a decade of quiet. Aldric had sent a scribe three times to ask after him, and three times the scribe had returned with the same report: His Grace walks the palace gardens at odd hours. He does not sleep well. He asks questions about people whose names are not in any record we can find.

Aldric understood that report better than anyone alive. He had spent twelve years being rewritten; he knew precisely what it meant to reach for a memory and find smooth stone.

* * *

On the eighth day, he held his first council without enchantment, and it nearly broke him before it began.

The council chamber smelled of beeswax and old cedar, and it was full of men and women who had served the Regent gladly. That was the truth he had to sit with, hands flat on the table, while Lord Chancellor Merek read out the morning's reports in his oiled, unhurried voice. Not coerced — that would have been simpler. The crown's enchantments had bent loyalties, yes, had smoothed suspicion into trust and fear into obedience, but the machine of the court had run on ordinary human fuel too: ambition, cowardice, the comfortable arithmetic of going along. Merek had drafted the purge orders. Lady Vashti of the Exchequer had funded the Questioners' cells. Old Ser Halvard, who had taught Aldric to ride, had personally delivered six ministers to their trials, and had done it, Aldric now understood, believing in his heart that he was protecting the realm from madness.

Because the Regent had shown them all the same thing: memories of a mad old king, raving, dangerous, undone by his own magic. Memories it had manufactured from whole cloth, stitched into the historical record until even the archives agreed.

"The matter of the inquisitors," Merek was saying. "Some four hundred remain in the capital, Majesty, dispersed since the... events at the solstice. They await instruction."

"They await instruction," Aldric repeated. The words tasted like copper. "And what do they believe they did wrong, Lord Chancellor? Tell me honestly. You know these men."

Merek's composure flickered — a hairline crack, quickly plastered. "They believe, Majesty, that they guarded the realm against chaos."

"Yes," Aldric said softly. "So do I, if I stand where they stand. Which is the entire problem, isn't it."

He let the silence sit. Around the table, his council exchanged glances — the quick, sidelong looks of men recalibrating, trying to determine which king sat before them today. The Regent's Aldric had been predictable. This one, they clearly suspected, might have edges.

Here is what he had learned in eight days of freedom: rage was easy. Rage arrived on schedule, hot and clean, every time he looked at Merek's careful face or thought of Corvel's family, scattered and disgraced, or walked past the Questioners' wing and heard, in imagination, the soft sounds Wren refused to describe. He could fill a scaffold by Friday. The realm would cheer. Kings who purge are popular; kings who forgive look weak.

But the oracle had shown him his future once already, and it had not been a throne built on bones. It had been a face gone blank, a self eaten hollow, a warning kept alive in a tower. Vengeance was simply the Regent's method wearing his own skin — cutting away the parts of the kingdom it deemed diseased, rewriting the record, calling murder justice. If he answered complicity with slaughter, he would spend the rest of his reign listening for the whisper behind his own thoughts and never quite sure whether he'd imagined it.

"I want the names," he said finally. "All of them. Everyone who drafted, signed, funded, or executed orders under the Regency. Everyone who sold a memory to the archive. Everyone who informed on a neighbor." He saw Merek blanch and raised a hand before the old man could speak. "No executions. No exiles. No show trials — the realm has watched enough theater performed over other people's graves."

"Majesty," Lady Vashti said carefully, "the scale—"

"The scale is exactly why. Four hundred inquisitors. Two thousand informants, by Wren's count of the archive ledgers. If I kill them all, I confirm every lie the Regent ever told about what happens when the people remember. I become the mad king of its story, and some clever chancellor in thirty years will write the history that makes my purge the proof that kings cannot be trusted with their own minds, and someone will go looking for a crown that thinks." He leaned forward. "No. Here is what happens instead."

* * *

What happened instead began the next morning, and it began with paper.

Aldric's first decree — posted in every square, read aloud by criers in Ashmarket and the river towns, nailed to the doors of every temple — abolished the crown's enchantments absolutely. The oath-oaths, the loyalty-threads woven into court appointments, the memory-tithes collected at every gate, the quiet taxes paid in recollection that citizens had never known they were paying. All of it ended, sealed with the royal signet and countersigned — this detail mattered to him more than almost any other — by a council of commoners drawn by lot from the guilds, so that no future king could undo it with a signature and a smile.

His second decree established the Court of Reckoning.

It was Wren's idea, though she'd have denied the word *idea*, delivering it instead as a shrug and a piece of practical larceny: *"You can't punish two thousand people. But you can make them carry it. In my trade we call it restitution — you take back what was stolen, and you make the thief watch the owner get it back."*

So the Reckoning worked like this: every official named in the ledgers appeared before a panel — one judge, one archivist, one citizen drawn by lot — and testified, publicly, in full, about what they had done. Their testimony was entered into the permanent record alongside the testimony of those they had harmed. Then they paid. Not with blood. With service: the inquisitors who had extracted memories were assigned to the memory-restoration wards, working under the very thieves and archivists they had hunted, learning to give back what they had learned to take. Lady Vashti surrendered her fortune to a fund for the families of the purged. Ser Halvard — this was the hardest audience Aldric had faced, harder than the solstice, harder than the Regent itself — asked to be assigned to the ward where Corvel's daughter was being treated for the holes in her childhood.

"You could refuse him," Aldric told her gently, when she came to petition. "You could spit in his face and the law would uphold you."

Corvel's daughter was nineteen and had eyes like her father's, and she looked at the old knight who had dragged her father away in chains for the crime of doubting a god in a crown. "I don't forgive him," she said clearly, in front of the whole antechamber. "But I want to know why. Nobody's ever had to say why out loud before. Make him say it where everyone can hear, and then make him stay until I'm finished being angry at him. However long that takes."

Aldric made it law that afternoon.

There were voices against it, of course. There always are. The hawks in the eastern provinces wanted blood; a few of the purged families wanted nothing except for the guilty to hurt the way they had hurt, and Aldric received their letters by the sackful and read every one, and answered the cruelest ones personally, in his own hand, which took him half the night and cost him more tears than he admitted to anyone. *You are right to be angry,* he wrote to a widow in Greyharbor whose husband's name he had signed off on without ever reading it, one of hundreds, ink moving under fingers that weren't entirely his. *Nothing I build will balance your ledger. I am not asking you to forgive me. I am asking you to let me be worth cursing.*

He kept that letter's draft. He kept all of them. He had a shelf built in his study for the correspondence of the harmed, and he decreed that it should outlast him, so that whatever king followed would begin every day within sight of what kingship had cost.

* * *

* * *

The third week, he went down into the undercroft.

It was where he had first met the conspirators, three terrified people and a stolen key, and he went alone, without guards, carrying a lamp. The stone remembered things here — the quarry-stone of chapter nineteen's legend ran deeper than anyone guessed, and the walls under the palace held the impressions of every hand that had ever cut them. He pressed his palm flat against the cold and thought: *my father stood here.* The letters had told him so. Letters in the wall, hidden behind loose masonry in the old nursery, naming the binding ritual, naming the keeper at Hollowmere, naming the price. His father had known. Had fought alone in these same corridors, writing warnings into stone and paper for a son he prayed would someday read them, while the thing in the crown smoothed the boy's memory each morning like a maid smoothing bedsheets.

"Dad," Aldric said to the empty dark, using the word he hadn't been permitted since he was nine. "I'm doing it differently. I hope that's all right."

The stone said nothing, but the lamp flame leaned, very slightly, toward him, and he chose — freely, with no voice in his head approving or correcting — to take it as an answer.

He was still there when the runner found him, breathless, boots loud on the stair.

"Majesty — the archive. The thieves' archive in the Warrens." The young man caught his breath. "Wren's people opened the public vaults this morning, like she promised. Anyone can come claim what was taken from them. There's a queue four streets long. But—" he hesitated, clearly unsure how to frame it, "—there's a problem. Someone's been going through the ledgers ahead of the queues. Cross-referencing names. Buying back specific vials before the owners arrive. An old man, they're saying. Grey robes. Nobody recognizes him, but he knows the catalogue better than the archivists do."

Grey robes. Aldric felt the words settle over him like a change in the weather. Four hundred inquisitors had dispersed after the solstice, and most had surrendered to the Reckoning panels as instructed. Most.

"Has anyone stopped him?"

"He pays, Majesty. Proper coin, proper receipts. Nothing stolen. He signs for each vial with a name, and the name's always different, but the hand is always the same. Neat. Small. Very precise." The runner swallowed. "One of Wren's archivists swears she saw the same handwriting once before. In the old Regency ledgers. The ones that recorded which memories the crown ordered destroyed."

Aldric stood a long moment in the dark, lamplight trembling on stone that remembered everything, thinking of a parasite that had believed, right up to the end, that it was saving the realm. Thinking of how beliefs survive their hosts. Thinking of his father's letters, and the line he had read forty times and never fully understood until now:

*A king is not the crown, and the crown is not one crown. Bind the mind and you only teach it patience.*

"Fetch Wren," he said. "And Master Dunmore. And my horse."

Above him, far above, the first true storm of the new reign was gathering over the mountains — but down here in the undercroft, the dark was older than weather, and somewhere in the Warrens an old man in grey robes was buying back the past, vial by vial, for reasons no one yet alive could name.
