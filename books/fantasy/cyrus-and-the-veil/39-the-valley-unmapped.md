## 39. The Valley, Unmapped

The road to the valley was longer than he remembered, which was a strange thing to think, because he did not remember it at all.

That was the shape of the journey from its first mile: a country of secondhand knowledge. Wren had drawn him a map — she drew everything now, as if paper could hold what his mind could not — and the map said *three days north of Ashmarket, past the burned mill, along the river with the white stones*. He followed it the way a man follows a stranger's directions through a foreign city, attentive, grateful, unanchored. The burned mill, when he came to it, meant nothing to him. The river with the white stones was only a river. And yet his horse, a placid mare borrowed from the palace stables, turned her head toward the northern track at a fork where the map showed no turning, and blew out her breath, and seemed to know the way better than he did. Animals, at least, remembered him. That was something. He clung to it the way a drowning man clings to driftwood, knowing it will not hold his whole weight, but holding anyway.

He had told no one where he was going. That was not entirely true — he had told Wren, because he had promised her he would stop doing things alone, and because she had looked at him with that thief's flat assessment and said, "You'll go whether I like it or not, so I'd rather know where to send the search party." He had told Aldric, who had gripped his forearm like a soldier and said only, "Come back." He had not told them he might not recognize the place when he reached it. He had not told them that the Regent had taken the valley the way a tide takes footprints — cleanly, completely, without malice — and that what remained in him was a blank space shaped like ten years, a hole in the cloth of his life with threads hanging loose at every edge.

He knew the facts the way one knows the facts of a battle read about in a chronicle. He had been sent here — or fled here, the chronicle of his own past was disputed even by the people who'd witnessed it — and he had lived ten years among goats and quiet people and a magic-dead air that had pressed on his storm like a hand pressing on a wound. He knew the name of the woman who had run the waystation. Marta. He knew it because Wren had told him, and because Sera's map had carried a marginal note in her cramped hand: *Marta feeds you too much. Let her.* He knew that he had buried something at the edge of a goat pasture — Wren said he'd told her once, drunk on plum brandy in Ashmarket, that he'd buried his storm-calling like a seed he hoped would never sprout — but he did not know the feel of the spade in his hands, or the color of the earth, or whether he had wept while he did it.

He suspected he had wept. The blank space had a wet look about it, the way a scar does in certain light.

* * *

He came down into the valley on the fourth morning, on foot, having left the mare at a shrine two ridges back because the path narrowed to something a goat would call generous. The air changed first. He felt it against his skin before his mind could name it — a flattening, a muting, the sensation of walking into a room where all the sharp edges have been wrapped in wool. Magic did not live here. It had never lived here. The valley was a wound in the world's fabric, deliberately made, and everything that passed its threshold was asked, gently and without appeal, to be ordinary.

Ten years ago — so he was told — that muting had been a mercy. It had pressed down on the storm in him until the storm forgot its own name.

He stood at the lip of the descent with his hands in his pockets and waited to feel it. The old pressure. The cage. The hand on the wound.

It did not come.

What came instead was a kind of stillness he had no history for. The storm was there — he could feel it the way a man feels a healed bone on a cold morning, a faint ache where the break had been, proof of the fracture without the pain of it. But it did not strain against the valley's silence. It did not claw. It sat in him like a banked fire sits in a hearth overnight: present, patient, *chosen*. He tested it, the way he had tested it a hundred times in the months since the unbinding, reaching toward it with something gentler than a grip. The storm turned over. It looked at him, if a storm can be said to look. It did not rise.

"Good," he said aloud, to no one, to it, to himself. His voice sounded strange in the hush. "Good."

He walked down.

* * *

The valley was smaller than he had imagined, which was its own strange grief — to grieve the loss of a thing you could not picture, and then to find the thing modest, unremarkable, a green cup of land with a river through the middle and smoke rising from a dozen chimneys. The waystation stood where Wren's map said it would, a long low building of grey stone with a sod roof and goat pens behind it, and the goats were exactly as absurd as goats always were, jostling and bleating and regarding him with horizontal-pupiled contempt.

A woman came out of the waystation wiping her hands on her apron. She was perhaps sixty, broad-shouldered, grey-haired, with the forearms of someone who had wrestled a living out of stubborn land for decades. She saw him and stopped. And then her face did something he was not prepared for: it broke. Not into fear. Into the particular ruin of a person seeing a ghost they had mourned properly and finished grieving.

"Gideon," she said.

He did not know her. He stood in the road with the wind moving the long grass in silver waves and looked at this woman who knew his name, who was already crossing the distance between them at a half-run, and felt nothing where something should have been — and the nothing was the loudest thing in the valley.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said when she reached him. She did not touch him. She looked at him the way one looks at a table where a beloved chair used to stand. "The inquisitors came back, after. They said — they said the man who'd been here was dead, that they'd found him, that there was a body. I put a stone up. Behind the pens. I *carved* it." Her voice cracked on the word. "Gideon Dunmore. Resting, at last. I cried over that stone, you great stupid—"

"Marta," he said, because the map had told him to, and because saying it was the only way to answer her.

She caught her breath. Her eyes went over his face, searching it, and whatever she found there — or failed to find — made her go still in a different way. "You don't know me," she said. It was not a question. She said it with the flat calm of a woman who had buried a husband and a son and a king she'd never met, a woman who had learned that the world's cruelties arrive on schedule and are best received standing up.

"No," he said. "I'm sorry. I was told — I'm told I lived here. Ten years. I'm told you—" He stopped. He did not know what he was told she did. Fed him too much. That was all the map had given him. *Marta feeds you too much. Let her.*

"I fed you too much," she said, and something in her mouth turned at the corner, not quite a smile, a smile's ghost haunting a grief. "You hated the turnip stew. You ate it every week. You said it was the only honest food in the world, that everything else was lying about being delicious and turnips didn't bother pretending." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "You talked like that. All the time. Ten years of it and I never once got tired of it, and now you're standing in my road looking at me like I'm a stranger, and you *are*, and I'm glad you're alive, and I don't know what to do with any of it."

"Let me be a stranger," he said. "For a day. Let me learn it back. That's why I came. I can't have the years. I know that. But I thought — maybe the place remembers what I don't. Maybe you could tell me where the bad fences were. Where I sat. Whether I was—" He hesitated. "Whether I was happy."

Marta looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved the grass. Somewhere behind the pens, a goat knocked over something metal and did not apologize.

"You were not happy," she said at last. "Not at first. You came here like a man carrying a crate he couldn't put down and couldn't open. You flinched at weather. First summer there was a thunderstorm over the far ridge and you went white as milk and locked yourself in the barn, and I sat outside the door all night talking to you about fence posts, because it was the only thing I could think of, and in the morning you came out and said, 'Fence posts,' like it was a prayer, and you went and mended the east fence which did not need mending, and you mended it *beautifully*, and that was the day I decided you'd stay." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she probably shooed goats. "And then somewhere — I couldn't tell you when, it was like watching a season turn — you got quiet in a good way. You'd sit on the wall in the evenings. You'd whistle at the birds, badly. You learned to make cheese and you were *insufferable* about it. And one night — this was maybe six years in — there was a storm, a real one, the biggest I'd seen, and you walked out into it. Stood in the middle of the pasture with your arms out. And I thought, he's going to die, or worse, and I ran out after him, and Gideon—" She stopped. Her voice went soft. "You were *laughing*. Not wild. Not like the storm had you. Like you'd finally agreed to be its neighbor instead of its prisoner. You stood in the rain and laughed, and the lightning went around you like water around a stone, and when it passed you came back to the house soaked through and you said, 'It's not my enemy. It never was. I just never asked it what it wanted.'"

Gideon stood very still.

He did not remember the pasture. He did not remember the rain or the laughing or the fence posts. But something in his chest turned over when she said it, the way the storm turned over when he reached for it — a recognition without an image, a truth arriving in a language his memory no longer spoke. *It's not my enemy. It never was.* He could have written that sentence. In the months since the cathedral, since the unbinding, since waking in a palace infirmary with ten years missing and a quiet where the roaring used to be, that was the sentence his whole recovery had been circling. He had thought he would have to learn it again from nothing. It seemed the valley had already taught it to him once, and the lesson had sunk deeper than the place memories are kept.

"Thank you," he said, and found his voice unsteady. "For telling me. For the stone. For—" He gestured at everything: the waystation, the pens, the ten years. "For all of it. Whatever I was to you."

"You were a nuisance who fixed my fences and made better cheese than me and never once asked what was over the ridge," Marta said. "You were the best ten years this valley ever gave me, and I'd do it again, and I'll thank you not to die a second time, it plays havoc with the flowers." She squared her shoulders. "Now. You'll come inside. There's turnip stew."

"I'm told I hated the turnip stew."

"You ate it every week," Marta said, and took his arm as if he were an old man, or a son, or both, and led him toward the door.

* * *

He stayed three days. He let her feed him too much. He let her tell him the valley back into him piece by piece, the way you might rebuild a shattered pot from the shards, knowing the cracks will always show, knowing the pot will never hold water the same way again — but holding, all the same, if you're careful, and if someone helps you fit the pieces.

She showed him the wall where he used to sit in the evenings, and he sat on it, and the stone was warm from the afternoon sun, and he thought: *this is where I learned to be still.* She showed him the east fence he had mended beautifully, and it was still standing, still true, and he ran his hand along it and thought: *I built this. My hands built this, and my hands are still mine.* She showed him the barn where he had locked himself against the first storm, and he stood in it a while in the dust and the smell of hay and old wood, and he felt the storm in him turn over once, gently, like a dog dreaming, and settle again.

On the second day she took him to the stone.

It stood behind the goat pens where the pasture gave way to rough ground, a flat slab of valley granite with letters cut deep and then filled with something dark so they would read from a distance. *GIDEON DUNMORE. RESTING, AT LAST.* Below it, smaller, in a different hand — Marta's, he guessed, added later — a single line: *He mended what was broken.*

He crouched beside it for a long time. He put his fingers in the grooves of his own name. It was an odd thing, to touch your own grave. Odder still to find that it did not frighten him. The man that stone mourned had been real; he had been Gideon Dunmore, and he had fled here carrying a crate he couldn't open, and he had learned turnips and fences and stillness, and he had walked into a storm one night and made his peace with it, and then he had left, and a map had found him, and a king had needed him, and he had gone, and the going had cost him everything soft.

But the hands were the same hands. The hands that had mended this fence and buried this stone — no, Marta had buried the stone — the hands that had once, in a life he couldn't see, learned to laugh in the rain. Those hands were here. They were resting on his knees in the grass, and they were steady, and when he reached inward, carefully, the way you reach toward a sleeping animal, the storm rose to meet them — not in fury, not in hunger, but like a shoulder coming to meet a hand. *Here*, it said. *Still here. Yours.*

Not caged. He had spent ten years — someone's ten years, his ten years — learning the difference without knowing it. A caged storm strains. A chosen one rests. The valley's dead air had not been his cage after all. It had been, perhaps, the only room quiet enough for him to hear what the storm actually was: not the thing that killed his brother, not the thing the crown had tried to harness, not the weapon or the curse or the sickness. Just weather. Just the sky's own heart, beating in him because it had nowhere else to go, waiting all this time for him to stop being afraid of it.

He stood. He brushed the grass from his knees. He did not weep at his own grave, and he thought the man the stone was carved for would have found that funny.

"Marta," he called. "What did I do with the spade? The one I — they tell me I buried something out here once."

Marta had come to the fence, arms folded, watching him with that unsentimental tenderness she wore like an apron. "You dug it up," she said. "The night before you left. I watched you from the window. You stood out here a long time with your hands open, and then you walked out the gate and never looked back, and I thought — well. I thought that was the last I'd see of you." She tilted her head. "Was it true, what they said? That you're some kind of hero now? That you pulled a god off a king?"

"I held a door open," he said. "Someone else walked through it."

"Mm." Marta did not look convinced, and Marta, he was learning, was never convinced by anything that wasn't true. "You'll stay for supper."

"Turnip stew?"

"Turnip stew."

He smiled, and found that the smile was easy, and that it reached all the way down to the blank space and filled a little more of it — not with memory, but with something better. With *now*.

* * *

He left on the fourth morning, at first light, because there was a palace two weeks south with a young king in it who had asked him to come back, and a thief on those palace steps who checked the eastern road every morning with a spyglass and pretended she didn't. He stood at the valley's lip one last time and looked down at the green cup of land, the river, the smoke, the small stubborn life of it, and he felt the ache of the healed bone and the peace of the banked fire, and he said goodbye to a place he could not remember and a woman he could not forget, and both goodbyes were real.

The storm walked with him down the southern road, quiet as a companion, chosen as a friend.

He did not look back. He had done his looking back, once, at a gate in the dark, and it had cost a decade. This time he looked forward — to the road, to the ridgeline, to the far-off smudge of the capital where a new reign was finding its feet and a first storm of the season was gathering over the mountains, big and slow and honest, rolling toward the palace steps like it had been invited.

He walked toward it, and for the first time in his life — whichever of his lives that was — he was not afraid of the weather.
