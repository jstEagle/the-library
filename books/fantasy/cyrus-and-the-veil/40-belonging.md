## 40. Belonging

The storm came up the river valley before dawn, and the whole city heard it coming.

Not with fear — that was the miracle of it, the thing Gideon kept turning over in his chest like a coin he could not stop testing for weight. The bells of Ashmarket rang no alarm. The watch on the walls did not run for cover. Somewhere below the palace terraces a fishwife was already at her stall, arguing cheerfully about the price of eels as if the sky were not filling itself with bruise-colored cloud, and a boy was chasing a dog through the wet grass of the processional way, and neither of them looked up.

They had learned, in one long terrible year, what weather meant now. It meant the storm-caller was home.

Gideon stood on the top step of the palace with his hands loose at his sides and let the wind find him. It came across the rooftops in a long slow breath, smelling of rain and hot stone and the green far mountains, and when it touched his face he felt it the way a man feels his own name spoken kindly — recognized, known, welcome.

*Invited,* he thought. *This time it's invited.*

He still could not remember the valley.

He had gone back to see it — walked the whole southern road in autumn, past the ford where a dying woman had once bled into the shallows, up the last mile to the gate that had swallowed ten years of his life. He had stood there a long time with the wind moving through the grass and tried to feel something, anything, grief or relief or even simple recognition. The cottage was still there. Someone lived in it now; there were beans growing in the garden and a child's wooden horse left out in the yard. A stranger's life, laid over his like fresh snow over old tracks.

He had felt nothing but peace. That was the strangest part — not the emptiness where the memories should have been, but how little the emptiness hurt. Wren had explained it once, in her sideways thief's way: *You didn't lose yourself, love. You lost a room. Turns out you were never just one room.* He had carried that sentence around for months like a smooth stone in his pocket.

The goats, though. He remembered the goats. No — he did not remember them either. But Sera's memory, the vial Wren had pressed back into his hands on the night everything ended, held a piece of them: her standing at the ford in the last light, watching him come down the hill with two white nannies trotting behind him like bad-tempered clouds, and her laughing — laughing at him, at the absurd picture he made, the storm-caller turned shepherd — and saying, *There you are. I've been looking everywhere for someone ordinary.*

He had played that memory so many times the edges had gone soft as sea glass. It was all he had of her now, and all he needed. She had found him ordinary, and she had loved him anyway, and she had died believing an ordinary man could carry an extraordinary thing. He had spent a year trying to prove her right.

The first fat drops hit the steps around him, darkening the pale stone in scattered coins.

* * *

Wren came up through the palace doors behind him without hurrying, because Wren never hurried anywhere she wanted to be seen arriving. She had a cup of tea in each hand — stolen from the kitchens, obviously; Aldric had given up pretending the palace larder was safe from her sometime around midwinter — and she pressed one into Gideon's hand without asking, the way she did everything, as if it had always been hers and she was merely returning it.

"You'll catch your death," she said, "standing out here getting rained on."

"I'm the reason it's raining."

"Then you'll catch your own death, which is worse. Very embarrassing at the funeral." She settled beside him on the top step, close enough that their shoulders touched, and looked out over the city with the particular satisfaction of a thief surveying a house she has already emptied. Below them the capital was waking under the storm — lamplighters snuffing their flames against the grey, market awnings going up, the river running high and brown and quick. "Look at them. Not one of them hiding."

"They used to hide," Gideon said. "From storms. From the crown. From their own neighbors." He thought of the square where Wren had cracked open the Regent's vaults and poured ten thousand stolen years back into their owners' heads — the screaming, the weeping, the man who had fallen to his knees in the middle of the riot because he had suddenly remembered a daughter the crown had taken from him, a daughter everyone else had been made to forget. "Aldric says they're still angry. He says some of them will be angry for the rest of their lives."

"Good," said Wren. "Angry means remembering. Remembering means it can't happen twice." She sipped her tea. Rain beaded in her hair and she did not bother to shake it off. "Your king's learning that lesson faster than most. I heard him in council yesterday — telling the Bellfounder's Guild they'd have to pay restitution to the families whose names got scrubbed out of the rolls. Half his ministers nearly swallowed their tongues. Restitution. From a throne. You'd think he'd suggested giving the crown away."

"He considered it."

"He considers everything. That's what I like about him." She glanced sideways at Gideon, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for true things, the one with no performance in it at all. "And you? Still sleeping?"

"Mostly."

"Mostly is good. Mostly is more than you had last spring." She bumped her shoulder against his. "I keep the nightmares' names in a ledger, if you want them. Old habit. Everything worth stealing deserves a record."

He laughed — really laughed, which still surprised him when it happened, surfacing out of him like a fish rising through dark water. A year ago he would not have believed laughter could live in the same body that had held the Regent. But the parasite was ash and gone, burned out of him by Wren's steady hands and the key made of three stones, and what remained was only himself: scarred, half-remembered, quieter than he used to be, but his. Entirely his.

"The storm's close," he said.

"I know. I can feel you listening to it. You get this look." She mimed it, badly — eyes half shut, head tilted, mouth soft — and then ruined the mimicry by grinning. "Like a man hearing music from a house he used to live in."

"That's exactly what it is."

* * *

Aldric came out last, as he always did on these mornings, because the king of a mending realm was never the first to arrive anywhere. He came through the doors still pulling off the weight of the council chamber — the papers, the arguments, the long slow work of justice done without executions, which everyone had told him was impossible and which he was doing anyway, one stubborn decree at a time. He had grown into his shoulders that year. He had grown into his name. But when he reached the top step and saw the two of them waiting, he stopped being a king so completely and so quickly that it always made Gideon's chest ache a little: just a young man in a plain wool coat, coming out to watch the weather with his friends.

"It's early," Aldric said, settling onto Gideon's other side. "The almanac said midday."

"The almanac doesn't know me."

"No," Aldric said, and smiled. "It doesn't. We do." He wrapped his hands around nothing — he had refused tea, as he refused most comforts before noon, some leftover discipline of a boy who had never been sure which pleasures were traps. Then, after a moment, he held his hands out anyway, palms up, into the rain. Letting it fall on him. Gideon had noticed the king did that often now, in small ways: touching things, tasting things, keeping his own counsel a beat longer than the crown ever allowed him. Relearning ownership of his own hours. "My father used to bring me out here," he said quietly. "Before. I don't remember it — the Regent took those mornings along with everything else. But the servants remember. They say he'd stand right here and let the rain soak him through, and my mother would scold him, and he'd say—" Aldric stopped. His jaw worked. "He'd say a king who fears weather fears his own people."

The three of them sat with that. The storm rolled nearer, dragging its shadow up the valley, and the city went on beneath it — unafraid, unhidden, alive.

"Do you miss it?" Aldric asked Gideon, after a while. He asked it gently, the way he asked everything now, having spent too many years inside a voice that never asked anything at all. "The valley. Even without the memories. Do you feel it — the shape of the missing?"

Gideon considered the question honestly, because these two deserved honesty; honesty was the whole foundation they had built, laid stone by stone in an undercroft and on a battlefield and across a hundred ordinary breakfasts since. "Sometimes," he said. "Like a coat I know I owned. I can describe the buttons. I can't feel the wool." He turned his cup in his hands. "But here's the strange thing. When I try to grieve for it, what comes instead is — this. This step. This rain. You two. And the grief can't get any purchase, because what I lost was a place, and what I have is people, and it turns out those aren't the same currency at all. I was rich in the valley and starving. I'm poor now and full."

Wren made a small sound — not quite a laugh, not quite anything else — and looked hard at the horizon.

"What?" Gideon said.

"Nothing. Just — a year ago I'd have picked your pocket for saying something like that." Her voice was rougher than she intended; he heard her hear it, and press on anyway, which was its own kind of courage, the kind she practiced daily and never named. "Now I want to write it down. Steal it properly. Put it somewhere safe." She pulled something from her coat — a small glass vial, empty, its cork worn smooth — and turned it in her fingers. "Sera's vial. I kept it. Seemed wrong to throw it away after everything it carried." She looked at the storm instead of at either of them. "She'd have liked this, you know. All three of us on a step in the rain like it's nothing. She died so sure it would cost more than this. So sure the price would break us."

"It did cost," Aldric said. "Don't make it smaller than it was. Brannoc paid. The oracle paid. You paid—" and here he looked at Wren with the particular gravity he reserved for the debts he could not repay, "—a sister's face. Gideon paid a decade. I paid—" he laughed, short and without much humor, "—my entire childhood, apparently, though I'm told I wasn't using it."

"We paid," Wren agreed. "And look what it bought." She swept her arm out at the city, the river, the waking roofs, the storm coming home. "Nobody's kneeling. Nobody's forgetting. There's a blind oracle living in comfort at Hollowmere with a pension from the crown and a queue of scholars at her door every week wanting to hear what the Regent sounded like, so no one ever mistakes that whisper for wisdom again. There are names going back on rolls. There's a quarry where the stone remembers, and a bell tower that isn't drowned anymore — Aldric had them raise it, did you tell him? They rang it at solstice. First time in sixty years. The ghost stayed to hear it, and then, they say, she finally moved on." Wren's eyes were bright and fierce. "That's what it bought. Don't you dare call it a hollow victory, either of you. I'll steal the word 'hollow' out of your mouths while you sleep."

"You can't steal a word," Aldric said.

"I've stolen harder things."

* * *

The storm arrived.

It came over the ridge like a procession — great grey banners of cloud, lightning walking far off among the hills with slow deliberate strides, thunder rolling up the valley floor and breaking against the palace walls in long soft detonations. The rain thickened. The fishwife below hauled her awning out with a practiced snap and kept selling eels. The boy and the dog gave up their chase and stood in the middle of the processional way, faces tipped up, letting it drench them.

And Gideon opened the door he had kept shut for twenty years.

He did not call the lightning. That was the difference, and he felt it in every nerve — the difference between summoning and welcoming, between a weapon and a guest. He simply stopped holding himself apart from the weather, and the weather, which had been circling him all morning like an old dog around a familiar chair, poured in. Power moved down his arms and out through his open hands and into the storm above the city, and the storm answered — not with fury, not with the seductive violence that had once sickened him with how good it felt, but with something gentler and vaster: a great slow turning, the whole system of cloud and wind and rain settling around the palace like a bird finding its perch. The worst of the rain bent aside and fell on the gardens instead, on the dry summer-parched fields beyond the walls, on the hillsides where the pines drank it in.

Beside him, Aldric breathed out slowly. "Every time," the young king murmured. "Every time you do that, I understand my father a little better. A king who fears weather fears his own people." He shook his head, water flying. "I signed six decrees yesterday. Two of them will change thousands of lives. And none of it felt as much like ruling as sitting here does."

"That's because none of it was ruling," Wren said. "That was paperwork. This—" she gestured at the three of them, soaked, shoulder to shoulder, laughing at themselves, "—this is the actual job. Somebody should put it in the coronation oath. *Do you promise to sit on a cold step in the rain with people who knew you before you were impressive?*"

"*I so promise,*" said Aldric solemnly, and then he was laughing too, helplessly, the laugh of a young man rather than a king, and Gideon thought: *there it is. There's the whole kingdom, right there. Not the throne. Not the crown — God knows we've buried enough crowns. This. Three people on a step.*

Lightning walked the far ridge again, unhurried, magnificent, and the thunder came after like applause.

They stayed until the sun broke through behind the storm and threw a double rainbow across the river, and even then nobody moved to go inside. There was no next crisis waiting — for the first time in any of their remembered lives, there was genuinely nothing waiting but the day. Wren finished her tea and immediately plotted the theft of more. Aldric spoke of riding out to Hollowmere next month, and of the school he wanted to build where the archive had been — a place where stolen things would be taught to speak instead of sold. Gideon listened, and added his own small plans to the pile: a trip south in the spring, maybe, to the ford, to stand where Sera had stood; a garden, perhaps, since his hands remembered tending things even if his mind did not; letters to write, because the world was full of people rebuilding and some of them needed to hear that it could be done.

The rain thinned to silver threads. The city glittered.

And Gideon Dunmore — storm-caller, exile, heir to a dead woman's oath, keeper of a valley he could not recall and a brother whose face had burned away with the parasite — sat on the palace steps between a thief and a king, wet through and entirely warm, and understood at last the thing the oracle had tried to tell him in her dusty hall at Hollowmere, the thing he had been too frightened to hear:

*Home was never the place the Veil hid. Home was whoever stayed when the storm came — and, this time, the storm had been asked.*

Far off over the mountains, another front was gathering, big and slow and honest, rolling toward a kingdom that no longer feared the sky.

He watched it come, and he was glad.
