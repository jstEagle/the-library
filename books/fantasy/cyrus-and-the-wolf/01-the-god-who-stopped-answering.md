## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The god of oaths had a name once. Oscar Joubert had known it the way he knew his own mother's face — worn smooth by use, spoken without thinking, *Vethram, Keeper of the Given Word*, murmured over contracts and knife-hilts and the small daily promises a man makes to himself. He had been nine years old when he first pressed his palm to a waystone and swore his apprenticeship to the mercenary trade, and he remembered — distantly, the way one remembers sunlight — how the stone had warmed under his hand like something alive acknowledging him.

Ten years ago, it stopped.

No thunderclap. No burning of temples. The altars simply went cold, all of them, from the great sanctuaries in Corvale to the roadside shrines where soldiers spat and wished each other luck. Priests woke to find their relics ordinary bone and brass. Oaths still *worked* — that was the cruelty of it. Break your sworn word and something still found you: bad luck curdling into catastrophe, wounds that festered, roads that closed. But when you kept faith? Nothing. Silence. A ledger that recorded only debts.

Oscar prayed anyway.

He did it now, crouched at the edge of Greywater Ford with his hands cupped around a match he was pretending to shelter from the wind, whispering the old morning words into the dark beneath the pines. *Vethram, Keeper of the Given Word. I rise. I keep what I have sworn. Watch me or don't.* The match flared, caught the end of his cigarette, and died. The words went out of him and into the trees and came back as nothing, as they always did, and he stood up and stamped the feeling down where it belonged, under ten years of callus.

"Talking to yourself again, Wolf?"

He didn't turn. "Talking to my better judgment. It's telling me not to work with you."

Maret Kell grinned at him from across the cold fire pit, her red hair tied back with a strip of leather, her crossbow already slung and oiled. She was the only person alive who called him by that name without irony and without fear, which made her either very brave or very stupid, and after fourteen years of partnership Oscar had concluded it was both in equal measure.

"The client's late," she said.

"The client's always late."

"This one paid half up front." She tossed him the purse. It was heavier than it looked — good silver, minted recently, imperial stamps filed off but not filed off well. Oscar weighed it in his palm and felt the shape of the job before anyone told it to him. Half up front meant someone who wanted discretion more than they wanted cheap. Filed stamps meant someone who'd rather not be traced. And a courier job over Thornfell in the last week of autumn, when the passes were still open but only just, meant someone who understood that the mountain was the best lock in the world if you could afford the key.

"What's the cargo?" he asked.

"You'll love this." Maret dug in her jacket and produced a folded paper, sealed with plain black wax. "We don't know. Sealed orders. We take a man up the Thorn Road to Kestrel Gate, we hand him to Brannoc's people, we get paid the other half. That's it. That's the whole job."

"A man?"

"A courier. Quiet type. Guild didn't give us his name, which is its own kind of message." Maret shrugged. "Brannoc wants him moved fast and unremarked. Somebody's letter they don't want the tax collectors reading, probably. Or somebody's mistress. Rich men pay gold for boring errands, Wolf. It's the interesting ones that pay in scars."

Oscar looked north, where the Thornfell massif stood up against the pre-dawn grey like the spine of some enormous animal sleeping under a blanket of cloud. He had crossed it ninety-one times. He knew that number because counting was a thing you did instead of praying, after your god went quiet. Ninety-one crossings, and he knew every fold of that stone body: the switchbacks above Greywater Ford where the fog pooled like milk; the Widow's Stair, three hundred steps cut by miners who'd never heard of switchbacks; the high shelf past the tree line where the wind sang through a notch in the rock and men had gone mad listening to it; the descent into Kestrel Hollow, steep enough that mules wore out their shoes going down. He knew which ledges held snow in shadow until midsummer and which streams froze from the bottom up, so that a man walking on them at noon would go through as if the ice had lied to him personally.

He knew the mountain the way other men knew their wives' faces, and it was the closest thing he had left to a relationship with anything holy.

"Two days' hard climbing," he said. "Weather holds for three, maybe. After that the front comes down off the Icecap and nobody crosses till spring."

"So we leave today."

"We leave now," Oscar said, and ground out the cigarette under his heel.

* * *

The courier arrived an hour later, and he was wrong in ways Oscar couldn't yet name.

He came up the river path on foot, no horse, which was sensible — horses died on the Thorn Road and everyone knew it — but he walked like a man who hadn't slept in days, moving carefully, favoring his left side. He wore a plain brown traveler's coat, good wool, unmarked, and carried nothing visible except a satchel that rode against his chest the way a mother carries an infant. Under the hood his face was pale as tallow, young-ish but aged by something other than years, with dark eyes that catalogued everything — the treeline, the fire pit, Maret's crossbow, Oscar's hands — in the flat, rapid way of a man deciding constantly whether the next moment would require running.

"You're the Wolf," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the guide. Names cost extra."

Something flickered at the corner of the courier's mouth — not quite a smile. "Kell said you were expensive and rude."

"Kell undersold me on one and oversold me on the other." Oscar held out his hand. The courier hesitated, then shook it, and Oscar felt the tremor in the man's grip, and the heat. Fever-heat. The skin of his palm was dry but burning, like touching a stone that had sat too long in summer sun.

"How long have you been sick?" Oscar asked.

"I'm not sick."

"You're feverish. Your pulse is wrong. You're favoring your left side, and you've got the walk of a man who's been riding hard for — I'd guess four days, maybe five, and dismounted recently in a hurry." Oscar let go of the hand. "I don't care what you're carrying, courier. But I do care if you die on my mountain, because dead clients don't pay the second half, and worse, they make messes. So: are you going to be able to climb, or are we turning around now while I can still return your deposit?"

The courier looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved through the pines above the ford, and somewhere downstream a heron cried out, harsh and solitary, the sound the river made when it had nothing else to say.

"I'll climb," the courier said. "Whatever it costs."

"That's the spirit that gets people killed." Oscar turned away. "Eat something. We move in twenty minutes, and the first day is the easy part. Enjoy it."

He walked back to the mules and began checking the girths, and behind him he heard Maret speaking low and quick to the stranger, offering bread, and the stranger refusing it twice before accepting, and Oscar thought: *he refuses food like a man who's learned kindness has a price tag.*

Then he thought about nothing at all, because that was the discipline, that was the whole art of the thing — emptying the head so the mountain could fill it. He checked hooves. He checked rope. He loaded the second mule with grain and the third with the dried meat and the waxed canvas and the little iron stove that had saved his life eleven times, and he did all of it with the smooth economy of long habit, and underneath the habit, quiet as groundwater, ran the prayer he never stopped saying anymore even though he no longer believed anyone heard it.

*I keep what I have sworn. Watch me or don't.*

* * *

They made the ford by midmorning and started climbing by noon.

Greywater Ford in autumn was a study in silver and rust — the river braided thin and bright over its stones, the alder thickets gone the color of old blood, the air smelling of cold water and coming frost. On the far bank the road narrowed and began to climb, and the world below fell away in stages: pasture, then pine, then the ragged krummholz where the trees grew sideways like men leaning into a gale, then nothing but stone and sky.

Oscar set a pace that was brutal without being cruel. He had guided lords and smugglers and once, memorably, a cardinal who had wept openly by the second hour, and the trick was always the same: never slow down for whining, but stop before the silence turns to collapse. He watched the courier out of the corner of his eye the whole first afternoon. The man climbed badly but stubbornly, breathing in a controlled rhythm that was clearly trained, planting each step with deliberate care, and twice — only twice — he put a hand against the rock and stood there with his head bowed for longer than rest required, as if listening to something inside the stone.

Or inside himself.

At dusk they camped in the lee of the first ridge, in a hollow Oscar had used forty times, sheltered from the north wind by a shelf of granite older than any kingdom. Maret built the fire small and shielded, the way he'd taught her, so the light wouldn't show from the valley. They ate barley porridge with salt pork, and the courier ate all of his and half of what Maret pushed at him, finally, and afterward sat looking into the flames with an expression Oscar had seen on many faces in many camps, usually shortly before those faces stopped existing.

"You've done this crossing before," the courier said suddenly. To Oscar. Not a question again. The man didn't seem to ask questions; he seemed to confirm things he already suspected.

"Ninety-one times."

"Why that number?"

"Because I count." Oscar fed a stick to the fire. "Counting keeps the head quiet. You'd know about that."

"Would I?"

"You listen to things that aren't there. I've watched you doing it since the ford. Listening to the rock, listening to the wind, listening to whatever's making you hot as a forge and shaky as a drunk. Whatever you're carrying in that satchel, it isn't paper." Oscar met his eyes across the fire. "I don't want to know. That's not modesty, it's professionalism. The less I know, the less I can sell, and the less I can be tortured out of, and the fewer reasons the empire or the guilds or anybody else has to come find me in ten years and ask me questions I can't answer. But I'll tell you what I do know, courier, free of charge: whatever it is, it's killing you, and the mountain doesn't forgive sick men. So tomorrow, if I say run, you run. If I say drop, you drop. If I say we're turning around, you argue with me after we've turned around. Swear that much, and I'll get you to Kestrel Gate if it's possible for a living man to get there."

The fire cracked. Sparks rose and died among the stars, which over Thornfell were dense and merciless, a spill of salt across black cloth, the kind of sky that reminded a man how small his prayers were.

"And if it's not possible?" the courier asked quietly.

Oscar shrugged. "Then it isn't. I don't make promises the mountain hasn't approved. I've buried nineteen people on these slopes, courier. Some of them were promised things by better guides than me."

For a moment the courier just looked at him, and something passed over his face that Oscar would spend the rest of this story failing to forget — a look of terrible recognition, as if the man had heard, in Oscar's careful mercenary cynicism, an echo of something he himself had once believed and lost.

"All right," the courier said. "If you say run, I run." And he pressed his thumb against his own sternum, briefly, some private gesture, some private seal, and Oscar felt the hair rise along his arms for no reason he could explain, the way animals feel weather before it comes.

Later, when Maret had first watch and the courier lay wrapped in his coat breathing too fast and too shallow, Oscar sat alone at the edge of the firelight and did the thing he always did.

He prayed.

Not kneeling — he'd stopped kneeling years ago; it hurt his knees and offended his pride in equal measure — just sitting, hands loose, eyes open, watching the coals breathe. The old words, the apprentice's words, the ones carved inside every waystone between here and the sea: *Vethram, Keeper of the Given Word. I have kept faith. I have broken none. Ten years you have not answered, and still I keep faith, because keeping is what I am, even if no one is counting. Even if you are not counting. Especially then.*

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. The coals settled, the wind moved, the courier's fever-breath rasped softly in the dark, and the silence of the god lay over the camp like snowfall, vast and white and utterly indifferent.

But — and this was new, this was the thing Oscar would turn over in his mind for weeks afterward — as he finished the prayer and reached for his bedroll, he felt something. Not an answer. Not warmth, not voice, not the old stone-warmth of acknowledged oath. More like the moment before lightning strikes, when the air goes strange and the cattle grow restless and old men look at the sky. A pressure. A leaning-in. As if the mountain itself had shifted its weight, very slightly, toward the sleeping courier by the fire.

Oscar sat back down. He listened the way he'd taught the courier to listen, without knowing he was teaching it. The wind sang through the notch in the far ridge. The river muttered below. Somewhere upslope, loose stone ticked and settled.

And faintly, from the direction of the pass they would climb at dawn — so faintly he might have invented it, and would tell himself for years that he had — he heard something deep under Thornfell that was not water and not wind and not any sound the mountain had made in ninety-one previous crossings.

A heartbeat. Slow. Enormous. Patient as stone.

It beat once, and Oscar's own heart stumbled trying to match it, and then the night was just a night again, and the coals were dying, and Maret called softly from watch that all was quiet.

Oscar lay down and did not sleep. Above him the stars wheeled, indifferent, beautiful, and the prayer ran on in him like a river under ice, and ninety-two miles to the north, though he did not yet know it, riders wearing no colors were already moving toward Greywater Ford, following the trail of a dying man.

In the morning, the fog on the switchbacks would pool like milk, and out of it, bleeding, would come the beginning of everything.
