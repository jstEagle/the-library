## 2. A Stranger on the Cold Road

The switchbacks above Greywater Ford were carved into the mountain's flank like the ribs of some drowned leviathan, and in the last grey hour before dawn they were slick with a frost that did not melt so much as surrender. Oscar Joubert knew every turn of them. He had carried contraband salt up this road at nineteen, silk down it at twenty-five, and once — a story he never told — a coffin that weighed far too little for what the widow claimed was inside it. Ten years of walking these passes had taught him the mountain's moods the way other men learned their wives': which ledges wept water in spring, where the wind funneled through the Narrows like breath through a flute, how the frost lied to you on the north-facing stone until your boots found the truth the hard way.

He came up out of Greywater Ford before first light because the ford's one innkeeper talked too much and Oscar had cargo he didn't want described to strangers. Three crates of Kestrelli brandy, honest enough work, bound over Thornfell to a buyer who paid half up front and asked no questions about the route. Routine. The word sat in his chest like a smooth river stone, comforting through sheer familiarity.

The cold had teeth that morning. It came off the high snowfields in long exhalations, pouring down the switchbacks ahead of him, and his breath plumed white and vanished as fast as he made it. His mule, Bess, picked her way with the sour patience of an animal who had done this road more times than she could count and resented each one.

Oscar prayed.

He didn't mean to. That was the obscenity of it. His lips moved around words older than the empire — *Vow-Keeper, hear me, keep my feet* — addressed to a god who had not answered in ten years, and he said them anyway, the way a man whistles in an empty house just to hear something that isn't silence. Habit. Rot. Whatever you wanted to call the thing that kept growing back after you cut it out.

Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. Somewhere above him, past the cloud deck, there was presumably still a heaven, but its doors had shut in his face a decade ago and no amount of knocking had changed that. He finished the prayer anyway, felt the familiar ash-taste of it, and spat into the frost.

That was when he heard the horse.

Not Bess — she stood hipshot and indifferent beside him, ears flicking. This was another animal entirely, coming down from the high shelf at a pace no sane rider used on these stones, hooves striking the frozen track with a rhythm too quick and too desperate to be anything but flight. Oscar had spent twenty years reading sounds on mountains: rockfall from avalanche, wolf from dog, hunter from hunted. He knew this sound. He had *been* this sound, more than once, in years he did not revisit.

He killed his lantern and stepped Bess off the track into the shadow of a boulder field, hand finding the haft of the axe at his saddle without any conscious decision. The axe was old and the grip was worn to the shape of his fist, and that shape was the only vow he'd kept faithfully in ten years.

The horse burst around the switchback's elbow below him — a rangy grey lathered white with sweat, foam freezing in its whiskers, running on the last furlong of itself. And then, as it swung wide on the turn, Oscar saw the rider, or rather saw what was left of one: a man folded forward over the horse's neck, one hand knotted in the mane and the other pressed hard against his own side, and even at forty paces in the pre-dawn murk Oscar could see the black shine of blood between his fingers.

The horse stumbled on the icy lip of the turn. Recovered. Stumbled again, worse, forelegs skidding on stone glazed with runoff frozen overnight. The rider slid sideways, caught himself, slid again —

— and then the mountain decided, as the mountain always eventually decides, and both of them went down.

It was not a long fall, only six feet of broken slope and scree, but it was enough. The grey horse screamed — a sound Oscar would hear for days afterward, high and human — and rolled, and the man struck the rocks with a wet, final-sounding weight and did not move again. The horse thrashed once, twice, tried to rise, and its hind leg bent in a way legs do not bend, and it screamed again, quieter now, and lay still except for the heave of its ribs.

Oscar stayed behind his boulder and counted.

That was the discipline of it. Twenty years of mercenary work taught you to count before you moved. He counted the sounds of the pass: wind in the funnel above, the tick of freezing water, Bess's slow breathing, the dying horse's ragged bellows. No second horse. No voices. But somewhere up the dark shelf, faint as memory, there was a rhythm he didn't like — hoofbeats, several, moving at the pace of men following a trail rather than chasing one. Men who knew their quarry was nearly spent.

He should leave. Every calculation he owned said leave. There was a wounded stranger on the road who was none of his business, pursued by riders whose business was clearly violence, and Oscar Joubert was carrying three crates of brandy and ten years of wanting nothing more than to be left alone. Helping meant entanglement. Entanglement meant questions. Questions, on this particular morning, on this particular road, meant the kind of trouble that got smugglers buried in unmarked snow.

He counted to sixty. Then, hating himself with a thoroughness that surprised him, he counted to sixty again.

Then he went down the slope.

* * *

Up close the stranger was younger than the fall had made him look, thirty perhaps, with the kind of fine-boned face that belonged in archive rooms rather than mountain passes — pale from blood loss, lips going the grey-blue of old slate. His hair was dark and plastered to his forehead. His clothes told a strange story: travel-worn wool of good cut, a courier's oilskin cloak gone stiff with frost, riding boots worth more than everything Oscar was carrying in brandy. A scholar's wardrobe dressed for a war it hadn't expected.

And wounds. Oscar had seen enough of them to read them like script. A long gash along the ribs — blade, not rock, and recent, the blood still too bright. An arrow graze across the shoulder, torn through cloak and flesh both. Bruising blooming along the jaw where someone had hit him, hard, with intent. Someone had worked this man over carefully and then let him run, which was a cruelty Oscar recognized; it was the cruelty of men who wanted information before they wanted a corpse.

He crouched and put two fingers against the man's throat. A pulse, thready but stubborn, like a fire refusing a draft.

"Hey." He said it low, then again, rougher. "Hey. You're on my road."

The man's eyes opened. They were a startling color, grey-green like sea glass, and they fixed on Oscar with an intensity that had no business coming out of a body this close to empty. They weren't confused. They weren't afraid. They were *measuring* — sweeping Oscar's face, his axe, his mercenary's mail beneath the sheepskin, doing arithmetic with whatever was left of the man's mind.

"You're not—" The voice cracked, barely a whisper. "You're not theirs."

"No," Oscar said. "Whoever theirs is."

"They'll come." The man's hand crawled across the scree, found Oscar's wrist, gripped it with a strength that startled them both. Blood-warm fingers. "They're behind me. On the shelf. Four riders — maybe five — they don't wear colors, they don't carry banners, they—" A cough took him, deep and wrong, and pink froth showed at the corner of his mouth. "Please."

Everything in Oscar's chest said *no*. It was a well-trained chest. Twenty years of saying no had built calluses inside it thick as armor plate. Say no, walk away, lead Bess up the next switchback, deliver the brandy, sleep indoors tomorrow night. Simple. Safe. The math was not complicated.

But the man's hand was on his wrist, and the man's eyes were open and burning, and underneath all the calluses, in some cellar of himself he'd bricked over years ago, something stirred and looked around and refused to sit back down.

"Damn it," Oscar said quietly, to himself, to the mountain, to whatever silent god was failing to watch. "Damn it all."

He got the man's arm over his shoulders and lifted. The stranger cried out once through clenched teeth — a disciplined sound, bitten short, the sound of a man who had decided not to make noise ever again — and then went mostly limp, head lolling against Oscar's collar, breath hot and rapid against his neck.

Bess watched the whole operation with an expression of profound equine disapproval.

"Yes," Oscar told her. "I know."

Getting the wounded man onto the mule was a grim, clumsy business conducted in increments, each of which drew a sound from the stranger that he clearly hated making. Oscar stripped the dead grey horse's saddlebags — habit; you didn't leave good leather lying on a battlefield, and besides, the man would need everything he had — and draped the ruined animal's blanket over its flanks. He couldn't do anything else for it. He didn't say anything to it either. Some things you just carried.

Then he stood in the track and listened.

The hoofbeats on the high shelf had stopped. That was worse. Chasing horses make noise; hunters go quiet, and spread out, and start reading sign. These switchbacks held frost like a page holds ink, and two sets of tracks — mule and dragged man — wrote themselves down it plain as scripture.

Oscar looked up at where the shelf vanished into cloud. Looked down at the switchback below, which wound toward Greywater Ford, toward warmth and people and witnesses, toward the riders who would arrive asking careful questions in careful voices while the innkeeper talked too much.

He thought about the innkeeper. He thought about the widow who ran the drying sheds, and the ferryman's kids who sold chestnuts on the landing, and every face in that small riverside town that had fed him soup and minded its own business for ten years. He thought about four riders wearing no colors, and what *no colors* meant — meant someone paying well enough that deniability mattered, meant professionals, meant a town full of witnesses being a problem rather than a protection.

His chest did the thing again. The untrained thing. The thing in the cellar.

"Right," he muttered. "Up, then."

There was a route off the main track a quarter-mile higher — a drover's path, barely a scar on the mountainside, that climbed away from the ford through pine shadow and deadfall. It added two days. It crossed ground that broke horses' legs and men's resolve, and no caravan used it, and no sane person used it, and it was the single most beautiful idea Oscar had ever had, because it went up instead of down, away instead of toward, and it was his.

He led Bess onto it with the stranger swaying in the saddle behind the brandy crates, tied loosely at the waist so a fall wouldn't be fatal, and the pines closed over them like a door.

* * *

An hour past dawn, in a hollow ringed with granite and old-growth roots, Oscar called a halt because the man's breathing had gone frighteningly shallow. He lifted him down, propped him against the bole of a pine, and did what field surgery he could: cleaned the rib wound with snow-melt and the last of his spirit flask, packed it with moss and linen, splinted nothing because nothing seemed broken outright, though the man's side was a map of purple already spreading. The arrow graze was filthy and would fester if it got the chance. Oscar stitched it with the same needle he used for tack, and the stranger bore it without waking, which told Oscar his body had made some private decision about conserving everything it had left.

While he worked, he went through the saddlebags. Sensible, necessary, and he told himself so twice. Mostly they held ordinary things: dried fruit, a wax-sealed letter case, a spare shirt of absurd fineness. But at the bottom of the second bag, wrapped in oilcloth, he found a scroll case of black lacquer, and when his thumb brushed its seal the case was *warm*. Not sun-warm. Warm like a living thing, warm from within, a heat that pressed up through the lacquer into his palm and lingered after he set it down.

He stared at it for a long moment. The warmth pulsed once, faintly, slow as a sleeping heart.

He wrapped it back in the oilcloth and stowed it under the brandy crates, and told himself firmly that it was none of his affair, that he'd deliver the man to wherever the man could be delivered, take whatever thanks was offered, and wash his hands of the whole business. He was a smuggler, not a rescuer. He had a schedule. He had a contract. He had, above all, a decade of practice at not caring, and he resented how rusty it suddenly felt.

The stranger woke near midday, when the light came slanting gold through the pines and the frost had given up the ghost. His eyes found Oscar immediately — that same measuring look, though dimmer now, the arithmetic slower.

"Why," he rasped. It wasn't gratitude. It was a genuine question, asked with the flat urgency of a man who needed the answer to plan around it.

Oscar considered lying. Considered a dozen easy answers — payment owed, curiosity, boredom. Instead he heard himself say, gruff and unwilling: "Because you asked. Nobody asks anymore. Everyone just takes." He shrugged, angry at his own mouth. "It annoyed me."

Something moved at the corner of the stranger's mouth. Not quite a smile. The place where a smile might have lived, if he'd had the strength to build one.

"Cyrus," he whispered, and pressed two fingers briefly to his own breastbone, as if sealing the name in place. "My name is Cyrus."

"Oscar." He didn't offer a hand. "And before you get ideas — I'm taking you as far as Hollowpine, because it's on my way and because leaving you in the open is the same as killing you and I've got enough on my conscience. After that, we part ways, and whatever's in that lacquer case stays yours. I don't want to know."

Cyrus's eyes drifted, deliberate and unmistakable, to the brandy crates. To the corner of black lacquer showing beneath the sacking where Oscar had stowed it badly. Something flickered across the grey-green — fear, sharp and total, there and mastered.

"It's warm, isn't it," Cyrus whispered.

Oscar didn't answer.

Above them, far up the hidden shelf, the wind shifted and brought with it, thin and brief, a sound that froze them both: hoofbeats. Many. Moving along the top of the world, parallel to their course, patient as winter.

Cyrus closed his eyes. "They've found my trail," he breathed. "Wolf — I need to tell you what I'm carrying. Before they catch us. You won't believe it. God help me, I barely believe it myself."

The name *Wolf* landed strangely in Oscar's chest — an old name, a dead name, from a life he'd buried — but there was no time to ask how a bleeding stranger knew it, because Cyrus's hand had found the lacquer case, and the case, impossibly, was glowing faintly through the oilcloth, pulsing in time with the heartbeat of the dying man who held it.
