## 4. Riders Without Colors

The smoke reached Oscar before the news did.

He was two miles below Greywater Ford, coaxing a stubborn mule around a scree field that wanted to eat her legs, when he saw it: a thin grey thread rising off the eastern ridge, too straight and too patient to be a cookfire. Woodsmoke wandered. Burning villages climbed.

He stopped and stood very still, the way he'd learned to stand in the war, back when standing still had been the difference between living and being shot by someone who noticed movement first. The wind came down off Thornfell carrying pine resin and cold stone, and beneath it — faint, sour, wrong — the smell of burning thatch and something else underneath it, something sweeter and more animal that he refused to name until he absolutely had to.

Cyrus lay across the second mule's back like cargo, wrapped in Oscar's spare blanket, breathing in shallow sips. He had slept through the night in fits, muttering names of people Oscar would never meet, and once — clearly, terribly — *the archive is already burned, I watched it burn.* In the morning his fever had broken enough for him to drink broth and ask sensible questions about the route, which Oscar had answered with lies so smooth they surprised even him.

Now Oscar looked at the smoke and understood that every one of those lies had just become true.

*Greywater Ford,* he thought. *They went to Greywater Ford.*

There were eleven buildings at Greywater Ford. A mill with a wheel that had turned since before the empire annexed the valley. A ferryman named Aldous who owed Oscar money and would never live to repay it, because Aldous talked to everyone, and everyone at the ford knew a man had come through two days ago slung over a smuggler's mule, bleeding through his bandages, asking nothing and saying less.

Oscar did the arithmetic without wanting to. Riders wearing no colors. A courier. Questions asked politely first, then not politely. A village of forty souls, maybe fifty, most of them related to each other, all of them now either dead or scattered into the high pines where children froze before they starved.

His hands were steady. That was the worst part. Ten years of mercenary work had taught his body to go calm while the rest of him screamed, and he stood there on the switchback with the mule's rope in his fist and felt that terrible professional stillness settle over him like a coat he couldn't take off.

"Get up," he said to Cyrus, not unkindly. "We're leaving the road."

---

The Ford had been the last easy crossing before the mountains proper. Every map Oscar owned — and he owned maps drawn by smugglers, which meant maps drawn by people who needed to know where the soldiers weren't — showed three ways over Thornfell from this side. The southern pass, wide and graded, built by imperial engineers a century ago to move armies and ore. The Kestrel Gate, narrower, controlled by the guild, passable if you paid the toll and didn't ask what the toll bought. And then there was the third way.

Nobody drew the third way on maps. That was rather the point of it.

Oscar's father had called it the Shepherd's Cut, though his father hadn't invented the name; shepherds had used it for generations to move flocks between summer and winter pasture, back before the wolves got bold and the empire got interested. It went up past the tree line, along a shelf of broken stone no wider than a coffin lid in places, across a glacier that ate men whole and gave their bones back years later at the snout, and down the far side by a route that existed mostly as rumor. No mule had ever made it. No horse. Half the shepherds who tried it in bad years hadn't either.

It was, in other words, exactly the kind of route a man chose when the alternative was worse than dying.

Oscar spread his maps on a flat rock in the lee of a boulder and weighted the corners with stones, and Cyrus knelt beside him, swaying slightly, his face the color of old tallow.

"You're looking at the southern pass," Cyrus said.

"I'm looking at everything."

"The southern pass is fastest."

"The southern pass has riders on it." Oscar tapped the Kestrel Gate. "And this one has Brannoc's toll-men, and whatever Brannoc knows, the guild sells. So." His finger moved up, up, past the last contour anyone had bothered to survey, into the blank white space above the tree line where the cartographers had written, in tiny dismissive letters, *elevations impracticable.*

Cyrus stared at the blank space for a long moment. Then he laughed — a dry, cracked sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man discovering that his situation had finally become absurd enough to be funny.

"That's not a route," he said. "That's a place where routes go to die."

"It's a route if you don't die."

"You've taken it?"

"Once. Coming down, in summer, with a full pack and both my legs working and a guide who knew the shelf better than his own prayers. He's dead now. Fell in the icefall two winters back." Oscar rolled the maps away. "I remember it well enough. Whether remembering is the same as knowing, we'll find out."

"And the mules?"

Oscar looked at the mules. Sorrel, his lead, fifteen years old and mean as a tax collector. The younger one, whose name he'd never settled on because naming things you might have to abandon was a habit he'd sworn off after the war. They stood hipshot in the cold, ears swiveling, wise in the way animals are wise about coming misery.

"One goes as far as she goes," Oscar said. "Then we eat well and go on without her."

Cyrus flinched. Not at the cruelty of it — Oscar suspected there wasn't much left in the world that could shock him — but at the casualness, the bookkeeping of it, a life converted to rations in a single sentence. He opened his mouth, closed it, and said instead:

"They burned the ford, didn't they. That's the smoke."

It wasn't a question, but Oscar answered it anyway, because lying to a dying man seemed suddenly like a debt he'd have to answer for. "Yes."

"How many?"

"At the ford? Forty. Fifty."

"No." Cyrus's eyes were clear now, fever-bright and horribly lucid. "How many riders?"

Oscar paused. "I don't know. A troop. Enough to seal a valley."

"Then it doesn't matter how many buildings they burned or how many people were in them." Cyrus pressed his palm flat against the rock, against the map, against the blank white space. "What matters is that they haven't found us yet, and every hour we stand here arguing, the circle they're drawing gets smaller. Take me up the death-route, Joubert. I'll die faster on the mountain, but I'll die *useless to them*, and right now that's worth more to me than another week of breath."

There it was again — that strange gravity Cyrus carried, the thing that had made Oscar haul him off the road when every instinct had screamed to keep walking. Men didn't talk like that about their own deaths unless they'd made peace with them long ago, and men who'd made peace with death carried secrets the way other men carried knives: always, everywhere, close to the skin.

"All right," Oscar said. "Up we go."

---

They came down to the valley's edge at dusk, close enough to Greywater Ford to see what remained of it, because Oscar needed water and the stream above the ford ran clean before it passed through the ruin.

He left Cyrus hidden in a fold of the hillside with the mules and went down alone, moving the way he moved when he hunted — weight on the outside edges of his feet, breath timed to the wind. The smell grew as he descended. Thatch and timber, yes, but under it now, unmistakable, the sweet reek he'd refused to name.

The mill was gone. The wheel lay split in the shallows, turning slowly in the current out of pure habit, creaking like something in pain. Of the eleven buildings, four still stood, and only because the fire had run out of appetite before it reached them. The rest were black ribs against the evening sky, smoking gently, and in the square — because there was always a square, villages arranged themselves around some central point where the burning could be done efficiently —

In the square they had left the ones who wouldn't talk.

Oscar counted nine. He made himself count all nine, because he owed them that much, because looking away was how men like the riders won without drawing steel. Aldous was among them. So was the miller's wife, and a boy of perhaps twelve who had once, two summers ago, followed Oscar around asking questions about the mountains until Aldous paid him a copper to stop.

Nine bodies. And scratched into the door of the one standing house, in careful imperial block-letters, a message for anyone who came looking:

**THE COURIER WILL BE FOUND. THE MOUNTAIN WILL NOT HIDE HIM.**

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, in a different hand — quicker, angrier:

**HE CARRIES WHAT WAS STOLEN FROM THE EMPEROR.**

Oscar read it twice. Then he filled his waterskins at the stream, upstream of the bodies, and walked back up the hill without letting himself think about the boy with the questions, or the boy's mother, or any of it, because thinking could come later, on the mountain, where there would be nothing else to do.

When he reached the fold in the hillside, Cyrus took one look at his face and didn't ask.

"We go tonight," Oscar said. "Dark climb, moonrise late. While they're still sweeping the low country, thinking no fool would take the Shepherd's Cut in autumn."

"And if they've thought of it?"

"Then we'll meet them on the shelf," Oscar said, "and the mountain will decide whose fool is worse."

He checked the girths. Loaded the waterskins. Took one last look south, toward the flat dark line of the lowlands where the empire lay sleeping on its hoard of stolen fire, and felt something shift in his chest — not the old silence where his god used to live, but something harder, newer, with teeth.

For ten years he had carried cargo and kept his head down and told himself that neutrality was a kind of honor. Now there were nine bodies in a square and a boy's name he'd never learned, and the arithmetic had changed. This was no longer smuggling. Smuggling had rules, prices, a shape you could hold in your hand. This was survival, and survival had only one rule: *get the mountain between them and us, and let the cold do the rest.*

"Move," he said softly, to the mules, to the dying man, to himself. "While the dark lasts."

And behind them, far to the south, torchlight kindled along the ridgeline like a slow red smile — riders, many riders, turning their faces toward the mountain.
