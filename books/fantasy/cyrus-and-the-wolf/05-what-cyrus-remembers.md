## 5. What Cyrus Remembers

The fever dreams came in fragments, like a map torn into pieces and scattered on the wind.

* * *

First, the vault.

He is young again, or younger at least — twenty-two, with ink still under his fingernails from his morning's copying and the particular hunger of a man who believes knowledge is a ladder he has only begun to climb. The archive beneath the Imperial Palace in Vharen does not smell of dust. That is the first thing everyone notices and no one speaks of. It smells of *heat*. Of stone warmed from somewhere far below, as though the mountain itself were a sleeping animal and the archive were built upon its flank.

Cyrus walks the long gallery with his lamp held high, past shelves of vellum and brass-bound codices, past the cartography wing where he has spent three years learning to draw coastlines that will never change and mountain ranges that will never forgive him for getting them wrong. He is an archivist of the fourth rank. He is nobody. He is permitted exactly where his lamp is permitted, and nowhere else.

But tonight the door at the end of the Gallery of Founding is open.

It is never open. He has worked here three years and it has never once been open, and now it stands ajar like a mouth about to speak, breathing warmth into the cold corridor, and Cyrus tells himself he is only going to look. Only look. Curiosity is not theft. Curiosity is the whole reason archives exist.

He steps through.

The room beyond is not a room. It is a throat of stone descending in wide spirals, and the walls are carved — no, he corrects himself, staring, the lamp trembling in his hand — the walls are not carved. They are *scaled*. Thousands upon thousands of overlapping plates of some dark material, each the size of a shield, each faintly warm when he lays his palm against one. And set into those scaled walls, recessed into alcoves like jewels into a crown, sleep the dragons.

Not bones. Not statues. Dragons. Dozens of them, perhaps a hundred, curled in postures of such perfect repose that he might believe them sculptures if not for the heat rolling off their bodies in slow tides, if not for the sound —

— and there is a sound. A low, vast, rhythmic resonance that he feels in his sternum before he hears it with his ears, a drumbeat so deep and slow it takes him several heartbeats to understand that what he is hearing is breathing. All of them breathing together. The whole vault inhaling and exhaling like a single enormous lung.

*Sleeping,* thinks Cyrus, twenty-two years old and weeping without understanding why. *They are not dead. Everyone says they are extinct. Everyone says the last dragon died at Redmark two hundred years ago. They are sleeping.*

And then he sees the emperor.

Not the current one — this is memory, and memory is treacherous, and the faces shift sometimes — but an emperor, crowned and robed in white, standing at the center of the spiral with his hand resting flat against the brow of the nearest sleeper the way a farmer rests his hand on the flank of an ox he intends to slaughter. There are men around him in the grey livery of the Ordinance Office, and they carry instruments Cyrus does not have names for: long hollow needles of black glass, chains whose every link is inscribed, braziers burning something that gives off light the color of an old wound.

The emperor says, and his voice carries down the whole spiral throat of the world:

*"Drain them slowly. If we take too much at once they wake, and waking they burn. A little fire every season, drawn off while they dream. We will have our weapons within a decade, and no one outside these walls will ever know dragons still breathe."*

Cyrus stands in the shadow of the doorway with his lamp guttering, and he understands that he is looking at the greatest crime in the history of the empire, and that the crime has been going on for longer than he has been alive, and that the empire he serves — the empire whose maps he draws, whose borders he renders in beautiful confident ink — is a farmer with its hand on the ox's brow, patient and gentle and absolutely certain of its right.

He should leave. He knows, even in the dream, even in the memory, that a wise man leaves. A wise man forgets. A wise man goes back to his coastlines and his ink and lives to be old.

Cyrus was never entirely wise.

* * *

Second, the order.

There is a document. Of course there is a document; he is an archivist, and crimes in his world are committed in triplicate. He finds it weeks later — or years later, time moves strangely here among the fevered galleries — filed under agricultural levies, which is either carelessness or contempt. It bears the imperial seal. It authorizes the "seasonal attenuation" of the Founding Vault's assets, and it lists, in a clerk's tidy hand, the numbers: how much fire drawn per dragon per year. How many dragons remain. When the count began at four hundred and twelve, and how many rows of that ledger are already closed out in red ink.

Four hundred and twelve.

He counts the sleepers in the vault over the course of many unlawful visits, and the number he arrives at is eleven.

Eleven dragons where there had been four hundred and twelve. Eleven hearts being bled a little at a time by men who call it *attenuation*, who file their paperwork neatly, who go home to wives and children and sleep soundly because the thing they are killing cannot scream.

Cyrus sits in his narrow room above the archive with the stolen ledger page hidden inside a treatise on irrigation, and he tries to think of someone he can tell. The Chancellery. The Temple of Scales — but the Temple blessed the Ordinance Office's instruments, he remembers now, he read the invoice. His fellow archivists, who would report him before he finished speaking, not from evil but from the ordinary decent cowardice that keeps empires running. There is no one. That is what an empire is, he understands finally. It is a machine for ensuring there is no one.

So he does what an archivist does. What he knows how to do. What he is, in the end, made of.

He copies everything.

* * *

Third, the theft.

Memory frays here, scorched at the edges, and Cyrus wakes once during the writing of this part — wakes in the cold, in the mountains, in a body that no longer feels entirely like his own — and hears wind, and hears a man breathing nearby, slow and watchful, a stranger keeping guard over him for reasons Cyrus cannot yet remember. Then the fever pulls him back down.

The theft took nine months. He wants to laugh at that, even now; nine months, like a birth. Nine months of small permissions gathered like drops of water. A forged transfer order here. A night spent memorizing the rotation of the door-wardens there. A fire-drill he himself reported — oh, the beautiful irony of it, the archivist reporting a safety violation so that the Gallery of Founding would stand unguarded for one quarter-hour while the drill emptied the wings.

He could not take a dragon. He understood that early, with grief. You cannot smuggle a sleeping mountain out through a corridor. But the vault had a heart of its own — a master-key, in a sense, the thing the Ordinance Office called the *Index Ignis*: a single tablet of translucent stone, warm always, on which was written the location of every vault, every sleeper, every vein of drawn fire the empire had tapped across three centuries. The map of all their crimes. Without it, the Ordinance Office could find nothing. With it —

With it, someone outside the walls might find the last of them first.

He remembers the night itself with terrible clarity, the way a man remembers the day his father died. The tablet warm in his hands like a living thing, like a bird with a racing heart. The alarm bells beginning somewhere above just as he reached the river gate — he never learned what tripped them, and has hated the not-knowing for two years. Running along the aqueduct with sirens behind him and the tablet strapped against his bare chest, feeling its heat through his shirt, feeling it *know* him somehow, feeling it choose him. The courier network that cost him everything he owned and half of what he was. Greywater Ford. The switchbacks. Riders in no colors at all, moving with a competence no mere bounty crew possesses.

And then the arrow, and the cold, and the road tilting up to meet him.

* * *

Now.

Cyrus surfaces from all of it at once, gasping, and for a moment he does not know where he is — the stone ceiling is wrong, the air smells of pine smoke and wet wool and horse, and there is a fire crackling low in a ring of stones, and a man sits beside it cleaning a blade with the unhurried economy of someone who has done it ten thousand times. Big man. Grey at the temples. Eyes that come up and fix on Cyrus the instant he stirs, the way a wolf's eyes come up.

"The fever broke an hour ago," the man says. His voice is gravel over iron. "Drink."

A tin cup appears at Cyrus's lips. The water tastes of snowmelt and something bitter. He drinks because his body insists, and when the cup lowers he lies there a moment, shaking, taking inventory of himself the way he'd take inventory of a damaged codex: binding cracked, pages water-stained, but the text intact. The text intact. The most important text in the world, intact.

"The map," he rasps.

"Safe." The big man's eyes flick toward the oilcloth bundle near the fire, then back. "Been trying not to look at it. Doesn't want to be looked at, that thing. Warm through the wrapping, like a sick animal."

Cyrus almost laughs, and it turns into coughing. *Like a sick animal.* The man has no idea how precisely he has spoken.

"You're the smuggler," Cyrus manages. "From the ford. You carried me."

"I carried a job that went strange. Don't make more of it than that." But there is something in the way he says it — something guarded, something that has already begun to be loyal and resents knowing it. "You've been talking in your sleep. Three days. Names I don't know. Numbers. Four hundred and twelve — you said that a lot. Four hundred and twelve."

"Dragons." The word comes out of Cyrus like a tooth being pulled. He has carried it alone for two years, carried it through rivers and checkpoints and a hundred lying smiles, and now he gives it away in a single breath because he is dying, and a dying man's secret is a stone he can finally set down. "In the Founding Vault under the palace in Vharen. Sleeping dragons. Not dead — sleeping. The empire has been draining their fire for generations, a little at a time, to build weapons. There were four hundred and twelve when they began. There are eleven left."

The knife stops moving against the whetstone.

The silence stretches. Wind presses at the tent flap. Somewhere outside, a mule shifts its weight and stamps.

"That's madness," the man says at last, but his voice has changed key, the way voices do when they are hoping to be contradicted and failing.

"It's a ledger," Cyrus says. "I'm an archivist. Was. Madness comes with footnotes." He drags himself up onto one elbow, and the effort costs him — the world swims, the fire smears — but he holds the big man's eyes because this next part matters more than his own breath. "Listen to me. The map isn't treasure. It isn't a route or a mine or anything anyone can spend. It's a list of graves, and one of them isn't finished. Under Thornfell — these mountains we're sitting in — there's a chamber the Index calls the First Hearth. And the Index doesn't lie, because I've read its sister-copies, and every one agrees. There is a dragon's heart still beating beneath Thornfell. The last one. Kept alive all this time by something the empire never found. And the riders who shot me aren't bandits. They're the Ordinance Office's own hounds, and they're not hunting a thief. They're hunting the address."

The man — Joubert, he said his name was, Oscar Joubert, though he wears the name like an ill-fitting coat — sets down the knife. Slowly. The way men set things down when their hands want to do something else with them.

"Why tell me?" he says. "You don't know me. For all you know I sell the map tomorrow and retire rich."

"I watched you for three days through a fever," Cyrus says. "You checked my wounds every few hours even when I wasn't awake to see you doing it. You fed me broth you complained loudly about making. You prayed last night — don't argue, I heard you — and you prayed like a man arguing with someone who's stopped answering, which means you haven't stopped asking. Men who've stopped asking don't check strangers' bandages." His voice is fraying now, the lucidity a candle burning down to the socket, but he pushes on. "Besides. It doesn't matter what you do. The secret is in your head now too, Joubert. I gave you the oath and the map, and the map gives up its secrets to whoever carries it long enough. That's why they'll come for you now, not just me. You're not a smuggler anymore. You're a witness."

Something crosses the man's face — fear, maybe, or its older cousin, recognition.

"The heart under Thornfell," Joubert says quietly. "If it's real. If any of this is real. What happens when they find it?"

"What happens when a butcher finds the last ox?" Cyrus lets himself sink back down, exhausted, the fever waiting at the edges like wolves at a firelight's rim. "They drain it. They forge it into fire-weapons. And then, Joubert, there are no more dragons anywhere in the world, ever again, and the men who did it file their paperwork neatly and sleep soundly." His eyes are closing. He fights it. "Unless someone gets there first. Unless someone stands between them and the mountain."

"And you thought that someone would be a mercenary you hired off a river dock?"

"No," Cyrus murmurs, and the dream is rising up to take him again, the vault, the breathing, the emperor's white-gloved hand on the sleeping brow. "I thought whoever found me bleeding on that road and *carried me anyway* would do."

He sleeps. And in his sleep he dreams of the vault again, of the great slow tide of breathing, and this time — for the first time — the dream changes. This time one of the sleepers opens one eye, ancient and gold and banked like a coal, and looks at him across two hundred years of dark, and Cyrus feels a question forming in that gaze like heat forming in stone, a question meant not for him at all but for the man keeping watch beside the fire.

Far to the south, down in the valley, Greywater Ford began to burn.
