## 6. The Widow of Hollowpine

The snow found them an hour past dusk, and Hollowpine found them an hour after that.

Oscar smelled the chimney smoke before he saw the house — woodsmoke and something else beneath it, rosemary and rendered fat and the faint mineral tang of a forge gone cold but not forgotten. It threaded through the falling snow like a hand reaching out of the dark, and Cyrus, half-asleep against his back, lifted his head like a hound catching a scent.

"Someone's home," he murmured.

"Someone's *home*," Oscar corrected grimly. "That's the problem."

Every instinct he owned told him to pass it by. A lit window in the mountains was either sanctuary or bait, and in ten years of this work he had learned that the ratio ran about even. But Cyrus had stopped shivering two miles back, which was worse than shivering — shivering meant the body still fighting — and the switchbacks below were already white to the depth of a man's knee, and the boy's map pressed against Oscar's ribs like a second heartbeat.

He turned up the goat-track toward the light.

* * *

The house sat in a fold of the mountain like a stone someone had set down and forgotten. Low-roofed, turf-banked on the north side, built from the same grey rock as everything else in Hollowpine, so that it seemed less constructed than grown. A barn leaned against the wind behind it. Two beehives slept under straw cloaks by the door. A garden plot, black and frozen now, lay in neat rows that spoke of a gardener who did not waste anything, not even winter.

The woman who opened the door was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders in the way of people who had spent their lives hauling things uphill, with iron-grey hair braided back from a face weathered into deep, permanent lines around the mouth and eyes. She held a long-handled ladle the way another woman might hold a spear.

She looked at Oscar first — took in the sword, the wolf-skin mantle, the scarred hands — and her expression said she had seen his kind before and buried some of them. Then she looked past him at Cyrus, slumped against the doorframe, lips grey, eyes sunk back in his skull, blood seeping fresh through the bandages where the wound had opened again on the climb.

She stepped aside.

"Well," she said. "Don't stand there freezing my doorway. In."

"I can pay—"

"You can carry him to the fire before he dies on my step and I have to spend all morning explaining the mess to the neighbors." She jerked the ladle toward the interior. "There are no neighbors. Move."

* * *

Her name was Sefa. She gave it without ceremony, the way you'd give directions, and asked for nothing in return — no name, no story, no explanation for why one man carried another man's death up her mountain in the dark. She put Cyrus by the hearth, cut away the ruined bandages with a small sharp knife, and made a sound low in her throat that was not quite disapproval and not quite pity.

"This went bad days ago." Her fingers probed the wound with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about her. "Fever's in it. He needs willowbark, honey, and sleep, in that order, and he needed them yesterday."

"He got them last night," Oscar said. "Mostly."

"Mostly." She snorted. She worked as she talked, unhurried, certain: boiling water, packing the wound with a paste that smelled of garlic and something sharper, winding clean linen with the economy of a woman who had done this so many times the motions lived in her hands instead of her head. Cyrus moaned once, deeply, like a man surfacing from underwater, and then went quiet in a way that frightened Oscar more than any scream.

Sefa noticed him watching.

"He'll live till morning," she said. "Whether he lives past it depends on things I can't mend with thread." She tied off the last knot and sat back on her heels, studying Oscar across the dying man between them. "You've been carrying him how far?"

"Since Greywater Ford."

"Greywater's three days down the south face." Something flickered in her eyes — grief, maybe, or its well-worn neighbor. "Three days, in this season, carrying a grown man. Through the ford country."

"The ford country isn't there anymore," Oscar said. "They burned it."

The ladle stilled in her hands. For a moment the only sound was the fire eating pine knots and the wind worrying the eaves.

"Burned it," she repeated.

"Riders. No colors. They burned the bridge, the mill, most of the houses. They were asking after a courier." He heard his own voice go flat, mercenary-flat, the voice he used for prices and distances. It was easier than the other voice, the one that wanted to say *I saw a granary burning with the grain still in it, I saw what the river looked like after.*

Sefa stood. She went to the window, pushed the shutter open a hand's width despite the cold, and looked out into the snowfall for a long time. When she closed it again her face had settled into something older and harder than hospitality.

"They came through Hollowpine asking questions too," she said. "Four days ago. Before the snow. Polite men. Well-shod horses. Wanted to know if anyone had passed north toward the high shelf." She turned. "I told them nobody passes here in winter but fools and corpses. They believed me, because I'm old and alone and widows are furniture to men like that." Her mouth twisted. "I let them believe it because I wanted them gone. Not because I care what you're carrying."

"Good," Oscar said. "Because I'm not telling you."

"I know." She picked up the ladle again. "Barley and mutton. Eat while it's hot. Then we'll talk about your gear, because your rope is rotten and your boots are lying to you about how much winter they can take, and I won't have corpses in my house twice in one month."

* * *

They ate at a table worn smooth by generations of elbows, under rafters hung with dried herbs and onions in braids and a fishing net patched so many times it was mostly knots. The stew was simple — barley, mutton fat, turnip, a handful of rosemary — but it landed in Oscar's empty stomach like a benediction, and he ate with the shameless speed of a man who had learned never to trust his next meal.

Cyrus woke enough to take broth. Sefa fed it to him spoonful by spoonful, holding his head up with one arm, murmuring to him in a low, tuneless way — not words, exactly, or not words meant to be understood. Just sound. The kind of sound mothers make. Cyrus's eyes found hers and then, drifting, found Oscar's, and something in them was apologetic.

"Sefa," he rasped.

"She knows your name because you said it in your sleep four times," Oscar said. "Along with some other things."

Cyrus closed his eyes. "Did I say anything about—"

"A library," Oscar said quietly. "A door that was supposed to be locked. And a child singing." He watched Cyrus flinch. "No. Nothing important."

Sefa looked between them and, with the tact of the truly formidable, said nothing at all. She refilled bowls that didn't need refilling.

Later, when Cyrus had gone under again into a sleep that at least resembled rest rather than collapse, Sefa took Oscar's gear without asking. She sat by the fire with his pack in her lap, working a bone needle through a torn strap, and spoke to him over the top of it.

"Your friend is going to die."

"Not tonight."

"No. Not tonight." The needle flashed. "But that wound has death living in it, the deep kind, the slow kind. I've dressed wounds like that before. My husband took a boar's tusk in the same place, thirty years ago. He lived eleven days." She said it the way you'd report the weather. "I know the sound a man makes when the fever takes his mind. Your friend started making it this afternoon, just before you reached the track. Did you hear it?"

Oscar didn't answer. He had heard it. He had been pretending he hadn't.

"So." She bit the thread. "You have a choice, and you're going to make it badly, so let me help you make it less badly. You can push north tomorrow with a dying man and whatever it is you're carrying that makes riders burn whole towns, and you'll probably get him to wherever you're going, and he'll probably die there, having seen nothing but the inside of your cloak for the last week of his life. Or—" the needle paused, "—you can stay here three days. Let me feed him. Let the fever break or don't. Let him walk the last of his road on his own feet, if he gets to walk it at all."

"And if the riders come here looking?"

"Then they'll find a widow and her lodgers." She shrugged, vast and unbothered. "I've been robbed before. They didn't kill me. Old women aren't worth the trouble, and I told you — to men like that, I'm furniture." Her eyes came up, and they were not furniture at all; they were the sharpest thing in the room. "Besides. If they do come, better they find you rested and armed than stumbling. You fight better than you lie, I'd wager. Everyone does."

Oscar stared at her. "Why? Why help us? You don't know us. You don't know what we've done or what following us might cost you. Any sensible woman would have barred that door."

"Probably." She considered this honestly, turning it over like a stone. "My husband used to say I wasn't sensible. He said it right up until the boar." The needle moved again. "You want a reason. Here's one. Forty years ago I came over these mountains with nothing, running from something, and a woman I never met left bread on her windowsill in Greywater Ford because that's what you did in those parts. Didn't ask. Didn't watch me eat it. Just left it out every morning until I stopped needing it." She tied the strap off and tossed the pack back to him. "Greywater's ash now. But the debt isn't. Debts don't burn."

Oscar held the pack and couldn't think of a single mercenary-appropriate thing to say.

* * *

He slept in the loft, on hay that smelled of summer, under a roof that didn't leak, and woke three times in the night convinced he heard hoofbeats. Each time it was only the wind, or the settling of the beams, or Sefa moving below with her lamp, checking on Cyrus. The third time he came down himself and found her sitting beside the sickbed with her mending in her lap, not sewing, just holding it, watching the fire.

"You should sleep," he said.

"When I'm dead." She didn't look around. "Sit down, Wolf."

He hadn't told her what they called him. His hand drifted toward his knife before his brain caught up, which told him something about how tired he was.

"Cyrus talks in his sleep," she said mildly. "Loudly, for a dying man. 'The Wolf,' he says. 'The Wolf will see it done.' So either you're the Wolf, or the Wolf is someone else entirely, and you stole his mantle along with his pack." Now she looked at him. "Which is it?"

Oscar sat down slowly on the far side of the hearth. The fire painted them both orange and shadow.

"It's a name for selling," he said finally. "Mercenaries wear names like badges. It tells employers what they're buying. The Wolf means I don't quit, I don't break contracts, and I don't ask questions." He flexed his scarred fingers. "It used to mean something else. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before the god I swore by stopped answering." He hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, into the warmth, into the dark above the fire, to a stranger — and that was its own strangeness, that he could tell this woman what he hadn't told Brannoc in ten years of business. "There's a god of oaths. Verrin, the old books call him. Every oath-sworn man carries a piece of him, a weight in the chest, warm when you keep faith and cold when you don't. Mine went silent ten years ago. No warmth. No cold. Nothing. Like praying into a well with no bottom." He laughed, short and ugly. "I kept swearing oaths anyway. Habit. Or spite. Hard to tell the difference anymore."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. She stirred the fire, and sparks went up the flue like tiny fleeing birds.

"My husband went quiet too, near the end," she said. "Not his words — those he had plenty of. His *answering*. I'd talk to him and I could see him deciding whether I was worth the effort. Eleven days of that." She set the poker down. "And then the last day, he looked straight at me, really looked, and said, 'You were the best of everything I ever chose.' One sentence. Ten years of silence wouldn't have been worth trading for it." She glanced at Cyrus, sleeping. "Maybe your god's not gone. Maybe he's saving up."

"That's not how gods work."

"How would you know? You've been talking to a well for ten years." She pulled her blanket around her shoulders. "Sleep, Wolf. Tomorrow I mend your argument as well as your boots. Tonight, sleep."

* * *

The argument lasted through breakfast.

"We go tomorrow," Oscar said. "First light. The shelf road's clear enough now, and every hour we sit here the net tightens."

"He can't ride a goat tomorrow, let alone the shelf."

"He doesn't have to ride. He has to *not die here*, and neither option guarantees that, but mine keeps the choice in our hands instead of theirs." He jabbed his spoon at the window, at the white silence beyond. "You didn't see the Ford, Sefa. You didn't see what they leave behind them when they're being thorough. This house is a candle. What we're carrying is the reason they'd cup their hands around the flame."

"Then put the candle out yourself, why don't you," Sefa said placidly, kneading dough, "and save them the trip."

"It's not—"

"A joke? No. I can see it's not." She punched the dough down with more force than the dough required. "Boy, I buried a husband and two sons in this valley. One to a tusk, one to a fever, one to the empire's last war, and I'll tell you what I learned standing over three graves: hiding from a thing has never once kept it from arriving. It only decides whether you meet it hungry or fed, rested or ragged, alone or—" she gestured at the loft, at Cyrus stirring weakly at the smell of bread, "—in company. Three days. Feed him. Mend him. Let him stand at the end, whatever the end is. That's not sentiment. That's tactics."

"It's three days I don't have."

"It's three days *he* doesn't have, and you know it, and you hate knowing it, and that's why you're shouting at a widow over porridge." She slid the loaf into the oven. "Shout all you like. Bread's ready at noon."

Oscar glared at her. Sefa hummed to herself and ignored him with the serene invulnerability of a boulder ignoring rain, and somewhere behind him Cyrus made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough, and Oscar decided not to check which.

* * *

The three days, in the event, became almost — he hated the word — almost easy.

Sefa's house ran on rhythms older than any empire: water hauled at dawn, the goats milked, herbs stripped and hung, the endless patient mending of a hundred small decays. She put them to work without discussion. Oscar split wood until his shoulders screamed and found, to his private fury, that the screaming felt good — honest pain, pain with a clear cause and cure, unlike the other kind he carried. Cyrus, propped in a chair by the window wrapped in three blankets, shelled beans and argued astronomy with Sefa and grew visibly, measurably stronger, color coming back into his face like dawn coming back into the sky.

On the second evening Cyrus managed the whole distance to the table unaided and ate a full bowl, and then a second, and then sat back with the deep contentment of a convalescent and said, "I dreamed about the sea."

"You're a thousand miles from the sea," Oscar said.

"I know. That's why I dreamed about it." He smiled, and for a moment he looked young, younger than Oscar had yet allowed him to be. "Your widow is remarkable, you know. She hasn't asked us a single question."

"I've noticed."

"Do you suppose she knows everything already?"

"I suppose," Oscar said, watching Sefa's broad back bent over her mending, "that she decided it didn't matter. Which might be the same thing."

On the third morning, Cyrus walked out to the beehives with Sefa and came back flushed and breathing hard but walking, actually walking, and Oscar began — reluctantly, resentfully, completely — to revise his arithmetic. Three days had bought more than strength. It had bought something he didn't have a price for yet.

It was Tam who ended the arithmetic.

Oscar heard the dog first — Sefa kept a lean yellow bitch of no particular breed who had spent three days ignoring everyone — erupt into frantic barking at the tree line, and he had his sword out and Cyrus behind the woodpile before his mind finished the thought. A figure came floundering up the goat-track through the knee-deep snow: small, ragged, staggering, wearing a burnt coat and one boot, a boy of perhaps twelve with a face grey with exhaustion and tracked by frozen tear-lines, carrying nothing but a hatchet with a charred handle.

Sefa reached him first. The boy collapsed against her, and over his head she looked at Oscar, and her eyes had gone very bright and very hard.

"He's from Greywater," she said. "Aren't you, love. You're from the Ford."

The boy lifted his head. His eyes found Oscar — found the wolf-mantle, the sword, the stranger his grandmother's stories had apparently described — and he said, in a voice scraped raw:

"They killed my mother. The ones without colors. Somebody said a man called the Wolf carried the courier north. I want to come with you. I want to learn how to kill them."

The wind moved through the pines, and somewhere far to the south, too far to see, too close to ignore, a thread of smoke rose against the pale sky.

Not one thread. Three.

Oscar closed his eyes. "*Tam*," Cyrus said softly behind him, reading the boy's name off his face like a page, "come inside. There's bread."
