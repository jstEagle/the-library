## 7. Blood-Oath Bleeding

I dream in a language I no longer speak.

It comes to me in the fever-hours, between the shivering and the burning: a tongue of high vaults and cold marble, the archive-tongue, the language they made us learn so that knowledge could be locked away from ordinary men. In the dream I am young again and my hands do not shake and there is ink under my fingernails instead of blood, and someone is calling my name down a corridor of books — my true name, the one I buried with everything else on the night I ran — and I wake with tears frozen in the creases of my eyes because the mountains do not forgive weeping; the cold takes it and turns it to salt-scars on your face.

We are camped in the hollow beneath an overhang of grey stone, three days above Greywater Ford now, three days since the Wolf pulled me out of my own blood on the switchbacks and decided, against every law of his profession, that I was worth carrying. The fire is small. Oscar builds his fires the way he does everything else — grudgingly, precisely, as if warmth were a debt he intended to repay. He sits across from it sharpening a knife that was already sharp, and every few minutes he looks at me, and when he looks at me I can see it happening behind his eyes.

He is seeing things that are not his.

* * *

It begins, I think, on the second night after the Ford, though neither of us has words for it yet. The blood-oath is old magic — older than the empire's neat categories, older than the archive's careful shelves — and old magic does not ask permission. It is a door cut between two lives, and once it is cut, what flows through flows both ways. I knew this when I pressed his hand to the wound in my side. I told myself I had no choice. Both of those things were true, and neither of them excuses me.

Here is what I did not tell him: that the oath would not simply bind him to my task. It would make him *me*, piece by piece, whether either of us willed it or not.

The first thing he took was the library. I know because I felt it leave me — a great warm weight sliding out of my chest like water through a cracked jar — and I woke in the black before dawn to find him sitting bolt upright by the dead fire, staring at his own hands as though they had grown a second set of fingers.

"You were talking," he said. His voice was rough, wrong. "In your sleep. You were — reciting something. Catalogue numbers." He swallowed. "There was a hall. Marble. Ten thousand volumes and a reading gallery with a dome painted like a sky, and I have *never been there*, Cyrus, and I could draw it for you right now if you put charcoal in my hand."

I should have lied. A good thief lies even to himself; it keeps the blade edges clean. But the fever had burned all my cleverness down to the quick, and what was left was only the truth, tired and bare:

"It's called the Vellum Hall," I said. "Third tier of the Imperial Archive. I shelved there for eleven years."

He looked at me for a long moment. Outside the overhang the wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages turning, ten thousand pages, ten thousand years, and somewhere in that sound I heard the oath settling deeper into him, roots going down through soil that did not want them.

"That wasn't in any contract I signed," Oscar said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "It wasn't."

* * *

By day it is easier to pretend nothing is happening. He walks. I ride the mule he will not name but clearly loves more than any living creature, and I catalog what the fever leaves me and what it doesn't. My legs still answer, mostly. My mind flickers like a lamp low on oil — moments of perfect clarity followed by long grey stretches where the world arrives smeared, sounds arriving late, colors slightly misfiled. And always, underneath, the slow hemorrhage: memories draining out of me along the thread of the oath and into the man walking beside me, who never asked to carry them and cannot set them down.

I feel each one go. That is the cruelty of it. Not the giving — thieves give up their secrets all the time, that is just failure, just the trade working as designed. No: the cruelty is that I *feel* them leave, the way you feel a tooth loosen, the way you feel a room go colder when a door opens somewhere in the house. Each memory crosses over with a little shudder, a small death that isn't death, and then it is his. I can tell when he has received one because his stride changes. He walks like a man whose pack has suddenly gained weight he cannot locate.

On the third day it was the burning library.

I remember the night it happened — the real one, not the dream. The Emperor's ninth year. The Purification Edicts, they called them, when the archives were ordered cleansed of everything that touched the dragon wars: flight-maps, heart-lore, the breeding records, the names. They burned four hundred years of remembering in the courtyard of the Vellum Hall, and we archivists stood in ranks and watched the ash come down like filthy snow, and I held a child's hand in mine — my own hand, I mean; I was the child, twelve years old, brought to witness as all archive-children were brought, so that we would learn what happened to those who kept forbidden things —

— and the smell. Gods, the smell of it. Vellum burns differently than paper. Sweeter. Like meat.

That memory went into Oscar somewhere on the scree below the second col, and I knew it the instant it landed, because the Wolf stopped mid-stride, swayed, and put his hand against the rock face as if the mountain had tilted under him.

"Smoke," he said. "Why do I smell smoke? There's no fire. There's no—" He turned and looked at me, and his face had gone the color of the ash in my memory, which he was now wearing from the inside. "A courtyard. Ash falling. Someone is holding my hand and telling me this is mercy, Cyrus, someone is holding *my* hand—"

"Yours," I said, "is the one doing the holding."

He stared at me. The wind came off the col and flattened the grass and carried away whatever he might have said next, and when it passed he simply stood there breathing, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the way soldiers breathe when they are deciding not to be sick.

"You were a child," he said finally. "They made a child watch."

"They made everyone watch. That was rather the point."

"And now I've got it in my head." He tapped his own temple with two fingers, hard, as though trying to knock the memory loose like water from an ear. "I've got your whole life leaking into mine and there's no — what, no valve? No way to close it?"

"The oath doesn't have valves."

"What does it have?"

"Gravity," I said. "Everything rolls downhill. You're downhill, Wolf. I'm dying, and a dying man's life weighs more than a living man's silence, and all of it is coming to you whether you kneel to receive it or not."

Something moved across his face then — not anger, though anger lived in the neighborhood. Something lonelier. He looked at the pass ahead of us, the white notch in the black rock, and then back at me, and I watched a man calculate the cost of a thing he had already paid for.

"Then give me the useful parts first," he said. "If I'm going to be haunted, haunt me with the map routes. Haunt me with whatever the riders without colors are actually chasing. Save the burning libraries for the end."

"I don't choose the order."

"Who does?"

"The blood," I said. "And the blood chooses what bleeds fastest."

* * *

The child's voice came to him that night.

I want to write this plainly, because everything else about the oath is tangled and strange, but this part was plain: there was a voice in the archive, a girl's voice, and she sang while she worked. Third tier, evening shift, dusting the map-cases. She was younger than me by two years and she sang songs her grandmother had taught her, songs in a dialect none of the masters recognized, and I loved her the way twelve-year-olds love: entirely, silently, from a distance of four shelves.

Her name was Wren. When the Purification came they did not burn the archivists — we were too useful, too well-trained, too expensive to replace — but Wren's grandmother had been a keeper of the old flight-songs, and someone informed, and informers were rewarded, and Wren and her grandmother left the city in a wagon one morning and no wagon ever arrived anywhere. The records say *relocated*. The records say many things. I was fourteen when I understood that the archive's greatest collection was its omissions, and I began, that day, to steal.

Not gold. Never gold. I stole *remembering*. A page here. A folio there. Songs copied out in my own hand and hidden inside treatises so dull no censor would ever open them. For twenty years I built a library inside the library, a remembering inside the forgetting, and every page of it I hid in the place no imperial searcher would ever look, because it did not exist in any catalogue, because it existed in the one archive the empire could never raid —

Me.

All of that poured into Oscar Joubert beside a small fire under a grey overhang, and he woke with Wren's song in his throat.

I heard him before I saw him. It was deep night, the coals banked to red eyes, and the Wolf was sitting with his forearms on his knees and he was humming — no, singing, badly, a melody in a dialect he had no right to know, and his voice kept breaking on the turns of it because the tune belonged to a dead girl and his mouth had never learned where the notes lived. He sang four lines before he caught himself and stopped as though he'd been struck.

"Cyrus." His whisper was hoarse. "Cyrus, wake up. There's a girl in my head."

"She's not in your head." I pushed myself up on one elbow, and the motion cost me more than I let him see. "She's in mine. Or she was."

"Who is she?"

"Wren."

"Whose voice — why do I know how the song goes? Why can I smell lamp-oil and dust and — there's a map-case, brass fittings, third shelf, and she's standing on a stool because she's too short, and I'm watching her from behind the atlases and I *hate* myself for watching because I never once spoke to her, not one time in two years, and then they took her and I never—" His breath went ragged. He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "This isn't memory. This is *grief*. This is your grief and it's sitting in my chest like a stone I swallowed and I don't — I never consented to carry your ghosts, old man. Do you hear me? I never asked for any of it."

"I know."

"You *knew* this would happen." He dropped his hands and looked at me across the coals, and his eyes were wet, which I suspect he would deny under torture. "You knew, and you swore me anyway. On the switchbacks. Bleeding out all over the rocks, you put my hand on your wound and you bound me to a task and a tide, and you didn't tell me the tide ran both directions."

"If I had told you," I said, "would you have sworn?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere upslope a stone cracked with the cold, a small sharp report like a distant door closing.

"Yes," Oscar said at last, and hated saying it; I could hear the hatred, thin as wire. "God help me, yes. Because whatever this is—" he gestured at his own chest, at the ghost-song lodged there, at twenty years of my stolen remembering pooled in him like rainwater in a bootprint, "—it answered, Cyrus. When you made me swear, something *answered*. First time in ten years anything has answered me. Do you understand what that's worth to a man like me? I'd have sworn it bleeding, I'd have sworn it blind. So yes, curse it, you chose well, and I hate you for choosing well, and I'll see it done. But you will lie there and you will tell me the rest. All of it. What am I going to wake up carrying tomorrow? Whose voice next? What else died in that library?"

* * *

So I told him. What remained of the night, and much of the next day's walking, I spent pouring out deliberately what the oath was already stealing furtively — better to hand a man the weight than let it fall on him unannounced. I gave him the archive in order: the dome painted with the true sky as it stands in midsummer, so that scholars could navigate by their own ceiling. The Master of Seals with his ink-stained cuffs and his kindness like a locked drawer. The night of the Edict read aloud, the Archivist Primus weeping without sound as the list of proscribed subjects unrolled to the floor and kept unrolling. The first theft. The tenth. The hundredth. The hiding-places that were not places: numbers memorized and burned, maps learned and destroyed, a whole country of knowledge carried in the soft tissue of one unremarkable man's mind.

And I gave him the reason, because he had earned the reason by bleeding for it, or rather by letting my blood into him, which amounts to the same economy.

"They think I'm a courier," I said. We had stopped to rest the mule in a fold of the hillside, and I sat against a boulder with my bandaged side screaming its slow red sermon, and the Wolf crouched across from me with his knife drawn and a strip of whetstone, not working the blade, just holding it, the way other men hold a talisman. "The smugglers who hired you. They think there's a document. A map worth coin. And there is a map, Wolf, and it is worth more coin than Brannoc — or whoever wears Brannoc's face these days — has dreamed of in his whole greedy life. But the map is the least of what I carry. The map is the *address*. What lives at the address—"

I stopped. Not for drama. Because the fever chose that moment to take me by the throat, and the world went white at the edges, and when it cleared Oscar had crossed the space between us and was holding a waterskin to my lips with a hand that shook very slightly, and his eyes were full of things that were half-his and half-mine and therefore belonged properly to no one.

"What lives at the address?" he repeated. Patiently. Terribly patiently, the patience of a man standing in rising water.

"A heart," I said.

His face did not change. Mercenary faces are built not to change; it is the first thing the trade teaches and the last thing it surrenders. But behind his eyes I watched my own memory arrive — the one I had guarded most jealously, the vault beneath Thornfell, the sleeping vastness, the slow amber pulse of the oldest living thing in the world — and I watched him recognize it, because he had already begun dreaming it, because the blood does not wait for permission and the deepest memories bleed first.

"The last dragon," Oscar said. Flatly. Testing the words the way you test river ice.

"The last dragon," I said. "Still beating. Still burning. Kept alive these hundred years by a spark no bigger than a coal — my spark, now, the thing I stole, the thing I am dying to deliver. And the empire has found the mountain, Wolf. The riders without colors, the burned Ford, the sealed passes — they're not hunting a courier. They're hunting the way in. And every hour that I grow weaker, everything I know drains into you faster, and you are going to stand someday very soon at the mouth of a vault with the whole weight of my life in your chest, and you are going to have to decide what a man with your history does with a treasure empires burn cities to reach."

Oscar Joubert looked down at his hands — the same hands that had taken my oath on the switchbacks, that had held a waterskin to a stranger's lips, that had sung a dead girl's song badly in the dark — and when he spoke again his voice was very quiet, and very steady, and it frightened me more than any shouting could have.

"When I was a boy," he said, "I swore my oaths to a god who listened. Then he stopped. Ten years of silence, and I learned that vows are just noise we make so the dark sounds less empty." He sheathed the knife. "But this — this thing in my chest, this bleeding of yours — it doesn't feel like noise. It feels like being *spoken into*." His jaw tightened. "So here is my vow, Cyrus, and I make it to no god at all: I will get your spark to its address if I have to carry you over the snow-line on my back and bury every rider the empire sends. Whatever you are, whoever you were — the library, the girl, the theft, all of it — it comes with me now. You've seen to that."

He rose, and shouldered my pack along with his own, and walked on toward the white notch in the mountains, and I rode behind him on the mule with the fever humming in my ears like a struck bell.

And it was only later — much later, in the grey hour before dawn, watching his broad back rise and fall with my stolen breaths — that I understood the thing I should have known from the first drop of blood:

The oath was not finished giving.

What slept at the address had begun, faint and far beneath the stone, to listen — and through the thread that joined us, through the Wolf's opened chest, something ancient had felt us coming, and turned over in its hundred-year sleep, and *answered*.
