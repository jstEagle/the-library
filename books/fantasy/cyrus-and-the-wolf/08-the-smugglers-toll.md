## 8. The Smugglers' Toll

The Kestrel Gate announced itself long before they saw it, the way all the guild's holdings did: by smell.

Woodsmoke first, then tallow, then the particular sour reek of the rendering vats behind the tannery row, where Brannoc's people boiled down everything a carcass could give and sold the rest to the empire at a markup. Oscar had smelled it a hundred times across fifteen years. It had always smelled like safety. Tonight, riding down the last mile of the old drover's road with a dying man lashed to a mule and a map that burned through three layers of oilcloth like a coal wrapped in wool, it smelled like something he couldn't name yet. Something with teeth.

Cyrus had not spoken since dawn.

That was the fact Oscar kept returning to, turning it over like a gambler with a bad hand. The fever had taken the man's voice somewhere in the night, left him muttering in a language Oscar half-recognized from the blood-oath's leavings — fragments of it surfaced now when he blinked, a vault door, a child counting stars, a library going up in sheets of white flame. He'd stopped fighting the memories. There wasn't time, and there wasn't room in him for the luxury of refusal. He simply let them wash through and tried to keep his hands steady on the reins.

The mule carrying Cyrus stumbled on the scree. Oscar dismounted, took the lead-rope in his fist, and walked.

*You're a fool, Joubert,* he told himself, in the voice his old sergeant had used for exactly this purpose. *You're walking your cargo straight into the guildhall with a noose already tied, and you're calling it delivery.*

But where else? The message had come through the usual channels — a dead-drop in Greywater Ford, a purse of imperial mint, no name. *A package over Thornfell. Kestrel Gate. Payment on arrival. No questions.* Standard work. And Brannoc's people ran the Kestrel Gate, which had always meant the job was guild work even if the client was faceless, and guild work was the closest thing Oscar had left to a religion. His god had stopped answering ten years ago. The guild's ledgers, at least, still balanced.

He was beginning to hate how much that had meant to him.

The gate itself was a wound in the mountainside — two towers of black granite joined by an arch of ironwood, the guild's kestrel sigil worked into the iron in hammered plates gone green with weather. Torches burned in the arrow slits. Below the arch, the road funneled between walls of stacked stone, and Oscar counted the men on the parapet without meaning to. Six. Seven. One more than usual. One of them had a crossbow that wasn't guild issue.

His stomach tightened. He made himself ride under the arch anyway.

* * *

Brannoc received him in the long room above the gatehouse, the one with the map of the Thornfell trade routes inked on the ceiling so that every man who sat beneath it was reminded, daily, of how small his own knowledge was. The guild chief was a mountain himself, wide and weathered and graying at the beard, with hands like root-balls and a voice that came out of his chest the way rockfall comes out of a cliff face. Fifteen years Oscar had known him. Fifteen years of contracts honored to the letter and never past it. Fifteen years of the only loyalty Oscar had trusted, because it was paid for.

"Joubert." Brannoc poured two cups of plum brandy without asking. "You look like a man who's been dragged behind his own horse."

"Feels about right." Oscar took the cup and didn't drink. The brandy smelled of the good cask, the one Brannoc saved for men he wanted something from. "Cargo's below. One mule down, one courier down. He's fevered. Needs a surgeon."

"He'll get one." Brannoc sat, which he rarely did, which meant the conversation had weight. "The courier. Describe him."

"You hired him. Or you hired me to move him. Either way you know his face better than I do."

"I hired you to move *a package*." Brannoc's eyes were pale river-stone, and they did not blink enough. "A sealed case. That was the contract. So tell me, Wolf, why my man at Greywater Ford says the package opened itself. Says you were seen on the switchbacks carrying a bleeding man with no seal, no case, and no guild marks. Says the riders who came through behind him burned the ford to the waterline asking after him."

Oscar kept his face still. It was a skill he'd paid for in blood over the years, and he spent it now like a miser. "The case was the man. Contract said no questions."

"The contract said a *sealed case*."

"The contract said what you paid it to say."

For a moment the only sound was the wind working at the arrow slits and, far below, the mule lines clinking. Brannoc drank. Oscar watched the guild chief's throat move and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man stepping onto ice and feeling it speak under his boot, that the moment had already happened — the moment where an old patron became something else — and he'd walked straight past it without noticing.

"You were the client," Oscar said.

Brannoc set down his cup. "I was the client."

"You don't pay in imperial mint."

"I don't *sign* in imperial mint." The guild chief smiled, and it was the smile of a man showing you the trap after you'd already stepped in it, because he respected you enough to want you to see the workmanship. "There's a buyer, Joubert. A serious buyer. They want what the courier carries, intact, and they want it quiet, and they're paying enough to retire every name on the guild's debt rolls twice over. All you had to do was walk it through the pass. Instead you took a wounded stranger off the road and carried him like a— what? A foundling? You, of all men."

"He was dying."

"People die. It's the one trade that never has a bad year." Brannoc leaned forward, root-ball hands flat on the table. "Give me the map, Wolf. Whatever he gave you. Whatever he made you swear. The buyer doesn't care about your oaths, and neither do I. You'll be paid triple for the delay. You'll be paid like a legend."

And there it was. The thing Oscar had been carrying since the ford, since the blood-oath, since the moment on the switchbacks when something had *answered* him for the first time in ten years — it had a name now, and the name was Brannoc's purse, and it was being held out to him like a hand across a card table.

He should give it up. That was the arithmetic. The guild had fed him through the silent years, through the decade when his god went quiet and every oath he'd ever sworn felt like shouting down a well. Brannoc had never once shorted him. In a world of emperors and burning fords and riders without colors, the guild's cold, paid-for honesty was the only honesty he'd ever been able to afford.

And Cyrus was burning up on a pallet below, and the map was warm against Oscar's ribs, and something in his chest — something that had answered once and might answer again — was very quiet, the way a held breath is quiet.

"The courier's my cargo," Oscar said. "Until delivery. That's the contract."

"The courier was never the cargo."

"The contract's ambiguous, then. My reading stands."

Brannoc laughed. It was a real laugh, which was somehow worse. "Gods, you're a bad liar, Joubert. You always were. You've got it on you right now. I can see you guarding it — the way you sit, the way you keep your left arm tucked. Fifteen years, and you think I can't read you like a toll ledger?" He stood, crossed to the window, looked out at the darkening pass. "I don't want to take it from you. I want you to understand that. I'd rather buy it. But my buyer has a schedule, and the riders burning fords behind you are *also* my problem now, and I solve problems in the order they threaten me. So. Tonight, Joubert. Give it to me tonight, or tomorrow I stop being your patron and start being your toll."

Oscar rose. He made himself do it slowly, made his legs remember they were his. "I need to see to my cargo."

"See to him." Brannoc didn't turn from the window. "Surgeon's in the east room. And Wolf — the gate bars at midnight. Everything inside them stays inside them."

* * *

The east room smelled of lye and old blood, the way all surgeons' rooms did, and Cyrus lay on the pallet looking like a man already halfway translated into memory. The guild surgeon, a stooped woman named Hesk with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a corvid's, was cutting away his bandages and clicking her tongue at what lay beneath.

"Fever's in the wound," she said, without looking up. "It's in the blood now. I can slow it. I can't stop it. Who stitched this — a drunk?"

"Me."

"Hm. It shows." She glanced up, corvid-bright. "You want the truth or the comfort?"

"The truth."

"He's got a day. Two if the fever breaks, which it won't. Whatever he's carrying, it's carrying him now too." She went back to her cutting. "There's a girl downstairs who'll sit with him if you pay. Dying men do better with someone talking to them. Keeps them from leaving early."

Oscar stood in the doorway a long moment, watching Cyrus's chest rise and fall in shallow, ragged pulls, and felt the blood-oath shift in him like a sleeper turning over. A fragment surfaced — unasked, unwanted: *a corridor of white stone, and a voice saying* **you were the only one I could trust** *— and he pushed it down, and it went, but slower than before, like something learning the shape of his resistance.*

"Nobody sits with him but me," Oscar said.

Hesk shrugged. "Your coin."

He took the chair by the pallet and sat, and for a while the room was only lamplight and the wet sound of Hesk's work and the wind worrying at the shutters. Then Cyrus's eyes opened — not all the way, a sliver of gray under the fever-glaze, but open, and finding Oscar's face.

"Gate," Cyrus rasped. "Whose gate."

"Guild. Brannoc's."

Something moved through the dying man's face — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has finally seen the shape of the country he's been lost in. "The Kestrel," Cyrus whispered. "Then it's true. It was never the emperor hunting me first."

Oscar leaned in. "Say that plain."

Cyrus's hand came up — trembling, burning-hot — and closed on Oscar's wrist with a strength that had no business being in it. "The map doesn't show a place, Wolf. It shows a *price*. Everyone who wants it wants to spend it differently. But they all want it." His eyes drifted, focused on the middle distance, on something Oscar couldn't see. "Your patron. Ask him who the buyer is. Ask him what a dragon's heart buys."

"Sleep," Oscar said. "You're raving."

"I'm *accounting*," Cyrus said, and almost smiled, and then the fever took him back down, and his hand slid from Oscar's wrist, and the room was only lamplight again.

Oscar sat in the dark after Hesk left, and did the only arithmetic that had never once balanced in his favor: what he owed against what he knew. Fifteen years of Brannoc's honest ledgers on one side. A burned ford on the other. A buyer with a schedule. Riders without colors. And beneath it all, warm against his ribs, the map — patient as a coal, patient as the thing that had answered him on the mountain, waiting to see what kind of man its keeper turned out to be.

Midnight, Brannoc had said. The gate bars at midnight.

Oscar looked at the shuttered window, at the stable yard below where his mules stood among a hundred others, at the mountains beyond the walls already going white under the moon. There was a route out of the Kestrel Gate that no one used. A route no one survived. He'd told Cyrus about it once, drunk, as a joke.

He stood, and began, quietly, to load his gear.

Below, in the yard, a bell began to ring the midnight count — and stopped, mid-swing, as if a hand had been laid on it.
