## 9. Imperial Banners in the Pass

Cyrus woke to the smell of woodsmoke and wet ash, and for a long moment he could not remember which life he was in.

There had been so many lives. The boy in the archive city of Vael Amaranth, ink-stained to the elbow before he was twelve. The apprentice who learned to walk the Emperor's stacks without letting his shadow fall on the older shelves, because the older shelves remembered being looked at wrongly and reported it. The courier who ran south with a stolen map pressed against his ribs like a second heart. And now — this: a bedroll that smelled of horse and pine pitch, a fire burning low in a hollow of stones, and a mercenary called the Wolf sitting at its edge sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

The Wolf. Oscar Joubert. A name Cyrus had known from guild gossip years ago, back when names were things other people carried. *The godless wolf of Greywater Ford,* someone had said once, laughing over cheap wine in a dockside tavern. *Hired three times by men who prayed, because his contracts always held.* Cyrus had not understood then what it meant to be a man whose god went silent. He was beginning to understand now, watching Oscar work the whetstone in small, patient circles, as if repetition itself were a kind of prayer and the knife were listening instead of heaven.

"You're staring," Oscar said, without looking up.

"I'm counting," Cyrus said. His voice came out sanded thin. "You have four knives. You're sharpening the one you keep behind your hip."

"Old habit." The whetstone paused. "It's the one I reach for when I'm thinking about running."

"And are you? Thinking about it?"

Oscar looked at him then. In the firelight his eyes were the color of river ice over something dark and moving. "Every hour," he said. "I keep deciding against it. Don't ask me why. It annoys me."

Cyrus laughed, and the laugh tore into coughing, and the coughing folded him nearly double until Oscar's hand was flat against his back, steadying him through the convulsion. When it passed, Cyrus tasted iron. He spat into the fire and watched the flames hiss green where the blood struck them.

"That's new," Oscar said quietly.

"It's not." Cyrus leaned back against the pack they'd wedged behind him. "It's been new for six days. I've just stopped pretending otherwise."

Above them the sky was going grey toward dawn, and the pines stood black against it, and somewhere below — far below, down past the switchbacks and the frozen ford and the smoking ruin of Greywater — the southern pass lay between two walls of stone like a throat waiting to be closed.

* * *

They had seen the banners the evening before.

Cyrus had been half-dreaming, sunk in the fever-country between waking and sleep, when Oscar's hand closed over his mouth — not hard, but absolute, the way a man quiets a frightened animal. *Don't move. Don't breathe loud.* Through the screen of juniper branches Cyrus had looked down into the pass and seen them.

Imperial outriders. Not many — thirty, perhaps forty — but arranged with a precision that said *many more behind*. They had sealed the pass at its narrowest point, the place the locals called the Kettle Spout, where the road pinched between cliffs close enough that a thrown rope could touch both walls. Their pavilions were white and grey, unmarked, but the horses were imperial remounts — tall-boned, deep-chested, branded on the left haunch with the triple-crown cipher that Cyrus had spent ten years learning to read and five years learning to fear. Banners hung furled on their lances. That was the detail that made his stomach drop through the mountain. An imperial patrol flew its colors to announce itself to friend and foe alike. Furled banners meant this force did not wish to be announced. It wished only to be *complete*.

They had built no cookfires. They had posted no pickets that Cyrus could see, which meant their pickets were posted where he could not see them, which was worse. And at the center of the camp, under an awning of grey canvas, a cartographer's table stood open to the cold air, and men bent over it with dividers and lamps, and even at this distance — even across a mile of freezing dark — Cyrus knew exactly what kind of map lay under their hands.

Not a map of roads. He'd drawn enough of those himself, in another life, in a tower room where the light came through alabaster and everything was beautiful and nothing was free. The men around that table would be looking at a map of *strata*. Of seams and vents and old, old heat. The empire had not come hunting a thief.

The empire had come hunting a heartbeat.

"They know," Cyrus had whispered, after the last light failed and the camp became a scatter of shuttered lanterns below. "Gods forgive me, Oscar. They already know."

* * *

Now, in the grey morning, Oscar fed the fire a knot of dry larch and said, "Tell me from the beginning. All of it. And don't arrange it to make yourself look better — I'll know, because I'm very good at arranging things to make myself look better, and I recognize the stitching."

So Cyrus told him.

He began where all the empire's sins begin, which is to say in a beautiful room. "There's a library in the Winter Palace called the Vault of Breath," he said. "That's not poetry; that's the official name. It holds everything the imperial house ever learned about dragons — every skull measurement, every scale-lore, every account of the hunts. It's warded so heavily that most archivists go their whole careers without seeing inside. I got in because I was good at wardwork and bad at ambition. Ambitious men get promoted away from the Vault. Contented ones get trusted with keys."

"And you weren't contented."

"I was *curious*." Cyrus smiled, and felt how thin the smile was. "There's a difference, though the tribunal didn't think so. I catalogued the collection, Oscar. Ten years. Do you know what it means to spend ten years reading how a living thing was taken apart? Every entry ends the same way. *Specimen destroyed. Specimen rendered. Specimen extinguished.* There's a word they used in the later records — *rendered*, like fat, like tallow — and after a while you stop flinching at it, and that's the moment you should run, and I didn't run. I kept reading."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them a bird started up its idiot morning song, indifferent to empires.

"The records end sixty years ago with the last hunt," Cyrus went on. "Officially, the dragons are gone. Extinct. A finished chapter. But archives have margins, and margins have whispers. There was an entry — a single entry, misfiled, in a clerk's hand eighty years dead — that referred to a *reserve specimen*. Not captured. Not killed. *Reserved.* And beneath it, in the same cramped script: *the heart was too great to render; the Emperor ordered it kept.*"

Oscar's whetstone had stopped moving. Cyrus hadn't noticed when.

"I spent two years finding what that meant," Cyrus said. "Two years of pulling threads in the dark, careful as a surgeon, because the moment anyone senior noticed my interest, I'd vanish into some provincial posting and never be heard from again — or worse, I'd be heard from again, reassigned to the Vault permanently, as its keeper, which is the empire's way of burying a dangerous man alive among books he can never speak of. And what I found—" He stopped. Swallowed. His hands had begun to shake, and he hated them for it, and pressed them flat against his knees.

"What I found was a ledger of fuel requisitions. Charcoal, saltpeter, crucible-clay, quantities no foundry could justify. Shipments routed to Thornfell for sixty years, under seals that predated the current dynasty's coinage. Something under that mountain has been *fed*, Oscar. Kept alive the way you'd bank a furnace — barely, grudgingly, a trickle of fuel to keep the coals from going out. Because the empire doesn't want a dragon. Empires don't want monsters. Empires want *engines*. And there is no engine in this world like the heart of the last dragon."

* * *

Oscar said nothing for a long time. He was very good at silence; Cyrus had learned that about him on the switchbacks above Greywater Ford, when the man had knelt in the snow beside a dying stranger and asked exactly one question — *"Can you walk?"* — before hoisting him onto his shoulders like a sack of grain. A talkative rescuer would have asked more questions. A merciful one might have asked fewer. Oscar asked the question whose answer determined whether mercy was possible, and then acted, and saved his words for later, when words were the weapon required.

"Why steal the map?" he said at last. "Why not just burn the records? Warn someone?"

"Warn whom?" Cyrus spread his hands. "The provinces? Half the provincial governors would sell their mothers for a share of whatever the vault yields. The temples? The temples blessed the original hunts. The guilds?" He nodded at the road behind them, toward the Kestrel Gate and Brannoc and the contract written in a smuggler's neat, greedy hand. "You tell me. You've worked for them longer than I have."

"So you stole a map."

"I stole *the* map. The master survey. Drawn by the Emperor's own geomancers, inked in dragon-fire — actual fire, Oscar, blood-warm, self-renewing, impossible to copy because the ink dies when it leaves the page. One copy exists because one copy can exist. It shows every approach to the vault beneath Thornfell, every seam, every door. And it shows the thing the empire has spent sixty years pretending it lost track of." He met the Wolf's ice-colored eyes. "It shows where the heart still beats."

"And you thought carrying that out the front gate of the Winter Palace would go unnoticed."

"No," Cyrus said. "I thought it would go unnoticed for nine days. It went unnoticed for seven." He managed something that was almost a grin. "I'm prouder of those seven days than of anything else I've done, including the theft itself. Two of those days I spent simply walking. Do you know what it's like to walk through a city that wants to kill you, slowly, in daylight, smiling at the guards? Every step is a wager. I drank wine in a tavern with the officer whose duty it was to arrest me. We talked about fishing."

"You're either the bravest man I've ever carried," Oscar said, "or the stupidest. I genuinely can't tell, and it's starting to bother me."

"They're often the same thing. Ask any martyr." Then the humor drained out of Cyrus, because what came next deserved no humor, and he made himself say it plainly, the way he'd rehearsed saying it in the dark hours while the Wolf slept and the fever walked him through his own memories like a creditor through a debtor's house.

"There's more. You deserve the whole shape of it, because you're going to die for this if we fail, and a man should know the size of the grave he's digging. The outriders in the pass aren't hunting a courier. Couriers get a blade and a quiet ditch. They've sealed the pass because they believe the map is still *at large* — and if they can't recover it, their orders are to hold Thornfell until the empire can move something larger into position. Something with siege-foundries attached. I saw the requisition drafts before I ran, Oscar. The plan isn't to crack the vault open and take a trophy. The plan is to tap it. To bleed the heart into reservoirs and feed it to the new weapons — the fire-lances, the burning-sieges. Enough heat under Thornfell to arm a legion of engines that never cool, never starve, never run out of war." His voice cracked, and he let it. "If they succeed, there won't be a border in the world they can't redraw. This stops being about a map the moment they seal that pass. It becomes about everyone."

The wind moved through the pines above them, carrying the smell of snow from the high shelf. Down in the valley, faint as memory, the bird was still singing.

Oscar stared into the fire. When he finally spoke, his voice was flat in the way Cyrus had learned meant the opposite of calm — flat the way ice is flat, all the violence pressed underneath. "Ten years," he said. "For ten years I've told myself the gods are stories and oaths are paper and every man looks out for his own hide, because looking out for your own hide is the only prayer that gets answered. And along comes a bleeding scholar with a warm piece of parchment who tells me the empire is about to build a furnace out of the last living miracle in the world, and expects me to care." He looked up. "I do care. That's what bothers me. I was doing fine, Cyrus. I had a system."

"I'm sorry," Cyrus said, and meant it more than he had meant anything in years. "I'm sorry I made you care. It wasn't fair. I chose you off a list because you knew the passes and your contracts held, and I told myself the rest of it — your silence, your cynicism — wouldn't matter. But here's what I learned in the archive, Wolf, the one true thing in ten years of cataloguing horrors: the empire counts on men with systems. Systems are how they get away with it. Every clerk, every guard, every smuggler who takes the coin and asks nothing — the whole machine runs on people deciding it's not their fire. The only thing it has never once survived is a man who decides it *is*."

* * *

They broke camp in silence after that, the kind of silence that has weight and direction, and took to the trail climbing north away from the pass, away from the furled banners and the cartographer's table and the future the empire had drawn in straight lines.

But Cyrus's legs were failing him now, and he knew it, and by mid-morning he had to admit it aloud. He sat down on a fallen trunk without meaning to, the way a building sits down when its beams give way, and Oscar crouched in front of him and studied his face with a frown that was almost clinical.

"How long?" Oscar asked.

"Days. Perhaps a week. The wound's poisoned and my body's spending everything it has keeping my head clear enough to finish the job." Cyrus laughed softly. "The irony isn't lost on me. I am, at present, the safest repository of that map's secret in the world, because I'll die rather than surrender it — involuntarily, but reliably."

"Don't say that."

"It's arithmetic, Oscar. I've never feared arithmetic."

"Well, fear it now," Oscar said, and there was something raw under the gravel of his voice that Cyrus had not heard before. "Because I signed a blood-oath to deliver you and that map, and in ten years I have broken exactly nothing I swore, and I will not start with you. So you'll walk, or you'll ride, or I'll carry you over my shoulder like the mule you are, but you don't get to lie down in the woods and turn this into a tragedy with a tidy ending. Is that understood?"

Cyrus looked at him — this hard, hollow, god-forsaken man kneeling in the pine duff with murder on his hands and a decade of unanswered prayers behind his eyes — and felt something loosen in his chest that had nothing to do with the wound.

"You know," he said, "in seven days of walking out of the Winter Palace, I never once believed I'd make it. I planned as if I would, because planning is a discipline, but I never believed. And now—"

"Now?"

"Now I look at you sulking about your broken oath, and I catch myself believing, and it's the most frightening thing that's happened to me yet. More than the riders. More than the fever." He gripped Oscar's offered forearm and hauled himself upright, swaying, holding. "Careful, Wolf. Faith is contagious. Even yours."

They climbed. Behind them, far below, the southern pass lay sealed and silent, and if Cyrus strained his hearing against the wind he could almost imagine he caught it — faint, rhythmic, patient as geology — the sound the empire had spent sixty years trying to muffle and was now coming to wake.

A heartbeat under the mountain. Counting the days it had left, and the men it had left to count on.

And ahead of them, somewhere on the high trail, the first snow of the season had already begun to fall.
