## 10. Ten Years of Silence

The fire burned low, the way Oscar liked it — small enough to hide from the valley, hot enough to keep the frost out of his joints. He sat with his back against a leaning stone and watched the flames eat down through the pine knots, and across the coals from him Cyrus shivered under both blankets and half of Oscar's cloak, his breath coming in shallow, wet pulls.

Fever. Three days of it now, climbing and falling like a bird that couldn't decide where to land.

Oscar fed the fire another knot and did what he had done every night for ten years, without deciding to, without wanting to, the way a man rubs the stump of a finger that isn't there anymore.

*Vethras. Keeper of the Given Word. Hear me.*

Nothing answered.

Nothing had answered for ten years, four months, and — he did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way you count days in a cell — eleven days. He knew the span the way other men knew their own birthdays. Not because he'd marked it. Because the silence had a shape, and his mind kept tracing it the way a tongue traces a cracked tooth.

He should have been used to it by now. He told people he was. He'd built a whole life on top of the silence the way you build a house over a sinkhole — carefully, never looking too closely at the ground beneath the floorboards.

But tonight the silence felt different. Tonight there was an oath sitting in his chest like a swallowed coal — *see it delivered, see him delivered, by road or by grave* — and every time he breathed deep it flared, and every time it flared he caught himself listening. Leaning inward. Straining toward a door he had nailed shut a decade ago.

*Cyrus*, he thought, watching the man's gray face twitch through whatever dream held him, *what in all the hells did you put in me?*

He pulled his knife and began working the whetstone along its edge, because his hands needed something honest to do, and because the sound covered the wet rattle of the stranger's breathing, and because — though he would have denied it under torture — the rhythm of it was a prayer. Sharpening was the only liturgy he had left.

The stone sang against the steel. And in the space between one stroke and the next, ten years fell away like snow off a branch.

* * *

He had been nineteen, the first time.

The shrine of Vethras stood outside the walls of Corven Hold, a plain square pillar of black granite with a bronze bowl on top, no roof, no door — because the God of Oaths kept no house and offered no shelter, only a place to stand in the open air and give your word where sky and stone could witness it. His father had taken him there before his first contract, the way other fathers took sons to be blessed at harvest.

His father had knelt — which he almost never did, not for lords, not for priests — and said, quietly, "This is the one thing we Jouberts have always had, boy. Coin comes and goes. Lords come and go. But a Joubert's word, given at the bowl —" He had tapped the granite with two fingers. "That stays given. Even if the god himself came down and asked you to unsay it. Understand?"

"I understand," Oscar had said, nineteen and certain of everything.

"And understand this too." His father's eyes, gray as slate, had gone strange then. Distant. "The god doesn't protect you. That's not what he does. Men think swearing an oath is buying armor. It isn't. It's buying weight. Your word becomes a stone in your chest, and everything you do afterward, you do carrying that stone. The god doesn't hold it for you. He just makes sure you feel it."

Young Oscar had frowned. "Then what's the point?"

His father had smiled — the rare smile, the tired one — and put a hand flat on the black granite. "The point is that some things are worth carrying. Now kneel."

So Oscar had knelt at the bronze bowl, in the wind off the mountains, and sworn his first contract-oath in the old words: *my blade for your purpose, my word for your trust, until the task is done or I am*. And he had felt it settle into him — a warmth behind the sternum, faint but unmistakable, like a coal wrapped in wool. Vethras had heard him. Vethras had accepted him.

For eleven years after that, the god answered. Not in words — Vethras never spoke, not even to his priests, or so it was said — but in presence. When Oscar swore, the coal warmed. When others broke faith with him, the coal flared hot enough that he learned to trust it like a lie-detector made of his own blood. When he kept his word at cost — and there were years when keeping it cost nearly everything — the warmth deepened, steadied, became a kind of companionship. A man alone on a cold road with a warm coal in his chest is never entirely alone.

Oscar had believed, in those years, that this was what faith was. Not temples, not tithing, not fear. Just that. A god who kept the books, and a warmth that proved the ledger balanced.

Then came Marrow Bridge.

* * *

Marrow Bridge. Even now, twelve years later, the name arrived in his memory the way cold water arrives through a boot seam — suddenly, and everywhere at once.

It had been the worst contract of his life, and it had looked, going in, like the best. Two years' pay in advance to hold a bridge over the Marrow river for six months while a succession war sputtered itself out around it. Oscar had been thirty by then, a sergeant with his own company of fourteen, a reputation, savings. The employer was a fat grain-merchant turned self-styled lordling named Aldous Renn, who wore a breastplate too big for him and talked about honor the way men talk about wine they've read about but never tasted.

The bridge guarded a mill town on the south bank. Four hundred souls, maybe five hundred. Millers and fishermen, a shrine — a proper one, roofed, with a priest who smelled of lamp oil — and in the spring, when the fighting crept closer, the town's headman came to Oscar's fire and knelt, and asked him formally, in front of his whole company, to extend his protection to the town. Not part of the contract. A separate oath, freely begged, freely given.

And Oscar — thirty years old, warm-coaled, certain of everything the way only a man whose ledger has always balanced can be certain — had stood up and given it. At their fire, in the old words, hand pressed to his own sternum where the coal lived. *My blade for your people. My word for your trust.*

The coal had flared so bright he'd half-laughed afterward, embarrassed, feeling like a boy again.

Six weeks later, Aldous Renn lost his nerve.

The enemy host was coming up the river road — three thousand at least, real soldiers, the kind that ended wars — and Renn decided the bridge was lost and the smart coin was on retreat. He ordered Oscar's company to fall back north with him. He did not mention the town. When Oscar raised it — calmly, professionally, a soldier raising a tactical problem — Renn had actually laughed.

"They're not in the contract, Joubert. The bridge is. Burn the bridge and come north. That's an order."

"The town has five hundred people."

"And you have fourteen men." Renn's laugh had died, and what was underneath it was worse than cruelty. It was arithmetic, clean and comfortable. "You'll die for nothing. That's not honor, that's vanity. March."

Oscar had gone back to his company and laid it out for them, because that was what a sergeant owed them — the truth, and the choice. Stay and die holding a crossing that wouldn't hold anyway, five hundred townsfolk evacuated ahead of the flood or not. Or march north, keep the contract, keep the pay, live.

Twelve of the fourteen voted to march.

And here was the wound — here, exactly here, the place he had spent ten years building the house over. Oscar could have marched. The contract said bridge, not town. Every law of mercenary work, every custom, every practical wisdom his father had ever taught him said *march*. No court of gods or men would have called him oath-breaker.

But he had given the second oath. Freely. At his own fire, hand on his own heart. *My blade for your people.*

He stayed.

Four of the fourteen stayed with him — Brannoc, of all people, young and foul-mouthed and inexplicably loyal; a one-eyed spearman named Doss; twin brothers, Halden and Marek, who'd never explained why and Oscar had never asked. They sent the rest north with the pay-chests. Then they went into the mill town and rang the bell and told the headman the truth: we will fight at the bridge for one day, no more, to buy you time. Get your people south into the marshes. One day.

They got three hours.

The enemy vanguard came fast and angry, and the bridge — narrow, old, good ground — became a meat-grinder, and Oscar fought on it the way he had never fought before or since, with the coal in his chest roaring like a forge, and for three hours five men and a few brave millers with boar-spears held three thousand soldiers to the width of eight planks. Long enough. Long enough for most of the town to reach the marshes. Not long enough for everyone. He still saw the ones who hadn't run fast enough — he had stopped cataloguing those faces years ago, and they still came anyway, uninvited, patient as moss.

Doss died on the bridge. The twins died on the bridge. And at the end, when the planks were finally cut and the river took the wreckage south, Oscar and Brannoc had stood in the reeds on the far bank, soaked and bleeding and alive, and watched the smoke of the burning town rise into a gray sky.

Brannoc had vomited, then cursed the god of oaths with a fluency Oscar almost admired. "Where was he?" the younger man had screamed, tears cutting through the soot on his face. "Five men against three thousand, and you *gave your word*, and where in the hells was your god, Joubert? Where was the great keeper of the given *word*—"

And Oscar had reached for the coal to answer him. Reached for the warmth, the witness, the ledger that balanced — the thing that had been with him since he was nineteen, the thing that had roared in his chest through three hours of slaughter —

— and found ash.

Cold. Cold as the river. Cold as a hearth swept out in the morning.

He had stood in the reeds with his hand pressed to his own sternum and felt, for the first time since childhood, nothing. And the terror of it had been worse than the battle. Worse than Doss's face. Because a man can survive losing a fight, losing friends, losing a town. But Oscar had lost the *witness*. The one thing that had made the carrying worth carrying.

Had the god abandoned him for breaking the first oath — for staying past his contract, for letting four men die outside its terms? Or had the god abandoned him for breaking the second one — because three hours hadn't been enough, because the town had burned anyway, because the oath had been given and the god's standard was absolute and Oscar had failed it? Ten years, and he had never been able to decide which sin the silence punished. Some nights it was one. Some nights the other. Some nights — the honest nights, the ones he drank to get through — he suspected there was no sin at all, no judgment, no meaning. That Vethras had simply never existed, and the coal had been his own youth, his own belief, burning itself out on schedule like everything else.

He had prayed at the next shrine anyway. Out of habit. Out of desperation. Nothing.

And the one after that. Nothing.

And somewhere around the third year of nothing, he had stopped kneeling, and started saying the words standing up, ironically, the way you toss a coin to a beggar you're sure is a fraud — *Keeper of the Given Word, hear me* — and somewhere around the fifth year he'd shortened it to a thought, and somewhere around the seventh he'd stopped pretending it wasn't habit rather than prayer, and now it was just the phantom limb, rubbed nightly, aching at weather changes.

Brannoc had gone the other way — no silence for him, just apostasy with a grin. He'd built the guild at Kestrel Gate on the principle that words were tools and contracts were levers, and he'd gotten rich, and whenever he saw Oscar he liked to say, "Still carrying that stone, Wolf?" and laugh.

Yes, Oscar thought, watching the fire now. Still carrying it.

That was the joke nobody else found funny. The god was gone. The stone remained.

* * *

Cyrus made a sound — not words, just a thin pull of breath, a man surfacing from deep water — and Oscar was on his knees beside him before he'd decided to move.

"Cyrus. Hey. You're on the mountain. You're with me. Drink."

The courier's eyes opened halfway, unfocused, finding the firelight and then, slowly, Oscar's face. His lips moved. Oscar leaned close.

"...still... carrying it..."

"What?"

A ghost of a smile. "Your... stone. I can feel it. Through the oath." His hand rose, trembling, and pressed — clumsily, deliberately — against Oscar's sternum, right where the dead coal lived. "It's not... what you think it is. It never... went out. It went..." His eyes rolled, the fever dragging him back under. "...down. Deep. Things go down... when something bigger... calls them..."

"Cyrus. Cyrus, what does that mean?"

No answer. The breathing steadied into sleep, or into something near it, and Oscar knelt there in the cold with a dying man's handprint of warmth fading on his chest.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing he had been refusing to look at for three days.

The blood-oath burned. Not metaphorically — he could map it, a line of heat running from the cut on his palm up the inside of his wrist, into the forearm, over the elbow, to the sternum. And it gathered there. Right there. Exactly there. Where the coal used to live. And when it flared — when he thought about the terms of it, *see it delivered*, and felt the shape of the vow like a key fitting a lock — the heat spread outward through his ribs, and for one lurching heartbeat at a time, it felt less like a new fire and more like an old one being *fed*.

Like something that had gone down deep, waiting, and had just been handed a reason to climb back toward the surface.

He sat back against the stone and stared at the coals and did the thing he had not done in ten years, four months, and eleven days.

He prayed. On purpose. In earnest. With his whole attention, the way he'd knelt at the black granite at nineteen, certain of nothing now except that he was about to make a fool of himself to an empty sky.

*Vethras. If you're there — if any of it was ever true —*

The oath flared in his chest, hard enough that he gasped, hard enough that Cyrus stirred — and for a single instant, on the very edge of hearing, in the register below sound where the coal used to speak, Oscar felt something turn over. Enormous. Slow. Attention like a mountain shifting its weight in sleep.

Not Vethras. He knew his god's touch the way he knew his own hands, and this was not it. This was older. Vaster. A patience with geological strata in it, a warmth like the deep roots of a fire that had been burning underground since before the empire had a name.

Then it was gone, folded away, and the night was just a night, and Cyrus was just a dying man, and Oscar was just a mercenary with a contract he was beginning to suspect had never really been about a delivery at all.

He looked at the courier's sleeping face. At the map-case bound tight against the man's chest, warm to the touch even now, even through leather and canvas. At the dark line of the pass above them, where tomorrow they would climb toward Hollowpine and the widow Sefa's roof, and beyond that toward Kestrel Gate, where Brannoc waited with questions Oscar was already lying to himself about.

"Things go down," he murmured to the fire, "when something bigger calls them."

The fire popped. The frost thickened on his cloak. And deep beneath Thornfell — he could not have said how he knew, and yet he knew the way he knew his own heartbeat — something enormous dreamed on, and had begun, very slowly, to dream of him.
