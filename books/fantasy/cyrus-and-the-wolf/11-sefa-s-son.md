## 11. Sefa's Son

The boy caught up to them on the third morning out of Hollowpine, on a stretch of trail where the pines grew sparse and the wind came down off the high snow in long, keening gusts that made the mules lay their ears flat and sulk.

Oscar heard him before he saw him — a small, arrhythmic crashing through the underbrush below the switchback, the sound of someone too tired to be quiet and too stubborn to stop. He held up a hand and Cyrus reined in without question. That was new, too. A week ago Cyrus had been a delirious weight across a mule's back; now he sat his saddle like he'd been born to it, gray-faced but upright, watching Oscar's hands for instruction.

The boy came up through the trees at a dead stagger, and Oscar knew him before he reached the trail.

He was maybe eleven, twelve — all elbows and hollows, wrapped in an adult's sheepskin coat cut down badly with a dull knife, the wool still grey with Greywater ash. His boots were two sizes wrong and tied with cord. He carried a woodcutter's hatchet in one hand, its edge nicked and rolled from hacking at things it was never meant to bite, and his face was so chapped by wind that the tears frozen on it had carved little white channels down his cheeks.

Oscar had seen those channels before. On soldiers. On widows. On men who had walked away from burning buildings because there was nothing left inside worth dying for.

"Tam." The name left Cyrus's mouth before Oscar could stop it — soft, certain, grief-struck. "Gods below. *Tam.*"

"You know him?" Oscar kept his voice low, eyes moving. Trees. Gullies. The wind. No riders yet, but no riders didn't mean no riders coming. "Cyrus. Do you know him?"

"He was at the ford." Cyrus swung down off his mule, and Oscar saw how much that cost him — saw the way his hand locked white-knuckled on the saddle horn, the way his breath went thin. "He pulled me out of the water. When they were shooting. He was hiding under the bridge pilings and he — he grabbed my collar and hauled me onto the stones. I'd have drowned. He couldn't have weighed more than—"

"I'm not going back." The boy had stopped ten feet away, chest heaving, hatchet hanging loose at his side. He said it to all of them, to the mules, to the mountain, in the flat voice of someone who had rehearsed the words until they'd worn smooth as river stone. "You can't make me go back. There's no back."

* * *

They made camp early that day, which was against every rule Oscar owned, in a fold of rock below a granite shoulder that would hide their fire. Which was also against every rule. He told himself it was because the boy needed warmth more than they needed caution, and then he told himself to stop lying.

Tam sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, exactly as far from the flames as he could be while still being warmed by them. He wouldn't eat until Cyrus put food in front of him directly and said, quietly, "It isn't charity if you earned it. You saved my life. This is wages."

Then the boy ate like something starved, which he was, and cried while he did it, silently, without stopping chewing, and nobody looked at him because looking was worse than pretending not to see.

Oscar walked the perimeter twice. When he came back the second time, Cyrus was sitting beside the boy, and the conversation had already gone somewhere Oscar didn't want to follow.

"—my father went out to meet them," Tam was saying, to the fire, to his hands. "When the riders came. Everyone did. They thought — I don't know what they thought. That they were just soldiers passing through? That you could *talk* to them?" He laughed, one dry bark with no humor in it anywhere. "My da put his hand up like this—" the boy raised his palm, an unconscious, terrible imitation of a man greeting a neighbor over a fence, "—and the one in front just drew and shot him off his feet. Didn't even slow down. Rode right past him into the square."

"Cyrus," Oscar said. "A word."

Cyrus followed him out beyond the rocks, into the blue dusk where the wind could take their voices. Even there, Oscar kept his own low.

"We're being hunted by riders who burn villages to cover a courier's tracks. Every hour we travel slower, we're easier to find. And you want to adopt a child?"

"I don't *want* anything." Cyrus's voice cracked on it, and beneath the crack was something harder. "I want to deliver a map and lie down and die somewhere warm. But he pulled me out of a river, Oscar. He's alive because of me and I'm alive because of him, and you're proposing we point him back down a road full of the men who burned his home?"

"There are settlements south of the pass. Farming valleys. He has family somewhere—"

"His family is *ash*." It burst out of Cyrus louder than he meant, loud enough that Oscar glanced back toward the firelight, and Cyrus dragged his voice down again, hand pressed flat against his bandaged side. "I watched that village burn from a riverbank, Oscar. I watched it because I was too weak to do anything else. Every person in it who raised a hand is dead and everyone who ran is scattered in the hills, and this boy walked *up* a mountain alone for three days to find the only people who might be going where the riders are going. Do you understand what that means? He's not lost. He's *hunting*."

"He's twelve."

"He's what you were, once. Don't tell me you weren't."

That landed somewhere under Oscar's ribs and stayed there, aching, and he hated Cyrus for it with a sudden clean heat.

"I was a soldier's son in a garrison town," Oscar said finally. "There's a difference between growing up around violence and walking into it with a hatchet."

"Is there?" Cyrus asked softly. "Where does the line fall, exactly? What age? What grief? Show me the measurement."

Oscar had no answer, and the silence stretched between them, cold and complete, until a voice spoke from behind them — small, steady, and wide awake:

"She said you'd try to send me back."

* * *

Tam stood at the mouth of the camp-fold with the hatchet back in his hand, though he held it now the way a man holds a thing he knows is useless — not a weapon, an argument. Behind him, the fire painted his shadow enormous against the granite, a giant with a stick.

"Sefa," he said. "The widow. She fed me yesterday, on my way up. Told me where your trail forked. She said — she said the big one with the wolf-scar would say go home, and the sick one would say stay." His chin lifted. "She was right about both of you."

Of course she was, Oscar thought. Sefa, who mended gear and fed strangers and asked nothing, had read all three of them in an evening over bread and broth. He wondered what else she'd seen that she hadn't said.

"Son," Oscar began, and stopped, because every word he owned sounded false in his own mouth. He tried again. "Those riders aren't soldiers on patrol. What they're carrying — what they're after — it's bigger than a village. Bigger than a valley. Men like that will kill a boy without slowing down, same as they killed your father, and I cannot—"

He stopped again. Swallowed.

"*I cannot protect you,*" he said, and it cost him more than any truth he'd spoken in ten years. "Do you hear me? Not on this road. Maybe not anywhere. I'm one mercenary with two bad mules and a dying man, and the only thing I've ever been good at is surviving, and I can't even teach you that in time. If you come with us, you will likely die on a mountainside for a secret that isn't yours."

Tam took this in the way children take hard truths — whole, without flinching, because no one had yet taught him that adults were allowed to lie to soften things.

"My da used to say the Wolf of Thornfell could walk through the empire's own lines and come out the other side whistling," he said. "Was he wrong?"

"That man's a story."

"So were the dragons." The boy's voice didn't rise. "Until somebody found the last one."

The silence after that was total. Wind. Fire-pop. Somewhere above, snow cracking loose from a pine bough under its own weight.

Cyrus made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been pain, and turned to Oscar, and in the firelight his face was terrible and gentle at once.

"You see it now," he said. "Don't you. Why it has to be him."

"No." Oscar said it flatly, immediately, a door slamming. "No. Absolutely not. He doesn't know what we carry. He doesn't know what's *hunting us* because of it. And he won't. Not ever. That's the price of him staying, Cyrus, if there even *is* a staying — he walks north ignorant or he walks south alive, and either way—"

"He already knows."

Oscar turned.

Tam stood very straight in the firelight. His hands had stopped shaking. When he spoke, each word came out level and strange, like a child reciting a lesson learned at a funeral.

"The night they burned Greywater, I hid under the bridge and watched them pull a man out of the river." His eyes stayed on Oscar, unblinking, old as the burned village behind them. "Old man. Half-dead. And the rider in front — the one who killed my da — knelt down next to him and said, *where is the map, old man, where is the dragon's heart.*"

The fire cracked. Snow fell somewhere far above.

"I don't know what a dragon's heart is," Tam said. "I don't know what's on your map. But I know they'll kill every village between here and whatever it is until they find it. My da. Sefa's valley, maybe. Everyone." He tightened his grip on the useless hatchet. "So I'm not asking to come with you for revenge. I'm asking because you're going where the fighting's going to be, and I'd rather stand in front of it with two strangers than run from it alone forever."

Behind him, Cyrus closed his eyes, and Oscar saw the exact moment the dying man decided — saw it settle over him like a mantle, like armor, like a vow.

"He stays," Cyrus said quietly.

"Cyrus—"

"*He stays,* Oscar." And then, softer, with something underneath it that Oscar would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to name: "You said you can't protect him. Fine. Then teach him to protect himself. Isn't that what a pack does?"

*A pack.* Not a company, not a crew, not a contract. A pack. Oscar opened his mouth to argue and found, to his horror, that the argument had gone out of him somewhere between *I cannot protect you* and *a dragon's heart*, and what remained in its place was something warmer and far more dangerous.

"Rules," he said at last, gruffly, because gruffness was the only weapon he had left against whatever was happening to him. "You stay within arm's reach on the trail. You learn to load and hold a crossbow before you learn to point it at anything breathing. You do what I say, when I say it, no questions, no heroics. And if I say *run*, you run, and you don't look back, and you don't come back for anyone. Not me. Not him." He jerked his chin at Cyrus. "*Especially* not him — he's half-dead anyway, he'll only slow you down."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate.

"And if I break a rule?"

"Then I send you down the mountain to Sefa, and I tie you to her fencepost myself."

Another pause. The wind gusted, scattering sparks.

"Deal," said the boy, and spat in his palm, and held it out.

Oscar looked at that small, ash-stained, outstretched hand for a long moment. Ten years ago he'd have shaken it without thinking. Ten years of silence had taught him that hands offered in good faith were the most expensive things in the world.

He shook it anyway.

The boy's grip was surprisingly strong. Ash and river-mud in the creases of his palm. Hatchet-callus already forming, though he couldn't be more than twelve.

"Welcome to the worst contract you'll ever sign," Oscar muttered.

"It's not a contract," Tam said, withdrawing his hand, suddenly shy. "Contracts end. My da said a vow doesn't."

And Oscar felt that word go through him like a blade finding the gap in armor — *vow* — and had to turn away and check the mules' pickets, which did not need checking, while the fire burned on behind him and neither of the two people he was now responsible for said anything at all.

* * *

He woke in the black hours before dawn to the sound of voices, low, and lay still in his bedroll listening.

"...and that one's the Shepherd's Eye," Cyrus was saying. "See how it hangs alone, apart from the flock? Dragons used it to find their way over the ocean. They say a dragon can close one eye and navigate by the other, and the Shepherd's Eye is the eye it closes."

"Why would it close one?"

"Because two eyes see everything, and sometimes you only need to see enough."

A pause. The wind moved the pines.

"My da showed me stars once," Tam said, smaller now. "Before."

"Then he's still showing you," Cyrus said. "Every time you look up. That's what teaching is — it doesn't stop when the teacher stops. It just gets quieter."

Oscar stared up at the wheeling, indifferent constellations and felt the ache in his chest that had lived there for ten years shift, slightly, like a man turning over in his sleep. He thought about oaths. About gods who answered and gods who didn't. About a boy spitting in his palm and calling it a vow.

*Contracts end,* the boy had said. *A vow doesn't.*

Far to the south, past the dark shoulders of the foothills, Oscar thought he saw — or imagined, or feared — a light flicker where no light should be. Small. Orange. Moving.

Torch-fire, on a road that led toward Hollowpine.

Toward Sefa.

He was on his feet and waking the others before he had consciously decided to move, and the last thing he registered, as the camp broke apart around him in efficient, frightened motion, was Tam standing with the hatchet gripped in both fists, looking south, and Cyrus watching Oscar with an expression that said, plainly: *now you understand.*

"Pack up," Oscar said. "We move in five minutes. And pray to whatever's listening that we're fast enough."

Nobody prayed. But everybody moved.
