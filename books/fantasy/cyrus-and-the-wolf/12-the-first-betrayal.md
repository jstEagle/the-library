## 12. The First Betrayal

The Narrows came at them on the third morning out of Hollowpine, the way bad news always comes — quietly first, then all at once.

Oscar had known the passage since he was seventeen, when he'd carried his first contraband through it with a cracked rib and a head full of his god's warm murmur. It was a throat of stone where the trail pinched between two faces of granite so close that a loaded mule's panniers brushed both walls at once. The floor was old riverbed, slick and pale, and the sound of water ran somewhere beneath it, under the stone, so that you walked all day to the music of a river you never saw. In summer the light reached the bottom for perhaps two hours at noon. The rest of the time you traveled in a blue half-dark that smelled of wet rock and cold iron.

It was the only place in ten days of travel where an ambush made sense, which is exactly why he should have scouted it ahead on foot.

He told himself later that exhaustion had dulled him, that grief for Greywater's smoke still hanging on the southern horizon had pulled his eyes down and inward. These were true things. They were also excuses, and he knew the difference, because a man who has lived by his caution for twenty years learns to recognize the moment he sets it down like a pack he's too tired to carry.

They entered the Narrows mid-morning, in a thin rain that couldn't decide whether to be snow. Oscar led. Behind him came the three mules on their lead-ropes — Brannoc's mules, Brannoc's men: Vetch, a wiry guild-hand with quick eyes and a laugh he spent freely, and Dole, big and slow-spoken, who whittled at the same piece of wood every night without ever appearing to change it. Tam walked beside the second mule, sullen and silent since Hollowpine, still wearing his dead town's ash somewhere under his skin. Then Cyrus, riding when he could bear the saddle, walking when the pain drove him out of it, wrapped in Sefa's good blanket with the map lashed flat against his chest beneath his coat. Ilsa took the rear, imperial-trained posture softened now into something more careful, her crossbow slung muzzle-down the way Oscar had taught her the first week, when trust between them was still a coin being tested by its weight.

The rain picked up. Somewhere above them, loose shale ticked against stone.

Oscar stopped.

It wasn't anything he could name. A bird should have been calling from the scrub on the south rim and wasn't. The river underfoot seemed louder than it had a moment ago, as if something upstream had shifted its course. And Vetch — Vetch had begun humming. Softly, tunelessly, the way a man hums when he is trying not to listen to his own heartbeat. Oscar had heard him quiet on every hard stretch of trail for eleven days. He had never once heard him hum.

"Vetch." Oscar didn't turn around. "How far behind do you figure Brannoc's sweep-team is? The ones clearing the Kestrel road for us."

A pause. Half a breath too long. "Three days," Vetch said. "Four, in this weather."

"Hm," said Oscar, who had done the arithmetic himself a dozen times and gotten five.

The shale ticked again above them. Closer. Deliberate.

"*Down*—" he started, and the sky fell in.

* * *

Later, piecing it together, he would understand how simple it had been. Two men on the rim with a fire-hardened timber laid across a shelf of balanced rock, waiting for the column's middle to pass beneath. Not soldiers — no discipline in it, no signal discipline at least, because the trap sprang four heartbeats early, before Cyrus had fully entered the kill-zone, and so instead of crushing the courier they only caught the lead mule and the wall of the Narrows.

But four heartbeats early is still enough to kill a mule outright, and enough to bring two tons of shattered stone down onto the trail behind it, sealing the way forward, and enough to fill that blue half-dark with a white roar of dust through which everything human became screaming.

Oscar hit the riverbed rolling, came up with his sword already clear — his body doing what his body did, twenty years of muscle folding around the blade like a hand around a familiar tool. Dust. Rain turning to mud in the air. The second mule shrieking, rope taut, dragging at a tangle of fallen stone. And shapes coming off the rim on ropes with the easy speed of practiced men — six of them, seven, masked with river-clay, unmarked, moving for the middle of the column where Cyrus had gone down under his blanket.

Not robbers, then. Robbers would want the mules first.

"Tam! *Get behind me!*" He saw the boy frozen in the open, knife out, face white with something older than fear — the same look, Oscar realized with a lurch, that he'd worn standing in Greywater's burning street. Revenge arriving to collect him again. "TAM."

Ilsa's crossbow spoke twice from the rear, fast and flat, professional. One of the descending shapes spun off his rope and struck the wall and slid. The others landed soft and spread wide, blades low, working toward Cyrus with terrible unhurried economy.

Dole surprised everyone. The slow man dropped his whittling and put his broad back between two attackers and Cyrus like a door swinging shut, roaring wordlessly, taking a cut across the forearm that opened him to the bone and giving back a mallet-blow that folded one clay-masked man over his knee. Oscar registered it in a flash — *Brannoc's man fighting for the package* — and hated how little it settled, because it meant the betrayal hadn't come from all of them. It meant it had come from one.

And Vetch was gone.

Not fled — Oscar found that out a half-second later when he turned to cover the flank and found the wiry guild-hand standing apart from the fight, back against the canyon wall, arms deliberately raised, and beyond him at the mouth of a side-scree four more figures rising out of the rocks where they'd lain flat since before dawn. Waiting on a signal. Waiting on a *hum*.

"There!" Vetch shouted, voice cracking with something that might have been apology or might have been terror. "The rider — take the rider, leave the rest!"

Oscar did the mathematics of the next three seconds faster than he'd ever done anything.

Six hostiles on the floor, two engaged with Dole, two closing on Cyrus, two breaking toward Ilsa's position. Four more incoming. Tam in the open. Cyrus trying to rise, one arm useless, the map-coil slipping from his coat, and Oscar watched — in the dust-hazed amber half-light of the Narrows, with the hidden river singing underfoot — watched the dying stranger plant his good hand in the mud and drag himself upright over the fallen map rather than away from it, shielding it with his ruined body like a mother over a child.

Something in Oscar went very cold and very simple.

He stopped fighting like a man defending a position and started fighting like the thing they called him. The Wolf. He went through the two closing on Cyrus the way winter goes through a shutters-left-open house — not quickly, though it was quick; not cruelly, though it was that too — economically, terribly, his blade finding the seams of them while his shoulder and boot did the rest. One died reaching for the map-coil. The other died looking at Oscar's face, and whatever he saw there made him stop struggling before the end came.

Then it was over. Or the loud part of it was. Three hostiles broke and ran up the side-scree, dragging a wounded fourth, and the rain came down harder and washed the dust out of the air, and the Narrows filled instead with the sounds that come after — moaning, the trapped mule's terror, Dole sitting down heavily in the mud holding his arm together and laughing once, a short bark with nothing in it.

Cyrus lay where he'd fallen, breathing in shallow stitched pulls, his hand still flat on the map.

"I'm well," he said, before anyone asked, in the voice of a man saying the opposite. "Mostly well. Largely."

"You're bleeding through the blanket," Oscar said.

"It was already bleeding," Cyrus pointed out. "Now it's bleeding *additionally*."

Despite everything — despite the dead mule and the sealed trail and the boy shaking behind him — Oscar felt the corner of his mouth move. Then he turned, and stopped feeling anything at all, because Vetch was still standing against the wall with his hands up, and the truth of the last eleven days stood with him.

* * *

They cleared what they could of the blockfall and retreated to a wider shelf two hundred yards back, out of bowshot of the rims, where the canyon opened just enough to make an ambush harder and a defense possible. Oscar set watches himself, paired them so that no guild-hand stood alone with any other single person, and did not explain why, because the explanation was obvious and explaining it would have been crueler than silence.

The dead hostile they'd left on the trail told them plenty once Ilsa went through the body with a professional's patience. Boots of imperial make, dyed over. No colors, no tokens — but the crossbow at his back was a Kestrel-pattern stock, the kind the guild's own smiths turned out by the dozen, and tucked inside his coat, wrapped in oilcloth, was half a page of cheap paper covered in Brannoc's unmistakable fist: route, dates, numbers. Their itinerary. Written by the chief's own hand, given to someone, sold onward or shared.

"He didn't sell us to the empire," Ilsa said quietly, crouched over the paper, her dark hair plastered to her forehead by the rain. "Or not only. These riders aren't imperial regulars — the boots are stolen or surplus. This is a contract crew. Someone paid the guild's price for the courier alive and the map intact, and Brannoc took coin from both sides of the same job. He hired you to deliver it and hired them to intercept it, and whichever party brings him a better bargain, he serves."

"That's not possible," Dole said. His arm was bound and splinted and his face was gray with pain, but his voice held steady the way big slow voices can. "The chief doesn't double a contract. Twenty years, he's never—"

"Twenty years he's never had a cargo worth a kingdom," Ilsa said. "Men don't change, Dole. Prices do."

Oscar stood apart with his back to a boulder and listened to the argument wash past him and felt the cold thing in his chest turn slowly, like a millstone taking the first grist.

Vetch sat where they'd put him, wrists lashed to a pack-frame, watching the fire with the fixed blankness of a man watching his own life burn down. He hadn't denied anything. When Oscar had dragged him here and cut the truth out of the air between them with three questions — *Did you signal them? Were you paid? Did you know about the map before Greywater?* — Vetch had answered yes to all three in a voice like wet gravel, and then, strangely, had kept talking.

"They knew my sister's name," he'd said. "Her married name. Her street, in Corvassy. You don't ask how they know that. You just — the coin was heavy and the alternative was heavy too, and I chose the one I could carry." He'd looked up then, and his quick eyes weren't quick at all anymore. "I didn't know about the boy. I wouldn't have — nobody said anything about a boy."

Nobody ever says anything about the boy, Oscar thought. That's the whole trade.

Now the others argued logistics — whether the blockfall could be climbed with the remaining mule, whether to go back to Hollowpine, whether Sefa's cabin was far enough off the hunters' maps to risk. Tam sat at the edge of the firelight sharpening his knife with slow strokes, too even, too rhythmic, a boy performing calm. And Cyrus slept propped against a saddle, feverish, one hand curled near the map even in sleep, and in the last hour his muttering had changed character — words surfacing that were not delirium's usual nonsense but something structured, liturgical almost, a list being recited to someone.

Oscar crossed to Vetch last, because he had saved him for last, and because he already knew what he was going to do and wanted to give himself every chance to be talked out of it.

"Look at me."

Vetch looked at him.

"The riders got three away clean. They'll report. By tomorrow there'll be another crew on this canyon, and the one after that, until somebody finishes the job you sold." Oscar crouched, forearms on knees, close enough to smell the fear-sweat on him. "Is there anything you can tell me that changes what happens next? Any name, any number, any gate-code — anything?"

Vetch's mouth worked. "The paymaster signs as the Gray Factor. That's real, that's not a guild alias, I've seen the seal — it's an old imperial courier-mark, retired. That's worth something. That's worth—" He stopped. Read Oscar's face. His shoulders dropped. "No. Nothing I say changes what happens next."

"No," Oscar agreed. "Nothing changes what happens next."

He stood. Drew his knife, because a knife was honest work and a sword would have been theater. Behind him he heard Tam stop sharpening. Heard Ilsa shift her weight and choose not to speak. Heard the hidden river running under everything, patient as law.

Here is what Oscar Joubert did not expect: that his hands would remember. That they would go about it without consulting him — the grip, the angle, the small mercy of speed — twenty years of butchery folded into two seconds, smooth as a key turning, so that by the time the part of him that had prayed to a silent god for a decade arrived to object, the work was finished and his hands were already cleaning themselves on Vetch's cloak with the unhurried competence of a craftsman tidying his bench.

That was the betrayal, in the end. Not Vetch's. His own. That the Wolf lived in his fingers, and his fingers had missed nothing, and some cold cellar of himself had *approved* — had noted, even mid-stroke, that this was efficient, that this was necessary, that this was fine.

He stepped back. Vetch sat slumped against the pack-frame, entirely at rest for the first time since Oscar had met him, wearing the expression of a man released from a debt.

Oscar turned away and was sick, briefly and privately, against the canyon wall, and then rinsed his mouth with rainwater from a stone cup and made himself walk back into the firelight, because the others were watching, and what they saw in the next minute would decide what they became.

"So," he said, and his voice came out level, which cost him more than the killing had. "Trust is a currency. We just learned our exchange rate." He looked around the circle — Dole gray and steady, Ilsa unreadable, Tam too pale, Cyrus awake now, watching him with those terrible lucid eyes that saw everything and accused nothing. "New rules. Nobody walks anywhere alone. Nobody receives or sends so much as a word outside this circle. And anybody who's carrying something they haven't shown us — a mark, an order, a reason — shows it tonight, by this fire, while it's cheap. Because out here the price of secrets gets paid in mules." His jaw tightened. "Next time it'll be people."

Silence. Rain on stone. Then, to Oscar's astonishment, Cyrus laughed — a thin, cracked, genuine sound.

"You first, Wolf," he whispered. "You're carrying something you haven't shown us. You've been carrying it since Hollowpine. Since before." His fever-bright eyes held Oscar's. "Every night you talk in your sleep. And it isn't your voice."

The fire popped. Somewhere under their feet, the hidden river sang on, indifferent, carrying what it carried.

"What do you mean, it isn't my voice?" Oscar asked.

Cyrus closed his eyes. "Tomorrow," he said. "Ask me tomorrow. While I still remember the answer."

Above them, far up through the broken throat of the Narrows, the rain was turning to snow, and the high shelf beyond the tree line waited, white and blind, the only road left that no one survived — except, perhaps, the desperate, the bound, and the recently betrayed.
