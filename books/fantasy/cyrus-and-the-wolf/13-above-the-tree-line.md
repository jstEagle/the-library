## 13. Above the Tree Line

The trees gave out on the third morning of the climb, and with them went the last of our illusions.

I had known mountains my whole life the way a scholar knows them — as lines on maps, as arguments in old treatises, as the spines of sleeping things. I had never known one from inside its mouth. Thornfell did not welcome us so much as tolerate us, the way an old animal tolerates lice, and above the tree line there was nothing left to hide behind. No pine shadow to slip through. No birdsong to cover our voices. Only stone the color of old bone, and sky so wide and pitiless it seemed to lean down and press against our shoulders like a hand saying *stay small*.

The wind up there did not howl. That was the worst of it. A howling wind is honest — you hear it coming, you brace against it. This wind was a whisperer. It came around shoulders of rock without warning, found every seam in our clothing, and took what heat we had left with the patience of a tax collector. By noon of that first day above the green world, I had stopped being able to feel the difference between my fingers and the staff I gripped.

Oscar walked in front. He always walked in front, which was his way of saying everything he refused to say aloud: *I will meet whatever comes first.* The Wolf moved through the high waste like something native to it, reading the snowfields for the pale blue glint of ice under crust, choosing our line across the shelf with a certainty that looked like instinct and was, I suspected, ten years of grief worn smooth into skill. He had not spoken since dawn except to count us. "Three," he would say, at each rest stop, not turning around. "Three," and walk on. It was not affection. It was arithmetic he refused to let fail.

Tam came behind him, and I came behind Tam, and behind me the mules — both of them now, the two we'd lost at the Narrows mourned in silence by everyone except Oscar, who had said only, "Mules are honest," in a voice that suggested the same could not be said of men.

And behind all of us, though none of us spoke of it, the pursuit. We could not see riders without colors at this altitude; no horse could hold this shelf. But I felt them anyway, the way one feels a storm building beyond a mountain's shoulder — not in the eye but in the skin. The empire does not abandon what it wants. I had worked in its archives long enough to know that its patience was not patience at all. It was appetite wearing patience's clothing.

We were starving. Let me be precise about that word, because precision was my trade and I have earned the right to use it even now, even here. Not hungry — starving. The ration bags had gone to crumbs at the Kestrel Gate's shadow, and what Oscar had bought from Brannoc's quartermaster with coin he clearly could not spare was barely enough to keep three bodies burning warmer than the air around them. Each night we divided a fistful of barley and a strip of smoked goat between us, and each night I gave Tam half of mine and told him I had eaten already, and each night Oscar saw me lie and said nothing, because he was doing the same thing, giving me his share when he thought I wasn't watching. We were three liars keeping each other alive with arithmetic of our own.

* * *

It happened on the second evening above the tree line, in a hollow of stone that Oscar chose for camp because — he explained in four words, which was generous for him — "wind can't find it."

He was wrong about that. The wind always finds you, above the tree line. But the hollow blunted it, and there was a shelf of overhanging rock blackened with old smoke where some traveler or herder or fugitive had sheltered before us, decades or centuries ago, and that small evidence of other human stubbornness was worth more than the shelter itself. Someone else had needed this place. Someone else had survived it, or hadn't, and either way we were not the first fools the mountain had swallowed.

We built the fire small — Oscar's rule, fire no larger than a cupped hand, smokeless fuel of dried dung and scrub root that smelled like a temple I did not want to remember — and while it burned we ate our barley, and Tam, who had been quiet all day in the way that worried me more than complaint ever could, sat hugging his knees and staring up.

Above the hollow, the stars were coming out.

I have seen stars from cities, from river valleys, from the deck of a smuggler's barge on the Inner Sea. They are pretty everywhere. But above the tree line they are not pretty. They are total. There was no haze beneath us, no dust, no breath of the low world to soften them, and the sky opened like the vault of some cathedral built by a god with more ambition than mercy, and the stars hung in it thick as sparks rising from a bonfire, thousands upon thousands, wheeling slow and cold around the spike of the North Star.

Tam stared at them the way starving men stare at bread.

"You're crying," he said to me, suddenly, accusatory.

"I'm not," I said, and then, because lying to children corrodes something in the liar, I amended: "Perhaps a little. It's the cold."

It wasn't the cold. Above those stars, faint as memory, I could see them — the true constellations, the ones no living navigator used, the ones that existed now only in sealed imperial records and in the head of a dying thief who had spent eleven years learning them so he could steal one map. The Dragon's Road. The Wing-Points. The Hearth That Walks. I had thought them lost knowledge, beautiful and useless, and here they wheeled above me every night, patient, waiting for someone who remembered how to read them.

"Teach me," Tam said.

It was not a request. It was one of those demands children make with the absolute authority of people who do not yet know what they are not entitled to. He turned to me, firelight on his face, and his eyes had the particular ferocity of a boy from a burned town — Greywater had given him grief, and grief had curdled into purpose, and purpose needed feeding.

Oscar, across the fire, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been contempt.

"The boy should sleep," the Wolf said.

"The boy," I said, "is asking to learn the oldest navigation in the world, and the boy is going to get it." I shifted myself upright against the stone, joints protesting, and patted the ground beside me. "Come here where it's warm."

Tam scrambled over. Oscar kept sharpening his knife. But he did not leave, and he did not tell me again to stop, and I had learned enough of the Wolf by then to know that staying was his way of listening.

* * *

"Now," I said, and pointed with my staff to the great sprawl of stars low in the southern sky, "forget everything the valley folk told you. Forget the Ploughman, forget the Sisters, forget the Shepherd and his dog. Those are farmer's stars. Pretty names for pretty patterns, and useful for finding north and planting barley, and wrong."

"Wrong how?" Tam asked.

"Wrong because they were drawn by people who only looked up." I traced the pattern I meant, the one I had memorized from a burned book in a vault that smelled of dust and dragon-bone. "Look there. Do you see it? Six bright stars in a curve, with the seventh dimmer one hanging below, like a claw reaching down?"

He squinted. His face did the thing faces do when the sky first resolves into meaning — the slow parting, like clouds. "Yes. Like a... a wing?"

"A wing," I said, and my chest did something painful, because the boy had seen it in three heartbeats when imperial cartographers had needed years. "That is the Near Wing of Vessarath. And there—" I swung the staff east, to a knot of stars above the jagged horizon, "—that red one, the ember-colored star, that is the Far Wing. And between them, these scattered fires, faint ones, hard to see unless the night is clean like tonight — those are the Road-Stones."

"Road-stones for what road?"

"For the Dragon's Road." I let the name sit in the dark a moment. "You've heard the stories. Everyone has. Dragons flying from the Sunfall Sea to the northern eyries without ever landing, crossing the whole world in a season. People used to say it was magic. It wasn't magic, Tam. It was mathematics. It was memory. Dragons navigated the way sailors do — by the stars — but their roads were older than any sailor's charts, older than the empire, older than the language we're speaking. These constellations weren't named by men looking up. Men learned them by watching what dragons did."

Across the fire, the knife had stopped moving.

"How do you know?" Tam asked. Not suspiciously — with the pure, bottomless trust of a child asking a grown-up to confirm the world still works. "How do you know what dragons did? They're all dead."

And there it was, the question, arriving on schedule, the way it always arrives, the way it arrived in every archive debate and every drunken argument in every tavern from Greywater to the capital. *They're all dead.*

"Not quite," I said, and heard Oscar's breathing change.

I had said too much. I knew I had said too much, and part of me — the courier, the careful man, the man sworn to secrecy layered on secrecy — flinched. But another part of me, the part that lay awake at night doing sums on how many days my wounds would let me live, looked at this orphan from a burned town and thought: *if not him, then whom? If the knowledge dies in me, it dies entirely, and I will have carried it across an empire for nothing.*

"The oldest stories," I said carefully, "say the dragons didn't die all at once. Stories say a few slept. Deep. Where no one would look." I tapped the frozen ground beneath us with the staff, once, softly, and watched Oscar's face go very still in the firelight. "Stories say the high places remember them."

"That's a story for children," Oscar said. His voice was flat as slate.

"Yes," I agreed pleasantly. "Most true things start that way."

* * *

He didn't argue further. That was the thing about Oscar Joubert — he hoarded his arguments like a miser, spending them only when the price was right. He banked the fire instead, feeding it just enough to last till midnight, and wrapped himself in his cloak facing outward toward the dark, the way he always did, the wolf at the edge of the firelight keeping watch on the world.

But Tam stayed beside me, pulled halfway under my cloak against the cold, and whispered, as if the stars themselves might overhear and be offended:

"Tell me the rest of the constellation. The whole dragon."

So I did.

I taught him the Wing-Points, and the Long Neck that arcs from the Road-Stones down toward the horizon like a question being asked of the sea. I taught him the Hearth That Walks — the wandering ember-star that is no star at all, that the old texts say was a dragon's own fire seen from a hundred leagues, moving across the night. I taught him the rule that governed all of it: *follow the wings to the Road-Stones, follow the Road-Stones to the Hearth, and the Hearth will bring you home.* Home, for dragons, meaning the eyries. Meaning the high places. Meaning, if my stolen map told the truth — and I had staked my life, my blood, and my dying body on the fact that it did — meaning the deep places under this very mountain, where something ancient had been breathing slow for a very long time.

Tam repeated the rule back to me twice until he had it perfect, his lips moving silently, tracing the patterns with his finger against the dark. Then, drowsy, his head heavy against my ribs:

"Why did they fly at night? Wouldn't day be safer?"

"Safer from what?"

"From falling. From getting lost. From..." he yawned enormously, "...hunters."

I looked up at the Dragon's Road, at those patient scattered fires, and I thought about hunters. About riders wearing no colors. About emperors who kept vaults of sleeping things and wrote orders in a hand too fine for the cruelty of the words. About a library burning, which sometimes still visited me at night whether I willed it or not.

"They flew at night," I said, "because the stars don't move. Mountains erode. Rivers change course. Forests burn and grow back as grasslands. Empires rise and fall and burn libraries and rewrite their own histories." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "But the stars are the same stars the first dragon ever saw. If you navigate by anything else, your map goes stale. Navigate by the stars, and the road stays true forever. That's why they flew at night, little one. Because they were traveling toward things that last."

He was asleep before I finished the sentence.

* * *

I sat awake a while after, the boy's weight warm against my side, and did my nightly accounting, which was less about coin than about time. The wound in my side had gone past pain into a kind of hot silence that I understood, from the archive of my own family's medical history, was not a good sign. The fever came and went in tides now. Some mornings my hands shook too badly to strike fire. I had perhaps two weeks of walking in me. Perhaps three. The math was simple and I did not flinch from it, because flinching is a luxury of the healthy.

Which meant the map could not simply be delivered. Delivery required a receiver, and receivers could be bribed, tortured, killed. Brannoc wanted it — I had seen the greed move behind his eyes at Kestrel Gate like fish under ice, and I trusted that fish exactly as far as I could weigh it. And the empire wanted it, and the empire's appetite made Brannoc look like a beggar at a banquet.

No. A map can be taken. What cannot be taken is what lives in a person — or better, in more than one person. In a boy who now, without knowing what he carried, held the Dragon's Road behind his eyes.

I am not proud of what I did next, or rather, I am proud of it in the way one is proud of necessary sins. While Tam slept I woke him — gently, with his name — and made him recite the constellations once more. All of them. Wings, Stones, Hearth, Neck. And when he had done it perfectly, grumbling, half-asleep, I kissed the top of his head the way my teacher once kissed mine and told him he'd earn his supper tomorrow by finding the Hearth That Walks before full dark.

A secret stored in a starving orphan. The empire has archives for everything, but it has no archive for that.

Across the dead fire, I heard Oscar shift. In the starlight his eyes were open, and they were fixed on me, and I could not read them — I never fully could — but I no longer believed they were empty. Ten years of silence had taught him to hide whatever lived in him, the way snow hides a spring. But springs run underneath regardless.

"You're teaching him to be a target," the Wolf said quietly. First words in hours. "Everything you give him, someone can take from him. You've seen what the people chasing us do to people who know things."

"Yes," I said. "I've also seen what happens to people who know nothing. Their town burns and they don't even understand why." I smoothed Tam's hair off his forehead. "Knowledge doesn't make him a target, Oscar. It makes him a survivor. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

For a long moment the Wolf said nothing. The wind moved around the mouth of our hollow, looking for a way in, failing, trying again. Above us the Road-Stones burned on, faithful, indifferent.

"Show me the Hearth That Walks," he said at last.

Not a question. A demand, in his four-word way, and I understood it for what it was — the closest thing to *teach me too* that a man like Oscar Joubert would ever permit himself to say. I pointed. Low in the northeast, ember-red, steady while lesser stars twinkled: the wandering light, the dragon-fire of the old stories, the star that walks.

He looked at it for a long time. Something crossed his face that I could not name — something that had to cross a great distance to arrive, like a letter sent from a country he'd sworn he'd never visit again.

Then he rolled over, faced the dark, and said, "Sleep. Dawn's early. And Cyrus—" a pause, the wind filling it, "—good lesson."

From Oscar Joubert, it was a speech.

I slept better than I had in weeks. And if, in the gray hour before dawn, I dreamed of a vast warmth far below us, turning over in its sleep beneath the stone like a banked coal remembering fire — and if, waking, I could have sworn I felt the mountain itself lean in to listen to a boy murmuring star-names in his sleep —

Well. Above the tree line, the cold plays tricks. And yet.

By midmorning of the third day, we saw them: a smudge of movement far back along the shelf, where no movement should be. Too deliberate for weather. Too organized for wolves.

Oscar stood a long time looking at it, and then at the sky, and then at me.

"We need to move faster," he said. "And we need to stop being visible." A pause, and his jaw set in a way I had learned to dread. "There's a route. Down through the icefalls. Nobody survives it."

He looked at each of us — the widow's mules, the orphan, the dying man — and waited, as if expecting us to refuse.

Tam, who had spent the morning navigating by a dragon's road, grinned and said, "Then nobody will follow us."

The Wolf almost smiled. Almost.

"Pack up," he said. "We go down."
