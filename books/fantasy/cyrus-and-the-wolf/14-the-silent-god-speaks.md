## 14. The Silent God Speaks

The dream began with snow that fell upward.

Oscar stood on a switchback he knew by heart — the third bend above Greywater Ford, where the mountain shrugged its shoulder and the road narrowed to a mule's width — but the snow rose past him in slow spirals, each flake catching light from somewhere below the world. He looked down at his boots and saw that the stone beneath them was translucent, like river ice over black water, and far beneath the ice something moved.

Something vast.

He had dreamed of his god before. In the first years of silence, the dreams had been cruel — Vethos standing at the end of a long table with his scales and his ledger, mouth moving, no sound coming out. Later the dreams had stopped entirely, which was worse. A man can survive being refused. What hollows him out is being forgotten.

But this was not that dream. This was not Vethos at all.

*Who carries my name in his blood?*

The voice did not come through his ears. It came up through the soles of his boots, through the bones of his legs, the way thunder comes through the ground before it comes through the air. It was old the way mountains are old, and tired the way mountains are tired, and beneath both of those things — faint as an ember cupped in two dead hands — there was warmth.

Oscar woke in the shelter of the leaning slabs above the tree line with Cyrus's breath rattling three feet away and Tam curled against the boy's side like a hound, and for a long moment he could not remember how to pray.

He had not truly prayed in ten years. He went through the motions — the touch of thumb to sternum where Vethos's sigil was inked, the muttered formula before a contract was signed or a blade was drawn. Habit, not faith. The words were a coin he flipped to himself: heads, the silence means nothing; tails, the silence means everything. Ten years of flipping, and every time it came up heads, because the alternative was to admit that the thing he had built his whole life around — the oath, the sworn word, the god who witnessed — had simply walked away from him and left the machinery running.

He touched the sigil now, out of habit, and the sigil was warm.

Not body-warm. Not the dull heat of skin under wool. Warm the way the map had been warm when Cyrus first pressed it into his hands on the cold road, inked in fire that never cooled. Warm like something answering.

"Cyrus." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Cyrus, wake up."

The old man stirred. Even in sleep his face was a map of pain, lines running from his eyes to his jaw like rivers on a dying land. Tam whimpered at the movement and burrowed closer, and Cyrus — even half-conscious, even burning with fever — put a hand on the boy's hair without opening his eyes. Oscar had watched him do it four nights running now. It was the most unconscious tenderness he had ever seen, and it unsettled him more than the voice in the stone.

"The oath," Oscar said. "It spoke."

Cyrus opened his eyes. They were clear this morning — one of his lucid hours, the ones that were becoming rarer, arriving like coins found in old coats. He looked at Oscar for a long time, and then he smiled, and it was the saddest smile Oscar had ever seen on a living face.

"Not your god," Cyrus said. Not a question.

"No."

"No," Cyrus agreed softly. "I didn't think so. I hoped—" He stopped. Coughed, wet and deep. "I hoped you'd have more time. To be angry about it first."

"Angry about what?"

"About being chosen." Cyrus pushed himself up against the slab, wincing, and looked east, toward the high shelf they would have to cross today, toward Thornfell's white crown hidden behind cloud. "Vethos is a god of oaths, yes? A witness. He holds the pen while men sign. But someone has to hold the *fire*, Oscar. Someone always has. And the one who held it is dying under the mountain, and it has been calling for a keeper the way a drowning man calls for a shore. You swore a blood-oath over a map. You thought you were signing a contract." His eyes came up, bright and terrible. "You were answering a call."

* * *

They climbed.

The high shelf above the tree line was a country of bone and wind — grey scree, white rime, lichen the color of old bruises clinging to rock that had never once known soil. The air was thin enough to taste of iron. Their mules picked their way along the shelf's spine with the grim patience of animals that understood, in their own wordless way, exactly how much sky surrounded them.

Oscar walked point because walking point was the only prayer he knew anymore. Watch the footing. Watch the weather. Watch the ridgeline for riders without colors. Keep the body moving and the mind will follow, and if the mind refuses, keep it moving anyway.

But the voice kept coming.

Not words, mostly. Impressions. When he set his boot on a shelf of granite, he felt the mountain feel it — a slow, deep attention, like a sleeper registering a fly on her cheek. When the wind gusted off the eastern face, carrying the smell of snow and distant pine, he felt a flicker of something that could only be called *longing*, and it was not his longing. He had not smelled pine and wanted anything in years. He wanted nothing. Wanting was how men died on mountains.

By midday the impressions had shapes. Heat, coiled and banked, deeper than any forge. Darkness that was not empty but *full* — full of centuries, full of names, full of a city of vaulted halls that had been alive once with wingbeats and now echoed only for one occupant. And threaded through all of it, patient as root-growth, a question:

*Will you keep it?*

"I don't know what *it* is," Oscar muttered into his collar, and felt absurd, and felt less absurd than he should have, because the attention behind the question did not withdraw. If anything it leaned closer, curious, the way a child leans over a well.

Tam caught him at it. The boy had been walking beside him for an hour without speaking — the boy had learned that Oscar's silences were not invitations — but children see everything and understand half of it, which is more than most adults manage.

"You're talking to the mountain," Tam said.

"I'm talking to myself. There's a difference."

"My mam talked to herself. She said it was because she was clever enough to get good answers." The boy's face did something complicated — grief crossing under humor like a fish under ice — and then smoothed itself flat, the way it always did. Greywater had taught him that. "Is the mountain going to answer you?"

Oscar considered lying. He was, by trade and temperament, a liar; it was one of the few honest things about him. But the warmth in his chest shifted when he opened his mouth to do it, a pressure like a hand laid flat between his ribs, and what came out instead was true.

"It already did."

Tam nodded slowly, accepting this with the terrifying ease of children, and asked the only question that mattered. "Was it nice?"

Oscar thought about the vastness. The patience. The drowning-man's hope threaded through it all, ancient and enormous and aimed at *him*, small as he was, like a cathedral's worth of light funneled through a keyhole.

"It was lonely," he said.

* * *

They made the shelter cave as the light failed — a hollow under an overhang of blue-grey stone, floored with old drift, walled against the wind. Cyrus was failing. Everyone could see it now; there was no more pretending the fever was a traveler's chill. His hands shook too badly to grip the tea cup, so Tam held it for him, and Cyrus drank with his eyes closed and his free hand resting on the boy's knee, and Oscar sat across the small fire and watched them and felt the dragon's attention watching with him, a second pair of eyes behind his own.

That was the part that terrified him. Not the size of the thing. Not the age of it. The *attention*. For ten years Oscar Joubert had lived inside a silence so complete it had become a country, and he had learned its borders, learned to farm its thin soil, learned to call the silence peace. Now something looked back at him from under the world, and the silence he had made his home turned out to be nothing but a room with the door bolted from the inside.

He had bolted it himself. That was the worst of it. Vethos hadn't left. Vethos had gone quiet the night Oscar had broken his first oath — the night in the border war he did not name, the contract he'd completed and the promise underneath it that he'd let die — and Oscar had told himself ever since that the god had abandoned *him*. Sitting in the firelight with a dead man's memories bleeding into his veins and a living mountain's heart beating under his boots, the truth arrived with the simplicity of all terrible truths: gods of oaths do not speak to men who have stopped meaning their words. The silence had been an answer all along. He just hadn't wanted to hear it.

And now something else had heard him anyway. Something older than Vethos, older than oaths, older than the whole tidy architecture of contracts and witnesses and signed names. It had heard the blood-oath — the one real vow he'd made in ten years, sworn over a map inked in fire to a dying stranger — and it had answered the way the sea answers a single drop of rain.

"Tell me about it," Oscar said quietly. "The heart. What am I hearing?"

Cyrus opened his eyes. The lucidity was still with him, fraying at the edges, a lamp low on oil.

"A heartbeat," Cyrus said. "Slower than seasons. It beats maybe once a year, near enough. Everything else you're feeling is the echo of the last beat, rolling around under the stone like a bell that's been struck and won't stop ringing." He smiled again, that same terrible gentleness. "You've been listening to a dying man's last word for three days, Oscar. It said *keeper*. It's still saying it."

"And if I don't answer?"

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes.

"Then it dies alone," he said finally, "and the empire digs it out of the mountain and drains what's left, and makes lamps of it, and weapons, and the world gets very bright for a little while and then very dark for a very long time. Or—" and here his voice dropped, went sly and soft, the voice of the archivist who had stolen fire from an emperor, "—you answer. And then you'll know what it costs. Because it isn't a god, Oscar. Gods take worship. This takes *keeping*. Every day. For the rest of your life. It will be in your blood and your dreams and your silence, and it will ask you for things, and you will not always want to give them." He leaned forward, fever-bright. "So I'm asking you now, while you can still walk away. Do you hear me? While you can still walk away."

Oscar looked at the fire. He looked at Tam, asleep against Cyrus's side, the boy's hand still curled in the old man's cloak. He looked at his own thumb, pressed against the sigil on his sternum, and realized he had stopped touching it days ago — that the habit had died without his noticing, the way a tooth stops aching before it falls out.

"I haven't been able to walk away since Greywater Ford," he said.

* * *

He took the last watch, as always.

The others slept. The wind worked at the overhang with its patient chisel. Far to the south, past the shoulders of the mountain, he imagined he could see the glow of imperial campfires — knew he couldn't, knew it was imagination, knew it didn't matter, because they were coming whether he saw them or not.

He sat with his back to the blue-grey stone and let himself listen.

At first there was nothing. Wind. Fire settling. Cyrus's rattling breath. Then, sinking down through the layers of sound the way a stone sinks through water, the other thing. Not a voice this time. A rhythm. One beat. Then a silence longer than any silence he had ever endured, long enough for him to doubt it, long enough for grief to rise in his throat like bile — and then another beat.

Once a year, Cyrus had said. But here, in the dark, with his blood-oath humming in his veins like a struck tuning fork, Oscar could feel the whole shape of it: the beat, and the vast echo rolling outward through miles of stone, and the long dark between, and at the center of the dark something that had waited so long it had forgotten waiting was a choice.

*Keeper,* it said, in the space between beats. Not a command. A question that had been asked so many times, to so much emptiness, that asking had become its nature.

And Oscar Joubert — mercenary, liar, breaker of one oath and keeper of another, a man whose god had gone silent because he had — felt his faith reattach. Not to Vethos, not to the ledger and the scales and the tidy witnessing of men's signatures. To this. To the wrong altar, the buried altar, the altar that asked nothing but keeping and offered nothing but company in the dark.

His eyes were burning. He discovered this with mild surprise.

"All right," he said aloud, to the stone, to the fire, to the boy and the dying man and the empire gathering at the passes. His voice cracked in the middle and he let it. "All right. I hear you."

Under the world, impossibly far down, something that had been holding its breath for a hundred years —

breathed.

And in the same instant, south along the ridge line, a signal fire bloomed on a peak that should have been empty — orange against the stars, deliberate, answered a heartbeat later by a second bloom farther off. Riders without colors. Or worse: riders with colors at last, done hiding.

Brannoc's men had found the shelf. Or the empire had. By morning it wouldn't matter which.

Oscar stood, drew his knife, and woke the others with a hand on each shoulder — the boy, the dying man — already counting steps to the next shelter, the next bend, the hidden door he was beginning to suspect had been waiting for him all along.

"Up," he said. "We move now."
