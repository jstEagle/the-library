## 15. Brannoc's Ledger

They took me at the mouth of the Kestrel Gap, where the wind funnels between two walls of black stone and a man's voice carries farther than his legs can run.

I had felt it coming for an hour before it happened — that animal arithmetic I had learned in the Emperor's archive, counting shadows against the sun. Oscar had gone ahead to test the scree slope with Tam at his heels, and for a quarter mile I walked alone, leaning on my stick, listening to my own breath rattle like a coin in a dry cup. That was the mistake, or perhaps it was not a mistake at all but the working out of something older — the way all my roads since the archive had bent toward their ending no matter how carefully I plotted them. The map in its oilcloth against my chest was warm as a sleeping bird. It should have warned me. Warm things draw hands.

Four men came down from the rocks on the eastern side, unhurried, boots wrapped in fleece so they made no sound at all. Guild-trained. I knew the walk. I had watched men move like that through the stacks of the Imperial Vault, quiet as dust falling, and I had flattered myself then that I was invisible too.

"Master courier," said the one in front, and lifted back his hood.

I knew his face from the toll-house at Greywater Ford, before the Ford burned. A heavy man with a beard braided into two grey ropes and a scar that split one eyebrow into a small white thicket. Dagny, they had called him. Brannoc's right hand, or the hand he showed to strangers.

"You have me mistaken," I said, which was true and useless.

"We have your smell," Dagny said. "Wolf-smell. You've been traveling three weeks with Joubert, and everything he touches takes his stink." He smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were pale and patient as river stones. "No insult meant, old man. We only want a word."

"A word," I repeated.

"And whatever you're carrying that makes the Wolf break his own rules. He doesn't take passengers. Everyone knows it. Ten years, he hasn't taken a passenger." Dagny tipped his head, studying me the way a horse-trader studies teeth. "So either you're paying him more than the guild pays him in a year, or you're carrying something worth more than the guild earns in ten."

The wind moved between the walls of stone. Somewhere above us, a kestrel cried once and did not cry again.

"I'm carrying," I said slowly, "a dying man's errand."

"Good," said Dagny. "We like dying men. They tell the truth."

They did not bind my wrists roughly, which I noted the way I noted everything now — with the strange double vision of a man whose body is leaving before his mind does. They gave me a mule. They gave me water warmed over their fire. They were courteous the way butchers are courteous to livestock they intend to keep healthy until market day, and some cold cellar of my understanding filed that observation away and began, without asking my permission, to plan.

We turned north, away from the route Oscar would expect me to be on when he came looking. That was the first thing. The second thing I learned that evening, around their low fire in a fold of the hills, when Dagny opened a ledger bound in green leather and began to write.

* * *

I have spent my life among documents. I know what a ledger is: a machine for making human beings legible, for turning blood and hunger into columns of numbers that will balance at the bottom of a page. In the Emperor's archive I catalogued ledgers of conscription, ledgers of requisition, ledgers of the dragon-hunts themselves, each kill recorded with a date, a location, a bounty paid, and a small clerk's notation — *specimen recovered* or *specimen burned* — that reduced the death of something vast and holy to a bookkeeping problem.

Dagny's ledger was smaller than those. It balanced the same way.

He wrote by firelight, tongue between his teeth, careful as any archivist, and because he thought me half-dead and harmless, he did not turn the page away when I looked. My eyes are old but they are sharp, sharpened further by the oath-fire that has been leaking out of me and into Oscar these past weeks, and I read what he wrote upside down across the fire, line by line, while my heart performed a slow and terrible settling, like a house sinking into thawing ground.

*Item: courier recovered, Kestrel Gap.*
*Item: map (claimed), pending verification.*
*Item: delivery south, imperial contact, Fort Andrel.*
*Sum: 40,000 crowns imperial, plus guild charter, plus cessation of border interdiction.*

Forty thousand crowns. I almost laughed. All this way, all this burning, and the last dragon in the world priced at forty thousand crowns and a smuggling charter — less than the empire had once paid for a single breeding female's skull. The world does not even raise its prices for miracles. Greed is not a grand thing. I understood that in that moment with a clarity that felt like a wound being cleaned: greed is ordinary. It wears fleece-wrapped boots. It writes neatly in green leather books. It buys peace cheap and calls it wisdom.

"Peace," I said aloud. I had not meant to speak.

Dagny glanced up. "What?"

"You wrote *cessation of border interdiction*." I kept my voice thin and papery, an old man's mumbling, though inside me something had gone very still and very clear. "The empire ends its harassment of your routes. That's what you're buying. Peace for the guild."

"Peace for everyone, if the emperor's ministers keep their word." Dagny resumed writing. "There's a war coming whether we like it or not, grandfather. The empire wants what's under Thornfell so it can end that war quickly instead of letting it grind on for thirty years. Millions live or die on how fast they get it. We're not selling a knife. We're shortening one."

"Is that what Brannoc tells you."

"That's what the arithmetic tells us." He tapped the ledger with his stub of pencil. "One old dragon, already dying — and don't look surprised, everyone who knows anything knows it's dying, the whole mountain's been warmer every winter for a decade — one dying dragon against a generation of fire-weapons that could hold the northern kingdoms hostage and end the border wars forever. What would you trade? One candle, or a hundred houses?"

"One candle," I said, "that is also a living thing."

Dagny looked at me for a long moment across the fire. Then he went back to his columns.

That night they tied my ankles with soft rope and set a man to watch me, and I lay beneath unfamiliar stars and did what I have done my whole life, which is remember. Not hope — I was long past hoping for myself; the fever had eaten through the last of my denial somewhere on the high shelf, and I knew with the calm of a man reading his own final entry that I would not see spring. But remembering is not hoping. Remembering is work, and there was so much left to do.

* * *

Here is what I remembered in the dark while the guild slept around me:

A vault beneath the Imperial Palace of Andrel, deeper than the cisterns, deeper than the old queen's tomb, reached by a stair of black stone worn hollow in the middle by three hundred years of clerical feet. I was young when they brought me down it, younger than Tam, a foundling with a gift for languages and no family to miss. They made me an archivist of the Restricted Collection, and they never told me — or perhaps they never knew, or perhaps they knew and did not care — that a boy raised among dead paper learns to hear the living things behind it.

The vault of sleeping dragons was not under the palace. It was under Thornfell, and the archive held only its records: the hunt-ledgers, the egg-counts, the orders signed in the Emperor Aurelian's own hand. *Let none be suffered to wake.* And beneath those orders, in a case of iron and glass that I alone was permitted to clean, a single scale the size of a shield, still faintly warm after eighty years, humming against my palms like a struck bell whenever I touched it. I cleaned that case every week for eleven years. I talked to it. God forgive me, I talked to it like a friend.

The theft itself took four years to arrange and eleven seconds to commit. The map was not drawn by any imperial hand; it was drawn in fire, pressed onto vellum by a claw that no longer existed, and it lived in the deepest case because the emperors could not read it and could not bring themselves to destroy it. Fire recognizes fire, the oldest archivists whispered. It might yet find its way home. So they kept it, the way misers keep a coin they cannot spend, and I — I who had talked to a scale for eleven years — simply took it, one night when the bells rang for a plague-death in the upper city and every clerk was drunk on funeral wine.

I did not steal it to sell. Let the ledger say what it likes; let Brannoc's neat columns balance however they balance. I stole it because the scale in its case had grown colder every year, and I understood before anyone else in that palace of clever men understood what the coldness meant. The heart beneath Thornfell was guttering. And when it went out — when the last fire in the world died under the stone — the emperors would not mourn. They would simply find another fire to hoard, or make one, or burn ten thousand men trying.

I ran with the map for six years. Six years of false names and winter roads and the slow, patient spread of the riders without colors across every kingdom that would harbor me. I told myself I only needed to buy time. Time enough for the dragon to recover, time enough for the empire to forget, time enough for someone better than me to decide what a miracle owed the world.

Time is the one thing no courier can deliver.

* * *

On the second day they let me walk beside the mule, my ankles free, because I convinced them I could barely stand, and a corpse is worth nothing to anyone. I used the freedom to count. Eight men besides Dagny. Two bows. Horses for five, which meant they expected to ride hard soon, which meant the rendezvous with the imperial contact was near. And on the second evening, as we camped in a ring of standing stones so old no one remembered raising them, a rider came up from the south wearing no colors at all, and Dagny went apart with him for an hour, and when he came back his face had the particular smoothness of a man who has just learned his price has gone up.

"Well, grandfather," he said, squatting by my fire. "Your friends caught up with you. The Wolf killed two of ours at the Narrows, did you know? And took our traitor's head off with hardly a word. Brannoc's decided you're worth more than we quoted." He smiled his butcher's smile. "Nothing personal. Just the ledger."

"The ledger," I echoed.

"You've seen it. Numbers are honest. People lie." He fed a stick to the fire. "You're wondering why we haven't just taken the map off you and been done."

The question hung there, and I understood — late, as I understand most things, but completely — and my stomach turned over with something worse than fear.

"It won't open for you," I said.

Dagny's eyes flicked up. For the first time the patience in them cracked, just slightly, showing the anxiety underneath, and I saw the whole shape of the guild's dilemma laid bare as a dissected frog.

"Brannoc thinks it's locked," Dagny said. "Some kind of ward. Two of the boys tried to cut the oilcloth off you while you slept and their knives came away blistered. Fire-ward, the kind the old books talk about. It'll give up its secret to whoever's sworn to carry it and no one else." He shrugged. "So we carry you. You'll swear the delivery over to Brannoc, proper and formal, the way oaths work — or you'll die inconveniently, and we'll see how much of the ward dies with you. Either way, Fort Andrel gets its coordinates before the month is out, and the guild eats well for fifty years."

"And the dragon," I said. My voice sounded very far away to me. "You're selling a door. You don't know what's behind it. None of you know what's behind it."

"Don't we?" Dagny stood, brushing ash from his knees. "Everyone knows what's under Thornfell, grandfather. It's the worst-kept secret in the world. The only question was who'd get there first." He paused at the edge of the firelight, and for a moment — I will grant him this — something almost human moved behind the river-stone eyes. "My father died in the border wars. Third conscription. I was nine. If forty thousand crowns and one dying lizard buys a treaty that keeps the next nine-year-old's father alive, I'll sleep fine. Better than fine."

"That is exactly," I said, "what the Emperor's clerks used to say."

He left me with the fire and the standing stones and the cold coming down off the heights.

* * *

I did not sleep. Instead I did the last piece of work remaining to me.

The ward on the map is not a lock, whatever Dagny believes. It is a promise — the same promise that binds me to Oscar, written in older ink than any archive holds. Fire recognizes fire; the map answers only to a keeper sworn in blood, and a keeper sworn in blood cannot transfer the vow like a deed of sale. Dagny's plan was built on a misunderstanding of how oaths work, and I could have told him so, and I did not, because a confused enemy is a slow enemy, and I needed slowness the way a drowning man needs air.

But I needed more than slowness. I needed a road out, and I am a dying man with rotten lungs and a stick for walking, surrounded by eight armed professionals in a circle of ancient stones. There was no road out on foot. There was no road out by strength. There was only the one road I have traveled my whole life, the coward's road and the scholar's road, the road of information moving faster than the men who own it.

They had not searched me properly. They feared the oilcloth — blisters teach respect — and so they never looked beneath it, at the lining of my coat, where for three weeks I have carried a second document: four lines of charcoal rub, taken secretly from the map's edge, showing the star-marks of the hidden door. Worthless without the rest. But if Oscar found them — if he understood what they meant, that his Wolf-knowledge of the passes could triangulate from those marks faster than any imperial surveyor —

If he found them, he would not need to follow Dagny's trail. He would know the destination, and the Wolf, whatever else he is, whatever silences he carries, does not abandon what he has sworn to carry. I had heard it in his voice when he argued with Sefa about turning back. Underneath all that frost, the man is a liturgy of one.

So in the deepest hour of the night, while the watchman dozed against a standing stone, I worked loose the stitching of my coat-lining with fingers that would not stop trembling — age, not fear, I told myself; mostly age — and I folded the charcoal rubs small, and I waited for morning, for movement, for any chance at all.

It came sooner than I dared hope, and from the wrong direction.

A sound came up the southern slope first — a single kestrel's cry, the same note I had heard in the Kestrel Gap, except that there are no kestrels hunting at this altitude before dawn. Tam's signal. The boy had invented it himself on the high shelf, a joke between him and Cyrus about birds that navigate by dragons' stars, and my heart slammed against my ribs like a fist on a door. They had followed. The fool, brave, impossible man had followed the guild's trail with a child and a defector, straight into eight spears and a butcher's smile.

And then, cutting through the pre-dawn dark from the east, thin and cold and utterly unmistakable, I heard something else. Not a bird. Not a man. A sound like a bell struck underwater, like a scale the size of a shield humming after eighty years — rising out of the bones of the mountain itself, faint but growing, as if something far below the standing stones had felt the approach of its stolen spark and begun, after all this time, to call it home.

Dagny burst from his tent, sword half-drawn, shouting his men awake.

"What in the name of every silent god is *that?*"

I smiled at him from beside the dead fire — an old man's smile, papery and mild, hiding everything.

"That," I said, "is the sound of your ledger failing to balance."
