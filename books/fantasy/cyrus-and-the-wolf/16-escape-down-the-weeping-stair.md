## 16. Escape Down the Weeping Stair

They kept Cyrus in the hollow beneath the Kestrel's overhang, three days' hard climb below the gate, and Oscar spent those three days learning the shape of his own fear.

It was not a shape he knew well. Fear for himself was an old coat; he wore it loose, shrugged at it, slept in it when he had to. But this was a different animal — a cold weight behind the breastbone that woke him before dawn on the second night with Cyrus's name already in his mouth, though Cyrus was a mile of scree and pine away, bound hand and foot in Brannoc's keeping, and no amount of waking would put Oscar beside him.

Through the blood-oath he could feel him. That was the worst of it. A faint warmth guttering somewhere south of his own heart, like a coal wrapped in wet wool — present, dimming, present. He had lived ten years without anything answering him from the dark, and now that something finally did, it answered in another man's dying.

Tam sat across the fire, sharpening a knife with the grim, mechanical patience of a boy who had learned recently that blades mattered. The firelight made him look younger than twelve and older than twenty all at once, which Oscar supposed was what burning villages did to faces.

"You keep touching your chest," Tam said, not looking up.

"Heartburn."

"Liar."

Oscar almost smiled. "Aye. Liar." He fed a branch to the flames. "I can feel where he is. Like a splinter under the skin, only it's under something deeper than skin. Fainter every hour."

"Then we're slow."

"We're not slow, we're careful. There's a difference, and it's the difference between two graves and one rescue."

Tam set down the knife and looked at him with Sefa's grey eyes — no, not Sefa's, the boy had never met Sefa before Greywater burned, but there was something widow-like in that stare anyway, something that had buried things and meant to outlive the rest. "My mother said careful men die of carefulness."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have liked me."

"She said she hated liars too."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass like a long exhalation, and beneath it — so faint Oscar could have imagined it — came the pulse of the oath. Guttering. Guttering.

Oscar stood, joints cracking, and looked north toward the black shoulder of Thornfell where the Weeping Stair hid behind its curtain of ice.

"All right," he said. "Not careful, then."

* * *

There were perhaps six men alive who knew the Weeping Stair existed, and Brannoc had killed one of them himself, years ago, over a debt Oscar had never asked the details of. That left five, and four of them were old smugglers gone soft or dead, and the last was Oscar Joubert, who had found the route at nineteen with a broken ankle and a winter's worth of stubbornness, and had sworn never to use it again because using it twice felt like tempting whatever god had let him live the first time.

The stair ran down the eastern face of Cwm Ddu, where a waterfall fell three hundred feet through a cleft so narrow the sun touched it perhaps forty days a year. In summer it was suicide. In winter, when the falls froze into a single ribbed pillar of blue ice — the Weeping, the old trappers called it, because meltwater bled weeping down its face even in the cruelest cold — it became a different kind of suicide, but a slower one, with handholds.

Which was precisely why Brannoc's men would not be watching it. They would be watching the goat trails and the Narrows and every road a sane man might use to come for their prisoner. Brannoc knew Oscar. Brannoc knew Oscar was many things — liar, wolf, apostate, mule — but he did not believe Oscar was stupid, and the Stair was stupidity wearing crampons.

They went up through the dark in single file, Tam first because he was lightest and because Oscar did not want to watch the boy's face while they climbed, then Oscar, hauling the rope and the gear and the whole grinding weight of his own doubt.

The cleft opened around them like a throat. Ice sheathed the walls in flows and curtains, thick as cathedral glass, and from deep inside them came a sound like the mountain muttering in its sleep — water still moving under the frost, patient, ancient, unimpressed by men.

"Gods," Tam breathed. His breath fogged and froze on his lashes within seconds.

"One," Oscar said. "Maybe. It stopped talking to me, so I've no basis for comparison." He drove his first picket into a seam of living rock, tested it with his full weight, grunted approval. "Rules. You don't speak on the ice unless I speak first. You clip in when I tell you and not a breath before. And if I fall—" he caught the boy's eye, held it, "—you cut the rope. Not because I say so. Because I'll say it falling, and falling men lie worse than careful ones."

"I'm not cutting you loose."

"You are, though."

"Then don't fall."

Oscar stared at him. Somewhere in the last week the boy had grown a spine like a whipstock, and Oscar kept being surprised by it, the way you're surprised by weather. "Fine. Don't fall either. We'll make a religion of it."

He turned to the ice, spat on his gloves for grip the way his first teacher had taught him, and began to climb down the face of the Weeping itself.

Three hundred feet of frozen waterfall. The ice spoke under his crampons in small complaining voices; the pick of his hammer struck sparks of frost; the world narrowed to the next hold, and the next, and the blue-white glow of the cleft like light through closed eyelids. Twice the surface groaned — a long tectonic complaint, the sound of a mountain shifting its weight — and twice Oscar hung from one arm in the dark and waited for the ice to decide whether he lived.

Below him, Tam descended like something born to it. Oscar noticed this with a complicated feeling. Greywater had been a ford town; ford boys grew up on wet stones. But this was more than sure-footedness. The boy read the ice. Saw the hollow-sounding patches by the color alone, the same way Cyrus read a map or Oscar read a man's intentions. He flagged each one with a tap of his knuckle before Oscar reached it, saving them both a dozen small deaths.

By the time their boots hit the frozen talus at the bottom, dawn was a grey rumor high in the cleft, and Oscar had revised his estimate of Tam Vane upward in a way he would deny under torture.

* * *

Brannoc's camp sat in the bowl below the old kestrel roosts, and Oscar watched it for two hours from a shelf of rock while the sun tried and failed to warm anything.

Eight men. Two fires, both burning low. Cyrus laid near the larger one under a heap of blankets — alive, then; the ember in Oscar's chest confirmed it, flickering faintly, like a candle seen through a dirty window. They were feeding him. Brannoc wanted him alive, which meant Brannoc wanted something from him, which meant time, but not much time, because Brannoc's patience had the lifespan of a mayfly with money problems.

Two sentries on the rim. One walking, one dozing against his spear, which would get him flogged in any proper company. The horses picketed apart, downwind, sensible. Mules with the guild's mark. And Brannoc's tent set square in the middle like a chess piece that believed itself a king.

"He's there," Tam whispered. "In the middle tent. I can see his boots under the flap. He never takes those boots off. Papa said smuggler chiefs sleep ready to run."

"Your papa was right about boots and wrong about staying in Greywater." Oscar said it flatly, without heat, and immediately regretted it — not because it wasn't true, but because Tam absorbed it without flinching, just nodded, filed his parents' deaths away in whatever ledger the boy kept, and went back to counting sentries. That absence of flinching frightened Oscar more than the ice had.

"Sentries change at the bell-cow," Tam said. "That bird. Listen — it calls every quarter hour, regular as a priest. When it calls, the walkers cross paths in the middle, backs to us for maybe thirty counts."

"How long have you known that?"

"Since we got here. You were busy brooding."

Oscar exhaled slowly through his nose. "Brooding."

"It's what Sefa called it." A pause, delicate as frost forming. "When she talks about you."

"She talks about me?"

"Constantly." Tam's mouth did something that was almost, dangerously, a grin. "Mostly complaints."

The plan, such as it was, relied on three assumptions: that the ice-grip of a winter dawn would hold their footing on the rim rocks, that guild mercenaries paid to guard a prisoner would not expect trouble from the direction of a cliff no sane climber would descend, and that Oscar Joubert still remembered how to move quiet as a debt collector.

The first two held. The third held barely.

They came over the rim during the bell-bird's call, thirty counts of grey half-light and held breath. Oscar took the sleeper first — a sap-hilted blow to the temple, precise, merciful by the standards of his old trade — and Tam, moving on his own initiative because apparently the boy had decided plans were suggestions, dropped the walker's legs from behind with a length of rope and had him gagged and trussed before the man finished deciding whether to shout.

Oscar looked at the boy. The boy looked back, breathing hard, eyes too bright.

"The tent," Oscar said finally. "Go."

Cyrus was awake inside, which none of them expected. His eyes found Oscar in the dark of the tent with terrible clarity, the lucidity that came and went like weather.

"You took your time," Cyrus rasped.

"You should see the other route."

"There is no other route. You used the Stair." A ghost of a smile moved under the fever-sweat. "You absolute madman. I stole that route from the imperial survey maps and I burned the original—"

"Later. Flattery later." Oscar cut the ropes at wrist and ankle, and the moment the blood-oath's tether came free of its binding — some sympathetic jolt he couldn't name — the ember in his chest flared, then wobbled, dimmer than it had been at dusk. Too dim. He got an arm under Cyrus and understood at once how bad it was: the man weighed nothing, a bundle of sticks with a heartbeat, and the wound in his side had soaked through fresh dressings and old ones both.

"He's torn something," Tam said. He'd appeared at the tent flap with a waterskin and Cyrus's boots, having somehow looted the camp's supply pile in the chaos, and his voice had gone very level, very adult, the voice of a boy performing calm. "He needs stitching and heat and he can't walk the Stair like this."

"He can if we carry him."

"On that ice? With him bleeding?"

"Yes," Oscar said. "On that ice. With him bleeding. Unless you've a better idea, and quickly, because that sleeper's going to wake with a head that rings like a chapel and a temper to match."

And that was when the camp's third fire — the one back among the horse lines that Oscar had dismissed as a cookfire — resolved into two men who had been sitting wrapped in cloaks all night, watching, and who now rose together with the unhurried menace of professionals.

Brannoc stepped out of his tent pulling on his gloves.

"Wolf," he said, conversational, into the silence. "You climbed my mountain, robbed my fire, hit my man Kell with a sap like a common cutpurse, and you didn't even come share a drink. I'm wounded."

"Brannoc." Oscar shifted Cyrus behind him, felt Tam do the same on the other side — a flank, suddenly, without being asked. "Step aside. This isn't your coin."

"Everything is my coin. That's what a ledger is." Brannoc's smile showed teeth and no warmth whatsoever. "You've been strange since the Ford, boy. Slow on the road, cagey at the Gate, lying like a first-year. And here you are, come all this way down a dead man's cliff for a courier nobody's paid you to protect. So either the map is worth more than you told me—" he flexed his hands, "—or you've gone soft. Either way, I'll take it off the body if I have to. Though I'd rather take it civil."

"Come take it, then."

What followed was short, ugly, and lit by a kicked-over cookfire. Oscar fought the way water finds cracks — no flourish, no anger, everything economical — and Tam fought the way boys fight when they've rehearsed a thing in their heads every night since their village burned: ferociously, badly, and effectively enough. Cyrus, half-fainting against the tentpole, threw his entire weight sideways into the legs of the nearest swordsman at exactly the wrong moment for that man and exactly the right one for Tam, and the boy's borrowed knife found the gap under the man's arm.

Then Oscar had Brannoc's collar in one fist and the man's own dagger hovering over the soft of his throat, and the camp went still except for the fire eating the tent canvas.

"Tell your ledger," Oscar breathed, "that the account's closed."

"You kill me, the guild hunts you to the edge of the map."

"The guild's already hunting me. At least now it'll grieve somebody."

He did not cut. He wanted to — his hands remembered wanting to with an ease that sickened him — but Cyrus's ember pulsed once against his ribs like a warning, or a request, and instead Oscar slammed the pommel of the dagger into Brannoc's temple and dropped him into the snow beside his own snoring sentry.

"Run," he said, and they ran.

* * *

The Stair again. But now it was worse than climbing down had ever been, because now they climbed up with a dying man lashed to Oscar's back, and the ice had decided, overnight, to stop pretending it was friendly.

Halfway up, at the great bulge where the waterfall kinked around a rib of rock, the surface had wept and refrozen into a slope of black glass, near-vertical, slick as a mirror. Oscar had climbed it in the dark coming down by luck and moonlight. By daylight he could see exactly how impossible it was, and Cyrus's weight pulled at his shoulders like regret made physical.

"I can't feel my feet," Cyrus murmured into his collar. Delirium creeping back in. "Wolf. Wolf, listen. If I go over the side, don't—"

"Shut up. Save your breath for bleeding."

"—don't let Tam watch. Boys remember. I remember." A shudder ran through the thin frame. "The library. I remember the library burning and I wasn't even—"

"Cyrus. Breathe."

The glass pitch took forty minutes and cost Oscar the feeling in eight fingers. He placed each pick and kick with a care he hadn't managed for anything in ten years, and Tam climbed above him the whole way — because the boy had refused, flatly and finally, to go first or wait behind, and had instead found the line himself, reading the weeping seams, calling holds in a whisper: "Left, a hand's width, there — solid. Now right, trust the right, it's thicker than it looks—"

And then the ice spoke.

Not the small complaining voices, but the long tectonic groan, rising, and the whole curtain of the Weeping shuddered like a struck bell. A seam split six feet above Tam's head with a crack like a felled oak, and a plate of ice the size of a barn door sighed, tilted, and let go.

Tam didn't scream. That was the thing Oscar would remember afterward, in the nights when memory ambushed him: the boy didn't scream, didn't freeze. He read the falling plate's path in the half-second it gave him — saw that it would shear the rope anchor and take all three of them into the void — and did the arithmetic no twelve-year-old should be able to do.

He unclipped.

He stepped off his hold onto open air, trusting the rope, and swung himself bodily into the falling ice's path so that his shoulder, not the anchor line, took the glancing edge of the plate. It clipped him spinning, a blow that would have cracked a grown man's skull, and his body slammed the main rope against the rock — and held. The anchor held. The plate boomed away into the depths and shattered on the talus far below like a dropped chandelier.

Tam hung there, swinging, one arm bent wrong at the elbow, white-faced, and said, through his teeth, "Told you. Don't fall."

Oscar climbed the last hundred feet in a state he had no name for. He hauled the boy up over the lip at the top of the pitch, lashed his arm to his chest with a strip of Cyrus's blanket, and for a moment — just a moment, standing on a ledge of ice above three hundred feet of empty blue air — he rested his forehead against the boy's frozen hair, because words had failed him completely and his body had to say something.

"You're not baggage," he heard himself say. Rough. Almost inaudible under the wind. "You understand me? You're not baggage. You're crew."

Tam blinked at him with eyes too bright and too wet, and nodded once, and pretended — being Sefa's kind of stubborn, being Greywater's son — that his face wasn't crumbling.

They gained the rim as the sun broke the eastern ridge, and the whole cleft of the Weeping Stair lit up around them, blue fire from crown to root, beautiful and lethal and theirs. Oscar set Cyrus down gently, gave him watered wine from the flask, watched color crawl a half-degree back into the man's lips.

Below, faint as insects, they could hear Brannoc's camp rousing into fury. Above, the high shelf waited, and beyond it the passes where no colors flew yet — though Oscar, looking north, felt the ember in his chest turn like a compass needle and throb.

Cyrus caught his expression. Even half-dead, the courier missed nothing.

"What is it?" Tam asked.

Oscar worked his ruined fingers inside his gloves. Far off, past the shoulder of the mountain, where the southern sky lay pale and hard as hammered tin, he could almost — not quite — hear them marching.

"Company," he said. "Worse company than Brannoc. We need to move."
