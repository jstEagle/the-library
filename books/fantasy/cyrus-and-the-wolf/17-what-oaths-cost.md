## 17. What Oaths Cost

The dreams did not come as dreams anymore. They came as weather.

Oscar woke on the third night above the tree line with frost on his eyelashes and a name in his mouth that was not his own — *Maerin* — spoken with a child's lisp, the way you'd call a teacher who kept bees and taught letters by carving them into slate. He sat up in the shallow cave they'd cut into the drift, and for three full heartbeats he could smell woodsmoke and honey, could feel a slate stylus small and smooth against fingers that had never held one.

Then the cold came back, and he knew where he was, and worse: he knew where *Cyrus* had been, thirty years before, in a schoolhouse with a slate roof that Cyrus had helped his father shingle when he was nine years old.

"That's not mine," he said to the dark.

Tam stirred in his bedroll near the fire. "What?"

"Nothing. Sleep."

The boy slept. Cyrus did not, and neither, now, could Oscar. He could hear the old man's breathing from across the cave — a wet, careful sound, like water finding its way through stones — and beneath it, fainter than either breath, the other thing: the slow pulse under the mountain, felt through the soles of his boots, through the rock itself, like a second heartbeat laid over his own and slightly out of time. Three days since they'd crossed the high shelf. Three days of the blood-oath opening in him like a door someone had only pretended to lock.

He pulled his cloak around his shoulders and went to sit at the cave mouth, because if the memories were going to come whether he wanted them or not, he would rather meet them looking at something true. Snow. Stars. The black teeth of Thornfell's southern ridge.

They came anyway.

---

It began with the bees.

Not his bees — Cyrus's, or rather Maerin's, the old cartographer-scholar who had found a boy of six hiding in his beehouse after the tax collectors came through Verel Hollow, and had not asked why the boy was hiding, only handed him a comb dripping gold and said, *Hold still and they won't sting you. Fear is what they smell.* And Cyrus had held still. Cyrus had learned, at six years old, the lesson that would run through everything else like a vein through marble: that fear has a scent, and that stillness is its own kind of courage, and that an old man with hives and a slate roof could be more of a father than a father.

Oscar gripped his knees and watched the snow and lived thirty years of another man's life in the space between two gusts of wind.

He saw Maerin teaching him the compass rose by walking him out into a beet field at dusk and turning him three times until he was lost. *Now find north,* the old man said, and the boy cried, and Maerin crouched down and said gently, *Good. Now you understand what a map is worth. A map is what stands between a lost man and weeping.*

He saw the archive — not yet the Emperor's archive then, just the great library at Carthenis where Maerin had gone to restore old charts, taking the boy along as a quill-carrier — and he saw Cyrus, fourteen years old, standing beneath shelves that rose out of lamplight into darkness, and hearing the whisper that ran through the scholars there like a fever: that the dragon-holocaust had not been a war won but a theft committed, and that somewhere in the deepest stacks were charts that proved it.

He saw Maerin die. That memory came last and it came hard, and Oscar made a sound low in his throat without meaning to. Fever, in the end. Thirty years of whispering about forbidden maps, and the empire never touched him — a fever took him, sweating and ramming in a rented room, gripping Cyrus's wrist with surprising strength and saying: *They'll look for it eventually, boy. The heart. They always look. Someone has to find it first, not to use it — just to know. Just so it isn't only the killers who know where it sleeps.*

And young Cyrus, twenty-two and terrified, saying the words back: *I swear it. By my blood, if it comes to that.*

*No,* Maerin had said, and here his voice had gone strange and clear, fever-bright. *Don't swear by your blood unless you mean to pay in it.*

Oscar came back to himself with his face wet and frozen and his chest burning where the oath sat, that knot of heat below his sternum that had answered him once on a cold road and had never stopped answering since.

"He meant to pay in it," Oscar said aloud, to no one. "The stubborn old bastard meant exactly that."

"You're talking to yourself again," said Cyrus, from inside the cave, in a voice worn down to threads. "Or worse — you're talking to me. I can hear you doing it. It's very rude to argue with a dying man while he's still present."

* * *

Oscar crawled back inside. Tam slept on; the boy slept like the dead these days, exhausted down to the marrow by grief and altitude and a rage he didn't yet have vocabulary for. Cyrus was propped against his pack, gray-faced, eyes unnaturally bright in the firelight. The bandage on his side needed changing again. Oscar didn't want to look at it. He changed it anyway.

The wound had stopped pretending to heal. The flesh around the stitch-marks had gone the color of old candle wax, and the smell was wrong — sweetish, bad. Oscar cleaned it in silence, hands steady, the way he'd cleaned a hundred wounds on a hundred bodies, and hated that this one made his hands want to shake.

"You were seeing it," Cyrus said quietly. "Weren't you. The schoolhouse. The bees."

Oscar tied off the bandage harder than he needed to. "Your teacher kept bees."

"Maerin." The name came out of Cyrus soft as a prayer. "You saw Maerin." He closed his eyes. "I wondered how long it would take. The oath was old blood-work, older than me — Maerin's design, actually, though he'd deny it. It wasn't made to carry a map, Oscar. Maps are just ink. It was made to carry a *witness*. Everything I know, everything I've seen, pressed into the blood so it couldn't be killed with the body." He opened his eyes and looked at Oscar with terrible gentleness. "I'm sorry. I should have told you what swearing it would cost. But you'd have said no, and I needed you to say yes."

"You're damn right I'd have said no."

"I know. You wear your no like armor. It's rather impressive, actually. All that iron over so much—"

"Don't."

"—kindness," Cyrus finished, mild as milk.

Oscar sat back on his heels and glared at him, and the glare collapsed halfway into something else, and he turned away and busied himself with the fire because there was nothing in the cave that needed doing and he needed one of those things desperately.

"It's not right," he said to the flames. "You're pouring thirty years of yourself into a man you hired off a cold road. A smuggler. You don't even know me. What if I'm not — what if I can't—" He stopped, jaw working.

"Can't what?"

"*Carry it.*" The word came out rough. "That's what this is, isn't it. You're not giving me a message. You're making me into the vault."

Cyrus was quiet for a long moment. When Oscar glanced back, the old man was watching him with an expression he recognized, suddenly and horribly, from the borrowed memory — Maerin's expression, the exact one, patient and sad and seeing straight through him.

"Yes," Cyrus said. "That is what I'm doing. And here is what you don't understand yet, Wolf, because your god went quiet before anyone taught you this: an oath is not a chain. It doesn't make you into anything you weren't already. It just — takes what's in you and gives it weight enough to stand on." He shifted, wincing. "Why do you think it answered? Ten years of silence, all your little prayers dropping down a dry well, and then one blood-oath on a mountain road and something roars back. Do you imagine that's accident?"

"I don't believe in accident. I believe in contracts."

"You believed in contracts when you carried a stranger up a pass no one survives. Out of contract." Cyrus smiled, thin and cracked. "Face it, Joubert. The well wasn't dry. You just stopped lowering the bucket."

* * *

Sleep took Cyrus near dawn, the drugged shallow sleep of the septic, and Oscar stayed up and let the rest of it come, because fighting it gave him a headache like a nail driven behind the eye, and because — though he'd have cut his own tongue out before saying it — some part of him had begun to *want* the memories. They were all he would have left soon. He understood that the way animals understand weather.

So he sat at the cave mouth and let Cyrus's life pour through him, and it hurt less than he expected, and that was the worst part.

He learned Cyrus's mother, who sang off-key and mended nets and died of the same fever that took Maerin, three years apart, so that Cyrus learned grief twice before he was twenty-five and built his whole self around the second one. He learned the archive at Carthenis from the inside: fifteen years of quiet scholarship, cataloguing, listening, following whispers down through the restricted stacks until the night he finally stood in front of the sealed case in the Emperor's private map-room and found — not a chart of where dragons had *died*, which was what every official record claimed, but a chart of where the eggs had been *gathered*. Where the fire had been carried. Where, in a single line of cramped imperial script, some dead archivist had written: *One clutch unaccounted. One heart presumed retained. See Thornfell annex.*

Presumed retained. As if a heart were a ledger entry. As if the last living fire in the world were a line item.

He learned the theft — and it *was* a theft, done badly, in terror, with a copied key and a forged chit and his own heart hammering so loud he was sure the whole palace heard it. He learned the flight south with the map sewn into his coat lining, the years of running, the safehouses that stopped being safe, the couriers who went out and didn't come back. He learned that Cyrus had spent two decades being hunted by an empire for the crime of knowing where its oldest crime was buried.

And he learned — sitting in the snow with another man's whole life settling into him like silt into a riverbed — why Cyrus had hired a mercenary instead of trusting a friend.

Because there were no friends left. Because everyone who'd ever helped him was dead or bought. Because a man carrying a secret that valuable learns, eventually, that love is just leverage with a warmer name — and so he'd gone looking for someone with no ties at all. A hollow man. A wolf with a silent god and nothing to lose.

And the joke, the cruel beautiful joke, was that the blood-oath had refused to stay in a hollow man. It had gone looking for the kindness Cyrus swore wasn't there, and found it, and rooted in it like ivy in a wall.

"You picked me because I was empty," Oscar muttered. "And then you filled me up. That's not fair, old man. That's cheating."

From inside the cave, in his sleep, Cyrus laughed.

* * *

Midmorning, gray light, the wind dropped for once and the silence rang. Oscar changed the bandage again and did not lie about what he saw, because Cyrus deserved better than lies, and Cyrus looked at the wound himself, dispassionately, the way he must have looked at the archive ledgers, and then looked up.

"Well," he said. "I think we both know the shape of it now."

"Don't." Oscar's voice came out flat and strange. "Whatever speech you've got saved up—"

"No speeches. I'm too tired for speeches; you've seen my twenties, you know I was never eloquent." He fumbled at his collar and drew out the spark.

Oscar had seen it before — a sliver of crystal no longer than his thumb, and within it a light that did not behave like fire or like glass but like something remembering both. Cyrus had shown it to him on the high shelf, explained it in his dry scholar's way: a splinter of the dragon's own flame, taken decades ago, the ember keeping the last heart alive. The thing the whole empire wanted. The thing he'd bled to carry.

Now Cyrus held it out across the space between them, and his hand shook, and the light inside the crystal leaned toward Oscar like a plant toward a window.

"The oath carries the knowing," Cyrus said. "But the knowing isn't the point. This is the point. If I die holding it, it goes dark, and everything — Maerin, the theft, the pass, the boy, all of it — goes dark with it. It needs a keeper. Blood-sworn. Willing." His bright eyes fixed on Oscar. "And I am asking, Oscar Joubert. Not binding. Asking. There's a difference, and I need you to know there's a difference."

The pulse under the mountain beat once, twice, slow as a bell through water, and the oath in Oscar's chest flared to meet it, and he thought — with sudden, blinding clarity — of the schoolhouse, of a boy of nine shingling a roof because his teacher asked, of *don't swear by your blood unless you mean to pay in it.*

He thought: I already paid. On the cold road, the moment I picked you up. Everything since has just been the invoice arriving.

"Not yet," he said hoarsely. "You're not dead yet, and I don't take oaths from living men. Ask me again at the door. You can buy me a drink after."

Something eased in Cyrus's face — relief, maybe, or amusement, or both. "Stubborn wolf."

"Learned from a stubborn owl." Oscar tucked the blanket around him, gruffly, and turned away before his face could do anything embarrassing.

And it was then, in that unguarded half-second, with the oath wide open in his chest and the mountain's pulse rolling up through his boots — that he felt something else arrive on the edge of it. Not the dragon. Not memory.

Down the southern slope, far below the snow line, something moved with purpose through the trees. Many somethings. And threading behind them, faint as smoke, came a scent the blood-oath recognized from Cyrus's nightmares and delivered whole into Oscar's mind:

Imperial steel. Cold-forged, mass-produced, marching in cadence.

Someone was climbing Thornfell.

Oscar stood at the cave mouth and watched the treeline, and the wolf in him — the one he'd sworn was retired — lifted its head and listened, and did not like at all what it counted coming up through the pines.
