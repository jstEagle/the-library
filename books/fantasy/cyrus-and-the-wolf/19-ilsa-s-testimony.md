## 19. Ilsa's Testimony

The fire was small. That was the first thing Ilsa Vane noticed about the way these people lived — they burned small fires, the kind that could be cupped in two hands and smothered in a breath, the kind that made no promise to the dark. In the imperial camps she had served in for eleven years, fires were statements. They roared. They said *we are many, we are fed, we are not afraid of you*. This fire in the lee of a broken watchtower above the Narrows said nothing at all. It only kept four people and one dying man warm, and it asked them to lean close.

She leaned close because her hands were still shaking. She had surrendered at knifepoint eight hours ago, walked beside her own captors through a pass she had personally mapped for the men hunting them, and eaten their salt-pork without asking whether it was poisoned. Her hands had held steady through all of that. They shook now, sitting at a fire, because now was when they would kill her or believe her, and both possibilities were worse than anything that had come before.

Oscar Joubert sat across from her with his forearms on his knees, the way he'd been sitting since they made camp, as though his body had forgotten every other position. The Wolf. She knew the name from guild ledgers and garrison gossip — a mercenary reliable as winter, godless in practice if not in confession, expensive and worth it. What the ledgers did not say was that he looked at people the way he was looking at her now: not hostile, not warm, simply *counting*. Weighing what she cost against what she carried.

Cyrus lay propped against Tam's folded blanket near the tower wall, gray as old tallow, breathing in shallow sips. He had insisted on being moved out of the wind so he could hear this. *If she's telling the truth,* he'd rasped, *I want to hear all of it. If she's lying, I want to watch her do it.* Even dying, the archivist in him wanted the record complete.

Tam sat between Cyrus and the fire, a boy of perhaps thirteen with Greywater's ash still under his fingernails somewhere, holding a whittling knife he hadn't used once since dark. He watched Ilsa the way children watch wolves — fascinated, furious, keeping track of where her hands were.

"Well," Ilsa said. "You want testimony. Sworn testimony is given under seal, before a magistrate, with—"

"We're not the empire," Oscar said quietly. "Nobody here cares about your seals."

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point."

She pulled off her gloves — cartographer's gloves, good leather gone soft at the fingers from years of holding pens — and laid them on her knee. It was a small ritual of hers before difficult work. Bare-handed work. She had learned it from an instructor who said maps should never be drawn by someone afraid to touch the world directly.

"My name is Ilsa Vane. Senior cartographic officer, Third Survey Corps, attached since last spring to the Office of Works." She let the title sit there. "You'll know what the Office of Works builds. Everyone does, eventually. Fire-lances for the border legions. The cistern-burners used at Sarrow. The new engines being tested at Caer Dulac that throw flame half a mile."

"War machines," Tam said.

"War machines," she confirmed. "And every one of them runs on the same fuel. You've seen the burn-vats, maybe, if you've been near a legion camp — the green-gold liquid, the one that keeps burning in rain. Alchemists call it *ignis perpetuum*. Everlasting fire. There is a reason it's called everlasting, and a reason it has become scarce, and those reasons are the same reason."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, snow slid off a branch with a soft sigh.

"The empire hasn't mined a natural vein of it in forty years," Ilsa went on. "What the alchemists brew in the vats is thin. Weak. It gutters in cold, it eats its own containers. The legions have been fighting with lamps running dry and calling it strategy. The Office of Works has spent eleven years and more gold than I can count trying to synthesize what the old world had for free." She paused, and found that the next words came harder than any report she had ever delivered. "Two winters ago, they stopped trying to make it. Because a survey team found something better."

Cyrus made a sound. Not quite a word. Ilsa looked at him — this wreck of a man wrapped in borrowed wool, whose pulse she could almost see beating at his throat — and understood suddenly, completely, why the riders wore no colors. Why the pursuit orders were verbal. Why her own commission papers for this hunt listed her objective as *cartographic reconnaissance, unlisted*.

"You already know," she said softly.

"I stole it from him," Cyrus whispered. It wasn't clear whether he meant the map or the knowledge. Perhaps both. His eyes had gone bright and terrible. "I thought — I thought I was stealing a secret. A location. Something to hide." A wet laugh, hardly a laugh at all. "I was stealing time. Just time."

"Tell them," Ilsa said gently. "Or I will. But you should tell them."

So Cyrus told them, in fragments, his voice fraying like rope dragged over rock: the Emperor's private archive beneath the Winter Palace, three floors down, behind doors that answered to blood-oath; a courier's sabbath leave spent copying what he was never meant to see; a vault record older than the dynasty itself, written by the dragon-hunters' own hands. *One heart remains. It beats beneath Thornfell. Its fire renews.*

"Renews," Oscar repeated. He said it flatly, but Ilsa had spent eleven years reading officers, and she heard the floor drop out of a man's voice even when he kept it level.

"That's the word in the record," Cyrus said. "Dragon-fire isn't fuel the way oil is fuel. It doesn't get consumed. It gets *drawn*. The heart makes more. Forever, or near enough. The hunters learned it too late to matter for the dragons themselves, but they wrote it down, because empires write everything down." He closed his eyes. "I took the map because I thought if the location died with me, the heart would stay hidden forever. Safe. Sleeping."

"And instead," Ilsa said, taking the thread back because he could not carry it, "you taught the empire that you existed. That the map existed. That the heart could be *found*, because a dying clerk had found it first. Every rider on this mountain is here because of that theft. And every engine at Caer Dulac is waiting for it."

Tam stood up. He didn't seem to know he'd done it; his body had simply refused to keep sitting. "Waiting for *what*," he demanded. "Say the rest of it. Say what they're going to do."

Ilsa met the boy's eyes. He had lost a town eight days ago. She was about to hand him the arithmetic of it, and there was no gentle way to hold that sum.

"They're going to drain it," she said. "That is the plan I helped draw the supply lines for. A permanent tap — pipes, siphons, condensers — sunk into whatever chamber holds the heart. The initial yield alone would refuel every fire-engine in the empire for a century. The sustained yield—" She stopped. Made herself continue. "The sustained yield means the Office of Works stops rationing. Means the legions stop counting shots. Means every border war the empire has postponed for lack of fuel becomes affordable again. All of them. At once."

"Forever," Cyrus breathed. "They'd light the world with it."

"They'd light the world with it," Ilsa agreed, "and call it a golden age. There are ministers who genuinely believe that. Who have models showing fewer wars overall, once the empire's supremacy is absolute. Peace through an inexhaustible flame." She laughed, briefly, horribly. "I drew the aqueduct routes. I drew them beautifully. Commendation in my file."

Silence took the camp then, the particular silence of people doing arithmetic with their futures. Snow ticked against stone. The small fire bent and recovered. Ilsa watched Oscar Joubert's face across it and saw nothing move except his thumb, which pressed slowly against the edge of his knee as though testing whether the world still had edges.

It was Tam who broke it, in the way of children, which is to say honestly. "Then we run," he said. "We go around. South, over the—"

"There's no around," Ilsa said. "There is no direction on any map I've ever drawn where this ends. You can outrun soldiers. You cannot outrun a supply decision already funded. If that heart is tapped, hiding the map changes nothing. The empire knows *enough* now. They have my surveys of Thornfell's southern approaches. They have the archive record. All your map ever bought was a head start." She spread her bare hands, palms up, offering them the empty truth. "Cyrus didn't fail to protect the secret. There was never any protecting it. He failed to protect the *dragon*."

Cyrus turned his face toward the tower wall. Ilsa saw his shoulders shake once, twice, and stop, the way grown men cry when they've practiced not crying for decades. Tam went to him and put a hand on his arm, awkward and fierce, and Cyrus covered the boy's hand with his own and held on.

Oscar spoke.

"So that's it." His voice was low, unhurried, the voice of a man laying stones rather than throwing them. "Empire finds the heart. Empire taps the heart. Empire burns brighter for a hundred years and calls the burning peace. And everyone on this mountain — the widow at Hollowpine, the boy, the smugglers at Kestrel Gate, the whole rotten lot of us — we're just weather to that. Something that happens to the landscape while important men build pipes."

"I didn't say it was right," Ilsa said. "I said it was the plan."

"Yeah." He nodded slowly. "You did. And here's what I know about plans." He leaned forward, and the firelight climbed his face, and for a moment Ilsa saw the mercenary the ledgers promised — hard, patient, dangerous in the specific way of men who have survived things by refusing to be surprised by them. "Ten years ago my god stopped answering me. You want to know why? Because I asked him for victory. Over and over, for years. Victory in contracts, victory in fights, victory over other men. And he went quiet, and I decided gods were stories mercenaries tell to sleep at night." His jaw worked. "But lately I've been thinking maybe he didn't go silent because he was dead. Maybe he went silent because I was praying to the wrong thing. Asking the wrong question. Because there was never going to be enough victory. There's never enough of a thing that's meant to be drained."

He pointed one scarred finger at her, and she did not flinch, and some part of her noted that she passed her own test.

"You're telling me the plan is a pump," Oscar said. "A pipe stuck into the last living heart of the oldest fire in the world, bleeding it out forever so some emperor's grandchildren can burn a bigger candle. That's not a plan. That's a wound wearing a crown. And I've killed men for less than what they planned to do to a well I drank from."

"Sentiment," Ilsa said, and heard how hollow it sounded even to her. "The Office of Works has ten thousand soldiers and siege engineers and—"

"Then we don't let them."

Four words. He said them the way he seemed to do everything — without performance, without heat, like a man stating the weather. *Snow tonight. Then we don't let them.* Ilsa opened her mouth to enumerate the impossibilities and found, to her genuine astonishment, that she didn't want to. Eleven years of drafting annexations in beautiful lines, and no one had ever said *no* to her with such simple bookkeeping finality. As if the empire's plan were merely an obstacle between him and a delivery, and obstacles were things the Wolf went through.

"You can't fight the empire," she said anyway, because someone owed the argument to the truth. "Not with five people and two mules."

"Didn't say fight," Oscar said. "Said don't let. Those are different jobs." He glanced toward the darkness beyond the fire, north, where the passes climbed into country even her beautiful maps rendered as blank white guesses. "Different job entirely."

Cyrus lifted his head from the wall. Whatever had broken in him had not stayed broken; something in his face had reassembled around the wreckage, the way a map reassembles around a burn. "The spark," he said hoarsely. "Ilsa. Your surveys. Did they find the door?"

"The door?"

"The vault records speak of a door. Dragon-sworn. Blood-locked." His eyes found Oscar, and held there, and Ilsa watched something pass between the two men that she had no key for — some weight shifting shoulders, some oath changing hands in silence.

"Our surveys mapped the southern approaches," she said carefully. "There were anomalies. Heat signatures in deep winter. Stone that reads warmer than it should, thirty feet below the scree. My superiors called it geothermal venting." She hesitated, then gave him the whole of it, because she had come this far past the edge of treason that further steps cost nothing. "I didn't believe it was venting. Venting cools. This has been warm for eleven years of readings. Something down there is *keeping* warm."

Cyrus smiled. It was the worst smile Ilsa had ever seen — joy and grief fused into one expression, a man watching his life's work arrive at exactly the place he'd tried to prevent it from arriving.

"Keeping warm," he repeated. He reached inside his coat, wincing, and drew out the wrapped bundle Ilsa had glimpsed once on the trail — the map, the fire-inked thing that had gotten a ford burned and a town orphaned and a clerk killed by inches. He did not unwrap it. He only held it against his chest like a child holds a lamp.

"Then it's not too late," he said. "Whatever they've funded, whatever they've surveyed — the door is still shut. And the door doesn't open for armies, Ilsa. It opens for a keeper." His gaze slid to Oscar again, deliberate now, unmistakable. "Or so the record says."

"Keeper," Oscar said flatly.

"The record is vague on particulars." Cyrus's smile turned wry, exhausted, human. "Vague on most things. It was written by frightened men in a hurry."

Tam looked between them all, the whittling knife finally moving in his hands, shaving a curl of wood he didn't look at. "So we go to the door first," the boy said. It wasn't a question. Children decide things; adults deliberate. "Before the pipes. Before the army. We just — get there."

"It's six days' climb," Oscar said, "in winter, with an army sealing the passes behind us and Brannoc's people somewhere ahead of us, and a dying man who can barely sit a mule." He counted it off on his fingers like a ledger. Then he closed the hand. "Which means we leave before dawn."

Ilsa stared at him. "You're deciding *now*? On my testimony? You've known me for eight hours and I was pointing a crossbow at your employer's gate four days ago."

"You gave up your knife at the ford," Oscar said. "Could've kept it. Could've taken Tam hostage in the dark, easy as breathing. Instead you sat down at our fire and told us the worst news you had, bare-handed." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "In my trade that's called paying a toll. Means you get to cross."

"It means I'm a traitor now," Ilsa said. The words came out of her steadier than she expected, and stranger. "Do you understand that? Everything I am — my rank, my pension, my name in the corps rolls — ended at that ford. If your dragon-heart plan fails, there is no version of my life afterward. No return. The Office of Works does not forgive, and it does not forget, and I have seen what it does to people who know too much and choose wrong." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she hated it, and kept talking. "I have burned my maps behind me. Do you hear me? I need you to understand what your four words cost the person hearing them."

The fire settled. Cyrus was watching her with an archivist's tenderness, cataloguing her, adding her to some internal record. Tam had stopped whittling entirely.

"I hear you," Oscar said at last. And then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of comfort, he added: "Cold night to be nameless. Get closer to the fire."

It was absurd. It was the least ceremonial welcome in the history of defections. Ilsa Vane, senior cartographic officer of the Third Survey Corps, traitor to the throne of nine provinces, shuffled sideways through snow to sit nearer a fire the size of two cupped hands — and felt, absurdly, tears start behind her eyes, and blinked them back down with eleven years of practice.

Because that was the thing she had not said aloud, the thing underneath the testimony, the thing that had actually brought her over the ice at the ford with her knife surrendered and her career in ashes: she had seen the requisition numbers. She had drafted the aqueduct routes. She knew, better than anyone alive outside the Office of Works, how much fire the empire intended to draw from that sleeping heart — and how little of it would ever be enough. Drained things always needed draining again. She had spent her whole beautiful career drawing the veins of a machine that ate, and ate, and called its hunger peace.

Somewhere below them, deeper than her instruments could reach, something ancient was keeping warm. Keeping. The word lodged in her like a splinter.

Above the tower's broken parapet, the clouds tore open for a moment and showed stars — the old constellations, the ones Cyrus had been murmuring to Tam about on the high shelf, the ones dragons had once steered by. Ilsa looked up at them the way she looked at blank spaces on a map: territory unclaimed, waiting for whoever got there first.

Dawn was four hours off. Six days of climbing after that.

She began, quietly, to plan the route.

* * *

They left in the blue hour, breath smoking, mules complaining in the low groaning register of animals that know worse is coming. Oscar set the pace and Ilsa walked second, by unspoken agreement — she knew the southern approaches better than any of them, including, she suspected, Oscar himself, and the admission cost her something every time she corrected his line and he took it without pride.

Near midmorning, pausing on a shelf above a frozen cascade, she caught Tam watching her again. Not with hatred anymore, she thought. With the wary arithmetic of a boy recalculating a number he'd already solved once.

"You drew their maps," Tam said.

"Yes."

"And now you're drawing ours."

"Yes."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then: "Are ours better?"

Ilsa thought of the Office of Works, of beautiful annexations, of commendations in her file for aqueducts built to feed engines that would feed wars that would feed hunger that would need feeding forever. She thought of a small fire in the lee of a broken tower that promised nothing and kept everyone warm.

"Yeah," she said. "Ours are better."

Ahead of them, Oscar grunted — the Wolf's entire vocabulary of approval — and turned north, up into the white, toward a door that opened only for keepers, and whatever waited behind it, keeping warm, running out of time.
