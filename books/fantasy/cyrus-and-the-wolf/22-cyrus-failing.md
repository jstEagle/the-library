## 22. Cyrus Failing

Oscar crossed the fire.

It was not a long distance — six paces of scree and trampled snow at the mouth of the hidden door, where the vault's warmth bled up through the stone like a heartbeat under a floorboard — but it felt like the longest walk of his life. Longer than the Narrows. Longer than the Weeping Stair with Cyrus's weight on his back and the ice singing its one thin note beneath them. He had carried the man for weeks without thinking of it as carrying. Now, crossing those six paces, he understood that he had been carrying him since Greywater Ford, and that the carrying was almost done.

Cyrus sat propped against the inner wall where the stone curved toward the sealed door, wrapped in every blanket they owned except the ones on Tam. The firelight did strange things to him. It made him look younger — a scholar's face again, the face from the memories that were not Oscar's and yet lived in him now like tenants who had stopped paying rent and simply moved in: the burning library, the teacher's voice saying *a map is a promise you make to a place*, the child in the archive corridor whispering *don't look up, don't look up*. It also made him look transparent. The light went through him somehow, or seemed to, as if he were already more memory than meat.

"Wolf." He patted the stone beside him. "Sit. You hover like a carrion bird."

"I've been called worse by better."

"No, you haven't." A ghost of the old dry smile. "You've been called worse by *me*, which is different, because I meant it affectionately."

Oscar sat. Up close, he could hear it — the wrongness in Cyrus's breathing, a wet rattle at the bottom of each inhale like water finding its level in a sinking hull. The wound in his side had never truly closed; the fever came and went in tides now, and the tides were winning. His hands, when they lay in his lap, trembled with a fine constant shiver, like the last vibration of a struck bell.

"You should be resting," Oscar said.

"I'll rest soon enough. Permanently, if the stories are true." Cyrus turned his head, and his eyes caught the firelight and held it too long, the way amber holds an insect. "I need to ask you something, and I need you not to answer quickly. I need you to answer the way you do everything else — badly, stubbornly, and only after you've argued with yourself into a corner."

"That's not how I do everything."

"It's exactly how you do everything. I've watched you for two hundred miles. You argue with your own god, and your god is *silent*." He coughed then, a small terrible sound, and pressed the back of his hand to his mouth, and when he took the hand away there was blood on it, dark in the firelight, nearly black. He looked at it with mild interest, the way a man looks at weather. "Ah. There it is. I wondered when we'd get to this part."

Oscar's chest went tight. "Cyrus—"

"Don't. Don't waste breath on the thing we both already know. I've known since Hollowpine. You've known since the Stair. Tam has known since the high shelf, because children always know, they just don't have the words, so they go quiet and watch you eat." He folded the bloody hand closed, deliberately, like shutting a book. "What I need from you is about what comes after. Not me. After."

The dragon-fire in the walls pulsed.

Oscar had learned its rhythms over the days since they'd come down through the door the oath had opened — slow swells of heat through the stone, faint light moving in the deep seams of the rock like dawn behind cloud. The last dragon, guttering in its chamber below, kept alive these eleven years by a single stolen spark carried in a dying man's blood. He could feel it even now, faintly, through the seat of his bones: *aware*. Watching. Listening the way mountains listen.

"Cyrus," Oscar said slowly, "what did you ask the dragon for, down there? When it spoke to you. What did you show it?"

"Nothing it didn't already know." Cyrus's voice was going thinner. "That's the horror of it, Wolf. It has been down there alone for eleven years, feeling my spark like a candle held at arm's length in an endless hall, and it has had nothing to do but *know things*. It knew I was dying before I did. It knew what would happen to the spark." He paused. "It knows what it wants to offer you."

The cold walked up Oscar's spine on small feet.

"Offer me."

"I swore a blood-oath to you on the switchbacks above Greywater Ford. Do you remember what it felt like?"

He remembered. He would remember until he died. Ten years of praying into silence — ten years of the habit of faith with nothing behind it, words falling down a well that never echoed back — and then Cyrus's blood on his palm and the oath rising out of him unbidden, and *something answering*. Not his god. Something older, deeper, stranger. A warmth in the chest like a coal breathed upon. Dread and returned faith tangled together so tightly he still couldn't tell them apart.

"I remember."

"The spark is bound to that oath now. It has been since the vault opened to you — since the lock turned for your blood instead of mine. I stole it, Wolf. I carried it. But it was never waiting for me." Cyrus smiled, and it was the saddest thing Oscar had ever seen on a living face. "It was waiting for a keeper. And the mountain chose one before either of us knew there was a mountain choosing."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned low.

Or rather, Cyrus talked, in the measured way of a man spending his last coins carefully, and Oscar listened, and the heat in the stone rose and fell around them like breathing.

"The spark isn't power," Cyrus said. "Understand that first, because Brannoc will promise you it's power, and the empire will promise you it's power, and they're both lying the way men lie when they've never held anything holy. It's a *life*. One life. The last ember of a fire that covered the sky. It can't burn cities. It can't forge weapons — not honestly, not the way the archivists dreamed. All it can do is what it's doing right now: keep one ancient heart beating, and wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For someone to give it a future." Cyrus's trembling hand found Oscar's wrist — light as a moth landing. "A dragon isn't made of fire, whatever the songs say. A dragon is made of *vow*. That's why they bonded to oath-keepers, once, before the hunting. Why do you think your god went silent, Oscar? Why do you think the oaths stopped answering all across the world, ten years ago?"

Oscar's mouth went dry. "Tell me."

"Because the gods of oaths were never gods. They were echoes. Old vows, worn smooth by ten thousand years of keeping, rising off the world like heat off a road. And when the last dragons began to die, the echoes began to fade — because the first vow ever sworn was sworn *to a dragon*, keeper to fire, fire to keeper, and everything after was built on that foundation." His grip tightened, feather-light and iron-firm at once. "Your god didn't abandon you. Your god *died*, slowly, the way a fire dies, and you've been praying into ash for a decade. But ash remembers heat, Wolf. And there is one heart left."

Below them, through forty fathoms of stone, something vast shifted in its sleep. The seams in the wall glowed, dim gold, and faded.

"And it's asking for you," Cyrus whispered.

* * *

Tam woke near midnight and pretended he wasn't listening.

Oscar saw it in the boy the moment his eyes opened across the dead fire — the careful stillness, the breathing slowed by will, the whole body bent toward their voices like a compass needle. Children spied the way water finds cracks: inevitably, and without malice. Oscar let him. There was no longer anything said in this company that Tam shouldn't hear. That was Sefa's son's inheritance now, poor lad — a war's worth of knowing, gathered young.

He heard Cyrus say: "If you take it, you won't be what you were."

He heard himself say: "I'm not much of anything now."

He heard Cyrus laugh, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing went on too long, and Tam sat up despite himself, and Oscar held a waterskin to the dying man's lips while the boy watched with his jaw set hard against tears.

When it passed, Cyrus lay back against the stone, spent, gray as the ash of the fire, and looked at Oscar with eyes that had gone very clear — the clarity of the dying, who waste nothing.

"There it is," he murmured. "There's the real question. Not whether you *can* take it. Whether you'll survive being it. The spark doesn't sit in a man like a coin in a pocket, Wolf. It unmakes and remakes. Every oath you ever broke will surface. Every oath you ever kept will *burn*. You'll hear vows the way you used to hear wind. You'll feel every promise in a hundred miles, kept and broken, like weather on your skin." He swallowed. "And when I die — and I am going to die, soon, days perhaps — the spark passes to you fully, or it goes out with me, and the last dragon follows it down, and that's the end of the oldest living thing in the world, and the end of any future but the empire's."

Silence. The stone breathed its slow gold breath.

"So that's the choice," Oscar said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Carry the secret or become it."

"You finally understand the job description." Cyrus's smile flickered. "Terrible pay. No contract. Certain death, eventually, of a kind no mercenary ever trained for." Then the smile fell away, and he reached up with both shaking hands and took Oscar's face between them, and his palms were hot — hotter than fever, hot as a stone left in summer sun, the spark rising in him now like a tide coming in. "I chose you on the road because you were the only soul in sight. I'll not pretend fate guided my bleeding body to the one honest man in Thornfell. But the *mountain* chose you. The lock turned for you. And in all the years I served the archive, in all the vaults and ledgers and lies, I learned one true thing: the fire does not choose badly. It cannot afford to. It has no second chances left."

"Cyrus—"

"*Listen.*" The word came out with heat behind it, literally — his breath stirred the air like a forge-door opening. "You have spent ten years believing your loyalty was hollow because nothing answered it. I have spent ten years watching the empire turn everything living into fuel and calling it order. Between us we have one chance to prove that a vow can still mean something. Not a god's vow. A *man's*. Yours." His thumbs pressed against Oscar's cheekbones, gentle as a benediction. "Take the spark. Keep the heart beating. Give it time — a year, ten years, however long a guttering flame can be coaxed — and let it make what it needs to make. Let there be one more egg in the world. Let there be one more *vow* in the world. And when the empire comes, and it will come, stand on this mountain with the people who chose you and mean it."

Oscar could not speak. The memories that were not his rose in him unbidden — Cyrus at nine years old, copying constellations by candlelight; Cyrus at twenty-five, standing in the Emperor's archive with a stolen spark cupped in his hands like a bird's heart, hearing the door locks begin to turn; Cyrus on the cold road, bleeding, seeing a wolf-shaped shadow come down the switchback and thinking, *there, that one, that one will do* — and underneath all of it, older than all of it, something vast and patient turning over in its sleep beneath the stone, regarding him through forty fathoms of rock with an attention like sunrise.

*Well, keeper,* said the mountain, in a voice that was not sound. *Will you?*

Not command. Not plea. An offering, laid open on the stone between them like a blade hilt-first.

Carry the secret, and deliver it to whoever paid, and live out his years as he had lived the last ten — hollow, competent, praying into ash. Or become it. Be remade. Burn.

"I'm not a good man," Oscar said hoarsely. It mattered to him, suddenly, desperately, that the dragon know this. That Cyrus know it. "I've killed for coin. I broke an oath once, early, to save my own skin, and I told myself the god was silent anyway so what did it matter. I executed a man three weeks ago and my hands didn't shake until after. If you pour yourself into that—"

"—then I pour myself into exactly what the fire has always chosen," Cyrus said softly. "Not saints. *Keepers.* Saints are for altars. Keepers are for doors." He slid his hands down and took Oscar's right hand — the oath-hand, the scarred palm where dried blood still sat in the lines like ink in a woodcut — and turned it over, and looked at it a long moment.

Then he looked up, and his eyes were full of the firelight, and behind the firelight, faintly, unmistakably, something else glowed. Gold, deep, banked. The spark rising to meet its ending.

"I'm going to die at dawn," Cyrus said, conversationally, terribly calm. "I can feel the tide now. I wanted you to have the night to decide, but I find I don't have the strength to wait for your answer, so I'll ask it plain, the way I should have asked everything from the beginning:

"Oscar Joubert. Wolf of no god, keeper of no altar, the man the mountain chose. Will you take what I carry? Will you keep the last fire of the world alive? Will you swear it — not to a silent god, not to me, but to the heart beneath this stone, aloud, with your own voice, so that the vow has somewhere to *live*?"

The stone beneath them pulsed. Once. Twice. Like a heart counting along.

Above the hidden door, in the seam of the rock, the gold light brightened — and held — and waited.

And Oscar, who had spent ten years speaking into silence, opened his mouth to answer.
