## 23. Tam's Question

The vault breathed.

Tam had learned to hear it in the three days since they'd come down through the hidden door — a slow swell and release in the dark, like the chest of something enormous sleeping just beyond the stone. At first he'd thought it was his own heart, too loud in the silence. Then he'd lain awake on the cold floor with his ear pressed flat against rock older than any kingdom, and counted. Sixty breaths of stone for every one of his.

He was learning to count a lot of things now. Rations, mostly. Ilsa had divided what remained into small cloth squares, one for each of them each day, and Tam had watched her hands do it with the precision of someone who had once drawn lines on maps that decided which villages burned. His square was smaller than Oscar's. He hadn't complained. He'd earned less; that was how it worked among wolves, and whatever else these people were, they were wolves who had chosen not to bite each other.

Mostly.

He sat now in the antechamber where Cyrus lay, because someone had to sit with him, and everyone else was busy being useful. Sefa wasn't here yet — she'd come up from Hollowpine with the last supply train two days ago and had immediately begun reorganizing their stores as though disorder were a personal insult. Ilsa was at her listening post near the outer door, copying down signal-codes by candle stub. And Oscar —

Oscar was standing at the mouth of the passage that led deeper in, the way they weren't allowed to go, the way only he could go now. He'd been standing there a long time. The dragon-fire in his veins gave off a light that wasn't quite light, a warmth you saw rather than felt, gold at the edges of him like a coal remembering being a fire. Tam had seen men lit by torches and men lit by bonfires and once, from the ridge above Greywater, men lit by the burning of everything they owned. This was different. Oscar didn't cast shadows so much as soften them.

Tam got up. His legs were stiff from sleeping on stone, and he was hungry in the dull way that had stopped feeling like hunger and started feeling like weather. He crossed the antechamber and stood beside the big man, close enough to feel the heat coming off him like a forge banked for the night.

"You're doing it again," Tam said.

"Doing what?"

"Talking to it without talking."

Oscar didn't look down at him. "You can tell?"

"Your lips move. Just barely. Like when Sefa prays." Tam paused. "Except she prays out loud, and you pray like you're ashamed of it."

That earned him a look — half warning, half something wearier and more honest underneath. "Careful, boy."

"I'm careful. That's all I am anymore." Tam wrapped his arms around his knees and lowered himself against the wall, and after a moment Oscar did the same, settling with his back to the ancient stone like any tired man resting between chores. Up close, the wolf-smell of him — leather, tallow, old blood washed and rewashed — was layered over something else now, something mineral and hot, like the air inside the mountain. Like summer lightning.

Below them, faint through fathoms of rock, the breathing swelled and fell.

* * *

Tam had killed a man nine days ago.

He still measured time that way. Before, and nine days since. He knew Ilsa thought about it differently — she'd told him once that soldiers counted forward, not back, or they drowned — but Ilsa had been made a soldier by an empire that trained its children carefully. Nobody had trained Tam. He'd been made this by four riders without colors and a burning ford and a crossbow bolt he'd loosed with shaking arms from behind a dead mule.

The bolt had gone into the man's throat. Not his chest, not his eye — his throat, because Tam had aimed high without meaning to, the way children aim high because they are small and everything is above them. The man had dropped his sword and put both hands around the wound, as if he could hold himself in, and he'd looked at Tam. Not angry. Surprised. That was the part that lived behind Tam's eyes now, that came visiting when the candles went out: not the blood, not the noise, but the surprise. As if until the very last moment the man had genuinely believed nothing bad would happen to him today.

Oscar had held him afterward while he shook apart. Hadn't said *it gets easier*, which Tam later understood was a mercy, because it didn't. Hadn't said *you did what you had to*, which would have been true and useless. He'd just held on, one huge hand cradling the back of Tam's skull like Tam was something breakable, and let him scream into the wool of his coat until there was nothing left in him but hiccups and the taste of salt.

Then he'd said: "Come on. There's water. We'll wash your hands."

And that was all. And somehow that had been exactly enough.

* * *

"Cyrus is worse," Tam said, when the silence had gone soft enough to hold words again.

"Yes."

"He talks to people who aren't there. Yesterday it was his teacher. The day before, a girl. He called her Wren."

"He'll talk to us too, if we listen right." Oscar's voice was low and even. "You don't correct a man walking through a dream. You walk alongside him a little way, so he isn't alone."

"Is that what you do? When you dream about your god?"

The question landed harder than Tam had intended. He heard it land — heard the small change in the rhythm of Oscar's breathing — and almost took it back, and then didn't, because taking things back was a luxury and also a kind of lie, and Tam had sworn to himself in the ashes of Greywater that he was done lying to be comfortable.

"My god," Oscar said at last, "isn't in my dreams. My god is nowhere at all. That was the whole problem with him." A pause. The stone breathed. "But yes. I talk to the silence anyway. Out of habit. You know what habit is? It's a prayer the body keeps saying after the soul gives up."

"That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Good. You're twelve. If that's the saddest thing you ever hear, you'll have had a better life than most."

"I'm thirteen."

"Congratulations. It gets worse."

Tam laughed — surprised by it, the sound cracking out of him before he could decide whether he was allowed. Somewhere below them the great slow lungs of the mountain drew in another breath, and he felt the laugh get pulled down into the dark and kept there, like a coin dropped into a well that wanted it.

* * *

He should ask now. He'd been carrying the question for three days, turning it over the way you turn a river-stone, looking for the angle. He'd asked Sefa first, sort of — he'd asked her why she'd come, why a widow with a warm house and a full larder had packed a mule and walked into a war, and she'd said, "Because my husband died of a fever in a warm bed, boy, and I've never trusted comfort since," which was an answer, but not to his question. He'd asked Ilsa, and Ilsa had given him a soldier's answer, clean and cold: protection, always, because revenge spends and protection saves. But Ilsa had also defected from an empire rather than draw one more line on one more map, and that didn't sound like a woman whose arithmetic had ever balanced.

So it had to be Oscar. Because Oscar had done both. Tam had watched him do both. Revenge, in the Narrows, with the traitor's blood steaming on the snow — quick and terrible and necessary, and Oscar had vomited afterward behind the rocks where he thought no one saw. Protection, every day since, in a hundred small unglamorous ways: mending Tam's boot, teaching him which gripes and grumbles of the mountain meant safe and which meant run, standing watch so the boy could sleep. Both scales. Both pans loaded.

And now the dragon was dying, and the choice was coming — Tam could feel it the way you feel weather in an old scar. Everyone in the vault could feel it. The air itself had gone taut, like a bowstring, like the last hour before a battle. Whatever the dragon asked its keeper, the keeper would have to answer soon, and Tam needed to know, before the answering, what kind of man was going to do it.

"Oscar." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. He cleared his throat and tried again, planting his feet the way Cyrus had taught him to plant them before hard words. "When the riders came to Greywater. When they — when they burned it. Did you want to kill them?"

"Yes."

"And did you?"

"Some."

"All of them?"

"No." Oscar turned his head then, finally, and looked at him, and his eyes in the ember-light were the color of tea left too long in the pot. "Two got away. Down the eastern trail, before we reached the ford. I chose to stay with Cyrus instead of following. He was dying on the switchbacks and every minute mattered, and I stood there at the fork in the road and did the sums, and I chose him." A muscle moved in his jaw. "I've told myself it was the right choice about four hundred times. It doesn't take. Some mornings I wake up already riding east in my head, chasing those two men down a trail I couldn't find with ten years to look."

"What happened to them?"

"Nothing. That's the answer. Nothing happened to them. They rode home and slept in beds and probably ate eggs." His voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing — it stayed level as a frozen lake, and you could hear the current under it. "And the people of Greywater are ash and crows, and no sum I've ever done makes those two facts balance. So don't ask me if I wanted to kill them. Ask me what I'd trade to have done it. That's the real question, and I'll give you the honest answer: anything. Anything except the ones I'm charged to keep. And that's not virtue, Tam. That's just a smaller cage."

* * *

The candle in the antechamber guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

Tam looked at his own hands, folded in his lap, the knuckles scabbed, the nails bitten to the quick. Nine days since. A lifetime since. He thought about the man's surprised face and about the eggs the two escaped riders were presumably eating and about his mother, whom he had not been able to protect and could not avenge, because the riders who fired her street had been smoke and shouting and then gone, dissolved into the general burning, addressless. Revenge needed a face. Protection needed only a door. That was the difference, maybe. That was why Ilsa chose doors.

"Here's what I keep snagging on," Tam said slowly, working it out loud because working it out loud was the only way he'd ever worked anything out. "Everyone keeps telling me revenge is empty. Sefa says it. Ilsa says it. Even Brannoc's men used to say it, back when they were pretending to be good people. 'It won't bring them back.' Right? That's the whole argument. It won't bring them back." He looked up. "But neither does protection. Nothing brings anyone back. That's not a reason to choose one over the other, it's just — it's true of everything. It's true of being alive. So the argument is rotten, and everyone keeps using it anyway because it sounds wise, and I'm sick of it."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. "How old did you say you were?"

"Thirteen."

"Hells." The big man rubbed a hand down his face. "Cyrus is going to be insufferable about you when he wakes up. Assuming he wakes up. Assuming any of us—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Tam watched him fold the future up and put it away somewhere, the way he'd seen him do a dozen times, the way men did when hope was a ration too.

"So answer me properly," Tam pressed. "Revenge or protection. Which matters more?"

"No."

"No what?"

"No, I won't answer it properly, because there isn't a proper answer, and if I invent one for you it'll be a lie with a ribbon on it, and you deserve better than ribboned lies." Oscar shifted, and the ember-glow along his arms brightened briefly with the movement, throwing their doubled shadows huge against the vault wall — a man-shape and, for just a heartbeat, something winged. "You want the honest answer? Here it is. I don't know. Ten years a mercenary, forty-odd men buried by my hand or bought by it, one god gone quiet, one oath burning in my chest like a swallowed coal — and I don't know. Revenge feels like justice for about as long as it takes to do it. Protection feels like love right up until it fails, and it fails, eventually, always, because you can't stand between the people you care about and death forever; death has all the time in the world and you don't. So both of them fail, boy. You've watched both fail. You told me so yourself. You've got more honesty at thirteen than most generals manage in a lifetime."

"Then what do you choose?" Tam's voice cracked on it. "If they both fail — if it's all just failing — what do you *choose*?"

And Oscar was silent so long that Tam thought he wouldn't answer at all, that he'd retreated behind that wall of weary competence the way he retreated behind everything, and Tam was already composing the bitter little speech he'd deliver to Ilsa about grown men and their cowardice —

But then Oscar spoke, and his voice had changed. Quieter. Rougher. Like a door opening on a room nobody had entered in years.

"When I was younger than you, I swore my first oath. To Vaelus, god of oaths. Kneeling in a shrine that smelled of tallow and wet sheep, because my village couldn't afford incense." He almost smiled. "And the priest told me what oaths were for. He said: an oath is how a small person becomes large. You bind yourself to something bigger than your fear, and it carries you past the fear. And I believed him. I built my whole life on it. And then Vaelus stopped answering, and I found out what I'd actually been kneeling to all along — not a god. A habit. A shape I'd cut for myself and called holy." He looked at his hands, at the faint gold threading the old scars. "So here's what I choose, Tam. Not revenge — revenge is looking backward, and backward is where the dead live, and the dead don't need us. Not even protection, not exactly, because protection is a wall, and walls get broken; you've seen the walls break. What I choose is *forward*. Building. Feeding people. Teaching a boy the grips of a knife and hoping to every silent god he never needs them. Keeping a dying stranger company on a road he begged me not to take, because he asked, and because asking is the bravest thing there is." His eyes came up, ember-bright. "Revenge burns what hurt you. Protection guards what you have. But building — building makes something that wasn't there before, something the world can't take back by taking it away, because it's already in you. In Sefa's soup. In Cyrus's stars. In your stubborn questions. The empire can burn every map and every mountain and it will never unmake the fact that we fed each other. Do you understand? That's the only victory I believe in anymore. Everything else is arithmetic."

Tam stared at him.

"That's not an answer," he said finally. "That's a speech."

A short, rusty laugh broke out of Oscar, startling them both. "Hells' teeth. So it is. Cyrus is rubbing off on me." He sobered. "No. It's not an answer. I told you — there isn't one. There's just what you do next, and then what you do after that, until the doing adds up to something. That's all anybody's ever managed."

"But if the dragon asks you—"

"Tam."

"If the dragon asks you to spend it," Tam insisted, leaning forward, urgent now, the question he'd carried for three days finally tearing free of its wrapping, "to burn the army, all of them, every imperial down to the camp-followers and the mule-drivers — to make Greywater *even* — would you do it? Yes or no. Not a speech. Not a philosophy. Yes or no."

The vault breathed. Once. Twice. Three times.

And Oscar Joubert, the Wolf, keeper of the fire, looked at the boy from the burned town with an expression Tam had never seen on him before — stripped bare, no competence over it, no cynicism, just a man standing at the edge of his own answer and finding no ground beneath it.

"I don't know," he whispered.

Tam exhaled. Something in his chest both broke and settled, the way a bone sets wrong but sets. Because that was the thing, wasn't it. The honest thing. No one knew. Sefa didn't, Ilsa didn't, Brannoc certainly didn't, and the empire least of all — the empire only pretended, loudly, in gold lettering. Everyone was guessing in the dark, holding their little candles, choosing and failing and choosing again.

Nobody knew. And still they had to choose.

"Okay," Tam said quietly. "Okay. I believe you."

He rose, stiff-legged, and started back toward the antechamber — toward Cyrus, toward the candle, toward whatever small task would fill the next hour and add itself, invisibly, to the sum.

Behind him, Oscar spoke one more time, so softly the stone nearly kept it.

"Boy."

Tam turned.

"When the time comes — and it's coming, I can feel it in the fire, in my teeth — I may need someone to remind me what I just said. Someone young enough to remember it straight." The ember-light flickered across the big man's face. "Will you do that? Will you hold me to it?"

Tam thought about the surprised dead man's face. About eggs in a bed far from the burning. About Sefa's soup, and Cyrus's constellations, and a stone egg in the deep dark that quickened, they said, only for trust.

"Yeah," he said. "I will. That's a promise."

And far below them, through sixty breaths of ancient rock, the last dragon stirred in its sleep — and the whole mountain seemed, for a single instant, to lean in and listen.

From the listening post by the outer door, Ilsa's voice rang out, sharp and low: "Signal fires on the southern ridgeline. Three of them. They've found us."
