## 24. Brannoc at the Door

The mountain told me before the scouts did.

I felt it through the soles of my boots first — a tremor too small to be rockfall, too rhythmic to be anything alive that walked on two legs. Then the dragon's heart, guttering somewhere beneath us in the dark like a coal wrapped in ash, pulsed once against the inside of my skull and said, in that voice that was not a voice: *They come.*

I had been standing at the inner door for an hour by then, watching Cyrus sleep. He slept more than he woke now, and each waking cost him something I could see leaving him — color first, then weight, then the sharpness of his eyes, as if the spark inside him were eating him from the edges inward to keep itself lit. He lay on Ilsa's bedroll with Tam's cloak tucked around his shoulders, and his breathing made a sound like wind through a keyhole.

*How many?* I asked the dark. I was getting used to talking to it. That frightened me more than the question.

*A hundred and forty feet,* the heart answered, in the way it answered everything — not words exactly, but pressure, image, certainty. *And iron. And one who smells of old smoke and broken promises.*

Brannoc.

I closed my eyes and let myself feel it properly for three breaths: the cold drop in the stomach, the animal urge to run, the old mercenary arithmetic that said *one keeper, one dying courier, a boy, a traitor to the empire, and a door between you and a hundred men.* The arithmetic came out wrong no matter how I set it down. There was no column where we survived this by fighting, and none where we survived it by running. The high shelf behind us was a graveyard of ice. The Weeping Stair was watched — Brannoc's men had found us once through it; they would be watching it now.

Which left the door.

I turned to look at it. It didn't look like a door. It looked like the end of a corridor, a wall of grey stone veined with something darker, and if you didn't know — if the oath weren't burning in your chest like a swallowed coal — you'd have put your shoulder against it and pushed until your shoulder broke. Only when I laid my palm flat against it did the heat come through, faint but unmistakable, the warmth of a living thing sleeping under stone. The last of the dragon's heat, Cyrus had called it. The spark he'd stolen twenty years ago from a vault full of sleeping gods, carried in his blood all this way, spent now in trickles to keep the great heart below from going out entirely.

There wasn't much left. I could feel that too, the way you can feel how much candle is left by cupping your hand near the flame.

"Oscar." Cyrus's voice, cracked and thin. He was awake. He was always awake now at the worst moments, as if dying had made him unable to miss anything. "How many?"

"A hundred and forty. Give or take."

"And the guild?"

"Smells of old smoke," I said. "That's Brannoc."

He tried to sit up and failed, and Tam — who had been sitting sharpening a knife he had no business sharpening, a boy's knife with a chipped edge — dropped it and caught him under the arms. Between them they got him upright against the wall, and he waved off further help with the imperious little gesture I'd learned meant *I am not finished*, which in Cyrus's case was both a statement about the moment and about his whole life.

"The inner door," he said. "You'll need to seal it."

"I know."

"You don't." He coughed, wet and long, and Tam's hand curled into a fist at his side. "Sealing it costs heat. Heat is fire. Fire is the spark. Every ounce you pour into that lock is an ounce taken from—" he gestured vaguely downward, at the floor, at the mountain's bones, at the ancient guttering thing beneath them, "—from her. Do you understand? You will be choosing how much life she has left. A week or a year. Maybe a decade. Maybe only days."

The words settled over all of us like snowfall. Ilsa, who had been at the corridor mouth keeping watch with an imperial spyglass she'd kept through her defection like a talisman, lowered it slowly. Tam looked from Cyrus to me with that expression he wore lately — the one that was trying very hard to be a soldier's face and kept failing into a child's.

"How much does a door weigh?" Tam asked quietly. "In fire?"

Nobody answered him. Nobody could.

* * *

They came up the last switchbacks at dusk, and I watched them from the shelf above the vault mouth because there was nothing else useful to do and because some part of me — the part that had been a Wolf for twenty years before it was ever anything else — needed to see them with my own eyes.

Brannoc's guild came first, as was proper; they knew the mountain better than the empire ever would. Forty-odd men and women in mismatched leathers, mules left tethered far below, climbing with the economical silence of people who had robbed caravans on worse slopes than these. I picked out faces I'd drunk with, bled with, split tolls with at the Kestrel Gate. Vess, who owed me her left arm. Old Hask, who taught me which ice holds and which sings. They moved like people doing a job they didn't love, and that was something, and it wasn't enough.

Behind them, spaced deliberately apart like oil and water pretending to mix, came the empire.

A vanguard company in slate-grey, banners furled — no point flying colors on a covert operation, though the cut of their armor shouted *imperial* louder than any silk could have. A hundred strong, maybe more, moving with drill-yard precision up terrain that should have slowed them to a crawl. And at their head, riding no horse but walking as if the mountain owed him passage, a tall officer in a half-mask of blackened steel, and beside him —

Beside him, wrapped in furs, hands bound in front of her like a prisoner being displayed rather than restrained, a woman I recognized even at four hundred yards.

Sefa.

My knees went soft. I crouched lower among the rocks and made myself breathe. They hadn't burned Hollowpine after all — or they had, and taken her alive, and marched her up here as leverage, as a question mark shaped like a widow. She stumbled once on the scree and a guildsman steadied her, roughly, and she shook him off with a motion I knew well, the motion of a woman who had buried a husband and a village's worth of neighbors and refused to be handled.

"They have Sefa." Ilsa had come up beside me without a sound. She did that. Ten years of imperial cartography had taught her to read ground the way I read weather, and she'd learned my habits fast enough to find me anywhere. Her spyglass was steady in her hands. "She's bait. They won't hurt her while the door stays shut. The moment it opens—"

"I know what she is."

"Do you?" Ilsa turned to look at me, and in the failing light her eyes were very pale and very tired. "Because I've stood in war-councils, Oscar. I've written the orders. Bait stops being bait the moment it becomes inconvenient. If that door doesn't open by tomorrow's dawn watch, they will kill her where your people can see it, and then they will start on the guildsmen, one at a time, until Brannoc either breaks the door or breaks ranks. That's not cruelty. That's schedule."

Below, the two forces had reached the flat shelf before the vault mouth and were making camp with the wary choreography of allies who trusted each other exactly as far as the next hour. Guild fires on the west side. Imperial fires on the east. Between them, a strip of trampled snow nobody's boots would cross. Brannoc himself stood at the center of it all, hands on hips, looking up at the blank cliff face where the hidden door hid behind its glamour of ordinary stone, and even at this distance I could see him talking, gesturing, arguing with the masked officer — and losing, from the set of his shoulders.

"He sold us," Tam said. He'd climbed up after us, silent as only a Greywater orphan could be silent, and now he knelt in the scree with his chin nearly on his fists. "He sold us and now he's sorry."

"People are allowed to be sorry," I said.

"It doesn't help."

"No," I admitted. "It usually doesn't."

We watched a while longer. Then Cyrus's voice came up the corridor behind us, thin but carrying, the voice of a man who had spent his life making himself heard in rooms where hearing him meant death:

"Come inside. All of you. We have a door to argue about, and I intend to win the argument before I lose anything else."

* * *

The argument lasted most of the night, and I lost it twice before I understood I'd already won it, and that winning it was the worst thing that had happened to me since my god went silent.

Cyrus wanted to seal the door fully. Pour everything. Burn the last of the dragon's spare heat — the heat that kept her alive through the winter, the margin between guttering and gone — and fuse the inner door into solid stone, a seal no force short of another dragon could break. Buy years. Let the empire go home empty. Let the heart below sleep on, safe, starving, alone, until the world forgot Thornfell again.

"You'd kill her," I said. "Slowly. To save her quickly."

"I'd keep her *hers*," Cyrus snapped, and then coughed, and then went on in a whisper, because whispering was all he had left. "Oscar. Listen to me. I have carried this spark for twenty years. I have watched it eat me alive from the inside, and I have never once regretted it, because regret requires imagining a different choice, and there isn't one. But I know what it costs. I am the cost. And if you spend her reserve on a door, you are spending her the way the empire would spend her — as fuel. Just for a kinder purpose."

"And if we don't," Ilsa said quietly, "they breach the outer vault in a day. Two. I've seen imperial sappers work. The outer mouth is defensible for exactly as long as it takes them to stop being polite about it. Then they're in the corridor, and the inner door is all that's left anyway, except by then they'll have Sefa and forty hostages and time, and time is the one thing a siege always buys the besieger."

"So we open it," Tam said. Everyone turned. The boy sat with his back against the wall, knees up, the chipped knife across his thighs. "We open it and give them the map and let them drain her and the war comes and everyone dies anyway. No. What Oscar said before. We fight at the outer mouth. We have the corridor. One man can hold a corridor."

"One man can hold a corridor for about nine minutes against a hundred," Ilsa said, not unkindly. "I've done the math. It's my job to do the math."

"Then do different math!"

The boy's voice cracked on it, and the crack went through all of us. He was fourteen. He had killed one man, eleven days ago, and still woke screaming some nights, and here he was asking to stand in a doorway and die, because the alternative was watching everything burn again the way Greywater had burned. I crossed the room and put my hand on his head, and he let me, which he almost never did anymore.

"Here's the math I have," I said, to all of them, because I'd been turning it over since dusk and it was time to say it out loud and hate it. "We can't hold the outer vault. We can't abandon it, because abandoning it means they reach the inner door with time and tools and Sefa's blood already spilled. So we do neither. We make the outer vault cost them. Corridor fighting, Ilsa's spoofed signals, falling rock, bad light, every trick this mountain has. We make a hundred men think they've bitten into a hornet's nest. And while they're bleeding for inches, I seal the inner door — partially. Not everything. Enough to hold against sappers for weeks instead of hours, but leave her enough reserve to live through the winter. A door that costs her a season, not a life."

"And if it's not enough?" Cyrus asked.

"Then it's not enough, and we die in front of it, and they still have to dig through a door sealed by a sworn keeper, which takes longer than any of them want to stay on this mountain." I looked at him. He looked back, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not approval exactly. Recognition. The look of a man seeing his own stubbornness wearing someone else's body. "You made me swear to deliver the secret, Cyrus. You never made me swear to deliver it intact. Keeping her alive *is* delivering it."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying at the outer mouth, far away, and Cyrus's ruined breathing, and underneath it all — so faint only I could hear it — the slow pulse of the heart below, waiting to learn what we would spend.

"All right," Cyrus said at last. "All right. But hear me, Wolf. When the moment comes, you'll feel her through the lock. You'll feel exactly what you're taking. Don't flinch from it and don't drown in it. A keeper spends. That's the whole office. Everything else is commentary." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the switchbacks above Greywater Ford, bloodied and certain. "Gods. Of all the smugglers on all the roads, I bled on yours."

"Statistically inevitable," I said. "I walk everywhere."

Tam laughed — a short, startled bark, surprised out of him — and Ilsa almost smiled, and for one breath the five of us in that stone room were something I had no name for yet, though I'd be given one soon enough.

* * *

At dawn I went down to the outer vault mouth to talk to Brannoc, because someone had to, and because I was the only one whose face he might still remember fondly enough to hesitate over.

They let me come to the line of trampled snow and no farther. Four crossbows tracked me from the imperial side; six more from the guild's, held by men who wouldn't meet my eyes, which told me they'd been told stories about what waited behind the hidden door, and believed them. Brannoc came forward alone, as I'd asked, and up close he was worse than I'd feared: not hardened, but hollowed. He'd aged ten years in ten days. His famous ledger-keeper's neatness was gone; his coat was buttoned wrong.

"Wolf," he said.

"Chief."

"You look terrible."

"You should see the other side of the mountain." I let the silence stretch. Below us, the valley smoked with morning mist; somewhere down there, mules stamped and men who thought themselves heroes shivered over cook-fires. "Forty guildsmen, Brannoc. I counted. You brought forty of your own people up a death-march to stand in the snow next to a hundred imperials who will use every one of them as a ladder over the wall the moment it's convenient. What did they promise you?"

"The same thing I promised them." He didn't flinch. Credit where due. "A seat at the table when the fire-weapons come. A guild charter signed in imperial ink. No more smuggling, Oscar. *Trade.* Legitimate. Generations of it. My grandchildren fat and lawful."

"You don't have grandchildren."

"I want the *option*." And there it was, the old Brannoc, the man who'd fed me and paid me and lied to me for fifteen years, his eyes wet and hard at once. "It's a heart in a jar, Wolf. That's all it is. One heart. They drain it, they make their weapons, the war happens or it doesn't — wars happen anyway, you know that better than anyone — and the guild eats for a century. Against that you offer me what? A dying courier, a runaway mapmaker, a widow's boy, and a door that opens for nobody?"

"For a vow," I said.

The word landed between us like a dropped coin. Brannoc stared at me, and I watched him remember — he had to remember — the years when the Wolf of the Kestrel Gate had prayed to his oath-god before every contract, and the years after, when he'd stopped, and how Brannoc had joked about it at the toll-tables: *the god finally came to his senses.* He opened his mouth. Closed it. Behind him, the masked officer was watching us with the stillness of a man reading correspondence over a shoulder.

"Step aside, Joubert," Brannoc said quietly. "Or be stepped on. I'm telling you as a friend, because I haven't got any others left."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm telling you as one: when this goes wrong — and it will go wrong, for somebody, today or tomorrow — pick a side fast, and pick the one that bleeds for its people instead of selling them. You've chosen poorly your whole life, old man. You get one more choice. Make it count."

I turned and walked back up the slope with crossbow bolts tracking my spine the whole way, and the mountain hummed under my boots like a struck bell, and far below, patient as winter, the last heart in the world beat on — and waited to see what I would spend.
