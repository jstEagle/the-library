## 25. The Siege of the Vault

The dark down here was not the dark of night. Night had edges — a horizon, a moon, the grey promise of morning. This dark was total, a darkness with weight and temperature, and Ilsa Vane learned its texture in the first six hours by counting her own heartbeats against it.

They had sealed the inner door on the third bell of what she would later learn to call the first day, because down here days were inventions, acts of collective will. Oscar Joubert had pressed his palm flat against the stone until the dragon-fire in his veins answered, and the great slab had ground shut with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat. After that: silence, and seven people, and air that tasted of cold iron and old stone and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke that came from nowhere.

She catalogued them the way she'd been trained to catalogue terrain. Oscar, the Wolf, sitting with his back against the door as if his spine were a second bolt. Tam, twelve years old and trying not to shake, curled beside a dead mule's pack with his arms around his knees. Cyrus, dying by inches on a bedroll of saddle blankets, breathing in a rhythm Ilsa recognized from field hospitals — the long pauses between breaths growing longer each time, like a man walking farther and farther from a fire he meant to return to. And Sefa — no. Sefa was still at Hollowpine. That left the smugglers: Vetch, who talked too much when he was afraid; Dunmore, who didn't talk at all; and Brannoc's man Corl, whose eyes kept sliding toward the sealed door with an expression Ilsa had seen before, on soldiers deciding whether today was the day they deserted.

And herself. Imperial cartographer, Third Survey Corps, defector of four days' standing. Surrendered to a mercenary's knife because she had read the vault records and understood what the empire intended to do with the last dragon's heart, and because she could no longer be party to arithmetic that counted millions of lives as fuel.

*Traitor*, said the voice in her head that wore her old commanding officer's face. She told it, as she had told it every hour since Greywater Ford, that treason was simply loyalty pointed in a truer direction, and then she got up, because lying still made the voice louder.

"We need light discipline," she said into the blackness. "And we need to talk about water."

Someone laughed — Vetch, high and thin. "We need to talk about getting *out*."

"Out is up," Oscar said quietly. "Up is where they are."

Ilsa struck one of her three remaining matches — imperial issue, waterproof, the small luxuries of a corps that planned to own the world — and lit the stub of candle she'd carried in her coat since the pass. A coin of light bloomed. Seven faces leaned into it like plants toward a window.

"Rationing," she said. "Now, while everyone can still think clearly."

* * *

The vault's antechamber was larger than any of them had guessed — a hollow throat of stone behind the inner door, with a cistern fed by some slow weeping of the mountain itself, so the water was there, at least, cold and mineral-bright and tasting faintly of lightning. But food was another matter. They inventoried by candlelight: hardtack enough for nine days if they ate thin, dried apples from Hollowpine in a cloth Sefa had tied, a heel of smoked goat, salt, and Tam's secret hoard of honey-sweets, which he surrendered without being asked and which made Ilsa have to look away for a moment at the smallness and enormity of it.

"Nine days," Dunmore said. "How long do sieges last?"

"As long as the besieger's patience," Ilsa said. "But this isn't a garrison. It's a door they want opened, and they can't open it themselves." She looked at Oscar. "Can they?"

"No." He said it with the flat certainty of a man reporting weather. "It answers to dragon-blood or to a keeper's oath. I'm the only oath-keeper on this mountain." He paused. "That doesn't mean they won't try to make me open it."

"They'll try worse than asking," Corl muttered.

"Yes," Ilsa agreed. "Which brings me to the second thing."

She took out her survey kit — the brass instruments, the codebook she should have burned, the slate and chalk she'd stolen from her own tent on the night she walked away from the empire. She set the slate upright against a fallen block of masonry and drew, in careful strokes, the signal-lamp alphabet of the Imperial Vanguard.

"Every imperial column carries signallers," she said. "Shuttered lamps, heliograph by day if the sun permits, drum-code in fog. The vanguard outside will be reporting to their relay station at Kestrel Gate every watch. Position, strength, requests." She tapped the slate. "And every relay expects confirmation codes back. If this mountain goes quiet, they'll assume the worst and escalate. If this mountain *answers*, they'll assume everything is proceeding."

Oscar's eyes narrowed. "You're proposing to lie to an army."

"I'm proposing to lie to an army *competently*. There's a difference, and it's measured in how long we live."

Vetch snorted. "She talks like Brannoc's ledgers read."

"She talks like someone worth keeping alive," Cyrus said.

His voice came out of the shadows by the wall, cracked and thin, and the whole room turned toward it the way faces turn toward sun. He hadn't spoken in hours. In the candlelight his skin was the grey of ash under snow, but his eyes were clear, and he smiled at Ilsa with terrible gentleness.

"Go on, cartographer. Teach us your empire's tongue. It's only fair — you learned ours."

* * *

She taught them in watches, because sleep had to be rationed too, and because teaching filled the dark better than fear did.

The drill-codes were not difficult — that was the empire's arrogance, that it never imagined its alphabet would be taught to the people it hunted. Three shutter-counts for *affirmative*, five for *negative*. The pause-pattern that meant *understood, proceeding*. The longer strings for supply requests, casualty reports, the bureaucratic poetry of an army that believed paperwork could conquer anything. Ilsa wrote them on the slate and made them repeat until the syllables lived in their mouths.

Tam learned fastest. Of course he did — he was twelve, and twelve-year-olds are the finest cryptographers alive, because they already spend their lives decoding the incomprehensible behavior of large dangerous creatures. By the second day he was tapping the codes against his knee in his sleep, and by the third he had invented, unbidden, a joke variant that meant *send more honey*, and even Dunmore laughed, a single rusty bark of a sound that seemed to surprise them all.

Oscar learned slowly and perfectly, the way he did everything. He sat across the slate from her through the middle watches while the others slept, and he asked questions that were not really about the codes.

"The relay at Kestrel Gate. How many men?"

"A hundred and forty at last count. Half reserves."

"And the vanguard at our door?"

"Sixty outriders. More coming. The records I saw promised a full cohort once the site was confirmed."

He absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain. Then: "When they realize the signals are false—"

"They'll know within a week. Maybe less. Someone always checks twice." She met his eyes across the little flame. "I'm not promising you forever, Joubert. I'm promising you time. Time is the only thing I know how to steal."

"That's more than my god ever gave me," he said, and almost smiled.

* * *

On the fourth day — or the fourth invention of a day — Cyrus worsened, and the camaraderie Ilsa had been carefully building cracked along the fault line she'd been pretending not to see.

Corl tried the door.

She heard it in the deep watch: the scrape of a boot, the whisper of metal, and then Corl's hands on the inner slab, feeling along its seams the way a thief feels a lock. She was moving before she'd decided to move, and Oscar was already there, materializing out of the dark with the terrible economy of a man who had spent ten years sleeping with one ear open.

"Just looking," Corl said, palms out.

"You were measuring," Oscar said.

"I was *thinking*—" and then Corl made his mistake, which was honesty: "Brannoc would want to know what's behind this rock. That's all. Guild business. You're keeping guild secrets behind guild stone, Joubert, and you know what happens to men who skim from the ledger."

"What's behind the stone," Oscar said, very quietly, "is a dying man, a boy, and the last fire in the world. If that's a skim, take it up with me after."

Nothing behind them moved except the candle flame. Ilsa found she had put herself between Corl and the bedroll where Tam slept, knife drawn without memory of drawing it, and the discovery frightened her more than Corl did — how quickly the empire's reflexes lived in her hands.

In the end it was Tam who broke it, sitting up sleep-rumpled, blinking at them all. "If you leave," he said, "you'll have to climb past sixty soldiers, and they've got your face memorized from the ford, probably, and anyway—" his voice wobbled and steadied— "anyway, Cyrus says people are allowed to change what side of the ledger they're on. He said that about her." A nod at Ilsa. "So it works both ways."

Corl stood in the dark a long moment. Then he went back to his corner and sat down, and no one spoke of it again, but Ilsa noticed that afterward Oscar slept facing the door and the room equally, and that Corl's rations were counted out loud, gently, unmistakably, every single time.

* * *

The fifth day she spent the lamp.

Not her candle — the shuttered signal lamp from her kit, imperial brass, its wick trimmed to a whisper. She'd explained it the night before: the vanguard would be sweeping light-signals across the upper slopes each dusk, calling down into the passes for confirmation from their pickets. Somewhere above them, on a ridge they couldn't see, an imperial signaller was flashing questions into the dark.

*I am going to answer*, Ilsa said.

Oscar watched her assemble the lamp with the expression of a man watching someone juggle knives over a child. "If you get the cadence wrong—"

"I wrote half these codes. Third Survey revised the vanguard manual two years ago. My name is in the appendix." Her hands were steady. Inside, she was a kettle at full boil, but ten years of imperial service had taught her that the body could lie beautifully even when the soul was screaming, and tonight the body would lie for the other side. "Pickets report in pairs of flashes. Watch-length intervals. If I hold the rhythm, they log us as a lost picket, repositioned, holding position."

"And what do you tell them you're holding?"

She thought of the vault behind them, the vast dark throat of it, the presence she felt sometimes through the stone when she pressed her palm flat the way Oscar did — a vast slow attention, older than the empire, drowsing at the bottom of the world. She thought of what the empire wanted to do to it. What her name in an appendix had helped make possible.

"That the pass is empty," she said. "That Thornfell holds nothing but snow and ghosts."

She climbed to the crack in the outer rock where starlight leaked through like water through a sieve — the vault breathed, Cyrus had told her once, laughing, through a hundred hidden fissures, the whole mountain a lung — and she opened the shutter, and she lied to an army.

Three flashes. Pause. Five. Pause. The old rhythms ran out of her fingers like blood from a cut. Across the valley, tiny and distant as a firefly, a light answered. *Understood. Proceeding.* Somewhere beyond that light, clerks would enter her lies into ledgers, and generals would plan around them, and for a handful of days the last dragon in the world would keep breathing because a woman with a lamp had committed the most elegant treason of her life.

When she came back down, Tam was waiting at the bottom of the fissure, holding out one of his honey-sweets — his last two, she knew, because she'd counted them with everything else.

"For luck," he said.

She took it. It tasted of summer, absurdly, here at the bottom of the world, and she had to turn away again, and this time she let him see her do it.

* * *

On the sixth day, the water in the cistern dropped an inch, and Cyrus called her to him.

She knelt by his bedroll. Up close the dying had a geography — the mapped territory of his cheekbones, the rivers gone underground beneath his skin. His breaths came now with pauses long enough to write a sentence in.

"You're good at this," he whispered. "The lying."

"It's the family trade. Empires are just lies with borders."

"Mm." His eyes found hers, and there was something in them that made her go still — the look of a man checking his maps one final time before committing to a route. "Listen. When I'm gone, the spark—" he touched his own chest, over the heart— "goes to Oscar. But the *map* — the truth of it — that lives in whoever remembers. Oscar carries the fire. Someone has to carry the *shape* of things. The routes. The codes. The way in and the way out." His hand crept across the blanket and closed on her wrist, light as a moth. "You drew the empire's roads, cartographer. Draw us a way to survive one."

Behind them, faintly, through six feet of living stone, came a sound none of them had heard before and all of them understood at once: the boom of a battering ram against the outer gate, patient as a heartbeat, beginning its work.

Cyrus smiled. "Better hurry," he said.
