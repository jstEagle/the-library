## 27. The Bargain with Brannoc

The mountain had a heartbeat now. Oscar felt it through the soles of his boots even before he felt it in his blood — a slow, deep pulse beneath the stone, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Three days since he had sealed the inner door with the last of the dragon's borrowed heat, and still the vault's outer galleries rang with it, faint as a second pulse under his own.

He stood in the antechamber above the great door, watching Brannoc's torches come up the switchbacks of the Weeping Stair.

There were more of them than Oscar had hoped. Thirty, maybe forty, climbing in the loose, economical file of men who had walked dangerous roads their whole lives — and behind them, further down where the snowfield flattened toward the tree line, the disciplined glitter of imperial steel. The vanguard had made camp within bowshot of the guild's fire, close enough to watch, far enough not to be mistaken for friends. Two wolves circling the same carcass, each pretending the other wasn't there.

"They're talking to each other," Ilsa said beside him. She had her spyglass up, though she hardly needed it; her eyes were better than glass. "Vessik and the imperial captain. Been at it an hour. That's either a negotiation or a eulogy."

"For whom?"

"Ask me tomorrow." She lowered the glass. Her face was pale with cold and something older than cold. "If Vessik sells you out first, you'll have maybe a hundred swords up that stair by nightfall. If the empire moves first, they'll just kill the guild and come for us anyway."

"Cheerful as ever."

"One of us has to be right." She handed him the spyglass anyway, out of habit, and went back down into the dark to check the barricades.

Oscar watched the torchlight climb a while longer. Then he rolled his shoulders, spat over the edge of the gallery, and went to find Tam.

* * *

They found him in the lower gallery where the dragon-warmth pooled thickest, sitting cross-legged on the stone with Cyrus's head cradled against his knee. The old man had been drifting in and out for two days now, his breath thin as paper, his hands cool even in the vault's strange heat. The spark inside him guttered like a candle in a doorway. Oscar could feel it whenever he let himself feel anything — a small flame cupped in dying flesh, and beneath it, vast and patient, the heart it fed.

"He asked for water," Tam said. "Then he said the constellations were wrong tonight. He kept counting them and getting a different number every time."

"That's the fever."

"It's not the fever." Tam's jaw was set the way it had been set ever since Greywater burned, but his eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them, because boys who wanted revenge didn't cry, and this boy had learned somewhere in the last month that wanting revenge and crying weren't enemies after all. "He's giving it away. Bit by bit. I can tell. He keeps smiling at nothing."

Oscar crouched. Cyrus's eyes opened halfway — milky, unfocused, then suddenly, terribly clear.

"Wolf," the old man whispered. "Is it time?"

"Soon."

"Don't lie to me. I've died before, in other men's dreams. I know the smell of the room." A ghost of the archivist's dry smile. "You're going to talk to Brannoc."

"You know about that?"

"I put a map in your hands, not blindfolds on your eyes. He'll want the secret. He's wanted it since the day he paid you." Cyrus's fingers twitched against Tam's knee, found the boy's hand, held it. "Guildsmen aren't loyal, Wolf. They're invested. Find the difference and speak to it."

"And if there's no difference?"

"Then speak to his fear. Everyone has one. Even emperors." His eyes drifted shut. "Especially emperors."

Oscar stayed a moment longer than he needed to. Then he rose, checked the knife at his hip out of ten years' habit, and left it there out of something newer.

"You're going to offer them a place," Tam said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going to offer them a choice."

"Same thing, if you mean it."

Oscar looked at the boy — thirteen winters, ash-grey eyes, a sword too big for him that he'd already used — and didn't bother lying. "Stay with him. If he wakes, don't let him stand. If he doesn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Just stay."

Tam nodded once, and Oscar went up into the cold.

* * *

Brannoc received him in the flat hollow below the Stair's last pitch, where the guild had raised windbreaks of stone and frozen brushwood and built a fire that smoked sideways in the katabatic wind. They had strung lanterns between the rocks — real light, wasteful light, the light of men who expected to sleep warm tonight. That told Oscar as much as any scout report. Guildsmen who expected battle didn't waste oil.

Four guards flanked the camp. Vessik, Brannoc's lieutenant, stood at his shoulder with his hand nowhere near his sword and his eyes everywhere else. And beyond the firelight, at the edge of the dark, imperial outriders sat their horses in perfect stillness, watching, counting.

"Joubert." Brannoc didn't rise from his camp stool. He was a big man gone soft in the middle but never in the hands, grey-bearded, with a ledger-keeper's squint and a butcher's forearms. Twenty years ago he'd given a starving cutpurse named Oscar Joubert his first honest job smuggling dishonest goods over Thornfell, and neither of them had ever mentioned it since. "Three days behind my own door and no map in your fist. You've gotten slow."

"I've gotten careful. There's a difference."

"There always is. Costs more, though." Brannoc gestured with his chin at a stool opposite. "Sit. You look like winter itself."

"I feel like it." Oscar sat. The heat of the fire touched his face and couldn't reach further; the vault's warmth still lived in him, deeper than skin. "Where's my payment?"

"Behind me, on thirty horses, minus whatever the empire's already promised them." Brannoc smiled without warmth. "You knew the job. Courier the package to Kestrel Gate. You didn't courier it. You ran. So we followed. That's not betrayal, Wolf, that's logistics."

"The package is people now. It's a widow who feeds strangers. It's a boy whose village your employers burned." Oscar leaned forward. "It's a dying man who trusted me."

"Dying men trust everyone. It's how they afford it." But Brannoc's smile had thinned, and Vessik had gone very still. "The empire's offering a king's weight in gold for what you're carrying. I'm offering you the same terms as before plus a bonus for the delay. Don't make this complicated, boy. Complication is where mercenaries go to die."

"Then let me complicate it."

Oscar reached into his coat — slowly, palms open, the way you move when forty men are watching — and drew out nothing at all. Just his empty hands, held up to the firelight.

"No map. There hasn't been a map since Cyrus burned it on the high shelf. What he carried, he carried here." He tapped his own chest. "And when he dies, it goes where he puts it. Which means there is nothing left in the world you can buy, Brannoc. Not from me. Not from anyone. The thing you climbed my mountain for stopped being cargo three weeks ago. Now it's a vow."

Silence, except the wind worrying the brushwood walls. One of the imperial horses stamped somewhere in the dark.

"That's a fine speech," Brannoc said at last, "for a man with no leverage."

"It's the only speech worth making. Listen to the rest of it." Oscar kept his voice level, almost gentle, the way you spoke to a man across a card table when the pot held everything either of you owned. "The empire didn't hire your guild because it trusts you. It hired you because you're deniable. When this is done — when they've drained whatever's under this rock and lit their new fires with it — do you think there's a Kestrel Gate anymore? Do you think there's a guild? Men who know where the last dragon sleeps are men who know too much, and empires solve that problem with a shovel and a lime pit. Ask yourself why they sent soldiers instead of coin. Coin comes later, if at all."

"You don't know that."

"Ilsa Vane knows it." He saw the name land; Vessik flinched like a struck horse. "Imperial cartographer. She's read the vault records. She read the orders for Greywater Ford — the burn order, Brannoc, signed and dated, a village of fishermen because one man ran through it. She defected rather than carry maps for what comes next. And what comes next needs fuel. The kind that burns cities. The kind that burns guilds."

Brannoc's squint had become something harder. He glanced at Vessik, and Oscar watched twenty years of patronage hang in the balance of that glance.

"You're asking me to turn on the empire," Brannoc said slowly. "With forty men. On a frozen mountain. For sanctuary." He laughed, short and ugly. "Under the rock. With your dragon. You've gone religious, Joubert. Ten years of silence and you've found the loudest god on the continent."

"Maybe I have. Doesn't change the arithmetic." Oscar spread his hands. "Here's the bargain. Every guildsman who stands with us gets a place beneath Thornfell. Warm stone, running water, the vault's stores — enough to outlast any siege the empire can mount before spring. No rent. No tolls. Your word and mine, sworn before witnesses, and I swear mine the old way." He paused. Let that settle. "And when the war comes — and it will come, whether you help or run — this mountain will be the one place on the map the fire can't reach. Because the fire will be ours."

"And if I say no?"

"Then take your forty men down the Stair, hand the empire whatever they want to hear, and spend the rest of your life waiting for the lime pit. Or fight them here, die here, and I'll bury you next to better men." Oscar shrugged. "I'm not threatening you, Brannoc. I'm describing Tuesday."

That got a bark of laughter out of Vessik before the man could choke it down. Brannoc shot him a look that would have flayed a lesser lieutenant.

"And the gold," Brannoc said. "A king's weight. You're asking me to walk past it."

"I'm asking you to notice whose king." Oscar stood, slowly, keeping his hands visible, an old smuggler's courtesy. "You taught me the first rule of the trade, old man. Never carry what can be confiscated. Carry what can't. Well — the empire's finally learned the same rule, and you're the cargo now. Decide which side of the ledger you want to die on."

He turned and walked out of the firelight without waiting for an answer, because waiting would have looked like begging, and begging would have cost more than the whole speech.

Behind him, the wind carried up fragments — Brannoc's low voice, Vessik's sharper one, the word *sanctuary* repeated like a coin being tested against the teeth.

* * *

Cyrus was awake when Oscar came back down. Sitting up, somehow, propped against the wall with Tam's shoulder under him, and his eyes had the terrible brightness of a lamp burning its last oil all at once.

"Well?" the old man rasped.

"He's thinking."

"Thinking is what greedy men do instead of deciding. It means you got close." Cyrus coughed, wet and long, and Tam held the waterskin to his lips with hands that shook only slightly. "Did you give him the vault?"

"Gave him everything but the door."

"Mm. Good. A bargain should always leave the other man hungry for the part you kept." Cyrus's gaze wandered to the ceiling, where the vault's veins of stone glowed faintly, dimmer than they'd been three days ago. The dragon was spending itself. Everyone could feel it now; the warmth in the galleries came in waves, like breath, and the breaths were shorter. "Wolf. Come here."

Oscar knelt.

"When I die," Cyrus said, very quietly, "the spark goes to you. That's already sworn. But the mountain — the wardenship — that isn't mine to give. It's yours to choose. So choose it while I can still see your face do it." A thread of a smile. "I stole fifty years of memories from the Emperor's archive and not one of them shows me a man who regrets choosing. Only men who regret waiting."

"Sentimental old thief."

"Archivist. Get it right on my stone." His hand found Oscar's wrist, gripped with strength that shouldn't have been there, the spark flaring hot between their skins until Oscar gasped. For one heartbeat he saw through Cyrus's eyes: a library burning, a child's voice singing scales, a vault of sleeping dragons going dark one by one as an emperor's clerks ticked names off a ledger. Then it was gone, and Cyrus released him, exhausted by the effort of remembering out loud.

"Go," the old man whispered. "Your wolf-pack needs counting. And I need to finish the sky. I still can't get the number right."

Tam met Oscar's eyes over the old man's head. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing yet to say that wasn't a goodbye, and goodbyes spent badly were wasted.

* * *

Near midnight, Ilsa woke him with a hand on his shoulder and her voice pitched below the wind.

"Movement on the Stair. Small party, no torches. Six." A pause. "Vessik's among them. Unarmed. And he's carrying a white rag on a spear like he learned diplomacy from a play."

Oscar was up and dressed before she finished. They met the delegation at the second barricade, where the vault's warmth bled out through the cracks in the outer door and melted a ring of bare stone in the snow — a circle of dark earth in all that white, like a wound that wouldn't close, like a hearth.

Vessik stood in it, shivering, holding the ridiculous white rag. Behind him the six others waited, and Oscar counted faces: four guild veterans he'd drunk with in a dozen border towns, one stranger, and — his stomach dropped — Brannoc himself, wrapped in a bear-fur cloak, breathing clouds.

"You came yourself," Oscar said.

"You made a speech worth coming for." Brannoc's breath steamed. Up close, in the vault's glow, he looked older than he had by the fire — or maybe just honest, which amounted to the same thing. "Half my captains want to sell you. Half want to run. The rest are listening to whichever half shouts louder." He worked his jaw. "So I'll ask the question straight, Joubert, and you'll answer it straight, because I've known you twenty years and I know when you're lying, and if you lie to me tonight I'll feed you to the imperials myself. Is it true? Is there really something under this rock worth dying for?"

Oscar thought of Cyrus counting stars and getting a different number every time. Of Sefa's kitchen. Of Tam's hands shaking around a waterskin. Of a voice older than gods, dreaming under stone, afraid.

"Yes," he said. "And it's worth living for too. That's the part nobody's offered you yet."

Brannoc stared at him for a long moment. Snow settled on the bear fur. Somewhere above, the wind moaned through the Weeping Stair like a choir rehearsing a funeral.

"Sanctuary for every man who stands," Brannoc said at last, heavily. "No tolls, no rent, your word sworn the old way. And in return, my blades against the imperial line when they come up that stair." He spat into the melted ring of earth, an old smuggler's seal. "One condition, Wolf. When this is done — if we live — I keep the ledgers. All of them. A guild survives on accounts, and I'll not be anybody's charity case. Not even a dragon's."

"Done."

"Then wake your dead man and your cartographer and your boy." Brannoc turned back toward the Stair, and the firelight caught the grim line of his mouth. "Because Vessik counted the imperial campfires on his way up. There were twelve yesterday. Tonight there are nineteen."

Behind him, far below in the dark, the empire's drums began to beat.
