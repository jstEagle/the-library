## 28. The Turning of the Guild

Brannoc Hale had counted coins in the dark for forty years, and he told himself the vote would be no different. A ledger was a ledger. Weights on either side of a scale, and a man's job was only to read which side sank.

The vault mouth at Thornfell gave him little light to read by. They had made camp in the black throat of the mountain's outer gallery, two hundred paces from the sealed inner door, and the only illumination came from the guild's pitch-pots and the strange, sullen glow that seeped through the cracks of the dragon-sealed stone — a red like coals breathing under ash. It lit the faces of his people badly. It made honest men look like conspirators and conspirators look like saints.

Forty-one of them had come up the Weeping Stair behind the imperial vanguard. Forty-one, minus the four the Wolf's people had killed on the ice, minus the two who had fallen and not been worth the rope to recover. Thirty-five now, huddled among the broken mule-traces and the old smugglers' caches that Brannoc himself had laid down in this gallery twenty years ago, back when Thornfell was just a mountain and a mountain was just a toll.

He stood on the old toll-stone — his toll-stone, his father's before him — and looked out at the upturned faces.

"You all know why we're here," he said. His voice carried the way it always did, low and even, the voice of a man reading terms. "The empire's vanguard sits at the pass mouth below. Captain Drevna and her outriders. Two hundred soldiers, siege-gear, fire-oil. They came up the same road we did, and they let us, because they want what's behind that door and they'd rather spend guild blood than imperial blood getting at it."

Murmurs. He let them run.

"The Wolf is behind that door. Cyrus's courier, the one who took the contract — my contract." He did not flinch from that. He had learned long ago that a man who flinched from his own ledger was a man who'd soon be robbed. "Behind the door with him is the last dragon's heart, or something near enough to it that the empire crossed a continent to hold a knife to it. The Emperor's men have offered a bounty that would buy every one of you a house in the lowlands and a title to go with it. And the Wolf has offered—" he paused, because this was the part that stuck in his throat like a fishbone, "—the Wolf has offered us a hole in a mountain and a war."

Silence. The coal-glow in the door seams pulsed, slow, like something sleeping and dreaming of being angry.

"So we vote," Brannoc said. "Old law. Every hand raised counts the same, from the newest cutpurse to me. And whatever the scale says, we do."

* * *

He had built the guild on the old law, and it was the one thing about himself he had never managed to be ashamed of. Everything else — the smuggling, the tolls, the selling of a dying man's route to whoever paid — he could dress up in the language of trade. But the vote was sacred. A guild that did not vote was just a gang, and gangs died young.

They voted by firelight, one at a time, each hand raised into the red glow so all could see.

The first dozen went the way Brannoc had privately expected. Vetch the lockman, old and gout-ridden, raised his hand for the empire without meeting anyone's eyes. "A house," he said simply. "I'm sixty-one years old. I want to die warm." The Corvane twins raised their hands together, as they did everything. Half the Kestrel Gate crew followed. The empire's silver was real, and the Wolf's promise was a promise made by a man famous for breaking them.

Then the count began to turn.

Maren Odo, who had run the river tolls for fifteen years and had once watched imperial fire-ships burn a smuggler's village on the Vane for the crime of existing — Maren raised her hand for the Wolf. "I've seen what the empire does with fire," she said. "I don't want to be the one who hands them more of it."

Young Pell, who had never voted before, whose hand shook — Pell for the Wolf. "Tam," he said, as if that explained it, and maybe it did. The boy from Greywater had drunk with the guild at Hollowpine once, before all this, and told stories about a burned ford and a widow who fed anyone who knocked.

The scale swung. Brannoc stood on his toll-stone and counted with a merchant's precision and a dying man's attention, and by the time the last hand went up he knew the number before the tally-keeper said it.

Seventeen for the empire. Eighteen for the Wolf.

One vote. His own had gone, an hour ago, for the Wolf — the first vote in his life he had cast against the money, and it had not even decided anything. The guild had decided it without him.

"Then it's done," he said. "Old law binds. The guild stands with the mountain."

Vetch stood up. So did the Corvane twins, and eleven more, gathering at the gallery's mouth where the descent to the imperial camp began. Vetch's face in the coal-light was an old man's face, tired and unashamed. "The law binds the guild," he said. "It doesn't bind men who are leaving the guild. I'm taking the empire's coin, Brannoc. So are they. You can't spend loyalty you don't have."

"I can spend what's in the ledger," Brannoc said quietly, "and every one of you took the toll-oath. You swore on the gate."

"I swore on your gate," Vetch said. "Not on your grave. They're different places, and I'd like to keep it that way." He turned and went, and the seventeen went with him, boots ringing on the old stair, and the sound of them going was the sound of half a life's work walking out of the room.

Brannoc stood on the toll-stone until the ringing stopped.

* * *

He had chosen poorly his whole life, and he knew it the way a man knows an old scar — not by looking at it but by the weather it brought. He had chosen the coin over the courier in Greywater, selling Cyrus's route to the empire's agents while the man himself was dying up the switchbacks. He had chosen the guild over the Wolf at the Kestrel Gate, taken the man's trust and spent it as bait. He had chosen, always, the side of the scale with the heavier metal, and told himself that was wisdom, that was survival, that was what his father's ledger demanded.

His father's ledger was in his coat now, oilskin-wrapped against his heart, forty years of names and debts and the small brutal arithmetic of a smuggling life. He had opened it that morning, in the dark before the vote, and read down the columns, and found what he had somehow never seen in forty years of reading it: that there was not a single name in it he had ever kept safe. Only names he had spent.

He sat by the pitch-pot after the vote with the ledger on his knees, and Maren Odo sat down across from him without asking.

"Eighteen," she said. "You tipped it."

"I voted with the losing side of my own nature," Brannoc said. "Don't make a song of it."

"Seventeen against eighteen. You were the song." She poked the pitch-pot with a knife-point. "What changed?"

He was a long time answering. The coal-glow in the sealed door pulsed, and pulsed, and he realized he had been timing his breath to it without noticing, the way you time your breath to a sleeping animal.

"A man bled out in my counting-room at the Kestrel Gate," he said finally. "One of the Wolf's people, cut down in the ambush we — that I — arranged the ground for. He was dying on my floor and he grabbed my sleeve and he said, *the map was never for sale, you fool, it was for keeping.*" Brannoc turned a page of the ledger he wasn't reading. "Forty years I've kept accounts. That was the first entry I couldn't balance. So I'm trying a different book."

Maren looked at him for a while. "You're going to get us all killed."

"Probably."

"Good," she said, and went to check the watch.

* * *

The imperials came at the third hour of the morning watch, which told Brannoc that Vetch had talked fast and Captain Drevna had believed him — or had believed the parts she wanted. They came with fire-oil and scaling ladders and the flat professional silence of soldiers who had done this before, and they came up the gallery in three waves with crossbows clearing the way.

The guild's eighteen held the toll-stone line, and it was the strangest battle of Brannoc's life because half of it was fought against men he had eaten with. He saw Vetch on the imperial side, waving the soldiers forward, pointing out the old caches, and the sight of it did something to Brannoc's chest that he decided he would examine later, if there was a later.

"First wave on the stair!" someone shouted, and then the gallery was noise and pitch-light and the wet heavy sounds of close work.

Brannoc fought the way he did everything: without flourish, without waste. He had a boarding axe and a short shield and thirty years of knowing every stone in this gallery, and he spent all three. He broke a soldier's arm at the elbow with the axe's back-spike and felt nothing about it, which was its own kind of knowledge. He heard Maren screaming the guild's rally-cry, the old Kestrel call, and heard young Pell answer it from somewhere near the door, and the sound of those two voices braided together was the first thing in forty years that had ever made him proud of the word *guild*.

They held the first wave. They held the second, barely, losing Doss Corvane to a crossbow bolt through the throat — one of the twins, and Brannoc never learned which, and the other twin's howl of grief was still going when the third wave came.

The third wave came with fire.

They had saved the fire-oil for the toll-stone line, and when it went up the whole gallery turned orange and the shadows of fighting men leapt forty feet up the vault walls, and Brannoc understood in one white-hot instant that Drevna was not trying to take the gallery. She was trying to burn them out of it, to smoke the door open or cook everyone behind it, and the empire had never cared which.

"Off the line! To the door!" he roared, and the guild broke backward through the flame, and he went last because he was the chief and that was the whole of the office. A soldier caught him at the edge of the burning line — a young one, imperial-issue from boots to blank eyes — and drove a short sword into Brannoc's side, under the arm, where the coat doesn't sit right on an old man.

Brannoc killed him for it. It was reflex, not courage; his hands had forty years of practice and no interest in his opinions. Then he walked — did not run, walked, because the guild was watching — through the fire to the sealed door, with the sword-wound opening and closing under his arm like a second mouth, and put his palm against the stone.

It was warm. Not fire-warm. *Alive*-warm, the way a horse is warm under a blanket in winter, and through his palm he felt it — faint, unmistakable, a slow vast rhythm underneath the mountain, patient as geology.

*Oh,* he thought, with the clarity of a man bleeding out. *It was never a treasure. It was never a sale. It's a *neighbor*.*

The stone under his hand brightened. The seams of the door glowed from dull coal-red to a fierce rose-gold, and the heat that came off it was not the empire's fire-oil heat, not hungry, not consuming — it was a heat like a hearth the size of a cathedral, and it rolled out over the gallery and caught the burning oil and *drank it*, in one long indrawn breath, every flame in the gallery bending toward the door and going out like candles in a closed room.

Darkness. Then the rose-glow. Then, in that light, the guild of Thornfell — what was left of it, eleven standing, four down, Brannoc among the standing with his hand pressed to a door that had just saved their lives — and beyond them, at the gallery mouth, the imperial third wave standing in the sudden dark with their torches dead and their fire-oil spent and their captain's voice cracking with something that was very close to fear.

"Fall back," Drevna was saying. "Fall back and hold the stair. Nothing gets past us. *Nothing*."

The imperials withdrew to the gallery mouth and began, with the terrible patience of soldiers, to build a siege line.

Brannoc slid down the warm door until he was sitting, which he had not intended to do. Maren reached him first, then Pell, hands pressing cloth against his side, and the cloth went red immediately, and Pell's face went white above it.

"Chief—"

"Don't," Brannoc said. He was looking at the door. The rose-glow had settled back to its slow coal-pulse, and he could still feel the rhythm under it, and he thought: *forty years of ledgers, and the only honest entry I ever made was a palm print on a door.* "Get me sitting straight. If I'm going to bleed, I'll bleed facing the right way."

Maren propped him against the stone. Through the wound under his arm he felt the door's warmth spread into him, or maybe that was just blood-loss lying to him, and he found he didn't care which.

"Pell," he said. "The ledger."

The boy dug the oilskin packet out of his coat with shaking hands. Brannoc took it, and held it a moment, and then held it out.

"Take it to the Wolf," he said. "When the door opens. Tell him every name in it is his now — the debts and the routes and the caches, all of it. Tell him the guild of Thornfell has one asset left." He coughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway, because it was funny, it had always been going to be funny at the end. "Tell him the asset is *us*."

Pell took the ledger like it might bite. Maren was shouting for the others to shore up the door approach with the old cache-stones, and her voice was steady, and Brannoc sat against the warm stone of a dragon's door with his life's blood soaking into a mountain that had finally, finally given him something worth spending it on.

Down the gallery, in the imperial lines, a signal-horn sounded — three long notes, then silence, then three again. Brannoc knew imperial signals the way he knew everything: for a price.

He had taught Ilsa Vane's codes to no one. But he had bought enough of them over the years to know what three-and-three meant, and the knowing went through him colder than the wound.

*Reinforcements,* the horn said. *Hold the door. We are coming with the heavy fire.*

Brannoc Hale closed his eyes, felt the heart under the mountain beat, and began — for the first time in his life — to plan for something other than profit.

"Wake the Wolf," he said. "Tell him the empire just called down the fire-weapons. And tell him—" he pressed his palm flat to the warm stone one more time, "—tell him the guild holds the gate. Whatever it costs."

The door pulsed beneath his hand, slow and vast and alive, like something answering.
