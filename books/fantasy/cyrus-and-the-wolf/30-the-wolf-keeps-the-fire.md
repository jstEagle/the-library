## 30. The Wolf Keeps the Fire

The first thing Oscar learned about keeping a fire was that it kept him back.

He had expected the spark to sit in his chest like a coal in a brazier — something he could bank, tend, ignore when the work of living demanded his hands. It did not. It breathed with him. When he drew breath on the vault's cold threshold, the spark drew breath; when his heart stumbled at the sight of Cyrus's shroud-wrapped body being carried down to the door he would never open again, the spark stumbled too, and for one terrible half-second the torches along the passage guttered in sympathy, as if the mountain itself had flinched.

Three days since Cyrus died at dawn. Three days since Oscar had screamed with a voice that was not entirely his own and felt the stone under his knees answer like a struck bell.

Now he stood alone in the antechamber where they had laid the dead — not Cyrus; Cyrus lay outside, beneath the cairn they had raised against the sealed door, facing east so that whatever came next would find him watching for it — but the others. Brannoc's dead. The imperial dead they had dragged into a side gallery because leaving them for the mountain to digest seemed kinder than leaving them for the crows. Two mules' worth of gear, salvaged. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell of spent fire: ozone and char and something sweeter underneath, like resin weeping from a cut pine.

"You should eat."

Ilsa Vane stood in the doorway with a bowl in both hands, holding it out the way you'd hold out a hand to a strange dog. She had been doing that for three days — feeding him, checking his bandages, speaking to him in the flat, careful voice of a woman who had defected mid-hunt and still wasn't entirely sure the knife wouldn't turn on her. He had told her twice that it wouldn't. He suspected she'd believe it around the same time he believed the war was over.

"I'm not hungry," he said.

"You haven't been hungry in three days." She set the bowl on a crate anyway. Barley and goat fat, Sefa's recipe, carried up the last four miles of switchbacks by a widow who refused to be told the pass was closed. "That's not grief, Joubert. That's the fire. It burns what you give it before it asks you for more."

He looked at her. Really looked, which was different now — everything was different now, everything arrived with more of itself attached. Where before he would have seen a tired woman in an ill-fitting smuggler's coat, he saw also the heat-map of her: the fever-ember of her fear, banked low and disciplined; the steadier warmth of her conscience, which she fed daily and pretended was only habit; and beneath both, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, something that flickered when she looked at Tam. Guilt, maybe. Or hope. With imperials it was hard to tell the difference until they chose.

"That's going to take some getting used to," he said.

"The seeing?"

"The seeing." He flexed his hands. They were his hands. They were also, now, instruments of something older than hands, and both facts were true at once, the way two maps of the same country could both be true and neither complete. "Cyrus warned me. In the end. He said — *it doesn't ask permission, Oscar. It just moves in.*" His mouth twisted. "I thought he was talking about the oath."

"He was talking about the oath." Ilsa leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. "The fire is just what the oath grew into. Like — " she searched — "like a cutting taken from an old tree. Same wood. Different roots."

*Different roots.* He turned that over as she left him to the barley he would eventually eat, because she was right and they both knew it, and because arguing with Ilsa Vane was a luxury the siege schedule didn't allow.

Because there was a siege schedule. That was the other thing.

* * *

On the second night after Cyrus died, Oscar had climbed to the high shelf above the vault mouth — the shelf where Cyrus had once taught Tam the constellations dragons navigated by, though none of them had known then how soon the boy would need the lesson without the teacher — and he had done what he always did when the world needed deciding. He had prayed.

Nothing came. That was familiar. Ten years of nothing had made silence feel like an old coat: ill-fitting, threadbare, but his.

Then the spark answered instead.

Not his god. Never his god — he understood that now with the terrible clarity of a man reading his own eulogy. Whatever had spoken to Oscar Joubert through twenty years of oaths and then gone dark had been small and distant and, perhaps, dead; he might spend the rest of his life learning whether its silence had been abandonment or extinction, and the spark did not care. The spark was not interested in his theology. The spark was interested in *fire* — and somewhere south and east, beyond Thornfell's shoulders, beyond Greywater's ashes, beyond the whole smoking breadth of the empire, fires were waking.

He felt them the way you feel a storm front in your teeth.

They were not hearth-fires. Hearth-fires had a texture he recognized, domestic and diffuse, ten thousand little warmths scattered across the lowlands like embers raked for the night. These were different. These were *organized*. They sat in long rows inside deep buildings — he could feel the buildings, or rather feel the absence the buildings made, cold stone drinking heat — and each row burned in time with the others, synchronized, disciplined, the way an army marches in step. And threaded through every one of them, thin as wire, ran a note he had heard only once before: in the vault, in the last hour of the last dragon's strength, when it had poured its final choice into a single stone heart.

Dragon-fire. Rendered, refined, *farmed*. The empire had learned to grow it the way you grow mushrooms — in the dark, on corpses.

He had come down from the shelf white-faced, and Ilsa had taken one look at him and said, "You felt them," and he had known she'd been waiting for exactly that.

"How many?" she asked now, in the map room they'd carved out of a supply gallery, by the light of lamps whose flames leaned toward him slightly, the way flowers lean toward a window. He tried not to think about that.

"Rows," he said. "Long rows. I counted — " he stopped, because counting was absurd, he hadn't counted, he had *known*, the number had simply been there the way the number of his own fingers is there — "eleven foundries. Maybe more past the range of — " he gestured vaguely at his own chest — "whatever this is. Each one big enough to arm a legion. And they're not at full burn yet. They're *kindling*. Warming up. There's a rhythm to it, like — "

"Like bellows," Ilsa said quietly. "Draw, hold, release. Draw, hold, release." Her face had gone the color of tallow. "That's the ignition cycle for a fire-lance battery. We tested it at Karsk Reach, before my transfer. One battery. You're describing eleven."

"One battery did what, at Karsk Reach?"

She met his eyes. She didn't flinch, and he gave her credit for that, because what she said next deserved flinching. "It melted a fortress gate, two hundred yards of curtain wall, and everyone behind them. In nine seconds."

The lamp-flames leaned. Somewhere below them, sealed behind a door that no longer opened for anyone, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and the stone floor hummed a note too low for ears — but not, anymore, too low for Oscar.

"It hears me thinking about this," he said. "The dragon. Or — the egg. Whatever's down there now. It gets restless when I — "

"When you what?"

"When I plan around it. Like it knows it's the thing being planned about." He rubbed his face with both hands. His beard was coming in grey at the jaw; he'd stopped noticing these things years ago and now noticed everything, because noticing was what the fire did, noticing and burning and wanting to be *used*. "Ilsa. How long do we have? Honestly. You know their logistics better than I know their fires."

Ilsa was silent long enough that the lamp nearest her guttered, recovered, steadied. When she spoke, each word was placed like a stone in a wall.

"A vanguard came through the pass and died at our door. The empire does not lose vanguards quietly. Within the month — sooner, if the survivors' signal-horns were heard — there will be a full cohort on the southern slopes, with engineers, with sappers, with enough fire-lances to make Karsk Reach look like a kitchen accident." She paused. "And they won't storm the door. They'll do what I would do. They'll seal the mountain and wait for us to starve, while the foundries finish kindling. We are not the objective, Joubert. We're the *delay*."

"And the egg is the objective."

"The egg is the objective." She said it without ceremony, which was its own kind of mercy. "Everything else is arithmetic."

* * *

He went down to the inner door that night, alone, because there are conversations a man must have standing on the grave of the person who got him into all this.

The cairn was simple — Cyrus would have hated anything else. Flat stones, stacked tight, with his broken spyglass laid on top because Tam had insisted a navigator should be buried with his instruments, and who was going to argue with a twelve-year-old who had killed his first man eight days ago and hadn't slept properly since. Oscar put his palm flat on the topmost stone. Cold. The mountain's cold, not death's — there was a difference, he knew now, and knowing it was both comfort and cruelty.

"I told you I'd deliver it," he said. His voice came out rough. "You never said delivered to *whom*. That was sloppy drafting, old man. A courier of your reputation."

The stones said nothing. But beneath them, beneath forty feet of granite and a door sworn shut, the spark in his chest turned toward something the way a compass needle turns north, and he felt — faintly, distantly, the way you hear surf through a hillside — the great slow respiration of the thing he kept.

It was dying. That was the truth he carried now alongside the fire, the truth that made the power a deadline instead of a gift. The last dragon had spent nearly everything making the egg; what remained was a candle-end, a pilot light, a heartbeat measured in weeks. He could feel its edges fraying. Every time he reached for it — and he reached constantly, helplessly, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth — the reach came back shorter. Warmth without depth. Light without weight. A fire that remembered being a furnace.

And the egg — the egg was worse, because the egg was *hopeful*. It pulsed. Slow, patient, blind, it pulsed with a rhythm that was almost but not quite his own heartbeat, and when the two rhythms drifted close the whole vault resonated like a wineglass at a singer's note, and Oscar had to stand very still and breathe until they parted again, because he did not yet know what happened if they matched, and some part of him, some part older than his cynicism and deeper than his scars, badly wanted to find out.

"Not yet," he told it. Out loud, because Cyrus had talked to things out loud — to mules, to maps, to constellations — and Oscar had learned late but thoroughly that the dead teach best by example. "You're not ready. I'm not ready. And there's an empire upstairs that melts fortresses in nine seconds, so we're all going to stay quiet and patient and *alive*, do you understand me?"

The pulse slowed. Sulked, almost. He could have sworn it sulked.

"Cyrus used to say you'd be stubborn," he added, softer. "He undersold you."

Behind him, boots on stone — light ones, quick ones, a stride he knew better than his own these days. Tam came and stood beside him at the cairn, not touching it, the way boys stand near graves when they haven't decided yet whether graves are for adults. The boy's face was thinner. His eyes had the flat shine of someone managing his nightmares by daylight instead of sleeping through them.

"He talks to you," Tam said. Not a question. "Down there. I can tell. Your lips move when you come up."

"Does it bother you?"

Tam considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young. "No. Cyrus talked to everything. Stars. Mules. That one lock on his strongbox that stuck." A pause. "I just want to know if it answers."

Oscar looked at the boy — at the ember of him, fierce and ragged and burning fuel he couldn't afford to burn — and told the truth, because lying to Tam had become impossible somewhere between the Narrows and the siege, maybe because the fire made liars of no one, maybe because the boy had earned it.

"Yeah," he said. "It answers."

"What does it say?"

"So far? Mostly that it's hungry, and that it doesn't like being planned about." He huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. "Which makes it the most honest member of this company."

Tam almost smiled. Then the flatness came back over his eyes, the look Oscar had learned to hate, the look of a boy inventorying the men he'd killed. "Oscar. If they come back. If the cohort comes, and the sappers, and we can't hold — " He stopped. Started again, quieter. "What happens to it? To the egg. If they get in."

And there it was. The question under every question, the one Cyrus had died carrying and Oscar now lived carrying, asked plainly by the youngest throat among them. Oscar put his hand on the boy's shoulder and felt the spark lean toward the contact, gentle, careful, the way a big dog is gentle when it understands it is big.

"Then it doesn't," he said. "Whatever else burns. That doesn't."

It wasn't a plan. It wasn't even a promise — promises required knowledge of the future, and the future currently consisted of eleven kindling foundries and a candle-end dragon and a mountain full of people he'd somehow accumulated like snow on a ledge. But Tam nodded, once, and some of the flatness went out of his eyes, and Oscar understood that the boy hadn't needed a plan. He'd needed to hear an adult say the important thing out loud, so it became real, so it became load-bearing.

Cyrus had been good at that. Oscar was learning.

* * *

He was climbing back toward the map room when the fire in his chest went rigid.

He stopped on the stair, one hand to the wall, and listened with senses that had no business existing in a mercenary's body. South. Far south — farther than Greywater, farther than the border forts, down where the empire's heart beat in its marble chest. One of the eleven foundries had changed its rhythm.

Draw, hold — and instead of release: *silence*. A held breath. Then, faint and terrible, a new note threading through the others, a note he recognized because it was kin to the one in his own ribs: dragon-fire, drawn thin, spun fine, being worked into something with an edge.

They've finished the first weapon, he thought. Or the first hundred. And they're not waiting for the cohort to dig us out. They're going to come and melt the mountain off the map.

Below him, through forty feet of stone, the egg's pulse stuttered — once, twice — and settled into a rhythm faster than before. Frightened. It was frightened, and it was reaching for him, and he was reaching back before he decided to, keeper and kept, wolf and fire, and the whole vault hummed like a struck bell that had finally learned why it was struck.

He stood in the dark on the stairs and let himself feel all of it: the deadline ticking in his chest, the enemy kindling on the horizon of his new sense, the fragile pulsing hope beneath his feet. Then he climbed the rest of the stairs, because there was a cartographer in the map room who knew imperial codes, and a message that needed forging, and not many nights left to forge it in.

Ilsa looked up when he came in. Read his face in a heartbeat, because she was learning him too.

"They've lit one," she said.

"They've lit one." He pulled the chair out across from her and sat, and the lamp-flames between them leaned toward him like a congregation. "How fast can you write a lie that saves all our lives?"
