## 32. The Empire Breaks the Door

### ILSA

She heard the door break the way you hear a bone snap in another room — a sound that arrives already understood.

Ilsa Vane stood at the inner junction with her ear against cold stone, and beneath her palm the mountain transmitted everything: the groan of the outer vault gate surrendering its hinges, the percussion of ram-iron against rock, and then, faint as rain through a roof, boots. Dozens of boots. The vanguard had stopped being patient in the night. Somewhere above, Brannoc's guildsmen were either fighting or dying or both, and the codes she had taught them to spoof — *Vane-actual, confirm perimeter* — had gone unanswered for two hours.

"Third signal," she said without turning. "They're inside the antechamber."

Oscar came out of the dark with a torch in one hand and his axe loose in the other, and behind him Tam, who was holding a spear the way boys hold things too heavy for them: with their whole body, as if grip alone could make it true.

"How many?" Oscar asked.

"If they sent the vanguard proper, sixty. If they sent what Vessic could scrape together after the pass collapsed behind us—" She corrected herself. *After we collapsed the pass.* The lie she'd sent south still sat in her chest like swallowed glass. *Vault empty. Site abandoned. Recommend withdrawal.* Somewhere an imperial quartermaster was filing it, and somewhere else a man named Corvel was not believing it.

"So thirty," Oscar said. "Maybe forty."

"Thirty," she agreed, because lying to him served nothing now. "And they'll have fire-pikes. The new kind."

Tam's voice cracked through the dark, too loud. "The kind that don't need dragon-oil?"

"The kind that will need it," Ilsa said quietly. "Once they take this mountain. That's why they can't be allowed to leave with what's down there." She looked at Oscar. "You know what I'm telling you."

"I know what you're telling me." He set the torch into an iron bracket, and the light swung across his face, hollowing it. Ten years of silence had carved him; the last month had filled the cuts back in with something she couldn't name yet. "Then we hold the junction. Nothing gets past it."

"They'll come in waves," she said. "That's doctrine. First wave is conscripts — scared boys with shields. They're meant to die slowly enough that the second wave can see where your blades are."

"And the second wave?"

"Professionals." She touched the scar at her collarbone where her own academy ring used to sit. "Like me."

Oscar nodded once, and went to wake the others.

### TAM

Tam had thought war would be louder.

He'd imagined it from the stories Sefa told over bread, from the burned skeleton of Greywater Ford he'd walked home through — all roar and trumpet, big as weather. But this was small and close and lit wrong. The torches guttered in air that moved when it shouldn't, and the dragon-fire in the deeper vault threw no shadows so much as a red pulse behind everything, like standing inside a heartbeat. His hands were slick on the spear shaft. He kept wiping them on his coat and they kept getting slick again.

"You're behind me," Oscar said, for the fourth time. "Whatever happens, behind me. You watch my left side. You call out what you see. That's your whole job."

"I know."

"Say it back."

"Behind you. Watch your left. Call it out." Tam said it fast, to prove he wasn't afraid, which proved nothing, because everyone could hear his teeth clicking when he shut his mouth.

Cyrus lay further back, near the sealed door, propped against a rolled cloak with Ilsa's field-dressing around his middle and his eyes open and terribly calm, the way they always were now, as if dying had already happened and he was just waiting for the rest of them to catch up. When Tam glanced back, Cyrus raised two fingers — their old salute, invented on the high shelf under the dragon constellations — and Tam raised his back and hated how much better it made him feel.

Then the first wave came around the junction wall, and there was no more time for feeling anything.

They were scared boys, like Ilsa said. He could tell because they came bent low, shields up but shaking, and one of them was crying — actually crying, wet hitching sobs Tam could hear over twenty feet of stone. Behind Oscar, Brannoc's remaining guildsmen braced with short swords and no honor at all, which was apparently the same as courage. Someone screamed a word that wasn't a word. The tunnel filled with the crash of steel and the strange flat echo of the mountain swallowing every sound and handing back its bones.

Tam called out what he saw. "Left! Two, left side, behind the pillar—"

A blade rang off Oscar's axe. A guildsman went down gurgling. A conscript's shield split and the boy behind it looked straight at Tam — straight into his eyes, close enough that Tam could see the down on his cheek, the chapped lips, a pimple at the corner of his jaw — and the boy was nobody, he was a farmer's son from some imperial province, he was Tam's own age almost, and then Oscar's axe took him and he wasn't anything anymore.

Tam didn't stop calling things out. That was the horror of it, he realized later. He didn't stop. His voice worked like it belonged to someone useful, and he watched men die by the dozen in torchlight, and the whole time the red pulse of the dragon's failing fire breathed behind them all like something sleeping through a massacre.

The first wave broke. Fell back. Left nine bodies and one man crawling, dragging his guts, moaning a name over and over — *Maret, Maret* — until a guildsman ended it gently, almost kindly, and spat, and Tam found he was sitting down without remembering sitting.

"That's doctrine too," Ilsa said beside him. Her voice was flat and steady and her hands weren't. "The moaning. It breaks the next wave's nerve less than you'd think, and ours more. Don't listen to it."

"How do you not listen?"

"You listen," she said. "You just keep working anyway. That's the whole trick of being a soldier, Tam. There isn't another one."

### OSCAR

Between waves, Oscar counted the living and prayed to no one, out of habit, and then — not out of habit — put his hand flat to the wall instead.

*I hear you,* he did not say, because saying it felt like begging. *Whatever's left of you. Whatever you are. We're about to need more than we have.*

The mountain answered the way it had answered since Cyrus died in his arms: not in words. In heat. A slow swell of warmth rising through the stone into his palm, ancient and guttering, a candle-flame the size of a heart doing its best. The dragon was nearly spent. He could feel its edges now, feel where the fire thinned to nothing, and every hour it spent burning for them was an hour stolen from whatever future Cyrus had bled a map to protect.

*Make it count,* the warmth said, or Oscar heard it say, which after ten years of silence amounted to the same faith.

"They're reforming," Brannoc reported from the shadow of the junction. The guild chief had blood on his beard that wasn't his and a look on his face Oscar had never seen there before: a man doing arithmetic he hated. "Forty-some left of yours, cartographer. Second wave?"

"Second wave," Ilsa confirmed. "Give it five minutes. They'll send fire-pikes this time, burn us out of the junction rather than fight us in it."

Brannoc laughed — a short, ugly bark. "Burn us. In a mountain. With a dragon under it." He shook his head. "Empire never learned a damn thing about enclosed spaces."

"It learned plenty," Ilsa said. "It just doesn't care. Fire-pikes in a low tunnel kill both sides, captain. That's the arithmetic. We're worth spending."

Oscar looked at her. "How long have you known they think that way?"

"Since I was nineteen." Her jaw tightened. "It's why I'm here."

The five minutes passed in silence, in checking straps and whispering positions, in Cyrus asking Tam to bring him water he drank half of and poured the rest into the crack at the base of the sealed door — an offering, maybe, or a joke only dead men would get. Then the second wave came, and it came correctly.

Fire first. The tunnel mouth bloomed orange, and the heat hit like a hand shoving them back, and Ilsa was shouting the drill-codes — *Vane-actual, hold, hold, misdirect!* — and two of the pike-men actually faltered at her voice, rank reflex overriding orders, before an officer's scream drove them on. The flames licked along the ceiling and died against stone that had waited ten thousand years for worse. The mountain drank the fire. Oscar felt it drinking, felt the warmth in the walls deepen by one greedy degree, and understood with a lurch that the empire's weapons were feeding the thing they'd come to harvest.

"Fall back to the narrows!" he roared. "Let them come to us!"

They came correctly too. Shields locked, stepping over their own burning, professionals with flat eyes, and the fighting in the narrow passage was not battle but butchery conducted at arm's length — no room to swing, only shove and stab and the awful intimacy of smelling the breath of the man you're killing. Oscar killed three and stopped counting. A guildsman named Ferro took a spear through the throat saying something to somebody whose name wasn't any of theirs. Ilsa fought like a textbook written in blood, economical, joyless, perfect.

And Tam—

Tam was behind him. Tam was supposed to be behind him.

But the line buckled at the seam where the guildsmen met Oscar's people, a gap no wider than a coffin, and an imperial sergeant saw it the way professionals see everything, and came through it low and fast with his sword reversed, and the target in his path — the smallest figure, the one in the oversized coat — was not supposed to be there.

Oscar turned and knew he would not be in time. He had known it before, in other years, other contracts, watching the moment arrive faster than iron, and every time he had learned the same lesson: the world does not wait for oaths.

The sergeant reached Tam. Tam screamed — no technique, no training, just a boy's whole terror flung forward — and drove the spear up under the man's chin with both hands and all his weight behind it.

The sound it made was small. That was what unmade everything. After all the clangor, the actual killing sound was small and wet, and the sergeant's helmet rang off the stone, and the man folded down onto the spear with his eyes open, looking surprised, looking at Tam, and Tam looked back.

Then the wave broke around them and receded, called off by horns, and in the sudden ringing quiet Tam stood over the body making a noise Oscar had heard only once before: the night Greywater burned, a boy standing in ash calling for his mother.

He dropped the spear like it had bitten him. He sat down against the wall beside the dead man and pulled his knees in and came apart, wholly, loudly, the way only children and the very old are permitted to, and his hands would not open, and every finger of them was asking *why* in a language older than words.

Oscar knelt and gathered him in.

There was nothing to say. That was the truth of it, the thing no ballad would ever survive admitting: there was nothing to say. *It gets easier* was a lie. *He would have killed you* was true and worthless. So Oscar held him, an axe-hard mercenary with a decade of corpses behind him, and rocked him slightly, the way he vaguely remembered being rocked once, by someone whose face the years had taken, and let the boy shake against his chest.

"I killed him," Tam said into the wool of his coat. "I felt it. When it went in, I felt it stop being a person."

"I know."

"Does it stop? Feeling it?"

"No," Oscar said. "It gets quieter. Some days. Those are the good days."

Tam cried until he had nothing left, and Oscar stayed kneeling on stone lit by a dying fire, holding the weight of the only part of this whole war that mattered. Behind them Cyrus watched with his terrible calm eyes, and when Oscar looked up, the dying man said, very softly:

"Now you understand what I stole it *from*, Wolf. Now you know what the archive smelled like."

### ILSA

She gave them an hour, because doctrine demanded the enemy regroup and because mercy demanded someone count the dead properly. She did both. Nine of theirs against eleven imperials in the first rush, six more in the narrows. Ferro. Little Wren, who had joined Brannoc for the coin and never once been late to share it. She laid the guildsmen out with their hands folded because Brannoc couldn't bear to, and noted, in the professional corner of her mind that never slept, the shape of the coming third wave.

It didn't come.

Instead, footsteps — unhurried, single, confident — approached the junction, and a voice she knew called out in flawless parade-ground cadence:

"Cartographer Vane. This is Correl Vessic. I have your last message here. *Vault empty.* Very tidy work, Vane. Almost good enough." A pause. "Come out and confirm it in person, and I'll write you into the record as captured under orders. Deep-cover attribution. Your family keeps its name."

Beside her, Oscar went very still. "Your handler."

"My handler," she agreed. Her mouth had gone dry. "And a decent man, by imperial standards, which means dangerous in exactly one direction." She looked down the black throat of the tunnel, toward the voice, toward the empire she had carried in her scars for fifteen years. "He won't believe the vault is empty until he's standing in it. And if he stands in it, he stands at the door of the last dragon in the world."

"So he doesn't stand in it," Oscar said.

"He has forty soldiers and fire-pikes, Joubert. We have a boy who's just learned what killing costs, a dying man, a guttersnipe guild, and a dragon that's burning its own life to keep our lamps lit." She smiled, and it felt like a wound opening. "No. We don't stop him standing in it. We choose *when*. And then—"

From deep below them, through half a mile of living stone, the mountain shuddered. Once. Gently. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep, having dreamed of fire, and woken to find the dream already at the door.

Every torch in the passage leaned toward the heart of Thornfell at once, as if bowing.

"—then it chooses too," Ilsa finished, barely above a whisper. "God help us all. It's awake."
