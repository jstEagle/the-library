## 33. The Guttering

I am old.

Not old as the mountains are old — the mountains were children when I was already gray about the muzzle — but old as a fire is old when it has burned through the last of its wood and begun to eat its own memory. I am that fire now. I have been that fire for longer than I care to count, and I count in centuries, so you may understand what I mean by *long*.

They named me once. I have let the name go the way dragons let go of names, the way we shed scales and years and griefs, one at a time, until what remains is only the essential shape: heat, hunger, patience. But there was a name. A small soft thing said it to me first, in a tongue that has since become the language of emperors and thieves, and I wore it like a warm stone in my chest for an age of the world. I will not say it here. Some things must be carried whole or not at all, and I am nearly done carrying.

Beneath me, the dark. Around me, the mountain, which I have worn hollow the way water wears a riverbed — not by force but by returning, returning, returning. Above me, through forty fathoms of granite and frozen scree, I can feel them coming. Not with eyes. Eyes are for creatures who live in the bright shallow world where light ends at the skin. I feel them as pressure. As intention. Two hundred hearts beating out their small hot rhythms on the surface of my sleep, marching toward the door my keeper sealed, carrying iron and oil and the particular greedy silence of men who have been promised a miracle they intend to own.

Two hundred hearts. It would take so little.

This is what I want you to understand, whoever finds these words pressed into the stone like flowers into a book: it would take *so little*. One exhalation. The smallest spending of what remains in me, funneled up through the veins of the mountain the way sap rises in spring, and the pass above would become a river of glass. Their armor would become part of the rock, and their banners would become smoke, and the empire that hunted my kind across three generations of its kings would learn, one final time, why its grandfathers built their walls facing away from Thornfell rather than toward it.

I know this because I have done it before.

That is the thing the songs never sing correctly. They give the burning to heroes and the dying to villains, and they leave out the middle truth entirely: that fire does not distinguish. That when I opened my throat over Vhalen's Reach, four hundred years ago, I did not see an army. I saw shapes. Small, screaming, running shapes, and some of those shapes had dropped their weapons and were running *away*, and the fire did not care. Fire is honest that way. It was the only honest thing about the war, and both sides used it, and neither side survived it intact, and here I am — the last coin left in a treasury that once held a civilization — still warm enough to spend.

*

Let me tell you how a dragon experiences time, because your keeper will need to know this if he is to carry what I mean to give him, and because no one else is left to teach it.

You live in time the way fish live in water: immersed, unseeing, borne along. We lived in it the way birds live in air — aware of it, working it, riding its currents deliberately. When I close my inner eyelids and sink into the deep rest, a century passes like a night of dreamless sleep. When I wake, the world has rearranged itself around me: forests grown and fallen, rivers moved their beds, languages died and been born from their bones. My first keeper's people called themselves something I can no longer pronounce. Their descendants do not remember they ever existed, though they build their roads over our graves without knowing.

But pain does not pass quickly for us. Pain is the current that runs beneath everything, and in the deep rest I have dreamed the same dream ten thousand times: the vault doors closing behind the last of us, the sound of the outer seals going cold, the smell of dragon-blood on imperial steel drifting down through the stone. I dreamed it while empires rose. I dreamed it while the man who became my last thief was born in a village whose name means *reed-place* in a dialect spoken by fewer than nine thousand souls.

Cyrus. Yes. I knew his name before he ever reached this mountain, the way a hearth knows a hand is reaching for it. He carried my spark against his heart for eleven years, a coal wrapped in flesh, and flesh is a poor lantern but a faithful one. Every mile he walked, I felt him. Every fever, every frozen night on the high shelf, every time he woke gasping from dreams of the burning library — I felt all of it, faintly, the way you might feel rain falling on a roof above your bed. And when he finally came down into the dark and knelt before me with his ruined lungs and his stolen light, and gave back what he took —

Ah. You should know that too. He did not steal from me.

The word *theft* belongs to men, and men use it to describe taking. But Cyrus took nothing I was not already losing. He came to the archive-vault in the capital where they kept the eggs — the empty eggs, the shells we surrendered after the war as terms of peace, thinking the war was over, thinking surrender meant survival — and among those dead shells was one that was not dead. One that still held a flicker. Mine. The last viable seed of my line, kept in a lacquered box between a tax ledger and a list of executed heretics, and the emperor's archivists did not even know what they were curating. To them it was a curiosity. An heirloom of a conquered age.

Cyrus understood what it was. He was a small man in a large machine, a cataloguer, a keeper of records, and he looked at the egg and understood that the empire had not preserved it out of reverence. They had preserved it the way a miser preserves a coin: against future need. And the need, he learned, was coming. There were memos. There are always memos. The fire-weapons program had stalled for lack of a living source, and someone in a high tower wrote, in beautiful calligraphy, that the recovered ovum might serve as kindling.

So he ran. Eleven years running, with my child's heartbeat cupped against his sternum, feeding it his own warmth because a spark needs tending and he was all it had. He thought he was stealing. He told himself stories about theft and treason and the price of conscience. But from inside the dark, from beneath the mountain, I will tell you what actually happened:

A man carried a cradle through a burning world, and refused to put it down.

There is no word for that in any human language I have learned. In ours there was. In ours there were nine words for it, and poems about each.

*

Now the army comes, and the choice stands before me like a forked road, and I find — this shames me, so mark it well — that I do not trust myself to walk either path alone.

Here is the shape of the choice, laid out plainly, because plainness is the last courtesy the old owe the young:

Path the first: I spend myself. All of it, every ember, the hoard of four hundred years of patient banking, poured upward through the stone in a single act of wrath. The vanguard dies. The guild dies with it, the ones who chose wrong, and likely some who chose right — fire being fire, honest and blind. The pass collapses. Thornfell seals itself behind a tomb of slag, and the secret of my existence dies with everyone who knows it, and the empire turns its hunger elsewhere, and perhaps the world gets another hundred years of uneasy quiet.

And the egg dies with me. Because the egg lives on my fire. That is the arithmetic no one wants to speak aloud: the seed I have guarded since before Cyrus was born draws its warmth from the same reserve I would spend on vengeance. To burn the army is to burn the nursery. To save my child, I must let two hundred men climb down to me, and trust to keepers and doors and the fallible courage of small creatures to stop them short of this chamber.

Do you see? Do you see why the old dragons went mad in the end, those few who outlived the purges? Every choice is a slaughter. Every mercy is a weapon turned on yourself. We were built for a world with more sky in it, and this world has too little room, and everything I love fits inside a shell the size of two clasped hands, and everything I hate carries torches.

Path the second: the seed.

To pour the last of me not outward in wrath but inward in hope. Into the stone heart. Into the single vessel Cyrus bled to carry here, that my keeper Oscar holds now like a man holding a sleeping infant he is afraid to wake. It would take everything. There would be no second flame, no final defiance, no glorious immolation to make the ballads weep. I would gutter out mid-gift, and the last sensation of the last dragon of the elder line would be the feeling of giving something away.

And then the fate of my line rests in the hands of — I use the term with love, but precision matters — *mammals*.

A wolf who talks to a god that stopped answering. A boy who has killed one man and come apart doing it. A cartographer who betrayed her empire for the crime of reading its mail. A smuggler chief who has chosen wrongly at every crossroads of his life and is standing, at this moment, at the mouth of my home with imperial soldiers at his shoulder, deciding whether to choose rightly for the first time. A widow walking up a mountain with supplies. And Cyrus, who will not see the spring this gift is meant for.

This is the treasury. This is the vault. Not gold — I ate most of the gold decades ago, it sits in me like gravel, and I regret nothing — but *these*. Five fragile, quarrelsome, mortal creatures, and whatever they decide to build around the thing I leave them.

*

My keeper is coming down to me now.

I feel him on the stair — the long spiral cut by my own claws when the world was younger and I still believed in building for tomorrow — and I feel the fire in his blood, my fire, the spark Cyrus passed him, answering my presence the way a tuning-fork answers its twin. He walks like a wolf. Even burdened, even grieving, even carrying a boy's screams in his ears and a dead man's memories in his head, he moves with that low-hipped economy of the predator who has decided, temporarily, not to hunt. I have watched wolves for four centuries, from the heights, when the world still held heights worth watching. They are maligned. They are not cruel; they are *clear*. They do not pretend their hunger is anything other than hunger, and they do not kill beyond need, and they keep faith with their pack past the point where keeping faith becomes fatal.

Oscar Joubert is the finest wolf I have ever known, and I include the four-legged sort.

He stops on the stair. I feel his hesitation, the way the fire in him flutters. He is afraid of me. Good. Fear is the beginning of wisdom, and the men upstairs who feel none are the ones who will die of it. But under the fear there is something else, and it is this something else that decided me, though I did not know it was deciding me until this moment, sitting in the dark, composing the testament no one asked me for:

He is afraid, and he keeps walking anyway.

Toward me. Toward the thing that could unmake him with a breath. Down the stairs, alone, leaving the others at the seal because he would not risk them — I felt that decision too, felt him order Tam and the cartographer and the wounded smuggler to stay, and felt the boy argue, and felt Oscar refuse with a gentleness that surprised even him. He keeps faith past the point where keeping faith becomes fatal. I told you. Clear as a wolf.

When he reaches the chamber, I will show him what I showed no one else: the stone heart, resting in the basin of my foreclaw where I have held it for eleven years, turning it as a miser turns a coin, as a mother turns a sleeping child. I will show him the quickening — the faint pulse within the shell, faster now than it was a decade ago, fed by Cyrus's devotion and my dwindling warmth. And then I will ask him the question, and I confess I do not know what answer I am hoping for, and this uncertainty is the strangest sensation of my long life, because dragons are not made for hoping. We are made for enduring. Hope is a mammal's organ. Perhaps that is why the egg must go to them. Perhaps hope, like fire, was only ever borrowed from us, and it is time to return it.

The question will be simple. Questions of consequence always are.

*Burn, or bloom.*

I will put it to him plainly, in the old way, the way oaths were put to keepers in the first age before your gods were even young: not as a request, because I have no right to request — I am asking a dying species' last question, and the asker does not command the answerer — but as a handing-over. Here is the wrath. Here is the cradle. You are the wolf, and the pack is yours, and I am done. Choose for both of us, because I have carried this choice for four hundred years and my claws can hold it no longer, and because — this is the truth beneath the truth — because I believe you will choose better than I would.

I would burn them. All of them. I have imagined it ten thousand times in the deep rest, the sweet architecture of it, the glass river, the silence after. I am old and I am full of graves and wrath is the last language I speak fluently.

But he buried a friend beside my door today, and stood over the grave with snow in his hair, and swore something out loud to people who could hear him — not to a silent god, not to an absent empire, but to the living — and when he swore it, the fire in his chest leaned toward his words like a plant toward light.

A creature like that does not burn nurseries.

His boots ring on the stair now. Closer. The fire in him stirs and recognizes me, and I stir in answer, and for one suspended moment — the last such moment I will ever have — the last dragon and her last keeper are two flames leaning toward each other across the dark.

Above us, muffled through forty fathoms of stone, the imperial sappers set their first charge against the outer door.

Below us, inside the shell in my claw, something very small turns over in its sleep.

The stair echoes. He is here.

It is time to ask.
