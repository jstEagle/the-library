## 34. Oscar's Choice

The dragon's question hung in the dark like frost forming on stone.

*Which, keeper? The fire that ends them, or the fire that begins again?*

Oscar stood in the vault beneath Thornfell with the last dragon's breath moving over him — a heat that was not heat, a pressure that was not wind, something older than either. Around him the great chamber breathed. He could feel it now as he had never felt anything in his life: every vein of ore in the mountain singing faintly, the slow pulse of magma leagues below like a second heartbeat answering the first, and above it all — far above, through half a mile of granite and ice — the sound of men.

Imperial outriders. Guild turncoats who had chosen coin over kin. Pickaxes already ringing against the outer door like woodpeckers working at the world's oldest tree.

The dragon waited for its answer.

It was vast beyond sense, even now, even guttering. Oscar had seen its shape only in fragments since they had descended into the vault — a shoulder like a cliff face, a wing folded against darkness like a sail furled for winter, one eye opening here and there in the black, amber and enormous, regarding him with a patience that made his knees want to bend. It had been alive down here for three hundred years. Kept alive by a stolen spark carried across an empire in the chest of a dying man. And now that spark lived in *him*, coiled behind his sternum where his god's silence used to ache, and he could feel how little of it remained. A candle cupped in a fist. A coal wrapped in ash.

Enough to burn an army. Enough to make a grave of the pass so deep and so wide that no cartographer would ever draw it true again.

Or enough to do what Cyrus had spent his whole life doing.

"Show me," Oscar said. His voice came out rough, scraped raw from days of smoke and siege-rations and shouting orders in corridors built for dragons, not men. "Both of them. Show me both."

The eye regarded him. Something moved in its depths, slow as continents shifting.

*I have never shown a keeper both,* the dragon said, and its voice was not a voice — it arrived fully formed inside his skull, the way memories from Cyrus had once arrived, unbidden and burning. *The first choice is easy to show. The second is hard.*

"Everything worth doing has been hard lately." Oscar braced himself. "Show me."

* * *

The vengeance came first, because vengeance always comes first.

It poured into him like swallowed brandy, hot and immediate and glorious. He saw it — no, he *lived* it, because this dragon did not know how to show anything small. He stood on the high shelf above the Narrows with fire pooling in his throat and looked down at the imperial column snaking through the pass, two hundred men in lacquered scale, banner-bearers and sappers and the fire-wagons with their iron cisterns meant to drain a dying heart. He opened his mouth.

What followed was not battle. Battle implies two sides.

He burned the vanguard where it stood, and the snow on the peaks flashed to steam in a white wall that rose higher than the crags. He melted the ice-bridges and dropped a hundred men screaming into the Weeping Stair. He caught the fire-wagons in his talons and crushed them like a child crushing beetles, and when the survivors fled back down the pass he herded them with flame the way a shepherd dogs sheep, patiently, thoroughly, until nothing moved in Thornfell but smoke. He felt their deaths. That was the part no one told you about dragon-fire: it did not kill at a distance. Every man it took, it took through *him*. He tasted the terror-sweat of the boy-soldier who had lied about his age. He heard the last prayer of the sergeant whose name he would have learned if he'd let him live another hour. Two hundred lives, passing through him like coins through a cutpurse's hands, each one leaving its weight behind.

And then the vision widened, the way visions do, showing him the morning after and the year after. The empire would not send two hundred next time. They would send twenty thousand, and after them forty, because empires are wounds that only grow when you cut them. They would come with ballistae and alchemist-fire and the new weapons Ilsa had described around the fire — heart-fed engines that could burn a city from the horizon. And he would burn those too, and take more of them into himself, and somewhere in that arithmetic he would spend the last of the spark, and the dragon would die anyway, and the egg —

There was no egg in that future. There was only a mountain full of ash, an empire hardened by grievance, and a war that would outlive everyone he loved. Tam grown old on a battlefield. Ilsa hanged as a traitor twice over. Sefa's kitchen cold forever.

The vision released him and Oscar found himself on his knees on the vault floor, gasping, tasting sulfur and someone else's blood.

"Now the other one," he said.

* * *

This time the dragon did not pour anything into him. It only opened, the way a hand opens, and let him look.

He saw the chamber as it truly was: not empty, not dead. At the center of the vault, in a basin of stone worn smooth by centuries of careful breathing, lay an egg.

It was the size of a wine cask. It was grey as riverstone and veined with hairline threads of gold, and it was cold — had been cold for three hundred years, cold as the day the Emperor's archivists catalogued its mother's capture and wrote, in ink that Cyrus had once bled to steal, *Specimen inert; recommend destruction.* It had lain in the dark while its mother was taken south in chains. It had lain in the dark while she was drained, year by year, to light the lamps of an empire that told itself stories about monsters. It had lain in the dark through everything, because a dragon's egg does not measure time the way we do. It simply waits for fire.

And it was waiting now.

Oscar understood, in the way the blood-oath had taught him to understand things — suddenly, wholly, without steps — that the choice had never really been between vengeance and mercy. Vengeance was just the fire spending itself loudly. Mercy was the fire spending itself *quietly*, all of it, every last guttering ember of a three-hundred-year life poured into a single vessel of stone with no guarantee that anything would ever wake inside it. No army destroyed. No pass sealed in righteous ruin. Just an act of faith performed alone in the dark, witnessed by no one, that might amount to nothing at all.

That was the harder thing. That was so much harder than burning.

He thought of Cyrus then — not the courier he'd carried off the switchbacks, not the delirious stranger who had pressed a warm map into his hands and made him swear. He thought of the man underneath all that, the man whose memories still lived in Oscar's chest like a second tenant: a boy in a library tower copying out imperial edicts by candlelight, growing sick at the margins, writing *no* in tiny letters beside orders signed by the Emperor's own hand. A young archivist smuggling out a single page, then a single spark, then a single impossible hope, knowing he would probably die for it and doing it anyway — not because he believed he would win, but because someone had to carry the truth even if carrying it changed nothing. *Someone has to be the one who doesn't put it down.*

Cyrus had died at dawn eleven days ago with his hand in Oscar's, giving away the last warm thing he owned, and he had smiled while he did it. Oscar had screamed with a voice that wasn't entirely his own and the mountain had answered, and ever since he had been walking around inside a grief so large it had its own weather.

He thought of Sefa, who fed strangers and asked nothing, who was climbing toward the mountain right now with a mule-train of flour and salt because that was how she fought wars — with bread. He thought of Ilsa, who had burned her career and her name to stand in the dark with enemies, drafting lies in perfect imperial cipher to buy strangers time. He thought of Brannoc, dead two days now on the vault steps, holding the gate with a guild-maul in his fists, having finally — *finally* — chosen right, and paid the whole price without flinching.

And he thought of Tam. Gods, Tam. Fourteen years old, with his first kill still living behind his eyes like a splinter, who had asked the question in the dark of the vault that had cracked Oscar open: *which matters more, revenge or protection?* And Oscar had given him no answer, because there wasn't one, and the boy had nodded as if that honesty were somehow enough.

But it wasn't enough, was it. Not tonight. Tonight the question had come back wearing scales, and it wanted a real answer.

The pickaxes rang above. Closer now. Through the dragon's senses Oscar could feel the outer door's great bolts shearing one by one, could taste torch-smoke filtering through stone, could hear a lieutenant's voice calling measurements — *breach by morning, the cisterns ready by dusk.* By dusk they would be standing in this chamber with drills and cisterns, and whether Oscar chose fire or egg, they were going to find the last dragon of Thornfell.

Unless.

His old god had gone silent ten years ago, and Oscar had spent ten years telling himself that silence was the truest sermon: that oaths were coin, loyalty was a ledger, and every altar was just a place where something had stopped listening. He had believed that so long it had grown into his bones like a splinter grows into wood.

But that was before. Before a stranger bled on the cold road and Oscar, against every instinct, had picked him up. Before a blood-oath had answered in his chest for the first time in a decade. Before smugglers and deserters and a defector and a widow and a grieving boy had gathered around him like embers around a coal, not because he commanded them but because he kept showing up, kept choosing them, kept refusing to put them down.

Nothing had ever answered his prayers. But everything he had built with his hands was alive, and breathing, and counting on him.

Faith wasn't a voice from the sky. Faith was the choosing. It had always been the choosing.

Oscar got up off his knees.

"I choose the egg," he said.

The great eye closed. When it opened again, Oscar would have sworn — and he was a man who knew oaths — that something in it had relaxed, some tension held for three hundred years finally set down.

*You are certain,* the dragon said. It was not quite a question. *They will breach the door by dawn. My fire will be gone. You will have a stone that may never wake, a mountain you cannot hold, and nothing left to fight with but your wits and your people.*

"That's more than most folk get," Oscar said. "I've seen men fight wars with less."

*And if the egg never quickens? If I pour myself into stone and nothing stirs, and the empire takes it anyway?*

"Then it never wakes," Oscar said, "and they get a rock. But they don't get your fire. They don't get a weapon. And we—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—we'll still be here. All of us. What we built doesn't burn just because you do."

The dragon was quiet a long moment. In that silence Oscar felt it considering him — not the oath in his veins, not the spark, but *him*: the mercenary called Wolf who had prayed to nothing for ten years and kept walking anyway.

*Cyrus chose well,* it said at last. *Hold out your hands, keeper.*

* * *

It did not hurt the way he expected. He expected fire.

Instead the dragon began to give itself away the way a long winter gives way to spring — gradually, gently, from the edges inward. Oscar felt it as warmth first, then as memory. Three hundred years moved through him: the taste of high thin air above the cloud-sea, the names of winds that no human tongue had spoken since, the slow green centuries when Thornfell's valleys had thundered with wings. A mate, lost to the hunters' harpoons on a coast far south of anywhere maps went. An egg laid in secret. The chains. The dark. And through all of it, held like a coal in cupped hands, the patient stubborn hope that someday, *someday*, someone would come down into the dark and choose the future instead of the past.

The spark behind his sternum rose to meet it — Cyrus's stolen fragment, the piece the archivist had carried across an empire — and the two fires recognized each other the way a river recognizes rain.

Then the dragon poured itself out.

Not into Oscar. Through him. He was only the door, the sworn keeper, the lock turned by an oath. The last fire of the last dragon of Thornfell flowed through his chest and down his arms and out of his palms in a light that was gold and white and soft as morning through linen, and it sank into the grey egg like water sinking into drought-cracked earth. The golden veins in the stone brightened. The whole egg began to glow from within, faintly, then steadily, a lantern lit in a cellar after three hundred years of night. Warmth rolled off it in waves. Somewhere in the mountain, deep seams of stone that had been cold since before the empire existed woke with a low harmonic hum, and Oscar felt the sound in his teeth, in his ribs, in the oath scar on his palm that had never properly healed.

And the dragon — the vast, ancient, guttering presence that had filled the vault like a cathedral fills with silence — grew smaller. Quieter. Dimmer. Not dying the way fire dies, Oscar realized. Dying the way a mother sleeps after a birth: emptied, spent, at peace.

*It is done,* it said, and its voice was very far away now, and very gentle. *The fire is in the stone. Whether it wakes is not mine to know, nor yours. Keeper—*

"Yes?"

*When my kind were many, we kept oaths too. Fire-oaths, older than your gods. Your silent one — the Oathfather your order served — he learned his trade at our hearths, did you know? Your gods are children playing at our games.* A pause, and something that might, in a being that old, have been amusement. *He did not go silent, Wolf. He simply had nothing left to say that his servants wished to hear. You have always been better at listening than your order ever was.*

Oscar laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound that echoed strangely in the vault. Ten years of silence, ten years of shame and hollow prayers, and the truth was that his god had simply run out of sermons. "Then what am I supposed to pray to now?"

*Nothing,* said the dragon. *Pray to nothing. Keep your vows to the living. That was always the whole of the law; everything else was commentary.* The last of its presence gathered itself, faint as breath on a window. *Guard the stone. Guard the girl who reads stars and the boy who asks questions and the woman who burns her letters. They are rarer than dragons, in this age. Rarer than I was.*

And then, like a tide going out, the dragon withdrew into whatever small dark corner of itself remained — a pilot-light, a seed-coal, barely there — and the vault was quiet except for the hum of warming stone and the glow of the egg, steady as a hearth.

Above, distantly, something boomed. The outer door's last bolt. Shouting. Torchlight flickering through cracks that hadn't been there yesterday.

By dawn they would be through. By midmorning they would be in the galleries. Oscar stood alone in the birthing-chamber with a glowing egg, a dying friend's fire in his chest, and perhaps four hours to move a stone the weight of a wine cask up a corridor built for creatures the size of cathedrals — or to find another way, because there was always another way, Sefa would have said, if you had bread and patience.

He put his hands flat against the warm shell. It pulsed under his palms. Once. Twice. Faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Or maybe — maybe — not faint at all. Maybe just new. Maybe just learning the rhythm.

"Tam!" Oscar shouted up the corridor, and his voice carried the way voices carry in mountains, gathering echo, gathering help. "Ilsa! Get down here — bring the sledges and everyone who can lift!"

The egg pulsed again under his hands, a little stronger, a little surer, and far above, iron gave way with a scream, and the empire came pouring into the mountain — into the dark, into the deep places, where something small and winged and impossibly patient had just begun, very quietly, to wake.
