## 36. Aftermath: Counting the Living

The mountain breathed ash for three days.

It came down in slow grey spirals, settling on the shoulders of the living like a benediction no one had asked for. Oscar stood at the mouth of the collapsed pass on the first morning and watched the dust drift through the light, fine as flour, softening every edge of the world. Somewhere beneath all that stillness lay an imperial vanguard — two hundred men who had marched into Thornfell with fire-weapons and certainty and come out as part of the geology. The mountain had closed over them the way a hand closes over a moth.

He should have felt triumph. He felt nothing of the kind.

Behind him, the vault's outer chamber was a hospital, a kitchen, a graveyard-in-waiting. Eleven of Brannoc's guild had survived the turning; four of those would not see another week. Ilsa moved among the wounded with the clipped efficiency of someone who had triaged battlefields before, her imperial training repurposed so thoroughly she'd stopped flinching when people called her by name instead of rank. Tam sat against the wall with his hands wrapped, having refused to let anyone look at the burns until the wounded were seen to first — a thing he'd learned from Cyrus, though he didn't know it yet, or maybe he did.

Oscar counted the living because someone had to, and because counting was easier than feeling.

"Forty-one," Ilsa said, appearing at his shoulder with a slate and a stub of chalk. "Forty-one alive inside the mountain. Another nine scattered in the high camps — Sefa's sent word up the goat trails. She'll bring them down."

"And the dead?"

Ilsa was quiet a moment. Her chalk hovered over the slate. "Twenty-three that we can reach. More under the collapse. We won't get them all."

"No," Oscar said. "We won't."

* * *

They buried the dead where the ground was softest — a shelf of meadow below the tree line, where the snowmelt ran silver over stones and the pines stood thick enough to break the wind. It took two full days. There weren't enough shovels, so they used mule-blades and helmet-pans and their bare hands, and by the end of it Oscar's palms were torn open again over the half-healed cuts from the siege, and he found he didn't mind. Pain was honest. Pain told him exactly what it was.

Brannoc they buried last, apart, facing the gate he had died holding.

The guild chief had gone out on the third day of the siege with a shield wall of smugglers behind him and nothing in his hands but a hooked blade and thirty years of grudges, and he had held the outer gate long enough for the dragon-fire to do its work. They'd pulled him from the rubble with his back against the doorframe, as if even dead he intended to keep standing there. Half the guild wept over him. The other half spat and turned away, remembering the ledger, remembering the men he'd sold. Both halves were right. Oscar said a few words anyway, because Brannoc had been the first person in ten years to pay him on time, and because the dead deserve one voice each, even the complicated ones.

"He was a thief," Oscar said, "and a liar, and at the end he chose the door over the coin." He looked at the assembled guildsmen, their faces grey with ash and grief. "That's more than most of us manage. Bury him deep."

Then they carried Cyrus up.

* * *

Oscar had known, since the moment the spark left Cyrus and entered him on that dawn that had screamed with a voice not entirely his own, that this burial was coming. He had thought himself prepared. He had rehearsed words in the dark hours between watches, tidy little sentences about courage and sacrifice that sounded well in the head and would surely die in the throat.

None of them came. What came instead was memory — Cyrus's memory, still lodged in him like a splinter he no longer wished to remove: a boy in a white archive coat, copying maps by candlelight while his teacher murmured that knowledge was a sacred trust; a young man running through a burning library with a stolen map pressed to his chest, embers in his hair, a child's voice screaming somewhere behind him; a courier crossing half a continent with death riding his heels, stopping only once — at Hollowpine — to mend a widow's fence without being asked, because kindness was the only currency he had left that couldn't be confiscated.

Oscar knelt beside the body and let the memories move through him like weather.

Cyrus looked small now. He had looked small for weeks, the illness and the road stripping him down, but death had finished the work, leaving something almost weightless, a man-shaped absence wrapped in the blanket Sefa had woven. His face had finally relaxed. In life he had worn a constant expression of apology — sorry for the trouble, sorry for the burden, sorry for the secret. Now there was only peace, and it made Oscar furious in a way he couldn't name, because Cyrus had earned peace and hadn't lived long enough to enjoy a single hour of it.

"We should say something," Tam said. The boy's voice cracked on *something*. His hands were bandaged to the wrists. He had killed his first man in the dark of the vault and come apart afterward, and he had not fully reassembled, and Oscar suspected none of them had either — they were all just better at hiding the cracks in daylight.

"You knew him best," Ilsa said quietly.

"I carried him," Oscar said. "That's not the same as knowing him." But it was a lie, and he knew it, and he suspected everyone standing there knew it too. He knew Cyrus better than he had ever known any living soul. He knew the smell of the archive's tallow candles. He knew the lullaby Cyrus's mother had sung, in a language dead a century. He knew why a man with no soldier's training had walked into the Emperor's own vault and committed treason with steady hands: because he had read the requisition orders, and understood what *drain the heart* meant, and could not un-understand it.

So Oscar spoke, and it wasn't the speech he'd rehearsed.

"His name was Cyrus Vael," he said. "He was a cartographer. He stole a map to save a creature most of the world thinks is a story, and he gave his life getting it here, and he never once asked for anything for himself except that we keep going." His voice held. He was almost surprised. "I swore an oath to him on a cold road above Greywater Ford. Blood-oath. Old magic, older than any god I ever prayed to. I made it thinking I was taking a job." He paused. "I was wrong. I was being chosen. And I have spent every day since trying to be worth the choosing."

Sefa was crying silently, her work-roughened hands pressed over her mouth. She had arrived three days ago with supplies and nine stragglers from the high camps, taken one look at the ash-choked pass, and started organizing burials before she'd set down her pack. That was Sefa. Grief was a chore like any other, and chores were meant to be done properly.

They lowered him into the ground beside the sealed door — not the great outer gate Brannoc had died holding, but the inner door, the one the oath in Oscar's veins had turned open, the one the dragon's final fire had fused shut forever. It seemed right. Cyrus had spent his whole life walking toward that door. He could rest against it now, keeper of the threshold even in death.

Tam placed the redrawn map on Cyrus's chest before they closed the grave — the copy, inked from memory in the last lucid days, its lines already burned into the boy's mind the way the original had been burned into Cyrus's. "So he has one," Tam explained, when Ilsa raised an eyebrow. "In case he needs to find his way somewhere."

No one argued. Some theologies you don't question at a graveside.

* * *

That night the survivors gathered in the meadow, because no one wanted to sleep underground anymore, not yet, not with the stone still groaning overhead in voices only the newly buried could interpret. Fires were lit. Someone produced a barrel of plum brandy from the wreckage of Brannoc's stores, and the guild drank to their dead chief with the particular ferocity of people who had hated him and followed him anyway.

Oscar sat apart, as he always had, on a flat stone at the meadow's edge. Below him the valley fell away into darkness. Above him the stars wheeled, indifferent and ancient, the same constellations Cyrus had taught Tam on the high shelf — the Dragon's Spine, the Ember Road, the Weaver's Loom. He found he could not look at the Dragon's Spine without hearing Cyrus's voice reciting the old names, patient, delighted, a scholar's joy surviving even delirium.

His god was still silent.

He noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth — the tongue keeps returning to the gap. Ten years of silence, ten years of praying out of habit to a god of oaths who had stopped listening, and now, after everything — after the vault and the dragon and the dying and the choice — still nothing. No warmth. No answer. The silence where his faith used to live remained exactly as silent as it had been on the worst night of his life, the night he'd learned that oaths sworn loudly mean nothing if the swearer's heart is empty.

But something else was listening. Something older. He could feel it faintly, the way you feel a hearth through a wall — the guttering warmth of the dragon's last fire, now poured into a single stone heart deep beneath the mountain, banked and waiting. And threaded through it, unmistakable, the spark that had been Cyrus's, that was now his own, burning low and steady in his chest like a coal that refused to go out.

He was not alone in his own skin anymore. He had stopped fighting it somewhere around the time he'd screamed with a dead man's voice and the mountain had answered.

Footsteps crunched behind him. He didn't turn. He knew the walk — Ilsa's, precise, military, softened lately by exhaustion into something almost human.

"The wounded will survive," she said, sitting down beside him without invitation. She'd earned that. "All but two. And the guild's voted — what's left of it. They want to stay."

"Stay?"

"Here. Under the mountain. Wardens, they're calling themselves." She snorted softly. "Smugglers. Wardens. The empire will laugh itself sick if it ever finds out."

"It won't find out," Oscar said. "That's rather the point of wardens."

She was quiet for a while. Down in the meadow, someone had begun a song — a smuggler's ballad, bawdy and off-key, and Tam's thin voice had joined it, wavering, and Sefa's strong one underneath, steadying the boy the way she steadied everything.

"I've been thinking about my report," Ilsa said. "The real one. The one I'm supposed to send telling them the vault is destroyed, the heart is lost, the operation failed." She turned the brandy bottle in her hands. "Every word true. Every word a lie. If they believe it, they stop coming. If they check—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to. If they checked, they'd execute her, and then they'd come anyway.

"Then don't send it yet," Oscar said. "Send it when we're ready to be found. Or never."

"That's not how duty works."

"No," Oscar agreed. "Duty is how they tell you to work. This is different." He looked at her. "This is choosing."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fires had burned to coals and the singers had gone quiet and the meadow held only the breathing of the exhausted — Oscar stood alone before the graves.

There were twenty-three of them now, mounded and marked with river stones, each stone carried up from the creek by someone who had loved the sleeper beneath it, or liked them, or simply served beside them, which in the end amounted to nearly the same thing. Cyrus's grave stood first among them, closest to the sealed door, and Oscar laid his palm flat on the turned earth and felt the faint pulse beneath — not the grave, not the dead man, but the door, the vault, the stone heart banking its fire in the dark.

*Cyrus,* he said, in the privacy of his skull, where the blood-oath had made a room for two. *You absolute stubborn, self-sacrificing fool. You made me care about something again. I hope you know what an inconvenience that is.*

And in the room behind his ribs, where the spark burned, he felt — not words, exactly, but something warmer than words. Amusement, maybe. Forgiveness, certainly. A dead man's blessing, folded small enough to carry.

Oscar Joubert knelt in the ash-fall and the starlight, and he swore.

Not to the god of oaths, whose silence had taught him everything vows were not. Not to the empire, nor the guild, nor any altar of stone or sky. He swore to the living — to the forty-one breathing souls under the mountain and the nine still coming down the goat trails, to the widow mending gear by firelight, to the boy with bandaged hands learning to sleep without screaming, to the defector weighing truth like a blade, to the smugglers learning that some doors are worth guarding for free.

"I swear by the fire I carry," he said aloud, and his voice carried across the meadow, waking a bird, startling no one who mattered, "to keep what is kept here. To guard the living who guard it with me. To spend myself before I spend the trust you've put in me. Not because a god commands it. Because I choose it. Every morning, I choose it. And if the day comes when I cannot choose it anymore—" his hand tightened on the earth over Cyrus's heart "—then I pray one of you is standing close enough to take the vow from my mouth and make it yours. That's the whole of it. That's the oath. No thunder required."

Silence. Then, from the darkness near the biggest fire, Sefa's voice, dry as her good bread: "Well. About time somebody said it out loud."

Laughter, tired and ragged and real, moving through the survivors like warmth through cold hands. Tam's voice, sleepy: "Does that make us your congregation, Wolf?"

"It makes you my witnesses," Oscar called back. "Congregations kneel. Witnesses get breakfast."

More laughter. Then quiet again, the good kind, the kind that comes after storms.

Oscar stayed kneeling a moment longer. Beneath his palm, beneath the earth and the stone and the fused-shut door, the dragon's heart pulsed once — slow, vast, patient as geology — and around it, smaller and quicker, something else stirred. Something that had not stirred before. A flicker where there had been only banked coals. A quickening, faint as a second heartbeat hidden inside the first.

He went very still.

Deep in the dark beneath Thornfell, in the vault where the last of the old fire had been poured into a single stone, something small was beginning to answer the warmth.

Oscar lifted his hand from the grave and stood, and looked toward the mountain, and felt the spark in his chest lean toward it like a plant toward sun.

*Tam,* he thought. *Someone's going to need to meet you.*
