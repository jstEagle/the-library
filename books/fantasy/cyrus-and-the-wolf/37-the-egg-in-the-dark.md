## 37. The Egg in the Dark

The vault had seasons of its own.

That was the first thing Tam understood, down there beneath the mountain where no sun had ever reached — that a place could have weather without sky. There were warm hours and cold hours, and the air moved in slow tides, drawn up from somewhere deeper, breathing out through the cracks in the old stairs like the whole mountain was sleeping with its mouth slightly open. In his first weeks as a warden he'd learned to read it: when the air went still and heavy, the dragon-fire in the deep chamber was guttering low. When it stirred and warmed, something down there was dreaming hard.

He kept count now. He kept count of everything. That was what Ilsa had taught him first — *a warden who doesn't keep records is only a guard, and guards get replaced* — so every morning he climbed the worn steps to the tally wall and scratched his marks into the stone with a bit of charred stick. Warm days. Cold days. The number of crates of salted fish left in the stores. The day Mirabel's cough started, and the day it stopped. The day Oscar came back up from the deep chamber without speaking to anyone, sat by the entrance for four hours watching snow fall past the broken lintel, and then got up and made dinner for everyone like nothing had happened.

Tam hadn't asked him about that day. He'd learned that too. Some questions you asked out loud and some questions you just held, and waited, and the answers came to you on their own time — usually at night, usually in the dark, usually from someone who couldn't sleep either.

It was on one of those nights, a little more than a year after the mountain fell on the imperial vanguard, that Tam heard the heart change.

* * *

He'd been sleeping in the second antechamber, the small round room off the main gallery where the walls were smooth black stone and held warmth like a banked oven. It was his favorite place in the vault. Sefa said it was because it reminded him of being held; Tam said it was because it was quiet. Both of them were probably right, and neither of them said so.

He woke not with a sound but with a feeling — the way you wake when someone has said your name softly in another room. The air in the antechamber was moving wrong. Not the slow tidal breath he knew. This was quicker. Lighter. Almost eager.

He sat up. His breath made a small ghost in front of him, which meant the room was cooling, which was strange, because the antechamber never cooled. He pulled his boots on over bare feet, took the lamp, and went out into the gallery.

The gallery ran the length of the buried hall — forty paces of floor polished by ten thousand years of taloned feet, though the talons were gone now and the feet were only Oscar's and Ilsa's and his own, and once a month Sefa's, when she came down with supplies and refused, every single time, to let anyone else carry the heavy basket. The dragon's body lay at the far end, in the great hollowed basin where it had settled itself a century ago to die slowly and deliberately, folding its wings like a letter being closed. Tam still couldn't look directly at it some days. Other days he swept the sand around it carefully, the way you'd straighten a bed for someone who was ill.

Tonight the light was coming from the basin.

Not the old light — the guttering amber glow that lived along the dragon's ribs like embers under ash, the light they'd all learned to measure their future by. This was different. Whiter. Younger. And it wasn't coming from the dragon at all.

It was coming from the stone heart.

They'd set it in the shallow depression beside the great head — the egg, though it didn't look like any egg Tam had ever seen. It looked like a river stone the size of a bread loaf, grey and unremarkable, the kind of thing you'd skip across water without a second thought. Oscar had carried it out of the dying fire himself, both hands cupped around it like a man carrying water in a drought, and they'd set it down there because the dragon had told Oscar — in whatever voice passed between keeper and kept — that it should be near.

For a year it had done nothing. Sat there. Grey. Cold to the touch except for the faintest warmth, like a stone left too near a hearth. Ilsa had taken its temperature every week and recorded it, and the numbers barely moved, and Tam had watched her face grow longer each month until she stopped writing the numbers down at all.

Now it glowed. Faintly, from inside, the way a lantern glows through paper — but white, and moving, and when Tam crept closer with his lamp held high and his heart going like a drum, he saw that the light pulsed.

Once. Twice. A pause. Twice again.

Like a heartbeat. Like a small heartbeat, quick and new, keeping time against the vast slow thunder of the old one fading beside it.

"Oh," Tam said aloud, to nobody, because there was nobody to say it to. "Oh, hello."

* * *

He didn't wake anyone.

He thought about it. He stood there for a long moment with his mouth open, ready to run and hammer on Oscar's door — and then he looked at the egg, glowing and pulsing in the dark, and he thought about what it would be like to be brand new and have six people come stamping toward you shouting your name before you'd even opened your eyes.

So instead he sat down cross-legged in the sand an arm's length away, put out his lamp, and stayed.

The dark was total without the flame, but it didn't matter, because the egg lit itself now — soft white pulses washing over the dragon's scaled jaw, over the black walls, over Tam's own hands where they rested on his knees. He could see the grain of the stone. He could see, or thought he could see, veins in it now, fine dark threads running through the grey like roots under ice, and every pulse the threads brightened a little and spread a little, reaching.

"You're early," he whispered. "Ilsa said spring at the soonest. She had a whole chart."

The egg pulsed. It didn't mean anything. He knew it didn't mean anything — Ilsa would have said it was coincidence, heat expansion, gas movement, any of the fifty dry words she used when she wanted to believe something couldn't be magic. But it had pulsed *when he spoke*, and Tam was eleven years old and had spent a year learning to trust the things that happened when he spoke, and so he tried again.

"I'm Tam." A pause. Pulse. "My mother died at Greywater Ford. The riders burned it. I don't — I don't remember her face right anymore, just her hands. Is that terrible?"

Pulse. Pulse.

"It's not terrible," he decided. "Cyrus said — well. Oscar remembers Cyrus, sort of, the blood-oath, he carries him around in his head like a coal in a pocket. And Cyrus said grief is just love with nowhere to go. So my love goes here now. If that's all right."

The pulse came quicker. Tam felt it in the sand under him, a faint tremor, a bird's-heart rhythm traveling up through his legs into his chest, and his own heart — he noticed this with a kind of wonder — began trying to match it. Speeding up to meet it. Two rhythms arguing, then overlapping, then —

Then something looked at him.

There was no other way to describe it. Nothing opened, nothing turned, no eye appeared in the stone. But Tam felt attention settle on him the way sunlight settles on a patch of floor — warm, weighty, deliberate. It considered him. He had the wild certainty that it was considering everything about him: the burn scar on his wrist from the night Greywater burned, the calluses on his fingers from the tally-charcoal, the exact weight of everything he'd just said, sorted and kept.

And underneath the attention, faint as a word spoken in another room, something reached for him — not words, not yet, but the shape of a question. He felt it the way you feel someone tugging gently at your sleeve.

"I don't know how to answer you," Tam said honestly. "Nobody taught me this part."

The question shaped itself anyway. Slowly. Patiently. Like frost forming on glass, patterns assembling out of nothing until suddenly you could read them:

*You are not afraid.*

"No," Tam said, and found it was true. "Should I be?"

A long silence. The pulse slowed, steadied, thoughtful.

*Everyone else is afraid,* the not-voice said. *The tall one is afraid of failing. The sharp one is afraid of being wrong. The one who keeps me is afraid of becoming what he killed. You are afraid of ordinary things — the dark, hunger, being sent away. You are not afraid of me.*

"Oscar isn't afraid of becoming what he killed," Tam said, stung on his behalf. "He just — thinks about it a lot. There's a difference."

The attention warmed. He had the sudden absurd sense of having amused something ancient, of a joke landing in a place where jokes hadn't landed in a hundred years.

*You defend him.*

"Somebody should. He won't do it himself." Tam hugged his knees. The egg-light moved on his face like water-light. "He carried you out of a fire, you know. Out of the middle of a battle. Brannoc died holding the door so he could walk. And Cyrus gave him — everything. So maybe I'm defending all three of them when I defend him. Maybe that's allowed."

*It is allowed,* said the egg gravely, and Tam laughed — a small startled laugh that bounced around the black gallery and came back sounding bigger than he'd made it, and far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone, he imagined it waking Oscar anyway.

* * *

He fell asleep there, sometime in the small hours, cheek against cool sand, the heartbeat pulsing against his ear like surf. He dreamed of Greywater Ford without fire in it for the first time — the ford brown and calm, the willows green, his mother's hands turning him by the shoulders toward the mountains, toward something waiting.

When he woke, gray dawn-light was seeping down the stairwell from the world above, and Oscar was sitting beside him.

Tam didn't know how long he'd been there. He was sitting exactly the way Tam had been sitting, cross-legged, elbows on knees, and his face was doing the thing it did when he was listening to the deep places — eyes half-shut, breath slow, jaw tight. The wolf-face, Sefa called it. But there was something else in it this morning. Something Tam had only seen once before, the day they'd buried Cyrus beside the sealed door, when Oscar had finished the rite and stood there a long time and finally said, to the grave, to the mountain, to nobody: *he'd have liked you all.*

"What did you say to it?" Oscar asked quietly, without opening his eyes.

"Just talked." Tam shrugged. "Told it about Mum. About the tally wall. About how you pretend you're not —"

"Tally wall," Oscar said quickly.

"That's what I thought." Tam grinned. Then, because the question had been sitting in him all night, growing heavier the way questions did: "Oscar. Why me? Why'd it talk to me first? You're the keeper. It should've called *you*."

Oscar was quiet for a while. Down the gallery, the egg pulsed its small white pulse, steady as a lighthouse, and the old glow along the dragon's ribs answered it — slower, dimmer, but answering, two rhythms calling to each other across the space of a grave.

"A year ago," Oscar said finally, "the dragon asked me to choose what to do with the last of its fire. Burn the army, or make an egg. Vengeance or a future. And I chose the egg because of you people — because of what we'd built instead of what we'd burned." He rubbed a hand over his face. "But I've been down here every week since, talking to that stone, and it never once answered me. I thought maybe I'd chosen wrong. Or maybe the spark was dead after all and we were keeping a rock."

"And it wasn't?"

"And it was waiting," Oscar said. "Waiting to see who'd come sit with it without wanting anything." He opened his eyes and looked at Tam — really looked, the way he rarely did, straight on, no wolf in it at all. "Brannoc wanted its fire. The empire wanted its fire. I wanted its fire, if I'm honest — a god that answers, after ten years of nothing, I'd have taken it. You walked down here in the dark and told it about your mother's hands and asked for nothing back." His voice went rough. "That's the right question, lad. *What do you need?* Not *what can you give me.* Turns out it's the rarest question there is."

Tam absorbed this. Outside, muffled by all that stone, he could hear Sefa arriving at the upper gate, calling down the usual greeting — *"Wardens! Open before my arms give out!"* — and the clatter of Ilsa's boots on the stairs, and the ordinary beautiful noise of the family starting its day.

"Does this change anything?" he asked. "The wardens' compact, and — does everyone have to redo the oath, or —"

"It changes everything," Oscar said, standing, offering Tam a hand up. "Starting with the fact that I'm no longer the most important person in this mountain, thank the silent gods. Come on. We'll tell them together. And Tam —"

"Yes?"

"When it starts asking real questions — and it will, sooner than you think — you answer them honestly. Even the ones with no good answer. Especially those." Oscar's mouth did the almost-smile. "You're better at that than the rest of us."

They climbed the stairs side by side, and behind them the egg pulsed on in the dark — quicker now, brighter, its root-veins of light spreading through grey stone like dawn coming up under the skin of the world — and far above, through the broken lintel where snowmelt dripped, Tam could hear the sound he loved best in the world: spring water running down Thornfell, loud and impatient, as if the whole mountain had decided, all at once, to wake up.
