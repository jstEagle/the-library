## 38. Letters Never Sent

The ink froze before it reached the page.

Ilsa Vane had learned to write in the Emperor's archive, where the air was kept warm as a bath and every quill was trimmed to the same length by the same hand, because the Empire believed that even handwriting should be uniform, obedient, identical. Here, in a vault carved beneath Thornfell where the cold came up through the stone like breath from something sleeping, her ink turned to slush in its horn before she could finish a sentence.

She held the horn over the small clay brazier Tam had made for her — he was good with clay, good with his hands, good in all the quiet ways no one had ever asked him to be — and waited for the ink to loosen, and watched the first line of her report dry on the page in front of her.

*To the Office of Cartographic Intelligence, Ninth Bureau, from Senior Surveyor Ilsa Vane, attached to the Thornfell Expeditionary Column.*

It was a lie already. The column was ash and ice under thirty feet of collapsed mountain. The Office of Cartographic Intelligence had not heard from her in forty-one days. Somewhere in the world above, a clerk was already writing *missing, presumed* in a ledger that would outlive everyone in this room.

She read the line again. Then she dipped the pen, and wrote the truth of what she had seen, because if this letter was going to exist at all, even for the length of time it took to burn, she wanted one honest document in a life that had been mostly composed of careful falsehoods drawn at scale.

*The vault is real. The dragon is real. It is dying. We are keeping it alive.*

Her hand shook on the last word. Not from the cold.

* * *

Forty-one days underground taught you things about yourself that no academy ever could.

Ilsa had learned that she slept better in a stone room with seven other people breathing than she had ever slept alone in an imperial billet with clean linen and a bolted door. She had learned that rationing hunger had a rhythm, and that the rhythm changed when Sefa's supply caches were found, and that Oscar Joubert counted portions the way he did everything else — silently, precisely, and with a grief underneath it that he thought nobody noticed.

She had learned that Brannoc's surviving smugglers sang when they worked, badly, songs about horses and theft and one interminable ballad about a woman in Kestrel Gate who had apparently ruined three men's lives, and that she had caught herself humming it while grinding grain, and had stopped, horrified, and then started again, because there was no one left to care whose tune lived in her head.

And she had learned — this was the lesson that had brought her to the table tonight with a frozen inkhorn and a half-written treason — that she could not go home.

Not *would* not. *Could* not. The distinction mattered. She had spent the siege telling herself that when the passes cleared, she would walk down to the nearest imperial waystation, present herself, spin some story about capture and coerced silence, and resume her life the way a river resumes its course around a fallen tree. It was a comfortable story. Rivers always found their old shape eventually.

But rivers had never stood in a vault and listened to a dying creature older than the Empire describe the sound of its own heart failing.

Rivers had never watched Tam — fifteen years old, a burned village's last living boy — press his palm flat against a stone egg and whisper to it as though it were a frightened animal, which, Ilsa supposed, in every way that mattered, it was.

The Empire's plan was written in her own hand. She had drafted the logistics tables herself, two years ago, in the warm archive: extraction yield projections for draconic cardiac tissue, projected weaponization timelines, casualty estimates for the border provinces where the new fire-weapons would be tested. She had signed those numbers. She had been proud of their elegance.

Now she sat in the dark with the people those numbers were meant to bury, and the ink wouldn't stay liquid, and the only honest thing she could think to do was finish the report and burn it.

* * *

"You're doing it again."

Tam's voice came out of the shadows by the grain store, soft as snowfall. He moved quietly these days; since the battle, since the man he'd killed, he had taken to moving through the vault like someone trying not to wake the dead. Perhaps he was. There were enough of them down here now.

"Doing what?" Ilsa didn't look up. Looking up invited conversation, and conversation invited questions she hadn't answered yet, mostly because she hadn't finished asking them of herself.

"Sitting like the paper owes you money." He came closer, and she saw he was carrying two mugs of the barley tea Sefa had taught them to stretch — roasted root and a scrap of dried apple each, a luxury by siege standards. He set one at her elbow without asking. "You get this line right here." He tapped the space between his own eyebrows. "Like you're arguing with somebody who isn't in the room."

"Perhaps I am."

"Oscar does it too. Cyrus used to do it." Tam said the name carefully, the way they all said it now, a coin passed hand to hand so often it had gone smooth. "Brannoc says arguing with ghosts is how you know you've got a conscience."

"Brannoc also says the woman in Kestrel Gate deserved everything she got."

"Well. He's right about one of those things."

Isla almost smiled. Almost. She looked down at the report instead, at the imperial cipher-heading sitting on the page like a knife laid on a table, and Tam followed her gaze, and she saw him understand — he was quick, quicker than anyone gave him credit for, except that now everyone gave him credit for it, because he had earned it in fire and worse than fire.

"That's for them," he said. Not a question.

"It's *for* them. Whether it goes *to* them is the part I'm still deciding."

"When the pass clears."

"When the pass clears."

He drank his tea. Outside the vault door — beyond the sealed inner gate, beyond the rubble-choked corridor where Brannoc had died holding the barricade with a smuggler's grin and a smuggler's stubbornness — winter still owned the mountain. But Ilsa had charted the Thornfell weather patterns for six years; she knew, better than anyone else in this room, exactly how many days remained until the melt. She had told Oscar. He had nodded and said nothing, which from Oscar was a full paragraph.

"The egg moved today," Tam said suddenly, quietly, the way you tell someone news you've been carrying around like a hot coal because you can't hold it alone. "While I was there. Just — a little. A tilt. Like a bird turning in sleep."

Ilsa looked at him then. His face in the brazier-light was still thin, still marked by Greywater's ashes in ways that had nothing to do with skin, but his eyes were bright, and there was something in them she recognized from the archive drawings she'd studied of pre-Imperial dragon-cults: the look of a person who has been chosen by something enormous and is trying very hard to deserve it.

"Did you tell Oscar?"

"He was asleep. He doesn't sleep much, so when he does, I let him." Tam shrugged, an old man's shrug on a boy's shoulders. "Anyway, I think it moved *for* me. Like it knew I'd come back tomorrow. Is that stupid?"

"No," Ilsa said, and found her voice had gone rough. "That is the least stupid thing I've heard in forty-one days."

* * *

She finished the report at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here, which was the hour when Oscar lit the great lamps and everyone pretended the day had begun.

It ran four pages. It was, she reflected, the finest work of her career: precise, complete, damning. It described the vault's location in terms accurate enough that any Ninth Bureau surveyor could find it blindfolded. It documented the dragon's condition, the keeper-oath that bound Oscar to its fading fire, the egg — *one viable ovoid, developmental status uncertain, warmth-dependent* — and it named every person sheltering below, with full particulars, because Ilsa Vane did not do things halfway, even her betrayals.

Especially her betrayals.

Then she carried it up through the corridors to the crack in the mountain's shoulder where the wind came through, the spot where they kept the signal-fire, and she stood in the freezing light of the actual morning — white, blinding, the sky the color of a blade — and held the pages over the coals.

They caught slowly. Imperial paper was treated against damp and did not want to die. She watched the cipher-heading curl and blacken first, the beautiful bureaucratic script blistering into nothing, and felt — she searched herself for regret and found, instead, something closer to hunger ending. Forty-one days of drafting this letter in her head, revising it, polishing it, telling herself it was a bridge home. And now it was smoke, and the smoke went up through the crack into the sky of a country that had never been hers, and her hands were empty, and they had never felt less empty in her life.

"You're burning your bridge."

Oscar's voice behind her. She didn't startle; you learned not to startle, around him. He came and stood beside her, a big grey man with a big grey silence, watching the last page catch.

"I burned it weeks ago," Ilsa said. "This is just the ash catching up."

"Mm." He watched the fire eat. "Sefa's party was sighted at the lower switchbacks an hour ago. Brannoc's lookout. Six mules, sledges, and the widow herself driving the lead team through drifts past her knees."

Ilsa turned. "In this season? The southern road—"

"—is suicide, yes. She was told that. Repeatedly. In writing, by me." Something moved at the corner of Oscar's mouth, the rare tectonic shift that those who knew him had learned to read as laughter. "She sent back a letter saying that she had buried a husband and nearly buried me, and that she did not take travel advice from men who prayed to nothing."

"She sounds delightful."

"She sounds like weather." He looked at the burning pages a moment longer. "You could still go down and meet her convoy. Walk out with it. Nobody here would stop you. Half of them would carry your pack."

Ilsa understood what he was doing. He had done it before, with Tam, with the deserters, with every soul who'd washed up against Thornfell: he offered the door openly, because a family that kept people by hiding the exits wasn't a family, it was a cell. It was one of the thousand small reasons she had stopped being able to imagine leaving.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"I'm defecting properly. You're all terribly bad at it. None of you have filed anything. There should be paperwork. A renunciation. Some kind of ceremony where I burn my seal—" She turned her hand over, and there it was in her palm: the Ninth Bureau signet, wax-brown and heavy, the eagle cartouche she had worn for eleven years. She'd been carrying it since the surrender, waiting, she realized now, for exactly this moment. "Witness me, Wolf."

Oscar looked at the ring. Then at her. Then he took it — his hands were warm, always unnervingly warm now, the dragon's heat banked in him like coals — and pressed it into the last ember of her report, and the gold went dull, and softened, and sagged into the char like a kneeling man.

"There," he said. "Ceremony's done. Now come help unload sledges. She'll have brought salt, and she'll count it in front of us, and she'll find one jar short, and there will be a whole thing."

There was, indeed, a whole thing.

* * *

Sefa of Hollowpine came up through the last of the snow like spring itself arriving early and uninvited, and the vault — the vault *changed*.

Ilsa watched it happen over the following days and marveled, because she had helped defend this place with drill-codes and rationing schedules and a crossbow she'd learned to use badly, and none of it had made the stone rooms feel like anywhere except a tomb with good company. But Sefa walked in, planted her fists on her hips, surveyed the great hall with the expression of a woman judging a poorly-run kitchen, and within a week there were herbs drying from lines strung across the ceiling, and the barley stores smelled of rosemary, and someone had repaired the drafty seam in the east wall with actual mortar instead of hope.

She had brought more than supplies. She had brought a chest of seeds. Turnip and kale and a hardy mountain barley that would ripen in the short fierce summer above the tree line, and she stood in the entrance cavern looking at the thin soil where the avalanche had folded back the mountainside, and said, "There. That's a garden. Come spring, that's a garden," in the tone of a woman announcing a law of nature.

"We're under siege," Brannoc's second, a scarred woman called Vetch, had pointed out.

"And sieges end, love, one way or another. Either you'll need the food, or you'll need the work. Grief keeps worse company than turnips." And she had handed Vetch a spade, and Vetch, who had killed four imperials at the gate, had taken it with the dazed obedience of the recently bereaved, and dug.

That was Sefa's whole method, Ilsa came to see. No speeches, no oaths — she'd waved away Oscar's solemn offer of a warden's vow with "you can swear at me after supper like everyone else" — just the relentless application of care to whatever stood in front of her. She mended Tam's coat and listened to his nightmares without comment. She sat with Vetch through the nights when the dead got loud. She found Ilsa hunched over her charts one evening and simply put a bowl of stew beside them and said, "Eat, or the work eats you," and stood there until she did.

On the ninth night, Ilsa found her on the entrance ledge, looking south toward Hollowpine.

"They'll ask why you don't come back," Ilsa said. "The villages. People talk."

"People talked about me for twenty years as the widow who kept to herself. Let them talk about the widow who went up the mountain. It's a better story." Sefa's face was lined and calm, carved by weather into something geological. "My husband's under the rowan at Hollowpine. He's not lonely. He's got the roots." She glanced sideways. "You thinking of going down with the next convoy?"

"No."

"Good." As simple as that. As though Ilsa's entire ruinous, irreversible choice weighed no more than a jar of salt. "Then tomorrow you'll help me lay out the seed rows. Cold-soil planting. Your hands look clever enough."

"My hands drew invasion maps."

"All the more reason they should learn to grow something."

* * *

Later — much later, in the smallest hours, when the vault breathed and the egg-warmth in the deep chambers pulsed slow as a distant heart — Ilsa sat at her table one final time and wrote.

Not a report. Those were finished forever, ash on the wind. This was different. This was the thing she had promised herself she would do once, privately, honestly, with no bureau formatting and no cipher and no audience.

*Dear Mother,* she wrote. *By the time you hear any version of this, I will be a traitor twice over, and the story they tell you will be that I was captured and broken and turned. Parts of it will even be true. What I need you to know is that I was not broken. I was shown the arithmetic of what we built, all of it, in one room, with faces on the numbers — and I could not keep signing my name.*

She wrote until the candle guttered. About Cyrus, whom she'd never met and yet grieved like a colleague, because his stolen map was the single most elegant act of resistance she'd ever catalogued. About Oscar's terrible honesty. About a boy teaching himself to be gentle again, and an egg tilting in the dark for him. About a garden plotted on a mountainside by a widow who treated apocalypse as a scheduling matter.

Then she read it twice, folded it, and did not burn it.

Instead she sealed it in oilcloth, and in the morning she gave it to the mule-driver taking Vetch down to the low country for the first trade run of the thaw — not to deliver, but to hold, in case someday the roads opened wide enough and brave enough for one envelope to find a house in the capital where a woman kept her late daughter's childhood drawings in a drawer.

"Some things shouldn't only live in the fire," Ilsa said, by way of explanation, when Oscar raised an eyebrow.

"No," he agreed. "Some shouldn't." And then, nodding southward, where the pass stood open for the first time in months, blue and glittering and dangerous: "Lookout spotted movement at midday. Not a convoy. Single rider, imperial saddle, riding easy, like he's expected. Flying no colors — but flying *something*. White scarf on the lance."

Ilsa went still. She knew that signal. Every Ninth Bureau officer knew it. It was the parley mark — the flag carried only by an envoy authorized to treat with enemies, which meant somewhere in the shattered hierarchy of the expedition, someone had decided the war for Thornfell might yet be won with words.

Or lost with them.

"Get Oscar," she said. "And someone fetch Sefa. If we're going to meet an emperor's man, we're going to do it fed. Nobody signs anything hungry."

Above the gate, the white scarf fluttered small and bright against the snow, coming on.
