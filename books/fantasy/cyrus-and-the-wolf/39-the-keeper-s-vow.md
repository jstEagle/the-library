## 39. The Keeper's Vow

Spring came late to Thornfell that year, the way it always did, creeping up the passes like a thing ashamed to be seen. It arrived first in the sound of water — the Weeping Stair running loud again, the ice groaning loose from the switchbacks, meltwater threading silver down the black rock in a hundred small veins. Then came the smell: wet stone and thawing pine and, faintly, impossibly, green. By the time I noticed it properly, standing at the mouth of the vault with my hands wrapped around a mug of Sefa's nettle tea, the lower meadows had already gone the color of old copper gone new.

One year. Nearly to the day since Cyrus died at dawn.

I had learned to mark time differently up here. Down below, in the world of roads and wages, men marked time by contracts — by the day the coin came and the day the job ended. Up here we marked it by the dragon. By how long her fire held between feedings, by the depth of her breathing when I pressed my palm to the warm stone deep beneath us and listened. She was guttering. I would not lie about that, not even to myself, and especially not on this day. But she was still burning, and the stone heart at her side — the egg, the future, whatever name you wanted for the thing that pulsed slow as a sleeping pulse in the dark — was still quickening. Tam swore it moved more each month. Tam spent more hours down there than any of us, sitting cross-legged on the cold floor with his hand flat against the shell, telling it about the weather, about his days, about constellations he'd learned from a dying man on a frozen shelf above the tree line. The boy had made himself a keeper before I ever got around to making it formal.

That was the trouble, if you wanted to call it trouble. Everything I meant to do today had already happened, informally, over months of shared bread and shared watches and shared graves. What remained was only the saying of it out loud. And I had learned — slowly, the way I learned everything, dragging my heels and grumbling the whole way — that saying things out loud was the whole point. That was what Cyrus had taught me without meaning to. An oath unspoken is just an intention, and intentions are cheap as rain. But speak it, in front of witnesses, with your blood warm and your heart listening, and it becomes something else. Something with weight. Something that answers back.

My god never answered. Ten years of silence, and then one year of something older than gods answering instead, and I had finally worked out the truth of it, the truth I'd been too proud or too broken to see: the silence had never been the god's failure. It had been mine. I had been making oaths to nothing — to coin, to contracts, to the next job and the one after — and no power under heaven or stone honors a vow made to nothing. Loyalty without truth behind it is just habit wearing armor. That's what I'd been, for ten years. Habit in armor. The Wolf, growling on command.

Not anymore. Today we would say it all out loud, and mean every word, and see what the mountain said back.

* * *

We gathered at midmorning in the great chamber just inside the sealed door — the one Brannoc died holding, though nobody called it that aloud. Time had softened the place. Sefa had hung lamps, real ones, with oiled shades she'd carried up from Hollowpine on the first of her supply runs, and their light turned the old dragon-carvings on the walls amber and alive. Somebody had swept the floor. Somebody had built a fire in a ring of stones we'd hauled in for exactly this purpose, and the smoke rose through a crack in the vaulted rock that Ilsa had mapped and named the Mountain's Throat, because cartographers cannot leave a thing unnamed.

They were all there. All of them, and more than I had expected, which should not have surprised me anymore but did, a little, the way grace always does.

Sefa stood nearest the fire, gray-haired and square-shouldered, wearing her good shawl — the one with the mended corner, because everything Sefa owned was mended and she wore those mends like medals. She had come up from Hollowpine three days early with two mules loaded full of flour and lamp oil and winter wool, and when I'd asked her, half joking, whether she was moving in, she'd looked at me the way she looks at a torn seam and said, "I was always going to. You're just now getting around to asking." She'd lost a husband to the empire's first war and a son to its second, and she had chosen, freely, with both eyes open, to spend what remained of her life guarding the thing that might prevent a third. I did not understand it. I honored it anyway. Some choices you don't have to understand to respect.

Ilsa Vane stood a little apart, as she always did, imperial posture worn down to something almost human after a year among us. She still kept her boots too clean and her notes in a hand too precise, and she still flinched, sometimes, at sudden noises — the reflexes of a woman who had spent years in rooms where maps decided who lived. She had burned her last report in the autumn. I had watched her do it, page by page, feeding the fire with the steady hands of a surgeon, and when the last corner curled black she had said, quietly, "There. Now the truth belongs only to the people who can carry it." That was her whole creed, I think. Truth, carried carefully, by few enough hands. She had given the empire eleven years and nearly given it her soul; she had given us a year and gotten most of it back.

The smugglers came next, and there were more of them than I'd bargained for. When word had gone down out of the mountains — carried by Brannoc's surviving crew, by the Kestrel Gate men who'd voted with their feet, by rumor wearing seven different coats — that the Wolf was keeping something under Thornfell and paying in sanctuary instead of silver, folk had started walking. Not all at once. In twos and threes. A deserter from the northern garrisons with frostbitten fingers and no trade but the bow. A pair of guild couriers who'd decided the ledger looked different from this side of the pass. Old Marta from the ford, whose inn the riders had burned, carrying nothing but a kettle and a grudge. Eleven of them now, all told, camped in the lower chambers, learning the mountain the way you learn anything worth knowing — slowly, and by hurting yourself on it a little.

And Tam. Tam stood at the front, because of course he did, thirteen years old and grown half a hand taller since Greywater burned, with his dead mother's scarf wound round his wrist and Cyrus's star-charts copied out in his own careful hand tucked inside his coat. He had asked to stand at the front. I had almost said no, out of pure reflexive wanting-to-protect-him, and then caught myself, because protecting him by refusing him was exactly the kind of lie I'd sworn off. He had killed a man in the dark of the vault and come apart in my arms afterward, and he had gotten up the next morning and done his watch, and that was its own kind of oath, spoken without words. He could stand wherever he damn well pleased.

"You look grim," Sefa observed, watching me stare at the fire.

"I'm rehearsing."

"Rehearsing a vow." She snorted. "It's not a wedding, Oscar."

"No," I said. "It's worse. Weddings end."

That got the laugh I didn't want, and deserved, and needed. And then Ilsa cleared her throat, and the room went quiet the way rooms go quiet when everyone in them has decided, separately and together, that the moment has arrived.

* * *

I had written the words out three times and burned each draft. In the end I stood there with nothing in my hands, because that felt right. Oaths made from paper are contracts. Oaths made from nothing but breath and blood — those were the old kind, the kind my god used to seal before he stopped answering, the kind the mountain understood in its bones.

"I'm not good at speeches," I said. "You all know that. So I'll keep it short, and then we'll eat, because Sefa's been cooking since dawn and it's cruel to make food wait."

Someone laughed. Good. A vow taken in silence is heavy enough; take it among laughter and it weighs less and holds longer. That's another thing I'd learned the hard way.

"A year ago I broke a contract," I said. "First one I ever broke. There were men paid to see a certain thing delivered, and an army paid to see it taken, and I stood between them with a dying man's memories bleeding into my skull and a choice in front of me that had no clean side. I could have sold the truth and bought myself a quiet life. I could have burned the truth and bought the world a little more sleep. Instead I carried it, because a man I barely knew made me swear, and for the first time in ten years, something answered."

I paused there. Not for effect. Because the pause was true. Even now, even a year on, I could feel it — that low warmth under my sternum, banked coals of borrowed fire, the spark Cyrus had poured into me with his last breath. It stirred when I spoke of oaths. It had opinions. It had never once told me what to do with them, which was, I suspected, the whole art of it.

"The compact is this," I went on. "Not mine. Ours. Thornfell keeps what sleeps beneath it — the last fire of the last dragon, and the egg she spends that fire to warm. No crown gets it. No guild sells it. No army drains it to fuel weapons, not while any warden draws breath. In exchange, the mountain keeps us: shelter, water, game in the high meadows, and the right to be people who chose each other instead of being spent by people who never knew our names." I looked around the lamplit circle, face by face. "Smugglers. Deserters. Cartographers. Widows. Boys. Every one of us the world had finished with, one way or another. Turns out the world has poor judgment."

"Turns out," Sefa agreed mildly, and the laughter ran around the room again like water finding its course.

"There's one condition, and it isn't courage and it isn't strength," I said. "It's honesty. Down in that vault, the fire doesn't care how tough you are. It cares whether you tell the truth — to it, to each other, to yourselves. Lies fester down there. The dragon can taste them through the stone. So we don't keep secrets from each other. Not comfortable ones, not useful ones, none. That's the vow. Loyalty built on truth, or it's nothing but a leash."

Then I did the thing I had been dreading and needing in equal measure. I knelt. Old knees on cold stone, in front of all of them, the Wolf of the passes going down like a man at an altar — which, I suppose, was exactly what I was, except my altar breathed.

"My old god went silent ten years ago," I said, "and I thought the silence was the wound. It wasn't. The wound was all the vows I made to nothing — to coin, to contracts, to whoever paid next. This one's different. I swear it to the mountain, to the fire beneath it, and to every living soul in this room: I will keep what is entrusted to me, I will tell you the truth even when it costs, and when I die, I die a keeper. Not a wolf. A keeper."

The spark under my breastbone flared — not pain, never pain anymore, but heat, a slow gold bloom like sunrise through a closed eyelid — and beneath my knees, through the soles of my boots, through the living rock itself, I felt the mountain feel it. A tremor, small as a heartbeat. Somewhere far below, ancient lungs drew a long, slow breath, and the lamps flickered once in a wind that had no source, and every person in that chamber went absolutely still.

"She heard," Tam whispered.

"She heard," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "She always hears. That's rather the point."

* * *

They came to me one at a time, after. That was Ilsa's suggestion — she'd studied how empires bind soldiers and wanted the opposite of it. Empires make you swear in masses, she said, hundreds of voices drowning together until no single voice can be blamed for anything. We would swear singly, witnessed, each name spoken aloud and remembered. Loyalty that knows your face. That was the whole architecture of the thing.

Sefa first, because she'd earned first. She knelt across from me — refused my hand up, planted herself down with a grunt and a glare daring anyone to help — and swore in her own words, plain as bread: "I'll mend what breaks, feed who's hungry, and bury who needs burying, and I'll not pretend to be sweet about any of it." The mountain shivered gently, like something chuckling. Then Marta and her kettle, then the bowman with the ruined fingers, then the couriers, each in their own tongue, some formal, some stumbling, one man so nervous he swore to "keep the dragon's heart safe and also the flour, if that's in scope," which Sefa declared was the finest clause she'd ever heard.

Ilsa came near the end. She knelt the way she did everything — precisely, spine straight, chin level — and when she spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"I served an archive," she said. "I believed knowledge belonged to whoever was strong enough to hold it. I was wrong, and it took me thirty years and a burned ford to learn it. I swear the truth to this mountain — kept, guarded, and never sold. And I swear that if I am ever the weakest link, you'll hear it from my own mouth before anyone else's." She looked up at me. "That last part is the hard one, Joubert. I need you to hold me to it."

"That's the deal," I said. "All of us holding all of us. Nobody carries it alone. Not even the strong ones. Especially not the strong ones."

She nodded once, the smallest movement, and something in her shoulders let go — a knot I hadn't known she was carrying, loosening by a single turn.

Tam went last, naturally, because Tam had argued for an hour that going last was a position of tactical importance and none of us had the heart to argue tactics with a boy who'd copied out star charts by candlelight. He knelt across from me and for a moment couldn't speak, jaw working, and I waited. I was done rushing children toward words they weren't ready for. The world had rushed him plenty.

"I don't have big words," he finally said. "But I know the dragon likes it when somebody just talks to her. So — I'll talk to her. Every day. And I'll learn everything Cyrus knew, the stars and the doors and all of it, so if something happens to the rest of you there's still somebody who remembers. And—" His voice cracked, steadied. "And I'm not doing it for revenge anymore. I checked. I really checked. It's for her. And for you. Mostly her." A beat. "Some for you."

The mountain hummed — an actual hum, felt in the teeth, low and warm and unmistakably pleased — and Tam's eyes went huge, and Sefa laughed until she had to sit down, and the ceremony dissolved, as all good ceremonies should, into bread and tea and people arguing happily about whose oath the dragon liked best.

Later, much later, when the fire had burned down to orange and most of the wardens had drifted off to their bunks, I climbed. Up through the Mountain's Throat, up the hidden stair to the ledge above the vault mouth where I did my best thinking, and stood looking south over the pass with the wind combing through my gray-streaked hair.

The old ache was gone. That was the thing I kept reaching for, every few days, like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth had been. For ten years the silence had sat in my chest like a splinter — every prayer a small humiliation, every oath a coin spent in a currency nobody exchanged anymore. I had thought faith was the ache and cynicism was the healing. Wrong on both counts. Faith wasn't the ache. Faithless loyalty was. And now, standing on the bones of a mountain with a stranger's fire banked in my chest and a found family sleeping in the stone below me, the silence of my old god was just — silence. Distant weather. A letter from someone I used to know, returned unopened, and me no longer waiting by the door.

Loyalty to truth. Truth worthy of loyalty. It had taken me forty years, a silent god, a dying stranger, and a dragon's borrowed spark to learn four words' worth of sense. Cyrus would have laughed himself sick.

Below me, deep in the dark, the heart beat on — slow, stubborn, patient as spring coming late up a mountain. I laid my hand flat on the cold rock, the way I did every night, and felt it answer.

And then I felt something else.

A flutter, where there had only ever been that slow patient pulse. Quick, small, bright — a second rhythm, faint as a sparrow's, skipping alongside the old one like a child matching a parent's stride. My breath stopped. The spark under my sternum flared so hard I swayed.

Down in the vault, faint through a hundred feet of stone, Tam's voice rose — talking to the egg, as he did every night, telling it about the stars.

And the small, new heartbeat skipped again — faster.

As if, for the very first time, it was listening back.
