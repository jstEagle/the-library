## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The rain came down on Thornfell's lower city like it had a grudge against the place, hammering the slate roofs and running in silver ropes off the eaves of Copperlane Row. Theo Blackwood stood beneath a butcher's awning with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, watching a fat merchant named Odell Brask hurry through the wet toward his own front door.

She had been watching him for eleven days.

Eleven days was longer than she usually needed. Most marks gave up their treasures inside three or four — grief made them porous, and loneliness made them careless. But Brask kept his first kiss locked up tight behind a wall of ledgers and brandy and the particular smugness of a man who had never once been poor. He carried it everywhere he went, the way some men carried a knife. She could feel it from across the street, a warm amber glow in the cold architecture of his mind: a girl named Lissbet, seventeen, an orchard in high summer, the taste of cider on someone else's mouth.

It was a beautiful memory. That was the problem. Beautiful memories held on.

Theo counted his steps as he fumbled for his keys under the awning. Twelve more to his door. She peeled herself out of the shadows and fell into stride behind him, close enough that the rain hid her breath, far enough that he'd have no reason to remember her face even if he saw it. Nobody ever remembered her face. She had spent thirty years making sure of that — a face like a closed book, a walk like water finding its level. In Thornfell they called thieves like her *tappers*, and tappers survived by being forgettable in every way that mattered.

"Master Brask," she said softly, and touched two fingers to the back of his neck, right where the skull met the spine.

He didn't flinch. He never did. The touch carried a whisper of her gift — just enough to smooth him open, like a hand smoothing the creases from a letter. His mind bloomed in her awareness, all its doors swinging loose on their hinges, and there it was: the orchard. The heat. Lissbet's freckled shoulder and her laugh, half-startled, half-delighted, and the moment before the kiss when the whole world went quiet as held breath.

Theo took it the way you'd lift a coin from a sleeping man's palm — gently, completely, without leaving a mark.

The memory came away whole. It always surprised people how whole things came away, how clean the edges were. They imagined torn pages, bleeding gaps. But a stolen memory left nothing behind except absence shaped exactly like itself. Tomorrow morning Odell Brask would wake and feel a hollow somewhere under his ribs, a room in his house he couldn't account for, and he would blame the wine, or age, or his wife's cold shoulder. He would spend years circling that empty space without ever once looking directly at it. People were good at not looking. People were built for it.

She withdrew her fingers and was three stalls away before he got his door open.

* * *

The widow Marren lived above a candlemaker's shop on Tallow Street, where the smell of rendered fat seeped through the floorboards and everything wore a faint golden patina. She answered Theo's knock already weeping, which saved time.

"You have it?" Her voice cracked on the second word. "You swear you have it?"

"I have what you paid the deposit for," Theo said. "The rest on delivery. You know the terms."

Inside, the flat was a shrine to a man named Aldous Marren, dead eight months now of a lung-rot picked up in the dye works. His face looked out from three portraits, none of them quite him — painted faces never were. His pipe sat in a dish by the cold hearth. His coat hung on its hook like a man still standing in it.

Widow Marren pressed a purse into Theo's hands, heavy and honest, coins earned over a lifetime of mending other people's clothes. Theo weighed it once, briefly, and put the thought of it away. Guilt was a luxury item. She'd stopped stocking it around her fifteenth year.

"He talked about it," the widow whispered. "Near the end, when the fever had him. He talked about an orchard, and a girl, and he said — he said *I never told anyone but I'll tell the stars*. And I thought, forty years I've loved this man, and there's a whole piece of him I never got to hold." Her red-rimmed eyes fixed on Theo with something between hunger and shame. "I want what he kept. Even if it wasn't me in it. *Especially* if it wasn't me in it."

They always said something like that. Theo had learned not to flinch at it.

She set the memory against the widow's brow with one fingertip, and sang — barely a sound at all, a hum below hearing, the note that unlatched a mind's receiving. The transfer took four seconds. The widow shuddered, gasped, and then her face did the thing Theo never watched closely enough to describe afterward: grief and joy arriving together, tangled, indistinguishable, like smoke from a snuffed candle.

"Oh," the widow breathed. "Oh, *Lissbet*. Oh, you foolish, lovely boy." Tears ran freely now, but her mouth was smiling. "He tasted of apples. Forty years, and he still tasted of apples."

Theo gathered her coat, took her leave, and let herself out into the rain.

Behind her, through the window's greasy glass, she saw the widow sit down in her dead husband's chair and press both hands over her heart, holding something warm that had finally, finally been given back to her.

Theo walked until the sight stopped following her. It took six streets.

* * *

There was a ritual to the end of a job, and Theo performed it the way other people performed prayers: faithfully, without belief.

Back in her rooms above the shuttered chandler's storeroom on Grate Lane — one room, one bed, one window that overlooked nothing but a wall and a slice of grey sky — she lit a single stub of candle and sat with the memory cupped invisibly in her mind. Not the widow's copy. A tapper always kept a trace, a residue; it clung to the fingers of the gift like oil. Rules existed for this. Vess's rules, guild rules, rules older than either of them.

*Never keep what you steal.*

So she opened her inner hands, so to speak, and let the orchard drain away. Lissbet's laugh, the held-breath quiet, the taste of cider — she poured it all out of herself deliberately, methodically, feeling it thin and gutter like a candle burning down, until nothing remained but the knowledge that it had existed. She was very good at emptying. Thirty years of practice. Some tappers couldn't do it; they hoarded, they grew heavy with other lives, and eventually the guild found them drowned in borrowed joys, unable to remember their own names. Theo had never once been tempted.

That was what she told herself, anyway. That she wasn't tempted.

The candle burned low. Rain ticked against the shutters. And in the silence after the ritual, the silence that always came, Theo sat alone in her own head the way you'd sit alone in an empty warehouse — vast, echoing, and full of nothing that belonged to her.

Because here was the truth of Theo Blackwood, the truth under the trade: she kept no memories of her own either. Not stolen ones. Not any at all.

Her childhood was gone. Not faded, not worn smooth by time — *sealed*, walled off behind something she couldn't name and had long ago stopped trying to breach. She knew facts about herself the way you know facts about a stranger: born in Thornfell, orphaned young, taken in by the guild at nine years old because Vess had seen her lift a baker's whole afternoon out of his head on a dare. She knew she'd once had a mother. She did not know her mother's face. There had been a fire somewhere in the sealed dark — she could feel its heat sometimes, shapeless, like warmth through a door — and beyond the heat, nothing. A corridor in her mind that ended in a bricked-up archway, and her own footsteps stopping in front of it, year after year, without her ever deciding to stop.

Other tappers sold their pasts to pay debts. Theo had checked the guild ledgers once, early on, hungry and half-sick with wanting. Nothing. No sale recorded. Whatever had been done to her had been done for free, or by someone who hadn't needed money.

So she lived on borrowed light. She walked through other people's weddings and first snows and deathbed reconciliations, handled them, delivered them, emptied herself of them, and went home to a head as bare as a swept floor. It was cleaner this way. It was safer. Wanting nothing, keeping nothing, being nothing worth remembering — these were armor, and she had worn them so long she'd forgotten they were armor at all. She thought they were simply her skin.

She pinched out the candle.

In the dark, the sealed archway seemed closer than usual. It always did, after a job. Something about handling other people's treasures made the walls of her own emptiness feel less like architecture and more like a wound dressed as architecture.

"Sleep," she told herself, the way you'd tell a horse.

Sleep came, eventually. It always did. And in the morning, the city would need feeding, and the guild would have work, and Theo Blackwood would go out into the rain again, invisible and empty and very, very good at her trade.

* * *

Vess found her at the Drowned Bell two nights later, which meant Vess had been looking for her since the night before, which meant trouble.

The Bell was a tapper's tavern — neutral ground, hushed, smelling of wet wool and juniper spirits. Memory-workers drank here in careful silence, each guarding their own skull like a miser guarding a vault. Vess claimed the corner table the way she claimed everything: permanently and without apology. Fifty years old, iron-haired, with a scar through one eyebrow she'd earned taking a knife meant for Theo's throat eleven years ago. She was handler, surrogate mother, and employer, in roughly that order of convenience.

"You're late," Vess said.

"The job ran long. Widows cry longer than merchants drink."

"Brask's wife noticed the hole." Vess turned her cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit that meant she'd already decided something unpleasant. "Not the memory — she never had it. The *hole*. She says he's been staring at the garden wall where the orchard used to be, crying without knowing why. She's asking questions in the wrong quarter."

Theo sat down across from her. "I was clean."

"You were clean the way a surgeon is clean. There's still a scar, girl. There's always a scar." Vess leaned forward, voice dropping beneath the tavern's murmur. "Which brings me to why I've been hunting you. New commission. Big one. Crown-adjacent." She slid a folded paper across the table, sealed with plain black wax. "Client wants a retrieval. Old material. Very old."

Theo didn't touch it. "Crown-adjacent means inquisitors mean means I disappear."

"Crown-*adjacent*," Vess repeated, "means the fee would set you up for life. No more widows' purses. No more counting coppers in the rain." For a moment — half a heartbeat, gone as soon as it came — something moved behind Vess's eyes that wasn't greed and wasn't quite fear. Something almost tender, which from Vess was more alarming than either. "You've given this city thirty years, Theo. Don't you think it owes you something back?"

"No," Theo said. "I don't. That's rather the point of me."

Vess smiled the way winter smiles. "Take the paper. Read it tonight. Decide by tomorrow's dark." She rose, dropped a coin beside her untouched cup, and paused at Theo's shoulder on her way out — close enough to speak into her ear alone. "And Theo. If anything should come looking for you — anything strange, anything old — you come to me first. Promise me that."

"What kind of strange?"

"The kind that gets written down," Vess said, and was gone into the rain.

Theo sat a while with the black-sealed paper unopened in front of her, listening to the tavern breathe. Then she tucked it inside her coat, unfinished with it, unwilling, and stepped out into the wet dark of Thornfell — not knowing yet that the seal was already broken on something far older than any commission, that a hundred-year-old date waited in the dust of a burned library with her own face sketched beside it, and that the life she had built out of emptiness had exactly one night left of being safe.

But that, as the rain would have it, was tomorrow's theft to make.

The bell above the Drowned Bell's door rang once behind her, soft and clear, like something remembering her — and forgetting, a moment later, why.
