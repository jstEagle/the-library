## 2. A Date Written in Dust

The library had been dead for eleven years, and it still smelled of its dying—char and old smoke worked so deep into the stone that no rain and no season had ever washed it out. Theo Blackwood slipped through the gap where the servants' door had burned away from its frame, and the smell folded over her like a coat two sizes too large.

Behind her, three streets down and closing, someone was shouting her description to a man who would sell it onward for a copper.

*Not her description,* she corrected herself, pressing flat against a wall furred with soot. *A description.* Small round-faced woman, dark hair cut at the jaw, left hand wrapped. That was what they had. It was not much. It was not nothing either.

Her left hand throbbed where the merchant's memory had come out wrong—not the memory itself, which had lifted clean, but the taking of it, because he had woken early and grabbed her wrist and looked straight into her face while she was still inside his head. Seven seconds of eye contact with a mark. In eleven years of work, she had never let that happen. She had been careless tonight, and she did not know why, and not knowing was worse than the bruising grip his fingers had left.

She went deeper into the ruin.

* * *

The Ashfall Library had been the pride of Thornfell's upper terraces once, before the fire took it in a single October night—before Theo was born, before the guilds carved the lower city into territories, before any of the things that made up her life had names. Nobody rebuilt it. The crown said there was no money; the guilds said there was no profit; the scholars who survived it said they could not bear to look at the bones. So the shell had stood ever since on its terrace above the river, four storeys of blackened arches open to the sky like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down here to die.

Thieves used it sometimes. Smugglers stashed goods in the deep stacks, where the shelves had collapsed into tunnels and no lantern light had reached in a decade. Theo herself had cached take in the reading room's iron safe three winters ago. But nobody came at night, not really, not anymore. Even the city's endless population of desperate people had decided the place was unlucky. They said the books still whispered. They said if you slept among the ashes you woke up missing pieces of yourself.

Theo had always assumed that last one was guild superstition, invented to keep children from using it as a flophouse.

Tonight she would have taken the word of anything at all, as long as it meant *stay hidden until dawn*.

She climbed. That was instinct: pursuers searched low, and the upper floors of a ruin were treacherous enough to discourage casual boots. She went up the service stair with her unlit lantern held ahead of her like a blind woman's cane, feeling the walls through its cold iron ring, and emerged onto the third floor, where the fire had been kindest—or perhaps only slowest—and half the shelving still stood in ranks along the walls, charred to the depth of a finger and no further, as if the flames had paused to consider and then lost interest.

Moonlight fell through the broken roof in long pale planks. Dust hung in it, motionless, thick as weather.

Theo stood in the doorway of what had been the map room and listened to the library breathe.

Ruins breathed. Everyone who worked the night city knew it—the way old buildings exhaled when the temperature dropped, sighing heat out of their stones. But this was different, or her nerves made it seem different: a long, slow rhythm underneath the silence, like something very large asleep, or something very patient counting.

She counted with it without meaning to. Four breaths. Eight. Twelve.

Then she made herself stop, because that was the kind of thing that happened to people who had looked at a mark's face too long, and she moved into the room.

There was a table at its center, oak, scorched at one corner but sound. A drawer. She checked the drawer first—that was simply who she was, the way other people checked locks or watched shadows—and found it empty except for a spoon-sized hollow in the dust where something small had lain for years and then been taken, recently, by hands that had disturbed the dust in neat arcs. Someone had been here within the month. She filed that away and felt, absurdly, less alone.

Under the table, wedged beneath the stretcher where the legs crossed, was a book.

She almost left it. Her whole body wanted to leave it—to find the deepest corner of the deepest stack, sit with her back to stone, and wait out the hours until the city forgot her again. But the book had not fallen there. It had been *pushed*, hastily, by whoever had taken the object from the drawer—pushed down into the shadow of the stretcher as if the owner had wanted it out of sight even while fleeing, or as if someone else had come after and hidden it from the first.

And Theo Blackwood had spent her life learning one lesson above all others: things hidden on purpose are worth more than things left behind by accident.

She knelt and drew it out.

* * *

It was heavier than it should have been. Bound in grey leather gone stiff as bark, corners capped in tarnished silver, no title on the spine or cover—just a stamped device that she turned toward the moonlight to read: a circle, and inside the circle another circle, offset, like a moon caught twice in the same lens. Thirteen marks ringed the rim.

Thirteen moons. There were twelve. Everyone knew there were twelve; children could count them across a winter sky. But the old calendars, the ones the guilds' oldest smugglers still swore by when they planned long runs, carried a thirteenth name—a turn that had been *left out*, struck from the count before Theo's grandmother was born, for reasons nobody living remembered. Vess had told her once, over watered wine in the back room of the Drowned Lantern: *They didn't lose a moon, girl. They put it away. There's a difference.*

Theo had laughed at that, at twenty, with a pocket full of someone else's wedding day.

She opened the book now, kneeling under the table with the dust settling around her like slow snow, and the first page said, in faded brown ink:

*Recorded in the hundred-and-ninth year of the Long Regency. Set down by hand of M., singer, third voice, so that what was seen will not be unsaid when we are forgotten. We are already being forgotten. This is the nature of the enemy. If you are reading, then something of us remains, and I am glad.*

The handwriting changed every few pages. Different hands, different decades, the same careful brown ink—a ledger kept across generations, an inheritance of words passed hand to hand like contraband. Theo turned pages with the tips of her fingers, quick and professional, cataloguing. Hymns. Lists of names, most struck through. Diagrams of the human mind drawn as a house with many rooms, or a palace, or a choir stall with seats for every year of a life. Instructions for songs written in notation she had never seen, each note marked with a word instead of a pitch: *remember, remember, grieve, forget-this-only, hold.*

Memory-singers. Her grandmother's generation had called them stories—women in grey who could walk into your head and rearrange the furniture, who sold secondhand childhoods at midwinter fairs until the crown burned the last of them out. Fairy tales for people who needed to believe the rich hadn't bought happiness fair and square.

Except that Theo herself did for coin what these pages described as sacrament, and she knew exactly how real the trade was. She had simply never met anyone else honest enough to admit it existed.

Near the back, the hands stopped changing. One writer, decades alone. And the entries stopped being records and started being *accounts*—of a king crowned young and bright, of a crown made for him by men who wanted a door into his head, of a thing that had come through the door and stayed. The Regent. It fed on forgotten things, the hand wrote. Every memory let slip, every name unspoken at a graveside, every promise allowed to lapse—it drank them all, and it sat behind the king's eyes and grew, and the kingdom forgot itself one kindness at a time and called the forgetting peace.

*We tried to reach him,* the last entries said. *We failed. Most of us are forgotten now, which means he has eaten us, which means our failure fed him twice.*

*But the moon is coming around. The thirteenth turn completes soon—we reckon it within the year. And the door it opens swings both ways. What was sung into him can be sung out, but only by one who can enter a guarded mind without breaking it, who owes the Regent nothing, whom the Regent cannot foresee—*

Here the ink smeared, as if the page had been closed on it, wet, a century ago.

*—a thief, in short. We have seen her.*

Theo turned the page.

The paper had been prepared differently for this one—sized and polished, made to take fine work. There was a drawing on it, done in silverpoint by a hand far steadier than the writing: two figures, side by side, rendered small and precise. On the right, a tall man wearing a crown drawn twice—once plain, and once again in heavier strokes, over and over, until the metal seemed to writhe, roots sinking into the skull beneath.

On the left, a woman. Small. Round-faced. Dark hair cut at the jaw.

Below the figures, a date. Theo read it once, and her mind slid off it the way a boot slides off ice, and she had to make herself go back and read it again character by character, forcing each numeral to mean what it meant, refusing the escape of misreading.

Tomorrow.

The date was tomorrow.

* * *

She did not remember getting to her feet. She was standing, then, in the middle of the dead map room with moonlight in planks on the floor and the book open in her wrapped hand, and her heart was going hard and strange, and the library breathed around her—four counts in, eight, twelve—as if it had been waiting a very long time for her to catch up.

*This isn't possible.*

She said it aloud, and the room accepted the words without comment, and that was somehow the worst part—that the ruin didn't argue, didn't echo, just absorbed her voice into its patience like a debt already recorded.

Because here was the arithmetic, and she could not make it come out any other way. The book was a hundred years old. The paper was a hundred years old; she had handled enough forged documents to know aged paper from abused paper, and this was the real patience of real decades. The silverpoint drawing was a hundred years old. And the face in the drawing was hers—not similar, not suggestive, not one of those coincidences where everyone has a double somewhere. Hers, down to the slight asymmetry of her brows, the scar she'd gotten at nine falling off the Tanner's Stair, the particular set of her mouth that she saw every morning in cheap mirrors and disliked.

Someone a century dead had drawn her face beside a drowning king and written tomorrow's date underneath, and then—she understood this slowly, the way cold arrives, from the edges inward—then someone had hidden the book under this table. Recently. Within the month. Within weeks, maybe days, of tonight.

Which meant the drawing might be old, but the *arrangement* was current. Someone had known she would come here. Tonight. To this floor, this room, this table.

Or—

Or no one had arranged anything, and the truth was worse, and it was this: that her whole life had been a corridor, and every choice she'd ever made in it—the jobs she took, the streets she ran, the marks she lifted, the wrist she had carelessly let a merchant grab tonight, of all nights, for the first time in eleven years—every single one had been a door swinging shut behind her, guiding her, herding her gently through the dark toward a table in a burned library where a book waited with her name already written in it.

She thought of the mountain roads the old runners talked about, how they shifted in winter, how you set out for one valley and arrived in another and told yourself you'd taken a wrong turn. She thought of the rhythm under the silence, the patient breathing, twelve counts and holding.

*We are already being forgotten. This is the nature of the enemy.*

The Regent feeds on forgotten things. And what had Theo Blackwood been, her whole life, if not a woman built entirely out of forgotten things—stolen goods in, own past locked away, a face no one could hold in mind past the second meeting, a name that opened no doors because she had paid good money to have it buried? She had thought invisibility was her craft. Her armor. Her one possession.

It had never occurred to her that it might also be an appetite being cultivated. That somewhere, something vast and patient had been fattening her emptiness the way a farmer fattens a goose—keeping her lean, keeping her blank, keeping her *unforeseeable*—because a door swings both ways, and you cannot eat a feast through a locked door, and you cannot steer a woman who wants anything.

Downstairs, faint through four storeys of ruined arches, gravel shifted under a boot.

Theo closed the book. Her hands were steady—they were always steady, that was the joke of her, the trick of her, hands like a surgeon's on a body that wanted to scream—but her mind kept sliding on the same patch of ice: *tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow*, the numerals glowing behind her eyes like coals.

Another boot. Then voices, low, more than one, spreading out below her in the dark with the ease of people who knew the ground—guild trackers, or worse, crown men with lanterns and warrants and her description in their pockets, and either way they would climb, because trackers always climbed.

She looked down at the book in her arms. Grey leather. Thirteen moons stamped around a doubled circle. A hundred years of dead singers whispering *we have seen her* into the dark.

Every instinct she owned said leave it. Leave it under the table where she'd found it, slip out through the burned doorway, vanish the way she had spent her whole life perfecting vanishing, and let tomorrow arrive for somebody else.

But the book was warm. Not sun-warm, not body-warm—warm the way a held hand is warm, the way something alive is warm—and through the stiff grey cover she could feel it very faintly against her breastbone, a rhythm, four counts in and eight and twelve, patient as dustfall.

Waiting for her to catch up.

Boots on the stair now. Two flights below and climbing.

Theo Blackwood, thief, invisible woman, the unforeseen, tucked the century-old prophecy of her own face beneath her arm and ran for the roof.
