## 3. What the Guild Owes

The message came the way all of Vess's messages did: folded into a paper boat and floated down the gutter of Cindermilk Lane, where it would bump against Theo's boot like a dead moth if she was meant to read it tonight.

Theo crouched in the doorway of a shuttered chandler's shop and fished it out with two fingers. The paper was good stock—rag paper, not pulp—which meant money had changed hands somewhere upstream of this gutter, which meant the job was either very large or very strange.

*The Gilded Scale. Midnight. Come alone. Wear your grief.*

That last part was Vess's shorthand for her mourning clothes: the plain grey dress and black shawl that made Theo look like any other widow in Thornfell's lower city, forgettable as rainwater. Vess had taught her that disguise years ago. *Nobody looks twice at grief,* she'd said. *Grief is the one face everyone is desperate to look away from.*

Theo burned the note over the chandler's cold lamp and went home to change.

* * *

The Gilded Scale sat wedged between a pawnbroker and a tannery on Sallow Street, its sign a set of brass weights gone green with damp. Inside, the air smelled of tallow smoke and spilled gin, and the patrons were the usual midweek wreckage of the lower city: dockhands drinking away wages they hadn't earned yet, a card sharp losing gracefully to a woman who cheated better, two off-duty watchmen pretending not to be watchmen.

Vess had claimed the corner booth beneath the mounted head of an ibex, its horns curling toward the ceiling like a question nobody wanted answered. She sat with her back to the wall—always her back to the wall—and a pot of tea for two already steeping, because Vess never drank where she couldn't watch someone else drink first.

She was fifty-something and looked carved rather than aged, all planes and angles under iron-grey hair cropped short as a soldier's. Her left hand rested flat on the table, and Theo noted, without letting herself note it, that the hand was very still. Vess's hands were never still unless they were holding something back.

"You're late," Vess said.

"The gutter flooded. Your boat nearly sank past Marlow's."

"Marlow's dead."

"Then it sank past his ghost." Theo slid into the opposite bench and poured herself tea, mostly so her hands had something honest to do. "What's the job?"

"No job." Vess turned her cup a quarter-turn, aligning the handle with the table edge, then a quarter-turn back. A tell. Theo had catalogued Vess's tells the way other people memorized prayers. "A conversation. About the archive job."

Theo kept her face still. Three nights ago she'd lifted a ledger key from a merchant's memory in the Saltmarket; the extraction had gone clean, but the man had woken screaming about a girl with grey eyes who wasn't there when the watch arrived, and the guild's rule was simple: *if you're seen, you're spent.* She'd been lying low since, waiting for Vess to decide whether she was an asset or a liability.

"The client got his key," Theo said. "He paid."

"The client got his key and then he got talkative. In a tavern. After four drinks." Vess's voice stayed level, unhurried, the voice of a woman discussing the weather or the price of coal. "Talked about a memory-thief with grey eyes. Talked about how the thief had been asking after old books. Burned libraries. Prophecy collections."

Theo's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, and she let none of it reach her face. The book was wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her flour barrel, beneath three inches of actual flour, because even paranoia deserved camouflage. *A Date Written in Dust.* Her own face sketched beside a crowned figure. Tomorrow's date written in ink a century old.

"I ask after lots of things," Theo said. "It's called research. Clients like a thief who knows what she's stealing."

"Mm." Vess sipped her tea. Set it down. Aligned the handle again. "And did your research turn anything up?"

Here it was. The fork in the road, laid out as casually as cutlery. Theo could lie—lying to Vess was easy, had been easy for years—but some animal instinct deep in her chest, the one that had kept her alive through eleven winters of thieving memories out of stranger's heads, whispered that Vess already knew. That this whole booth, this tea, this careful stillness of the left hand, was theater staged for an audience of one.

So Theo did the thing she was worst at. She told the truth.

"A prophecy book. Century-old. It has my face in it."

Vess didn't flinch. That was worse than flinching. Flinching would have meant surprise.

"How much do you know," Vess said, not quite a question, "about what the crown pays for prophecy these days?"

* * *

They talked for two hours, and afterward Theo could not have said how much of it was true.

Vess laid it out in her dry, inventory-keeping way, the way she might itemize a shipment: the crown's Office of Inquiry had doubled its bounty on unlicensed memory-workers last spring. Inquisitors had been seen in Thornfell asking after a specific text—a burned library, a prophecy concerning the king. King Aldric, who hadn't been seen in public in nine years, whose portraits were now painted from memory by artists who'd never met him, whose silence the broadsheets filled with increasingly inventive explanations. And the guilds, Vess said, the great smuggling syndicates that ran stolen memories up and down the coast like so many barrels of salt fish—the guilds had received an offer. A large one. For information leading to the recovery of a certain prophesied individual.

"They're calling you a relic," Vess said. "Like you're a saint's fingerbone. Something to be enshrined before it does damage."

"And you're here to warn me." Theo made it flat, neither grateful nor accusing. "Out of the goodness of your heart."

"I'm here because I owe you a debt, and I pay my debts." Vess finally lifted her eyes from the teacup, and for one unguarded moment Theo saw something underneath the iron of her—not warmth exactly, but the scar tissue where warmth had once been. "You've been mine for eleven years, girl. I found you half-starved picking pockets in the Drowned Quarter when you were what, twelve? Thirteen? I taught you the trade. I kept the guild off your back when you refused to work the binding houses. Whatever else happens, you should hear it from me first, not from an inquisitor's boot."

"Hear what from me first?" Theo asked quietly.

Vess aligned the teacup handle. Turned it back. The ibex above them stared down with its brass-green horns.

"The guild council votes tomorrow night," Vess said. "On whether to sell your name to the Office of Inquiry."

The noise of the tavern seemed to recede, as though Theo had plunged underwater. She heard the card sharp laugh across the room. She heard the tapman drawing a pint, foam hissing against wood. Ordinary sounds, in a world that had just quietly rearranged itself around her.

"Sell me," Theo repeated.

"For your own protection." Vess said it fast, the words worn smooth as river stones, clearly rehearsed. "There's a faction on the council—Corvane's people—who want to take the crown's coin and wash their hands. But there are others who remember what you've earned. If we control the sale—if we deliver you ourselves, on our terms, with our conditions—we can negotiate protections. Sanctuary. A place in the palace service instead of an interrogation cell. Better us than Corvane selling your name to whoever bids highest, and believe me, girl, there will be bidders beyond the crown. There are people who've waited a century for that book's contents."

"You keep saying *protection* like it means something different from *sale*."

"It does." Vess leaned forward, and her voice dropped into the register she used for truths too heavy for the open air. "Theo. Listen to me. If the council sells your name and you run, you're a fugitive with no papers, no coin, no contacts, hunted by every cutpurse and informer from here to the coast. If we sell you *well*, you go to the capital fed and clothed and alive. I've seen the alternative. I'm offering you the only version of this where you survive."

Theo looked at her handler—at the woman who had pulled her out of the Drowned Quarter mud, who had taught her to fold grief around herself like a cloak, who had never once in eleven years raised her voice—and felt the ground tilt beneath everything.

Because that was the thing about Vess. She almost never lied outright. She just arranged the truth until it pointed wherever she needed it to point, the way a smuggler arranges cargo.

"You've already voted," Theo said. "Haven't you. This conversation is happening *after* the vote."

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes—there and gone, quick as a pickpocket's hand.

"The vote's tomorrow," Vess said.

"You're wearing your voting face." Theo stood, slow, keeping her knees loose. Eleven years of training: never rise quickly in front of a handler, never show the panic, never let them see where you'll run. "You already know how it goes. You wouldn't waste tea on a lost cause."

"Theo—"

"What did they offer you? A cut? Or just the chance to be the one who handed me over gently?" She pulled her shawl close. "For my own protection. Say it again. I want to hear how it sounds the second time."

Vess's jaw tightened. For a moment—one long, terrible moment—Theo thought she saw the truth surface, whole and ugly: that the debt Vess owed her had been weighed against the debt Vess owed the guild, and the guild had won, and Vess had come here tonight not to warn her but to make sure she didn't run before the transaction could be completed. A frightened asset runs. A compliant asset walks calmly to the altar.

But Vess only said, very quietly: "Don't make me choose between you and the only family I have left."

"That's the thing, Vess." Theo backed toward the door, into the smoke and the lamplight and the ordinary noise of the lower city. "I never got to be family. I was always just inventory."

* * *

She went home the long way, doubling back twice through the fish market and once through the drainage tunnels under Tanner's Row, and even so her hands shook the entire time.

The flour barrel was untouched. The oilcloth bundle was untouched. She unwrapped it anyway, kneeling on the floor of her rented room with the shutters barred, and looked again at the page that had ruined her life.

Her own face, sketched in faded sepia by a hand dead a hundred years. Beside it, a crowned figure whose features had been left deliberately blank—erased, or perhaps never drawn, as though the artist had known the king's face and chosen mercy. Around them both, in a script that predated modern Thornfell by generations:

*When the moon turns thirteen, the thief shall stand before the hollow throne, and by her hand the Regent shall be fed—or unmade.*

Tomorrow's date. Written in dust and a century of patience.

She had told herself, these past three nights, that it was coincidence. That faces repeat. That a sketch done a hundred years ago could resemble anyone, given enough squinting. She had been very good at telling herself that, the same way she was good at lifting a man's first kiss out of his head and selling it to a widow who needed it more—he was good work, clean work, necessary work, and the fact that it hollowed something out of her each time was simply the cost of doing business.

But Vess knew. The guild knew. The crown knew. Somewhere between the burned library and tonight, her invisibility—the armor she had spent her whole life forging, memory by locked-away memory—had developed a crack, and everything with teeth had smelled blood through it.

She packed in ten minutes. She'd practiced packing in ten minutes since she was twelve years old; Vess had drilled it into her the first month. *One bundle, one knife, one exit. Anything you can't carry, you never owned.* The book went inside her shirt, against her skin, warm as a second heartbeat. Coin, flint, the thin blade in her boot, the vial of dreamless sleep she kept for emergencies. She hesitated over the rest—the chipped blue cup, the blanket her hands knew better than her head did—and left it all. Anything you can't carry, you never owned.

At the window she paused, looking down at Cindermilk Lane, at the gutter where paper boats floated messages to thieves, at the city that had been the only shape her life had ever held.

Thornfell at night was beautiful the way a knife is beautiful: all gleam and edge, and nothing soft anywhere. She had loved it without ever admitting she loved it, because love was a memory, and memories were liabilities, and liabilities got people sold.

*Don't make me choose,* Vess had said.

Too late. Someone had chosen for both of them.

Theo went out through the roof, dropped into the alley behind the cooperage, and ran.

* * *

The north gate guard took her bribe without looking at her face—grief was useful that way—and by the third hour past midnight she was climbing, the city shrinking below her into a smear of orange lamplight against the black harbor.

The mountains rose ahead like a wall built by something with more ambition than skill: the Thornfell passes, jagged and snow-hung, the spine of the world as far as anyone in the lower city cared to imagine. Nobody fled north. North was cold and empty and full of things the maps declined to name. South was the coast, the ports, the ferries—that was where fugitives went, everyone knew that, which was precisely why the guild's watchers would be thick as gulls along the southern roads by dawn.

North, then. Over the top of the world, and down the far side into whatever country would have her.

The book pressed warm against her sternum with every stride. *By her hand the Regent shall be fed—or unmade.* She didn't know what a Regent was. She didn't know why a century-dead prophet had drawn her face. She knew only that the guild had put a price on her, that the crown had put a bounty on her, and that the only direction left was up.

Behind her, far below, a bell began to ring in the lower city—the night-watch alarm, three strokes, pause, three strokes. Someone had found the empty room. Or the empty flour barrel. Or perhaps it was nothing, a fire, a brawl, the ordinary music of Thornfell.

She climbed faster anyway.

The pass narrowed. Snow crunched under her boots, loud as confession in the frozen dark. And somewhere in the hour before dawn, pausing to catch her breath against a boulder furred with ice, Theo became aware of a sound drifting down from the heights above her—thin, distant, almost lost in the wind.

Singing.

Harmonized voices, many voices braided into one, coming from somewhere high in the gorge ahead—from nowhere, from under the world. It should have been impossible. No village lay in these passes; no road ran through them; the maps showed nothing but white.

Theo stood in the snow with her breath smoking and her stolen destiny against her heart, and listened to the impossible singing call her northward like a hook through water.

Every instinct she owned said *run the other way.*

She started walking toward it.
