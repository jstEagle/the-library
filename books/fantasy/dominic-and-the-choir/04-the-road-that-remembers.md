## 4. The Road That Remembers

The mountains did not want her there.

Theo understood this the way she understood most things—without proof, without evidence, with only the deep animal certainty of a thief who had survived Thornfell's lower city by trusting the hair on her neck. For three days she had climbed away from the city, away from Vess and his soft voice and his softer betrayal, and for three days the passes had been closing around her like a fist deciding, slowly, how hard to squeeze.

She told herself it was weather.

The weather was bad enough to justify almost anything. Rain came sideways off the peaks in grey sheets, then turned to snow without announcing the change, then back to rain that froze in her hair overnight so that she woke each morning with a crust of ice cracking along her scalp like a second skull. The book rode against her sternum, wrapped in oilcloth, tied twice with cord she'd knotted in three different patterns because one knot felt insufficient against what the book was. *A century old,* she thought, for the hundredth time. *And my face is in it.* She made herself think it deliberately, the way you press a bruise to check if it still hurts. It still hurt. It still didn't feel true.

On the fourth morning she stopped telling herself it was weather.

She had camped in the lee of a granite shelf above a valley called, according to a road-marker she'd passed at dusk, the Shepherd's Throat—a name that suggested nothing good about the road through it. She woke before dawn to the particular silence of high country and lay listening to it, cataloguing sounds the way she'd once catalogued the contents of a merchant's house: wind in the scree, meltwater somewhere below, her own breath fogging, the creak of her boots drying by the dead fire. And underneath all of it, wrongness. A shape of silence where sound should have been.

She got up and walked back along the trail to the place where she'd left it last night: a cairn, waist-high, one of hundreds the shepherds built across the passes. Except the cairn wasn't where she'd left it. Or rather—it was, but the trail wasn't. The path she'd climbed yesterday afternoon had run along the eastern lip of the valley, narrow, exposed, dropping sheer into a ravine choked with pine. This morning it ran along the western lip. Same width. Same loose shale underfoot. Same shepherd's staff leaning against the same boulder, its wood silvered by decades of weather—but the boulder was forty feet from where it should have been, and the drop beside it fell away in a direction that made her stomach tilt, as though the valley itself had been picked up and set down again while she slept.

Theo stood very still for a long time.

She was not, by nature or trade, a woman who frightened easily. Fear was a luxury; fear slowed your hands. But her hands were slow now, hanging at her sides, and the cold feeling spreading through her chest was not fear exactly. It was the vertigo of Chapter Two all over again—the day in the burned library when she'd opened a book a hundred years old and found tomorrow's date written in fresh ink and her own face sketched beside a crowned figure. A life built on invisibility, and someone had written her down.

*The road knows,* said a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like her mother's, from a memory she did not have. She hated that voice. It had been surfacing more often since the library, fragments of a past she'd sealed so thoroughly even she couldn't find the seams, whispering nonsense at the edges of sleep.

"It doesn't know anything," she said aloud, because saying things aloud helped, and because there was no one for miles to hear a thief arguing with a mountain. "Roads don't know. Roads are rocks."

The road, being rocks, declined to answer. But when she started walking—eastward, deliberately, against the grain of whatever had happened—the shale crunched under her boots in a rhythm that felt, after an hour, less like coincidence and more like accompaniment.

* * *

By noon she had tested it four ways, because Theo Blackwood did not accept a haunting on faith.

First she'd tried doubling back. The pass behind her, which yesterday had descended toward a bridge she remembered crossing, now descended toward no bridge at all—just a river running too fast and too wide for its banks, white water hammering over stones that hadn't been stones yesterday. Second she'd tried climbing, leaving the trail entirely, cutting straight up a slope of scree toward a saddle she could see clearly two ridges over. Three hours of brutal ascent later she'd topped out onto the saddle—and found herself looking down at the Shepherd's Throat again, the same valley, the same granite shelf where she'd camped, small and grey below her, exactly as far below as it had been at dawn. Third she'd tried following the trail obediently, letting it take her wherever it liked. It took her east. Always east. When the trail bent north it bent in a way that curved back, like a sentence that pretends to digress but returns to its subject.

The fourth test was the one that frightened her most, because it worked.

At midafternoon she sat down on a flat stone, took out the book, unwrapped it, and laid it open on her knees to the page with her face. She'd studied it a dozen times since the library and knew every line of it: the crowned figure rendered in faded ink, tall, its features smudged as though the artist had drawn a man and then lost confidence in the drawing halfway through the mouth. Her own face beside it, younger, sharper, done in surer strokes—as though whoever held the pen a century ago had known *her* better than they'd known their king. Beneath them both, a line of verse in Old Common that she'd translated and retranslated until the words wore grooves in her:

*When the thirteenth moon has turned its wheel,*
*the thief shall walk the road that remembers,*
*and the crown shall drink, or the crown shall break.*

"The road that remembers," she murmured. Wind riffled the pages, tugging at them like fingers.

Then she closed the book, wrapped it, stowed it, stood up—and looked at the trail. It had changed while her eyes were off it. Not dramatically. A switchback that had climbed to the left now climbed to the right. A fallen pine that had barred the path now lay neatly aside, rolled clear, as though the mountain were a host tidying before guests arrived.

"Stop it," Theo said to the mountain.

The mountain did not stop. The mountain, if anything, seemed pleased.

She walked on because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the vertigo again, and vertigo on a ledge was how thieves died. She walked east because everything walked east, and because east, according to a rusted signpost whose letters she could barely read—*VHAERMOOR 9*—was a town, and towns had back doors, and back doors had roads out, and roads out were roads that didn't remember.

Nine miles, the sign promised. By dusk she had walked eleven and the sign appeared again, half-buried in snowdrift, reading *VHAERMOOR 9.*

Theo looked at it for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, ugly bark of sound that startled a ptarmigan out of the gorse—and sat down right there in the snow and let herself feel it properly at last: not fear, dread. The clean, cold, structural dread of a woman realizing the trap is not behind her. The trap is the ground she's standing on.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape. Vess had sold her—or tried to. The guilds wanted her because the prophecy made her valuable, and the crown's inquisitors wanted her because the prophecy made her dangerous, and either way they needed her *found*. And she was a thief, a good one, invisible for twenty years, untraceable, a rumor with quick hands—and yet some ink-splattered prophet a century dead had known not only her face but her *route*, had written *the road that remembers* as casually as a man writing *turn left at the well*. Which meant this wasn't pursuit. Pursuit she could handle; pursuit had a shape, pursuers had feet and habits and exhaustion. This was something else. Something was walking her, patiently, the way you walk a dog on a long lead, eastward through the passes toward a destination it had chosen before she was born.

And the question that curdled it all, the question she kept turning over like a coin with two identical faces: *who else can read me?* If a book could hold her, could a mind? Could something old and patient be following her thoughts the way she followed a mark's, watching her intentions the way she watched a merchant watch his strongbox?

She built no fire that night. Fires drew eyes, and lately every eye she imagined had a crown in it.

* * *

The fifth day brought the crows.

They came at first light, six of them, riding the thermals above the col in wide unhurried circles—not scavenging, not calling, just watching, and Theo knew watching when she saw it because she'd spent her life doing it from rooftops. They kept pace with her all morning, gliding from thermal to thermal, and when she stopped to eat they landed on the rocks ahead of her, spaced evenly, like an honor guard that had read about honor guards in a book. Their eyes were black and wet and entirely without malice, which somehow made it worse. Malice she could work with. Malice wanted things.

"You're not real," she told them. "Crows don't escort."

One of them tilted its head, considering this, and answered with a single croak that echoed off the valley walls in three directions at once, which crows' croaks do not do, and then they rose as one and continued east, and she followed, because there was nowhere else in the world to go.

It was midday, on a shelf of trail above a frozen waterfall, when she finally admitted the truth she'd been circling for two days. She could not outrun the road. She could not out-climb it, out-think it, or steal her way past it; there was nothing to steal here but distance, and distance kept giving itself back. Whatever moved these passes had been doing it long before Vess, long before the library, possibly long before the book. The only choice left was the one choice she'd never once made in twenty years of taking things from other people's heads: she could choose her destination before the road chose it for her.

So she stopped, in the lee of the frozen falls, and forced herself to think like a planner instead of prey. East lay Vhaermoor, nine miles forever, a town she distrusted on principle because the road wanted her to reach it. South, over the Serrated Col, lay the high passes where even smugglers went only in summer. North, the passes dropped into the Thornwood, and the Thornwood was guild territory—Vess territory—so north was suicide wearing a green cloak. West was Thornfell itself, and behind her came whatever Vess had loosed.

Every direction was a door with something waiting behind it. Very well. In her trade you didn't look for the safe door; you looked for the door nobody expected, and then you made it yours.

There was one direction she hadn't counted, because it wasn't a direction. Below the frozen waterfall, half-hidden behind a curtain of ice, she'd noticed it that morning and dismissed it: a seam in the rock, dark, breathing a faint warmth that melted the frost around it in a hand-width halo. A hot spring, maybe. A vent. Or—

Or a door. Doors breathed. She'd learned that much in the lower city: every hidden thing exhaled.

She spent an hour working the seam with her knife and a length of kindling, chipping ice, testing stone, telling herself she was being ridiculous, that the mountain was not offering her an exit, that mountains did not make offers. But the ice came away easier than it should have, and the crack widened into a gap, and the gap opened into a throat of warm darkness sloping down and inward—westward, she realized, with a wild grin she couldn't suppress. *Down and west.* Away from east. The first thing in five days that had moved contrary to the current.

She should have asked why. She would think, later, that she should have asked why. But five days of cold and bread-ends and being herded had worn her cunning thin, and the breath of warm air rising from the dark smelled of stone and deep water and—faintly, impossibly, beneath it—of something like singing.

She hesitated one last moment on the threshold, looking back at the pass. The crows had gone quiet. Six of them sat in a row along a dead branch above the trail, watching her with their wet black eyes, and not one of them moved to follow.

"That's right," she whispered. "You can't come."

Then she turned her back on the sky, on the east, on the whole patient conspiracy of the open country, and squeezed herself down into the warm dark, dragging the book behind her through a gap barely wider than her shoulders.

The passage narrowed, then narrowed again, until she was moving on her elbows through stone that pulsed faintly with heat, and the sound she'd caught outside resolved unmistakably now, rising up through the rock to meet her. Singing. Many voices, low and layered, harmonized in a way that made her teeth ache and her eyes sting and some sealed room behind her ribs rattle on its hinges.

She knew that harmony. She didn't know how she knew it, but her body knew it the way a body knows falling—she had heard voices braided like this once, long ago, in a memory she had sold or buried or lost, and every note of it was saying *come down, come down, we've been waiting.*

Theo pressed her forehead against the warm stone and breathed, once, twice.

"This is a trap," she informed the darkness conversationally. "Obviously. Fine. I've been in traps before. At least this one sings."

She went down toward the voices, and behind her, far above, the road quietly rearranged itself, and waited.
