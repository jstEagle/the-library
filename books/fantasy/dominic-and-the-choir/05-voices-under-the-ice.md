## 5. Voices Under the Ice

The gorge had teeth.

Theo felt them before she saw them—jagged ridges of blue ice crowding in from both walls, leaning toward each other like conspirators, narrowing the passage until the sky above her was nothing but a seam of grey. Three days in the Thornfell passes, and the mountains had stopped pretending to be indifferent. They wanted her somewhere. She could feel it the way a thief feels a marked purse: the pull of intention, patient and vast, all around her.

She had stopped trusting the paths two days ago. The trail she'd chosen at the last village—a smuggler's route, worn into her memory by years of guild work—had simply *ended* one morning, swallowed by scree that no map had ever shown. Another path had appeared to replace it, faint but walkable, bending always eastward. Always east. She'd tried walking west twice. Both times, the weather turned. Both times, she'd found herself at dawn with her back to the sunset, her boots pointing east, and no memory of having turned.

A thief who kept no memories of her own could not afford to lose the ones she stole. But these losses were different. These were losses of *hours*, of intention, of the small sovereign territory of her own will. Something in these mountains was editing her, the way she edited merchants and widows—gently, expertly, leaving no scar.

So when the gorge closed around her and the temperature dropped so sharply her breath cracked in the air like breaking glass, Theo did the only sensible thing a hunted woman could do.

She stopped running and started listening.

* * *

The gorge floor was a river frozen mid-motion, its surface sculpted into waves and whirls, as if the water had been caught fleeing and turned to glass for the crime. Theo picked her way along it, boots wrapped in strips of blanket, hands tucked into her sleeves. The cold here was not the honest cold of altitude. It had a texture. A *direction*. It flowed past her toward some point deeper in, the way air flows toward an open window, and she understood with a thief's certainty that she was being drawn along a current not of wind but of *attention*.

Her fingers found the book inside her coat for the hundredth time. The prophecy. Her own face, sketched in faded ink beside a crowned figure, above a date that had come and gone while she hid in the ashes of a burned library. She still hadn't read past the first pages. Reading it felt like picking her own pocket—reaching inside herself for something she hadn't chosen to carry.

The light was failing. In the gorge it failed faster, the walls drinking the last grey hour until only a dim phosphorescence remained, seeping from the ice itself, a ghost-light that turned her shadow into a smear of ink on the frozen river.

That was when she heard the singing.

It came up through the soles of her boots first. She felt it as a tremor, fine as a hair drawn tight, and thought for one lurching moment that the ice was cracking beneath her, that the river was waking to take her down. She froze, weight balanced, ready to throw herself toward the bank—

But the tremor was rhythmic. Measured. And then it rose, past her ankles, her knees, into the bones of her ears, and became sound.

Voices. Many voices, singing in a harmony Theo had no name for. It was not a melody, exactly—or it was a melody the way a river is a line. It moved in layers, in weaves, one voice holding a single low note like a root while others climbed and folded above it, and the strange thing, the thing that made her breath go shallow, was that the harmony was *familiar*. Not that she'd heard it before. That it sounded like something she had lost.

She knew that feeling intimately. She spent her life watching people touch the shape of what they'd lost—the merchant with his first kiss gone, the widow buying back a dead man's voice. She knew the face they made. She was making it now, alone in a gorge at the edge of the world, her cheeks aching with an expression she hadn't authorized.

The singing was coming from below the ice.

"Right," she said aloud, because saying it aloud made it marginally less impossible. Her voice fell flat and small against the vast hush. "Right. Of course it is."

She should have turned back. She was a professional; she knew the anatomy of a trap better than anyone alive, and every instinct she owned was screaming that a voice beneath the ice in a gorge that steered travelers east was not a coincidence but a *lure*. Anglerfish sang too. She'd stolen the memory of a fisherman once, a man who'd spent his life reading water, and in his mind she'd learned that the sea never sang to anything it didn't intend to eat.

But her boots were already moving. That was the horror of it, the thing she would turn over and over in the hours after: she had decided to walk away, and her body had decided otherwise, and she could not find the seam between those two decisions. The current of cold air tugged at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades. The harmony swelled, gentle as a hand extended in a dark room.

The ice beneath her feet began to glow.

It started far ahead—a bloom of soft gold deep in the frozen river, like a lamp behind stained glass—and spread toward her in slow pulses that matched the music. Theo crouched, laid her palm flat against the surface. It was warm. Not warm the way a body is warm. Warm the way a memory is warm, the way a kitchen is warm in a recollection of childhood, warmth that has nothing to do with temperature and everything to do with *welcome*.

Under her hand, the ice cleared. And she looked down into a corridor.

It ran beneath the river—a passage of grey stone, arched and ancient, lit by that same gold. Figures moved in it. Robed figures, grey as the stone, walking in a slow procession, and as she watched, they lifted their faces toward the ice above them, toward her, and sang.

They were singing *to her*. She understood that with total, sickening clarity. The harmony bent as she watched, the melody resolving, and though the words were in no language she knew, their meaning arrived in her chest fully formed, the way stolen memories arrived in her hands: *You are expected. You are expected. You are expected.*

"No," Theo said. Her voice shook. "No, I'm not. I'm not expected anywhere. That's the entire point of me."

The ice glowed brighter. The singing deepened. And twenty paces ahead, where the gorge bent and the walls pressed close, she saw it: a door.

It was set into the rock itself, so cunningly wrought that she would have walked past it a hundred times without seeing it—she might have walked past it already, she realized, might have passed it twice in the dark. It was black wood banded with pale metal, and it bore no handle, no hinge, no lock. Only a carving at its center: a circle of thirteen moons, twelve full and one a sliver of shadow, and beneath them a face with its eyes closed.

The same face as the crowned figure in the book. Older, unlined, at peace. A face she had seen only in faded ink and was somehow, impossibly, beginning to know.

The singing stopped.

The silence that followed was worse—total, pressurized, the silence of held breath. The gold light in the ice dimmed to embers. And the door, with a sound like a long exhale, swung inward.

Theo stood in the dark with a dead man's knife in her boot, a stolen book against her heart, and three days of stolen hours behind her, and she made the last free choice she would make for some time.

She ran the other way.

* * *

She made it perhaps forty paces.

The gorge had changed. She was certain of it—certain with the flat, cold certainty of a professional recognizing another professional's work. The walls that had been a spear's length apart were now a hundred paces of sheer glassy stone. The frozen river behind her had become a slope, rising gently, impossibly, so that the way back was uphill and the door was downhill and the whole geography of the place had been rearranged around her like furniture in a room while she slept.

The current of cold air pushed her, gently, between the shoulder blades.

"Fine," she snarled, and her breath tore white across the dark. "Fine. You want me? I want to see who's doing the steering."

She walked to the door. It stood open on a corridor of grey stone, and the warmth that came out of it was the warmest thing she had felt in three days, and she hated how much she wanted it, and she stepped inside.

The door closed behind her with the soft finality of a purse-string drawn tight.

* * *

The corridor was lit by lamps of frosted glass that held a light like moonlight caught and kept. The walls were hung with tapestries so old their colors had gone to whispers—she caught the ghost of a harvest, the ghost of a coronation, the ghost of a burning tower—and the air smelled of cedar smoke and cold stone and, underneath it, something sweet she couldn't name. Something that made her chest ache the way the singing had.

*Like something you lost,* said a small voice in her head. *Everything here smells like something you lost.*

"Then it's a trap," she muttered, "or a market."

"Neither. Though we do trade."

The voice came from ahead, where the corridor opened into a hall. Theo's knife was in her hand before the echo finished—she hadn't decided to draw it either, and she was beginning to take a grim professional interest in how many of her decisions tonight were not her own.

The hall was vast and low-ceilinged, carved from the mountain's living rock, and it was full of people.

They sat in a great ring around a central fire, dozens of them, grey-robed, grey-haired, grey-eyed, so uniform in their dress that at first they seemed like one creature in many bodies. Old and young, and every age between. Some held instruments she didn't recognize—strings and pipes and bowls of water that trembled with the memory of song. And every face in the ring was turned toward her.

Not curious. Not hostile. *Recognizing.* The way a family recognizes a face in a doorway.

Theo had been looked at by crowds before. She had been hunted through Thornfell's lower city by men who wanted her hands and women who wanted her services and children who wanted nothing at all because they couldn't see her—she made sure of that, she wore her invisibility like a second skin, she was the best at not being seen in a city of seeing. But she had never once been looked at like *this*. Like a letter finally delivered. Like a key finding the only lock it fit.

"Stay back," she said. The knife made a small, insufficient argument. "All of you. I've taken memories from armed men in their own homes. I'll take yours and walk out of here not knowing your names or that you ever existed."

The nearest of them—a woman, silver-haired, with a scar that split one eyebrow—did not step back. She smiled, and it was a terrible kind smile, the kind you give someone standing on a ledge.

"You couldn't," the woman said gently. "Not ours. That's rather the point of us, little thief. You can't steal a song that's being sung. And we are always singing."

"Then I'll cut the song out of your throat and be gone before—"

"You won't." The woman's eyes flicked, just once, to the book-shaped bulge against Theo's coat. "You've carried that a long way. Through passes that don't want you to leave. Past a door that opened only for you. You could have burned it in a hundred fires between here and Thornfell. You didn't. May we see it?"

"No."

"Then keep it." The woman spread her hands, palms up, empty. "We have waited a hundred years. We can wait while you decide to trust us. That is, after all, what we do. We wait, and we remember, and we sing, so that the world does not forget what it is." Her voice softened. "We are the Choir. And you, Theo Blackwood, are the reason we are still singing."

The name went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor.

No one in the mountains knew her name. No one in Thornfell had known it in years—Vess had known it, and Vess had sold her, and the book had known her face but not her name, she was almost sure it hadn't, she would have remembered, she remembered everything she stole—

"You knew my name," Theo said. The knife point wavered, and she hated it, and steadied it. "The guild didn't know my name. The crown doesn't know my name. Who told you?"

"No one told us." The silver-haired woman's terrible kind smile faded into something older and sadder. "We sang it. A hundred years ago, a singer of ours heard the future the way you'd hear music through a wall—muffled, half-remembered. She wrote down what she heard. A thief with no past. A king with no self. A crown that eats. And a thirteenth moon." She stepped aside, and the whole ring of grey robes shifted, opening a path through their center, toward the fire and the shadows beyond it. "Our leader has waited her whole life to explain it to you properly. She's waited through things I hope you never learn. Will you come and meet Sera Vane?"

Every instinct Theo owned said *run*. The gorge, the door, the current in the cold air, the name in a stranger's mouth—all of it, one long con, the most patient and elaborate mark she had ever seen worked, and she was the mark, she had been the mark since the day she was born, perhaps, since the day something had burned—

The thought arrived and stopped. She didn't know why it stopped. She didn't know what *burned*.

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the ring, softly, a voice began to hum the harmony from the ice, and another joined it, and another, and the sound rose around her gentle as snowfall, and Theo Blackwood—who had spent her whole life taking other people's pasts because she could not bear to carry her own—stood in the warm heart of the mountain, surrounded by strangers who knew her name, and found that she could not make herself leave.

"Fine," she said, and it came out smaller than she meant it. "But I keep the book. And I keep the knife. And if anyone sings *at* me, I walk."

"Of course," said the silver-haired woman, and behind her eyes, briefly, Theo saw something that was not kindness at all but grief, vast and old and patient, the grief of someone who knows exactly what a thing will cost before it is paid. "Come and sit. Sera has been waiting for you since before you were born."

"And she can start," Theo said, following the ring of grey robes into the firelight, "by telling me why her prophecy has my face in it."

The singing rose to meet her, and beneath it, so faint she almost missed it, Theo heard—woven into the harmony like a single thread of darker wool—a melody she had never heard before in her life, and would have sworn, weeping, that she had known all along.
