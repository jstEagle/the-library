## 6. The First Verse

Sera Vane had not slept in the room they gave her guests for eleven years, and she did not sleep in it now.

She stood instead at the window of her own cell—the word was honest, even if the Choir preferred *chamber*—watching the gorge ice catch the last grey light of evening and thinking about the girl locked three floors below her feet. The girl with the thief's calluses and the prophet's face.

*The ink doesn't lie,* she told herself, the way she'd been telling herself since Bram had carried the stolen book up from the gatehouse, snow in his hair and triumph all over him like a boy showing off a dead bird. *The ink doesn't lie, and that is precisely what frightens me.*

The book lay open on her desk behind her. She did not need to look at it anymore. She had looked at it so many times in the past two days that the sketch had worn itself into the back of her eyelids: a crowned figure on a throne of thorns, and beside it, smaller, sharper-eyed, a young woman rendered in a few confident strokes of a pen dipped a hundred years ago by a hand that could not possibly have known—

But it had known. That was the unbearable part. The hand had known.

Sera pressed her forehead against the cold glass and let the mountain's silence press back.

Below her, muffled through stone and ice, the evening verse had begun. She could not hear the words at this distance—only the shape of them, the low sustained tone that the younger singers called *the floor* because everything else stood on top of it. Forty voices holding one note until the note stopped being sound and became something more like weather.

It had taken her thirty years to learn to hear the difference between a memory being sung and a memory being mourned. The evening verse was both, always. That was the price of their discipline. You could not carry other people's pasts without your own becoming heavier.

She thought of Aldric. She thought of him every evening at this hour, though she would have cut out her own tongue before admitting it to the Council of Verses. She thought of a young king in a coronation hall, laughing at something his fool had said, the crown still new enough that he kept touching it as if to confirm it was real—and she thought of the moment, four hours later by her count, when the laughter had gone out of his eyes like water down a drain, and something else had moved in behind them and settled, patient as frost.

*I was there,* she reminded herself. *I was ten paces away, and I sang, and it wasn't enough.*

The guilt was an old companion now. It knew her habits. It knew that at this hour, in this light, it could sit down beside her without being asked.

She turned from the window and went down to meet the thief.

* * *

They had put Theo Blackwood in the Listening Room because it was the only chamber in the fastness with a door that locked from the outside, and because its acoustics were gentle—a mercy, Sera felt, for someone whose skull had presumably spent the last day feeling like a struck bell. Memory-singers were not gentle people by nature. They simply practiced gentleness the way archers practiced archery: daily, deliberately, with attention to form.

The girl sat on the stone bench beneath the eastern window with her arms crossed and her weight on her heels, the posture of someone who had already catalogued every exit. Her eyes tracked Sera across the room and dismissed her in the same motion—or tried to. Sera watched her try again, slower, and fail again, and something in Sera's chest that had been clenched for two days loosened by a single degree.

*Good,* she thought. *Be wary. Wary is survivable.*

"You're the one who runs this place," the girl said. Not a question. Her voice was lower than her frame suggested, roughened at the edges, the voice of someone who had learned early that whispering carried better than shouting in the places she worked.

"I keep the verses," Sera said. "Whether that amounts to running anything, the mountain can decide." She seated herself on the opposite bench, leaving the full width of the room between them. Distance was a courtesy here. "My name is Sera Vane."

"Theo."

"Just Theo?"

Something flickered across the girl's face—quick, guarded, gone. A door closing on a room Sera suddenly very much wanted to see. "Just Theo."

"Then just Theo." Sera folded her hands in her lap, palms up, an old singer's habit that meant *nothing hidden, nothing held.* "Bram tells me you found the book in the Ashfall archive."

"Bram talks too much."

"Bram is nineteen and in love with the idea of destiny. It's a disease of his age. We treat it with chores." Sera let the ghost of a smile touch her mouth, then set it aside; there was no time for charm, and the girl would smell charm anyway the way a cat smells rain. "You read the prophecy."

"I read the part about me." Theo's jaw tightened. "Which was generous of whoever wrote it, considering I hadn't been born yet."

"And the rest?"

"I skimmed the rest. It's the usual scripture soup. Moons, thrones, blood on the snow." Her eyes narrowed. "Except the moons part keeps repeating. Thirteen turns. Like whoever wrote it was counting down to something."

Sera exhaled slowly. Here it was, then. The conversation she had rehearsed in front of the window and abandoned six times, because no rehearsal survived contact with those grey eyes.

"There is no scripture soup," she said. "There is a ledger, and we are all of us in arrears. Will you hear it properly? All of it? It will take some time, and some of it will be ugly, and none of it will flatter you."

Theo unfolded her arms—one degree of openness, grudging as a toll paid under protest. "I've got nowhere else to be. Your ice seems to have opinions about letting people leave."

"The ice does what we ask of it." Sera inclined her head, acknowledging the hit. "That is the first thing you should understand about the Choir. Everything you saw on the mountain—the paths that bent toward us, the storm that herded you into the gorge—that was us. We have been singing the roads since before Thornfell had a name. The land remembers what we teach it, and it obeys."

"You steered me here."

"We invited. Steering implies you weren't walking under your own will. You took every step yourself, thief. We only made sure the steps led somewhere warm." Sera held up a hand before the anger could finish forming. "And yes—I know how that sounds to someone whose entire life has been spent avoiding exactly this kind of attention. I know it feels like a trap. Sit with the feeling. Don't act on it yet. By the end of what I have to tell you, you may find the trap was the safest place in the kingdom."

Theo didn't sit so much as settle, coiled. "Talk."

So Sera talked.

* * *

She began, as her own teacher had begun with her, with the crown.

"It was forged two hundred years ago for a different king entirely—Edmund the Third, who was a vain man and wanted a coronation band no one could copy. The smith who made it was not merely a smith. He was what we once called a *hollow-craftsman*: a maker who could work memory into metal the way we work it into song. He poured things into that crown. Blessings, mostly. Oaths of protection sworn by the whole assembled court. A vessel of remembering, meant to hold the kingdom's love for its king and pour it back into him on the bad days."

"What happened to Edmund?"

"He died. Kings do." Sera's mouth twisted. "The crown passed to his son, and his son's son, and each reign it thickened. Because here is what no one understood, what the smith perhaps never understood himself: a vessel built to hold memory does not stay empty between kings. It fills. And filled things grow heavy, and heavy things grow hungry, and somewhere in the long centuries—no one knows when, that is the horror of it, no one noticed the exact night—something woke inside the crown. Something made of everything it had ever swallowed. We call it the Regent."

"Because it rules while the king can't."

"Because it rules *instead of* the king, and lets everyone believe otherwise." Sera leaned forward. "Listen carefully now, because this is the heart of it. The Regent does not eat bread or gold. It eats forgetting. Every memory that dies unremembered—a face no living person recalls, a promise broken so thoroughly even the promise-breaker forgot making it, a village's name worn smooth off the map—it drinks these. It has been drinking for two hundred years. And Aldric—" she made herself say the name plainly, without ceremony, because ceremony was where her courage went to hide, "Aldric has worn the crown for twenty-three years."

Twenty-three years. She had done the arithmetic so often the numbers had grooves in them. She had been thirty-one when he was crowned. She remembered standing in the third rank of the Choir's delegation, singing the old blessing-verse, watching the crown descend onto his head, and she remembered—she would always remember—the half-second delay before his smile arrived, as if something behind his face had to approve it first.

She had told herself she imagined it. She had told herself that for fifteen years.

"Aldric isn't dead," she said aloud. "He isn't even gone, not wholly. The Regent keeps him alive the way a farmer keeps a field—fed enough to produce, never enough to thrive. Some days the man surfaces. Lucid minutes. An hour, if you're lucky. I've seen him twice in five years, and both times he knew exactly how much of himself had been eaten, and both times he asked me—" her voice caught, and she rode over it, "he asked me whether the harvest festival had happened yet. Whether the children had danced. Because the Regent eats from the edges inward, and the first thing it took from him was the ordinary joy. It saved his mind for last. Minds are the richest meal."

Theo was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge—not softened, but focused, the way a blade focuses. "You said it'll consume the kingdom."

"It will. It must. It's running out of food." Sera spread her hands. "Think of what forgetting means at scale. Two generations ago, every household in the valley could sing their grandmothers' lullabies. Now? Ask a child in Thornfell's lower city to name her great-grandmother and watch her stare. The Regent's hunger has accelerated. The moon-tide carries memories the way a river carries silt—we can measure it, thief. We have measured it for forty years. And the measurements converge on one date."

"The thirteenth turn."

"The moon completes its great cycle on the night of the Moonturn festival, ninety-one days from now. On that night the tide of forgetting will be at its fullest in three hundred years. If the Regent feeds on that tide—if it takes the whole flood at once—there will not be a kingdom left to consume. There will be a country of people who wake up and cannot remember why the fields are planted, why the borders run where they run, why they owe each other anything at all. Forgetting is not peaceful, Theo. Forgetting is how wars start and famines get mismanaged and children die of cures that existed once and exist no longer."

The words hung in the cold air of the Listening Room. Somewhere above them the evening verse had ended, and the silence it left behind seemed to lean in and listen too.

Theo turned her head toward the window, where the ice glowed faintly blue with the last of the light. "The book," she said finally. "The one I stole out of a burned library because my handler wanted it burned properly. It says a thief opens the king's mind. Why? Why would it take a thief? Why not one of you?" She gestured at the walls, the ceiling, the whole buried order. "You people do this professionally. You walk around in other heads for a living."

"Because our doors don't open from the outside." Sera rose and crossed to the shelf where the archive book lay, and lifted it, and set it on the bench between them, open to the page she had come to know like her own knuckles. The crowned figure. The sharp-eyed woman. The line of verse beneath, in a hand like iron filings:

*Only the one who owes no debt to song shall pass the crown's last door.*

"We are singers," Sera said. "Every one of us has sung since childhood. Our minds are built of memory the way coral is built of shell—layer on layer, given and received. The crown's defenses recognize that architecture. They would seal against any of us before we got within a mile of the throne room. But you—" she met the grey eyes squarely, "you have spent your life taking memories without singing them. Taking without giving. Your mind is a house of borrowed furniture with no debts attached. To the crown's magic, you are not a singer. You are weather. You are nothing it knows how to stop."

"That's flattering."

"It's a death sentence wearing a compliment's coat, and we both know it." Sera's voice came out harder than she intended, and she let it. Let the girl see the cost sitting openly on the table. "I won't dress this in silk, Theo Blackwood. Entering that mind means facing the Regent in its own territory, at the height of its feeding season, with nothing but whatever you can carry and whatever we can sing to shield you. The prophecy promises victory. Prophecies promise many things. I have read enough of them to know they are written by people who will not be present when the bill comes due."

For the first time, the girl laughed—a short, bitter bark with no humor in it. "There it is. There's the sales pitch underneath. You need a sacrifice and the universe conveniently wrote down my face a century ago, and I'm supposed to swallow that?"

"No," Sera said quietly. "You are supposed to hear it. Swallowing is your decision, and no one under this roof—including me, including the whole Council of Verses—will take that decision from you. That is Choir law older than the crown itself: *no voice is spent unasked.*" She paused. Drew breath. And because she was tired, and guilty, and because the girl deserved at least one true thing offered freely, she said the rest.

"But I will tell you why *I* believe. Not the Council. Me."

* * *

Sera returned to the bench. Sat. Looked at her hands.

"Nine years ago, we attempted what we are asking of you. Before the prophecy surfaced—before we knew the crown could only be opened from outside its own architecture. We believed a strong enough chorus, sung close enough, could reach Aldric through the dream-tide and shore up what remained of him. Buy him years. Four of us went to the capital under festival cover. Myself, Elder Corrin, and two others whose names I will not say tonight because saying them costs me more than the story is worth."

Theo had gone very still. It was the stillness of a professional recognizing another professional's tells, and Sera respected it enough not to soften what came next.

"We reached the outer corridors of his mind. Beautiful, terrible country—palace halls built of his memories, every tapestry a summer, every doorway a year. And the Regent was already there, wearing his shape, sitting his throne, and it looked up at us with his eyes and it *smiled*, and it said—" her throat closed. Nine years, and it still closed. "It said, *Welcome home, little birds. I remember all of you. Shall I prove it?*

"And then it sang *back* at us. Our own voices. Every secret verse we had ever whispered, every memory we had ever carried for another person, laid out in our own tones like evidence on a table. Corrin broke first. He tried to flee his own mind and the flight tore him loose from his body, and he died in a rented room in the capital's tanners' quarter with his mouth full of someone else's wedding song. The other two followed within minutes. I survived because I did the one thing we are trained never to do." She looked up. "I stopped listening. I sealed myself deaf and dumb inside my own skull and crawled out along the tide-line, and I lived, and I have hated myself for it every single day since, because sealing yourself deaf means you leave your people behind screaming and you don't even get to hear their names as they go."

The silence after was total. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

"So when I tell you the danger is real, thief, I am not reciting scripture. I am reading you a receipt." Sera straightened her spine, because her teacher had taught her that grief was permitted but slumping was not. "And when I tell you I believe the prophecy, it is because of what the Regent said next, in the last moments before I fled. It looked at me with Aldric's exhausted, drowning eyes and it said: *Bring the one with no debts, or the feast ends and I starve.* It told me the prophecy's terms itself. It is afraid of you, Theo Blackwood. Whatever else is true tonight, that is true. The thing that ate a king is afraid of a pickpocket from Thornfell—and a frightened enemy is an enemy that can be beaten."

Theo stared at her. Then at the book. Then at her own hands, turning them over as if checking them for marks.

"Ninety-one days," she said at last.

"Ninety-one days."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we grieve, and we begin teaching the valleys which songs to write down, and we lose gracefully, and the world forgets us along with everything else." Sera smiled, and was surprised to find the smile almost steady. "But you won't say no. I've watched you since Bram dragged you through our door. You checked the exits twice, you counted the singers at evening verse—you think we didn't notice—and then you stood closest to the wall where you could hear us best. You want to know what we know. Curiosity like yours doesn't sign contracts. It signs heists."

Something moved behind the grey eyes—anger, maybe, at being read; maybe something warmer wearing anger's coat.

"One condition," Theo said.

"Name it."

"Nobody sings *my* memories. Whatever's mine stays mine. You want a thief with no debts? Fine. But the ledger runs one direction. Understood?"

Sera held her gaze and thought of the sealed room in her own chest, the one labeled *Corrin* and *the tanners' quarter* and *the screaming I chose not to hear,* and understood the request more completely than the girl could guess.

"Understood," she said. "No one touches what's yours."

Later—much later, alone again at her window with the ice glowing blue-black below—Sera would admit to the dark what she had not admitted to the girl: that she had lied. Not maliciously. Not even knowingly, at the time.

But the rite they would need to prepare Theo for the descent—the rehearsal rite, the one that let a stranger practice walking through a foreign mind—it required material. And the only safe material, the only mind shallow and sheltered enough for training, belonged to the trainee herself.

Tomorrow, Sera would have to tell Bram to open the girl's past anyway.

Or, said a small cold voice that sounded unpleasantly like the Regent's borrowed baritone, tomorrow she would have to find out what else the girl was hiding, and why a thief who claimed to keep no memories of her own slept with her fists closed so tightly, every night, as if guarding a room even she had never entered.

Outside, far above the gorge, the moon rose thin as a paring knife, thirteen nights short of its first quarter, counting down.
