## 7. Lessons in Losing

Bram had been singing memories since he was six years old, and in all those years he had never once seen anyone fight a memory the way the thief fought them.

She fought them the way a drowning woman fights the sea.

He watched her from the edge of the practice chamber, arms folded, trying not to look as entertained as he felt. The chamber was a hollowed-out pocket in the mountain's belly, its walls veined with pale quartz that caught the light of the hanging lamps and scattered it like frost. The Choir called it the Stillroom, though nothing about it was still. Memory moved through it constantly—pale ribbons of song drifting from singer to singer, from shelf to shelf, from the great stone basin at the room's center to the minds that came to drink from it.

The basin was where the thief stood now, up to her elbows in someone else's wedding day.

"Stop *grabbing* it," Bram called. "You're not picking a lock. It's not a lock."

"It's fighting me." Her voice came out strained, gritted, like she was hauling on a rope. Her sleeves were shoved to the elbow, though her hands touched nothing—she was working in the between-space, the place singers went when they listened with more than their ears. Sweat beaded at her hairline despite the chamber's chill.

"It's not fighting you. It's *drowning*. You're the one thrashing." He crossed the room, boots quiet on the stone. Around the basin's rim, the memory shimmered—a bride's laughter caught mid-note, the smell of lilies, a groom's trembling hands. It should have been the gentlest thing in the Stillroom. Under the thief's grip it churned like a storm-tossed sea.

"Breathe," he said, and put two fingers lightly against her wrist.

She flinched hard enough that the whole memory shuddered, and the bride's laughter curdled into static. Bram snatched his hand back.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. I should have asked."

"You should have." She flexed her fingers, staring at the churning basin like it had personally insulted her. "Everyone here keeps *touching* me. Sera put a blanket over my shoulders the first night like I was a stray cat. You people have no concept of—"

"Of what?"

"Of what it means when someone gets close enough to touch you." She said it flatly, without drama, the way a person reports the weather. *It will rain tomorrow. People will hurt you if you let them near.* "In Thornfell, distance is the only thing that keeps you alive."

"Then how do you ever steal anything? You have to get close for that."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, maybe, that he'd thought it through. "Stealing's different. Stealing, you get close and then you're gone before they feel the absence. This—" she gestured at the basin, at the Choir's whole soft-lit world of shared song, "—this is the opposite. You want me to *stay* inside someone else's head. To let it stay inside me."

"That's the whole art, yes."

"Then the whole art is insane."

Bram laughed. He couldn't help it. She glared at him with those flat grey eyes, and he realized she hadn't been joking, and that made it funnier somehow, and worse.

"Fair," he said, raising his hands in surrender. "It is insane. My mother wept for three days when she first sang a stranger's grief, and she was Choir-born. You're asking your heart to hold things that never happened to you and grieve them anyway. Nobody does it without breaking a little." He nodded at the basin. "But you can't break it by gripping it. That's the first lesson. Memories aren't locks. They're birds. You hold them open-handed or you crush them."

"Charming. Do you practice these little sayings in the mirror?"

"Every morning. I have a whole book of them."

The corner of her mouth moved. Not a smile. But the *neighbor* of a smile, and Bram, who had spent nineteen years learning to read the small weather of people's faces, marked it and said nothing.

* * *

Her name was Theo. He'd learned that much from the others, whispered in the corridors—*the thief, the one from the prophecy, the one Sera says we need.* They said it the way sailors say the name of a storm.

Bram had read the prophecy book himself, twice, in the days since she'd arrived. He'd looked at the sketch on the vellum—the sharp face, the watchful eyes—and then at the woman who now stood in their practice chamber failing, magnificently, to do what Choir children learned at seven. It was strange. The drawing had looked like destiny. The woman looked like a cornered animal that had decided, for the moment, not to bite.

He understood cornered animals. He'd been one, once, before the Choir found him half-starved in a border town with a voice full of other people's sorrows and no idea where they'd come from. Sera had sat with him for a whole winter, teaching him which griefs were his and which he'd swallowed from strangers. He remembered what it was to not know the shape of your own self.

So when Sera said, *Bram, work with her, she trusts you more than the rest of us—she trusts you because you're the least threatening thing in this mountain,* he'd taken it as the compliment it was.

They tried again. This time he stood beside her at the basin, close enough that his shoulder nearly brushed hers, and sang the opening note of the wedding memory low and steady, a ground-note to build on.

"Don't reach for it," he murmured. "Just listen. Where does it want to go?"

"It doesn't *want*—"

"Everything wants. Even memories. This one wants to be *finished*. Look—here." He let the note bloom, and the bride's laughter uncurled again, and the lily-scent thickened, and somewhere in the shimmer a fiddle began tuning. "It's a day that got cut short. The groom died two years after; the widow sold the memory to us herself, couldn't bear to carry it. It's been sitting in this basin waiting for someone to hear the whole of it. All you have to do is follow."

"Follow where?"

"Wherever it's going. You don't steer a memory, you—"

"—ride it. Yes. Sera said." Theo closed her eyes. Her jaw was tight, her shoulders up around her ears like a drawn bow. Bram sang the ground-note again, softer, and watched her fight the oldest fight in the Choir's book: the fight against being carried.

For a moment—just a moment—she let go.

He saw it happen. Saw the tension drain out of her hands, saw her breathing slow and deepen, saw her face go slack and then *lit*, the way faces did when a memory opened its wings. She followed the bride through a doorway he couldn't see, into the middle of a day that had ended decades ago, and for three heartbeats she was *there*, truly there, open-handed, and she was—she was *good*. Not clumsy. Not a student. The thief moved through memory like she'd been born in it, quick and light and sure, and Bram felt his mouth fall open.

Then she hit the edge of the groom's fear—the memory's one dark seam, the shadow of the illness that would take him—and she *seized* it. Instinct. He watched her try to pick it apart, probe its structure, find its seams, the way she must have done a thousand times in a thousand heads in Thornfell. The memory shrieked. The fiddle warped. The whole wedding day folded in on itself like a burned page, and Theo staggered back from the basin with a gasp, one hand pressed flat over her sternum as though something in there had been struck.

"I *had* it," she snarled, at the basin, at him, at herself. "I was in. I was all the way in, and then it turned on me—"

"It didn't turn on you. You turned on it." Bram kept his voice gentle, which took effort, because his heart was still pounding with what he'd seen her do. "The dark part of the memory. You went for it like it was a lockbox."

"It *was* a lockbox. Everything dark is a lockbox. That's where people keep the valuable things."

"That's where people keep the *wounds*." He let the basin settle, singing it quiet, the scattered wedding day drifting back down into stillness. "You don't break into a wound, Theo. You sing to it until it opens on its own. Or you leave it shut and carry the whole person gently around it. That's what we do. That's what the Choir *is*."

She was quiet a long moment. Her hand was still pressed to her chest.

"It felt like him," she said at last, and her voice had gone somewhere he hadn't heard it go before—small, and young, and startled. "The groom. When I touched the fear. It felt like—" She stopped. Shut her mouth so fast her teeth clicked.

"Like what?"

"Nothing. It felt like nothing." She straightened, and the shutters came down behind her eyes so smoothly he almost missed them, and Bram understood he had just watched a door close that she'd spent her whole life building. "Again. From the top. I'll get it this time."

* * *

They worked until the lamps burned low. She was tireless in the way that frightened people are tireless—practicing not to learn but to *arm*, turning every lesson into a weapon, every technique into a tool she might one day need to survive. She learned to ride a memory's current. She learned to find a mind's doors without forcing them. She learned, on the fourth hour, to touch a wound without seizing it, and when the groom's fear finally opened under her song—gently, like a hand unclenching—she came out of it pale and silent and did not speak for a full ten minutes.

Bram didn't push. He sang the basin still, and he shared out the bread and hard cheese he'd brought in his pocket, and they ate sitting on the cold stone with their backs against the quartz-veined wall, and he talked about nothing. The weather in the passes. The terrible cooking of Brother Anselm. The way the mountain sang back sometimes, if you hummed at it long enough, in a voice like wind through a bottle.

She didn't talk. But she listened, and she ate, and she didn't leave, and Bram had worked with enough wounded creatures to know that staying was its own kind of speech.

"Can I ask you something?" he said eventually.

"You can ask."

"What's it like? Taking a memory out of someone's head. Really."

She turned the bread crust over in her fingers. "It's like lifting a stone off a grave," she said. "You feel how much lighter they are. And you feel how much lighter you aren't, because now you're carrying it." A pause. "Most of them thank me. That's the worst part. They *thank* me, and I walk out into the rain with their first kiss or their dead son's laugh in my head, and I sell it to someone who'll weep over it, and everyone's a little less heavy and nothing is any better."

"Doesn't it hurt? Carrying them."

"Of course it hurts."

"Then why—"

"Because the alternative is worse." She said it fast, too fast, and he watched her realize she'd said too much, watched the calculation run behind her eyes. But she didn't take it back. She sat with it, jaw tight, staring at the far wall.

"The alternative," Bram said quietly, "being that you feel your own."

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. The silence answered for her, vast and walled and very old.

"You know," he said, "the Choir could help with that. That's the other half of what we do. Not just holding other people's memories. Finding your own. Singing them back to you if they've been—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice. She was on her feet before he could blink, bread crust dropped, whole body angled toward the door. "Don't. I don't have anything to find. There's nothing. It's not a wound, it's not a lockbox, it's just *nothing*, it's an empty room, and I like it empty, and if any of you grey-robed saints come singing at my head in the night I will—"

She stopped. Because Bram hadn't moved. Hadn't flinched. He was still sitting against the wall, looking up at her, and he was humming.

Low. Almost under his breath. A lullaby-shape, four notes rising and falling, the kind of tune that has no words and needs none. He didn't even know where he'd gotten it—some half-remembered scrap from the Choir's songbooks, a cradle-song from the eastern valleys. He was humming it the way you'd hum to a spooked horse, mindless and steady, and he watched her fury hit it like a wave hits a rock and break apart into something else.

Her face did something complicated. The anger drained out of it and left behind a look he couldn't name—hunger, maybe, or grief, or the place where hunger and grief are the same thing.

"What is that?" she whispered. "What song is that?"

"I don't know. An old one. Why?"

"Nothing." She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth for a moment, then dropped it. When she spoke again her voice was level, and only someone who'd spent the whole day inside her defenses would have heard the tremor underneath. "It's nothing. It sounds like nothing. Like every cradle-song ever sung."

"Right," Bram said, though it wasn't right at all, because her eyes were shining and she was gripping her own wrist hard enough to leave marks.

* * *

He walked her to her cell that night, because the mountain's corridors were a maze and she still got lost, and because he suspected she'd rather cut off her own hand than admit she got lost. At her door she paused, one hand on the cold stone frame.

"The groom," she said, not turning around. "When I touched his fear. Before it went wrong."

"Yes?"

"It felt like a house burning." Her voice was very quiet. "Just for a second. His fear, in the memory—it felt like smoke, and running, and someone singing. And then it was gone." She laughed, short and hollow. "Memories bleed into each other. That's all. That's what you said. They're not sealed. So it's nothing."

"It's nothing," Bram agreed, though his skin had gone cold, because he had spent his life among memories and he knew the difference between a bleed and an echo, and that had been no bleed. That had been a *door*, somewhere deep in her sealed and guarded past, rattling on its hinges for the first time in years.

"Good," said Theo. "Goodnight, Bram."

The door closed. He stood in the corridor a long while, the lullaby still humming in his own chest, four notes rising and falling, and he thought about the burning house, and the singing, and the way she'd said *someone singing* like a person naming a wound they didn't know they had.

Then he went to find Sera, because some things were too large to carry alone—and because tomorrow, Sera had said, they would need to begin preparing the road to the capital, and there was a relic of the old king's coronation in the deep vault that Sera wanted the thief to touch.

Bram only hoped she'd be ready for what touched her back.
