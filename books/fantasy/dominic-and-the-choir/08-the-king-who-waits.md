## 8. The King Who Waits

The wagon smelled of pickled fish and old canvas, and Theo hated it with a completeness that felt almost restful.

Three days now she had lain beneath the false floor of a salt merchant's cart, wedged between barrels and burlap sacks that wept brine into her hair whenever the road dipped wrong. Above her, Sera Vane played the part of the merchant's widowed sister—grey-robed no longer, but dressed in a trader's drab wool—and traded pleasantries with checkpoint guards in a voice Theo barely recognized as hers. It was warm under there, in a suffocating way. The Choir's singers had packed the space around her with something they called hushmoss, grey-green and damp, which muffled not sound exactly but *notice*, so that guards' eyes slid off the cart like rain off oiled leather.

"Thief," Bram had whispered to her before they sealed her in, pressing a strip of dried apple into her hand like it was sacrament, "if you suffocate, try to do it quietly. We're on a schedule."

She had decided, somewhere in the second day of brine and darkness, that she would remember that joke when she killed him.

Now the cart lurched over stones, and Theo pressed her palm flat against the underside of the false floor and listened. Wheels on cobbles. A gate groaning. Voices—Sera's voice, bright and grieving in all the right places, telling some story about her dead husband that was almost certainly stolen from someone real. A guard laughed. Papers rustled. Then wheels again, slower, and the particular quality of silence that meant walls on both sides.

The capital. Or close enough to smell it.

* * *

They let her out after dark, in the cellar of what appeared from the outside to be an ordinary dyer's shop, though the vats were dry and had been for years. The Choir's waystation. There were eleven of them gathered when she climbed up out of the cart on legs that had forgotten their own length, and every one of them looked at her the same way: carefully. As if she were a lamp carried through wind.

"You look terrible," Bram said. He was nineteen and had a cowlick he fought daily and lost, and he handed her a bowl of barley stew as if offering it to a stray dog that might bite.

"I've been marinating in fish," Theo said, "for three days."

"It suits you." But his eyes went past her shoulder, quick, checking the stairway, and she understood: even here, even underground, they were afraid. She had seen that look before, in Thornfell, on smugglers who'd crossed the guilds. It was the look of people who knew exactly how far the reach of the thing hunting them extended.

Sera Vane stood apart, talking low with two older singers—a stooped man called Ondrel whose voice, they said, could unmake a memory entirely, and a broad woman named Kess who never spoke at all and communicated by tapping rhythms on things. When Sera finished and turned, Theo saw the map spread on the trestle table behind her: the capital, inked in careful lines, and one street marked with a small circle of red.

"The Moonturn festival begins in six days," Sera said. "The whole city will be drunk and singing and looking up instead of ahead. That's our door." Her finger moved to the circled street. "But first we need you ready, and we need you to understand what you're walking toward. Not the Regent. Not yet. Him. Aldric."

"The hollow king," Theo said. She'd heard the phrase a dozen times since the gorge, always said the same way, the way you'd say the name of a storm or a plague.

"The man," Sera corrected, softly, and something in the softness made Theo go still. "Everyone forgets there was a man. Come here. I want to show you something—but touch nothing until I tell you."

* * *

What Sera showed her was kept in a lead-lined chest behind a wall panel that opened at a sung note too low for Theo to hear. Inside, wrapped in cloth gone soft with age, lay objects that made Theo's thief's hands itch before her mind even understood why.

A ring of plain silver, worn thin at the band. A folded letter, sealed with wax stamped by a sigil she didn't recognize. A scrap of embroidered linen, the stitching clumsy, a child's work. And a goblet—no, not a goblet, a chalice, dented and unremarkable, its rim darkened where a hundred mouths had drunk.

"The coronation chalice," Sera said. "Or so Ondrel swears, and Ondrel has never once been wrong about an object. Twenty-two years ago, wine was poured from this for a young king who had just sworn an oath on the high altar." She paused. "Objects hold echoes. You know this—you must. Things that pass through many hands grow heavy with them. Singers can read them, the way you might read a letter left in a book. Most objects only murmur. This one..."

"This one sang," Theo said quietly.

Sera looked at her then, really looked, and Theo realized she'd said something revealing without meaning to. Of course she knew objects held echoes. Half her trade depended on it—a wedding ring lifted from a nightstand told you more about a marriage than either spouse would say aloud. She had simply never thought of it as singing.

"I want you to touch it," Sera said. "Not me. Not any of us. We've all read it already, and what we carry colors what it shows. You're the one the prophecy chose—or the one it made room for; we don't know which, and I won't pretend otherwise. But if you're going to walk into that palace and into his mind, I want you to have met him. The real one. Before the crown."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you refuse, and we find another way, and probably die of it." Sera's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You're not obligated, Theo. I need you to understand that too. Whatever the prophecy says. Obligation is how the Regent feeds—it takes people who feel they have no choice and eats them down to the choosing."

Theo stared at the chalice in its wrappings. Around her the other singers had gone quiet, watching, and Bram's face wore an expression she couldn't parse—something between warning and plea.

Her hands had been steady for fifteen years of theft. They were not steady now.

She reached out and touched the rim.

* * *

The world went white, then gold.

Not the gold of coin—she knew that gold intimately, had counted it in the dark a thousand times—but the gold of late sun through honey-colored glass, falling across stone floors worn smooth by centuries of feet. Incense. Cold air and candleflame. And singing—not the Choir's harmonized weaving but one clear voice rising alone through a vast space, a boy's voice cracking on the high notes and refusing to be ashamed of it.

*Aldric,* she understood, though no one told her. *This is Aldric, twelve years old, singing in the chapel where he is not supposed to be.*

He was caught, of course. An old priest found him mid-verse and Theo felt the boy's mortification bloom hot and specific—the fear of punishment braided with the stubborn refusal to stop singing, because the song was *for* something, the song mattered more than the trouble it cost. She felt him take the scolding with his chin up. She felt him run afterward, laughing, through cloisters, and feel the sun on his face and think, with the enormous casual certainty of a child who has never doubted his place in the world: *I will be good at this. Whatever they ask me to be, I will be good at it.*

Then years, sliding like cards. The same certainty aging with him, hardening. Aldric at twenty, learning law until midnight by candlelight and falling asleep over his books. Aldric promising a frightened ambassador that yes, the border villages would be fed, and meaning it so completely that the promise became a kind of architecture, something built and load-bearing. Aldric dancing badly at his own betrothal feast and not caring, because the woman laughing at his clumsiness had eyes that—

That. Theo flinched inside the echo. There was grief in it already, layered under the joy, the way amber holds an insect. *Elenna.* The name arrived with a smell—rosemary, bread baking—and then the memory turned, gently, terribly, the way memories do, toward a sickroom and a hand growing light in his, and Theo wanted to pull away and could not, because the echo did not care what she wanted.

She surfaced once, gasping, and heard Sera's voice saying her name, and plunged again—because the chalice was not done, because the echo had saved its center for last, and the center was the coronation itself.

Him, kneeling. Twenty-six years old and terrified, genuinely terrified, not of the weight of the crown but of his own unworthiness, praying silently—*let me be enough, let me be enough*—while the archbishop lifted the circlet that had crowned nine kings before him. The moment of contact. The cold weight settling onto his brow.

And then—

Theo had broken into minds before. She had stood inside strangers' most guarded rooms and taken what she came for and left, and she had told herself for years that this made her a connoisseur of the human interior, that nothing in another person could surprise her anymore.

Nothing had prepared her for the thing that entered Aldric's mind at the moment of crowning, because it did not enter like a thief enters. It entered like winter enters a house. Slowly at first, politely, finding the gaps under doors. And Aldric felt it arrive and did not know what it was—he thought it was awe, thought the strange spreading numbness was the gravity of the oath—and by the time he understood that something else had come in with the crown, that something was already curling around the warm rooms of himself, learning them, tasting them, deciding where to begin—

It began, Theo saw with a clarity that would live in her forever, with the rosemary smell. With Elenna. The Regent ate the grief first. Grief, it turned out, was the tenderest meat.

* * *

She came back to herself on the cellar floor with four singers kneeling around her and Bram holding her head off the stones, and her first coherent act was to grab his wrist hard enough to leave marks, because the alternative was screaming.

"She read the whole echo," someone was saying, awed. "In one touch. Sera, she read the whole—"

"Give her air." Sera's face swam above her, pale. "Theo. Breathe. You're back. You're here."

Here. The cellar. The brine-smell of her own clothes. Bram's hand warm against her cheek, steadier than she'd ever felt anything.

"What year is it?" she rasped, because her mouth needed to say something ordinary.

"Yours is the right question to ask," Ondrel said gravely, from somewhere behind the others. "Most singers come back asking who they are."

Who indeed. Theo lay still and took inventory, the way she did after every job: her name, hers. Her mother's face—wait. No. That wasn't hers to inventory; that door stayed shut, that door had *always* stayed shut. Fine. Her trade, her skills, Thornfell's wet streets, Vess's face with its smile she had trusted for ten years. All present. All hers.

And now, uninvited, permanent: the weight of a silver crown settling on a young man's brow. *Let me be enough.*

She sat up slowly. Everyone gave her room, which she appreciated, and also watched her, which she did not.

"How much did I miss?"

"An hour," Sera said. "And you missed nothing. You were given something. Whether you wanted it or not—that's the cruelty of echoes." She crouched, brought her eyes level with Theo's. "Tell me what you saw. Only what you're willing to say."

What was she willing to say? That she had spent an hour inside a stranger and come out grieving for his wife, whom she'd never met? That she had felt a king pray to be enough and understood—with the awful intimacy of a thief who has picked every lock in a house—that he had never once stopped praying it, that the prayer had become the last lit window in a burning building?

"He was kind," Theo said finally. It sounded absurd. She said it anyway. "Before all this. He was actually kind. He learned law at night because he was afraid of failing people he'd never meet." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I steal from merchants and widows and I have never once, not one time, worried about failing anyone. And this man—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Why does everyone call him the hollow king like he's already gone? There's someone still in there. He's been holding a door closed for twenty-two years with his bare hands."

No one answered immediately. In the silence Theo heard the drip of the cellar's condensation, and Bram breathing beside her, and her own heart doing something complicated.

"Because hope," Sera said at last, very quietly, "is easier to survive without. We tried to save him once, Theo. Eight years ago. Three of us entered his mind, and we were good—we were the best the Choir had. And the Regent let us see exactly what you just saw. The man. The kindness. And then it showed us what it would take." Her hands, resting on her knees, had gone white-knuckled. "It doesn't just guard him. It prices him. And the price is always the same: everything the rescuer loves. One of my singers came out of that palace with no memory of her children. She looked at them like weather. She's still alive, in a hospice in the eastern hills, and her daughters visit her every month, and she greets them like polite strangers." Sera lifted her chin, and her eyes were dry in the way of eyes that had cried themselves out long ago. "So yes. Hollow king. It keeps us safe from wanting to go back."

Theo sat with that. Around her, the singers pretended to busy themselves—banking lamps, stacking bowls—and let her have her silence, and she noticed them noticing, and noticed that being noticed didn't sting the way it usually did.

"The price," she said slowly. "Everything the rescuer loves."

"Yes."

"For him." She looked at her own hands, thief's hands, scarred at the knuckles, hands that had taken hundreds of memories and kept none. "And what does he love? After twenty-two years of being eaten? What's left in him that costs anything?"

Sera's silence stretched a beat too long.

"That," she said finally, "is the question we've been afraid to answer. And I think you already have."

* * *

Later—much later, when the cellar lamps had burned to coals and the singers slept in their rows of bedrolls—Theo lay awake between hushmoss and stone, and the echo replayed behind her eyelids whether she willed it or not.

The boy singing in the chapel. The young king dancing badly. The prayer. *Let me be enough.*

And threaded through it, fainter than all the rest, almost lost: a fragment of the echo she hadn't understood in the moment, surfacing now in the dark with terrible patience. At the very edge of the coronation memory, just before the crown came down, Aldric had glanced up—past the archbishop, past the incense smoke—to the gallery where the common folk were permitted to watch. And there had been a face. Unimportant to him, a stranger's face, a girl with dark hair and watchful eyes who had caught him looking and hadn't smiled.

Theo had known that face her whole life.

She saw it every morning, in polished metal, in still water, in the rare honest mirror.

She sat up in the darkness, heart slamming, and did not wake anyone, and did not sleep again, because the prophecy book was three feet away in her pack and she suddenly needed—needed—to look at the sketch again, the sketch she had memorized months ago and told herself she'd imagined the resemblance, the girl drawn beside the crowned figure with a thief's shadow falling from her hands—

The page hadn't changed. Of course it hadn't changed.

But now, by coal-light, with a king's borrowed grief still lodged under her ribs like a swallowed coal, Theo read the prophecy's final line for the hundredth time and heard it new:

*She will give what cannot be bought back, and the throne will remember her, and she will not remember herself.*

Outside, above the sleeping city, the moon was rising—fat, and one turn short of full, and counting.
