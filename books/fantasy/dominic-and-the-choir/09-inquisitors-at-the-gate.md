## 9. Inquisitors at the Gate

Sera Vane had always known the waystation would not hold.

It was built into the ribs of an old watchtower on the Grey Spur, half-swallowed by lichen and snow, its stones humming faintly with the songs laid into them three generations ago. It was meant to be forgotten — that was the whole architecture of it, stone and spell arranged so that eyes slid past, so that maps mislabeled it, so that even the crows forgot to nest there. For sixty years it had kept the Choir's eastern singers safe through winters that killed shepherds in their beds.

But forgetting was no longer a shield. The Regent had learned to eat forgetting, and everything the waystation relied upon was food.

She felt it first as a wrongness in the morning song. The Choir rose before dawn, as they always did, twelve voices in the cold chapel hall, singing the day into shape — small things, homely things: the memory of warmth in the hearthstones, the memory of the path down to the stream, the memory of their own names spoken kindly. Sera stood at the head of the crescent with her hands folded, listening the way a physician listens to a chest, and heard a flatness where there should have been resonance. The tower's hum had gone thin. Like a bell cracked inside.

"Odell," she said quietly, when the verse ended. "Walk the perimeter. Don't sing anything large. Just listen."

Odell Marren was fifty-three years old and had been a singer longer than Sera had been alive. He had a shepherd's face and a shepherd's patience, and he went out into the blue-grey dawn without argument, pulling his grey robe close against the wind. Sera watched him from the arrow-slit window as he made his slow circuit of the walls, pausing at each of the old anchor-stones, laying his palm flat against them the way you'd check a horse for fever.

At the fourth stone he stopped. He stood very still for a long moment. Then he turned toward the tower and looked directly up at her window, and though he was too far away for any expression to survive the distance, Sera felt her stomach drop as if he had shouted.

He didn't come back inside. He began, instead, to sing.

* * *

The alarm-song of the Choir was not loud. That was the horror of it, later — how quiet it was. A rising third, held and held, passed from voice to voice through the stones themselves until every wall of the waystation carried it like a struck tuning fork. Sera heard it in her teeth, in the water in the basin, in the bread rising by the hearth. *They are coming. They are coming now.*

She moved without deciding to move. Years of hiding had carved these corridors into her body: the chapel, the dormitory, the archive below, the hidden stair behind the shelf of coronation relics that let out into the scree field on the mountain's blind side. "Bram!" she called, and the boy came skidding around the corner with his boots unlaced and terror already blooming on his face, because everyone in the tower had heard the song and everyone knew what it meant, even if they had never once heard it sung in earnest.

"Get the girl," Sera said. "Not the front way. The reliquary stair. Take her down to the ice cave and wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For me. Or don't wait. If I'm not there by moonrise, take her west to the Hollow Chapel and tell them Sera sent you." She gripped his shoulder hard enough to hurt, and saw him understand that this was not a drill, that the drills were over. "Bram. Whatever happens — she is the prophecy. Not the book. Not the words. *Her.* Keep her breathing."

He went. Sera turned back down the corridor toward the chapel, toward the archive, toward the things that could not be allowed to burn, and that was when she heard the horses.

Not many. Six, perhaps seven, moving at a canter up the switchback road with the muffled discipline of soldiers who have stopped pretending to be pilgrims. And over the crunch of hooves on frozen gravel, a sound that made Sera's blood go to ice water: someone below was singing counterpoint to Odell's alarm, a smooth, practiced descant that unwove his melody note by note, the way a smuggler picks a lock.

Sera knew only one person alive who could unmake a Choir alarm alone.

* * *

They came through the gate without breaking it, which was somehow worse than if they had. The waystation's outer door simply opened, because the door had been sung to forget how to be locked, and the men who stepped through wore the plain grey travel clothes of crown inquisitors — no livery, no sigil, nothing so honest as a uniform. Their faces were the worst part. Sera had seen inquisitors before, in another life, in the years after the failed rescue; they were usually either zealots with burning eyes or bureaucrats with ledgers. These had neither. They had the pleasant, unfocused blankness of people whose attention had been borrowed by something else, and they fanned out across the courtyard with the unhurried competence of ants.

And at their center, riding a fine dark mare with a crown-inquisitor's satchel across her saddle, sat Vess.

Sera had last seen her eleven months ago, in a rented room above a dye-house in Thornfell's lower city, when Vess had still been Theo Blackwood's handler and Sera had still believed the woman could be bought. She remembered the negotiation clearly: Vess leaning back in a chair that wasn't hers, turning a stolen signet ring over her fingers, naming a price for delivering the girl to the Choir that was insultingly precise. *You're not buying my loyalty,* she'd said. *I don't have any. You're buying a delivery. There's a difference, and smart people respect it.*

Now Vess swung down from the mare in the courtyard of the waystation and looked up at the tower Sera had defended for twenty years, and her face did something complicated — something almost like grief — before it settled into business.

"Sera Vane," she called, in the carrying voice of someone who has done this before. "Come down. Bring the book and bring the girl, and nobody else has to remember today."

*Remember.* Sera's hands closed slowly into fists. So it was true, then — the thing the scattered singers had whispered since autumn, the rumor she had refused to believe because believing it meant the war had already started. The inquisitors weren't erasing witnesses anymore. They were harvesting. Every person they took, they took twice: once in body, once in memory, and the memories went somewhere. Fed somewhere.

She stayed in the shadow of the arrow-slit and sang instead — a small working, a thread of sound pushed down through the stones to find Odell. *Where? How many? Are you hurt?*

His answer came back frayed. *Gate road. Seven riders but eight hearts — there's something with them that doesn't breathe. I'm in the bell-cote. Sera, the anchor-stones are dead. All four. They've been dead for days. Someone fed them.*

Someone had hollowed out the waystation's defenses while they slept, patiently, invisibly, the way rot works through a beam. Which meant this was not a pursuit that had caught up with them. This was a trap that had been set before Theo Blackwood ever arrived — before the prophecy book, before the mountains, possibly before Sera herself had gone looking for the girl in Thornfell.

The Regent had known. The Regent had always known.

"Last chance, Vane." Vess's voice again, closer now; she had dismounted and crossed to the chapel door, and Sera could see her through the slit: the same lean frame, the same ink-stained fingers, a new scar splitting one eyebrow. "I've read the file on you. Twenty years building a sanctuary for children who sing other people's lives. It's almost admirable. But the moon turns in nine days, and what's wearing the king is going to finish eating regardless. The only question left is whether it eats politely, through us, or whether it comes down off the throne and does it the old way. I'm offering you the polite version."

"You're offering it a meal," Sera said, stepping out of the stairwell into the chapel's antechamber, because there was no more use in hiding. "A very long, very organized meal. How many villages, Vess? How many names in your ledger?"

Vess looked at her across the empty nave, and for a moment — just a moment — the blankness cracked and Sera saw the woman underneath, tired and certain and genuinely convinced of her own mercy. That was the most frightening thing about her. Zealots could be argued with. Bureaucrats could be bought. But Vess believed she was saving people.

"The ones we harvest forget nothing," Vess said softly. "That's the difference between us and the Regent, whatever your order thinks. We keep everything. Every memory we take goes into the vaults, catalogued, preserved. When this is over — if it ends well — they all go home to their owners. No one is erased. Things are only... held in trust." She spread her hands. "Your Choir sings memories loose into the air where anything can eat them. We put them somewhere safe. Tell me honestly, Sera. Which of us is the thief?"

"*I* am," said a voice from the gallery above.

Theo Blackwood stood at the railing with Bram's hand locked white-knuckled around her wrist, having ignored every instruction Sera had given, and her face was the face from the century-old woodcut — drawn, furious, lit from within by something that was not quite hope and not quite despair. "I'm the thief. I've stolen hundreds of memories and sold every one, and I never told myself it was kindness. At least I know what I am." Her voice shook. "What did they give you, Vess? What did they promise to give *back*?"

Vess's composure flickered — visibly, painfully — and Sera understood, with a lurch, that the girl had found the wound in under ten seconds where Sera's twenty years of statecraft had missed it entirely. There was something Vess wanted returned. Something the vaults held. Something she had sold her soul on credit to reclaim.

"That's not your concern," Vess said, too quickly.

"It's exactly my concern. It's the only currency I trade in." Theo leaned over the rail. "You want me to walk out that door with you. Ask yourself why they need *me* delivered, Vess. Ask why a thing that eats kingdoms needs one specific thief brought to it alive. Because I read the book too. And I don't think I'm the prize. I think I'm the *plate* they're serving the feast on."

For three heartbeats, Sera thought it might work. She watched doubt move through Vess like weather, watched the inquisitor's hand drift toward her satchel and stop, watched seven blank faces in the courtyard turn in perfect unison toward the gallery —

— and then the eighth heart arrived.

It came up the gate road on foot, and it did not breathe. It was tall and wrong, wrapped in a cloak the color of unwritten paper, and where its face should have been there was a smoothness like the inside of a mask, and the cold that came ahead of it was not mountain cold. The horses screamed and broke their lines. The blank-eyed inquisitors did not react at all, which told Sera everything: they were not Vess's soldiers. They were its.

Vess went pale. "That's not — it wasn't supposed to *come itself*, the agreement was—"

"The agreement," said the thing, in a voice like pages turning, "was that you would open the door. You have opened the door."

* * *

Afterward, Sera would remember the next minutes as sound rather than sight.

Odell came down out of the bell-cote singing, and she would remember that forever — a man of fifty-three with a shepherd's hands and no weapon but his voice, throwing himself between the faceless thing and the tower stairs, pouring out the oldest song the Choir knew, the Lament for the Unforgotten, the one they sang only for their own dead. The stones of the courtyard rang with it. Snow shook from the eaves. For a few shining seconds the thing in the paper-colored cloak actually stopped, its smooth face tilting, as if tasting something it had not been fed in a very long time: a memory offered freely, loudly, refusing to be taken quietly.

"ODELL!" Sera screamed, and threw herself down the stairs, and knew even as she ran that she was too far, that she had always been too far, that the whole architecture of her life — the hiding, the caution, the careful distance she kept even from her own singers — had been built precisely so that she would always be too far when the bill came due.

Odell's song climbed. The thing reached out with a hand that was not quite a hand.

And the memory of Odell Marren — his mother's kitchen, his first lamb born in a storm, forty years of other people's lives carried gently in a shepherd's heart — went up out of him like a flock of birds startled from a field, all at once, visible for an instant in the freezing air as a shimmer of light and sound, and the thing drank it, and Odell Marren folded to the courtyard stones like a coat dropped from a hook.

He did not die screaming. He died mid-verse, on the high note of the Lament, and the note hung in the courtyard after he fell, sustained by the stones he had spent his life tending, until the faceless thing walked past his body without a glance and the song finally, horribly, ended.

* * *

They escaped because Vess let them.

Sera understood that later, replaying it, hating herself for understanding it. In the chaos — the inquisitors suddenly purposeless without their master's direction, the horses bolting, fire climbing the archive where a lantern had fallen among the coronation relics — Vess had stood in the smoke with her satchel open and her face wet, and she had looked directly at Sera across the burning nave and mouthed two words. Not *run*. Not *surrender*.

*I'm sorry.*

Then the paper-cloaked thing had turned toward the gallery where Theo and Bram crouched, and Vess had shouted an order — *the girl unharmed, the agreement stands, UNHARMED* — and in the seconds that bought, Sera reached them, shoved them through the reliquary stair, and sealed it behind them with the last strong song she had in her body, collapsing the passage in a roar of stone.

They came out into the scree field under a sky going white with afternoon, three of them where thirty-six hours before there had been thirteen. Behind them the waystation burned, sixty years of sanctuary, the archive, the relics, the dormitory with its carved initials, all of it going up in a column of black smoke that the whole valley would see. Somewhere in it, she knew, inquisitors were moving through the rooms with vials and ledgers, gathering up whatever memories the fire spared.

Bram was crying without noise, the way choirboys learn to. Theo stood apart, staring at the smoke, and Sera saw her hands — the thief's hands, quick and steady through every job, every escape — shaking like a novice's.

"He knew my name," Theo said. "The thing in the cloak. Before it — when it looked at the gallery. It said *there she is*." Her voice cracked. "Sera. It wasn't hunting us. It was *collecting* me. This whole time. The book, the guild selling me out, the road that led here —"

"Yes," Sera said. Because the girl deserved truth now; lies had cost enough for one day. "I think it wrote the prophecy. I think it has been writing it for a hundred years. And I think we just played our part exactly as composed."

Theo laughed — one broken, terrible note of it — and then pressed both hands over her mouth.

Sera turned back to the burning tower. Grief was a luxury; she gave herself exactly ten seconds of it, kneeling in the snow, forehead nearly touching the frozen ground, singing the first line of the Lament for Odell into the wind where no one living would hear it. Then she stood, and wiped her face, and became what the Choir needed her to be.

"Nine days," she said. "The moon completes its thirteenth turn in nine days. It has its trap half-sprung and it thinks we're running." She looked at Theo, and then at Bram, and felt something settle in her chest that was colder and harder than grief and would carry her further. "So we won't run. We'll go to the capital. We'll go straight down its throat. And we will spend those nine days learning everything it never imagined a thief could teach us — how to take something back."

Far below, the waystation's roof beam gave way in a gout of sparks, and the bell — Odell's bell, silent for a century — rang once, unbroken, as the flames finally reached it.

It sounded, Sera thought, almost like an answer.
