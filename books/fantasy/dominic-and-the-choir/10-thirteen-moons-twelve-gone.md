## 10. Thirteen Moons, Twelve Gone

The moon over the Choir's sanctuary had grown fat again, and everyone counted it like a debt.

Theo counted it too, though she pretended not to. She had learned the rhythm of the place in the weeks since the ice: bells at dawn, bread at midmorning, the long grey hours of practice where singers stood in circles and hummed themselves into other people's pasts. She had learned which floorboards creaked in the dormitory, which of the old women snored, which of the novices cried at night and pretended in the morning that they hadn't. She had learned that Bram left the last of the honey-bread on her plate without ever saying why.

And she had learned that the moon was a clock, and that it was running out.

Twelve turns gone. One remaining. She heard it in the way Sera Vane spoke now—shorter sentences, longer silences. She heard it in the harmonies that drifted up from the undercroft at night, rehearsals for something none of them would name in her presence. The thirteenth turn. The turn when, according to a book a century dead, the Regent would finish eating the king and begin on everything else.

Tonight they would rehearse her.

"The rite is called the Threshold," Sera said, standing in the center of the singing chamber with her hands folded, as if she were delivering a lecture and not preparing to crack open a stranger's skull. The chamber was a hollowed cavern beneath the sanctuary, its walls veined with pale stone that caught the lamplight and gave it back soft. "You will not enter a true mind tonight. You will enter a vessel—one of ours, holding a prepared memory. Think of it as a room built for you to practice in. Nothing in it can hurt you. Nothing in it is real."

"And if I refuse?" Theo asked, because she always asked.

"Then we find another way," Sera said, "and we all die slower."

Bram, seated cross-legged near the brazier, snorted. "That's the version for the brochure."

"Bram."

"I'm just saying. Honesty is a kind of kindness." He was nineteen and had a singer's loose way of sitting, like his bones had been tuned to a chord. He'd been assigned as her practice partner weeks ago, and he was the only person in the sanctuary who looked at her like she was a person learning a skill rather than a prophecy learning to walk. "Theo. It's safe. I've done the Threshold four times. The worst that happens is you come out crying about someone's dead dog."

"That happened once," said an older singer named Maren, without looking up from tuning her small silver bell.

"Once," Bram agreed solemnly.

Theo looked at the vessel.

It stood at the chamber's center: a basin of black water the size of a wash-tub, ringed with runes that had been freshly cut and packed with silver dust. A memory had been poured into it—she could feel it from across the room, the way she could always feel a strong memory, a warmth behind the sternum, an itch in the teeth. Old habit. Old hunger. She was a thief standing in a vault being told the locks were decorative.

"Whose memory is in it?" she asked.

"Mine," said Maren. "A market day, forty years ago. Nothing tender. Nothing that matters." But her hands were very still on her bell, and Theo, who had spent her life reading the small betrayals of people's bodies, marked it and said nothing. Everyone in this place had something they kept behind glass. She recognized the posture.

They arranged themselves in a circle around the basin—seven singers, Sera among them, grey-robed, barefoot on the cold stone. Bram took the place at her left, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers. "When it gets strange," he murmured, "follow my voice. Don't follow anything else. Especially not anything that smells like smoke."

"Why smoke?"

"Because fire is the most common memory in the world," he said, "and because you flinch every time someone strikes flint. I've been watching. It's kind of my job."

She wanted to tell him to mind his own past. She didn't. That was the trouble with Bram; he made silence feel like agreement.

Sera raised her arms. "Theo Blackwood. You are the door tonight. We are only the wind. Sing with us, and go where the song says."

They began.

It was not like anything the guilds had ever taught her. In Thornfell, memory-work was a mugging: you got close, you found the seam, you ripped. It was fast and ugly and it left bruises on both of you. But the Choir sang the way water works on stone—patient, total, everywhere at once. The harmonies braided together and became a corridor, and the corridor became a pressure behind her eyes, and the pressure became—

Falling. But gently. Like sinking into a feather bed that someone else had already warmed.

She opened her eyes inside Maren's market day.

It was overwhelming in the way only the past could be. The light was wrong—too golden, too generous, light the way a child remembers light. The stalls were hung with oranges and dyed wool, and the noise was enormous and specific: a haggling woman, a knife-sharpener's wheel, someone's baby laughing at absolutely nothing. The smells arrived in layers, bread and horse and tallow and under it all a green smell, cut stems, flowers someone had carried through in armfuls.

Forty years ago. Nothing tender. Nothing that matters.

Theo walked the market the way she'd been taught in the first hour of her training: hands in, eyes moving, cataloguing the exits. Except there were no exits. This was a room built of one afternoon, and every direction ended in the same warm gold.

"Find the seam," Sera's voice said—not in the market, but under it, everywhere, like the singing itself had learned to speak. "Every memory has a seam. The moment the keeper stopped paying attention. That's where a thief enters."

Theo found it. It was absurdly easy—the moment Maren had looked away from her purse toward a boy selling ribbons, a half-second of inattention preserved in amber for forty years. Theo slid her attention into that gap the way she'd slid a hundred lockpicks into a hundred doors, and felt the whole market shiver and open, felt herself become, for one vertiginous instant, a woman named Maren holding three coppers and wanting a ribbon the color of new leaves—

She pulled back. Hard. That was the rule: rehearse, don't take. She was a guest here.

The market dimmed at the edges like a candle guttering. Good. She'd done it. She'd entered a mind that wasn't hers and come out clean, and somewhere above the sanctuary the moon could tick however it liked, because she would be ready when—

The smoke reached her.

It shouldn't have been there. It was a market day in high summer, forty years old, and there was no fire in it anywhere. But the smell arrived anyway, threading through the bread-smell and the horse-smell, acrid and intimate and wrong, and Theo knew it the way you know your own name. Not smoke in general. This smoke. Pitch and thatch and burning linen and underneath it something sweeter, something she had spent twenty years not remembering, and the market's golden light bent toward it like iron toward a lodestone—

"Follow my voice," Bram said, very calm, very close. "Theo. Follow my voice."

But the smoke had a voice too.

It was a woman's voice, and it was singing.

Not words she could hold. The melody slipped through her grasp like water through fingers, but her body knew it—her throat ached with the shape of it, her hands remembered the motion of keeping time, small hands, a child's hands, clapping along on a floor that was warm from the stove. The market was tearing now, peeling back in strips like old paint, and behind it was a house. A kitchen. A woman with dark hair coming loose from its braid, standing at a stove, singing with her back half-turned, and the light through the window was the same impossible gold as the market because memory didn't care about accuracy, it cared about love—

"Theo." Bram's voice, sharper now. "That's not in the vessel. That's not Maren's. Come back."

The woman turned.

Theo had spent her whole life keeping this door shut. She had built her trade on the principle that a memory taken was a memory gone, that the past was a purse to be emptied and abandoned, that whatever she had been before Thornfell was a debt she'd paid in full the night she'd walked away from a burning house with soot in her lungs and nothing in her hands. She had sealed it so well that even the guilds' best mind-breakers had found nothing but a smooth wall and called her a natural.

The woman turned, and the wall was paper.

Her face was—Theo couldn't see it. The memory wouldn't give her the face, the way dreams refuse the one thing you need, and that refusal was somehow worse than anything, because it meant the wanting was hers, the face was hers to lose, and she was losing it in real time, right now, in front of seven singers and a boy with kind eyes—

"Come back," Bram said, and this time he was not calm. This time his voice had a hook in it, and she felt it catch in her chest like a fisherman's line, and the kitchen tilted, and the smoke thickened, and the woman's song cracked in the middle the way a voice cracks when—

When the beam came down.

Theo came back to her body all at once, on her hands and knees on the cold stone, gasping like a landed fish. The singers had broken the circle; the basin's black water was churning with no hand near it, runes flaring silver and then dark. Someone had knocked over a lamp. The chamber smelled of smoke.

No. It didn't. It smelled of lamp oil, that was all, that was all it had ever—

"Theo." Bram was beside her, on his knees, not touching her, which she understood was a courtesy. "Look at me. You're in the chamber. You're you. Breathe."

"I was—" Her voice came out shredded. "That wasn't in the vessel. You said—Sera said nothing in it was real."

Sera stood at the basin's edge, and her face was doing something complicated. "Nothing was," she said carefully. "The vessel held a market day. What you saw came from you."

"Then it's mine." Theo got her feet under her. Standing took two tries. "It's mine and it's sealed and it's none of your—"

"It was sealed," said Maren quietly. She had not moved from her place in the broken circle. "Child, I have kept memories for fifty years, and I know a seal when I sing against one. Yours was not made by you. It was made *on* you. And tonight, when you opened yourself to enter another mind, it cracked." Her hands were folded on her bell, and they were not still anymore. "Seals like that don't mend. They tear."

The chamber was very quiet. The overturned lamp had gone out. In the dark, the runes around the basin glowed faintly, like embers deciding whether to die.

Theo looked at her own hands. They were shaking, and she hated them for it.

She was a thief. She knew what it meant to want something in a room full of people who could see you wanting it. And she wanted it—gods, she wanted it—the woman's face, the name of the song, the reason the beam came down, whether anyone had come out of that house besides her, whether anyone had *not*. Twenty years of discipline and one hour in a stranger's afternoon had cracked her open like an egg, and the worst of it, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, was that she didn't want the seal back. She wanted to go back under. She wanted to stand in that kitchen until the woman turned around properly, until the song finished, until—

"Out," she said. "All of you. Out."

"Theo—"

"*Out.*"

They went. Even Sera, though she paused at the door, and the look she gave Theo was not pity, which Theo would have known how to hate, but something older and more careful, like a woman looking at a lock she had once failed to pick. Then the door closed, and Theo was alone with a basin of black water and the ghost of smoke.

She knelt at the basin's edge before she decided to. Her reflection looked back at her, wavering, and beneath the reflection the runes were still warm, still faintly lit, still a door.

One touch. That was all it would take. She was the best thief in Thornfell; she could slip into her own sealed past the way she'd slipped into Maren's market, and this time she wouldn't pull back, and the woman would turn around, and—

Her hand stopped a finger's width above the water.

Because Maren's voice was in her ear again, patient as stone: *it was not made by you. It was made on you.*

Someone had sealed her. Someone had looked at a child walking out of a burning house and decided what she would be allowed to remember, and had built the wall so well that twenty years of guild mind-breakers had found nothing. And if someone had built the wall, then someone had the key, and the key was not in a basin of market-day water. The key was somewhere else, in someone's keeping, and every hour she spent clawing at her own mind was an hour the moon spent ticking toward the thirteenth turn, and Sera Vane knew more than she was saying—Sera had looked at her like a lock she'd failed to pick, and Theo had spent her whole life around people who lied, and she knew the weight of a withheld thing when it stood in a doorway wearing a leader's face.

She took her hand back from the water. It was the hardest theft she had ever committed: stealing a thing from herself.

At the door she paused, once, and looked back at the basin, and let herself want it—openly, for one breath, the way you let yourself look at a thing you've decided not to steal. Then she climbed the stairs toward the lamplight of the sanctuary, where Sera Vane was waiting with an expression Theo had seen a hundred times on the faces of people about to be caught.

"We need to talk," Sera said, "about what's really behind your seal."

* * *

They talked in Sera's cell, which was bare as a held breath: a cot, a desk, a shelf of memory-vials racked like wine. Theo catalogued the vials out of habit and made herself stop.

"The rite cracked you," Sera said. "I won't pretend otherwise. And I won't pretend I'm surprised, because I'm not. We've known what you are since the night we pulled you out of the gorge."

"I'm a thief."

"You're a *sealed* thief. There's a difference, and the difference is going to get you killed." Sera sat, and for a moment she looked every one of her years, and more. "Theo. Before you go into the king's mind—before you stand in front of the Regent and offer it anything—it deserves to know what it's standing in front of. And so do you." She drew a breath that seemed to cost her. "The Choir didn't find you by accident. The paths didn't guide you by accident. And the seal on your childhood—"

The bell in the sanctuary above began to ring. Not the dawn bell. Not any bell Theo had learned in her weeks here. It was the alarm, ragged and urgent, and beneath it she could hear shouting, and the smell reaching the undercroft now was not lamp oil and not memory.

It was smoke. Real smoke. And voices—too many voices, with the clipped cadence of soldiers.

Sera was already on her feet, and her face had gone white as her robes.

"Inquisitors," she said. "They've found us."
