## 11. The Price of the Door

The rehearsal had left something behind.

Theo felt it in the days that followed—a thinness behind her eyes, like a window scraped too clean. The Choir called it *resonance*, the lingering hum of having walked through another mind. Bram called it nothing at all; he simply watched her across the refectory with that careful, unguarded kindness of his, sliding the honeyed bread toward her plate without comment when she pushed her own food around in circles.

She was dreaming of fire now. Not every night. But enough.

On the fourth morning after the rite, Sera Vane sent for her.

Not to the teaching hall, where the young singers practiced their scales of recollection—not to the archive with its shelves of bottled years. She was led instead down a stair Theo had never noticed, past a door of black iron banded with silver, into a round chamber carved directly from the mountain's heart. No windows. A single brazier burning blue-white at the center, its light casting everyone's shadow upward onto the vaulted stone, so that they all seemed to hang upside down above their own bodies.

Sera stood waiting beside the flame. Alone. That alone should have warned Theo—the leader of the Choir did nothing alone unless the thing she had to say required no witnesses.

"Sit," Sera said.

"I'll stand."

Something flickered across the older woman's face—weariness, maybe, or respect. "You would."

Theo folded her arms and waited. The cold of the stone seeped up through her boots. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, she could just hear the Choir's morning song beginning, a thread of harmony thin as wire.

"You've been dreaming," Sera said. "Fire. A woman singing."

Theo's arms tightened. "Bram told you."

"Bram told me you wouldn't sleep. The rest I know because I've seen it before." Sera lifted her hands toward the brazier, and the blue-white light bent, gathered, rose—and became an image hanging in the air: a corridor of pale stone, endless, lined with doors. "This is what waits inside King Aldric. This is where we can send you. The singing opens the way; your talent walks it. You've proven you can do both. You are—" a pause, weighted, deliberate— "*magnificent*, Theo Blackwood. Better than any thief we trained in a generation."

"Then say what you're not saying." Theo's voice came out flat. Twenty years in Thornfell's lower city had taught her every cadence of a lie, and this one had the particular texture of a truth being arranged carefully around a wound. "You didn't carve a room out of a mountain to tell me I'm magnificent."

Sera let the image of the corridor dissolve. For a long moment she only looked at the fire.

"The Regent feeds on forgotten things," she said at last. "That is its nature. It cannot take what is freely given—it can only devour what is *held*. What is clung to. What is loved." Her eyes came up, grey and steady and terrible. "When you enter Aldric's mind, it will know you're there within moments. And it will reach for the memories you value most. Your first kiss. Your mother's voice. Whatever small hoard of warmth you've carried through your life without knowing you were carrying it." She drew breath. "It will eat them. All of them. And there is no song we know that can stop it once it begins."

The cold went through Theo then, deeper than the stone. Deeper than the mountain.

"That's the price," she said. "That's the door."

"That is the price of the door."

* * *

For a moment neither of them moved. The brazier hissed. Above them, faint as memory, the morning song climbed through some bright interval and held there, suspended, beautiful, indifferent.

"You knew," Theo said quietly. "From the prophecy. From the first day. You read my face in that book and you knew what it would cost me, and you sang me out of a frozen gorge anyway."

"We saved your life—"

"You *recruited* me." The word cracked out of her like ice splitting. "Every lesson. Every kindness. Bram sharing his bread. Sera Vane telling me I'm magnificent. All of it building toward sending me through a door that eats whatever I love on the other side." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "I've been stolen from professionally for twenty years, Sera. I know a setup when I'm standing in the middle of one."

"It is not a setup. It is a war." Sera's composure held, but her hands had gone still at her sides, too still, the stillness of someone gripping something invisible. "Aldric has reigned forty years and been dead inside them for thirty-five. Every year the Regent grows stronger, and every year more of the kingdom forgets—names, griefs, debts of gratitude, the reasons rivers were named and bells were rung. In seven turns of the moon it completes its feeding and takes everything. Not just the king. The *world's* remembering. And the prophecy names exactly one person who can walk into that mind alive." She stepped closer, and the firelight made her face look carved, ancient. "I have spent eleven years looking for you. Eleven years praying the book was wrong about your face. Do you think I wanted this?"

"You wanted a weapon that could feel pain," Theo said. "Congratulations. You found one."

"Theo—"

"No." She backed away a step, and the motion put the brazier between them, its blue-white light shivering across the floor. "Here's what I want to know. When you found me in the gorge—you'd already decided, hadn't you? Before you ever heard me speak. The foretold thief walks into the king's mind, the Regent feeds, the kingdom is saved. One girl's childhood against a nation's memory. Simple arithmetic." Her throat was tight. "Did anyone in this mountain vote on it? Did *I*?"

"There was no one else," Sera said. "There has never been anyone else. If there had been—if any singer alive could survive the crossing—I would have gone myself years ago, and gladly, and you would never have heard of the Choir at all." Her voice roughened, and for the first time since the gorge Theo heard something underneath the authority: guilt, old and worn smooth as river stone. "Thirty years ago we tried. Four of us. The finest singers of our generation. We got as far as the outer corridors before the Regent turned our own songs against us. Two came back hollow. One didn't come back at all. I came back because I ran." She said it plainly, without flinching, offering it like a wound. "I have run from that throne room for thirty years, Theo Blackwood. I will not pretend I'm brave enough to ask you to be braver than I was. But I am honest enough to tell you the truth, which is more than the guilds ever gave you, more than the crown would give you, more than anyone in this world will give you: *you can refuse.*"

"Can I?" Theo's voice dropped low. "With the moon turning? With Vess and her inquisitors hunting these mountains? With the whole weight of your prophecy pressing on my name?" She shook her head slowly. "You gave me a written destiny and then told me I could walk away from it. That's not freedom, Sera. That's a cage with the latch painted on."

* * *

She didn't remember deciding to leave the chamber. She was simply moving, past Sera, toward the iron-banded door, and Sera didn't stop her—only said, quietly, to her back:

"The rehearsal tore loose a fragment of your sealed childhood. A burning house. A woman singing." A pause. "We didn't do that. The Regent's mark was already on those memories. It took them from you long ago, Theo. Everything else you've guarded so carefully—ask yourself why it was so easy to guard. Ask yourself who emptied the room before you built the walls."

Theo stopped with her hand on the iron.

The metal was freezing. It burned worse than heat does, that kind of cold, and she held onto it anyway because holding onto something was better than turning around, because if she turned around Sera would see her face and Theo Blackwood had spent her whole life making sure no one saw her face.

*Emptied the room.*

She thought of the hollow where her childhood should have been. She had always imagined it as a choice—her choice, the first and best theft she'd ever committed, stealing her own past from herself so the guilds could never touch it. She had been proud of that. Proud of the emptiness, the way other people were proud of scars.

But an empty room isn't a locked room. An empty room is just... empty. And she had never once asked herself why the lock had been so easy to build around nothing at all.

"The vial," she said, without turning. "In the archive. The one labeled with my name that Bram swears he doesn't remember cataloguing."

"Yes."

"What's in it?"

Silence. Long enough to be an answer.

Theo pulled open the door and walked out into the corridor, and the morning song above her broke off mid-phrase, as if the mountain itself had drawn breath.

* * *

She went to the archive.

She told herself it was to think—to get warm, to be somewhere the walls weren't carved with Sera's confessions—but her feet knew better, and by the time she admitted it she was already standing in the narrow aisle between the shelved years, lamplight gold on glass, thousands of small bottles glinting like a frozen sea.

The vial sat where it always sat, third shelf, tucked behind a row of unremarkable decades. Small. Dark glass. A curl of silver script around the neck that she still couldn't read, though she'd tried a dozen times, though Bram had squinted at it and gone strangely quiet and changed the subject.

She picked it up.

It was lighter than she remembered. Or her hands were heavier. Inside, something shifted—a slow swirl of color, deep red shot through with gold, like embers suspended in oil. Like a house burning, seen from very far away, from very long ago.

*A woman singing.*

Her thumb moved over the glass. The ember-light seemed to lean toward her touch, the way a cat leans toward a hand it knows.

And Theo understood, with the terrible clarity that comes only at the exact moment a thing can no longer be undone, that Sera hadn't shown her the vial to tempt her. Sera had hidden it here—in plain sight, in the one place Theo was drawn to anyway—because Sera Vane was honest in the way that knives are honest, because she believed Theo deserved to see the shape of what might be demanded of her, all of it, every piece, before she chose.

Or because Sera believed she wouldn't choose at all until she'd held it.

Either way, it worked.

Theo stood in the gold lamplight with her childhood cupped in one hand, and she hated it—hated how badly she wanted it, hated that wanting it made Sera's arithmetic right, hated most of all that some traitorous part of her had already begun calculating the exchange. *One childhood, freely given, against a kingdom's remembering. One girl against the world's forgetting.* The scales balanced so neatly. They had been built to balance neatly. That was the cruelest thing about prophecies—they didn't predict the future, they *priced* it.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty aisle, to the vial, to the hundred feet of stone between her and the singing. Her voice sounded small and it echoed anyway. "No. You don't get to decide what I'm worth."

She slipped the vial inside her coat, against her heart, where the warmth of her body made the ember-color stir and glow like something waking.

Then she walked out of the archive, down the corridor, past the teaching hall where Bram's voice rose sweetly over a child's fumbled scale—he glanced up as she passed, and smiled, and she couldn't smile back, couldn't even slow down, because if she slowed she would stop, and if she stopped—

The outer door was never guarded. That was the Choir's pride: no locks, no wards, only the mountain's mercy and the snow. Theo pushed it open and the cold hit her like a wall, and beyond the wall the world lay white and silent under a sky the color of an old scar.

Behind her, faint through the closing door, someone began calling her name. Bram's voice. Then Sera's.

Theo pulled her collar up, fixed her eyes on the tree line, and walked out into the snow alone—carrying, pressed against her heart, the only thing in the world she had ever truly owned, and no plan at all for keeping it.
