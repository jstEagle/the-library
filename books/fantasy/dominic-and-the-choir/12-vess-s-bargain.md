## 12. Vess's Bargain

Vess had always believed that the hardest part of any capture was the waiting, and she had been wrong. The hardest part, she discovered in the third hour of sitting across from Theo Blackwood in the interrogation cell beneath the waystation's ruins, was watching someone you loved refuse to look at you.

The cell had not been built for cruelty. That was one of the small ironies Vess catalogued to keep her hands steady. The inquisitors' waystations were converted granaries, mostly—cool stone, low ceilings, good acoustics—and this one still smelled faintly of old wheat and lamp oil. A table. Two chairs. A brazier burning clean and smokeless. They did not need chains here, because there was nowhere to run and because the ones they brought here rarely wanted to leave by the end.

Theo sat with her wrists bound in front of her—not tightly; Vess had given the order herself—and stared at the wall above Vess's shoulder as if it might open into a passageway back to the Choir, back to Sera Vane and her grey-robed singers and their songs about moons and kings.

"You could at least be angry at me properly," Vess said. "You're doing it wrong. You're doing the thing where you pretend I'm furniture."

"I've stolen memories from furniture," Theo said. "Furniture is more honest."

There she was. Vess felt something in her chest loosen painfully at the sound of that voice—dry, flat, guarded as a strongbox—even though everything in her wanted to weep. Nine years she had pulled this girl out of gutters and guild wars and the beds of people who meant her harm. Nine years of teaching her which doors opened inward, which merchants counted their coin twice, which streets flooded on the spring tides. Nine years of never once asking what had been taken from her, because Vess understood better than anyone alive that a thief's sealed past was the only thing she truly owned.

And now here they sat, and Vess was about to do the cruelest kind thing she had ever done.

* * *

It had started, three days ago, with the ledger.

Vess kept ledgers. It was the first thing she had taught Theo—that memory-work without records was suicide, that you wrote down every extraction in cipher, dated, cross-referenced, because a thief who couldn't account for her takings would one day take something that got her killed. The ledgers lived in a lacquered box under the floor of her rooms in Thornfell, and when the crown's summons had come—when the inquisitors had arrived at the guildhouse with their grey warrants and their questions about a prophecy book—Vess had burned them all before answering the door.

She had expected torture. She had expected the rack, the needle, the slow questions under lamplight. What she had gotten instead was tea.

The man who received her in the inquisitors' temporary quarters wore no mask and carried no instruments. He was middle-aged, soft-handed, with the pleasant forgettable face of a customs clerk, and he poured the tea himself and asked after her health. Then he said, "You've raised the girl since she was eleven," and Vess understood, with the cold sliding sensation of a trap closing, that he knew things about Theo Blackwood that even Vess did not know.

"We serve the king," he told her. "I want to say that plainly, because you'll have heard stories. We serve the king. The king *is* the realm. And the king has been—" he turned his cup slowly on its saucer, "—unwell for a very long time. There is a thing inside him that eats. Do you understand me, Mistress Vess? Not a disease. An appetite. It eats forgetting. Every memory a person loses—every name that slips away, every face that fades, every childhood that goes dark—it gathers those leavings like a beggar gathering crumbs. And it grows."

"And you harvest for it." She had said it flatly, testing him.

He had smiled, unoffended. "We *rescue*. There's a difference, though I grant it's a fine one. The memories people lose are not gone, Mistress Vess. Nothing is ever truly gone. They fall through the world like coins through a grate, and most of them land in its mouth. We simply get there first. We keep them safe." He had produced, then, a small glass vial from his coat, amber liquid swirling with motes of light too fine to be dust, and set it on the table between them. "Do you know what this is?"

She hadn't. But she had known, in the marrow of her, whose it was.

"The girl keeps nothing of her own," the clerk had said gently. "That's unusual, you know. Most thieves hoard. They steal little pieces of other lives and wear them like borrowed coats. Your Theo takes and takes and keeps none of it—because somewhere, long ago, someone taught her that keeping things gets them taken away. Someone taught her that lesson so thoroughly that she sealed herself shut." He slid the vial an inch toward Vess. "This fell through the grate twenty years ago, from a house fire in the river districts. We've kept it safe ever since. It's hers. It's the beginning of her. And we are prepared to return it—the whole of it, every year we hold, restored entire—in exchange for the only thing standing between Theo Blackwood and her own destruction."

"Which is?"

"The Choir." His voice had softened further, into something almost tender, and that tenderness had frightened Vess more than any threat could have. "They mean to put her inside the king's mind. They mean to open her like a door. And the thing inside him will eat everything she loves out of her while she stands there smiling, thinking she's being brave. We can prevent that. You can prevent that. Bring her to us. Give her back her childhood. Save her life."

He had paused at the door on his way out, and added, without turning: "For what it's worth, she'll hate you. For a while. But hatred fades, Mistress Vess. Memory doesn't come back on its own. Never once, in all our years, has anything come back on its own."

* * *

Now the tea was cold between them, untouched, and Theo was still doing the furniture thing.

"They told me where to find you," Vess said. "The gorge. The ice door. I could have brought forty soldiers down on that hollow full of singers and ended all your problems at once, and instead I waited until you went out alone. Ask yourself why."

"Because you wanted me alive. Prophecy's worth more breathing." Theo's voice didn't change at all. "What's my price these days? Or does the crown haggle?"

"The crown isn't paying for you." Vess leaned forward, laid her bound wrists on the table so the girl would see them—see that she wasn't held, wasn't coerced, that whatever happened next came from Vess's own free and ruined will. "Theo. Look at me. Please."

A long moment. Then, grudgingly, those dark eyes came around—the eyes Vess had first seen over the edge of a grain sack in a Thornfell warehouse, eleven years old and feral, already knowing how to go invisible in plain sight. Nine years of watching those eyes learn to lie, to flatter, to go empty on command. Nine years, and Vess had never once seen them afraid until now.

"There is no bounty on your head," Vess said. "There is no order to bring you in. The inquisitors don't want you captured. They want you *whole*." She drew a breath. "They serve the Regent. All of them. The whole grey machine of them, top to bottom. And the Regent doesn't want you dead either, child. It wants you delivered."

Something moved behind Theo's expression—a flicker, quickly mastered. "Sera said the moon turns thirteen times. Said the Regent finishes eating the kingdom when it does."

"Sera Vane says a great many true things wrapped in lies." Vess kept her voice level, gentle, the voice she'd used to talk Theo down off rooftops. "Yes, there's a thirteenth turn coming. Yes, the Regent means to feast. And yes—the Choir believes a thief can walk into the king's mind and starve it. They believe it so hard they've spent a century preparing for it. Do you know why a hundred years of prophecy lands on *you*, specifically? Not because you're chosen. Because you're *empty*, Theo. You're the one person in this kingdom with nothing left to lose, because someone took it all before you could defend it. They looked at that wound and they saw a weapon. They saw a girl with a hole where her life used to be, and they thought: perfect. Perfect vessel. Send her in. Whatever the Regent eats out of her, well—there was hardly anything there to begin with."

"That's not—" Theo stopped. Her jaw worked. Vess watched her fight it, watched the girl reach for the armor of indifference and find it wouldn't buckle on.

"It is," Vess said quietly. "I've read their texts. I've sat in their circles wearing their grey. They call it the Door rite. They sing a mind open, and the singer walks in, and the Regent feeds on whatever the intruder treasures most. With anyone else, that's a lifetime of loves and losses—decades to devour, time enough for it to grow fat and finish the job. With you? You'd be a *snack*. A taste. An appetizer before the main course, and then it takes the kingdom anyway, and the Choir's great sacrifice accomplishes nothing except proving their prophecy right." She let that sit. "Unless. Unless someone walks in carrying something the Regent cannot digest. Something whole. Something returned."

Theo's bound hands had gone white-knuckled against the table. "Returned," she repeated.

Vess reached into her coat.

She had rehearsed this moment a hundred times in the past three days, and in every rehearsal her hand had been steady. In reality it shook. She set the vial on the table between them, beside the cold tea, and the light inside it moved—not flickering, not reflecting, but *swimming*, the way light moves underwater, the way light moves behind closed eyelids when you dream of a place you can't name.

"This is yours," Vess said. "Taken from a burning house in the river district twenty years ago. The night your family died. The night someone decided you'd be safer not remembering. It fell through the world, and the inquisitors caught it, and they have kept it—kept *all* of it, every fragment of your childhood that's ever gone missing—waiting for exactly this." Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "Every year. Every birthday. Your mother's face. Her voice. The song she used to sing—you don't know there's a song, but there is, Theo, there's a song, and I have heard it, and it's about *you*."

The silence in the granary cellar was total. Even the brazier seemed to burn quieter.

When Theo finally spoke, her voice came out sanded flat, stripped of everything. "You're lying."

"I have lied to you exactly four times in nine years," Vess said. "Once to keep you from following me into the Sable Quarter. Once about the fate of a boy named Corren, may he rest. Twice to protect you from yourself. I am not lying now. I swear it on the only thing I have left, which is that I never once asked you what was taken, because I knew if you ever started remembering you would break, and I could not—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she let it crack anyway because Theo needed to see that this cost her too. "I could not watch you break."

"You're selling me to them." Theo's eyes had fixed on the vial and would not move from it. Vess recognized the look. She had seen it on gamblers, on addicts, on grief-mad widows buying back a dead husband's last words. Hunger wearing the mask of suspicion. "This is the recruitment pitch. This is how they turn me."

"Yes," Vess said. "It is. And I'm the one making it, because I'd rather you hate me alive than love me empty. Listen to me." She leaned across the table, close enough that the vial's swimming light caught them both, gilding Theo's cheekbone, gilding the scar above her brow that Vess herself had stitched when the girl was thirteen. "The Choir wants your sacrifice. The Regent wants your surrender. Neither of them wants *you*. I want you. That's the whole difference, and it's the only one that matters. Come with me. Take it back—all of it, every stolen year, your mother's voice singing your name. Walk into that palace with a childhood in your chest instead of a hole. Let the Regent try to swallow a person who knows exactly who she is and what she's fighting for. That's the only way anyone survives that throne room. Not by being empty. By being *full*."

"And the Choir?" Theo whispered. "Sera. Bram."

"Are believers," Vess said. "Believers are the most dangerous people alive, because they'll feed you to their faith and call it love. Sera Vane already fed one rescue to the Regent—ask her about it sometime, really ask her, watch her face. She's not trying to save you, child. She's trying to *finish something*. You're just the key she finally found after a hundred years of looking." Vess straightened. Her hands, freed of the vial's weight, would not stop trembling, so she folded them. "Give them up. Give me their door-rites, their ways into the palace, their names. Help the inquisitors end the Regent the sane way—the slow way, the way that doesn't require putting a twenty-year-old girl into a monster's mouth. And when it's done, when the kingdom still stands and you still stand inside it, you'll have your mother's song. You'll have your name. You'll have everything they were going to burn as an offering."

"You keep saying my mother sang my name." Theo's voice had gone very small. "I don't have a name. I picked 'Theo' out of a gutter when I was eleven because it sounded like nobody."

"I know," Vess said, and her heart broke cleanly, like ice underfoot. "I know you did. Wouldn't you like to know what she called you? Before?"

The light in the vial swam. And Theo Blackwood—who had stared down guild enforcers and crown inquisitors and the frozen dark of the gorge without blinking—reached out with her bound hands and touched the glass as gently as if it were sleeping.

* * *

Later—much later, when the lamps had burned low and the tea had gone stone-cold and Theo had asked, in a voice like a wound, *how much of it is real, how much can you prove*—Vess walked her down the corridor to the holding room where the archive waited: shelf upon shelf of vials, thousands of them, each glowing with its own drowned light, each a life someone had dropped and the inquisitors had caught.

She had not planned to show her this. It was too soon, too much. But the girl had stood at the threshold of that corridor and Vess had seen her waver, and wavering was a door that closed fast, and so Vess had done the unforgivable kind thing again and opened the way.

"Twenty years of harvest," Vess murmured, as the light washed over them both. "Every forgotten thing in the eastern provinces. Somewhere in here is your whole childhood, Theo. Every piece of it. And it stays right here, safe, whether you help us or not—that's a promise—but it can only be *returned* if the return is earned. If the Regent falls. If you're still you, on the far side of it, to receive it."

Theo moved down the aisle slowly, trailing her fingertips along the shelves the way other people touch the shoulders of the dead. Halfway along, she stopped. One vial among thousands had caught her attention—or something had caught the vial's attention, its inner light pressing outward, brightening as she neared, dimming as she passed.

She stood before it a long time. Her breath fogged in the cold air. When she spoke, she didn't turn around, and her voice was the flattest, quietest thing Vess had ever heard from her, flatter even than the day they'd met.

"This one's warm," Theo said. "Why is this one warm?"

Vess crossed to her side and looked. The vial held light the color of woodsmoke, and within it, faintly, unmistakably, something was moving that was not light at all—a shape, small and quick, like a figure running through flame. And from somewhere inside the glass, thin as thread, there rose a sound that made the hair stand up along Vess's arms:

Singing.

A woman's voice. A lullaby. And threaded through the melody, rising and falling with it, a second voice—smaller, younger, wordless, humming along the way children hum when they don't yet know the words but already know they belong to the song.

Theo's hand rose to the glass. Behind them, at the end of the corridor, boots were approaching—measured, unhurried, the clerk's soft tread—and Vess felt the trap close around them both, the one she had built and the one she had walked into, and she understood suddenly, terribly, that she had brought this girl not to safety but to the exact center of the thing they were all caught in.

The vial brightened against Theo's palm.

And somewhere far above them, beyond the stone, beyond the snow, the moon began its final turn.
