## 13. What Was Taken

They did not tie her hands.

That was the first insult. Theo had been a prisoner before—twice in the guild cells beneath the Saltmarket, once in the back of a smuggler's wagon with her wrists lashed to an axle—and every one of her captors had known better than to leave her fingers free. Fingers were all she needed. A lock, a knot, a sleeping guard's collar. But Vess's people had searched her thoroughly, taken the prophecy book and the knife in her boot and the thin picks sewn into her sleeve seams, and then they had simply left her hands bare, folded in her lap like a guest's.

Because they didn't think of her as dangerous anymore. They thought of her as merchandise.

The room they kept her in was high in the inquisitors' waystation, a converted watchtower on the Thornfell road with windows too narrow to climb through and walls lined in grey felt—the same grey as the Choir's robes, Theo noticed, though here it had been hung to deaden sound rather than carry it. No singing in this place. No memory escaping by accident. Somewhere below her, she could hear the muffled rhythm of boots, the clink of vials being packed into padded crates, the industrious quiet of people who believed their work was holy.

She had been sitting on the narrow cot for what felt like hours when the door opened and Vess came in carrying a tray: bread, hard cheese, tea gone lukewarm in its clay cup. She set it down on the stool beside the cot and did not sit herself. She never sat in front of Theo. Some old handler's habit—keep your mark looking up at you, keep them small.

"You didn't eat last night," Vess said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You ate like a stevedore from the day I found you." Vess's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You were eleven and you stole half my lunch on the ferry across the Mire and I decided right there I'd rather have you working for me than against me."

"I remember."

"Do you?" Something flickered across Vess's face—too quick to name, gone before Theo could catalogue it. "Eat. We'll talk after."

Theo picked up the bread because refusing it would be a conversation, and conversations with Vess were chess games where Vess had already read three moves ahead. The bread was good. That was the worst part. Vess had always made sure her people ate well; it was cheaper than loyalty and lasted longer.

"Where are we going?" Theo asked, around a mouthful. "You said the capital. Inquisitors don't haul prisoners up mountain roads for the scenery."

Vess moved to the window, or the suggestion of a window—a slit of grey daylight in the stone. Outside, snow was falling again, fat and slow, erasing the road they'd climbed. "The Regent wants you brought whole. Not damaged. Not frightened." A pause. "Well. Not *damaged*."

"And when I get there?"

"Then you'll be given a choice." Vess turned. In the flat light her face looked older than Theo remembered, the lines around her eyes carved deeper, as if the last weeks had cost her something she hadn't budgeted for. "The same choice everyone gets, eventually. What to forget."

"The Choir says the Regent doesn't give choices. It just takes."

"The Choir says a lot of things." Vess crossed the room and crouched—not sitting, never sitting, but lowering herself enough that for once Theo wasn't looking up at her. Up close, there was something wrong with her eyes. Not madness. Worse: certainty. The serene, polished certainty of someone who has stopped checking her premises. "Theo. Listen to me. I've served the crown's memory office for nine years. I've seen what forgetting does to people who aren't ready for it. I've watched strong men beg to have their grief cut out, and I've watched weak ones lose everything down to their own names because no one taught them how to let go." Her voice dropped. "What I'm offering you isn't a cage. It's a door."

"You're offering me a leash."

"I'm offering you—" Vess stopped. Breathed. Started again, slower, the way she did when she was about to spend real coin. "There's something I want to show you. Before you decide anything. Before you hate me past the point of listening."

"I passed that point at the waystation gate, when your friends put a singer in the ground."

"Then hate me and look anyway." Vess stood and held out her hand. "Please."

Theo stared at the hand. Rough palm, ink-stained knuckles, the old burn scar along the thumb from the night the Saltmarket warehouse burned—Vess had pulled two children out of that fire, and Theo had been one of them, and neither of them had ever spoken of it since.

She took the hand. She told herself it was because a captive who cooperated got opportunities, and opportunities got her out of locked rooms. She told herself that all the way down the spiral stair, past the felt-lined walls, past two guards who did not meet her eyes, into a chamber at the tower's root where the cold came up through the floor like the building itself was standing in deep water.

* * *

The vault was small. Smaller than the archive of the burned library where her whole life had come apart; smaller than the Choir's ice-bound sanctuary. One lamp. One table. And along one wall, shelf upon shelf of vials racked in oiled wood, each one stoppered and sealed with wax, each one faintly luminous with the light that lived inside memories—the pale gold of a wedding day, the bruised violet of a funeral, colors Theo knew better than anyone alive, because she had spent fifteen years pulling them out of other people's heads and selling them to whoever could pay.

But this wasn't a smuggler's stockroom. The vials here weren't sorted by value. They were sorted by date.

Decades of dates. A century of dates.

"What is this?" Theo asked, though she already knew, and knowing made her voice come out wrong, flattened, like she was hearing it through the felt on the walls.

"Harvest," Vess said quietly. "Everything the Regent takes, it stores. It can't digest it all at once—you understand? It feeds slowly, the way a man eats. And what it saves, it keeps here, in vessels, until it's needed." She walked the length of the shelves, trailing one finger above the wax seals without touching them. "The guilds think they're the only thieves in Thornfell. The guilds are amateurs. This—" she gestured at the wall, at the soft glow of ten thousand stolen lives, "—this is the oldest theft in the kingdom. Older than the crown. Older than the moon-count."

"Why show me this?"

"Because of this." Vess stopped at a rack near the end, low to the ground, child-height almost, and lifted out a single vial.

It was unremarkable. Wax-sealed, thumb-sized, holding a curl of light so faint it was nearly colorless—that washed-out silver of very old memories, memories that had been stored so long they'd begun to fade even in preservation. Theo had handled hundreds like it. She'd sold dozens.

Then she saw the label.

Not a date. Not a catalog number. A name, written in a careful clerk's hand:

**WINTERBOURNE.**

The word went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. She didn't move. Fifteen years of training held her still, face blank, breathing even—but inside her chest something was screaming, some animal part of her that had slept for twenty years and had just smelled smoke.

"That's not possible," she said.

"Read the rest."

Below the name, smaller: *Household of Aldous Winterbourne, millwright. Contents: one (1) earliest recollection, subject age approximately four years. Provenance: requisitioned under Crown Seal, autumn of the Ash Year.*

The Ash Year.

The burning house. The woman singing.

Theo's hand rose without her permission and took the vial from Vess's palm, and the glass was warm, warmer than the vault, warm like something alive, and through the wax seal she could feel it—the pull, the faint tidal tug that every thief learned to feel and ignore, except this time the tide was pulling *her*, this time the memory behind the glass was reaching for her the way a child reaches—

"Twenty-two years ago," Vess said, and her voice had gone gentle in a way Theo had never heard it, not once, not ever, and somehow that gentleness was more terrifying than cruelty. "A house fire outside Millbrook. A millwright named Aldous Winterbourne, his wife Lira, their daughter. The daughter survived. The parents' bodies were never recovered from the ash." A pause. "Neither were their memories. Or the neighbors'. Or anyone within a mile of that fire. The official record calls it the Ash Year because eleven houses burned in one season across three villages, and in every one of them, the survivors couldn't say what they'd lost beyond the house itself. They remembered the fire. They couldn't remember what came before it."

The vial trembled in Theo's grip. She steadied it against her sternum.

"The Regent was young then," Vess went on. "Hungry. It fed on a whole valley and left nothing but smoke and orphans, and the crown covered it up because the crown already belonged to it, and the orphan—" Vess's throat worked. "The orphan was four years old and remembered nothing, and a thief named Vess found her on a ferry eating stolen bread, and never once asked herself why a child that young had a hole where her life should be. Because asking questions was bad for business."

"Stop." Theo's voice cracked on it. "Stop talking."

"Theo—"

"*Stop.*"

The silence rang. Somewhere above them, boots crossed a floor. The lamp hissed. And Theo stood in the cold heart of the inquisitors' vault with her own first memory sealed in glass against her chest, and twenty years of careful, professional emptiness came apart all at once, quietly, the way wet rope parts—not with a snap but with a long unraveling she could feel in her teeth.

The burning house was real.

She had thought—she had *told herself*, all these years, in the ledger-voice she used for everything—that the fragment the Choir's rite had torn loose was a dream, a residue, a stray scrap of someone else's grief caught in her thief's hands and mistaken for her own. She had built her whole self on the absence. *I keep none of my own.* It had been discipline. Craft. A philosophy.

It had been a wound dressed up as a philosophy.

Her mother had been singing. In the fragment, through the smoke, a woman's voice rising over the roar of flame—not screaming, *singing*, steady and clear, the way you sing to a child when you want the last thing they hear to be beautiful. And now Theo knew why the melody had lodged in her like a splinter, why she'd woken humming it in the Choir's sanctuary and hated herself for it. It wasn't a stranger's song.

It was a lullaby. It was *hers*. It was the last thing her mother had chosen to give her, sung into the smoke while the Regent drank everything else.

"They took my family," Theo said. The words came out level. That frightened her more than screaming would have. "Not killed. *Took.* Their memories. Everything they were. And then it let the fire finish the job, and it left me on a ferry with a hole in my head, and nobody—" Her breath hitched, caught, held. "Nobody ever came looking. Because there was nothing left to find."

"No," Vess said. "There wasn't."

"And you knew." Theo finally looked up, and whatever Vess saw in her face made the older woman step back a half pace. "You've known since the waystation. Since before. How long, Vess? How long have you known what I am?"

"I suspected after the archive job. I confirmed it the day the prophecy surfaced." Vess's jaw tightened. "And yes—I let the inquisitors take you rather than warn you, because I thought I could control where you landed. I thought if you arrived at the capital in *my* custody instead of theirs, I could keep you off the harvest table." She gestured at the glowing wall of vials, bitter and tired. "I was wrong about how much of you they'd already claimed. They had your file before I ever swore my oath. Your whole family's file. The Regent has been keeping your name in a drawer for twenty years, Theo. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means it planned this." The understanding arrived cold and total, like ice water finding the seam of a boot. "The prophecy. The book. The thirteenth turn. It wrote me into the story because it needed a thief who could walk into the king's mind—and it made sure the thief had nothing left to lose except the one thing it was holding."

"It needed you angry, too," Vess said softly. "Angry people make clean sacrifices. They run straight at the blade."

For a moment the vault swam. Theo pressed the vial harder against her breastbone and breathed—in through the nose, out slow, the way Bram had taught her during the lessons in losing, *the mind is a corridor, walk it, don't run it*—and beneath the rage she found something else, something older and quieter and far more dangerous.

Purpose. Not the guild's kind, which was transactional. Not the Choir's kind, which was prophetic. Hers. Personal as a scar.

The Regent had eaten her childhood. It had eaten her mother's voice, her father's laugh, the smell of the mill, the name of her village, the shape of her own small hands. It had kept the first piece in a drawer like a tax receipt. And it had arranged her entire life—the invisibility, the trade, the hollow ritual of taking without keeping—as a runway leading to the moment it could devour her wholesale at the moon's thirteenth turn.

Fine. Then she would walk the runway. On her own terms.

"Give me the seal-knife," Theo said.

Vess blinked. "What?"

"The wax on this vial. I need it broken before I can take the memory in—it has to be received willingly or it won't bind. Every thief knows that. You know I know that." She held out her free hand, palm up, steady. "Unless this was all theater. Unless you dragged me down here to dangle my own mother's voice in front of me and then keep it."

Something warred in Vess's face—handler's caution against something rawer, guiltier. Then she drew the small bone-handled knife from her belt, the one she'd carried since the Saltmarket days, and set it in Theo's open palm.

"If you run," Vess said, "they'll hunt you to the coast. If you fight, they'll put you down. There's no version of tonight where you walk out of here clean."

"There's always a version," Theo said. "I'm a thief. Finding the version is the whole job."

She set the vial on the table. She worked the knife's edge under the wax seal with the tenderness she'd never once shown a client's memory, and the wax parted with a sound like a sigh, and the smell that rose out of the opening vial stopped her heart: woodsmoke and milk and rain on a millpond and underneath it all, unmistakable, a thread of melody, a hummed phrase, her mother's voice preserved in amber and light.

She didn't drink it. She couldn't—not here, not with Vess watching, not with the whole tower between her and the sky. Instead she re-stoppered it with her thumb, tucked it into her bodice against her skin where the warmth would keep the memory dreaming, and looked up at the woman who had raised her for the trade and lied to her for a lifetime.

"I'm sorry," Theo said, and meant it, and used it anyway, because the next part required Vess looking away.

"For what?"

"For this."

She drove her heel down on the lamp. Darkness dropped like a lid. In the same motion she swept the tray-table over into Vess's shins, heard the crack of wood and Vess's grunt of pain, and ran—not toward the door, which would be barred by now, but toward the shelving wall, because a thief knows that every vault has a second exit and every second exit is hidden behind the thing they most want to protect.

The vials. Of course it was the vials.

Behind the lowest rack, exactly where the draft had been kissing her ankles since she entered, a maintenance hatch stood in the stone, iron-latched, sized for a child or a stooped adult. Theo hit the latch, shouldered the hatch wide, and poured herself into the dark beyond it as the vault door burst open behind her and lamplight and shouting flooded the room she'd left.

The tunnel was low and freezing and smelled of old mortar. She ran bent double, lungs burning, one hand pressed flat over the vial at her chest as if she could hold her mother's voice in place by pressure alone. Behind her, boots and torchlight and Vess's voice, hoarse with something that might have been fury or might have been grief, ordering the search.

Ahead, the tunnel sloped down and forked, and Theo stopped in the black and listened the way the Choir had taught her—not with her ears but with the deep, patient attention she'd spent a lifetime refusing. Snowmelt, somewhere left. Wind through stone, right. And threaded through both, so faint she might have invented it, a harmonic, a chord, rising out of the mountain's bones like warmth rising from a hearth.

The Choir. Close. Closer than Vess could know—close enough that the mountain itself seemed to lean toward them, the way the passes had leaned toward her on the road, the way everything in this kingdom seemed to bend around the shape of the prophecy.

Let it bend, Theo thought, and began to climb toward the sound. Let the whole cursed country tilt. It had spent twenty years arranging her life for its feast. Now she knew the price of the ticket, and she intended to arrive at the king's side carrying the one thing the Regent had never planned for: its own oldest crime, sealed in glass, held against a thief's furious heart.

Above her, through miles of rock and snow, the moon was turning toward its thirteenth face.

And somewhere in the dark ahead, a woman's song waited to be finished.
