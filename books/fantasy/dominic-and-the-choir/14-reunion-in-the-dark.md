## 14. Reunion in the Dark

Bram had been singing to the snow for three days before he found her.

It wasn't a song anyone had taught him, exactly. The Choir's oldest members said the mountains remembered every footstep ever taken across them, and that a singer who listened closely enough could hear where the paths had been. So Bram walked the high passes above the gorge with his hood back and his ears open, letting the wind carry him fragments—*a shepherd's whistle, a mule's bell, the creak of a door long rotted away*—and followed whatever sounded like it might lead somewhere warm.

The mountain wanted him to find her. He was almost sure of it. The paths kept arranging themselves in front of him like a hand smoothing wrinkles from cloth, and twice he'd crested a ridge to see, far below, a single line of dark footprints stitched through otherwise untouched snow.

On the third morning, the footprints stopped being footprints.

He found her in the lee of a fallen pine, curled so tightly around herself she looked less like a person than like something the storm had tried to swallow and failed. Snow had drifted over her legs. Her lips were the color of slate. One fist was clenched at her chest, and even unconscious—even half-dead—she held on to whatever was inside it the way a drowning woman holds a rope.

"Theo." He knelt and brushed the snow from her shoulder. "Theo Blackwood."

Her eyes opened a crack. For one terrible moment there was nothing in them at all, no recognition, no fear, just the flat white blankness of deep cold—and then something flickered, some stubborn ember of the girl who'd stolen a merchant's first kiss without breaking stride, and she rasped:

"If you say I told you so, I'll haunt you."

"You can't haunt me. You're not dead yet." Bram shrugged off his pack and dragged out the bread he'd baked that morning in the waystation's ovens, dense and dark with rye, still faintly warm against his ribs where he'd carried it. "Eat first. Haunt later."

She tried to take it and couldn't close her fingers. So he broke off a piece and held it to her mouth like feeding a bird, and she glared at him with what strength the glare could muster, and ate.

They sat like that for a long while, the storm muttering around them, Bram feeding her bread piece by piece and Theo refusing, between bites, to look at anything but the middle distance. He didn't ask why she'd left. He didn't ask how far she'd meant to walk, or whether she'd known the passes were death this time of year, or whether the vial was still clenched in her fist—he could see its shape through her frozen fingers, a small glass heart beating amber when the light caught it.

Sera would have asked. Sera would have needed to ask, because Sera carried guilt the way other people carried knives, always checking the edge. But Bram was nineteen and had been raised by singers, and singers knew that some silences were load-bearing. You didn't pull them out to see what they held. You sang around them until the walls grew strong enough to hold themselves.

So instead, when the bread was gone and color had crawled back into Theo's face—not health, exactly, but the suggestion of it—he did the only other thing he knew how to do.

He sang.

It wasn't a grand working. It wasn't the kind of harmonized verse that opened doors in ice or walked through another mind's corridors. It was a walking song, the kind Choir children learned before they learned letters, four notes rising and falling like breath, old enough that no one remembered who'd written it or what it had originally been about. Some said it was a lullaby. Some said it was a map. Bram's mother had sung it while kneading dough, which seemed as good an answer as any.

Theo went very still.

"What is that," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"Walking song. Older than the guilds, probably older than the crown." He kept his voice easy, kept the melody level. "Why?"

"Because—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. The vial shifted in her fist. "Someone used to sing that. Someone I—" The sentence collapsed. She stared at the snow as if it had betrayed her. "I don't remember. That's the whole point, isn't it? I don't remember, and your stupid song poked a hole in me anyway."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Don't—" She pressed the heel of her free hand against her eyes. When she spoke again her voice came out low and cracked down the middle. "Do you know what it's like, Bram? To have spent your whole life making sure you felt nothing, because feeling nothing was safe, and then to have people keep—*offering* you things? Bread. Songs. A place by the fire. Like it costs them nothing. Like they don't understand that every time they do it, they're taking something from me too."

Bram considered this with the seriousness it deserved. The storm filled the pause with its white noise.

"It costs us plenty," he said finally. "You're exhausting. You glare like you're charging rent on your own face. You called our training hall a 'creepy echo box.' You nearly broke Wren's wrist during the second lesson."

Despite everything—despite the frostbite and the vial and the hole the song had poked—a muscle at the corner of her mouth twitched. Nearly smiled. Retreated.

"But we keep offering," he went on, "because that's the whole trick of it. That's the entire secret of the Choir, the thing Sera will lecture you about for six hours if you let her. Memory isn't a thing you *have*. It's a thing people hold for each other. A memory only one person carries dies with them. A memory a whole choir carries—" he shrugged, snow sliding off his shoulders, "—that one outlives kingdoms. We're not offering you things, Theo. We're offering to hold them with you. There's a difference."

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that he thought she'd gone back under, and leaned forward to check her breathing—and found her looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her before. Unguarded. Almost young.

"Your mother sang that song," she said quietly. "While baking."

"Yeah. How'd you—"

"Lucky guess." She looked away fast, but not fast enough. "We should move. If I'm going to freeze to death, I'd rather do it somewhere with worse company."

Bram grinned and hauled her upright.

* * *

The waystation smelled of woodsmoke and melted beeswax, and it was full of grey-robed singers who all, to a person, pretended not to notice her.

That was the part Theo hadn't expected, Bram realized, watching her come through the door half-carried on his arm. She'd braced herself for questions—for Sera's grief, for Wren's suspicion, for the whole order closing around her like a fist. She stood in the doorway dripping meltwater, chin up, armor on, ready to be interrogated.

Instead, Wren handed her a towel. Old Maren, who never spoke above a whisper since the Regent had eaten most of her hearing, pressed a cup of something hot into her hands and patted her elbow twice. A boy of maybe ten, one of the newest novices, scooted sideways on the bench to make room without being asked, the way you make room for family at supper.

Nobody asked where she'd gone. Nobody asked if she'd betrayed them, or whether Vess's inquisitors had followed her trail, or what was in the vial she still wouldn't unclench her fingers from.

Theo sat down on the bench like a woman sitting on a trapdoor.

"They know," she said to Bram, low, as he settled beside her. "They have to know. I walked out. I accused Sera of—I said the worst things I could think of, and I meant them, and now everyone's just—handing me towels—"

"Everyone heard you leave. Everyone also heard the storm." Bram tore his last heel of bread in half and put the bigger piece in front of her. "There's a saying here. *The mountain does the judging.* Anyone who walks into that weather angry comes back either humbled or not at all. You came back. That's the whole ceremony."

"That's insane."

"Probably."

"And Sera?" Her voice dropped further. "Where is she?"

"In the archive room, pretending to reorganize the same shelf she's been reorganizing since dawn, waiting for you to stop hiding behind the bread." He nodded toward the corridor. "Go on. She won't bite. She'll lecture, but she won't bite."

Theo looked at the corridor. Then at the vial in her fist. Then, briefly and terribly, at Bram—with an expression that on anyone else he'd have called *scared*—and got up, and went.

Bram didn't follow. But the waystation was built of old stone and older habits, and sound carried, and he was a singer besides; he could hardly help hearing.

He heard Theo stop in the archive-room doorway. Heard the long silence. Heard Sera set down whatever she was holding—the soft clack of a book being closed, a shelf given up on.

"Sera," Theo said. "I need to say something, and if you interrupt me I'll lose my nerve, so."

Another silence. Waiting.

"I'm not sorry I left." The words came out flat and fast, thrown down like cards. "I'm not going to apologize for that. You should have told me the price before you trained me, before you—you fed me and taught me songs and made me think I was becoming a person again, and then told me the whole time I was meat for the Regent's table. That was wrong. I'd walk out again tomorrow."

"Yes," Sera said quietly. "I know. And I've spent every hour since you left agreeing with you."

That seemed to knock the wind out of Theo more thoroughly than any argument would have. Bram heard her breathe.

"So what are you sorry for, then?" Sera asked. Gentle. Terribly gentle.

A longer silence. When Theo answered, her voice had changed—smaller, stripped of its armor, the voice of someone standing in a burned house.

"I'm sorry I assumed. All of it. From the first day. I assumed you recruited me because I was useful. I assumed the training was investment. I assumed the bread was strategy, and the songs were technique, and when you told me the truth I thought—*there it is, there's the bill.* Because that's how everything works where I'm from. Nobody holds out a hand unless there's a hook in it." A shaky breath. "And then I walked out into a blizzard rather than find out I was wrong, because finding out I was wrong would mean every year of my life I'd spent alone was a year I didn't have to. And I couldn't stand that. So I decided to freeze instead. That's what I'm sorry for. Not leaving. Assuming you were like me."

Bram closed his eyes. In the main room, Maren had stopped mid-task, head tilted, listening too. Even the fire seemed quieter.

"Oh, child," Sera said. "We're exactly like you. That's the tragedy of this whole order. Every singer in this house lost something to forgetting, and every one of us chose stone walls afterward, and every one of us had to be dragged, singing, back out into the world. You're not the exception, Theo. You're the tradition."

Something that might have been a laugh, wet and short.

"The vial," Theo said. "When I'm ready. I want to show you what's in it. What they took. I want—I want you to hold it with me. Is that—is that how it works? Is that allowed?"

"It's the only thing that's ever worked."

Footsteps. Theo came back into the main room with her face scrubbed raw and her spine straight, and Wren, without looking up from the kettle, said, "There's stew if you want it," in exactly the tone she'd have used for the weather, and Theo sat down among them, and ate.

Later, much later, when the novices were asleep and the elders had drifted to their cells, Bram found her awake by the fire, turning the vial slowly in her fingers. Amber light. Something small and golden suspended inside it, like a seed, like a spark caught mid-fall.

"My mother's," she said, without looking up. She didn't explain further. She didn't have to; the shape of it was plain. "Vess has the rest. A whole childhood, in jars, in the inquisitors' vault. And the moon turns in—" she counted silently, lips moving, "—eleven days."

"Eleven days," Bram agreed. He sat down beside her. Outside, the storm had passed, and through the frost-ferned window the moon hung fat and white and nearly finished, one sliver shy of its impossible thirteenth turn.

"The rehearsal tore something loose," Theo said. "In my training. A burning house. A woman singing. I thought it was nothing. A fragment, noise." Her thumb traced the glass. "It wasn't noise. It was the first page of a book someone stole from me, and I just found out the rest of the book exists, and it's sitting in a vault in Thornfell behind Vess's teeth." She looked up at him at last, and her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely certain, and Bram felt the hair rise on his arms, because he'd seen that look once before—in the old stories, on the faces of singers who went to do the thing that could not be undone.

"Eleven days," Theo said, "is enough time to rob the inquisitors' vault. I'd need a thief."

"You *are* a thief."

"No." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "I need one who doesn't have a prophecy painted on her face. Someone invisible." She tipped her head toward him. "Someone who knows the lower city. Someone the Choir trusts."

Bram stared at her. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked—Sera, listening at the top of the stairs, pretending to pass by.

"You want me to break into Thornfell," he said, "with you. Eleven days before the end of the world."

"I want you to help me steal my childhood back," Theo said. "And then I want to give it away."
