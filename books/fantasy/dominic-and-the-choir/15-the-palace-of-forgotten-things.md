## 15. The Palace of Forgotten Things

Sera Vane had sung at coronations. She had sung at funerals for kings who never learned her name, and once—only once—for a boy prince who had pressed a copper coin into her palm and whispered *thank you* like it was a secret. She knew the palace of Aldric's capital the way a singer knows a song she performed in her youth: imperfectly, tenderly, with the certain knowledge that it had changed since.

It had changed.

She stood in the shadow of the Moonturn arch with thirty grey-robed singers folded into the festival crowd around her, and she looked up at the high windows of the royal wing, and she felt the change the way one feels a wrong note—not in the ear, but in the teeth.

The banners were wrong. Not their colors; the crimson and silver of the Aldric line still snapped in the cold festival wind. It was their *number*. There were too many. They hung from every balcony, every lamppost, every eave, as though someone had commanded the palace dressed in the king's colors and no one had thought to ask why, and no one had remembered to stop.

"Too many banners," murmured Odell at her shoulder. He was the oldest of them, his voice a low cello of a thing, and his hands were tucked into his sleeves the way they always were when he was frightened. "Someone was very enthusiastic."

"Or no one was anything at all," Sera said. "Someone gave an order years ago, and the order was never countermanded, because the person who could countermand it—" She did not finish. The person who could countermand it had a crown growing through his skull like ivy through a statue.

Below them, the Moonturn festival spilled gold across the capital's great square. Thirteen lanterns on thirteen poles, twelve of them lit, the thirteenth dark and waiting—the old rite, the honest one, the one that said *the year is not finished until the thirteenth moon has turned and the last light is given back*. Children ran between the poles with sparklers. Roast chestnuts perfumed the cold. Somewhere a fiddler was playing the Turnsong, slightly too fast, the way fiddlers always did when they wanted to be done before the ale ran out.

It was, Sera thought, the most ordinary evening she had ever been terrified in.

"Positions," she said softly.

The Choir moved like water finding its level. Grey robes became beggars' cloaks, pilgrims' mantles, the drab wool of servants come down from the hill to see the lanterns. Odell drifted toward the servants' gate with two of the younger voices. Bram—she felt him rather than saw him, a bright thread of attention at the edge of her awareness, and beside that thread, quieter, more guarded, Theo Blackwood in a borrowed festival cloak with the prophecy book wrapped against her ribs like a second heart.

*Keep her close*, Sera had told Bram, and Bram had said, with the particular gentleness he saved for things that mattered, *She'll keep me close. She just won't say it.*

The servants' gate opened for them because Odell sang it open—a three-note phrase pitched beneath hearing, a suggestion laid into the gatekeeper's mind like a coin laid into a palm: *you have already checked these faces; they are expected.* Sera watched the man's eyes go soft and unfocused for exactly four seconds, and then he waved them through with a smile that lingered a moment too long after they had passed, as though he were trying to remember why he was smiling.

She had done this a hundred times. She had sung doors open in a dozen courts. It had never made her skin crawl before.

It crawled now.

* * *

The servants' corridor smelled of bread and tallow, and it was empty.

That was the first wrongness Sera let herself name. On Moonturn eve, with the whole capital feasting, the palace kitchens should have been a roar—scullery boys and spit-turners and a steward shouting himself hoarse. Instead the corridor was silent, the ovens cold, a row of clean bowls stacked beside a basin of water gone grey and still. Someone had set out bowls for a meal that no one had cooked. Or someone had cooked a meal that no one had remembered to serve. The distinction, Sera was learning, did not matter. Both were the Regent's handwriting.

They passed a chambermaid on the landing. She was young, freckled, with a smear of flour on her cheek that had clearly been there for hours. She stood facing the wall. Not leaning—standing, upright, her hands folded, her eyes open and aimed at the stone as though the wall were a window and something wonderful were on the other side.

Sera stopped. The Choir stopped with her, a held breath of grey fabric.

"Child," Sera said gently. "Are you well?"

The girl turned. Her face arranged itself into a smile with a half-second's delay, like a reflection lagging behind its source. "Oh—yes, m'm. I'm going somewhere."

"Where?"

The smile held. The eyes behind it did not. "I'm going somewhere," the girl said again, with the same bright blankness, the way a bell that has been struck keeps saying its one note. "I'm sure of it. I was just—remembering which way."

Sera sang to her. Quietly, a single thread of the finding-verse, the little melody every Choir child learned before they learned letters, the one that helped the lost find the shape of their own names. She watched it land in the girl like a stone dropped into a well that was deeper than any well should be. She waited for the splash.

There was no splash. The finding-verse went down and down and did not touch anything.

"I'm going somewhere," the girl said a third time, and turned back to the wall, and Sera had to press her knuckles against her own sternum to keep from shaking her.

Odell's hand settled on her shoulder. *Not now. Not here.* She nodded. They moved on. Behind them the girl stood in the torchlight, flour on her cheek, waiting to arrive at a place she would never reach, and Sera carried the sound of her voice up the stairs like a coal carried in bare hands.

* * *

The upper corridors were worse, because they were better.

That was the thing Sera could not make the others understand until they saw it: the palace was not ruined. It was *polished*. The floors gleamed. The garlands for the festival were fresh, the candles trimmed, the silverware counted. Everything was tended. Nothing was *inhabited*. They passed footmen who bowed with perfect grace and could not say to whom. They passed a steward with a ledger open on his desk, making entries in a fine careful hand, and when Sera glanced at the page the entries were the same line written over and over, forty times, in ink that had gone from black to brown across the hours: *Inventory to follow. Inventory to follow. Inventory to follow.*

They passed a room where six courtiers stood around a table laid with cards no one was playing, and one of them laughed—a real laugh, warm and sudden—and the other five turned their heads toward the laugh in perfect unison, and no one spoke, and the laughing man's face slowly forgot what it had been doing.

"The feast," whispered one of the younger singers, a girl named Ilse whose voice could crack ice. "Sera, the great feast. Where is it?"

They found the great hall, and it was laid for a thousand guests. The tables ran the length of the hall like roads. Candles burned in their hundreds. And the seats were full—full of the court, full of lords and ladies in their festival finery, and the hall was utterly, cathedral-silent. A thousand people eating nothing, drinking nothing, speaking nothing, sitting in the light of a thousand candles with the patient stillness of furniture. At the far end, below the dais where the king's chair stood empty, a toast had been raised at some point; a hundred goblets still hung in the air at the height of the gesture, held there by arms that had forgotten the rest of it.

Odell made a sound Sera had never heard from him in thirty years. It was very small. It was the sound of a man swallowing his own scream.

"We go around," Sera said. Her voice came out steady, and she was grateful to it, because the rest of her was not. "Bram. The choir-loft. If the palace has a heart that still beats, it will be where the singing was meant to be."

* * *

The choir-loft above the great hall was where Sera had sung for the boy prince, twenty years ago, and her feet found the worn places in the floorboards as though no time had passed at all. From here she could see the whole silent feast spread out below like a painting of a feast, and beyond it, through the tall windows, the twelve lit lanterns of the Moonturn rite, and the thirteenth pole standing dark.

And from here she could see the throne room doors.

They were open. They should not have been open. Every instinct she owned said that the heart of the palace, the seat of the Regent, would be the most guarded place in the kingdom—and the doors stood wide, flung open onto darkness, unguarded, unbarred, *welcoming*.

"It wants us to come in," said Theo.

Sera had not heard her climb the loft stairs. The thief stood in the doorway with her hood back, and in the candlelight from below her face was pale and set, and her eyes were fixed on the open doors with the expression of someone reading a trap she had personally built a hundred times. "That's how it works, isn't it? You don't chase the mark. You make the door easy. You make them think it was their idea." Her jaw tightened. "It's been making everything easy since Thornfell. The road. The gorge. The book with my face in it. It's not guarding the throne. It's *setting the table*."

Below them, in the silent hall, one of the thousand seated guests turned her head. Just one. A lady in silver, her goblet still raised, her face tilting up toward the choir-loft with the smooth unhurried motion of something that had all the time in the world.

Then another turned. And another. Not all of them. A dozen. Two dozen. Faces lifting out of the stillness one by one, in no hurry at all, the way flowers turn toward a sun that is not the sun.

"Sera," Bram said quietly. He had come up behind Theo, and his hand was on her arm, and his voice was the steadiest thing in the room. "The thirteenth lantern. Look."

Out in the square, in the heart of the festival, in front of a thousand cheering people who saw nothing wrong with their world, the dark thirteenth lantern lit itself.

No hand had touched it. No flame had been carried to it. It simply bloomed alight, cold and white, a color that was not fire's color, and the crowd cheered louder, because it was beautiful, because it was the rite fulfilled, because no one could remember that the thirteenth light was supposed to be *given*, not taken—and Sera felt the note of it through the floor, through her bones, through the twenty years of guilt she had carried since the night the Choir failed and a young king was left alone with his crown.

The moon was rising. One turn remained.

And the doors of the throne room stood open, and everything in the palace was waiting, with terrible patience, for them to walk through.

"Sing," Sera said to her Choir, and her voice did not shake, because she would not permit it to. "Sing like the world is listening. Because it is."
