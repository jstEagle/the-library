## 16. Audience with the Hollow King

The king was dreaming again.

Aldric knew it was a dream because in dreams he could still walk. His legs remembered their old work when his waking mind had forgotten how to ask them for it, and so he wandered the corridors of a palace that no longer existed—the summer palace at Vaelmoor, with its open colonnades and its fountains that sang in three-part harmony because his mother had commissioned them to. He walked past rooms full of light. He walked past his own reflection in a long window, young and unlined and wearing no crown at all.

He was always without the crown in dreams. That was the mercy of them.

Then the dream began to thin, as it did sometimes, like fog burning off a morning field, and Aldric felt the other presence stir beneath him—the vast cold weight coiled through the foundations of his mind, feeding, patient, *waiting*—and he understood that this time the thinning of the dream was not the Regent's doing.

Someone else was in here with him.

He felt her before he saw her: a small bright wrongness moving through the eastern galleries of his memory, quick-footed, careful, touching things the way a thief touches—by the edges, never leaving prints. He had not felt another presence inside himself in twenty years. The Choir's singers, on the one terrible night they had tried to reach him, had been driven out screaming before they crossed the threshold of the outer wall. But this one moved differently. This one moved like someone who had spent her whole life in houses that were not hers.

*Come to the throne room,* he thought, and wondered if she could hear him. *Please. I have so little time when I have any.*

He gathered himself and walked toward the center of the palace-that-was-not-a-palace, and with every step he felt the lucidity come, rare and rationed, like water measured out to a dying man. It came from somewhere deep below the Regent's coils—a pocket of himself the thing had never managed to digest, sealed behind walls of its own making, though Aldric had never learned why. Perhaps even a parasite needs somewhere to keep the host alive. Perhaps the part of him that remained was simply too bitter to swallow.

The throne room assembled itself around him as he entered: the black marble, the pillars carved with the faces of every monarch back to Elandra the First, the great windows showing a sky he had not seen with his own eyes in two decades. And on the throne—not the golden crown of Vaelmoor, but the other one, the one the smiths had made in secret during the war years, iron and moonstone and something older than either—

There it sat. Wearing his shape when it wished to. Wearing nothing at all when it did not.

Aldric sat down on the steps beneath the throne, deliberately, an old man's deliberate slowness, and put his hands flat on the cold stone, and waited.

She came through the far doors.

For a heartbeat he thought the Regent had conjured her—that this was some new cruelty, some lure dressed in grey wool and road dust. Because he knew her face. He had known it for a hundred years before he'd ever seen it, the way a man knows a scar he hasn't earned yet. She was sketched on the vellum page that haunted his worst nights: the thief of the thirteenth turn, drawn by a prophet's hand beside a crowned figure whose face had been left deliberately blank.

But the sketch had not captured her. The sketch had shown a symbol. This was a girl—young, sharp-eyed, hollow-cheeked in the way of people who have learned to eat last—and she stood in the doorway of his memory like someone expecting the room to bite her.

"You're real," she said. Her voice echoed strangely; everything echoed here, in the spaces where memories had been eaten away. "You're actually—" She stopped. Squared her shoulders. "I'm Theo. The Choir brought me. Sera Vane. You may not remember—"

"I remember everything," Aldric said, "that it lets me keep. Which is less each year." He gestured to the hall around them. "What you see is what remains. A palace with most of its rooms bricked over. I apologize for the state of it. Kings are supposed to be better hosts."

She didn't smile. He hadn't expected her to. She was cataloguing him—he recognized the look; his quartermasters used to wear it, assessing what could be salvaged from a flooded granary—and he let her look, because being looked at was itself a kind of gift he had almost forgotten existed.

"They told me you'd be lucid for a few minutes," she said. "They weren't sure you'd be lucid at all. Or whether anything in here would be *you*."

"And what do you think?"

"I think the Regent doesn't beg," she said slowly. "Sera said it only takes. So whoever is sitting on the floor instead of the throne—" Her jaw tightened. "That's probably you."

"It's me." He heard his own voice crack on it, the way it always did, the small humiliating relief of saying the word after so long. "It's me, Theo Blackwood. Though I suspect you already know that name isn't quite right either. Don't flinch—it wasn't a theft, only a guess. The Choir sings fragments. Your mother's song carries a name in it. They'll tell you when they choose to."

Her whole body went still, the stillness of a hunted animal hearing a sound it cannot place, and Aldric understood that he had just spent a portion of his precious minutes badly. He raised a hand, placating.

"Forgive me. There is so little time, and I am greedy with it—I want to ask about harvests and borders and whether the elms on Coronation Row still stand, and none of it matters, none of it matters, and yet I find I want it anyway." He drew a breath he did not need, here, in this place of borrowed air. "Theo. Listen to me. We may only have minutes. I need you to hear the one thing no one else will tell you, because everyone who might tell you is either dead or deceived."

"The Regent," she said.

"The Regent." The word moved through the hall like a draft, and far above, on the throne, the iron crown seemed—did it?—to tilt slightly, the way a sleeping predator's ear tilts toward a sound. Aldric lowered his voice anyway, though he suspected privacy here was a fiction the parasite permitted for its own amusement. "You've been told you can enter my mind and cut it out. That the Choir can sing the door open and you, the foretold thief, can steal it away the way you steal kisses and first snowfalls and dead mothers' voices. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And they told you the cost. They must have. Sera Vane is many things, but she has never once lied to protect herself—only others, which is its own sin." He watched the girl's face do something complicated. "Ah. They told you late. Or partially. Very well. Then let me say it plainly, so there is no version of this where you can claim you didn't know. The Regent does not fight thieves. It *feeds* on them. Whatever memory you value most—whatever you carry closest, whatever makes you *you*—it will take that first, and take it completely, and it will leave you standing, breathing, smiling, emptied. I have watched it do this for twenty years to everyone who came near me. Servants. Physicians. My brother." His voice caught. "My wife. Not killed. Worse. Returned to the world with the centers of themselves scooped out, walking, working, laughing at the right moments. The court calls them the quiet ones. There are four hundred of them in the palace tonight, Theo. Four hundred people who once were people."

Theo had gone very pale. But she didn't run. He noted that, and admired it, and hated that he needed to admire anything about her because of what he was about to ask.

"So hear my terms," Aldric said. "Hear the only command your king has left in him to give. If the price of pulling this thing out of my head is another person's self—if it is *your* self—then you will turn around and you will walk out of here, and you will let it finish eating me."

"No," she said immediately.

"Yes."

"No." She took a step forward, furious now, and the fury lit her up so beautifully that for a moment the ruined hall seemed almost a court again. "Do you know what I am? I'm a memory-thief. I have taken a merchant's first kiss and sold it to his widow. I have stolen griefs, vows, the smell of a house someone grew up in. I have never once given anything back, because giving means being *known*, and being known means being caught, and being caught—" She stopped. Breathed. "The Choir opened a door for me. No one has ever opened a door for me. So don't sit there on your cold steps and order me to preserve myself like I'm one more thing in your vaults worth protecting. I decide what I'm worth. Not you. Not the prophecy. *Me.*"

The words rang off the eaten walls. And Aldric, King of Vaelmoor, who had not laughed in nineteen years, felt something crack open in his chest that was very nearly laughter and very nearly weeping, and knew it for what it was: pride. Fierce, unreasonable, entirely inappropriate pride, as if this stranger were his daughter.

"Oh," he said softly. "*Oh*, you are exactly what the book promised. God help us both."

Above them, on the throne, the crown shifted. The temperature of the hall dropped. Somewhere in the walls, something vast turned over in its sleep, and Aldric felt the lucidity begin to gutter like a candle in wind—the Regent stirring, sensing its host awake, sensing the thief, sensing perhaps that the hour it had cultivated for a century was finally arriving. He had minutes. Maybe fewer.

He had meant to spend them persuading her. Instead he heard himself say:

"Then I won't waste them arguing. Come here. Sit. Please." He patted the step beside him, absurdly domestic, a king inviting a thief to share his stair. "If you won't obey me, then grant me this. Everyone who comes into this place comes to fight something or steal something or save something. No one has ever simply *sat with me*. I would like, before the end, to be in the company of one person who is not afraid of me and does not want anything from me except my conversation."

She hesitated. Then, wary as a cat approaching a fire, she crossed the black marble and sat down on the step below his, close enough that he could have touched her shoulder, though he did not.

For a moment neither spoke. In the high windows the false sky wheeled, and far off, muffled by stone and distance and twenty years of silence, Aldric could almost hear singing—faint harmonies rising through the foundations, the Choir at their work below, opening doors. They sounded, he thought, like rain on the roof of the summer palace at Vaelmoor. Like the fountains. Like his mother's household, all those voices braided together on festival nights.

"Do you want to know what he was like?" Aldric asked quietly. "Before. The man I was."

"I read the histories."

"The histories describe the coronation. The plague year. The border wars." He shook his head. "None of it describes how he liked his eggs. Whether he sang in the bath. What his mother's voice sounded like when she called him in from the gardens." He looked down at his hands—his dream-hands, still young, still whole. "That's what it eats first, you know. Not the great memories. The small ones. It starts with the taste of bread and ends with your own name. The grand tragedies survive longest, because they're the hardest to digest. So I am a king made entirely of catastrophes. Ask me about the war and I can recite it. Ask me about any ordinary happy Tuesday, and there's just—hole."

Theo was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, rougher, the market-street voice under the choir-trained one.

"My childhood is gone," she said. "Sealed off. I thought I locked it myself. Turns out maybe I didn't. Maybe it was taken." She stared straight ahead at the throne, refusing to look at it. "So I suppose I know what the holes are like."

"Yes," Aldric said gently. "I believe you might understand me better than anyone living."

And then, because the candle of his lucidity was burning low, guttering, the edges of the hall beginning to soften and darken like paper held near flame, he did the thing he had climbed all the way up out of the dark to do.

"Theo. Look at me."

She turned. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"When you go down from here—when you go back to the Choir and they make their plans and you make yours—I need you to carry something out. Something it cannot touch, because I am giving it away freely, and free gifts poison its mouth." He pressed his hand to his own chest, and reached in—not flesh, but deeper, into the sealed pocket where the last unspoiled thing lived—and drew it out. It came into the light between them as a small warm shimmer, shapeless, humming faintly, the way all loose memories hummed. "This is mine. One of the few it never found. I kept it buried under the worst day of my life, beneath the fire, beneath the grief, because I knew it would never dig through that. Cowardice, perhaps. Or wisdom. I've had twenty years to decide and I still don't know."

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A Tuesday," Aldric said, and smiled, and the smile hurt his face because his face had forgotten the shape. "An ordinary one. I'm nine years old. It's summer. My mother is alive, and she's teaching me a song in the garden—not a hymn, not an anthem, just a foolish song about a fox who wanted to be a baker—and I'm getting the words wrong and she keeps laughing, not unkindly, just laughing, and the fountains are going, and the bees are in the lavender, and there is nothing at stake in the entire world. No crown. No prophecy. Just a boy, a song, and a Tuesday." His voice frayed. "It's the truest thing I have left. The rest is ceremony and catastrophe. This is just—me."

"Why give it to me?" Her voice cracked. "Why not keep it? If it's the last—"

"Because a memory kept in the dark dies anyway. It just dies alone." He leaned forward and pressed the shimmer lightly against her brow, and it sank in like warmth sinking into cold hands, and he felt the severing of it all the way down, a small clean wound that he knew would ache forever and that he welcomed. "Carry it out of here. Sing it to the world. Let someone hear the fox song who isn't me. That's all. Not a weapon, not a key, not a sacrifice. Just—let it exist outside these walls. Let me have been *real*, somewhere other than in here."

Theo was crying openly now, silently, the tears of someone who had taught herself long ago that crying loudly got people killed. She nodded.

"And Theo—" He took her hand. His dream-hand around her small callused one. "When it offers you the bargain—and it will, it knows your holes as well as I know mine, it helped dig some of them—remember what I said. Remember the four hundred quiet ones. Remember that a self traded away is not a self saved. Whatever it shows you, however much it hurts to refuse—" His grip tightened. "Be seen. Don't be saved. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I learned it too late to use."

The hall was dissolving. The pillars ran like wax. Above, the throne was no longer empty—the Regent had noticed them now, had risen from its long digestion, and the shape it wore was Aldric's own face rendered in iron and hunger, and its attention settled over them both like a closing lid.

"Go," Aldric said, standing, drawing himself up, and for one moment—one flickering, mortal, magnificent moment—the Hollow King stood straight in his own house, in his own body, in his own voice. "Go now. And whatever happens on the thirteenth night—know that a boy in a garden once sang a foolish song about a fox, and that it mattered, and that meeting you mattered too."

The darkness came down like a curtain, and the last thing he heard was her voice, fierce through the fading, promising nothing and everything:

*"I'll sing it back to you. I swear it. On the thirteenth night—I'll sing it back."*

* * *

Then he was in the dark again, in the coil and the cold, the lucidity folded away like a letter resealed, and the Regent poured itself back onto the throne of him, savoring, turning the encounter over like a jeweler examining a stone.

*Audacious,* it murmured through his mouth, in a voice that was almost his. *Sending the girl at last. A hundred years of cultivation, and here she stands in my orchard.*

But beneath the coils, in the small bitter pocket it had never digested, Aldric lay still and kept his own counsel, and held the place where the Tuesday had been like a tongue holds the space of a lost tooth—and listened, far below, to the singing getting closer.

And in the vial strapped against the thief-girl's heart, wrapped in wool and wards and a promise, a fox who wanted to be a baker waited patiently for the moon to turn.
