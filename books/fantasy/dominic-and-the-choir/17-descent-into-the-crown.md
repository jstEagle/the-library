## 17. Descent into the Crown

The rite began at moonrise, in the hollow beneath the palace chapel where the Choir had hidden for three days among the bones of forgotten kings.

Theo knelt at the center of a ring of singers, nine of them, grey-robed and barefoot on cold stone. Sera Vane stood opposite her, hands raised, and Bram was there too—young Bram with his cracked voice and his stubborn kindness—and Theo tried not to look at him because looking made the fear worse. If she looked at him she would think about coming back, and thinking about coming back would make her hesitate inside the king's mind, and hesitation was how the Regent ate.

*Sing,* Sera had told her that afternoon, when they'd walked through the plan one last time. *When you feel yourself fraying, sing anything. Your mother's song if you have it. The Regent can't digest what's being sung; it slides past him like water through a fist.*

*I don't remember my mother's song,* Theo had said.

*You do,* Sera said gently. *It's only buried. Dig.*

Now the nine voices rose around her, low at first, a sound like wind moving through a canyon, and the air above the stone floor began to change. It thickened. Shimmered. A door formed where no door had been—a tall arch of pale gold light, standing free, casting shadows that fell the wrong way.

Through it, Theo could see a corridor. Marble floors. Tapestries she somehow recognized without ever having seen them. And at the far end, faint as a candle behind fog, a throne room glowing with a sickly amber light.

"The door opens onto his memory of this place," Sera said, her voice steady even as sweat beaded on her brow. Singing took everything; Theo could see the effort dragging at all nine of them. "The mindscape is Aldric's palace as he remembers it—which is to say, as he *knew* it, forty years ago. It will not match the waking palace. Trust what your eyes tell you."

"And the Regent?"

"Eats outward from the throne room. The center is death. The edges are still him—the man he was. Find the man. The man can unmake the crown." Sera lowered her hands, and her voice dropped, went private, went raw. "I failed him once, thief. I stood where you're standing and I ran. Don't run."

"You didn't fail," Theo said, surprising herself. "You survived. That's different."

Sera laughed—short, wet, real. "Go," she said. "*Go.*"

The choir swelled. The arch flared white. Theo stepped through.

* * *

Falling. That was the first thing—three heartbeats of falling, stomach-lurching, endless—and then her boots struck marble and she staggered, caught herself against a wall, and looked up into a palace that took her breath away.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. She had expected ruin, had braced for rot and shadow, but this corridor gleamed. Banners hung from rafters of dark oak, deep green shot through with silver thread. Windows tall as trees let in light that came from no sun she could name—just warmth, diffuse and golden, everywhere at once. The air smelled of beeswax and cedar and, underneath it, something older: rain on hot stone, woodsmoke, a horse's breath in winter. The smell of a life.

And everywhere, woven into the very stones, she heard singing. Not the Choir's singing. This was quieter, ambient, like a house humming to itself—a mind at rest, murmuring its own contents. Snatches of melody drifted down the corridors. Voices half-heard: a woman laughing, a man saying *again, again*, a child demanding a story. Aldric's memories, alive and going about their business, unaware that anything was wrong.

For one vertiginous moment Theo understood what it would be like to walk through another person the way she walked through houses at night. To know someone from the inside. Her trade had always been theft—quick fingers, quick exits, nothing held. But this—

This was being *invited*. Or as close as a mind could come to inviting anything.

She started walking.

The corridor bent as she moved, which she'd been warned about. Minds were not architecture; they were habit and feeling wearing architecture's clothes. Corridors here led where Aldric's attention wanted them to lead. So she kept her purpose fixed in her thoughts, sharp-edged, a blade she refused to set down: *the man, find the man, the man before the crown.* The hallway unspooled ahead of her, obedient.

But the walls were already starting to die.

She noticed it first as a sound—a soft crumble, like bread torn. Then she saw it: at the far end of the gallery, a section of wall sagging inward, the marble gone grey and porous as old bone. As she watched, a window folded shut like an eye closing. The golden light dimmed by a fraction. And from somewhere below—far below, deeper than any cellar—the amber glow of the throne room pulsed once, slowly, like the throat of something swallowing.

The Regent was eating. Had always been eating. Forty years of slow consumption, course after course, and now it had reached the upper rooms. The banners nearest the dead section were faded, their silver thread tarnished brown. When Theo passed beneath them, she felt them flicker—felt the memories they held *notice* her, lean toward her like plants toward sun.

She pulled her hood up. Old habit. Useless habit—there was no one here to hide from except everything.

"Keep moving," she said aloud, just to hear a voice that was hers.

The gallery opened into a great hall, and here the damage was worse. Half the hall was intact: long tables laid for a feast that had never ended, candles burning with patient flames, ghost-light guests seated and laughing at jokes Theo couldn't hear. The other half was gone—not ruined but *unmade*, dissolved into a pearly mist that hung in the air like breath in winter. Where the mist touched the surviving half, colors drained. A table leg went grey, then translucent, then wasn't.

And in the doorway between the living half and the dying half stood a servant.

He wore palace livery, silver and green. His face was kind and forgettable and utterly blank—no, not blank. *Wiped.* His eyes were the color of the mist. He did not move. He did not breathe. He simply stood, and as Theo watched, he slowly turned his head toward her, and his mouth opened, and what came out was the Regent's voice—that voice she knew from the vials and the visions, silk over grinding stone.

"You came."

"I came," Theo said. Her hand found the knife at her belt out of pure reflex, and she almost laughed at how pointless it was. You couldn't cut a memory.

"We wrote you into the book a hundred years ago," the servant said pleasantly. "Did they tell you that? Did the little grey birds tell you what you are?" His head tilted. "A door that walks. A delivery that delivers itself."

"I'm not listening to you."

"No," he agreed. "You're walking toward me. Which is the same thing." He smiled with the wiped mouth. "Dinner is nearly served, little thief. We've been hungry so long."

Then the mist surged—not at her, past her, rolling up the corridor behind her, and where it passed, the great hall's laughter cut off mid-note. The feast-guests flickered like snuffed candles. The Regent wasn't even bothering to stop her. It was just eating while she walked, casual as a farmer reaping, and every step she took forward was a step through a kingdom being swallowed from the inside.

She ran.

* * *

The corridors beyond the hall were worse and stranger. Time pooled unevenly here. She passed through a winter that lasted ten steps—snow swirling in a stairwell, and a young man's voice shouting joyfully somewhere above, *the river's frozen, come and see*—and then emerged into a summer garden grown wild inside a dining room. She crossed a bridge over a canal that was actually, impossibly, a memory of a coronation: crowds roaring on either bank, and a younger Aldric riding past her so close she could have touched his knee, twenty-five years old and laughing, crowned and terrified and radiant.

She stopped. She couldn't help it.

Up close, the young king's face was open in a way no living face was. Every thought showed. He glanced toward the crowds and she saw him cataloguing them—not as subjects but as *names*, hundreds of names, and she realized with a lurch that this was what the man had been: someone who remembered everyone. A king who kept his whole kingdom in his head like a song he refused to drop a note of.

That was why the Regent had chosen him, she thought. Not despite that. *Because* of it. A mind that full was a feast that never ended.

The coronation-memory rode on past her, oblivious. The canal-bridge shivered. Somewhere beneath her feet, deep in the structure of the mind, something groaned—a long, structural groan, the sound a ship makes in a storm—and a crack raced across the sky of the mindscape, thin and bright as a hairline fracture in porcelain.

*Hurry*, said the palace itself, in no words at all.

She hurried.

The stairs to the throne room were guarded by two figures, and Theo's heart seized before her mind caught up: inquisitors, grey-and-black, masks of beaten tin. Then she saw they were faded at the edges, worn soft as old coins. Memories of fear. Guards Aldric had posted at his own nightmare, or nightmares that had posted themselves. They reached for her as she passed and she ducked between them—they were slow, secondhand things, fear remembered rather than felt—and their grasping hands closed on the space she'd left.

At the top of the stairs stood the doors.

They were enormous, black iron banded with silver, and they were the first truly *real* thing she'd seen since entering—the rest of the palace had been memory-warm, dream-soft, but these doors had weight, presence, the density of fact. Carved across both panels, deep enough to bleed shadow, was a single word:

*REMEMBER*

And beneath the doors, seeping out through the seams like light under a cellar door, glowed that amber light. From behind the iron came a sound she felt in her teeth more than heard: chewing. Slow. Patient. Enormous.

The last stretch of corridor before the doors was already half-eaten. The mist had taken the ceiling; stars of the mindscape's false sky showed through gaps, and they were going out one by one, each star some memory of Aldric's winking dark. Theo counted five go dark while she stood there. She thought of the Choir below, nine voices holding the door open, spending themselves to keep her path home from closing. She thought of Bram's face that morning, trying to be brave and failing and being kind about the failure.

She thought of a burning house, and a woman singing, and a vial in her coat pocket that held the first four years of her life.

"Right," Theo Blackwood said to the emptying corridor, to the dying stars, to the thing behind the door that had steered her whole life like a cart-horse with a bit in her teeth. "You've eaten everything else."

She put her shoulder against the word *REMEMBER* and pushed.

"It's my turn to take something back."

The doors swung inward on silence, and the amber light poured over her like honey, and far below—so far below, in the waking world, in the chapel crypt—she felt rather than heard the Choir's song falter, just once, as every singer at once saw what she saw.

Seated on the throne, wearing the king's body like an ill-fitted coat, the Regent lifted its head and smiled with Aldric's stolen mouth.

"There you are," it said. "We've kept your seat warm."

Behind the throne, chained in chains of his own forgetting, the true king raised his head—and his eyes, for one flickering instant, were lucid, and human, and full of a terror that was not for himself.

"*Run*," Aldric mouthed.

And the doors slammed shut behind her.
