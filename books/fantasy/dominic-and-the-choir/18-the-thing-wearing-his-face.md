## 18. The Thing Wearing His Face

The throne room of Aldric's mindscape was the most beautiful thing Theo had ever seen, and that was precisely why she should have run.

Every corridor behind her had been collapsing—hallways of his boyhood eaten down to white nothing, the memory of a garden rotted to gray lace—but this room blazed. Light poured through windows that remembered a sun. Banners hung from rafters carved with oak leaves still green after thirty years. And at the center, on a dais of pale stone, sat a crown on a cushion of crimson velvet, turning slowly, as if stirred by breath.

No king wore it. That absence should have told her everything.

"You came," said a voice behind her.

Theo spun, knife already in her hand—a habit so deep it lived in her body even here, where flesh was only a courtesy. The man standing by the far window was tall and broad-shouldered, with dark hair going silver at the temples and a face like weathered oak: kind around the eyes, worn at the mouth. She knew him from the relic, from the vision in the waystation, from the few minutes of lucidity in the palace above. King Aldric. But not quite. The light didn't touch him right. It bent around his shoulders the way water bends around a stone, refusing to land.

"You're not him," Theo said.

"No," it agreed, pleasantly. "He's sleeping. He sleeps more every year." It drifted toward the dais—not walking, exactly; the space between one place and another simply agreed to contain it—and settled onto the throne's arm like a cat claiming a chair it had owned longer than the house. "But you know what I am. You've known since the ice. Since Sera Vane first sang you the prophecy in that cold little hall. You've been walking toward me for months, thief. I only thought we ought to finally be introduced."

"The Regent."

"At your service." It inclined its head, wearing Aldric's face with the easy competence of long practice. "Or perhaps I should say—at *my* service. Yours, I mean. You've been in my service rather a long time. You simply weren't told."

The words slid past her the way all lies slide past her—she'd heard ten thousand of them in Thornfell's back rooms, had learned to feel their texture like counterfeit coin. But this one was warm. This one fit her hand.

"Prophecies," Theo said carefully, "are songs. Sera told me. Songs about things that are already true."

"Mm. And who taught the Choir to sing?" The Regent smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was terrible because it was gentle. "A century ago, there was a singer who dreamed of a girl with a thief's hands standing before a crowned figure. She wrote it down because dreams that vivid demand ink. What she never asked herself was why the dream chose her. Why that night. Why the moon stood where it stood." It lifted one finger, and somewhere above them—in the sky of this mind, beyond the painted ceiling—the light shifted, and Theo's stomach dropped, because she could see through the ceiling to the actual moon, hanging fat and white and nearly whole, one sliver short of full. One turn remaining. "I have been dreaming into sleepers for three hundred years, Theo Blackwood. A prophecy is just a trap written in rhyme. And you are the mouse who read the cheese."

* * *

She did not remember deciding to move, but she was moving—back toward the door, knife low, the way Vess had drilled into her in the guildhouse yard when she was nine: *never show them your spine, girl, never give them your back.* But the doorway wasn't there anymore. Where the great doors had stood there was only wall, seamless, hung with tapestries depicting a coronation she recognized from the relic—Aldric young and terrified, kneeling before an archbishop whose face was smudged, unfinished, forgotten.

"None of that," the Regent said mildly. "We're having a conversation. I've been looking forward to it for a very long time. Do you know how tedious centuries are? Kings are such small meals. A memory here, a name there, sipped across decades. But you—" It rose, drifting closer, and up close its borrowed face began to slip at the edges, features swimming like reflection in disturbed water. "You were raised in the dark, fed on other people's brightest hours, taught that remembering is weakness and belonging is a leash. Do you understand what that makes you? Not a weapon. A *vessel*. Fresh memories keep poorly, thief. They curdle if stored too long. But carried—" Its voice dropped to something almost tender. "Carried, they stay warm. Carried, they arrive believing they're still loved."

Theo's mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, because this wasn't her body. "The memories I took. Every job. Every—"

"Delivered beautifully. Thank you. The widow's kiss was exquisite—I do love grief, it seasons everything. The merchant's childhood dog. The smuggler's mother's voice. All of it passed through your hands and arrived at my table without you ever once wondering where the forgetting went." It spread its hands, Aldric's scarred knuckles, the signet ring he'd never removed even in dreams. "The guilds think they sell memories. I let them think it. Men guard what they believe is theirs, even their sins. But nothing taken in Thornfell stays in Thornfell, child. Everything runs downhill, and I am at the bottom of everything."

Her mind was doing what it always did under pressure—going cold, going fast, sorting exits that didn't exist. "Sera knew. The Choir. Did they know?"

"Ah." Something flickered across the stolen face—hunger, or satisfaction; in that face they were hard to tell apart. "Now we arrive at the part I've most anticipated. Ask me what happened to the last thief they sent."

"There wasn't one."

"Wasn't there?"

And the room changed.

* * *

The banners burned.

Not metaphorically—this was a mind, everything was both metaphor and horribly literal—the tapestries caught with a sound like paper breathing flame, and the light through the windows went orange, and the floor beneath Theo's feet became floorboards, warped and hot, and smoke rolled thick and sweet across the throne room until she couldn't see the dais, couldn't see the crown, could only hear the Regent's voice moving around her like something circling in reeds.

"Do you recognize it yet? Give it a moment. You will."

She did. Of course she did. Her body recognized it first—her lungs tightening, her palms sweating, some animal archive deeper than memory opening doors she'd welded shut. The heat pattern was wrong for a throne room. It was right for a kitchen. For a narrow stair. For a bedroom above a bakery with flour dust in the corners of the ceiling, and a window that stuck, and a woman's voice on the other side of the wall—

"*Hush now, little ember, the moon has come to bed—*"

Theo stopped walking. Stood in the burning dark of a dead woman's song and felt the trap close around her with a click she felt in her teeth.

"Yes," the Regent breathed, delighted, everywhere. "*There* it is. Oh, I've waited years to watch your face. The house was real, Theo. The fire was real. The woman singing was real. Your mother. And do you know why the Choir found you, all those years later, in a city you'd fled to specifically to be forgettable? Why Bram's training sessions cracked you open? Why the rehearsal tore that exact fragment loose—fire, song, the one memory strong enough to make you *hungry* for the rest?" The smoke parted. The Regent stood six feet away, wearing Aldric's compassion like a mask over a mask over a mask. "Because I fed it to you. Crumb by crumb. A burning house in a rehearsal. A vial in Vess's keeping. I built your longing the way a cook builds an appetite—because a thief who doesn't care can be redirected, but a thief who *wants*, a thief starving for her own name—she walks exactly where you point her and calls it destiny."

The floor was cool again. The fire was gone. That was somehow worse—that it could take it away, that her mother's burning had been scenery, adjustable, a prop in someone else's play.

"The prophecy," Theo said. Her voice came out flat. Dead calm. The voice she used when lifting. "The thirteenth turn. The kingdom devoured. Was any of it true?"

"Oh, all of it. I never lie about the important things—it ruins the flavor of the deception." The Regent circled her slowly, robes whispering where Aldric would have had legs. "The moon turns tomorrow night. My hunger is nearly bottomless—your kind keeps *forgetting*, it's your one talent—and when the feast begins I will eat this kingdom down to its bedrock. Every name. Every love. Every grief and lullaby and grudge, until Thornfell and the capital and every village between are smiling blank-eyed servants asking why they came into the room." It paused beside her, close enough that she could smell it—cold stone and old honey, the smell of the palace corridors where no one remembered anything. "Unless, of course, the foretold thief reaches me first. Unless she stands where you're standing and does the brave, tragic, prophesied thing. Fights me. Grapples with me for what she loves. Clings."

"Clings," Theo repeated.

"I feed on held things." Its voice went soft as snowfall. "Memories clutched tightest are richest. A memory surrendered freely—given away, sung out into other mouths—has no purchase. No grip. Nothing to devour. Which is why, in a hundred years of dreaming singers into prophecy, I made very sure none of them ever learned that particular verse." It smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was proud. "You're going to fight me, Theo Blackwood. Tomorrow, under the thirteenth turn, you're going to stand in this room and refuse to let go of the vial, of the burning house, of your mother's voice—and you're going to pour yourself into my mouth piece by piece believing you're winning, right up until the moment there isn't enough of you left to lose. And then I will wear your courage out of this room and into the world, and I will finish my meal, and the prophecy will come true exactly as written, because *I wrote it*. You aren't the hero of this story, little thief. You're the delivery boy. And you've finally made your final delivery."

* * *

Silence.

The throne room held its breath around her—banners, light, the slow-turning crown—all of it waiting, the way a spiderweb waits, for her to struggle.

Theo looked at the crown.

She thought about Vess saying *I'm doing this to protect you,* and meaning it. About Sera Vane's guilt, real and heavy, but arriving suspiciously on schedule. About Bram sharing bread in the snow without a single word of lecture, and how that kindness had been the one thing in months that hadn't felt steered—or maybe it had, maybe even bread was bait, maybe the Regent had learned long ago that the surest way to move Theo Blackwood was not fear but *belonging*.

Maybe. Maybe every hand extended to her had been a glove on a marionette's wire.

But standing in the wreck of her own destiny, Theo found that the terror she expected—the vertigo of a life unmoored—didn't come. Because she had spent twenty years being nobody, trusting nobody, owning nothing, and she had survived it, and what the Regent had never understood—what none of them understood, guild or crown or choir—was that a person with nothing to lose is the one thing in the world that cannot be threatened.

It could only be lied to.

"You're wrong about one thing," Theo said.

The Regent tilted Aldric's head, indulgent. "Am I?"

"You said I'd cling." She reached into her coat—to her body's habits, though here the coat was made of whatever she believed about herself—and drew out the vial. Small. Glass. Swirling faintly gold, like lamplight trapped in honey: her earliest memory, her mother's voice, the song before the fire. She'd carried it since the inquisitors' keep. Had slept with it against her heart. Had told herself it was evidence, leverage, a key.

It had been a leash. She saw that now. Saw the whole shape of it—the dream-singer a century dead, the guild that raised a girl to carry warmth without keeping any, the Choir that needed a vessel and found a volunteer, the Regent that needed a courier and wrote her a prophecy to make the delivery feel like glory.

"You need me to hold on," Theo said. "So here's my counteroffer."

The Regent's smile faltered—one degree, one hairline crack in the borrowed face—and Theo, connoisseur of faces, collector of expressions, catalogued it with savage satisfaction.

Above them, beyond the painted ceiling, the moon hung one sliver short of full, and somewhere far below, muffled by stone and snow and the walls of a dying king's mind, she could just barely hear it: voices. Harmonized. Grey-robed, stubborn, impossible people, already singing the opening measures of a door.

They had come early. Or—they had come exactly on time, and only the Regent believed otherwise.

The thing wearing the king's face turned toward the sound, and for the first time in three hundred years, it sounded afraid.

"What are you doing?" it demanded. "What are you—*thief*—what did you *do*—"

Theo Blackwood smiled, opened her hand, and let the vial fall toward the floor of a memory that was no longer hers to keep.

"*Gave it away,*" she said.
