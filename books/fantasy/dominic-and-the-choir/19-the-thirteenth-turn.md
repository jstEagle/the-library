## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon hung above the palace like a coin about to be spent.

Theo stood in the collapsing corridor of Aldric's mindscape and watched it through a window that was not a window—the thirteenth turn beginning its slow climb to fullness over the memory of a city that had once been whole. Below her, far beneath the frozen architecture of the king's mind, she could feel the Choir singing. Not hearing it exactly. Feeling it, the way you feel a heartbeat through a floor: Sera Vane's voice at the center of it, Bram's bright thread woven through, dozens of grey-robed singers in the Moonturn square below the palace, their harmony rising like heat.

And between her and them, filling the throne room at the end of the corridor like a tide filling a drowned house, was the Regent.

It wore the king's face, but it wore it badly now—features sliding, eyes too deep, the mouth opening wider than a mouth should when it smiled.

"You understand," it said, and its voice was every voice Theo had ever forgotten, every name she'd ever lifted from a stranger's head, "that you came here for me."

"I came here for him." She nodded at the figure slumped on the throne behind the Regent—Aldric, what remained of him, a man worn down to a sketch of himself, his crown fused to his skull by decades of slow consumption.

"You came because I wrote you into a book a hundred years before you were born." The Regent drifted closer, and where it passed, the corridor's memories dimmed—wallpaper fading, a child's laughter thinning to silence. "I needed a thief, little one. Kings are guarded against armies, against assassins, against sorcery. But no one guards against a girl with empty hands who only wants to *belong*. I made the prophecy. I let the Choir find it. I let Vess find it. Every hand that pushed you toward this throne was mine."

Theo had known. Some part of her had known since the archive, since the date written in dust. Knowing didn't make it hurt less. It made it hurt in a cleaner place, which was almost worse.

"Then you know how this ends," she said. "You get your feast. You eat whatever I love most, and then you finish eating him, and then the kingdom."

"Yes." The word came out tender, almost loving. "Give me the childhood. The burning house. The woman singing. Give me the vial you stole back from my servants, and the years inside it, and the name your mother sang over your cradle. Give me those, and I will leave you the rest—your guild years, your cleverness, your cold little competence. You'll walk out of here whole enough. You'll barely miss it. No one misses what they can't remember missing."

And that was the trap, wasn't it. That was the elegant hinge of it. The Regent didn't need to fight her. It only needed to wait for her to clutch, because everything that clutches can be pried open.

*Everything you value most,* Sera had told her, weeks ago, beside a fire in the mountains. *That is the price of the door.*

Theo looked down at her hands. They were steady. They had always been steady; that was the first thing anyone ever said about her work. *Steady hands, that Blackwood girl.* Steady hands, and nothing worth holding in them, because holding things was how you got hurt, and taking things was how you got paid, and keeping things—keeping anything—was the one trade she'd never learned.

She thought about the vial in her coat pocket. Glass no bigger than her thumb, stoppered with wax, and inside it swam a smear of gold light: her own earliest memory, harvested from her by the Regent's servants when she was small enough to be carried out of a burning house. She had killed two men to take it back. She had crossed a kingdom with it against her heart like a second pulse. She had wanted it so badly she could taste it, the way you taste blood.

She thought about Bram sharing bread in the snow without asking a single question. About Sera's voice cracking on the word *again*. About a woman singing, somewhere in the fire, a song Theo had spent her whole life not remembering.

"You're right," Theo said.

The Regent's smile widened.

"It was all steered," she went on. "Every step. The book, the road, the Choir, the door. You built the whole maze and walked me down it. And here's the thing about mazes, your majesty—" she took a breath, and found, strangely, that it didn't shake, "—you built yours around one assumption. That I would rather lose everything else than lose myself. That a thief always keeps something back. That's what thieving *is*, isn't it? Keeping something back."

"That is what you are," the Regent said, and for the first time something flickered under its voice—not doubt, not yet, but attention. The attention of a thing that has fed for a century on certainty.

"No," Theo said. "I'm what you made me instead. Someone with nothing left to protect. You should have checked your arithmetic, Regent. You gave me to the guilds when I was six. There was never any self left to save."

She reached into her coat and drew out the vial.

* * *

Far below, in the Moonturn square, Sera Vane felt the change before she understood it.

The Choir stood in a ring of grey robes and bare throats, two hundred voices braided into the Unbinding Rite, singing the thirteenth turn up the sky. Their song was a door held open. It had been designed, a century ago by singers braver and more foolish than any of them, as a weapon: a harmonic blade to cut into a king's mind and drag the Regent into the open where it could be starved or slain. Sera had sung that version once before, eleven years ago, and it had failed, and a singer named Ollen had died with the melody still in his throat, and she had carried the failure like a stone in her chest ever since.

But tonight they were not singing a blade. Tonight, after Theo had walked out into the snow and come back and apologized—not for leaving, but for assuming—they had rewritten the rite together in three sleepless days. Because Theo had asked a question none of them had ever thought to ask: *what if we don't cut the door? What if we open it?*

"What if I don't fight it?" she'd said, standing in the waystation kitchen with flour on her hands because Bram had insisted she learn to bake bread, of all things. "What if I give? Everything. Freely. Before it can be taken. Can the Regent feed on a gift?"

Sera had gone very still. Memory-singers knew the old laws the way farmers knew weather. A stolen thing could be consumed forever. A lost thing could be consumed forever. But a given thing—a thing surrendered freely, witnessed, named aloud, sung back into the world—

A gift did not vanish. A gift *passed*. And the Regent could only devour what belonged to no one else.

Now, in the square, Sera raised her arms, and two hundred voices shifted key, and the blade became a bridge.

* * *

In the corridor of the dying mind, Theo unstoppered the vial.

Gold light spilled over her fingers—warm, impossibly warm, warm as a kitchen, warm as a voice. For one moment she saw it entire: a low room with a crooked beam. A woman kneeling by a hearth, dark hair escaping its braid, singing a lullaby in a language Theo didn't know but recognized in her marrow. A man laughing low so as not to wake someone. Smoke. Then smoke. Then only smoke.

Her mother's face. Her father's laugh. Her own small self, lifted out of the burning house by hands she couldn't see, and the song still hanging in the air behind her, unfinished.

"There she is," the Regent whispered. Hunger rolled off it like heat off a forge. The corridor groaned; walls of memory buckled inward; somewhere above them the palace of Aldric's past shed another wing into darkness. "There it is. One hundred and thirty years I have waited to hold that song. Give it to me, thief. Set it down gently, and I swear by the hollow crown you'll keep the rest—"

"I'm not giving it to you."

The hunger stilled.

Theo turned from the throne room, from the Regent, from the thing wearing the king's face, and faced the window-that-was-not-a-window, where the thirteenth moon climbed toward fullness and the Choir's song rose through the stone like water rising through a well.

She held the vial up to the moonlight.

"To the Choir below," she said clearly. "To Sera, who failed once and stayed anyway. To Bram, who never asked me a single question I wasn't ready to answer, and asked the ones I was. To every grey robe in that square, and every singer who came before them and died trying. I give you my earliest memory: my mother's kitchen, my mother's song, my mother's face. I give it freely. Witness it. Keep it. Sing it back into the world."

She tipped the vial.

The gold light poured out—and instead of falling, it *fell upward*, streaming out the window into the night, down the miles of frozen air, into the mouths of two hundred singers who caught it mid-verse and wove it into the rite. Sera, below, felt it land in her chest like a struck bell, and heard the lullaby rise unbidden through two hundred throats in a language older than Thornfell, older than the crown, and wept without breaking the harmony.

Inside the mindscape, the Regent screamed.

Not with pain—with *hunger denied*. It lunged for the fading light and closed its hands on nothing. The gift was already elsewhere. Already held. Already being sung back into the world, note by note, in voices that would carry it to children not yet born. There was nothing left to steal. There was nothing left to eat.

"What—" The king's face slid sideways on the thing's skull. "What did you DO—"

"My childhood," Theo said. "I give my childhood. The burning house. The smoke. The running. All of it—witnessed, kept, returned. Take nothing. There's nothing here that's mine alone anymore."

Memory tore out of her like ribbons off a maypole. She didn't fight it. That was the terrible, holy strangeness of it—she didn't fight, and so it didn't hurt, or it hurt the way birth hurts, the way any passage hurts that is also an arrival. She felt the fire go. She felt the smoke go. She felt the woman's voice go—but it went *outward*, into the song below, where two hundred throats caught it and gave it back, and so it was not gone. Nothing given away is ever gone. That was the law the Regent had never understood, because the Regent had been built from hoarding, from hunger, from the belief that everything loved must eventually be swallowed to be safe.

"The vial," Theo said, and hurled the empty glass against the wall, where it burst into frost. "My guild years—I give the first theft, the rain, the merchant's kiss. I give Vess—her kindness and her betrayal both; let the world remember her whole, not just her ruin. I give my fear of the Choir. I give my loneliness. I give the name I hid behind—Blackwood, Winter, the girl with steady hands and nothing in them."

Each offering struck the rite below like a coal landing in snow, hissing into music.

The Regent shrank.

It tried to hold its shape—it gathered itself, swelled, threw the king's face at her one last time, mouth wide, wide, wide—and Theo stood in front of it and opened her arms, and offered the last thing.

"And my name," she said. "The one my mother sang. The one I never got to know. Dominic—whatever it means, whoever I was supposed to be—I give it to the world. Let them sing it. Let it belong to everyone. Then there's nothing left in me you can claim, Regent. Nothing held. Nothing hidden. Nothing *hoarded*."

The moon reached fullness.

The thirteenth turn completed itself above the palace, and every bell in the capital rang at once, though no hand touched them, and in the Moonturn square the Choir hit the final chord of the rite—a chord that had no resolution, because it wasn't meant to end, only to continue—and the sound went up through stone and snow and bone and broke over the throne room like dawn.

The Regent unraveled.

It did not die dramatically. That was the last mercy of it. It simply came apart the way fog comes apart when the sun insists—threads of it peeling loose, each thread a stolen thing finding its way home, a hundred years of devoured memories streaming out of the dissolving shape like birds startled from a burned forest, pouring down through the mindscape, out through the window, into the world. Somewhere a servant in the palace blinked and remembered her daughter's name. Somewhere an old soldier remembered why he'd kept a ribbon in his pocket for forty years. Somewhere a king began, very slowly, to fill back in.

The last of the Regent—the hungry heart of it, the original greed—tried to flee deeper into Aldric's skull, into whatever crevice might still hide it. But the corridors were singing now. The whole ruined palace of the king's mind rang with the Choir's chord, and there was no silence left anywhere for a hoarder to hide in.

It came apart mid-lunge, reaching for Theo with the king's beautiful, ruined hands.

It starved reaching.

* * *

Silence, afterward—or not silence, but the enormous quiet that lives underneath music, the way the sea lives underneath a wave.

Theo stood alone in the throne room. The walls had stopped collapsing. Light came through windows that remembered themselves, gilding a room she somehow knew had once been painted with orchards. On the throne, King Aldric breathed—one breath, then another, each one deeper than the last, color climbing back into his face like dawn climbing a wall.

His eyes opened. They were clear. Clearer than any eyes she'd seen in the palace, clearer than her own had been an hour ago.

He looked at her for a long time.

"I know you," he said at last, and his voice was rusty, a hinge unused for decades. "Or—I knew you. Before. In the part of me it hadn't eaten yet. The girl from the prophecy. The thief." He tried to rise; managed only to lift a hand; let it fall. "You gave—everything. I felt it go through me like a river. Child, do you even know what you—"

"No," Theo said honestly. Her voice sounded strange to her. Thin. New. Like a room after the furniture has been carried out. "No, I don't. I think I gave away more than I planned to. I think some things went that weren't on the list."

Grief arrived, distantly, the way news arrives from a far country. She couldn't find the fire anymore. Couldn't find her mother's face—only the fact of a face, the idea of one, held safely now in two hundred strangers' throats. Her own name was a word she recognized the way you recognize a coat in a photograph: *yes, that was mine once.* She was, by every measure that had ever mattered to her, empty.

And she was not afraid.

That was the discovery that undid her, more than the loss did. Standing in a stranger's mind, stripped to the studs, she felt—light. Not hollow. Light. Everything she'd surrendered was still *somewhere*, being held by people who sang, people who had promised, people who would spend the rest of their lives giving it back to the world piece by piece. She had spent twenty-three years believing that keeping was the only safety. She had just bet her entire self on the opposite, and the wager had come back true.

Footsteps on the stair. Many of them.

They came up through the singing corridors—grey robes, snow in their hair, Sera Vane in the lead with her voice worn ragged and her eyes blazing, and Bram behind her, and behind them singers and more singers, filling the throne room of a king who had waited a century to be seen.

Bram reached her first. Looked at her—at the emptiness in her, which Bram, of all people, would see most clearly—and didn't lecture, didn't fuss, didn't ask a single question. Just took off their own scarf and put it around her neck, and pressed a heel of bread into her hands, saved from the waystation, gone hard as scripture, and said:

"Welcome back, whoever you are."

Theo laughed. It cracked in the middle. It turned into something else halfway through, and she let it.

Sera knelt in front of her, took her hands—steady hands, still steady, always steady—and said, "We have it. All of it. Every note you gave us is safe, and we'll sing it back to you as long as it takes, and if some of it never comes home—" her voice caught, held, went on, "—then it will live with us, and you will never once have to be without it. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain."

"I know," Theo said. "I remember making it." A beat. "Mostly."

Above them, through the high windows of the restored throne room, the thirteenth moon began, very slowly, to wane—and in its light, on the throne, King Aldric watched the girl the prophecies had argued over for a hundred years stand surrounded by singers, empty-handed, full-hearted, belonging at last to everyone.

He opened his mouth to speak—to thank her, to ask her name.

And Theo realized, with a jolt that ran from her scalp to her heels, that she did not know what she would say.

Because the name her mother had sung—the name she'd thrown to the world like a torch, certain she'd never want it back—was already moving. Already being passed from throat to throat around the throne room, two hundred voices learning it, testing it, giving it weight and warmth it had never had before. She could hear it coming toward her through the choir like a wave coming toward shore.

And she had no idea, yet, what it would mean to hear it spoken *to* her—as hers—by the first person who ever said it.
