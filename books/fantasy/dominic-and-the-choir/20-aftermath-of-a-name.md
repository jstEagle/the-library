## 20. Aftermath of a Name

The first thing Aldric knew was the weight of his own breath.

For eleven years it had not been his—had been borrowed, rationed, doled out by something that wore his face to its own satisfaction. Now each inhale was clumsy, greedy, human. His chest ached as though he had run a great distance, though he had not stood in what felt like lifetimes.

He was lying on cold stone beneath a window, and through the window came moonlight so bright and so wrong that for a moment he thought the world had caught fire. Then he understood: the thirteenth moon had turned, and turned *empty*, and the light falling across him was only light—the plain, unremarkable silver of an ordinary moon finishing an ordinary arc over the capital—and yet he wept at the sight of it like a man shown water after a decade in the desert.

*Ordinary*, he thought. *Let everything be ordinary. Let me never again mistake the extraordinary for salvation.*

He tried to sit up and failed. Hands found his shoulders—grey-robed hands, steady, singing something low under their breath that was less melody than scaffolding, holding him together the way timbers hold a wall. A woman's voice said, "Slowly, Majesty. You've been a house with the hearth burned out. The stones need time."

"I remember," Aldric said, and stopped, astonished.

He remembered. Not everything—not nearly—but the shape of himself, the outline where a self had been, and within that outline whole rooms suddenly lit: his mother's garden, the smell of ink on his father's ledgers, the first time he'd stood before the assembled court and felt terror rather than triumph. The Regent had eaten outward from the center for years, hollowing him room by room, and he had learned not to look at the doors it closed. Now those doors stood open, even if the rooms behind some of them were bare.

"The girl," he said. "The thief. Where is she?"

"Alive." It was the Choir's leader—Vane, Sera Vane; her name surfaced from somewhere deep and unspoiled, a pond below the frost line. Her grey eyes were red-rimmed, her voice scraped raw from hours of song. "Sleeping. Emptied, mostly. She gave—" Vane's throat worked. "She gave everything freely, Majesty. We sang what we could back into the world so it couldn't be devoured, but a gift freely given can't always be returned intact."

Aldric lay back and stared at the ceiling beams, and understood, with a clarity that felt like a second crown being set upon him, exactly what had been done in his name. In his defense. For him—a king who had begged a stranger, minutes before the end, not to spend herself on his account.

"And she is—" He could not finish. Kings learned early to finish sentences. He found he no longer wished to be that kind of king.

"She will wake," Vane said. "What she wakes *as*—that is not mine to promise. That belongs to whatever she carries out the other side."

* * *

They moved him, in the days that followed, from the ruined throne room to a narrow chamber in the palace's east wing—one of the few places the Regent's rot had never fully reached, because no one of consequence had remembered it existed. The irony did not escape him. For eleven years he had ruled from a throne at the center of a web of forgetting, and now his recovery began in a servant's room with a cracked washbasin and a window facing the kitchens, where the smell of bread rose every morning like a small, stubborn argument against despair.

The palace itself was worse than he had feared.

He walked it slowly, leaning on Bram's arm—young Bram, the singer with ink-stained fingers and a habit of humming when nervous, who had appointed himself Aldric's escort and would not be dissuaded. Together they catalogued the damage the way survivors catalogue a flood: what could be salvaged, what must be rebuilt, what was simply gone.

Servants wandered the corridors with blank, pleasant faces. Some of them had been forgotten by the Regent's feeding and were only now remembering their own names, haltingly, like children reciting lessons. A chambermaid burst into tears in the west gallery when she recalled she had a daughter in Harrowmere. A steward stood in the middle of the counting room for an hour because he had come there for a reason neither he nor the room retained.

"There are rooms," Bram said quietly, "where no one remembers why they exist. There's a door on the third floor that three hundred people walk past every day and not one of them has ever opened it. We think it was the nursery."

Aldric stopped walking.

"The nursery," he repeated. His daughter. His son. The names rose in him whole—Elira, Tomas—and with them a tide of memory so fierce he had to grip the wall. But beneath the names, beneath the warmth, there was a hole, smooth-edged as a stone pulled from a riverbed. He could remember loving them. He could not remember—

"Bram," he said carefully, "do I have grandchildren?"

The young singer looked away. "We're finding out," he said. "It's taking time. People are coming forward. There's a woman in the river provinces who says your son wrote to her every season until six years ago, and then the letters stopped, and she thought—" Bram swallowed. "She thought she'd imagined knowing him at all."

Aldric stood in the corridor a long moment, hand flat against the stone, feeling the building breathe around him—the way old palaces do, all settling weight and held breath.

"Then we start," he said, "with letters."

* * *

The memories Theo had carried out came to him in fragments, and they came strangely—not as recollections of his own but as *objects*, as though she had packed them in her mind the way she once packed stolen goods: wrapped, labeled, carried carefully through dark country.

He understood now what the Regent had truly wanted from him. Not his kingdom. Kingdoms were merely larders. It had wanted the *harvest*—every forgotten thing in Thornfell, every memory abandoned by grief or age or deliberate cruelty—and it had used him, a crowned man at the center of a web of roads and taxes and courts, as the spider uses the still point of the web. Everything forgotten anywhere flowed eventually toward the crown. Toward him.

And the girl had walked into that web and given the spider nothing, because she had already given it all away.

On the fourth morning, Sera Vane brought him a shallow bowl of water and asked him to look into it while she sang.

"This is how she carried them," Vane explained. "Not in sequence. In *weight*. What you receive will arrive by its own logic. Don't fight the order. Fighting the order is how singers go mad."

"I am already half mad," Aldric said. "I spent eleven years talking to the thing wearing my face. Proceed."

The first fragment was a smell: woodsmoke and rain on hot shingles. Then a burning house, seen from outside, seen from *within someone else's arms*—a child's view, low to the ground, a woman running with a small girl bundled against her shoulder. The woman was singing. The song had words in a dialect Aldric didn't know, and a melody that hooked into him like a fishhook behind the sternum, and he understood, with a lurch, that this was not his memory at all.

This was hers. This was the thing the thief had guarded all her life behind locks she built herself: a burning house, a mother's voice, the last night of a childhood.

The fragment dissolved. Aldric sat gasping above the bowl.

"That one isn't mine," he said hoarsely. "Why do I have it? Why would she carry *that* out?"

Vane's face was very still. "Because it was the payment," she said. "The door required a price. She chose which memories to surrender, and she chose them the way you'd choose—she kept the worst ones herself and sent the precious ones to safety. She gave us her childhood to sing into the world. And she gave you—" Vane hesitated. "She gave you the pieces she thought a king might need. Evidence. Proof of what the Regent took. And, I think, without quite admitting it to herself—company. So that you wouldn't wake up entirely alone in your own head."

* * *

The fragments came over days, and Aldric learned to receive them the way one receives rain: standing still, face up, letting them fall where they fell.

There was a memory of a coronation—not his, an older one, a king crowned two centuries past, and the strange knowledge that the Regent had worn other crowns before his. There was a memory of Vess, the handler, young and laughing, teaching a small dark-haired girl to pick pockets with cold fingers in a Thornfell winter—proof that the woman who had hunted Theo had once, impossibly, loved her. There was a memory of a Choir safehouse, lamplit, a dozen grey-robed singers arguing amiably about whether a particular chord was lazy or profound, and Aldric had wept at that one, absurdly, because it was so utterly ordinary and because he understood it was the ordinary that the Regent had spent a century devouring, plate by plate, and here it was, preserved, *saved*, carried through fire by a girl who claimed she wanted nothing.

But the fragment that undid him came on the ninth night.

He was alone in the east-wing room. Rain against the window. He had asked for solitude, and the Choir, wisely, had granted it, and he sat with the bowl of water between his knees and let the next piece fall.

A kitchen. Low rafters, a blackened kettle, flour dust hanging in a bar of afternoon light. A woman at the table with a small girl standing on a chair beside her, both of them kneading dough, and the woman singing—not a lullaby this time, not a warning-song like the burning-house fragment, but a working song, a *naming* song, verse after verse in that hooked, unfamiliar melody, and in the third verse the woman sang:

*Dominic, Dominic, little storm of mine,*
*born on the turn of the thirteenth sign,*
*the moon went dark and the bread went flat*
*and I loved you anyway, I loved you for that—*

The fragment ended. Aldric set the bowl down very carefully on the floor, because his hands were shaking too badly to trust it to the table.

*Dominic.*

Her mother had named her. Not Theo—Theo was a smuggler's alias, a lock she'd built around herself, a name chosen for its forgettability. *Dominic* was the name sung over a floured table by a woman who loved her anyway, loved her for the storm of her, for the darkness she was born under, for all of it.

And the girl had surrendered even that. Had packed her own name into the bundle of things carried out of the fire, and given it to a stranger, a hollow king, so that he might wake with company.

Aldric put his face in his hands and grieved—for her, for Elira and Tomas whose fates were still scattered letters across a kingdom, for the steward in the counting room, for the nursery door no one would open, for the whole long century of forgetting. He grieved the way he had not permitted himself to grieve in eleven years, loudly, alone, with the rain for witness. And when it was spent, he knelt, stiff-jointed, and picked up the bowl, and made a vow to no one and everyone:

*I will give it back. Whatever can be given back, I will carry to her myself. And what cannot be returned—I will make sure she is surrounded by people who remember it for her, so that she is never again the sole keeper of her own life. That is the only kingship worth the name. I am done ruling webs. I will learn to tend gardens.*

* * *

He began the work of restoration the next week, and found it was less like rebuilding a palace than like replanting a burned forest—slow, humbling, and dependent on seeds he did not possess.

The Choir became his instrument, though they never once called themselves subjects. Sera Vane stood in his audience hall—it had been scoured, re-floored, the throne itself removed and stored in a cellar, because Aldric refused to sit in it until he understood what thrones cost—and laid out the ledger of the lost. Singers were dispatched to every province with satchels of restored fragments: a lullaby here, a wedding day there, the name of a drowned village, the recipe for a funeral bread. Each fragment was sung back into the place it had been taken from, and each return was witnessed, recorded, written into a new archive the Choir was building—the Book of Returned Things, Bram called it, though officially it had no name yet, because Aldric insisted that naming should belong to the people doing the remembering, not the king.

Some things came home easily. A ferryman in the delta wept for an hour when the Choir sang back his brother's face. Some things came home crooked—a memory returned slightly wrong, a detail misplaced, and the recipients had to choose whether to accept the flawed version or live with the gap. Aldric decreed that the choice must always be theirs. Grace, he was learning, meant offering the gift without dictating its shape.

And some things did not come home at all.

The woman from the river provinces—her name was Odile, and she had traveled three weeks to stand in his hall—stood before him with her hat in her hands and asked, plainly, "Is he alive? Your son. Tomas."

Aldric looked at her—at this stranger who had known his son through letters, through seasons of correspondence, better in some ways than the father who had forgotten him—and told her the truth, because he had sworn off every other currency.

"I don't know. The Regent ate the memory of his fate out of my head, and out of the heads of everyone close enough to know. But letters leave traces. Paper remembers when minds don't. I have people searching every post road and relay station in the northern reaches for the last letter he ever sent, and where there's paper, there's a trail."

Odile nodded slowly. "And the girl," she said. "The one they sing about in the market squares already, though nobody agrees on her name. Is it true she gave up everything? Even her name?"

"It's true."

"Then someone should hold onto it for her," Odile said, "until she's ready to take it back. That's what my mother did for me when I was fevered as a child. She kept my name safe and said it over me every morning until I woke up strong enough to claim it." She smiled, tired and fierce. "Kings keep crowns. Someone ought to keep her name."

That evening, Aldric went down to the infirmary wing where Theo lay.

She slept most of the time now, in a narrow bed by a window, grey-faced and quiet, her hands curled loosely at her sides like a child's. The Choir kept a rotation of singers at her bedside day and night—not healing her, exactly. *Holding* her. Singing the fragments of what remained of her into coherence, the way you cup your hands around a match in wind.

Sera Vane met him at the door. She looked as though she had aged a year in a month.

"She surfaced yesterday," Vane said softly. "For about a minute. Looked at me and said, 'Who's holding my name?' And then she went back under."

Aldric stood in the doorway a long moment, watching the sleeping girl who had emptied herself to save a man she'd met for minutes. On the table beside her bed sat a small glass vial, stoppered with wax—the vial she'd stolen back from the inquisitors, the one containing the earliest shard of her childhood. She had clung to it through the descent, through the Regent's feast, through everything, and in the final moment had pressed it into Vane's hands with the rest, saying, according to Bram, only four words:

*"Sing it all. All."*

Inside the vial, presumably, was the beginning of her—the first thing she ever saw or felt, the seed from which Dominic grew. The Choir had not yet dared open it. Vane had confessed to Aldric that they were afraid: afraid it was too fragile, afraid it was bait left by the Regent, afraid most of all that returning it might mean taking something else from her in exchange, because the old arithmetic of memory had never once allowed a free return.

"We'll open it soon," Vane said, following his gaze. "When the moon is right. When we're sure."

"Take your time," Aldric said. "But Vane—when you do, I want to be there. Not as king. As the man who owes her a debt no ledger holds. If her mother's song lives anywhere in what remains, I have pieces of it. I've been learning the verses." His voice roughened. "I intend to sing them back to her, however badly, until she has them again."

Vane studied him, and something in her weathered face eased—the first true softening he had seen there since the gorge.

"She'd hate that," Vane said. "Being sung to by a king."

"Yes," Aldric agreed. "Which is why I'll do it before she's awake enough to object."

He crossed to the bed and stood looking down at her. Outside, the moon was rising—thin now, waning, one ordinary moon among the twelve, keeping honest time. Her brow furrowed in sleep, and her lips moved, shaping a word too faint to hear.

Aldric leaned closer, listening the way he had once listened for the Regent's voice in the deep rooms of his own skull—and heard, or almost heard, or perhaps only hoped he heard, a single syllable that was not *Theo*.

Behind him, in the corridor, hurried footsteps: Bram, breathless, a scrap of paper in his fist, mud to the knees.

"Majesty," the boy said, eyes wide. "A rider just came in from the north relay stations. They found the last letter. Tomas's last letter." He swallowed. "Majesty—it wasn't posted from the north at all. The seal is Thornfell. And it's addressed—to *her*."
