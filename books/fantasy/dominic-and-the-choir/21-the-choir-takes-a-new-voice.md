## 21. The Choir Takes a New Voice

Sera Vane had sung at a coronation once, twenty years ago, when the world still believed kings were simple things. She remembered the weight of the anthem in her throat, the thousand voices of the court rising beneath her like water, and King Aldric—young then, unbowed, his hair still dark—watching from the throne with the careful politeness of a man who trusted everything he had inherited.

She remembered it because remembering was her trade, and because she had spent twenty years wishing she could forget.

Now she stood in the same hall, and the throne was empty, and the anthem had not yet been written. Morning light fell through windows that servants had scrubbed clean of a season's grime, and the glass was old enough that the light came through green and gold, laying long lozenges of color across the flagstones where the court would soon gather. The palace smelled of lye and beeswax and, faintly, of snow carried in on the boots of workmen repairing the east wing. It smelled, Sera thought, like a house being cleaned after a long illness. Like a room opened to air.

Aldric stood at the base of the dais steps, refusing—for now—to ascend them. He wore plain grey wool, no crown, no chain of office. His face was thinner than the relic-memory Sera had touched on the road north, thinner than the man in her oldest recollections, but his eyes were his own again. That was the miracle she kept testing, the way a tongue returns to the gap where a tooth was. He looked at things and saw them. He forgot nothing that mattered and, more remarkably, he had begun to remember things that everyone else had lost.

"The scribes want a ceremony," he was saying to Master Ellery of the Chancery, his voice dry as old paper. "Oaths, trumpets, a new crown forged before noon. I want none of it. If the court learns one lesson from what wore my face these ten years, let it be this: that a man is not made whole by the things placed upon his head."

Ellery, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and the harried look of someone who had kept the kingdom's paperwork running through its decade of haunting, blinked. "Your majesty, the realm requires *symbol*. The people have spent ten years watching a king who never aged, never erred, never wept. They need to see the difference."

"Then they will see me stumble," Aldric said. "They will see me ask names twice, and mourn strangers whose funerals I cannot recall attending, and sit in council with singers instead of sorcerers. Let the difference be visible. Let it be *ordinary*." He turned, and found Sera at the edge of the light, and something crossed his face that she had no clean word for—gratitude braided with grief, perhaps, or the particular shame of a man who has been shown the inside of his own rescue. "Sera Vane. You've been avoiding the throne room."

"You needed nothing from me here," she said.

"I need everything from you here. That's rather the point." He gestured, and she came forward across the colored stone, her grey robes whispering. Up close he smelled of cedar smoke and the bitter tonic the physicians still made him drink, as if recovery were an illness to be dosed. "I have spent the morning being told what the court will tolerate. I intend to spend the afternoon deciding what it will become. Sit with me. All of you."

*All of you.* He meant the Choir. Sera felt the words settle over her like a mantle she had not asked to wear, heavy and warm at once.

* * *

The council chamber had once held thirteen chairs for thirteen advisors; now it held eleven, two having been quietly removed along with their occupants, whose names appeared in the ledgers Vess had surrendered—the ledgers of the harvested, the lists of the forgotten. Sera took her seat near the window and watched Bram fidget beside her, the young singer unable to keep his hands still, turning his tuning fork over and over like a talisman. On Bram's other side sat Dominic.

Not Theo. Not anymore. Dominic, named for a song, wearing Choir-grey cut down to fit her narrow frame, her dark hair tied back with a plain cord. She sat very straight and very quiet, the way she had sat since the thirteenth turn, since the moon had completed its cycle above the palace and a girl had opened her hands and given away everything she had hoarded for a lifetime.

Sera had seen thieves surrender their takings before. She had seen penitents give away their sins. She had never seen anyone give away their *self*, freely, deliberately, singing each memory out of her own skull into the mouths of the Choir so that the thing starving on the throne could claim nothing—not the burning house, not the woman's voice, not the vial of first-light memory, not even her own name. Starved mid-feast, the Regent had unraveled like a song cut off mid-verse, and what remained in its wake was a girl with wide unmarked eyes asking, politely, who all these people were who seemed to love her.

Dominic caught Sera looking and offered a small, careful smile—the smile of someone performing warmth from sheet music, hitting the notes correctly. Sera smiled back and hated herself for measuring the distance behind it.

The door opened, and the chamber went still.

Vess came in between two guards, though the guards' hands stayed loose at their sides, because there was nowhere left for her to run. Ten years serving the Regent through the inquisitors' office—ten years harvesting forgotten memories by the vialful, feeding them up through channels she had believed, Sera suspected until the end, were merely bureaucratic—and now she walked into a room full of the people she had hunted, and stopped three paces in, and knelt.

Not gracefully. Her knees cracked on the stone and she winced, and her grey inquisitor's coat—she still wore it; no one had made her change—pooled around her like dirty snow.

"Your majesty," she said. Her voice was steady. Sera, who knew every register of a human voice, heard the ruin underneath it. "I am here to confess. All of it. Every name in the ledgers, every order I followed, every—" She faltered, just once, on the word that mattered. "Every person I emptied. I ask for no mercy. I ask only to be of use in putting back what I helped take, for as long as it takes, and then to be dealt with as the law requires."

Aldric regarded her for a long moment. Sera watched him do it—watched him reach, visibly, for memories that were not there, because the Regent had eaten the last decade of his reign and Vess had been a fixture of it. He did not pretend otherwise. That was his strange new courage: he confessed his own gaps aloud.

"I don't know your face," he said at last. "I'm told we met nine times. I'm told you reported directly to the thing that wore me. I can't judge you for what I don't remember, Mistress Vess—but the ledgers exist, and the Choir remembers what was taken, and judgment will come from those who *can*." He looked to Sera. "If your order will have her labor."

Every eye in the chamber turned to her. Bram's hand stilled on the tuning fork. Dominic leaned forward slightly, and Sera understood, with the sick lurch of foresight, that the girl remembered nothing of Vess—nothing of the cell, the vials, the offered childhood—but the Choir remembered *for* her, and somewhere among the verses they had sung back into the world, Vess's face lived in Dominic's borrowed knowledge, a villain in a story she could no longer feel.

"We'll have her," Sera said, and heard her own voice come out harder than intended. She softened it with effort. "She'll start with the archive. There are four hundred vials in the vault below the ice, and every one of them holds a life, and someone must carry each one home. It will take years. It should be done by the hand that filled them."

Vess bowed her head until her forehead nearly touched the floor. "Thank you," she whispered, and Sera thought: *no. Don't thank me. Carry it.* But she said nothing more, and the guards led Vess out to begin her sentence, which was memory-work of the least glamorous kind—ledger by ledger, name by name, the slow arithmetic of restitution.

* * *

The days afterward assembled themselves into a shape Sera would later think of as the rebuilding, though at the time it felt less like construction than like thaw.

The palace woke in stages. First the servants—those who remained, blinking, in rooms they could not entirely explain, some of them missing years, some missing only the habit of why they had come upstairs. The Choir went among them with candles and songbooks, and Sera devised the practice within the first week: they sang each servant's own lost moments back gently, a measure at a time, letting the cook remember her mother's kitchen, letting the chambermaid reclaim the name of the village she'd fled. Word spread through the lower city the way word does, and soon there were lines outside the gates—not petitioners now, but the hollow-eyed, holding scraps of paper with other people's handwriting describing what they'd lost.

The court reformed slowly, reluctantly, like a body remembering how to stand. Aldric insisted that three seats of the thirteen go to the Choir, and when the old families protested—he could almost hear their protests through the walls, that rusted aristocratic machinery grinding against anything new—he received them one by one and told them, mildly, exactly what their grandfathers had sold to the inquisitors during the hungry years, and the protests subsided. Memory, Sera reflected, made a sharper sword than any in the armory. The old houses had spent a decade forgetting their own bargains. Aldric simply handed the receipts back.

She found him one evening on the balcony above the great hall, watching the lamplighters, and stood beside him in the cold without speaking, because some silences were the truest form of courtesy between two people who had both been broken by the same entity.

"You're leaving," he said.

It wasn't an accusation. Sera looked out at Thornfell's capital, at the smoke rising from a thousand chimneys, at the pale sliver of the new moon just past its thirteenth turn—a moon whole and ordinary now, its impossible cycle complete, the count reset to one. "The Choir belongs under the ice," she said. "That's where the vault is, the archive, the young ones' training. And the kingdom will need us more in the dark places than at court. Hollow men are made in dark places."

"And the girl?" Aldric asked. "Dominic. Will she go with you?"

"She hasn't decided." Sera hesitated. It was not her habit to hesitate. "You should know what she is, your majesty, so you can weigh it honestly. She gave away nearly everything. What the Choir holds for her, we can sing back in pieces, small doses—we've been giving her a little each week, her mother's hands, the smell of the sea, the way bread smells in a cold house. But it's like watering a plant from a borrowed jug. She may always be thin, inside. She may always be learning herself from the outside, from our memories of her rather than her own."

Aldric was quiet a long time. Below, a lamplighter moved from post to post, and the light followed her like a slow tide.

"When the Regent was dying," he said finally, "in those last minutes while your people sang and the girl gave everything away—I was inside my own mind, did you know that? Watching my own halls burn. And I felt what she did. Not the details. Just the *shape* of it. Someone handing over the last of their bread." He turned to Sera, and his eyes in the lamplight were wet and steady. "I owe her a self, Sera Vane, and I haven't got one to give. But I have a kingdom, and a court, and a great many archives, and I am going to spend the rest of my reign hunting down every scrap of her that exists in the world—her mother's song, her father's letters if any survive, the parish records of whatever village burned. When she wants it, it will be here. Whether she stays or goes."

Sera found, unexpectedly, that she had to look away. Twenty years of guilt had taught her every flavor of debt; this was the first one that tasted like hope.

* * *

They gathered in the Choir's temporary quarters on the last night before departure—a converted chapel with good acoustics and bad furniture, where the singers had hung their robes on nails meant for tapestries and generally made the place look like what it was: a family of vagabonds temporarily rich. Bram had procured, by means he declined to specify, a wheel of cheese and a bottle of plum wine, and the younger singers were passing both around with the solemnity of an actual sacrament.

Dominic sat cross-legged on the hearthstones, close enough to the fire that her face was all orange light and shadow. She was laughing at something Bram had said—a real laugh now, Sera noted, not the performed kind; three weeks of small returned memories had loosened something in her—and Sera stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, watching, committing it to the deep archive where she kept the moments that mattered.

Then she came in and sat, and the circle made room.

"We leave at dawn," Sera said, when the wine had gone around twice. "Wagons south to the pass, then up. The mountain paths should behave themselves now"—a ripple of tired laughter at that; the road that remembered had become the Choir's favorite grim joke—"but we'll take the northern route regardless. There's work at the gorge. Four hundred vials, and Vess carrying them up the ice stairs one at a time, and every singer we can spare doing restoration verse."

She paused. This was the part she had rehearsed, and rehearsing had not made it easier.

"Dominic. The offer isn't conditional and it doesn't expire. You have a place under the ice for as long as you want it—train, sing, sleep, sulk, steal the occasional memory off a merchant if that's still how you pray. We will hold what's yours and give it back whenever you ask and never when you don't. That's the whole vow. No debt. No prophecy. No price at the door." She met the girl's eyes. "You saved a king and broke a god, and neither of those is why you belong to us. You belong because Bram can't tune a lyre without you criticizing him, and because Maren talks in her sleep and you're the only one who finds it funny, and because when you walked into that frozen gorge half-dead and furious, something in this order recognized itself. Belonging isn't earned, child. It's just claimed. Claim yours or don't. It will be there either way."

The chapel was very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere above, a shutter banged in the wind off the river.

Dominic looked around the circle—at Bram, who had gone red and was studying his wine very intently; at old Maren; at the young ones who had known her only weeks and already arranged their bedrolls near hers. Sera watched her search their faces the way she had once searched pockets: professionally, thoroughly, for anything of value. And finding value everywhere, and having no theft left in her to take it with, she did the only thing remaining.

"I don't remember most of you," Dominic said. Her voice was low and rough. "Bram tells me stories about what I was like when I got here, and I listen like they're about someone else. Some nights I lie awake trying to feel sad about the burning house, because I know I'm supposed to, and I can't—I just feel the shape where sadness used to be." She stopped. Started again. "But when we sing, I know exactly where my line comes in. Every time. I don't know why. I don't remember learning it. My voice just... goes home."

She swallowed.

"So yes," she said. "I'll come north. I'll stay. Under the ice, in the choir, wherever you keep the belonging." A wet, disbelieving laugh escaped her. "Apparently I'm a singer now. God help the archive."

Bram made a sound that was half sob and half cheer, and the circle collapsed inward into an embrace, and Sera let herself be pulled into the middle of it, grey robes and cold hands and plum-wine breath, and thought: *twenty years I carried the guilt of one failed rescue. This is what was on the other side of it. This whole room.*

* * *

Much later—past midnight, the others asleep or singing quietly in corners—Sera climbed to the chapel roof for air and found she was not alone.

Aldric stood at the parapet in his plain grey wool, no guard, no crown, looking north toward the mountains where the moon was setting behind the pass. He didn't turn when she joined him. For a while they simply stood, two old survivors watching the dark.

"There's one more thing," he said at last. "Something I haven't told the council. Something I haven't told *her*."

The wind off the river was cold enough to bite. Sera waited.

"When the Choir sang the door open, and the girl went into my mind—my halls, my corridors, whatever we're calling it this week—I felt her pass through. And I felt the Regent rise to meet her. And I heard, for just a moment, before she gave everything away—" He stopped. His hands on the parapet were white-knuckled. "The Regent spoke to her about the prophecy. About how it had written her into the book a century ago. How it had steered her, all her life, every choice. And she chose to give anyway. To spite it. That's the version we've all agreed on, because it's a beautiful version."

"But," Sera said quietly.

"But I was inside my own mind, Sera, closer to the source than any living soul. And what I heard, in the last instant before it starved—" Aldric turned to her at last, and in the dying moonlight his freed face looked older than the ruins it had escaped. "Was it *laughing*. Not in defeat. In satisfaction. Like a farmer hearing the barn door close behind the harvest it wanted." He exhaled slowly. "It's gone. I'm free—I know I'm free; I forget my own appointments and misplace my keys like any honest man. But the book of prophecies survived the fire, and the vault below the ice holds four hundred vials, and somewhere in this kingdom the Regent spent a century arranging its own dinner. I would like the Choir to keep reading. I would like them to keep *singing*. Because if it wrote one prophecy, it may have written others—and the next one might not end with a girl generous enough to give away her own name."

Far to the north, beyond the passes, the thirteenth moon slipped behind the mountains, and the sky began, slowly, to count again from one.
