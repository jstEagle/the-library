## 22. Dominic and the Choir

The moon over Thornfell had turned thirteen times since the night Dominic gave her name away, and it hung now over the lower city like a coin someone had finally paid.

She stood on the rooftop of the old countinghouse with her hands loose at her sides and her breath fogging silver in the cold, watching three of her students fumble their way through a theft. Below them, in the lamplit mouth of a tavern alley, a cooper named Hallow was drinking away the memory of his daughter's wedding—not because he wanted to lose it, but because he wanted to sell it, because grief was expensive and wood did not carve itself when your hands shook too badly to hold a chisel. It was an old trade. It was her trade, once. The guilds still worked these streets, leaner now, watched by a crown that no longer fed on what its people forgot.

"Steady," she murmured, though none of them could hear her. "Don't grab it. *Ask* it."

Below, the youngest of the three—a wiry girl called Nettle with ink-stained fingers and a habit of biting her thumbnail when she concentrated—closed her eyes and began to hum. It was barely singing at all, more breath than melody, the way rain is barely music until you stop trying to hear it as anything else. Dominic felt the shape of it in her own chest, that low harmonic the Choir used to open a mind gently, like a hand laid flat against a door rather than a shoulder thrown against it.

Hallow's shoulders dropped. His glass sank to the table. And then—there—the memory rising off him like steam: a girl in a yellow dress spinning in a yard full of wood shavings, a fiddle wheezing somewhere out of frame, the smell of varnish and honey-cake. Dominic saw it secondhand, the way she always did now, through Nettle's borrowed eyes, and her throat went tight in a way she had learned not to fight.

Because this was the part they got wrong, back in the old days. This was the lie she had built her whole first life on: that taking a memory cost nothing, that a thing lifted from one head was a thing no longer missed, that forgetting was just a room with the furniture removed. She had stolen ten thousand moments from ten thousand strangers and told herself each one weighed nothing.

Then she had stood in the Regent's throne room with her childhood cupped in her hands like water, and learned exactly how much nothing weighed.

Nettle drew the wedding free, wrapped it in song the way Bram had taught her, and held it—shining, whole—in the space between herself and the drunk old cooper. And then, voice trembling with effort but holding, she did the thing that made her a singer of the Choir and not a thief of Thornfell:

She sang it back.

Hallow wept. He didn't know why; he never did, the first time. He just put his scarred hands over his face and cried into them while his daughter's wedding poured home into him, and Nettle sat down hard on the wet cobbles, shaking, emptied out and grinning like a fool.

*That,* Dominic thought. *That's the trade. That's what it was supposed to be all along.*

* * *

They came up to the roof flushed and stumbling, and she let them catch their breath before she said anything, because Sera Vane had taught her that too—that praise delivered to a gasping student lands like weather on stone, and silence lets it soak in.

"You dropped the third verse," Dominic said at last. "When you were pulling it out. You rushed."

"There wasn't time," Nettle protested. "He was starting to come around—"

"There's always time." She crouched so they were eye to eye, all three of them, moonlit and sheepish. "A memory knows when it's being handled roughly. You yank it, it frays. You frayed the edges of his daughter's face. He'll get her back tonight, but for a week he'll look at her and feel something slipping, some wrongness he can't name. That's a wound. We don't leave wounds. That's the whole difference between us and everyone who ever bought or sold a memory in this city. Not the taking. The *care*."

The boy beside Nettle—Pell, solemn, fourteen, with a voice already deepening toward manhood—frowned. "But you stole for years," he said. It wasn't cruelty; he was young, and young singers asked honest questions. "Before you knew us. Didn't you fray things?"

"Every day." Dominic straightened and looked out over the city, at the crooked rooftops and the harbor lights and the distant pale gleam of the palace where a king walked his gardens every morning, relearning his own history one restored memory at a time. "And I tell you this so you don't have to learn it the way I did: everything I took, I took from myself too. Every theft hollowed a little room in me and left it dark. I spent twenty years being invisible because invisible was safe, and safe was starving." She looked back at them, and let them see her smile—small, real, hers. "You're not thieves. You're rememberers. Act like it."

Bram would have teased her for the speech. Bram wasn't there—he was in the capital this season, teaching the court musicians the old harmonies, teaching Aldric's new archivists how to sing a memory into a book instead of writing it flat—and she felt his absence the way she felt the missing years of her childhood: not as a wound anymore, but as a shape. A place where something beloved fit. You could build a life around such shapes. You could even call them family.

* * *

Later, alone, she climbed to the highest ledge of the countinghouse roof and unwrapped the bundle she carried everywhere now, the way other people carried knives or coins.

Inside the oilcloth lay a small glass vial, empty. It caught the moonlight and threw it back in pieces.

It had held her earliest memory once—a burning house, a woman singing, a name sung soft against smoke. The Regent had kept it in this vial like a fly in amber, bait for a thief it had written into prophecy a century before she was born. She had carried it out of the dark and given everything inside it away, freely, to the Choir, to the thirteenth turn of the moon, so that nothing she loved could ever again be currency.

Most days she didn't miss what was gone. That was the strange mercy of it. You couldn't ache properly for a thing if you didn't know its outline, and hers had been sung back into the world rather than destroyed—scattered into the Choir's great memory-choir, held by hundreds of voices so no single hunger could ever gather it up again. She lived beside her own lost childhood the way you live beside a grave that holds only a shoe: grief without a body, love without a face.

Except for the song.

Aldric had kept fragments. Fragments only—the king who had begged her not to save him if the cost was another person's self, who after his freeing had spent months doing the one thing the Regent never could: giving memories back. Most of what she'd surrendered was too dispersed to retrieve whole, and some of it was simply gone, and the story did not pretend otherwise, and neither did she. But the song—her mother's song, the one sung against the smoke—he had found enough of it, threaded through a dozen different keepers' minds, to give her back eight bars.

Eight bars. She hummed them now under her breath, there on the roof with the empty vial in her palm, the way she did every night, a small stubborn liturgy. They were nothing, technically. A lullaby fragment in a mode the Choir had nearly lost, about a river, about a girl whose name the singer loved. But the last line bent on a syllable she could feel even without remembering learning it, and that syllable was her. *Dominic.* The name her mother had sung before anyone wrote it anywhere. The name the prophecy-book had never known, because the woman in the burning house had chosen it herself, in secret, out of love.

Sera had offered to have the Choir search deeper. Dig through a hundred keeper-minds, reconstruct the rest, rebuild the burned house beam by beam.

Dominic had said no.

Not because she didn't want it. She wanted it with a want that scared her, some nights, lying awake in the Choir-house with Bram snoring two rooms away and the moon turning outside. She wanted her mother's face. She wanted the smell of that kitchen before it burned. She wanted the years the Regent had eaten and the years she'd surrendered both.

But she had learned the difference between a wound and a door. Her past was a door now, and behind it was a house on fire, and she had walked through fire twice already—once as the girl who survived it, once as the thief who chose to let it go. Some doors you knock on when you're ready. She wasn't ready. Maybe someday. The Choir would be there when she was. That was the whole point of them.

For now: eight bars, an empty vial, a city below her that knew her name.

* * *

She heard the footsteps on the tiles before she saw the figure—light, deliberate, a walk she knew. Sera Vane came up over the roofline with her grey hood back and her white hair loose, older-looking than she had been a year ago, which everyone said was impossible and everyone privately understood: grief ages you, even grief that ends in victory. Marta had died in a waystation doorway holding three inquisitors off with a song, and Sera carried that the way Dominic carried the vial. Everywhere. Wrapped carefully. Sometimes heavy.

"The students did well," Sera said, settling beside her on the cold lead.

"They did well except for the part where they nearly frayed a man's dead wife out of existence."

"Mm. You nearly frayed worse, your first month. You tried to lift a memory from me while I was awake and singing." Sera's mouth curved. "I still have the headache."

"I was making a point about my independence."

"You were making a point about being frightened. It's the same gesture, in the young." Sera was quiet a moment, watching the harbor lights blur and sharpen in the river wind. Then: "Word from the capital. Aldric's archivists finished restoring the coronation records—all of them, the true ones, not the Regent's edited versions. There's going to be a reading. Public. First time in a hundred years the crown shows the country its own unaltered memory." She paused. "He asks about you. Every letter. He says—" and here her voice did the thing it rarely did, softened, went almost young, "—he says the kingdom owes its king to a thief, and he'd like to thank her in person, and also she still owes him a game of cards, and she cheats."

Dominic laughed. It still surprised her sometimes, laughing—how easy it had gotten, how it rose now without permission, the way tears once had. "Tell him I cheat because he lets me. Tell him—" She stopped. Looked at the vial in her hands, then closed her fingers over it, warm glass against warm skin. "Tell him I'm coming for the reading. All of us. Bram can't wait to show off the court harmonies anyway."

"He'll be glad." Sera rose, brushing frost from her robe, and paused at the roof's edge. "Dominic." The name still landed strangely sometimes, spoken aloud by someone else—it was hers now, truly hers, worn smooth by daily use—but it landed sweetly, always. "Nettle asked me today if she could train under you permanently. Full apprenticeship. She said—" the old singer's eyes glinted, "—she said you're the only person she's ever met who gives things back."

Dominic watched her go, down the ladder and across the rooftops, a grey shape folding into the grey city. Below, in the tavern alley, the cooper Hallow was walking home sober with his daughter's wedding bright in his head, humming something he didn't know he'd been given.

* * *

She stayed on the roof a while after, because endings deserved witness and beginnings did too, and this—she was slowly learning—was both.

Thirteen moons ago she had been a girl with no name she'd admit to, no past she'd claim, no future she believed in, selling pieces of other people's happiness to buy her next invisible hour. Now she had students who argued with her. A king who owed her a card debt. A brother-shaped friend in the capital who sent letters full of bad jokes and good harmonies. A leader who had become, without either of them naming it, something between a mentor and a mother. An order of grey-robed strangers who had taken her in not because prophecy required it—they'd gotten that part wrong, mostly, the prophecy was a trap—but because she'd frozen half to death on their doorstep and Bram had fed her bread and sung to her, and kindness, it turned out, needed no justification.

And somewhere in the world, scattered through hundreds of faithful minds, a woman was still singing eight bars about a river and a beloved name. Not lost. Just held. Just waiting, the way the Choir waited, the way the moon turned, patient and certain, for the door to open.

Dominic slipped the empty vial back inside her coat, over her heart where it had always belonged, and stood.

Below her, Thornfell glittered—every window a memory, every sleeper a song, every forgotten thing in the city held now by someone who would give it back. Somewhere far north, in the mountains, a hidden door stood open for the first time in a century. Somewhere east, a king rehearsed honesty. Somewhere in a cell in the capital, a former handler named Vess spent her days copying memory-records for the archivists, penance by ledger, and had asked—last month, in a letter no one had expected—to know whether the girl she'd sold was well.

*I'm well,* Dominic thought, and found it was simply, astonishingly, true.

She took a breath, filled her lungs with river-cold air, and sang the eight bars out over the city—one thread of one song, joined by the wind to all the others, rising toward a moon that had finished turning and begun, like her, to begin again.

Down in the streets, faintly, someone answered.

She leaned over the roof's edge to see who—and stopped, smiling, because of course. Of course it was Nettle, walking home through the lamplight with her students' satchel over one shoulder, singing the same eight bars back up at the rooftops, clumsy and bright and slightly off-key, the way all new songs start.

Dominic listened until the two melodies met somewhere in the middle of the dark, hers above, the child's below, the lost and the found singing the same name.

Then she shouldered her own satchel, and went down the ladder, and home.
