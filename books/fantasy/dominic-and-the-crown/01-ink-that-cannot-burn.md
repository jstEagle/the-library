## 1. Ink That Cannot Burn

The ink was the first thing Xanthe Yarrow had ever loved, and it would very likely be the last thing he was permitted to love.

He had learned its moods the way other boys learned the temperaments of horses. Temple ink—ground from soot and gallnut and a binder of boiled hide that cost more per jar than Xanthe earned in a season—did not like to be rushed. It pooled if you tilted the pen too far, feathered if the page was damp, and went dull and gray as old ash if you mixed it with water to stretch it, which Brother Halvard always claimed not to notice and always noticed.

So Xanthe did not stretch it. He sat at his desk in the scriptorium's third row, where the light through the horn window fell just right for ledger work, and he copied. Debts owed by the miller. Debts owed by the cooper. Debts owed by the widow Sennet, whose late husband had borrowed against their roof beams and whose interest Xanthe calculated each quarter with a small, private flinch he had trained his face never to show.

*Debt is holy,* the temple taught. *Debt binds the valley together as roots bind soil.* The Prior said it every Feastday, hands folded over his belly like a man warming them at a fire only he could feel. And perhaps it was true. Everything in the valley was bound: fields to the temple, families to fields, scribes to families who could not pay their tithes and so gave up a son instead.

Xanthe had been given up at nine years old, when the blight took two harvests running. His mother had held his face in both hands and told him the temple would feed him and teach him and make him useful, and she had smiled while saying it, which was how he knew it had cost her something terrible.

Fifteen years later, he was useful. He was also careful, which was the more valuable skill.

Careful meant arriving first and leaving last but never being seen doing either. Careful meant knowing which brothers wanted conversation and which wanted silence, which warden counted steps on his rounds and which counted coins. Careful meant that when Brother Odo made one of his jokes—the same four jokes, rotated like crops—Xanthe laughed at the correct volume: enough to seem warm, not enough to seem eager. A man who seemed eager got assigned extra duties. A man who seemed nothing at all got left alone, and being left alone in the scriptorium after dark was worth more to Xanthe than meat on Feastdays.

Because after dark, with a stub of candle shielded behind his inkwell, Xanthe did not copy ledgers.

He copied everything else.

There was a shelf in the archive no one visited, holding registers older than the current Prior, older than the current walls, full of entries in a hand so faded it looked like breath on glass. Xanthe had transcribed fragments of them into a little book of his own making, sewn from offcuts of vellum the binders threw away. The old registers mentioned things the new ones did not. They mentioned *the gate*. They mentioned travelers who once came through it, and tolls paid in goods Xanthe had no names for. One entry, in a clerk's cramped script, simply read: *No crossing this year. The road is closed by decree. Praise the quiet.*

Praise the quiet. That was the valley's whole liturgy, really. Magic could not live here—that much every child knew before they knew their letters. Nothing bloomed unnaturally, no charms held, no hedge-witch's poultice worked better than plain fat and moss. The Hollow Order had swept the world clean a hundred years ago, or so the temple taught, and the valley was simply a place where the sweeping had been thorough. A blessed emptiness. The Prior called it peace.

Xanthe called it small. He did so silently, in the privacy of his own skull, because even thoughts were safer kept invisible.

That evening, the light died early behind rain clouds, and Brother Halvard dismissed the row with the grunt that served him for speech. Xanthe banked the common candles, squared the ledgers, and stayed behind on the pretense of finishing the Sennet account—which was true, though not the whole truth. He had just uncorked his private ink, the good black he hoarded like a miser, and set his hidden book beside the ledger, when he heard the door.

Not the knock. The door itself—a sound like a body falling against wood, followed by a second sound that was unmistakably a body sliding down wood to the floor.

Xanthe froze, pen lifted, a drop of ink swelling at the nib like a black seed.

The scriptorium was at the temple's western edge, past the counting room, past the wardens' stair. Nobody came here by accident. Pilgrims knocked at the front gate. Debtors were summoned. Nobody *fell against the scriptorium door*, and certainly nobody did it at dusk in the rain.

He should call the wardens. That was the rule, and rules were the architecture of his life; he lived inside them the way a snail lives inside its shell, safely, narrowly. Call out, and the wardens would come with lanterns and staves, and whatever lay on the other side of that door would be handled by men whose business was handling things.

The drop of ink fell onto the vellum and spread, a small perfect circle, ruining the line he'd spent an hour on.

Xanthe put down the pen.

He crossed the room quietly—he was good at quiet—and lifted the latch.

The stranger came in like a tide, all at once and smelling of iron. He was tall, or had been before he folded; now he was mostly a shape in soaked grey wool, one arm pressed to his side where the cloth was dark and heavy with blood. His other hand was clenched around a fold of paper so tightly his knuckles had gone the color of tallow. Rain ran off him onto the flagstones in a spreading pool, and somewhere under the smell of blood there was another smell, faint and wrong, like a fire that had burned something that shouldn't burn.

"Gate," the man said. His voice was gravel dragged through water. "Is this—" He swayed. Caught himself on the doorframe hard enough to crack it. Looked at Xanthe with eyes that were doing arithmetic Xanthe didn't understand, some desperate sum of *where am I, how long do I have, can this one be trusted.*

"You're bleeding," Xanthe said, which was the stupidest sentence he had ever produced, and he had once transcribed a Prior's sermon on the sanctity of debt, all nine pages of it.

"They're coming." The man took a step, failed at it, and slid down the doorframe to the floor, leaving a red smear on the pale wood that Xanthe's mind, helplessly, catalogued as *a cleaning problem.* Fifteen years of invisibility will do that to a person. A bleeding heretic in the scriptorium was not a moral crisis first. It was a stain that would have to be explained.

"I'll fetch the wardens," Xanthe began.

"No." The word cracked like a whip, and then the man coughed, wetly, and softened it to almost nothing: "Please."

*Please.* From a dying man, in a valley where no one had begged in living memory, because begging implied want and want implied lack and lack was unseemly. Xanthe stood there with his hand still on the latch, rain blowing in cold across his ankles, and felt the first crack run through the careful shell of his life—not a large crack, not yet, just the kind a teacup gets that you can't see until you pour hot water in and it weeps.

The man's fist opened, slowly, finger by finger, like a lock surrendering. Inside was the paper: folded small, sealed with a blob of wax stamped with a mark Xanthe didn't recognize—a circle broken by a vertical line, like an eye shut tight.

"Take it." The man pushed it toward him across the wet stone. "When I'm gone. Not before. They can't know it passed."

"I don't—"

"You're a scribe." The man's mouth twitched, which might have been a smile in a healthier face. "I saw your light. A scribe keeps words safe. Keep this one."

From the courtyard, distant but not distant enough, came the sound of the gate bell. Once. Twice. The wardens' signal for an unknown arrival—or a known one they'd rather announce than surprise.

The man's eyes closed. Opened again, slower. "Floor," he rasped. "Behind the presses. There's a gap. Binders store the spoiled vellum there. No one counts spoiled vellum."

Which was true, and which frightened Xanthe more than the blood did, because it meant the man had watched the scriptorium, had learned its habits, had known exactly which corner of Xanthe's small world was uncounted. *No one counts spoiled vellum.* No one counts Xanthe, either. Perhaps that was why, when he should have been shouting for the wardens, he instead hooked his arms under the man's shoulders and dragged him—grunting, slipping, smearing a trail he would have to scrub before dawn—across the scriptorium floor to the press room, to the gap behind the great binding presses where the ruined vellum waited to be pulped.

The man fit badly, the way too-large things fit into spaces built for smaller ones. Xanthe piled spoiled sheets over him—mispricked folios, pages blurred beyond saving, the temple's failures—and the man looked up at him from under them with those arithmetic eyes.

"Name," the man whispered.

"Xanthe. Xanthe Yarrow."

Something moved behind the man's expression—recognition, or relief, or grief; Xanthe couldn't read it, and it unsettled him the way a misprinted line unsettles a page. "Yarrow," the man repeated, softly, as if tasting it. "Good. Good boy. Keep the lamp low."

Then his eyes shut, and his breathing went shallow and ragged, and Xanthe was left kneeling in the dark among the temple's discarded mistakes, holding a sealed map he had not agreed to take, listening to the gate bell ring a third time.

* * *

He hid the map where he hid everything: inside the false spine of a damaged ledger, the one trick of his invisible life. Then he did what scribes do when the world comes apart—he cleaned. Scrubbed the blood from the flagstones with sand and lye until his knuckles were raw. Wiped the doorframe. Mopped the rain. By the time boots sounded in the corridor, the scriptorium smelled of nothing but lye and ink, and Xanthe sat at his desk with the Sennet account before him, pen moving, heart hammering a rhythm no ledger could hold.

Brother Wenna entered with two wardens, their lanterns throwing jittery shadows up the shelves. Wenna was the temple's chief auditor, a narrow woman with a memory like a trap and a voice like a key turning in it.

"Yarrow." She surveyed the room. "You're late at your desk."

"The Sennet interest, Sister. It wanted finishing before quarter's end."

Her eyes moved over him the way they moved over accounts: looking for the entry that didn't balance. "A traveler was seen on the west road at dusk. Running. Hurt, by the report. The gate bell rang three times."

"Three times," Xanthe agreed, and wrote a number.

"Were you at your desk all evening?"

Here it was, then. The moment the shell either held or broke. Xanthe kept his pen moving, kept his voice at the correct volume—warm, not eager, nothing at all. "All evening, Sister. Ink doesn't keep. You've told us often enough."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Whether it was approval or suspicion, he genuinely could not tell, and that, he thought, was the mark of a truly excellent auditor.

"Hm." She turned to go, then paused at the door. "The west road is closed by decree, Yarrow. Has been for a generation. So whoever our traveler is, he came from nowhere, which means he is either lost or lying. If he's found on temple ground, wake me first. Not the Prior. Me." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all, and then she was gone, and the lanterns with her, and Xanthe sat alone in the dark with his heart going like a bird in a box.

He waited a full hundred count. Then another.

Then he rose, lit his stub of candle, and went back to the press room.

The stranger was awake. Barely. His lips were gray, and when he tried to speak, the sound came out in pieces, and Xanthe had to lean close to gather them.

"Vey," the man breathed. "My name... doesn't matter. Yours does. Listen." A wet, rattling pause. "They erased it all. Every book. Every name. But it isn't gone, boy. It's *hidden*. And the hiding place—" His hand crawled out from under the spoiled vellum, found Xanthe's wrist with shocking strength, and gripped. "You'll dream it tonight. Don't be afraid of the burning library. It's only a memory. It's only mine."

"What are you talking about?" Xanthe whispered. "What library? Whose memory?"

But Vey's eyes had gone past him, fixed on the middle distance where dying men keep their appointments, and the last thing he said, clear as a bell over water, was a name:

"Tamsin. Tell Tamsin... the seal held."

Then his grip loosened, and his breathing changed, and Xanthe—who had copied death entries into ledgers for fifteen years, neat columns of names and dates and sums outstanding—discovered that no ledger had ever taught him what to do with the actual thing.

He sat back on his heels among the spoiled vellum, in the uncounted corner of his small life, and outside the rain kept falling on a valley that praised its quiet, and somewhere beneath his breastbone the first crack widened, letting in a draft of a world he had never once believed was his.

By morning, Vey would be cold, the wardens would be asking sharper questions, and Xanthe Yarrow would lie awake having dreamed of a crown of black glass burning in a library that had never existed anywhere he knew—

—but that night, all he could do was sit in the dark, hold a dead man's secret, and whisper, to no one at all:

"The seal held. What seal? *Whose* seal?"

The spoiled vellum, naturally, said nothing. It had been trained by the temple too.
