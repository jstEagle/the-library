## 2. The Stranger's Last Ledger

The stranger lived until moonrise.

Xanthe had expected him to die within the hour — the wound in his side was the sort that temple physicians described in their ledgers with a kind of tidy finality, *subject expired*, and moved on to the cost of bandages. But the man had other ideas. He lay on the floor of the scriptorium's back room, among the stacks of misprinted hymnals that no one had bothered to burn, and he kept talking. Low, cracked sentences that didn't seem aimed at anyone. Names. A river with a name Xanthe had never heard. The word *ashfall* repeated like a prayer said wrong.

Xanthe knelt beside him with a basin of water he had no idea what to do with. In fifteen years of indentured service, his duties had been enumerated precisely: copy ledgers, balance columns, sweep ink dust, remain unremarkable. Nowhere in the contract was there a line item for *keep bleeding stranger alive*. He suspected the creditors would not look kindly on an unauthorized expense.

"You shouldn't have brought me in," the stranger said. His voice was rough as a file drawn across soft wood. "They'll ask questions."

"They always ask questions," Xanthe said. "I just usually have answers."

That earned him something that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be bled out of a man instead of into him. The stranger's eyes were dark and fever-bright, and they fixed on Xanthe with an unsettling steadiness, as though he were reading a document rather than looking at a person.

"What's your name?"

"Xanthe Yarrow."

"Yarrow." He tried the word like a coin bitten for gold. "A weed that grows where nothing else will."

"I've heard worse descriptions."

The stranger's hand found Xanthe's wrist — grip shockingly strong for a dying man, the fingers cold as ledger clasps. "Listen to me. My name is Vey. I need you to remember it, because in a little while I won't be able to." A wet breath. "There are people coming. Grey coats, faces like closed doors. The Hollow Order. If they find me, fine. I'm spent. But they'll want to know who saw me last. They'll want to know what I gave away."

Xanthe's mouth went dry. "Gave away? You haven't given me anything. You've bled on my floor, which is technically my problem now, but—"

Vey pressed the folded map into his hands.

Xanthe had seen it earlier, when he'd dragged the man inside: a square of heavy paper, creased into eighths, sealed with a blob of black wax stamped by a ring he didn't recognize. He had very deliberately not opened it. Curiosity was a debt like any other, and he'd learned young that debts accrued interest.

"Now you have," Vey said. "Congratulations. It's the worst thing anyone's ever given you, I promise."

* * *

He died a little after moonrise, between one sentence and the next, mid-word — Xanthe would later swear it was mid-word, which seemed unbearably rude of death, interrupting a man like that. The name Vey had spoken last was not Xanthe's. It was some other name, two syllables, spoken softly and with terrible tenderness, the way one speaks to a child or a country. Xanthe couldn't hold onto it. It slipped through memory like water through cupped hands.

He sat for a long while in the dark, holding a dead man's map and listening to the valley wind push against the shutters. Outside, magic could not survive — everyone knew this, it was the first lesson taught in the temple school, before letters even. The Hollow Order had burned it out of the world generations ago, root and branch, and the valley was the cleanest place left, scrubbed down to honest stone and honest arithmetic. Xanthe had built his whole small life on that honesty. Numbers didn't lie. Ledgers balanced or they didn't. Nothing here hummed.

And yet the map in his hands felt warm. That was probably just body heat. Probably.

He hid the body.

This was, he reflected grimly, exactly the sort of unauthorized activity that got scribes sold to worse creditors than the temple. But the wardens would come asking questions at dawn regardless, and a corpse in the scriptorium would make the questions so much harder to answer. He wrapped Vey in the ruined hymnals — the man would have appreciated the irony, Xanthe thought; he'd had the face of someone who appreciated irony — and maneuvered him down to the old cistern beneath the east wing, which hadn't held water in a century and hadn't held anything else since the last warden with a curious mind retired. It took three trips' worth of strength from a body that had never been strong, and by the end Xanthe was shaking and ink-stained and quietly horrified at how practiced he'd become at concealment in a single night.

Then he climbed back to his cell, put the map under the loose floorboard where he kept the three things he owned that weren't temple property, and lay down expecting to stare at the ceiling until the bells.

He did not stare at the ceiling. He dreamed.

* * *

He was running through a library that was on fire.

Not the valley library — he knew every shelf of that library, knew which volumes leaned and which had warped in the damp. This place was vast beyond sense, galleries rising in rings toward a ceiling lost in smoke, and every ring was burning. Books screamed as they burned. He understood, in the way one understands things in dreams, that the screaming was not metaphor. These books had been alive in the manner that books can be alive, and they were dying, and someone was singing while it happened. The song was beautiful. He hated it.

In his hands — in the dream-hands, which were larger than his own, scarred across the knuckles — he carried a crown.

Black glass, or something like glass, something that drank the firelight rather than reflecting it. It was heavier than any crown had a right to be, and it hummed. The valley did not hum. This hummed like a struck bell that refused to finish, a sound he felt in his teeth and behind his eyes, and part of him — the dreamer wearing another man's hands — loved it, the way you love a thing you know will ruin you.

*Don't look at it,* said a voice that was his own voice and not his own. *You look at it, it looks back.*

He looked anyway. Inside the crown, deep in the black, something turned over. Like an eye opening. Like a page.

He woke with dawn grey in the window and tears drying on his face and the word *ashfall* sitting in his mouth like a stone.

Xanthe sat up slowly. His heart was going like a debtor fleeing a creditor. Dreams were dreams. Everyone had strange dreams; Brother Aldous said spicy food was mostly to blame, though the temple diet contained no spice whatsoever, which Aldous acknowledged made his theory unfalsifiable and therefore safe.

But Xanthe had never dreamed in colors he hadn't seen. He had never dreamed in hands that weren't his.

He knelt, pried up the floorboard, and looked at the map without touching it. Just looked. The wax seal caught the thin light, black and glossy, and for one vertiginous moment the seal seemed less like wax than like a window, and something on the other side seemed aware of being looked at.

He put the board back and went to work. There were ledgers. Ledgers were real. Ledgers balanced.

* * *

They came at the third hour, as the morning bells finished, and Xanthe knew before they spoke that it was about Vey.

Warden Corvel led them — two grey-coated men behind him with faces like doors indeed, doors in houses long abandoned. Corvel himself was different: soft-spoken, meticulous, the kind of man who balanced his own soul quarterly. He had interviewed Xanthe twice before, once over a miscounted candle inventory (not Xanthe's error, but proximity was guilt enough in an audit) and once over a novice found reading forbidden marginalia. Xanthe remembered both interviews with the clarity of trauma.

"Scribe Yarrow." Corvel's smile was a formality, like a seal on a letter. "You were on late duty last night."

"I was, Warden. Quarter-end. The grain tithes wanted reconciling."

"Mm. And during your duties, did anything... irregular occur?"

Here it was, then. The crack in the floor. Xanthe had spent his whole life learning to walk around cracks. "Define irregular. We had a mouse in the storeroom. I set a trap. Also Brother Pethel sneezed forty-one times during vespers, which some might call an omen."

One of the door-faced men almost smiled. Corvel did not.

"A traveler was seen on the south road at dusk. Bloodied. Heading toward the temple grounds." Corvel produced a small notebook — of course he had a notebook; the man probably catalogued his dreams. "Did he reach you?"

Every instinct Xanthe possessed, honed by fifteen years of careful invisibility, said: *give him nothing, admit nothing, be furniture.* But lying to a warden was a debt with catastrophic interest, and Corvel would check the trap, the flour stores, the basin. Lies required more maintenance than truths.

"A man did collapse at the scriptorium door," Xanthe said. "I brought him water. He died within the hour. I wrapped the body per protocol and placed it in the old cistern pending the warden's instruction. I intended to report this morning." He let a beat pass. "I'm sorry, Warden. It was my first death. I may have handled parts of it badly."

It was, he thought, essentially true. Every word defensible. He was growing quite good at this, and hated himself a little for how good it felt.

Corvel wrote in the notebook. "Cause of death?"

"A wound. A blade, I think. He was cut deeply along the left side."

"Did he say anything? Names, especially. Did he give you anything?"

"No names," Xanthe said, and it was true, because the name Vey had given was spoken to the air, to the fire, to nobody — and *ashfall* was a place, wasn't it, not a name. As for the rest: "He gave me nothing but blood on my floor and a great deal of mumbling about rivers."

Corvel looked at him for a long moment. It was the look of a man checking a column against its total and finding it balanced — but only barely, only suspiciously so.

"The Order will want details," he said at last. "There will be a formal inquiry. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps the day after." He tucked the notebook away. "Until then, Scribe Yarrow, continue your duties. And Yarrow — if you recall anything further, however small..."

"I know where to find you, Warden."

"Yes," Corvel said mildly. "Everyone does. That is rather the point of me."

They left. Xanthe stood in the doorway watching the grey coats cross the courtyard, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been chased in their lives. The valley lay bright and quiet around them, terraced fields, white walls, the mountains standing guard like patient accountants. The safest place in the world. Magic cannot survive here.

So why, Xanthe wondered, did the Hollow Order send wardens in pairs, and carry notebooks, and ask about names — unless somewhere, despite everything, they were afraid of something surviving?

He went back inside, knelt at the loose floorboard, and drew out the map at last. His fingers found the fold. He told himself he would simply look at the seal again, verify it hadn't changed, that he hadn't imagined the warmth—

The wax cracked open under his thumb like an egg, all on its own, eager.

Inside, in ink that shimmered faintly like oil on water, the first line read:

*Xanthe — or whatever you are called now. If you are reading this, then I am sorry, and it worked.*

Below it, in a different hand — older, hurried, half-familiar the way the burning library had been familiar — someone had written a single word:

*Run.*
