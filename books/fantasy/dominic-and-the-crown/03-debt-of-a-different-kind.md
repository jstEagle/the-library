## 3. Debt of a Different Kind

The summons came at dawn, which was rude of it.

Xanthe had been awake since the fourth bell anyway—he always was, his body still keeping the ledger-hours of the scriptorium even on the one morning in nine the temple allowed him for himself—but he had hoped to spend those stolen hours doing something small and pleasant, like eating bread that belonged to him. Instead there came a knock at his door, three measured raps, and the voice of Warden Ostell saying the word *summons* as though it were a diagnosis.

He dressed carefully. That was a habit of invisibility: dress like a man who has nothing to hide and less to say. Grey tunic, grey coat, ink-stained fingers tucked out of sight. In a valley where magic had died generations ago and everyone pretended this was a blessing rather than an amputation, the safest thing a person could be was forgettable.

Warden Ostell walked him through the cold corridors without speaking, which Xanthe understood to be a courtesy. It meant the questions hadn't started yet. The warden was a broad, patient woman with a face like a closed ledger, and she had known Xanthe since he was seven years old and indentured, brought in with a debt his dead mother's apothecary shop hadn't covered. She had taught him his first letterforms. She had also, twice, reported him for asking questions, and both times had softened the report before it reached the provost. He counted her among the few people who might, under specific and limited circumstances, wish him well.

They stopped outside the provost's study. Ostell looked down at him.

"Whatever happens in that room," she said quietly, "remember you are a scribe. Scribes record. They do not volunteer."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone has said to me all week."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to be tactical." And she opened the door.

* * *

Provost Halden was not alone. That was the first wrongness. Provosts heard accusations alone; it preserved their air of reluctant mercy. But behind Halden's desk sat two strangers in ash-grey coats, their collars cut high and plain, their hands folded with a neatness that looked rehearsed. They did not look like creditors. Creditors wanted money, and these two looked like men who wanted something money couldn't buy.

"Xanthe Yarrow," Halden said. "Sit."

Xanthe sat. He folded his hands too, matching the strangers' posture out of pure spite, though only he knew it was spite.

"You are accused," Halden continued, "of harboring a heretic."

"I am accused of—"

"A man arrived at your scriptorium door four nights ago. Wounded. You concealed him. You did not report him. He died on temple grounds without receiving the rites of the Ledger, and before dying he passed you—" one of the strangers produced a slip of paper and read from it in a voice like dry paper tearing, "—*a map, and a name not yours*."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a bell rang the sixth hour, indifferent as weather.

Xanthe's mind performed the scribe's trick it always performed under pressure: it began transcribing. He saw Vey again, bloodied against the doorframe, whispering *take it, take it, they can't burn what's already read* — and he saw himself folding the map into the false spine of a ledger titled *Grain Tithes, Year of Ash*, where no one had ever willingly looked.

"I found a man dying," Xanthe said. "I gave him water and a blanket. I don't know what a heretic looks like. He looked like a man who'd been walking a long time."

"He told you things," the second stranger said. "Names. Places."

"He told me he was cold."

Halden sighed, and here was the second wrongness: the sigh was tired in a way that wasn't about Xanthe at all. "Xanthe. Your debt stands at eleven years. The temple has held it patiently because you are useful and because I asked them to be patient." A pause. "These gentlemen represent the Hollow Order. This morning, the temple sold your debt to them."

The floor did not move, but Xanthe felt it move, the way you feel a stair that isn't there.

"Sold," he repeated.

"The Order pays debts of the faithful in full," the first stranger said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than cruelty. "You will come with us. You will tell us everything the heretic gave you. And when we are satisfied, your account will be considered settled, and you may return to your copying."

*Return to my copying.* As if that were the prize. As if the cage were the reward for entering a smaller cage.

Ostell, by the door, had gone very still. She caught his eye—one flicker—and her gaze dropped meaningfully to his left sleeve, where the map was not, and then to the window, where the morning light lay over the valley like a lid.

"I'll need to collect my tools," Xanthe said, standing. "A scribe without his pens is useless to anyone."

"Two minutes," said the stranger who talked like torn paper. "The warden will accompany you."

* * *

His room was six paces by four, and he had lived in it for nineteen years, and he had never once thought of it as a cell until the moment he began packing it into a single bag.

The map came out of the grain-tithe ledger first. His hands were steady—scribe's hands, trained for steadiness—and while they worked, his mind raced ahead along roads he'd never dared imagine. The valley gate. Everyone knew the gate; children drew it in chalk, a tall arch of pale stone at the valley's throat, and every child also knew the rule: *no one crosses*. Not because of walls or wardens, but because of what waited beyond—the world, which was large, and the Order, which was larger, and magic, which was dead everywhere but whispered about here more than anywhere, the way people whisper most about the thing they've lost.

No one crossed. No one had crossed in a generation. The gate stood open, in fact—that was the joke of it, the terrible gentle joke—open as a door in an empty house, and the whole valley simply agreed not to walk through it.

*Xanthe,* Ostell said at his shoulder, low. "Listen to me once, and then never repeat this. The Order does not settle debts. It collects people. Whatever the heretic gave you—it's already inside your head, isn't it? That's why they want you breathing."

He looked at her. Nineteen years, and he had never lied to her, mostly because she made lying feel like a waste of good ink.

"There are memories in my head that aren't mine," he said. "A burning library. A crown of black glass. I don't know how they got there. I'm frightened of them."

Something moved behind the warden's closed-ledger face—grief, maybe, or its cousin. "Then run," she said. "And when you're far enough away to do it safely, write it all down. Everything. Paper remembers longer than people do." She pressed something into his hand: a stub of pencil and three sheets of good paper, stolen, presumably, from her own office. From Ostell this was a love letter.

Below, a bell rang. Voices in the corridor. Two minutes, spent.

There was a back stair behind the scriptorium that the novices used for smuggling honey cakes, because even the devout are only human. Xanthe took it. Behind him he heard the strangers' voices sharpen, heard Ostell say something in her flattest, largest voice—a wall of a voice, built to delay—and he did not look back, because looking back is how you learn whether someone is paying for your escape with their own standing, and he could not bear to watch that transaction.

He ran.

* * *

The valley gate stood exactly as it had always stood: pale stone, worn smooth, framed by hills the color of old wool. Morning fog pooled around its feet. Beyond it, the road descended into a country Xanthe knew only from maps he had copied and never believed—maps with rivers that forked and rejoined like handwriting, mountains marked with names that sounded invented.

Behind him, faint now, shouts. Ahead of him, nothing. Just the arch, and the road, and the rest of his life on the wrong side of a line nobody crossed.

"This is insane," he told the arch conversationally. "I should go back. I should negotiate. I'm very good at negotiation; I've argued with creditors my entire life."

The arch offered no opinion.

He thought of Vey, dying against the scriptorium door, spending his last breath on a stranger because there was no one else. He thought of eleven years of debt, and nineteen years of grey tunics, and a life so carefully small it fit in a six-by-four room. He thought of Ostell saying *run*, which meant she believed running was possible, which meant—

He stepped through the gate.

Nothing happened. That was the first miracle: nothing happened. No alarm, no thunder, no divine displeasure. Just fog thinning around his ankles and gravel under his boots and the extraordinary, vertiginous fact that he was walking and nothing was stopping him. He laughed—a startled, undignified bark of a laugh—and kept walking, and the laugh turned into something that was half laughing and half the other thing, and he let it, because there was no one there to see, and being unseen had been his whole life and suddenly it was optional.

It was a mile down the road, where the valley's shadow finally let go of the land, that he felt it.

At first he thought it was the memory-dreams again—the burning library, the black crown, Vey's borrowed past pressing at the backs of his eyes. But this was different. This wasn't in his head. This was in his *feet*. In the gravel. In the air itself, faint as warmth rising off stone at dusk: a hum, low and continuous, like a note held by an instrument too large to see.

Magic. Faint, threadbare, forbidden—and alive. Out here, where the world was supposed to have gone hollow, the world was *humming*.

Xanthe stopped in the middle of the road and turned around. Behind him, the valley sat in its bowl of hills, green and orderly and silent as a page. All his life he had been told the valley was the last clean place, the protected place, the place where the Order's mercy kept chaos out. Standing outside it, feeling the faint wild pulse of everything the valley lacked, he understood the truth with the clarity of a fresh-inked line:

The valley wasn't protected. The valley was *kept*. A locked drawer in a house full of locked drawers, and somewhere above it all, someone held the key—and had decided no one inside needed to know the house existed.

His peace had been a prison. His debt had been bars. And the Order hadn't bought his indenture this morning; they'd come to retrieve an escaped book.

"Well," Xanthe said aloud, to the humming road, to nobody, to himself. "That explains a great deal."

He settled the bag on his shoulder, took out Ostell's stolen pencil, and wrote on the first stolen sheet, in his best careful hand: *Day One. Outside. It hums.*

Then he walked north, toward the mountains on the map, unaware that behind him the fog was filling with ash-grey coats—and that ahead, leaning against a milestone as if she'd been waiting for him specifically, a woman with a smuggler's smile was already watching him approach.
