## 4. Road of Borrowed Names

The valley gate had never taught Xanthe how to walk on open ground.

For three days he had moved along the spine of hills the old ledgers called the Shepherd's Teeth, and for three days the world had refused to behave. There was too much of it. In the temple scriptorium, a page ended at its margins; out here, every horizon simply opened into more horizon, as if some scribe had forgotten to rule the lines.

He was also, he had discovered, terrible at being a fugitive.

He hid from a shepherd who only wanted directions. He slept in a ditch because it looked like the sort of place a clever man would hide, then woke soaked and sneezing. He rationed his bread with monastic discipline and then ate all of it at noon on the second day, because hunger, unlike faith, did not respond to discipline.

And through all of it, Vey's memories kept arriving like uninvited guests.

They came most often in the gaps between thoughts—when he was watching a hawk, or chewing, or doing nothing at all. A flash of a woman laughing in a doorway full of smoke. The smell of a library burning: not wood, exactly, but knowledge itself, ink and vellum and centuries going up together. Once, walking a ridge path, he suddenly knew—with total, useless certainty—how to pick a lock. His hands had made the motions before he could stop them, fingers curling around an invisible tension wrench, and he had stood there in broad daylight working an imaginary lock while a goat watched him with what he chose to interpret as pity.

"That's not mine," he told the goat.

The goat left.

The map stayed quiet in his shirt, folded against his chest like a second heartbeat. He had not dared open it since the valley. It wasn't that he feared pursuit—not yet, though he checked the skyline behind him so often he'd developed a crick in his neck. It was that opening it felt like agreeing to something. As long as it stayed folded, he was still Xanthe Yarrow, scribe, runaway debtor, coward. The moment he unfolded it, he became whatever Vey had intended.

On the third evening, the hills dropped toward a road—a real one, gravelled and rutted, running east toward a smudge of chimney smoke on the horizon. And on the road sat a wagon.

It was the kind of wagon that tried very hard to look unremarkable and failed. Its canvas was patched in seven places, each patch a different cloth, arranged with no discernible logic. A mule grazed nearby with the air of a creature who had seen everything and been impressed by none of it. And leaning against the wagon's wheel was a woman, sharpening a knife with the unhurried rhythm of someone who could do it in her sleep.

She looked up. She did not stop sharpening.

"You've been circling my camp for half an hour," she said. "Either you're deciding whether to rob me, which would be a poor investment on your part, or you're hoping I'll leave first."

"I was—" Xanthe began.

"Your boots are temple-issue. Your coat is temple-issue. You have ink on your fingers, ink on your chin, and—I want you to appreciate this—you have a quill behind your ear." She pointed with the knife. "Nobody robs anyone with a quill behind their ear. Sit down before you fall down. There's stew."

Xanthe should have kept walking. Every rule the temple had ever carved into him said keep moving, say nothing, be no one. But the stew smelled of onions and salt, and his legs had opinions of their own, and so he sat.

"I'm Tamsin," she said, ladling stew into a tin bowl. "This is Buttercup." She indicated the mule, who did not look up. "You're a runner. Fresh one. Valley-bred—the way you flinched when I said *rob* gave it away. Nobody from outside flinches at robbery anymore. It's practically a greeting."

"My name is—"

"Don't tell me." She held up a hand. "Borrowed names are cheaper than real ones out here. Call yourself whatever keeps you upright. I only care about two things: can you pay, and are you followed?"

"No," Xanthe said. Then, honestly: "I don't know. Maybe. Probably."

Tamsin nodded slowly, as though this were the most reasonable answer she'd heard all season. "A man who admits he might be followed. That's either honesty or stupidity, and your face is too honest for stupidity." She took the bowl back before he'd finished eating, which struck him as unfair until she refilled it. "Now. The map. Don't bother looking surprised—your shirt's been riding up whenever you reach for the stew. Black wax seal on the corner, pressed with a ring sigil. Seven spokes."

Xanthe went very still.

"I carried cargo under that seal once," Tamsin said, quieter now. "Years back. Never knew what was inside the crates. Just knew the men who paid wore grey gloves and didn't blink, and the men who sealed the crates used wax the color of dried blood." She leaned forward. "Where did you get it?"

"A dying man gave it to me."

"Dying men don't give things away. They spend them." Her eyes narrowed. "What did he buy with it?"

Xanthe looked at her—at the knife, the mismatched wagon, the mule named after a flower who despised them both—and found, to his own astonishment, that he wanted to answer. Not because she'd earned it. Because he had spent three days carrying a secret alone, and the weight of it had begun to feel like drowning.

"He gave me a name that isn't mine," he said. "And memories. I don't fully understand how. And the map leads somewhere he wanted me to go, and people with grey gloves want the map, and possibly me, and I don't know how to do any of this. I'm a scribe. I copy ledgers. I'm very good at marginalia."

To his credit, Tamsin did not laugh. Much.

"A scribe," she said. "Chosen by a dying courier with a blood-sealed map and stolen memories in his head." She rubbed her jaw. "Well. Either you're the most interesting fool on this road or the most dangerous, and frankly the wages are the same." She stuck out her hand. "I'll take you where the map points. For a price."

"What price?"

"Fifty crowns, half up front."

Xanthe opened his mouth.

"And," she added, "you tell me what's in your head as we go. All of it. The memories. I've spent ten years wondering what was inside those crates, and the gods, in their infinite comedy, have finally sent me a man who might know." Her grin was sharp and genuine. "Consider it interest on the loan."

Xanthe shook her hand. It was, he thought later, the first contract of his life that he'd signed willingly—and the only one where he genuinely couldn't read the fine print.

* * *

Inquisitor Serah Kol knelt in the ashes of the valley temple's scriptorium and did not pray.

Prayer required belief, and belief required a story, and the story the Hollow Order had given her—the one she had recited every morning for fifteen years, *we emptied the world of sorcery so the world could be safe*—had begun, sometime in the last three days, to develop cracks.

It started small. The temple records listed the stranger Vey as arriving alone, but the gate-warden's log noted *two sets of tracks*, the second set already half-erased. Someone had walked beside Vey. Someone the Order's own archives insisted did not exist.

Then there was the archive itself. Serah had requested the founding chronicles—the true ones, the pre-Cleansing originals kept under seal—and been refused twice, then told they had burned in the Ashfall fire, then told they had never existed, all within the same afternoon, by three different superiors who would not meet her eyes.

Orders did not lie to themselves. That was the whole point of the Order. They were the ones who remembered so the world could forget.

Except.

Serah had her own gap. Everyone in the Order did—a blank stretch of years between recruitment and initiation, scrubbed clean by the rite they called the Mercy. She had always accepted it. You cannot mourn what you don't remember.

But kneeling here, in a dead man's cell, she had found scratched into the underside of the cot frame, in a shaky hand, a single line:

**THEY KEPT IT. ASK THE VAULT.**

And beneath it, almost illegible:

**S.K. — YOU KNEW ME BEFORE.**

Her own initials.

Serah Kol rose, sheathed her sword, and gave the order to ride. The trail led east, toward the Teeth, toward a runaway scribe who carried something in his skull that a hundred grey-gloved men wanted badly enough to burn archives over.

She told herself she was tracking a fugitive.

She told herself the cold in her chest was just the morning wind.

Neither lie held its shape for long.

* * *

They made good time on the fourth day, the wagon rattling east along the old trade road, Tamsin driving, Buttercup radiating grievance, Xanthe sitting on the tailboard with the map finally unfolded across his knees.

"It's a route," Tamsin said, studying it upside-down. "See? These aren't mountains, they're waystations. Marked in an old cipher—temple hand, actually. You'd read it faster than me."

"I might," Xanthe admitted. And then his mouth continued without consulting him: "The seventh spoke is the vault-key. Vey always said—" He stopped. His voice hadn't sounded like his voice.

Tamsin pulled the mule up. "Vey always said *what*?"

"I don't know." Xanthe stared at his own hands. "That wasn't me talking. I've never said those words in my life. I don't even know what a vault-key is, except apparently I do, somewhere, in—" He pressed his palms against his temples. "It's getting worse. On the road it was just images. Now it's words. Sentences. This morning I caught myself humming a song I've never heard, and Tamsin, I knew all the verses."

Tamsin watched him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face made her decide something.

"When I ran crates under that seal," she said slowly, "my contact told me the keepers had a trick. A last resort. When a keeper was about to die, he'd pour what mattered into someone else's head. Memory, knowledge, purpose—the whole load." She flicked the reins. "Sounds to me like somebody poured, and you're the cup. Question is whether you're big enough to hold it all, or whether it holds you."

"That's not comforting."

"Wasn't meant to be. Comfort costs extra." But her voice gentled. "Look. We're two days from the ruins marked at the route's end—old city, Ashfall, if the map's honest. You want to know what's in your head, that's where the answers live. So we drive fast, we camp cold, and you tell me everything Vey knew about those crates. Deal?"

"Deal," Xanthe said. Then, because the borrowed tongue was quicker than his caution, he heard himself add:

"The seventh spoke is the vault-key. Vey always said the Crown remembers what the world was made to forget."

The road stretched empty ahead of them. Somewhere far behind, dust hung above the hills where no cart traveled.

And in the silence after his own voice, Xanthe understood, with a scribe's precision, that he had just spoken words he had never learned—in a cadence that was not his own—and that whatever Vey had poured into him was no longer waiting politely at the edges.

It was learning to use his mouth.
