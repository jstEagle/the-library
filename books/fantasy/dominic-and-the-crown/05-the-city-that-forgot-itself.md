## 5. The City That Forgot Itself

Ashfall did not so much welcome visitors as tolerate them the way a ruin tolerates moss.

Xanthe felt it the moment they passed beneath the broken gate—a low hum in his teeth, like biting foil. Three days on the road with Tamsin had taught him to name some of these sensations. The warmth behind his sternum near standing water was one thing. The way birdsong doubled, faintly, an echo arriving a half-breath late, was another. But this was new. This was the whole city humming at once, a chord held by ten thousand dead stones.

"Stop making that face," Tamsin said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to say something scribe-shaped. *Fascinating, the masonry exhibits anomalous resonance.* Yes, very good. It's haunted. Everything outside your valley is haunted. Walk faster."

He walked faster. He also wrote it down later, in the little book he'd begun keeping against her advice—*anomalous resonance, citywide*—because if he was going to lose his mind he intended to do it in neat columns.

That was the other thing three days had taught him: he was losing it. Not all at once, not dramatically. In margins. Yesterday he'd reached for his mother's face and found only the shape of reaching. He'd stood in the middle of the road with his eyes shut while Tamsin watched him with something dangerously close to sympathy, and finally he'd said, "She had brown hair," and Tamsin had said, "Sure, kid," in the voice people use for things that aren't true but cost nothing to agree with.

And Vey's memories kept arriving like unpaid debts. A staircase of white marble that went up into smoke. A woman laughing over her shoulder in a doorway hung with copper bells. A word in a language Xanthe had never studied, which he nonetheless now knew how to write, and had caught himself doing it twice—in the margins, in a hand steadier than his own.

"It's the seal," Tamsin said, when he'd confessed as much on the second night. "The map. You've been staring at that thing for days. Your head's just chewing on it."

He hadn't told her about his mother's face. Some ledgers you keep closed.

Now Ashfall closed around them. The city had been beautiful once—you could tell because it had been built to be beautiful rather than sensible, all sweeping bridges and towers that leaned toward each other like gossiping aunts. Then something had burned it from the inside out, gently, the way a candle burns, leaving the shape intact and the substance gone. Whole buildings stood hollow. You could put your hand through a wall if you wanted; the wall would let you, with the mild embarrassment of a thing that has forgotten its job.

"People lived here," Xanthe said.

"People always live places," Tamsin said. "Then they stop."

She moved through the ruins like she'd been here before. Xanthe noticed this, filed it, didn't ask. She'd gone quiet since the gate, checking corners before he'd even registered there were corners, and twice she'd stopped him with an arm across his chest for reasons he never learned. The map, meanwhile, tugged in his pocket—not physically, nothing so honest, but there was a direction his thoughts kept drifting the way a compass needle drifts north, and the direction was down a street of collapsed arcades, toward a hill, toward a squat stone building that had somehow survived by being too plain to burn.

"That's it," Xanthe said. "The safehouse."

"You don't know that. You've never been here."

"I know—" He stopped. Tried again, honestly. "Vey knows. And Vey is currently the part of me that knows directions."

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. "Right," she said. "We're going to talk about that. Later. When we're not standing in a haunted street like two candles waiting for a draft."

* * *

The safehouse door opened to Vey's hand—or Xanthe's hand remembering Vey's—and beyond it lay a room that made grief feel small and domestic. A desk. A cot. Shelves sagging with books someone had loved enough to save and not enough to carry. Dust lay over everything in a smooth gray tide, undisturbed except for a single line of small footprints leading from door to shelf and back, years old.

Rats, probably. Xanthe chose rats.

"He lived here," he said. "Before. He kept supplies in the floor cache under the cot, left side, third board—" His hand was already lifting the board. "I've never seen this room."

"I know," Tamsin said quietly. "You keep saying that."

Under the boards: oilcloth bundles, a waterskin gone to vinegar, and a book bound in gray leather with no title. Xanthe sat down on the dusty cot with it in his lap the way you hold a thing that might be either a gift or a debt, which, he was learning, is most things.

It was Vey's journal. And Vey, it turned out, had been a scribe too.

The realization went through Xanthe like warm water. Here were ledger-hands, column-minds, the same itch to record that Xanthe himself couldn't scratch. Page after page of careful entries—but the entries weren't grain shipments or candle counts. They were *memories*. Numbered. Catalogued. *Entry 41: the coronation of Queen Ilsa, as witnessed, retained, verified.* *Entry 42: the burning of the Amber Library, first-hand, degraded 10%, transferred to keeper Dossa, lost with Dossa.*

"What is this?" Tamsin asked, reading over his shoulder. She had climbed onto the desk, boots on, entirely at home, which should have annoyed him and instead felt like company.

"I think—" Xanthe turned pages. "I think they were keeping it. All of it. History. Real history, not temple history." His finger stopped on a diagram: two circles connected by arrows, labeled in Vey's tidy script. *Keeper. Bearer. Transfer, voluntary, incomplete. The mind makes room by giving something up.*

There was a passage underneath, and Vey had underlined it twice, and then, apparently after some interval, written beside it in smaller letters:

*I told myself I would only take what was needed. I have Dossa's last winter now, and her mother's lullaby, and I no longer remember whether the scar on my thumb came from the chandler's knife or hers. This is the price. Write it plainly, whoever finds this: the memories do not add. They replace.*

Xanthe read it four times. Somewhere in the middle of the fourth reading the room got very far away and very quiet, and Tamsin's voice came to him down a long tunnel saying his name, and he realized his hands were shaking.

"I know what's inside me," he said. "This is how Vey survived. The Order was killing the keepers, so they—the memories, they passed them along, person to person, like—" he laughed, and it came out wrong, "—like debt. Like my indenture. Sold on and sold on, and every holder pays interest."

"And what's the interest?" Tamsin asked, though her face said she already knew.

"Yourself." He looked down at the little book he kept. *Anomalous resonance, citywide. Birds echo late. Mother had brown hair.* Had. He'd written *has* three days ago. "Every night I sleep and Vey gets a little bigger in there. He doesn't mean to. He's not—he's not *doing* anything, he's just more practiced at being a person than I am. Twenty more years of being a person. My memories are copies made carelessly, quickly, by someone who didn't know he was living them. His are originals."

Tamsin said nothing for a moment. Then, uncharacteristically gently: "You're still the one holding the book."

"For now." He closed the journal. "There's more here. Coordinates, dates, a whole network of keepers. And—" he found the page he'd half-dreaded, half-hoped for, "—the Crown itself. Where it's kept. What it does." He read aloud, slowly, translating Vey's shorthand: "*It remembers everything perfectly. And what is remembered perfectly can be rewritten. The Order calls this heresy. The keepers called it temptation.*"

"Well," said Tamsin. "Good thing nobody's tempted by anything in this story."

That was when the lamp in the doorway went out.

Not blew out—went out, snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, and the humming of Ashfall changed key, and Xanthe knew before he saw her, knew from Vey's memories, knew the way you know a word once you've learned to spell it.

"Serah Kol," he said, to the dark.

* * *

She came through the door with a lantern relit and a blade not drawn, which was somehow worse. Up close she looked worse than she had on the road—the road's distant figure had allowed for dignity. Here, in the dust of Vey's sanctuary, Serah Kol looked like a woman who had been arguing with herself for weeks and losing.

"Don't," she said, when Xanthe stood. "Sit. Both of you. I've crossed half a kingdom to talk, which my superiors find very funny, so let's be efficient. The journal. Give me the journal."

"No."

"No," she agreed, wearily. "That's the answer I wrote down in advance. Xanthe Yarrow. Indentured scribe. Debts of four crowns, eleven silver." She recited it flatly, like scripture. "You copied temple ledgers for nine years and broke no rules until the night a bleeding man fell into your life. You are the least heretical person I have ever been ordered to kill."

"Ordered," said Tamsin, from the desk, in a bright dangerous voice.

"Tamsin." Serah didn't look at her. Something passed between them anyway, some old current Xanthe couldn't read, and Tamsin's brightness dimmed a degree. "You're out of the trade, whatever you're telling yourself. This isn't your ledger."

"Everything's my ledger, Kol. That's the trouble with ledgers."

Serah took one step forward. "Here is what I know," she said. "I know the Order sent me to retrieve that journal and silence its carrier. I know the Order has known the Crown's location for two hundred years and calls the search *eradication*. I know—" her voice caught on the next sentence, and she pushed it through anyway, "—that I have gaps. In my memory. Gaps with clean edges, like pages cut out. And I know that whatever's in your head, scribe, my superiors want it buried deeper than I've ever buried anything. So. The journal."

"Why?" Xanthe asked. "Why do they want it so badly?"

And Serah Kol, inquisitor of the Hollow Order, opened her mouth to answer—

—and Tamsin threw the lamp at the wall.

Flame went up the dry curtain like it had been waiting years for permission, and the room filled with light and shouting, and Tamsin grabbed Xanthe's collar and hauled him through the back window into the alley, running hard, Serah's voice behind them—*stop, STOP*—and then, cutting through everything, Tamsin yelling back over her shoulder words Xanthe didn't understand but Serah clearly did, words that stopped the pursuit cold:

"—and ask yourself who signed off on *yours*, Serah! Ask who holds *your* ledger!"

They ran. Alleys, arches, the humming stones of Ashfall blurring past, until Tamsin dragged them both into the flooded cellar of a collapsed bathhouse and stood there breathing like a bellows, laughing in the ugly way people laugh after nearly dying.

"What did you say to her?" Xanthe gasped.

Tamsin wiped her eyes. When she answered, the laughter was gone. "A price," she said. "Everything costs. I told her a true thing about myself I'd have paid a great deal to keep, and now she knows it, and now the Order will know it, and I can never go back to the life I had before I met you." She looked at him, and for the first time in three days her face held no armor at all. "That's what I bought your escape with. My own past. So you'll forgive me if I'm short on sympathy for the lad who's losing memories he didn't even know he had."

Xanthe's breath went out of him. But before he could find anything to say—something kind, something true, a scribe's work and he fumbled it—the journal, soaked now, fell open in his hands to a page he hadn't read.

Vey's handwriting changed halfway down. Sharper. Hurried. As if written by someone else wearing Vey's hand.

*If you are reading this and the memories have begun to blend,* it said, *come to the drowned mountain. There is a door under the water. I have left instructions. And know this: I did not choose you at random, Xanthe Yarrow. I chose you because of where you grew up.*

*I chose you because your valley cannot lie to you.*

*Neither of us can survive what the Crown offers. But you might be the one person alive who can refuse it.*
