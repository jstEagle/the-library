## 6. What the Order Buried

Serah Kol had interrogated forty-one heretics in her nine years with the Hollow Order, and she had learned one thing above all others: liars were easy. Liars overexplained. Liars gave you details you hadn't asked for, like a merchant pressing extra fruit into your bag to hide the bruised ones underneath.

The truth, on the other hand, was quiet. The truth sat still and looked at you.

Which was why, standing before High Inquisitor Aldous Vane's desk at dawn, she could not stop thinking about the way he had flinched.

Not when she'd reported the scribe's escape. Not when she'd named Tamsin Ferrow, smuggler and occasional nuisance, as an accomplice. He had taken both of those with the smooth calm of a man listening to weather. It was only when she'd said *the map bears the seal of the Crown-keepers* that his quill had stopped moving. A half-second pause. Ink pooling where it shouldn't.

She'd filed it away the way she filed everything: neatly, alphabetically, and without opinion. That was her gift. Everyone said so. *Serah doesn't jump to conclusions,* they said, approvingly, the way one approves of a well-made lock.

Now, three days later, she stood in the Order's archive beneath the Citadel of Ashfall, holding a ledger that did not exist, and the lock inside her chest had begun, very quietly, to come apart.

* * *

The archive was Serah's favorite room in the world, though admitting this felt vaguely sinful. It was a long vaulted cellar where the Order kept its founding records, and it smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sweetness of old vellum. She came here when the interrogations went badly — when some trembling farmer wept about a grandmother who could light candles with a word, and Serah had to write *unrepentant sympathy for the erased arts* in the margin of his file afterward and hate herself a little.

The records were supposed to be simple. The Order's catechism was simple. Magic had been a plague upon the world; the founders had burned it out root and branch; the Crown — whatever fragmentary legends mentioned a Crown — had been destroyed in the Purification, shattered beyond mending, because anything that powerful could only be a wound.

*We hollowed the world to save it.* First lesson, first day, age seven. She had recited it ten thousand times. She could recite it in her sleep, and possibly had.

So why, then, did the accession rolls of the first High Inquisitors list an annual expenditure labeled only as *maintenance, item unspoken*?

She had found it by accident, which was how all dangerous things were found. She'd been cross-referencing grain shipments — the Citadel consumed an absurd quantity of lamp oil for an order that claimed to prize austerity — and there it was, year after year, century after century, a line item with no description and no explanation, paid from funds that should not have existed.

An order built on truth did not have line items it was ashamed of.

Serah pulled the oldest ledger off the shelf and set it on the reading table, and her hands were steady, because her hands were always steady, but her heart was doing something undignified.

* * *

It took her two nights. She worked by candlelight, cross-referencing, and somewhere past midnight on the second night she found the cipher key tucked inside the binding of a hymnal that had never been sung from — a hymnal whose pages, when held to the flame at the correct angle, revealed faint brown lines of hidden ink.

Gentle humor had never been her strength, but even she appreciated the joke: the Order's own archive hid its secrets the same way the heretics' books did. They had simply never taught anyone to look.

The hidden text was brief. It was a letter, unsigned, from the second High Inquisitor to the third, and it read:

*You ask again why we do not destroy the thing. I will answer plainly, since you are new and still believe what we told the world. We cannot destroy it. We tried for eleven years. Fire does not want it, water will not take it, and every hand that raises hammer against it forgets why it raised the hammer. So we keep it. We keep it, and we feed it silence, and we tell the novices it is ash. The valley wards are ours. The forgetting is ours. Understand me, brother: we did not end magic. We imprisoned it, and we have grown fat guarding the prison, and if the world ever learns this, the world will come for us with torches, and it will be right to.*

Serah sat back. The candle guttered. Somewhere above her, the Citadel creaked like an old ship, settling into its lies.

*We did not end magic. We imprisoned it.*

She read the letter four more times. Then, because she was Serah Kol and process was the only prayer she knew, she began verifying. She found the warding schedules — the "maintenance" — rotations of sworn brothers sent each season to a drowned mountain in the far north, carrying supplies no garrison needed and returning with reports sealed in the cipher. She found a requisition for black glass dust, ordered annually for two hundred years,用途 unstated. She found, at the very bottom of a locked drawer she had no business opening and opened anyway, a sketch.

It was a drawing of a crown. Black glass, seven points, rendered in careful archival ink by someone who had clearly seen it up close. Beneath it, in the same hand: *Item unspoken. Do not catalog.*

Someone had seen it. Someone had drawn it. Which meant someone had stood close enough to touch it, recently enough that the paper hadn't yellowed.

Her faith did not shatter. She would later wish it had; shattering was quick, and clean, and dramatic. Instead it did what old plaster does — it loosened, quietly, everywhere at once, so that when she pressed her thumb against any part of her life, it gave.

* * *

"You've been down here a long time," said Brother Cassian, appearing in the archive doorway with two cups of chicory and the air of a man who had never once suspected anyone of anything. He was sixty, round, kind, and the closest thing Serah had to a friend, which said something damning about her life that she chose not to examine.

"I'm cross-referencing," she said.

"Cross-referencing." He handed her a cup. "In my day we called that *avoiding the dining hall*, but I suppose scholarship has its fashions." He lowered himself onto a crate with a grunt. "Vane's asking after you. Wants the scribe found. There's talk."

"What kind of talk?"

"The kind that ends with a name on a writ." Cassian blew on his chicory. "They're calling it a mercy killing now. Did you know that? Used to be *execution*. Then *removal*. Now *mercy*. Words wear out faster than boots around here."

"Cassian." She turned the cup in her hands. "What do you remember about the Purification?"

He blinked. "What every novice remembers. The burning. The founders' courage."

"Do you remember it, or do you remember being taught it?"

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his eyes, like a fish under winter ice. Then he smiled his mild smile and said, "Careful, girl. Questions like that are how people end up on writs instead of writing them."

"Yes," Serah said. "I know."

* * *

The summons came at dusk. Vane received her in his study, where the lamps were lit against the early dark and the map of the known world hung behind him with the dead valley marked by a small, neat pin.

"Brother Kol." He did not look up from his papers. "Your report on the archive work?"

"Ongoing," she said. "I've found irregularities in the maintenance ledgers."

A pause. "Irregularities are common in old records."

"Not these. These are consistent. Two hundred years of consistent." She laid the cipher letter on his desk, face up, and watched his hands go still the way they had gone still over the words *Crown-keepers*. "Explain this to me, High Inquisitor. Explain it to me as though I were a novice, since apparently that is what I am."

Vane looked at the letter for a long time. When he finally raised his head, Serah braced herself for denial, for fury, for the smooth merchant's voice piling extra fruit over the bruises.

What she got was worse. What she got was tiredness.

"So," he said. "You found the hymnal."

"You knew."

"I have known for thirty years. My predecessor knew for forty. It is the inheritance of this office, Serah — the one true thing we pass along, along with everything else we're required to bury." He rose and walked to the window. Outside, Ashfall's ruined towers caught the last red light. "Do you imagine I sleep well? Do you imagine any of us do? We hold the prison. That is the whole of our faith now. Everything else is scaffolding."

"The catechism—"

"Is necessary. You've seen what belief in magic does to people, Serah. You've written the files. Whole villages tearing themselves apart over a woman who could warm milk. The world is *safer* believing magic died. And the Crown stays locked, and the wards stay fed, and the lie stays fed, and everyone lives." He turned. His face in the lamplight was older than she'd ever allowed herself to see. "That is the arithmetic. I won't apologize for it."

"And the scribe?" Her voice came out flat. "The boy with another man's memories in his skull. Where does he fit in your arithmetic?"

"He is a vector. The memories in him lead to the vault. Kill him, burn the body, salt the ground, and the prison holds another generation." Vane picked up his quill. "You'll take the writ yourself. You found him; it's fitting. Consider it an honor."

"Consider it—" She stopped. Breathed. "High Inquisitor. He is a debt-scribe from a valley we warded into blindness. He didn't choose any of this. He is carrying a dead man's ghosts through a world he's never seen, and you're sending me to murder him and call it mercy."

"I'm sending you to protect eight million people from a war over a weapon that rewrites history." Vane's voice hardened. "Yes. Call it mercy if it helps you sleep. It helps me."

She looked at him across the desk — at the tired, decent, damnable man who had recited *we hollowed the world to save it* to a thousand children while signing orders to feed the thing he'd told them was ash — and she understood, finally, what the stranger Vey must have understood, what made a man run bleeding through the dark with a folded map against his ribs.

Some prisons have walls. Some have ledgers.

"I'll take the writ," Serah said.

Vane nodded, already bent back over his papers. "Dawn departure. Take two blades from the third company."

"I won't need them."

* * *

She left the Citadel an hour before dawn, before the bells, before the third company woke. In her satchel: the cipher letter, the sketch of the black glass crown, two hundred years of maintenance ledgers, bread, cheese, a change of clothes, and a writ of execution bearing her own signature — which she had signed, in a small act of rebellion she suspected she'd treasure forever, with the wrong date.

At the gate, Brother Cassian was waiting with a lantern and a bundle.

"Dried plums," he said, handing it over. "For the road. You get sour without them."

"Cassian—"

"I know what you're doing, girl. I've known since you asked me about the Purification with that look on your face." He sighed, a great bellows-sigh of a man setting down something heavy. "My grandmother could light candles with a word. I wrote the file on a family for less, once, and I've regretted it for twenty years. So." He patted her shoulder once, briskly, the way men of his generation permitted themselves affection. "Go carefully. And Serah — if you find the thing under the mountain—"

She waited.

"Don't let them feed it anymore silence," he said. "It's been hungry a long time."

Then he turned back toward the Citadel, and Serah Kol walked out into the dark with her faith in a satchel and no idea, yet, that the road north would teach her the difference between a prisoner and a keeper — or that the scribe she'd been sent to kill would be the one to show her.
