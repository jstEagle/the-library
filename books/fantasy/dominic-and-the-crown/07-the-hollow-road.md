## 7. The Hollow Road

Three days out of Ashfall, Xanthe forgot his mother's face.

He didn't notice at first. That was the horror of it—the forgetting came quietly, like ink fading in a ledger left too long in the sun. One moment he was walking the old pilgrim road with Tamsin's grumbling commentary in his ears and Serah's wary silhouette ahead; the next he was trying to conjure his mother's smile and finding only a warm shape where her face should have been. He knew she had laughed with her whole body. He knew she'd smelled of lamp oil and bread. But her eyes—her eyes were gone, replaced by a blankness that made his stomach drop through the road itself.

In their place, unbidden, rose a woman who was not his mother: Vey's mother, tall and silver-browed, standing in a library of ten thousand burning books, saying *run*.

"Xanthe." Tamsin had fallen into step beside him. She was watching him with the flat, assessing look she used on cargo she suspected of being worth more than claimed. "You stopped walking."

"I did?"

"For twenty paces. You were staring at your hands." She glanced at them too, then back up. "You do that a lot lately."

"I'm counting my fingers," he said. "Making sure they're still mine."

Tamsin snorted. It should have been dismissive, but there was something underneath it—not quite kindness, because Tamsin would have bitten her own tongue off before admitting to kindness, but its rough country cousin. "Still ten?"

"Still ten." He flexed them. They obeyed. That was something.

"Good. Keep it that way. I've grown attached to the man who owes me money." She lengthened her stride to catch up with Serah, calling out something about the ford ahead and whether inquisitors could swim, and Xanthe stood alone on the road for one breath longer, holding his own name in his mouth like a stone he was afraid might dissolve.

*Xanthe Yarrow.* Scribe, third class, indentured to the temple of the valley of—

The valley of—

His heart stuttered. He reached for it the way you reach for a stair in the dark: *the valley of* — green terraces, the temple bell, the smell of wet slate after rain — all there, vivid, beloved, and utterly unnamed. The word itself had been scooped out of him like the flesh of a fruit.

Behind him, faint as a bell heard across water, another name rang instead. *Vey's* valley. A place called Sorrel's Hollow, with a river the color of hammered tin, where a boy once caught fireflies in a jar and pretended they were stars that had fallen just for him.

"That's not mine," Xanthe whispered. "That's not mine."

But his feet, walking on, felt like they belonged to someone else entirely.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of an old waystation, its roof half-collapsed, its hearth still sound enough for a small fire. Serah allowed it—a small fire, screened by stones, burned low—and sat with her back to the wall and her sword across her knees, which was how she did everything: sleeping, eating, once, memorably, praying.

"You're worse," she said, when Tamsin had gone to check the snares. Not cruelly. Serah never managed cruelty; it kept slipping into something more like grief.

"I'm fine."

"You said 'fine' twice today when asked about the weather. You told me the road east was west. And this afternoon—" she hesitated, and Xanthe braced himself, "—you called me by a name. Aldric. You apologized for missing our meeting in Corvane."

"I don't know anyone named Aldric."

"Vey did," Serah said quietly. "Aldric Vane. He was a keeper. We hunted him for six years before we took him in Corvane." She looked at the fire. "I was there. I signed the order."

Xanthe's skin went cold. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean—I don't remember doing it."

"That's rather the point, isn't it?" She drew her knees up, and for a moment the inquisitor's armor of composure thinned, and underneath was just a tired woman who had once believed the world was simple. "When I was trained, they taught us what memory-theft looks like. The vacant hours. The borrowed names. I've watched it happen to heretics we captured—watched them become someone else while their body kept breathing. I always thought it was a punishment. Something done *to* them." Her jaw tightened. "Nobody told me it could be a gift. Given freely. By a dying man who had no right."

"He was dying," Xanthe said. "He was afraid the Crown would be lost. He chose me because—" He stopped. Because his valley was magic-dead. Because whatever lived in Xanthe's head, the Crown's pull slid off him like rain off oiled cloth. He had known this since the vault beneath the drowned mountain, and still, saying it aloud felt like confessing to a crime.

"Because you're hollow," Serah said. Not unkindly. "Like the Order always claimed the whole world would have to be, to be safe."

"I'm not hollow." It came out sharper than he intended. "I'm a scribe. I copy ledgers. I have a mother whose face I can't—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, and pressed on anyway, because if he stopped he would not start again. "I had a life. Small, yes. Debt-bound, yes. But it was *mine*, every dull inch of it, and it's draining out of me day by day so that a dead man's memories have somewhere to live. I forget my valley's name. I forget my mother's face. Yesterday I caught myself reaching for a sword hilt I've never owned. So don't tell me I'm hollow, Serah Kol. I am the opposite of hollow. I am *overfull*, and everything that matters is being pushed out the top."

The silence after was long. Somewhere outside, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"I'm sorry," Serah said at last. "You're right. And I'll tell you something in return, since we're trading confessions tonight." She fed a stick to the fire. "I don't remember my childhood. Not any of it. The Order takes its initiates young and gives them new histories—clean ones, loyal ones. For years I told myself the erasure was mercy. A fresh page." A dry, terrible almost-smile. "I suppose I know now what a fresh page feels like from the other side."

Xanthe looked at her—at this woman who had been sent to kill him and had instead become the most reliable thing on the road—and felt something loosen in his chest. Two people, then, built out of borrowed rooms. At least neither of them was alone in the dark.

"We'll get it back," he said. "When the Crown is destroyed. Whatever's left of me—I'll write it down. Everything I still remember. Before it goes."

"A ledger of yourself."

"It's the only kind of book I know how to make."

Tamsin returned then, two thin rabbits dangling from her fist, and announced that anyone who wanted to eat had better help with the skinning, and that sentimental conversations by firelight made her teeth itch. But she handed Xanthe the knife first, and put the good flat rock under his elbow without being asked, which from Tamsin was practically a sonnet.

* * *

The ambush came at dawn, in a place called the Hollow Road because the land on either side had sunk away into fog-filled channels, leaving the track itself a raised spine of stone barely wide enough for two carts.

Serah smelled the trap first—or rather, smelled the absence of birdsong—and had time to say exactly one word, "*down*," before the crossbow bolts came hissing out of the mist.

Xanthe hit the roadbed hard, cheek against cold stone, and rolled toward the cover of a leaning milestone as a second volley splintered the air above him. Through the fog he saw shapes moving along the channel rims—white-and-grey, the colors of the Hollow Order, six of them at least, fanning out with the drilled patience of people who had done this many times.

"Inquisitor Kol!" a voice called, carrying clearly over the fog. "High Inquisitor Dren extends his regards. Bring the vessel and your reinstatement is assured. Your heresy will be entered as zeal. Think of what you were."

"I *have*," Serah called back, rising out of cover with her sword already drawn, and Xanthe heard in her voice something new and hard and entirely decided. "I thought about what I was all the way here. Then I read your founding charter, Dren. I know what the Order actually keeps in its vaults. Come and take the vessel if you're man enough to learn what I know!"

The answer was a third volley.

What followed, Xanthe would later piece together from fragments, because much of it happened too fast and too loudly for a scribe's mind to hold in order. Serah went up the near slope like something loosed from a bowstring, meeting two Order soldiers in the fog and turning the channel rim into a ringing, sparking chaos of steel. Tamsin grabbed Xanthe's collar and hauled him low along the roadbed. "Not that way," she hissed, pointing back down the spine of stone, "that's where the third squad isn't yet. Move."

They moved. Bolts struck the milestone behind them; a soldier dropped onto the road ahead, and Tamsin met him with a short blade and a smuggler's total absence of ceremony, and then they were past, running, the fog swallowing the fight behind them—

—except Tamsin stopped.

Xanthe turned. She had gone rigid, staring back the way they'd come, where a fourth figure had risen between them and Serah: an Order marksman, reloading with calm, terrible efficiency, bolt leveled not at Xanthe at all.

At Serah's unprotected back.

There was no calculation in what Tamsin did next. Later, Xanthe would understand that this was the whole of her: a woman who had spent her life calculating, weighing price against profit, and who in one unguarded instant threw all of it away. She stepped into the line of the shot.

The bolt took her high in the chest, below the collarbone, punching through with a sound Xanthe felt in his own ribs. She made no cry—only a grunt, almost annoyed, as if the world had overcharged her—and went down against the milestone.

"TAMSIN!" He was beside her before he knew his legs had decided anything. Blood spread across her shirt in a bloom too fast, too wide. Her hand found his wrist and gripped it with startling strength.

"Shut up," she said, before he could speak. "Whatever speech you're drafting—shut up. Listen." Her breath whistled. "The map. The seal on the map. There's a second fold. Under the river-mark. You never—" a cough, red at her lips, "—you never opened it. Open it. Promise me."

"I promise. I promise, but you have to—"

"Don't waste promises on me, scribe. Spend them on the Crown." Her grip tightened, then slackened by degrees, like a rope paying out. "Get her. Get Serah. Get out. And Xanthe—" her eyes found his, fierce and unfogged, "—whoever you end up being... make sure somebody chooses it. Don't let them choose for you."

Then her eyes rolled back, and the fog closed in around them both, and Xanthe screamed for Serah with a voice that came out of him sounding, for one horrible moment, like a dead man's voice—deeper, older, trained to carry across battlefields he had never seen.

Serah came. Of course she came—leaving two enemies broken behind her, cutting down the marksman before he could nock a second bolt, kneeling in the blood-soaked fog with her hands pressing hard where Xanthe's couldn't seem to stop shaking enough to press at all.

"She's breathing," Serah said. "It missed the lung—barely. We move her *now*."

Down the road, through the thinning fog, the surviving Order soldiers were regrouping. And ahead—Xanthe saw it with Vey's eyes before he saw it with his own, and that frightened him more than the soldiers ever could—a narrow goat-track climbing out of the hollow, toward a ruined watchtower on the ridge.

Toward shelter. Toward the second fold of the map.

Toward whatever Vey had hidden there, waiting all these years for a scribe with borrowed memories to come looking.

"Can you carry her?" Serah asked.

Xanthe slid his arms beneath Tamsin's shoulders and knees and lifted. She weighed less than he expected. People did, he thought, when they had given something away.

"Yes," he said—and meant his own voice, his own arms, his own choice, even as the fog behind them filled again with white-and-grey, and the tower waited above like a held breath.
