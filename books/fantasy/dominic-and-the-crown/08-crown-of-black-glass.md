## 8. Crown of Black Glass

The mountain had drowned a thousand years ago, and it had never learned to stop drowning.

Xanthe felt the water before he saw it—a cold that seeped through his boots and settled into his bones like a debt collector who refused to leave. The pass Tamsin led them down was littered with the skeletons of ships, their ribs arched against the grey sky as though they'd been beached mid-fleece. Except no sea had ever reached this high. The water had come up from below, once, in the age when magic still moved the world, and it had swallowed the mountain whole before draining away again and leaving only its memory: salt-stained stone, kelp fossilized in black ribbons across the rock, and everywhere, everywhere, the faint hum.

The valley had taught him silence. The world outside hummed. But here, at the throat of the drowned mountain, the hum became something closer to singing.

"Stop gawking," Tamsin said, though she said it kindly, which for Tamsin was roughly equivalent to a sonnet. "Gawk later. Walk now."

"I'm not gawking," Xanthe said. "I'm observing. There's a difference. Scribes are trained observers."

"You're standing in the middle of the path with your mouth open."

"That's how scribes observe."

Serah, walking behind them with her hood down and her Order insignia torn off days ago—she'd buried it under a cairn like a small funeral—said nothing. She hadn't said much since the ambush. Tamsin's wound had closed, thanks to Serah's field-craft and Tamsin's truly heroic refusal to complain, but the ambush itself had cost them their mule, half their food, and most of their margin. The Hollow Order knew where they were going now. Of course it did. The map had always pointed here; they had simply been following it loudly enough to be heard.

"Left fork ahead," Tamsin said. She leaned on her spear more than she'd admit. "Then we go down. Then we keep going down. Then, if my grandmother's stories hold, we stop going down because there is no more down."

"And if her stories don't hold?"

"Then you'll have plenty of time to think about it on the way."

* * *

They descended through halls that had been built by hands that understood stone the way Xanthe understood ink—not as material but as language. Pillars carved with spirals that seemed to turn when he wasn't looking. Doorways sized for people taller than any he'd met. And threaded through everything, veins of glass, black as dried blood, running through the rock like the mountain's own handwriting.

He caught himself reading them.

That was the thing he could no longer pretend away: he *read* them. Not the way a traveler reads a strange inscription, squinting and guessing. He read them the way he read temple script—fluently, without effort, the meaning arriving before he'd decided to look. *The door remembers. The water keeps what it takes. We were wrong to dig.*

"Vey again?" Serah asked quietly. She'd learned to recognize the signs: the stillness, the slight unfocusing of his eyes, the way his accent shifted toward vowels from a country he'd never seen.

"It's not exactly Vey." Xanthe pressed his palm against the cold wall. "It's older. It's like... imagine someone copied out a ledger, and then someone copied the copy, and then a hundred copies down the line, someone copied mine. That's what these memories are. Vey carried keepers before him. They carried keepers before them. I'm not holding one man's life. I'm holding a library."

Tamsin snorted. "A library that walks and complains about its feet. Marvelous."

"A library that bleeds," Serah said, and the humor went out of the corridor.

Because it did bleed. Every day the borders thinned further. Two nights ago he'd woken calling Tamsin by a name that belonged to some keeper dead four centuries, and Tamsin had sat up with her knife drawn before either of them knew what was happening. Yesterday he'd reached for his mother's face—the round, flour-dusted, eternally tired face of her—and found a burning library instead, shelves collapsing in slow amber light, and somewhere in the smoke a voice saying *save the index, save the index*, and beneath that, fainter every day, his mother's voice saying *Xanthe, love, mind the ink.*

He still knew her name. He still knew his own. But knowing and feeling were drifting apart like two boats cut loose in the same current, and he could no longer say which of them would reach shore first.

"How much longer do you have?" Tamsin asked. It was the first time she'd asked it directly. Smugglers, Xanthe was learning, priced things honestly eventually.

"I don't know. A while. Not forever."

"Good," she said. "Forever's a bad business model."

* * *

The last door was not locked. It didn't need to be.

It stood at the bottom of a stair that had ended in open water once—the tide line was still visible on the walls, a dark ring like a bruise—but the water had withdrawn, or been withdrawn, leaving a chamber vast enough that their single lantern seemed an act of charity rather than illumination. And in the center of the chamber, resting on a pedestal of the same black glass, was a shape Xanthe had seen only in dreams and borrowed memories: a crown. Simple, almost plain, a circlet of black glass worked into points like frozen flames. It gave off no light. It drank the lantern's instead, so that looking at it was like looking at a hole cut in the world.

None of them spoke. Even the hum went quiet, as if the mountain were holding its breath.

"Well," Tamsin said at last, hoarse. "There it is. The thing everyone's killed each other over. Looks like a fancy ashtray."

"Tamsin." Serah's warning tone.

"What? I've smuggled relics before. Half of them are ashtrays. The other half are paperweights with good marketing."

But she didn't move toward it, and neither did Serah, and after a moment Xanthe understood why. The Crown was not pulling at them. It was waiting for him specifically, the way a ledger waits for the hand trained to open it, and the others could feel themselves excluded—polite guests outside a private office.

He walked forward. His legs felt borrowed. Everything felt borrowed now; that was the joke of it, the bitter little joke he kept telling himself to stay upright. He stopped an arm's length from the pedestal.

And Vey rose in him like floodwater.

Not gradually. All at once, the final memory unlocking as if it had been waiting behind a door marked *only upon arrival.* Xanthe gripped the pedestal's edge and let it take him—

—

*A room of white stone. Vey, younger than Xanthe had ever seen him, kneeling before three other keepers, old ones, their faces worn smooth as river stones.*

*"It must be a valley," the eldest is saying. "You understand why. The Crown speaks to anyone who comes near it—it has spoken to all of us our whole lives, and we have spent our whole lives pretending we don't listen. We cannot carry it anymore. We cannot even guard it honestly. So we send it beyond its own voice, to a place where magic cannot survive. Where its whisper arrives as silence. Whoever grows up in that silence—"*

*"Will be deaf to it," Vey says. "Immune."*

*"Will be free," the old woman corrects. "There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Listen to me, Vey. You will be tempted to choose someone strong. Someone clever, someone ambitious, someone who wants the world remade. Resist it. The Crown does not need a strong bearer. It needs a bearer who does not want it. Find us a scribe. Find us a man who copies other people's words for a living and knows exactly how heavy they are."*

*Vey laughs—actually laughs, wet-eyed, exhausted. "You want me to find a man who doesn't want power, in this century?"*

*"We want you to find a man whose debts are paid in ink," she says. "They exist. They are rarer than kings, but they exist. And when you find him, you will give him everything—your memories, your mission, your mistakes—and you will not tell him why he was chosen until he stands in front of it. Because if he knows too early, he will start wanting to deserve it. And a man who wants to deserve the Crown already belongs to it."*

*And Vey, kneeling in white stone, says: "Then I'll need a valley where nobody believes in magic at all. Somewhere the Order already won. Somewhere safe and small and quiet."*

*"Yes."*

*"Somewhere," Vey says slowly, "like Yarrowmere."*

—

Xanthe came back to himself with tears freezing on his cheeks and both friends gripping his arms.

"Yarrowmere," he said aloud. "He chose my valley on purpose. The temple, the debt, the copying—all of it. My whole life was a... a preparation I never agreed to."

"No," Serah said fiercely. "Listen to me, Xanthe. Listen. They prepared a *place*. They didn't prepare a man. No one made you hide a bleeding stranger. No one made you cross the gate. No one made you learn Tamsin's jokes or carry her for six miles or argue with inquisitors. The valley was a shield, not a script. What you did inside it was yours."

He wanted to believe her. He examined the thought the way he examined a suspect ledger line, looking for the flaw, the falsified entry—and found, to his surprise, that it balanced. Vey had chosen a valley. Vey had not chosen the boy who hesitated at a door and opened it anyway.

"The Crown can restore magic," he said. "All of it. Everywhere. Or..."

"Or," Serah said, "its bearer rewrites history. Unmakes what was done. Undoes the erasure so completely that it never happened—including, if he wishes, the people who did it." Her jaw tightened. "Including me. I know what I am to that offer, Xanthe. A mistake to be corrected."

"And Vey couldn't touch it," Xanthe said. "None of the keepers could. They'd listened to it too long. So they needed someone deaf. Someone from the silence." He looked at the Crown—at the hole in the world wearing a circlet. "And now I'm standing in front of it, and I'm not entirely deaf anymore, am I? Because I've been carrying Vey, and Vey carried them, and somewhere in this library of mine there's a hundred voices that all listened."

The hum returned. Faint. Patient. It sounded, horribly, like his own name spoken by someone who loved him.

* * *

"They're here," Tamsin said.

She'd heard it before any of them—the smuggler's ear, tuned to boots on stone. Through the great doorway, far up the descending passage, lanterns bloomed one after another, dozens of them, a constellation crawling downward. Steel caught the light between them. The Hollow Order had not sent an inquisitor and a squad this time. They had sent a verdict.

"How long?" Serah asked, already moving, unshouldering her pack.

"An hour. Less."

"Then we do it now." Serah turned to Xanthe, and her eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely certain. "Touch it. Take what it offers—everything, Vey's whole life, all the strength of it—and end this. You can rewrite them. You can make them never have existed. You can—"

"Serah." He said it gently. "You just told me what that costs."

"I told you what it costs *me*. I'm offering to pay it."

"And I'm declining the payment." He looked at the Crown. The hum swelled, tender, insidious: *you could have her back, the mother, the flour dust, the voice saying mind the ink—you could have it all, and more, you could have every version of yourself you might have been—*

His hand rose toward the black glass.

Behind him, far above, the first war-horns of the Hollow Order began to sound, rolling down the drowned mountain like thunder remembering an old argument.

His fingers stopped a breath from the surface.

"Not yet," Xanthe whispered—to the Crown, to Vey, to the hundred keepers stacked inside him like pages. "First, I want to hear exactly what you're selling. Because I've read enough contracts to know the fine print is where you bury the body."

The Crown, for the first time in a thousand years, laughed.
