## 10. A Scribe of Two Hands

The valley still had no name that Xanthe was allowed to write down, but he had decided, privately, to call it Home, with a capital H and a small apology to no one.

He came back in the spring, when the thaw turned the mountain paths to mud and the mud to green, and the gate — the same gate he had fled through with Hollow Order hunters at his back and a dead man's memories rattling in his skull — stood open. It had been standing open for months now. Nobody guarded it anymore. There was nothing left on either side worth keeping apart.

The first thing he noticed was the bees.

There had never been bees in the valley. The temple's ledgers said so, in his own careful hand: *pollination achieved by wind and diligent hand-carried pollen cloth; apiaries forbidden.* But the orchard rows along the eastern terraces hummed now, softly, the way a struck tuning fork hums after the sound has technically ended, and the bees moved through the blossoms in threads of gold light instead of wings. They weren't quite bees. They were what magic remembered about bees, drawn from somewhere deep, sketching them back into the world the way a scribe sketches a letter he has only heard described.

Xanthe stopped and watched them for a long time, and felt the shard in his chest warm gently, like a stone left too near a hearth.

*Slowly,* Vey's memory whispered. *It must come back slowly, or it will come back as it was.*

"I know," Xanthe said aloud, because talking to a dead man's memory was no longer the strangest thing he did before breakfast. "I'm writing it down."

---

The temple had not changed, except in all the ways that mattered.

The scriptorium windows were open now. That alone would have told him everything, even if he hadn't already known. For sixty years the valley's windows had been shuttered against the world, because the world was full of things the temple's creditors didn't want drifting in — songs, rumors, the word *magic* spoken carelessly by travelers who didn't know better. Now the shutters leaned against the walls collecting dust, and the morning air came through carrying beesong and the smell of wet stone, and nobody flinched.

Brother Aldous met him at the scriptorium door. He was older, grayer, and he had taken off the warden's gray sash entirely, which for Aldous was roughly equivalent to dancing naked through the refectory.

"You're late," Aldous said. "Your debt was dissolved in the winter. Formally. There was a document."

"There's always a document."

"There is. It's on your desk." Aldous paused, then added, in the tone of a man confessing a crime: "We kept your desk. I don't know why. It seemed important not to move it."

Xanthe crossed the scriptorium slowly, reading the room the way he'd once read ledgers. The copying stands held new work — not temple records but letters, petitions, a half-finished translation of something with a foreign seal. On the wall where the Rules of Containment had hung, framed behind glass like scripture, there was now a child's drawing of the valley with enormous golden bees over it, contributed by someone's daughter and defended fiercely when anyone suggested taking it down.

His desk sat in its old corner by the north window. On it: the dissolution document, stamped and countersigned and slightly ridiculous. A pot of ink gone dry from waiting. And beside them, stacked with the reverence usually reserved for relics, the ledgers.

Every ledger he had ever copied. Every falsified record, every erased name, every entry where the temple had written *nothing of note occurred* over the top of something very much of note. Sixty years of a valley pretending it had always been empty of magic, copied faithfully by a boy who had been too well-trained to ask why the falsifications needed such beautiful handwriting.

Aldous cleared his throat. "The new prior thought you might want them. For the burning."

"The new prior is an optimist," Xanthe said. "I'm going to read every one."

* * *

It took him until midsummer to understand what he was actually writing, and until autumn to admit it out loud.

He had started, as he told himself, with restoration — matching each falsified ledger against the shard's long memory of what the Order had buried everywhere, cross-referencing dates, recovering names. Vey's memories were good for this. Vey had spent forty years cataloguing exactly this kind of theft, and when Xanthe reached for that part of the borrowed past, it came willingly, tidy and patient, a colleague leaning over his shoulder rather than a ghost trying on his skin.

That was the difference, he had learned, between carrying a memory and being carried by one. He checked often. He knew his mother's face again — he'd wept into Tamsin's shoulder on a mountainside when it returned, and she had pretended to find this extremely annoying while gripping him hard enough to bruise. He knew the valley's real old name, the one from before the temple. He knew which thoughts were stitched from Vey and which were his own, and the seam between them had healed clean, the way a well-set bone heals.

But the work grew. It kept growing. Because the falsifications had a shape, and the shape pointed somewhere, and by autumn Xanthe was no longer restoring records. He was writing a history. *The* history. What the Order found when it was young and poor and afraid; how it discovered the Crown instead of destroying it; how a handful of men decided the world was safer without magic and quietly arranged for the world to agree. How they had not burned magic out of the world but hoarded its single source beneath a drowned mountain and called the resulting silence peace.

He wrote it in two hands. That was the joke Tamsin had coined, and it stuck, and eventually it became simply true. His right hand — his own — wrote the valley's story, small and precise and full of ordinary people doing their best inside a lie. His left hand, trained up over months from Vey's habits, wrote the Order's story, in a sharper script, with margins full of citations from a library that no longer existed except in his head.

Some nights both hands ached and he fell asleep at the desk with ink on his cheek, and dreamed — rarely, gently now — of a burning library whose books stepped off their shelves and walked out through the smoke to safety, one by one, unhurried, because someone had planned for the fire.

* * *

Tamsin arrived in the first week of the harvest, loudly, on a cart pulled by a mule that glowed faintly at the hooves and was, she insisted, entirely normal.

"Don't start," she said, before Xanthe could say anything. "The farrier says it's a coat condition. I've decided to believe the farrier."

"How's the leg?"

"Better than the leg deserves." She'd taken a spear through the thigh on the Hollow Road buying him twenty minutes, and had spent the following four months alternately healing and threatening to haunt everyone involved if she died of anything so undignified as a wound honestly earned. "Serah sends her regards. Also a box. Don't shake the box."

He shook the box later, of course. It clinked. Inside, packed in wool, lay a dozen shards of black glass, each no bigger than a thumbnail, each humming faintly — fragments of the shattered Crown recovered by the new Order, catalogued, warded, and distributed to keepers across the world. Serah's note was brief and in her exact voice:

*Not for use. For remembering. If we lose the memory of what it cost, we'll build another one within a century. Humans are like that. — S.K.*

*P.S. The Order has a new oath. You're quoted in it. I fought this and lost.*

Below the fold, someone — presumably Serah, presumably under protest — had copied out the line: *Let those who guard power remember they are made of smaller, better things.*

"That's going straight on the wall," Tamsin said, reading over his shoulder and stealing a plum from his bowl. "Right next to the bee drawing."

"Serah will be mortified."

"Serah rebuilt a hundred-year conspiracy of liars into a school for librarians with swords. Let her be mortified, it builds character." Tamsin dropped onto the bench across from him, and her voice changed — quieter, the way it only did once or twice a year. "I passed the gate coming in. Stood there a while. Last time I came through it, I was smuggling you out under a false bottom and a false name, and I genuinely thought we'd both hang." She turned the plum in her fingers. "Strange, isn't it. All that, and the valley just... opens the gate."

"It was never locked from outside," Xanthe said. "Only from in."

She looked at him a moment, then laughed, short and real. "Write that down, scribe. That's a whole chapter."

* * *

Winter came early and kind, and Xanthe finished the first draft of the true history on the shortest day of the year, at dusk, by lamplight, with snow ticking against the scriptorium window and the golden bees dormant somewhere in the heart of the orchard, dreaming whatever bees dream.

He sat back and read the last page, and did not feel triumphant. He felt — and this surprised him, though perhaps it shouldn't have — ordinary. Tired in a good way. Hungry for supper. The way he'd felt at the end of any ordinary ledger, any honest day's copying, back when his life was small enough to fit inside a scriptorium and he'd ached against the walls of it without knowing the walls were the kindest thing about the place.

Vey had chosen him, Vey's last memory said, because the valley had made him immune to the Crown's pull. A man with no appetite for greatness. A man who hid a bleeding stranger rather than turn him in, not out of courage, but because hiding him was the decent thing and decency doesn't check first whether it will survive.

Well. Here he was, then. Immune, ordinary, alive. Two hands inky to the wrist, one borrowed memory kept like a seed in a folded map, and a world outside the window slowly, thread by golden thread, remembering itself.

He blew out the lamp. Snow, moonlight, the smell of ink.

He was halfway to the door when the shard warmed — sharply, urgently, the way it had not warmed since the vault beneath the drowned mountain — and Vey's voice, clear as a bell across sixty years of death, spoke one sentence into the quiet of the scriptorium:

*"Xanthe. There is a second Crown."*
