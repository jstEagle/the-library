## 2. A Knock at the Thirteenth Hour

There was no thirteenth hour. Every thief in Grayharbor knew that. The clock in the Lantern District struck twelve and then, if you were foolish enough to keep listening, it struck nothing at all, and the silence after was where the Ember did its business.

Freya Reyes had spent eleven years learning to love that silence. He stood in the guild's receiving hall now, hands still cold from the night's work—a bottle of grief lifted from a merchant's widow who'd paid to have it taken, a coil of borrowed years wrapped in oilcloth against his ribs—and he let the quiet settle over him like a coat that fit too well.

The hall itself was built to be forgotten. It sat behind a chandler's shop on Candlemass Row, accessible through a door disguised as a delivery entrance, warded with sigils that made the eye slide off it the way water slides off wax. Inside, it was all dark wood and low light, shelves of stoppered vials glinting like a pharmacy for the soul, which, Freya supposed, was exactly what it was. Somewhere above, Maren the cook was banging pots in the kitchen, arguing with the chimney about draft. Somewhere below, the ledger room waited with its iron door and its thousand books of impossible accounts.

Freya was cataloging the widow's grief—*one sorrow, bottled, provenance: marital, age: nineteen years, condition: thick as tar*—when the knock came.

Three knocks. Soft. Almost apologetic.

Everyone in the hall stopped moving. Pell, the lockman, froze with his tools half-sunk in a padlock he was picking for practice. Sefa and the twins looked up from their card game, and old Hollis set down the knife he'd been sharpening with the deliberate calm of a man who had survived four decades of thievery by assuming the worst of every sound.

"It's past midnight," Pell said unnecessarily.

"Obviously," said Hollis, and drew his knife anyway.

The hidden door of the Ember had no knocker. That was rather the point of it. You didn't knock on the Ember; you were brought, or you found it by means that usually ended with you in the river. And yet: three soft knocks, spaced like heartbeats, patient as snowfall.

Freya set down his pen. Later, he would try to explain to himself why he was the one who crossed the hall, why he put his eye to the brass viewing-slit that Hollis himself had installed thirty years ago. He would fail to find a reason. There was only the fact of it: his feet carried him, and he let them.

Through the slit he saw snow, blue-white under a cloud-shredded moon. He saw the alley wall, the drainage grate, the refuse that never quite made it to collection. And then he looked down.

A child stood at the door. Barefoot. In snow that came to his shins. A boy, perhaps seven years old, perhaps younger—it was hard to tell, because he was so thin, because his hair was white as the snow around him, because his eyes, when they lifted to the viewing slit with terrible precision, were the color of banked coals. As if he knew exactly where Freya's face would be.

"He's alone," Freya heard himself say.

"Then he's bait," said Hollis.

"Or mad," said Sefa.

"Or sent." Pell had gone pale. "Nobody finds this door by accident."

Another knock. Softer now, as if the child's small fist was tiring. Freya noticed the boy's feet then, really noticed them: they weren't blue. They weren't even shaking. He stood in the freezing snow like a boy standing in summer grass, and the snow around his ankles had not melted but had *arranged itself*, falling in slow spirals toward him as though drawn by something gentle and vast beneath the ground he occupied.

Freya unbolted the door.

He heard Hollis swear. He heard Maren come out of the kitchen wiping her hands, heard her sharp intake of breath. None of it mattered. Cold air poured in, smelling of iron and winter, and the child looked up at him without fear, without much of anything except an ancient patience that had no business living in so small a face.

The boy opened his mouth and spoke.

It was not any language Freya knew, and he knew many—he'd been required to learn them, because you cannot steal from people whose words you can't read. This was older. The sounds came out rounded and strange, consonants like stones turning underwater, vowels held long past comfort, and each word seemed to arrive slightly *before* the boy spoke it, a half-second echo running backward. The hair on Freya's arms stood up.

"I can't understand you," he said gently, crouching. "Do you understand me?"

The child tilted his head. Then he spoke again—the same phrase, Freya thought, or nearly the same—and this time Freya felt it somewhere behind his sternum, a vibration like a struck bell heard through a wall. *Come in. I'm cold. Come in.* No—that wasn't right. It wasn't a plea. It was more like a statement of fact about the world, delivered in the tone one uses for *the sun rises.*

"Gods' teeth," Maren breathed. She pushed forward, all flour and ferocity, and knelt beside Freya. "Look at his feet. Look at—" She reached out, and the boy watched her hand come toward him with mild curiosity, and when she touched his ankle he flinched, just once, the way you flinch at a memory rather than a touch. "He's frozen solid and he isn't. What is he?"

"He's a boy," Freya said. He didn't know why he said it with such certainty. He didn't know why his voice had gone quiet.

"Get him inside before someone sees the door open," Hollis snapped. "And Reyes—if this goes wrong, it's your name in the book."

That was the law of the house, and everyone knew it: whoever brought a thing through the door owned its consequences. Freya had invoked it a hundred times, taking responsibility for stolen memories, for goods that screamed softly in their wrappings. He had never once had it turned back on him like a blade.

"Fine," he said. "My name in the book."

They got the boy inside. Maren wrapped him in the good blanket, the one from her own cot, and sat him by the kitchen fire, and here was the second impossibility of the night: the boy held his hands to the flames the way you'd hold them to a painting of fire, politely, curiously, as though warmth were a custom he'd heard of but never practiced. His feet stayed bare and pale and utterly unharmed. When Maren pressed a bowl of stew into his hands, he studied the spoon for a long moment, then ate with his fingers, carefully, methodically, watching their faces between bites as if cataloguing how eating was done.

And all the while he kept speaking. That low, stone-under-water language, murmured mostly to himself, sometimes rising at the end like a question. Nobody answered, because nobody could.

Except, Freya discovered, one thing could.

He had drifted back to the receiving desk, uneasy, reaching by habit for his pen and the ledger page he'd abandoned—the widow's grief, still uncataloged. And as the boy's voice rose and fell behind him, something happened to the ink.

Freya stared. The entry he'd written an hour ago—his own tidy script, *one sorrow, bottled, provenance: marital*—was moving. Not smearing. *Rearranging.* The letters crawled like ants, broke apart, and reformed into shapes he had never seen and yet somehow recognized: the same spiraling, deep-water characters the child was speaking. They settled onto the page and sat there, elegant and wrong, waiting.

"No," Freya said quietly.

"What?" Pell came over. Followed his stare. Went whiter than the boy's hair. "Reyes. What is that."

"That's my handwriting. Was my handwriting."

"The ledger doesn't do that." Pell's voice cracked. "The ledgers don't *do anything*. They're paper. Corvane says—"

"Corvane says a lot of things," Freya murmured, and closed the book, and felt, through the leather cover, a faint warmth. Like holding your palm over a sleeping animal.

Down in the ledger room, behind three locks and a ward-sealed door, other books were doing the same thing. He was sure of it. He could feel it in his teeth, the way you feel a storm coming.

* *

Corvane arrived within the hour, which told Freya that someone had run for him, and also that the guildmaster had been awake, dressed, and waiting for something at one in the morning. He came into the kitchen still buttoning his coat, silver-haired, unhurried, his face arranged into its usual expression of reasonable concern. Thirty years of running the Ember had worn Corvane smooth, like a river stone—all edges long since polished away. People trusted that smoothness. Freya had trusted it once.

"Well," Corvane said, surveying the scene: the boy by the fire, the blanket, the ring of thieves keeping a careful distance. "What have we here?"

"A child," said Maren. "Barefoot in the snow. Found him at the door."

"Found him," Corvane repeated, tasting the word. His eyes moved across the boy once, quickly—the practiced sweep of a man appraising goods—and Freya watched it happen and hated that he recognized it, because he did the same sweep himself, nightly, professionally. "Alone? Speaking?"

"Speaking nothing anyone knows," Freya said. He laid the ledger on the table, open to the corrupted page. "And this."

For the first time in eleven years, Freya saw Corvane hesitate.

It lasted less than a breath. But Freya caught it—the fractional stillness, the way the guildmaster's gaze touched those crawling spiral letters and then jumped away, the way a man's eyes jump away from something he recognizes and wishes he didn't. Then Corvane smiled, warm as ever, and picked up the book.

"Clerical mischief," he said lightly. "One of Pell's jokes, I expect."

"It happened while the boy was talking."

"Ink moves. Old ink especially. There are compounds in these storerooms that could—" 

"Guildmaster." Hollis's voice, flat. "I've been in the trade forty years. I know what mischief looks like. That's not mischief."

Silence. The fire popped. The boy, seemingly unaware that his fate was being argued over the top of his head, had fallen asleep sitting up, mid-word, his chin dropping to his chest. Even asleep, his lips moved faintly. Even asleep, Freya realized, the man was still speaking. Praying, maybe. Counting. Reciting something he could not put down, the way a drowning man cannot put down his own name.

Corvane set the ledger down. "The question," he said pleasantly, "is what to do with him. We are thieves, not an orphanage. A child in this house is a liability—a witness, a weakness, a thread that anyone competent could pull until our whole sweater unravels. The sensible course—" he spread his hands, "—is to turn him out at dawn. Perhaps deliver him to a temple. Wash our hands."

"And if he freezes?" Maren demanded.

"He walked through a blizzard barefoot, Maren. I suspect the cold has already rendered its opinion."

"The door," Pell said. His voice was thin. "Nobody finds that door. Nobody. Whatever he is, whatever sent him—he came *here*. On purpose. Turning him out doesn't un-ring that bell."

"Then we're agreed there's mystery," Corvane said smoothly. "Mystery argues for caution, not charity. Hollis?"

Hollis looked at the sleeping boy for a long time. "Forty years," he said finally, "I've held to one rule: leave nothing at the door that can follow you back inside. But rules are for keeping us alive, not for making us small." He sheathed his knife. "He stays till dawn. Then we vote."

"We don't need a vote if the guildmaster decides," Sefa said, and there was something careful in the way she said it, something that made Freya look at her, and then at Corvane, and file both faces away.

"I decide operational matters," Corvane agreed mildly. "The disposition of strays is a house matter. We'll vote." He turned to go, and paused at the doorway, and looked back at the boy with an expression Freya couldn't read—too complex for disgust, too controlled for fear. Hunger, maybe. Or recognition. "Interesting times," he said, to no one in particular, and left.

* *

The vote, at dawn, went the boy's way—but barely. Six to five. Maren counted the hands twice and announced the result with grim satisfaction, and half the room wore the expression of people who had lost money on a fight.

Freya had voted yes. He'd raised his hand before he fully understood he meant to, and afterward he sat with the strange aftershock of it. Eleven years in the trade had taught him a discipline of not-feeling: you cataloged the widow's grief and felt nothing, you moved the stolen years and felt nothing, you went home to your narrow room and slept without dreaming and called it professionalism. It worked. It had kept him whole, or whatever passed for whole.

But when he raised his hand, something had cracked in him—a hairline fracture, fine as a scratch on glass, running through all that careful nothing. And through the crack came cold air, and the smell of snow, and the image of a barefoot child arranging the winter around his ankles like a familiar dog.

He didn't know what to do with it. Feeling, it turned out, was like any contraband: dangerous to hold, hard to fence, impossible to unsteal once it was in your hands.

In the kitchen, Maren had named him already. "Ash," she declared, pointing her wooden spoon at the boy, who watched the spoon with deep suspicion. "White hair, coal eyes, turns up out of a fire nobody saw. Ash. It's not up for debate."

The boy considered her. Then, slowly, deliberately, he reached out and touched the back of her hand—the first time any of them had seen him initiate contact—and said something in his rolling, underwater language. Two syllables. Soft ones.

"What did he say?" Maren whispered, as if Freya would know.

"I don't know."

But later that morning, seeking refuge in routine, Freya sat at his desk and pulled the ledger toward him to finish the interrupted entry—and stopped.

The corrupted page had changed again. The spiral letters had shifted, gathered themselves into a new line beneath his ruined entry. Not many words. Just two.

And though no one had taught him, though there was no earthly reason he should understand them, Freya read them plainly, the meaning arriving behind his eyes the way the boy's voice arrived ahead of his mouth:

*Ask him what he sold.*

Freya looked up. Through the doorway, down the hall, he could hear Maren talking to Ash in her kitchen, teaching him the names of things—*spoon, bread, fire*—her voice bright and fierce and kind.

And in his teeth, faint but unmistakable, Freya felt the ledger room humming.
