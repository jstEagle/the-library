## 3. Ink That Remembers

The ledger room smelled of tallow and iron and the particular dust that gathers on things no one is meant to touch.

Freya had spent eleven years learning to love that smell. He told himself it was the smell of order—the Ember's true cathedral, where every impossible thing the guild bought, sold, or held in trust was rendered into columns of ink so neat they might have been typeset rather than written by human hands. Stolen years, filed by decade. Bottled grief, cataloged by hue. Memories pried from sleeping minds, cross-referenced by the street they'd been taken from, the season, the weather. The ledger room did not judge. The ledger room only *recorded*, and there was a cold comfort in that which Freya had built his whole life upon, the way a man builds a house on a foundation he refuses to look at too closely.

He lit the third lamp because two were not enough for what he intended, and then stood for a long moment with the taper still burning, listening to the guild sleep above him.

They had put the boy in the room off the kitchen. Maren's doing—Maren, who ruled the kitchen the way small kings rule valleys, absolutely and without appeal—and Corvane had allowed it, which surprised Freya more than he liked to admit. Guildmaster Corvane allowed very little. But he had looked at the child shivering in the entryway, snow melting off him in gray puddles, mouth opening around words that made the lamps gutter, and he had said only: *"Warm him. We will decide in daylight."*

Daylight had come and gone twice since, and no decision had been made, and the boy—still nameless; Maren called him *little wren* and fed him honey in his porridge as though naming were something you earned—had simply... stayed. Like weather. Like a debt.

Freya sat down at the long table, drew the oldest ledger toward him across the scarred wood, and told himself this was routine.

That was the lie he would carry through the rest of it, he understood later. Routine. A keeper of accounts does not go looking for trouble in thirty-year-old folios at the hour when even thieves sleep. A keeper of accounts balances what is placed before him and asks nothing else. But the boy had spoken last night—not to anyone, just standing at the kitchen window watching the snow, his voice a dry rustle like pages turning—and Maren had gone white and dropped a spoon, and Freya had felt something turn over in his chest that he had thought long since amortized down to nothing.

And the sigils. That was the true reason, if he let himself be honest, and honesty in the ledger room was a dangerous habit. When the boy spoke, certain marks appeared at the corner of Freya's vision—not heard, exactly. Not seen, either. More like the afterimage of a flame pressed against closed eyelids. Little hooked characters, angular, deliberate, like entries in a hand he almost recognized.

Almost recognizing a hand was the kind of thing that could get a man killed in his trade.

* * *

He started with the year ledgers because they were arranged chronologically, and whatever had happened to the boy—if anything had happened, if the sigils meant anything at all—would have an entry somewhere, because everything had an entry. That was the first law of the Ember, older than Corvane, older than the guildhall itself, older than the false wall behind the wine cellar and the door with thirteen locks: *What is not written does not exist.*

Freya had once believed that law was protection. It had taken him most of a decade to understand it was also a cage, and another three years to stop caring about the difference.

The old volumes came off their shelves with a sound like sighing. Their spines were leather gone soft as cloth, dyed the deep oxblood that was the guild's color, stamped in blind with the sigil of the ember-in-a-closed-hand. He worked backward through them methodically, lamp by lamp, page by page, and the hours folded themselves away the way they always did when he was among the books—cleanly, without complaint. This was the thing he was best at in all the world, maybe the only thing: sitting alone with the record of other people's lives and feeling precisely nothing.

Except tonight the nothing had a texture to it. Tonight the nothing kept glancing toward the ceiling, toward the kitchen, toward the small sound of breathing he could almost convince himself he could hear through four floors of stone.

Thirty years back. Thirty-five. Forty. The hands changed—Corvane's script, always, in the master entries, but the subordinate clerks came and went, their flourishes rising and falling with fashion, and Freya read them all like faces in a crowd. Purchase of one memory, residential, spring rain. Sale of eleven years, term certain, buyer's mark obscured. Grief, decanted, one measure, premium grade, the weeping of a mother at a graveside—those always fetched well; grief was the guild's most reliable vintage.

It was in the forty-first volume back, in the deep shelf where the ledgers went to be forgotten rather than archived, that the handwriting changed.

Not changed, exactly. *Returned.* The clerk's hand for those years was competent enough until a certain date in autumn, and then, without explanation, the entries began to appear in a different script altogether—angular, hooked, deliberate, each character formed with a precision that was almost architectural, as though the writer were not recording language but constructing it.

Freya's breath stopped in his throat before he understood why.

The sigils. The boy's sigils—or whatever lived behind the boy's voice and surfaced at the edges of Freya's sight when he spoke—were the same marks. Not similar. *Same*, stroke for stroke, the little hooked ascenders, the base lines struck through with a single decisive motion. Someone had written these pages who wrote the way the boy dreamed.

His hands were steady. He noted that with distant professional approval, the way he noted everything about himself, and turned the page.

*Received, one soul entire,* said the entry, in that angular hand. *Origin: village of [the name here was not letters but a cluster of the sigils themselves, and looking at it made Freya's teeth ache]. Consideration rendered: winter unending, per contract annexed. Payable beneath the mountain. Term: thirteen turns of the moon from receipt.*

Below it, in Corvane's unmistakable hand—tighter than usual, the ink laid down with a pressure that had nearly cut the page—was the countersignature. And below *that*, in the margin, small enough that only someone reading with a lamp at full flame and his face six inches from the paper would ever find it, someone had written:

*not purchased. taken.*

* * *

Freya sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above him a beam settled with a groan, the way old buildings do, complaining of their loads.

*One soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain.*

He read it again. Then again, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape. The consideration line was the part that snagged him, the part his trained mind kept circling like a tongue finding a broken tooth: *winter unending.* Every transaction in the Ember's books balanced. That was the second law, Corvane's own catechism: nothing given for nothing, nothing taken without payment. The guild did not deal in theft, Corvane liked to say when the moral questions got close; the guild dealt in *exchange*. Fair value, fairly rendered, and the ledger was the witness.

So a village had been paid a winter that never ended—in exchange for a child's soul. And now a child had walked out of the snow speaking the language of that payment, barefoot, mute except for words that made candleflames flinch, and the moon—

Freya did the arithmetic without wanting to. Thirteen turns of the moon. He was a keeper of accounts; dates were his native tongue. If the entry was forty-one years old, the term had expired decades ago. Which meant either the debt was already collected—which raised the question of what had come up out of the mountain wearing a boy's body and walking to their door—or the debt had never been collected at all, had sat unpaid in some gilded office beneath the stone, accruing interest the way such debts did, waiting.

Or. His pen-hand wanted to write it and his mind refused. Or the entry was wrong. Misdated. A copyist's error, a folio bound out of order. Ledgers lied all the time—that was half of what they were *for*—and the lies were usually mundane. A smuggled shipment recorded as grain. A bribe entered as a fee. He had spent his career making records tell comfortable stories. It was possible—probable, he told himself, probable—that this was the same species of lie, merely stranger in its costume.

It was possible the boy was nobody. A foundling, a stray, a mute child with a fever dream for a vocabulary, and Freya was sitting in a freezing cellar at the thirteenth hour inventing hauntings out of a dead clerk's eccentric penmanship.

He copied the entry anyway. All of it. The sigils he reproduced stroke for stroke, though his hand shook doing it, though each mark seemed to look back at him as it dried. The marginal note he copied too—*not purchased. taken.*—in his own smallest hand, and then he hid the copy inside the binding of the current year's volume, between the pages recording this week's routine receipts, where no one would ever think to look because no one ever looked at the boring parts.

Then he sat in the dark he hadn't noticed gathering, and listened to the building breathe, and admitted the thing he had been refusing to admit since the boy arrived.

He was frightened. Not for himself—for the boy. For a child he did not know, whose name he didn't have, whom the guild should by every law it possessed have turned out into the snow. Freya Reyes, who had cataloged the grief of mothers without blinking, who had filed away the stolen childhoods of strangers between dinner and supper, was lying awake over a ledger entry feeling something hot and useless rise in his chest, and he could not file it, and he could not price it, and he could not make it balance.

*What is not written does not exist.* He had believed that once. Now, holding his secret copy in the dark, he understood the corollary he'd never let himself see: what is written cannot be unwritten. The entry existed. The debt existed. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in whatever court had drafted that angular, patient hand, an account remained open.

Accounts, in Freya's experience, were always eventually settled.

* * *

He found Corvane at dawn, or rather Corvane found him, which was worse.

The guildmaster kept early hours the way cats keep watch—apparently asleep, actually attentive. Freya came up from the ledger room with the original volume under his arm meaning to return it to the archive shelf unseen, and there was Corvane in the corridor outside the wine cellar, dressed, shaved, holding a cup of black tea he never drank, watching Freya emerge from the false wall with the expression of a man checking a clock against the sun.

"You've been in the deep shelves," Corvane said. Not a question. Corvane did not ask questions he knew the answers to; asking implied doubt, and doubt was the one currency the guildmaster refused to hold.

"Reconciliation," Freya said. "The forty-one volume was misfiled. I'm returning it."

"Mm." Corvane stepped closer. He was not a tall man, but he occupied space the way debt occupies a life—quietly, totally. His eyes went to the ledger under Freya's arm, and lingered, and Freya felt the copy sewn into the newer book beneath his coat like a coal against his ribs. "And did the reconciliation satisfy?"

"There's an entry," Freya said. He had rehearsed nothing and was grateful, suddenly, that his voice had been trained by years of reading figures aloud into a flatness nothing penetrated. "Old. A soul-sale. The origin notation isn't in any cipher I know—it's in those hooked characters. The ones the boy makes."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes. Freya had watched that face for eleven years and could count on one hand the times it had moved at all, and none of those times had ended well for whoever caused it.

"Clerical error," Corvane said.

"The whole entry?"

"The whole *hand*. There was a period—we don't speak of the period—when the guild employed a scrivener of foreign training. Affected mannerisms. Wrote his dates in a calendar nobody used and was dismissed for it." Corvane sipped tea he didn't drink. "The entry means nothing. The boy means nothing. He'll be gone within the week; I've already sent word to the foundling house at Marlowe's Cross."

"He doesn't talk—"

"They never do, at first." Corvane turned away, and over his shoulder, lightly, in the tone of a man discussing the weather: "Burn your notes, Freya. Whatever you copied. Old ink has a way of sticking to fingers, and I would hate to see yours stained."

He walked off down the corridor, unhurried, and Freya stood in the cold air from the cellar stairs and understood, with the slow sick certainty of a man watching figures refuse to sum, that Corvane had known exactly which volume Freya had touched. Had probably known within the hour. Perhaps within the minute.

*Clerical error.* The guildmaster had countersigned that entry himself. Freya had seen the hand—had seen how hard the pen had pressed. Men do not grip their pens like that over errors.

Above him, faint through the floors, he heard the kitchen sounds beginning: Maren's kettle, the scrape of the stove iron. And threaded under it, thin as wire, a child's voice saying something in a language older than any kingdom—a language, Freya now knew, that had once been written in the Ember's own books by a hand the guildmaster swore had never existed.

Freya climbed toward the warmth and the smell of porridge, carrying his stolen copy against his ribs, telling himself he would burn the notes as ordered.

He already knew he wouldn't. That was the trouble with keeping accounts: once you opened a folio, you had to know how it ended.
