## 4. The Language of Before

The boy did not sleep so much as sink.

Freya noticed it on the third night, watching from the doorway of the small room above the kitchens that Maren had claimed for him—a cot, a quilt that smelled of cedar and old smoke, a candle burned down to a coin of wax. The child lay with his eyes open at first, and then his eyes closed, and then he was somewhere else entirely. His breathing changed. His hands, which had been curled at his chest like two sleeping birds, went slack and open, palms up, the way a man lies when he has surrendered something.

And he spoke.

Not the thin, wordless sounds he'd made in the snow outside the guild door. Not crying, not muttering. Words—long, rolling, syllables that rose and fell like water moving over stones in the dark. Freya had spent eleven years in the Ember learning to listen: to footsteps on stairs, to the particular silence a locked door makes when someone is about to pick it, to the lies people tell with their hands while their mouths say something else. He had never heard anything like this. The language had no sharp edges. It sounded older than the walls around them, older than the city, older perhaps than the habit of building walls at all.

Freya stood in the doorway and felt the hair rise along his arms, because the ledger room was three floors below, and even from here he could have sworn he heard paper turning.

* * *

In the morning he brought the boy down himself.

It was against every rule Freya held sacred. The ledger room was the heart of the Ember, and hearts were not for visitors, and certainly not for barefoot children who ate like sparrows and watched everything with eyes the color of wet slate. But Corvane was away until evening—gone upriver to audit a buyer whose payments had grown irregular—and Maren had cornered Freya at breakfast with flour still on her knuckles and said, in the voice she used when she was done being kind, "That boy talked all night. In his sleep. And I'll tell you what else—I understood some of it. Or my bones did. And you're going to find out why, or I'm going to start asking louder questions than either of us wants answered in this house."

So Freya carried a lamp down the spiral stair, and the boy followed, silent as ever, and Maren brought up the rear with her arms folded like a woman attending a funeral she disapproved of.

The ledger room at midmorning was its own gray country. Light came through one high window, filtered through glass so old it had gone the color of weak tea, and fell in a single slanted column across the reading table. Shelves climbed the walls into shadow, row upon row of ledgers bound in leather gone stiff and dark, spines stamped with years going back past living memory. The air tasted of dust and iron-gall ink and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit rotting very slowly in a cellar—the smell of recorded things aging.

Freya set the lamp on the table. "Sit," he said to the boy, and then, feeling foolish, patted the chair.

The boy sat. He looked at the shelves the way other children looked at fires.

Freya had prepared. He had spent an hour before dawn copying sigils onto a sheet of scrap paper—the strange marks the boy had scratched into the frost on the window the morning after his arrival, which Maren had preserved by pressing her palm over them and swearing at anyone who came near. He laid the paper out now between them, next to the oldest ledger he dared pull from the shelf without Corvane knowing: a volume from a hundred and forty years ago, its cover scarred, its pages soft as cloth.

"Look," Freya said. He pointed at the frost-sigils. Then at the ledger's frontispiece, where the founding scribes had inked the guild's first mark—a flame inside a circle, and beneath it a line of characters no current member could read. "Do you know these?"

The boy leaned forward. His breath fogged the cold air of the room. He studied the old characters for a long moment, and then he did something that stopped Freya's heart where it stood.

He laughed.

It was a small sound, startled out of him, half delight and half grief, and it was the first time Freya had heard him make any sound resembling joy. The boy touched the page with one fingertip, careful as a man touching a sleeping animal, and said a short phrase in his rolling language, and then looked up at Freya with an expression of such naked, patient expectation that Freya understood, with a lurch, what was being asked.

*Say it back.*

"I can't," Freya said aloud, uselessly. Then, because the boy deserved better than uselessness, he tried. He shaped the sounds as best he could—clumsy, flattened, a bird trying to swim. The boy's face crumpled with something that was almost pain, and he reached across and gently pressed two fingers to Freya's mouth, stopping him.

Then he began again, slower. Breaking each phrase apart. Pointing—at the ledger, at the flame-and-circle mark, at himself, at the floor, at the high dark window where daylight failed to reach. Teaching.

Maren made a low sound in her throat. "Sweet saints," she whispered. "He's not asking us to learn his language." Her hand had come up to rest against her collarbone. "He's telling us what happened to him."

* * *

It took the whole day, and it took Freya further down than he had meant to go.

The method, once he surrendered to it, was simple and terrible: the boy would speak, and gesture, and draw. He drew on the backs of Freya's scrap papers with a stub of charcoal Maren found for him, and he drew the way some people breathe—constantly, effortlessly, images flowing out of him faster than Freya could lay fresh sheets down. A village. Low houses with turf roofs, smoke rising straight in windless cold. A river frozen so deeply that people had cut rooms into the ice and hung lamps inside them. Fields white to the horizon, and beyond the fields, a mountain shaped like a closed fist.

And always, everywhere in the drawings, the moon. Drawn large, drawn repeated, drawn in sequences—circle, crescent, circle, crescent—marching across the tops of the pages like a row of teeth.

"Moons," Freya said slowly, tracing the sequence. "You're counting moons."

The boy nodded, quick and grave.

Thirteen circles. Thirteen crescents. Freya counted twice, because his hands had begun to feel unsteady. Twenty-six marks. More than two years. He looked at the boy—small, maybe seven years old, wrists like kindling—and tried to do the arithmetic of what a child could have walked through in twenty-six months, and found his mind sliding off the sum the way a boot slides off ice.

The boy watched him count. Then he turned over a fresh sheet and drew something new, and this drawing he worked on for a long time, tongue caught between his teeth, charcoal grinding.

A long table. That was the first thing Freya recognized, because the Ember had tables like it—long, dark, formal. Around the table sat figures the boy had drawn as adults are drawn by children who do not love them: too tall, faces blank ovals, hands exaggerated into long deliberate fingers. Before each figure sat objects the boy rendered with obsessive care: cups, coins, a scale, and—Freya's stomach tightened—books. Small dark books, drawn with the same double-line binding as the volumes on the shelves around them.

Ledgers. The deep figures had ledgers.

At the foot of the table, drawn much smaller, huddled together, were children. Six of them. The boy drew himself last, and Freya knew it was himself because the figure held a stick of charcoal in its drawn hand, a picture nested inside the picture, and because the other five figures were drawn facing away from the viewer while this one faced forward, looking out of the page with two dots of eyes that the boy had pressed so hard they shone.

The boy tapped the long table. Then he tapped the six small figures. Then he drew a line from the children to the tall blank-faced adults, and another line from the adults' side of the table to the mountain in the corner of the page, and on the mountain he drew a door, and through the door—

He stopped. His charcoal hovered. For the first time all day, his hand shook.

"He can't draw it," Maren said quietly. She had not left the room since noon. She stood with her back against the shelf wall, arms wrapped around herself, and her face in the lamplight was gray. "Whatever's behind that door, he can't put it on paper."

The boy set the charcoal down. He looked at Freya, and then he did the thing that Freya would remember, later, in the dark, in the years after, whenever he thought about what had been done to this child and who had signed for it.

He smiled. Reassuringly. As if Freya were the one who needed comfort.

Then he spoke—a long passage, unhurried, in that water-over-stones language—and as he spoke he gestured, and Freya, who had spent the whole day learning the shape of the boy's meanings, caught fragments the way a man in fog catches glimpses of road:

Cold. The word for cold came often, and it was not a complaint; it was spoken the way one speaks of weather, of a fact long accepted. *Winter.* Another recurring root, longer, heavier. And threaded through both, again and again, a phrase the boy returned to like a refrain, touching his own chest each time, then spreading his hands outward toward the drawn village, toward the drawn children, toward the drawn door in the drawn mountain.

Freya bent over the founding ledger, the one with the old characters, and compared. The frontispiece characters shared roots—he could see it now, now that his ear and eye had been tuned by a seven-year-old teacher. The guild's oldest script and the boy's living speech were kin. Which meant the language predated the guild. Which meant the guild had learned it from somewhere. From someone. From *beneath*.

"The winter never ended," Freya said slowly, testing the sentence in the air. "Your village. The cold came and it didn't stop. Crops. Animals. People." He looked up. "And someone came to the table. Someone with books. And there was a trade."

The boy's face did something complicated and devastating: relief and grief arriving together, the relief of being understood cracking open the grief that understanding cost. He nodded. He picked the charcoal back up and drew one more thing, quickly, as if afraid the courage would leave him.

A hearth. A fire burning in it. And around the fire, the same six children—but warm now, drawn with curved lines instead of hunched ones, and above the hearth he wrote a string of his own characters, pressing hard, underlining them twice.

Then he pointed at the fire. Then at his own chest. Then at the mountain door.

Fire for the hearth. Something of himself for the door.

"One soul," Freya whispered. The phrase arrived in his mouth already wearing the ledger's exact wording, and he hated it, hated how naturally it fit. "One soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain." He had read those words only days ago, in a hundred-and-forty-year-old entry he'd dismissed as a curiosity. He thought of the entry now with a new and sickening clarity. It hadn't been a curiosity. It had been a *pattern*. One soul per village. One child per winter that wouldn't end. And the guild's founding script was the court's script, which meant—

Which meant the Ember had been the pen in the mountain's hand for longer than anyone alive could remember.

Maren had turned away. Her shoulders moved once, twice. When she turned back her eyes were red and her jaw was set like a keel. "Twenty-six moons," she said. "He's been walking for twenty-six months. Seven years old." She looked at Freya, and there was no kindness in her expression at all, only a ferocity that Freya recognized because he kept a version of it locked in his own chest and called it professionalism. "Whoever sold him is dead or damned, and whoever bought him is neither. So which one of us is going to tell Corvane?"

* * *

They told him nothing, that night. It was Maren's counsel, and Freya agreed with a swiftness that surprised them both: Corvane should hear it from the ledgers, formally, in daylight, with witnesses—not from a cook's rumor and a thief's suspicion. "Let the man be surprised in front of paper," Maren said, "or he'll eat the surprise and call it nothing."

But Freya did not go to bed.

He sat in the ledger room alone after midnight with the lamp trimmed low, and he pulled the hundred-and-forty-year-old volume toward him again, and he read the entry the way he now knew how to read it—one soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain—and then he did the thing he had been avoiding since dawn.

He started counting entries.

There were more. Not many—never many; the intervals were decades apart, spaced like a man spacing out crimes he knows are crimes. A village in the high passes, sixty years back. A fishing town on the grey coast, ninety-one years ago. Each entry brief, clean, written in the beautiful clerk's hand the guild trained into its best scribes. Each one noting payment received, terms honored, account closed. Account closed. As if a child were a debt that could be settled and forgotten.

Freya sat back in the dark and listened to the building settle around him—the tick of cooling stone, the far-off sigh of the river under the street, upstairs the small sounds of a boy sleeping, or sinking, or speaking his water-language to whatever waited behind the door in the mountain of his dreams.

Thirty years, the intervals said. Roughly thirty years, give or take a hard winter. Someone had been keeping this rhythm a long time. Someone had been feeding the deep court on a schedule, quietly, entry by entry, while the guild above went about its respectable trade in stolen years and bottled grief.

Freya looked at his own hands on the ancient page and thought, with a coldness that felt like honesty: *I copy things for a living. I have never once asked what the master ledger keeps that we aren't shown.*

Above him, faint through three floors of stone, he heard the boy begin to speak in his sleep.

And below him—so faint he might have imagined it, so faint he would spend the rest of his life deciding whether he had—the ledger room answered. A whisper of paper. A turning of pages, unhurried, patient, in a room where no one stood.

The mountain, Freya thought, keeps its books too.

He snuffed the lamp and climbed the stairs toward the sound of a child's voice, and did not look back at the shelves, because he was afraid of what he might see turning there.
