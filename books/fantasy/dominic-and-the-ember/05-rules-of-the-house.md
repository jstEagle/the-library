## 5. Rules of the House

The child had been with them nine days before anyone thought to name him.

Freya noticed it on the ninth evening, in the long kitchen below the counting room, where Maren set a bowl of barley stew in front of the boy and said, without ceremony, "Ash. That's what I'm calling you. You came out of the snow like something pulled from a fire, and you've been leaving gray footprints on my clean floors ever since."

The boy looked up at her. He did not smile—Freya had not yet seen him smile—but something moved behind his eyes, a flicker like a lamp catching after a draft. He touched his own chest, then touched the bowl, as if to ask whether the name belonged to him or to the stew.

"To you, goose. The stew has its own name, and it's supper." Maren ruffled his hair with a flour-dusted hand and went back to her ovens, and that was that. Ash he was.

Freya stood in the doorway of the kitchen, ledger under his arm, and felt the strangest sensation: warmth, arriving somewhere in him that had been cold so long he'd forgotten the geography of it. He catalogued the feeling the way he catalogued everything—*unbidden, unearned, potentially costly*—and found, for once, that he didn't want to put it away in its drawer.

He stayed for supper. It was the first guild meal he'd eaten below stairs in four years.

* * *

The Ember had rules, and Freya had written most of them down himself, in his own neat hand, in the front of the house book. They were rules of invisibility, mostly. *Take nothing that will be missed before it can be missed. Speak of the guild to no one who does not already speak of it. Let every door behind you close as if you were never there.* They were good rules. They had kept him alive through thirty years of thefts that would have hanged a less careful man, and they had one great virtue: they required nothing of the heart.

Ash broke all of them within a fortnight, simply by existing.

He could not be made invisible. He left marks everywhere—the gray footprints Maren complained of, which never quite swept away; small drawings in the condensation on the pantry window; a trail of the strange sigils he scratched into frost and flour dust and the margins of anything printed, always the same looping characters, always in sequences of three. He spoke only in that language no one knew, a language of soft consonants and long vowels that sounded, Perrin the lockman said, "like water deciding where to go." And he watched everything. He watched the way Freya catalogued a stolen memory at the sorting table, glass vial turning in the lamplight, and he watched the way Quill the forger licked his thumb before turning a page, and he watched Corvane—always Corvane—with an attention that made the hair rise on Freya's neck, though he could not have said why.

So the house adapted, because houses do, and because Maren refused to let a child starve on principle.

The rules of the house, as they evolved around Ash, were these:

First: Ash ate at the long table, and no one commented on how much he ate, though everyone noticed. He ate like someone who had learned hunger as a season rather than a meal—methodically, completely, without waste—and Maren took this as a personal commission. She fed him the way other people prayed.

Second: Ash's boots were everyone's problem. They had come with him across the snow—cracked leather, split soles, a size too small—and on the fourth day Freya found them by the hearth, stuffed with wool, being worked over with tallow and awl by old Sef, the guild's retired cracksman, whose hands shook too badly for locks anymore but not for leather. "Boy walks like the ground owes him money," Sef grunted, not looking up. "Can't have that. Ground and us ought to be on good terms." When the boots were mended, Ash carried them to Freya's door and set them outside it, aligned perfectly parallel, toes pointing toward the room. Freya understood it was thanks. He also understood, looking at those small careful boots, that no one had ever thanked him for anything that wasn't stolen.

Third: no one asked Ash about the mountain. Not yet. Not until they knew what questions were safe.

It was the third rule that Freya broke, privately, every night, in the ledger room.

* * *

He told himself it was diligence. The Ember's ledgers recorded everything that passed through the house—every stolen year, every bottled grief, every memory pried from a sleeping mind—and a child who arrived speaking a dead language, bearing sigils that matched entries three centuries old, was inventory whether anyone liked it or not. Inventory required documentation. Documentation required reading.

So Freya read.

He sat in the ledger room with its smell of dust and tallow candles and the faint iron tang of ink that had been mixed with stranger things than gall, and he traced the sigils Ash scratched into everything against the faded entries in the oldest volumes. *One soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain.* The entry appeared eleven times across two hundred years, each time in a different hand, each time recording a different village, a different winter, a different desperate bargain. Eleven children, given away whole, their names rendered in those same looping characters because—as Freya slowly realized with a coldness that settled in his stomach like swallowed river-stone—the old script had no way of writing a soul's name in any living tongue. The names *were* the sigils. Ash had been signing himself, all this time, on windows and in flour dust. Signing himself the only way he still could.

Freya closed the ledger and sat for a long while in the dark, listening to the house settle around him, and felt the first true fear since the night the child had knocked.

Because there was a twelfth entry. He had found it on the second night of searching, tucked into a volume from sixty years past, and it was different from the others. The others recorded sales *received*—souls delivered to the guild's keeping, held in trust, awaiting collection. The twelfth recorded a sale *made*. A soul sold onward, by the Ember itself, to a buyer whose mark was a coiled serpent biting its own tail. And the payment column, where every other entry listed years or griefs or memories, listed only a single word in Corvane's unmistakable hand:

*Held.*

Not paid. Not collected. *Held.* As if the transaction had been begun and then—frozen. Suspended. Left open like a door that someone intended to walk back through.

Freya copied the entry twice, hid both copies in places only he knew, and told no one. Some part of him said this was prudence. Another part, quieter, older, knew it was the first thing he had ever stolen from the guild, and that he had done it because of a pair of mended boots sitting outside his door with their toes pointed faithfully toward his sleeping body.

* * *

Winter deepened. The house grew used to Ash, and Ash—to everyone's quiet astonishment, including his own—grew used to the house.

He began following Freya on his rounds. Not asking—asking was beyond him still—but appearing at Freya's elbow at the start of each evening, small and solemn, ready to be included or dismissed. Freya dismissed him the first three nights. On the fourth, running low on patience and high on some feeling he declined to examine, he handed the boy the tally-slate and chalk and showed him how to mark quantities.

Ash took to it with terrifying aptitude. His columns were perfect. His arithmetic was instant, silent, correct. By the end of the week he was catching Freya's errors—a transposed figure here, a miscounted vial there—and correcting them with a single tap of chalk against slate and a look of grave reproof that Freya found, to his horror, he couldn't help but respect.

"You're wasted on us," Freya told him one night, in the warm clutter of the sorting room. "You should be robbing banks."

Ash tilted his head. Then he picked up a vial of stolen memory—one of the routine acquisitions, a cooper's grief for his drowned daughter, decanted last Tuesday—and turned it in the lamplight, watching the slow silver drift inside the glass. He looked at it the way Freya had seen appraisers look at forged paintings. Then he set it down, very gently, and pushed it half an inch toward Freya, and made a sound—not a word, but a note of pure disapproval, low in his throat.

Freya frowned. "What? It's catalogued. It's legitimate."

Ash tapped the vial again. Pushed it further. Then he pressed his own hand flat against his chest, over his heart, and looked up at Freya with an expression of such plain, unguarded sorrow that Freya had to turn away and busy himself with the shelving for a full minute.

The boy thought the grief should go home. Back to the cooper who had sold it because he couldn't afford to feel it and keep working. Freya knew this because the logic was simple, and because Ash was right, and because Freya had spent thirty years telling himself that what the Ember did was neutral—that moving grief from one place to another was honest work, the way millers move grain—and here was a child of perhaps eight winters dismantling that argument with a push of one small hand across a table.

"I can't," Freya said finally, aloud, to the shelves. "There are rules."

Ash considered this. Then he nodded slowly, as if rules were a thing he understood better than anyone in the building, and filed the knowledge away behind his eyes, and said nothing—which was, Freya was learning, how Ash said the most.

* * *

Corvane watched.

That was the phrase Maren used, in the kitchen, in a voice pitched under the clatter of pans. "He watches that boy like a man watches a purse he hasn't decided to cut yet."

Freya had seen it too. The guildmaster had a way of appearing at the edges of rooms—doorways, stair-heads, the mouth of the corridor to the vaults—with his hands folded behind his back and his head tilted slightly, observing Ash with an attention that never resolved into affection or irritation or anything else a child might reasonably attract. It was the attention Freya himself gave to inventory. To assets. To things with prices.

Once, Freya came upon them together in the upstairs gallery, Corvane crouched—actually crouched, which Freya had never seen him do—to bring himself level with the boy. The guildmaster was speaking softly, in that reasonable baritone he used for negotiations, and Ash stood very still, the way animals stand still when they cannot tell yet whether the thing approaching is friend or weather.

"—and you understand," Corvane was saying, "that everything in this house belongs to someone. Nothing is free. Nothing is *given*. Even kindness is entered in a book somewhere, and books must balance." He smiled. It was a kind smile. It was the smile of a man explaining interest rates to a borrower. "You'll remember that, won't you?"

Ash looked at Corvane for a long moment. Then he raised one hand and drew, slowly, deliberately, in the dust on the banister rail, three looping sigils—the same sequence Freya had seen in the ledgers, the sequence that meant, if the old entries were honest, *one soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain*.

Corvane went very still. For the space of three heartbeats the gallery was silent enough that Freya could hear the fire ticking in the grate below. Then the guildmaster straightened, brushed the dust from his knee, and said pleasantly, "Yes. Just so." And he walked away down the corridor with measured steps, and did not look back, and Freya—who had been standing in the shadow of the linen closet with a basket of napkins he had no earthly reason to be holding—felt the cold arrive in his bones and stay there.

That night he checked the twelfth entry again. *Held.* Sixty years the transaction had sat open in the guildmaster's hand. Sixty years, and now a child arrives wearing the sigils of the deep court's debtors, and Corvane begins to watch him like a purse.

Freya lay awake in his narrow room above the counting floor and listened to the house breathe—Maren banking her ovens, Sef's uneven tread on the stairs, the tick of the great case clock marking hours that suddenly felt numbered—and he understood, with the clarity that comes only at the worst times, that the warmth growing in the walls of the Ember was real, and fragile, and sitting directly in the path of something old and patient and hungry beneath the mountain.

And beneath that understanding, colder still, another thought surfaced, the way drowned things surface:

*The moon was turning. Someone in this house was counting.*

* * *

On the morning of the seventeenth day, Freya woke to find the boots outside his door again—newly mended, newly oiled, though he hadn't touched them since Sef finished them weeks ago. This time there was something beside them: a scrap of slate, chalked in a careful, wobbling imitation of Freya's own handwriting, the letters laboriously copied from the labels on the sorting shelves.

It said: *THANK YOU FOR NOT SELLING ME.*

Freya stood in the doorway for a long time, the slate in his hand, the corridor cold around him, and did not trust himself to move. Downstairs, he could hear Maren singing to her ovens, off-key and defiant. Somewhere above, snow slid from the eaves. The house went on being a house, warm and ordinary and complicit, and Freya Reyes—thief, keeper of impossible ledgers, a man who had catalogued ten thousand stolen things and felt nothing—held a piece of slate with five words on it and felt everything.

He turned the slate over. On the reverse, in the same careful hand, was a single looping sigil he hadn't seen before. He checked it that night against the oldest volume, the one with pages soft as cloth, and found it in a marginal gloss in a dead archivist's hand, and the translation made him sit down slowly on the stool.

The sigil meant: *counting.*

And beneath it, in the margin, in fresher ink—ink Freya recognized, ink mixed with gall and something darker, ink he had watched dry on a thousand entries across thirty years—someone had added a single line:

*Nine turns remain.*
