## 6. What the Moon Owes

Maren had been counting things her whole life. Breathes between a laboring mother's cries. Spoons of honey in a fever draught. The days since a child last laughed, the hours since a wound went bad, the small increments by which hope thinned or thickened like broth on a slow fire. Counting was what midwives did when there was nothing else to do, and Maren had learned, over forty years and more births than she could number—though of course she could number them, that was the whole trouble with counting—that most terrible things arrived not all at once but in installments.

So when the old woman came up out of the snow-mist at the guild's back gate on the third evening after Ash's arrival, Maren counted the ways this could go wrong before she'd even lifted the latch.

The woman was small and wrapped in gray wool gone silver at the seams, and she stood in the falling light as though she had been standing there for some time already and intended to stand there for some time yet. Her face was seamed like old leather left out in weather, and her eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of ice over deep water. In her hands she carried nothing at all, which in Maren's experience was the most alarming thing a visitor could carry.

"You're the cook," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm the cook, the mender, the keeper of stores, and twice a year the surgeon, if it comes to that." Maren kept her voice flat and her hand near the iron poker just inside the door. "And you are?"

"Sela. I caught babies in Hollowmere, before Hollowmere stopped needing anyone to catch them." Something moved behind her face then, quick and buried, like a fish under winter ice. "I'm told you've got a boy inside who speaks the old tongue."

Maren felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She hadn't told anyone outside these walls about Ash. Hadn't had time to, hadn't wanted to. "Who told you that?"

"The wind told me. Then a tinker. Then the wind again, more insistently." Sela's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "News travels, cook. Especially news that smells like the mountain."

* * *

She let her in, because what else was there to do. Some visitors you turned away and some you fed, and the ones who arrived carrying nothing and asking for nothing were the ones you watched closest while you fed them.

Ash was in the kitchen, as he always was now, sitting on his stool by the hearth where the warmth reached him first. He had claimed that stool the way he claimed everything—quietly, completely, without negotiation—and Maren had long since stopped pretending she minded. He was helping her shell peas into a bowl, his small brown fingers working with a patience that shamed the grown men of the household, and when Sela came through the door he looked up.

Maren saw the moment the boy understood something. It passed across his face like cloud-shadow over a field—not fear exactly, not recognition either, but the particular stillness of a creature that has heard a sound it knows means weather is coming.

Sela knelt. Slowly, the way you kneel for children and frightened animals and queens, and she said something in a language that made the hairs on Maren's arms rise.

It wasn't that the words were ugly. They weren't. They fell out of her mouth round and low and resonant, like stones dropped into still water, like the sounds a house makes settling at night. But they were *old*. Maren couldn't have said how she knew that; she only knew it the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air. The kitchen itself seemed to lean in to listen. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, in the ledger room, a shutter banged against its frame though there was no wind.

Ash answered.

His voice was thin and rusty from disuse—he had spoken almost nothing since arriving, and what little he'd offered came out halting, half-remembered—but the shape of the language fit him. It fit him the way his own name would have, if he'd had one. He spoke perhaps ten words, and then he stopped, and his chin began to tremble, and Maren crossed the kitchen and put her hand on the back of his neck the way she'd done for forty years of frightened children, and he leaned into her like a door swinging shut.

"What did you say to him?" she asked.

"I asked him how many moons." Sela rose, joints cracking like green kindling. "He says—" and here the old woman paused, and Maren watched her decide how much truth a stranger could be given, and then watched her decide that all of it could, because the alternative was worse. "He says he doesn't know anymore. He says the counting stopped when they took his voice. But he knows what was promised. He remembers the promise."

"Whose promise?"

"Hollowmere's." Sela sat down heavily at the kitchen table, uninvited, and Maren found she didn't mind. "Mine too, God rot me. Sit, cook. Pour something strong. This is a story that goes better with drink, and I have carried it thirty years, and my back is done carrying it."

* * *

What Sela told her, over two cups of plum brandy that Maren drank and the old woman barely touched, was this:

Hollowmere had been a valley village, high and poor, where the winters came early and stayed late and the soil gave less each year than it had the year before. And one winter—the worst one, the one the survivors still didn't speak of plainly—a man had come down from the mountain. Not a mountain man. A mountain *man*, if you took the meaning. Dressed beautifully. Polite beyond bearing. He'd come to the village hall and spread documents on the table like a merchant, and he'd explained, with great courtesy, that the court beneath the mountain was prepared to offer terms.

"They were starving," Sela said. "You can't understand starving unless you've watched it. Not hungry—starving. The difference is that hunger ends and starving doesn't. The children went quiet first. That's how you know it's bad, when the children go quiet. They stop crying because crying costs more than it pays." Her hands lay flat on the table, palms down, as though holding something in. "This gentleman offers us an endless growing season. Warmth without end. Our valley would bloom in January. All we had to do was sign."

"Sign what?"

"A contract. One page. Beautiful paper. Ink that shimmered." Sela laughed, and it was the worst sound Maren had heard in years. "We couldn't read half the clauses. Didn't matter. Starving people don't read contracts, cook. Starving people sign whatever's put in front of them and thank the hand that holds the pen."

"And the price?"

"One soul per generation. Just one. Chosen by lot, the document said, though we all knew whose lot it would be—the poorest family, the family nobody would miss, the family too broken to make noise about it." Sela's eyes went to Ash, hunched over his pea bowl, shelling with fierce concentration, pretending—or maybe not pretending—to hear nothing. "The document called it a *lease*. That was the word that fooled us. A lease sounds temporary. A lease sounds like something you get back."

"But it wasn't."

"It was a sale. We learned that later, when we tried to buy him back. The court's own clerks were very clear, very polite. *The asset has changed hands permanently.* Asset. That's what they called my grandson." For the first time, Sela's composure cracked, and Maren saw the thing underneath: not grief exactly, older than grief, the worn-smooth place where grief had been rubbed so long it had become something else, something like bedrock. "We scraped together everything. Sold the good cow, sold the wedding silver, sold my mother's ring. And the court took our money and gave us back—" she gestured vaguely at the air, at nothing, at the shape of a boy who no longer existed "—a child who could see us and hear us and smile at us and was not *there*. Whatever they'd taken, they'd taken the part that was him. He sat in his chair by our fire for eleven years and never once asked for supper."

Maren's hands had gone cold around her cup. "And this boy? Ash?"

"Ash is the second payment. Or the third. I've lost count of which villages, which years." Sela shook her head slowly. "That's the trick of it, cook. The contract renews. Every thirteen years, the debt comes due again, and the court collects. And every thirteen years, some desperate valley full of starving people signs a beautiful page. The mountain never runs out of hunger, and the low country never runs out of winters."

* * *

"How do you know about Ash, then?" Maren asked. "How do you know he's one of theirs?"

"Because I've spent thirty years learning to know." Sela pulled her sleeve back. On her forearm, inked in faded blue, was a row of small marks—tallies, Maren realized. Thirteen groups of marks, each group containing... she counted, and her stomach turned. Each group contained fewer marks than the last. "I mark the moons. Every turn of the moon since the night they took Tam, I've cut one notch. When a group fills, I start fresh. It's how I keep the count honest. Because the count matters, cook. The count is the only weapon we were ever given."

"The count of what?"

"The count of when they collect." Sela leaned forward, and the firelight caught the pale blue of her eyes and made them glitter like chips off something very old and very cold. "A leased soul isn't taken all at once. That's the mercy in it, if you want to call it mercy. The court takes the voice first—that's why your boy is mute, or was. Then the dreams. Then the name. Bit by bit, moon by moon, they harvest him remotely, the way you'd strip a field rather than burn it. And on the thirteenth turn of the thirteenth moon—" she tapped the barest sliver of skin remaining on her arm, where only three notches remained uncut "—they come for what's left. Bodily. The vessel walks to the mountain on its own feet, and the doors open, and that's the end of the accounting."

Maren looked at Ash. He had stopped shelling peas. He was watching Sela's arm with an expression Maren had no name for—not understanding, perhaps, but something adjacent to it, the way a dog watches a door it knows someone once left through.

"How long?" she heard herself ask. Her voice sounded far away. "How long does he have?"

Sela turned her forearm toward the firelight and counted the notches one more time, though Maren could see she knew the answer the way she knew her own heartbeat.

"Thirteen turns," she said. "Thirteen months, give or take the odd blue moon. The lease on this one was signed—" she closed her eyes, calculating, and Maren watched her arrive somewhere she didn't want to be "—the autumn before last. Which puts collection at the turn of the thirteenth moon. Next winter. After that, the mountain sends its gentleman down the hill, polite as you please, and your boy walks up it, and no power under heaven short of the court's own clerks can call him back."

* * *

They sat in silence for a while after that. The fire ticked. Ash resumed shelling peas, one by one, each pod opening with a soft sound like a small secret being told.

Maren found herself doing what she always did, which was counting. Thirteen turns. She thought of the boy's boots, which she had resoled twice already because he walked everywhere at a run, as though the ground might be taken from under him at any moment. She thought of how he'd begun, in the last week, to leave a portion of his supper on the windowsill every night—an offering, she'd realized belatedly, the way you'd feed a spirit, or apologize to one. She thought of how Freya had started standing in the doorway of the kitchen in the evenings, saying nothing, watching the boy with an expression Maren had never seen on that careful face before, something raw and unguarded that the ledger-keeper probably didn't even know he was wearing.

Thirteen turns. Two hundred and eighty-some days. She had delivered babies with less warning than that. She had buried husbands with more.

"He stays," Maren said finally, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. "Whatever's coming, he stays here. With us."

Sela looked at her for a long moment. "You don't know what you're offering."

"I know exactly what I'm offering. I'm offering supper and a warm bed and somebody to mend his boots, for as long as there is an as-long-as." Maren set down her cup. "I've caught enough babies to know you don't refuse a life because keeping it will hurt. You just keep it."

Something moved across the old woman's face—envy, maybe, or its gentler cousin, longing. "The boy's village signed him away and called it survival. His own kin wouldn't look at him after the taking, said he was marked, said the mountain's eye was on their houses now." She stood, joints crackling, and gathered her gray wool around her. "You'd take in what his own blood threw out."

"His own blood threw out a *child*," Maren said. "That's not a hard sum."

At the door, Sela paused. She looked back at Ash—at the boy himself, not the debt of him—and said something soft in the old tongue. Ash answered without looking up from his peas, and whatever he said made the old woman close her eyes briefly, as if in pain or prayer.

"What did he say?" Maren asked.

"He said, *I remember the warmth here.*" Sela's voice was rough. "In the old tongue, that word—warmth, hearth-warmth—it's the same word they use for *home*. There isn't a separate word. There never was. The old people figured if a place was warm, it was home, and if it wasn't, no amount of naming would make it one." She stepped out into the darkening evening, then turned back one last time. "Keep your count, cook. Cut your notches. And when the moon starts getting fat and strange in another month or two—when it looks wrong, when it looks *hungry*—you send for me. I'll teach you the rest of what I know. There's more, and none of it is comfortable, and some of it involves the guildmaster of this fine establishment, whom I'd wager my last tooth you've never once seen eat a meal."

Then she was gone, down into the snow-mist, a small gray shape swallowed by the larger gray of the world, and Maren stood in the doorway until the cold drove her back inside.

* * *

That night, after the kitchen was banked and Ash was asleep in the alcove off the pantry that had somehow become his without anyone deciding it, Maren took a knife and a scrap of pine board and cut a single notch near the edge.

One.

She set the board on the shelf above the hearth, where she'd see it every morning, every evening, every time she reached for the salt. Twelve more notches and then—she refused to finish the thought. Twelve more notches, and she would cut them one at a time, and every day between them she would feed this boy and mend his boots and teach him card games and guard his sleep, and if the mountain wanted him, the mountain could learn what Maren Okafor's grandmother had taught her about debts:

That some things were not for sale. That some accounts could not be collected. That a woman with a knife and a pine board and nothing else to lose was a force the finest contracts in creation had never once managed to price.

She banked the fire. Above the ledger room, the shutter banged again in a wind that wasn't blowing, and downstairs in the vaults, Maren would have sworn she heard the stored memories whispering—restless, murmuring, like a congregation that had heard its name read aloud.

And in the pantry alcove, Ash slept with his fists curled tight, and dreamed, though Maren didn't know it yet, of gilded halls and patient voices and a clerk with beautiful manners asking, in a tone of infinite courtesy, whether the account holder was *comfortable*, and whether he was *ready*, and whether he had *enjoyed his stay*.

Twelve notches to go.
