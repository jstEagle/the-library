## 7. The Ledger Room at Night

The Ember had three laws, and Freya had written two of them himself.

*Take nothing that is missed. Leave nothing that is traced. Never steal from the house.*

He had carved the third into the ledger-room door with a stylus the year he turned nineteen, when his hand was still steady enough to make letters beautiful and his heart still foolish enough to believe that rules were a kind of armor. Corvane had watched him do it and said nothing—only smiled in that way of his, like a man watching a child build a wall of sand against a tide he already owned.

Twelve years later, Freya stood before that door at the hour between hours, with a lockpick in one hand and a candle he had no right to be carrying in the other, about to break the law he had made.

The guild slept above him. He knew its breathing by now—the creak of Maren's cot as she turned toward the kitchen warmth, the slow tide of Tam's snoring through the floorboards, the particular silence of Ash's little room, which was not silence at all but a held breath, the quiet of a boy who had learned somewhere beneath a mountain what it cost to be heard. Freya had listened outside that door more nights than he would admit. Not to guard the boy. To remind himself there was something in the world his hands could still protect without first pricing it.

The lock gave on the second try. It always had; he had picked it a hundred times for honest reasons—inventories, audits, the small clerical sins of any house that traded in stolen things. Tonight the click sounded different. Tonight it sounded like the first stone leaving a wall.

Inside, the ledger room waited the way it always did: patient, warm-smelling, faintly sour with old tallow and older ink. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling in ranks, each volume bound in hide whose animal Freya had long ago stopped asking after. The Ember kept its accounts the way churches kept their saints—reliquary-fashion, in order, in darkness, each book a spine of someone's life reduced to entries. *One memory, autumn rain on a specific street. One grief, bottled, medium weight. One name, unspoken, sold twice.* He had catalogued ten thousand such lines. He had felt nothing at ten thousand funerals, and told himself this was professionalism.

Tonight he lit the candle anyway, though the room's everburning lamps would have served, because a flame he carried himself felt less like permission and more like company.

He went to the deep shelves. The ones Corvane touched alone.

* * *

It had started, as these things do, with arithmetic.

Three nights past, unable to sleep, Freya had done what he always did instead of sleeping: he counted. And the count had refused to come out clean. The Ember's intake ledgers—the ones any journeyman could read—recorded acquisitions honestly enough. But the guild's wealth had never matched its thefts. Thirty years of small miracles, and where were the profits? No grand house. No hoarded coin beyond comfort. Corvane dressed well and lived plainly and paid every member generously, and the sums simply *went*, poured into some channel Freya had never been cleared to see.

Thieves notice missing money the way sailors notice weather. It is not curiosity. It is survival.

And then there was Ash—or rather, there was the sigil on Ash's collarbone, the mark Freya had copied onto scrap paper weeks ago and could not stop comparing against everything. He had found its twin once, buried in a margin note of a sixty-year-old acquisition ledger: *sold, payable beneath the mountain*. Corvane had called it clerical error. Clerical error. As if ink wandered off its page to draw itself in the skin of children.

Freya had spent the days since doing what he did best—waiting, watching, mapping the habits of a mark. Except the mark was his own guildmaster, and the habit he'd mapped was this: every new moon, Corvane came down alone, took a single black-bound volume from the deep shelf, and sat with it until the lamps burned low. He never wrote in it. He only *read*, the way a man reads letters from a creditor.

Tonight was a new moon. Corvane had gone down an hour ago and come up empty-handed an hour after, and Freya had watched the shape of the book under his arm, and had known, with the terrible clarity that comes to thieves at the worst possible moments, exactly which shelf it lived on.

*Never steal from the house.*

He set the candle down and pulled the first black volume free.

* * *

The binding opened with a sound like a sigh, or a gasp, and Freya chose not to think about which.

Black ledger. No title. The pages inside were not the Ember's cream stock but something finer, almost translucent, and the ink—when he tilted it to the flame—was not black at all but a brown so dark it might have been rust, or wine, or something else entirely that he declined to name even in the privacy of his own skull. The handwriting was Corvane's. That was the worst of it, at first. Not some foreign agent's script, not the court beneath the mountain writing upward into the world. Corvane's own neat, patient, schoolmaster's hand, the same hand that entered the guild's honest takings every week, that had corrected young Freya's figures with gentle red strokes twenty years ago and said, *numbers are promises, boy; keep them carefully.*

The first entry was dated thirty-one years back.

*Delivered: one soul, male, aged nine. Source: debtor family, Millrow. Received in trade: remission of debts, 340 crowns equivalent. Payable beneath the mountain. Court satisfied.*

Freya read it three times. His lips moved around the words without voicing them, the way they did over particularly elegant forgeries, and then he caught himself doing it and stopped, appalled, as if reading aloud might make it *true*-er than it already was.

He turned the page.

*Delivered: one soul, female, aged eleven. Source: orphaned, Southreach. Received: 500 crowns equivalent, court's discretion.*

Turned again.

*Delivered: two souls, twins, aged six. Source: purchased from mother, willing. Note: mother wept. Note struck through. Note re-struck.*

There were dozens. There were *dozens*. Page after page, year upon year, and the dates marched on through all the history Freya had lived inside these walls—through the famine winters he remembered, through the years he himself had been a boy selling pieces of his childhood for bread-quiet, through the decade when Freya had believed Corvane's plain living was a kind of virtue, austerity as philosophy. Every entry in that careful hand. Every soul priced like a bolt of cloth. Some entries had annotations in a second script, angular and wrong, letters that seemed to lean away from the eye rather than toward it—the language of the deep court, Freya realized, the language Ash spoke in his sleep—and beside those annotations, small marks of receipt. Paid. Paid. Paid. A merchant's tick, cheerful as a bird.

Thirty years. If the pattern held—if Corvane had begun before this volume, and the shelf held five more like it—then the guildmaster of the Ember had fed the mountain court longer than Freya had been alive.

Freya discovered he was sitting on the floor. He did not remember sitting down.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about betrayal: it does not feel like anger first. It feels like *misreading*. You go back over the account looking for your own error, certain the fault lies in your arithmetic, because the alternative is that the ground you have stood on for twenty years was never ground at all.

Freya had come to the Ember at eleven, hollowed out, having sold so much of himself that what remained barely cast a shadow. His father's debts had gone to the guild when his father's lungs failed, and Corvane had looked at the frightened boy across the counting table and said—not cruelly, that was the horror of it, never cruelly—"You have nothing left worth taking, so we will give you something instead. A trade. Learn to hold what others cannot bear to keep."

And Freya had learned. Had become *good*. Had built a self out of competence the way a man builds a shelter out of shipwreck, plank by plank, rule by rule: take nothing missed, leave nothing traced, steal nothing from the house. The guild had given him walls and warm meals and Maren's cooking and the deep satisfaction of a ledger balanced to the farthing. He had told himself for years that this was family. He had told himself it harder whenever the word *home* drifted too near, the way you press down a floorboard that has begun to creak.

Because he had known, hadn't he. Somewhere under the competence, under the calm, under the beautiful cold inventory of his heart—he had always known that a family you earn by being useful is not a family. It is employment with better lighting. And a man who cannot remember why he once wept has no business believing in homes.

But knowing a thing in the marrow and reading it in another man's handwriting are different wounds. One aches. The other bleeds.

Ash's face rose in him unbidden—the boy at the thirteenth-hour door, barefoot in snow, speaking a language older than kingdoms while the guild debated turning him out like a parcel delivered to the wrong address. *Sold, payable beneath the mountain.* And Corvane had dismissed the matching ledger entry with a smile and a shrug, *clerical error*, while all along—

All along he had been checking the delivery date.

Freya pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until he saw constellations that were not the mountain court's. When he lowered his hand, the candle had burned a thumb's width lower, and the decision had made itself in the dark while he wasn't watching, the way decisions do.

He would copy the pages. All of them.

Not to sell. Not yet, not ever perhaps. But a thief knows that evidence unrecorded is evidence that walks away, and a man who keeps ledgers for a living knows that ink is the only witness that doesn't flinch. If he was going to break the third law—if he was going to become, tonight, in the eyes of the only code he had ever kept, a thief among thieves—then he would do it properly. Completely. With margins noted and dates verified and the second, angular script transcribed letter by wrong-leaning letter.

Numbers are promises, Corvane used to say. Keep them carefully.

Freya found fresh paper, the good stock, the kind that takes fine lines without feathering. He found his sharpest nib. He set the black ledger open on his knees, laid the blank page beside it, and began the work he had been trained for since childhood: the faithful theft of what was written.

* * *

The copying took until the candles wept themselves to stubs and the sky beyond the high slit window went from black to bruise.

He worked the way he worked a vault—method, breath, method. Entry by entry. Name or no-name, age, source, price, the court's receipt-marks. Twice he had to stop because his hand shook and a shaking hand makes lies of even honest transcription. Once he stopped because he reached an entry dated twelve years back, the year of his own arrival, and saw in the source column a description that might have matched any of forty families ruined that winter, and he could not finish the line until he had convinced himself it wasn't his father's debt wearing a stranger's date. It probably wasn't. Probably. The *probably* sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

Near dawn he reached the final filled page, and there he found the most recent entry, fresh enough that the rust-dark ink still had a faint sheen:

*Reserved: one soul, male, aged approximately eight. Source: village of the old tongue (extinct). Delivered: pending. Payable beneath the mountain. Collection: thirteenth turn of the moon.*

Below it, in the angular script, a single annotation. And below *that*, in Corvane's hand, one word:

*Confirmed.*

Freya sat very still. The lamp-flames leaned as if listening.

*Pending*. Not yet delivered. Which meant that in the eyes of whatever law governed souls and their sale, Ash was not yet gone—which meant there was a window, narrow as a knife-slot, and at its end a moon completing its thirteenth turn like a coin dropping into a slot. Which meant Corvane had taken the boy in, fed him, let Maren mend his boots, let the household fold itself around his small silent gravity—all while holding him in reserve. An asset awaiting transfer. A delivery scheduled.

And Freya had watched that boy learn to sleep without flinching, had heard him laugh once—a cracked, astonished sound, as if laughter were a language he was relearning too—and had thought: *this is what I protect now.*

He closed the black ledger. Gently. The way you close a coffin.

Then he gathered his copies—forty pages, front and back, thirty years of souls rendered faithfully in his small precise hand—and wrapped them in oilcloth, and hid them not in his room, where rooms get searched, but in the one place no thief would ever look: sewn into the binding of the guild's own public ledger, the great open book that sat in the common hall where anyone might read it, hiding truth inside transparency the way he'd been taught to hide keys above doors.

He stood a long moment in the doorway of the ledger room, looking back at the shelves, at the third law carved into the wood in his own younger hand. *Never steal from the house.*

"I'm sorry," he told the carving quietly. "I'm stealing from the house."

It occurred to him, descending the stairs into the grey beginning of morning, that he did not know anymore which of them the apology was meant for—the boy who'd carved the law, or the house that had taught him every other kind of stealing first.

In the kitchen, Maren was already up, banking the fire, and she looked at him—at the ink on his fingers, at whatever was written on his face that he couldn't wipe away—and her ladle went still.

"Freya," she said. Not a question. She'd raised half the thieves in this city by proxy and she knew the look of a man who'd found a body in the water. "What did you find down there?"

And before Freya could decide how much truth the morning could carry, small bare feet sounded on the stair, and Ash appeared in the kitchen doorway—awake far too early, pale, wide-eyed—and pointed one thin finger at the oilcloth bundle under Freya's arm, and spoke four words in the old tongue.

Freya didn't know their meaning.

But he knew, by the way Maren's face drained white as flour, that she did.
