## 8. Buyers and Sellers

The mark was small enough to miss, which was, Freya supposed, the entire point of it.

He had been copying pages for three nights now, working by candle stub in the corner of his own room with the shutters barred and a wet cloth laid over the floorboards to catch the drip of wax. The stolen pages from the ledger room lay in a false bottom beneath his bed, wrapped in oilcloth, and each night he read them the way a man reads the diagnosis of a disease he already suspects he carries: slowly, wincing, unable to stop.

Thirty years of entries. Not clerical errors, whatever Corvane wanted anyone to believe. A pattern as clean as stitching. Every seventh winter or so, sometimes longer gaps, sometimes less, an entry in Corvane's own hand—Corvane, who never wrote anything himself, who kept scriveners the way other men kept dogs—recording a *transfer outbound*. One soul. Two souls. Once, horribly, three, from a fishing village on the Greywater whose name had been struck through so thoroughly the paper had thinned beneath it.

And beside each entry, a mark. A buyer's mark, the way a merchant signs the back of a stolen bill of lading.

Most of the marks were the same: a circle bisected by a descending line, like a coin falling into a well. The mountain's mark. The court beneath, taking its due, patient as geology. Freya had stared at that mark until his eyes watered, trying to make it mean something other than what it meant. It did not. It was a signature on an invoice. The deep court bought the way it did everything else—formally, and in bulk.

But on the fourth page, dated nineteen years past, there was a different mark. A single entry in all those decades where the descending line bent. Where the coin, having fallen, had been *drawn back up*.

A thistle in a broken ring. Inked in silver, not the ledger's brown-black iron gall, as if someone had insisted on paying for the ink separately, on making even the correction expensive.

Freya sat back on his heels and looked at it for a long time.

*One soul, bought back. Price: ruinous.*

The entry did not say whose soul. It did not say who had paid. It said only *paid in full*, in a clerk's cramped hand, and beneath that, in Corvane's elegant script, a single word: *settled.*

Nineteen years ago. Before Freya had ever sold his first memory. Before he had ever walked through the guild's hidden door with nothing in his pockets but the shape of things he could no longer name. The rescue had happened in a world that existed before him, or so it felt, and yet here it was, inked in silver, the only entry in thirty years where the ledger's arithmetic had run backward.

Someone had gone down to the mountain—or up, or under, or however one went to the court beneath—and come back. With a soul.

Freya blew out the candle and sat in the dark and let himself, for exactly the length of one breath, feel hope. It was an unfamiliar sensation, thin and sharp as a fish bone caught sideways in the throat.

* * *

The thistle was the hard part. The silver ink was the easy part.

He started with Maren, because Maren was the only person in the guild whose loyalty he trusted further than he could throw her, and because Maren had been with the Ember for thirty-one years—one year longer, he now understood, than Corvane's entries. She had seen the ledger's whole life.

He found her in the kitchen at the dead hour past midnight, rendering fat for the morning's candles, and he did not ask directly. Asking directly was how people vanished from the guild's rolls, their rooms reassigned before their beds went cold. Instead he said, "Do you remember silver ink?"

Maren did not look up from her work. The fat spat in the pot. "Silver ink is for settlements," she said. "Corrections that cost somebody everything. We keep it locked with the seals. Why?"

"Has it always been locked?"

That made her look up. Her face in the firelight was all planes and patience, a face that had watched thirty years of thieves lie to each other and had learned to wait out every one of them. "You've been in the ledger room," she said quietly. Not a question. Freya's stomach dropped, and then he saw that her expression was not accusation. It was something worse. It was *relief*.

"Maren—"

"I'm not asking," she said. "I've been here thirty-one years and I have never once asked what the ledgers hold, because the ones who ask end up as entries." She wiped her hands on her apron, slow and deliberate. "But there was a night, nineteen years ago. I was younger. Corvane called me up to the front room at the thirteenth hour and told me to prepare a bed and draw a hot bath and speak to no one, and then a carriage came, and in the carriage was a woman in mourning grey with a boy of about six asleep against her shoulder, and the boy did not breathe like a sleeping child breathes. He breathed like a house with no one home." Her voice did not waver, but her hands had gone still. "She carried him up the stairs herself. She would not let anyone else touch him. And when she left, three days later, the boy walked out on his own feet, and he looked at me—Maren, he *looked* at me, the way a lamp looks when someone has finally lit it—and she paid Corvane in a chest of coin so heavy it took two men to carry, and I heard her say one thing before the door closed."

Freya realized he had stopped breathing.

"She said, 'You will sell to me again if I ever ask. Swear it on whatever you have left that is worth swearing on.' And Corvane swore. And that was the only time in thirty-one years I ever saw Corvane afraid."

"What was her name?"

"I don't know her name." Maren turned back to her fat and her fire. "But the carriage bore arms on the door. A thistle, in a broken ring. Northern arms. The Vess arms."

* * *

Lady Vess. Freya turned the name over all the next day while he catalogued a stolen memory—a fisherman's last summer, bottled grief-adjacent, the taste of salt and a woman's laugh, the kind of ordinary miracle the guild moved a hundred of in a good season. He priced it, sealed it, shelved it, and felt the familiar nothing where a feeling should have been, and thought: *nineteen years ago a woman looked at her son and decided he was worth a chest of coin and a debt owed to a man like Corvane, and she got him back, and the ledger says it is the only time it has ever worked.*

Everyone knew of Lady Vess, the way everyone knew of weather. Widow of a border lord dead these twelve years. Holder of lands in the north where the roads gave out and the king's writ was more suggestion than law. Patron, it was whispered, of various enterprises of flexible legality—because a woman alone with a northern holding survived by being useful to people who worked in the dark, and Lady Vess had been useful for a long time.

The guild had done work for her. Freya was almost certain of it. Somewhere in the commission rolls there would be a job with the payment routed through intermediaries, and intermediaries left traces the way water left marks on stone.

He found it on the second day, in the commission ledger for eleven years past: a retrieval. *One object, sentimental value, from a house in the capital. Buyer: unnamed. Payment: routed.* The object had been a portrait. Freya knew because the scrivener's note in the margin, in the shorthand the guild used for things too delicate to write plainly, read: *portrait of the buyer's son, age six. Buyer wept at delivery. Do not record.*

Do not record. The guild's oldest instruction, its truest scripture. And yet the scrivener had recorded it anyway, in the margin, in pencil, in a hand that had probably thought itself invisible.

Freya copied the page. Then he sat with it, and with the silver-inked entry, and with Maren's story, and he did the thing he was best at in all the world, the thing the guild had ground into him until it replaced whatever he'd been before: he made a plan.

* * *

You did not simply call on Lady Vess. A woman who bought back souls did not receive visitors who arrived by the front gate with their names spoken aloud. So Freya went the way the guild taught: invisibly, expensively, and with a gift.

The gift took him two days to prepare, and it was the most honest work he had done in years. He went down into the vaults, past the shelves of bottled grief and the drawers of stolen years, to the section where the guild kept the things it could not sell—the returns, the mistakes, the items whose owners had died or vanished before collection, which the guild held in perpetuity because Corvane said it was policy and Freya now suspected it was leverage. There he found what he was looking for: a memory, small and old, catalogued nineteen years past. *Provenance: northern. Content: a boy, age six, learning to whistle. Seller: the boy's mother. Reason for sale: recorded as "collateral."*

Collateral. She had sold a memory of her son as *collateral* on the debt of getting him back, and the guild had kept it, all these years, a hostage of the heart in a glass jar on a shelf.

Freya took it. Not stole—took, deliberately, leaving in its place a chit with his own mark, which was either honesty or the most arrogant theft of his career, and he genuinely could not tell which. He wrapped the little vial in wool and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where it felt less like cargo and more like a coal.

The journey north took four days. He traveled as a scrivener for a wool factor, which was the least interesting person in any room in the world, and by the fourth day he had been stopped twice, searched once, and entirely forgotten by everyone who had seen him, which was exactly as intended.

Vesshold sat where the road gave out, as the whispers promised: a grey keep on a grey crag above a grey valley, its walls patched with stone of three different centuries, its banner—the thistle in the broken ring—hanging motionless in air too cold to move it. Freya presented himself at the postern gate at the thirteenth hour, because if Lady Vess had once conducted business at the thirteenth hour with Corvane, she would understand the grammar of it.

The gatekeeper was an old woman with a crossbow and no curiosity whatsoever. "State the gift," she said, which told Freya everything: Lady Vess received no one without one, and the gatekeeper knew it, and the whole household had learned to speak in this currency.

"A memory," Freya said. "Hers. Sold nineteen years ago as collateral. I've come to return it, free of charge, and to ask about a mark in a ledger."

The gatekeeper looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "Wait here in the cold until I decide whether you're a fool or a dead man," and shut the wicket, and Freya waited in the cold for the better part of an hour, and it was, he had to admit, fair.

* * *

She received him in a room with one window and too many books, a room that smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and Freya felt a shock of recognition so violent he nearly staggered: it smelled like the ledger room. Like home. Like the thing he was in the process of betraying.

Lady Vess was perhaps fifty, tall, grey at the temples in a way that looked less like age than like weather. She did not offer him a chair. She stood by the window with her hands folded and said, "Show me."

He set the vial on the table between them.

She looked at it the way Maren had described the boy breathing—like a house with no one home, except inverted. She looked at it like a house with *everyone* home, all at once, every room lit, and it was too much light for one face to hold. Her hands unfolded. Refolded.

"Open it," she said, and her voice was steady, and her hands were not.

Freya unstoppered the vial. The memory came out the way memories did, as a faint warmth and a suggestion of sound—a boy's laughter, thin and bright, a whistle rising through three wobbling notes—and Lady Vess closed her eyes and stood in it for the length of four heartbeats, five, six, and when she opened her eyes they were wet and furious and absolutely clear.

"Nineteen years," she said. "He whistles now the way he whistled then. I taught him again, after. I taught him everything again. His name. His colors. That I was his mother." She picked up the vial and held it as if it were a bird with a broken wing. "Do you know what I paid? Not the coin. The coin was nothing. I paid Corvane a standing debt. I swore that if he ever needed a door opened in the north, a cargo moved, a witness silenced, a border looked away from—I would do it, and ask nothing. Nineteen years I have been the Ember's door in the north. Do you know how many souls have passed *through* that door, scrivener, on their way beneath the mountain, while I stood there holding it open because the price of my son's life was to become part of the machine that eats sons?"

"No," Freya said. "Tell me."

She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, polished and hollow. "You already know. You've read the ledgers. That's why you're here." She set the vial down with exaggerated care, the care of a woman managing her own hands. "So. You have found the only rescue in thirty years of sales, and you have come to me because you think I know the way down." Her eyes fixed on him, and Freya understood that he had never in his life been in a room with anyone this dangerous, because she was not dangerous the way Corvane was dangerous—patient, greedy, well-fed. She was dangerous the way a person is dangerous who has already lost the only thing that could be taken from her.

"Here is what I know," she said. "I know the road through the abandoned mine at Cael's Hollow, because I paid to have it mapped and then paid to have the map burned, and the map exists now only in the head of a man I trust, which is to say a man I have ruined so thoroughly he cannot afford to betray me. I know the court beneath keeps accounts, and that its accounts are its law, and that in nineteen years of studying them I have found exactly one weakness." She leaned forward. "They honor their own paperwork. Absolutely. Without mercy, without exception, without *sense*. It is the only thing they love."

"And you think that's a weakness."

"I know it is. I exploited it once. It is the only reason my son is upstairs asleep instead of breathing like an empty house." She straightened. "Now. You will tell me why you've come, whose soul you mean to buy back or steal back or argue back, and you will tell me everything, because I will not move one step toward that mountain on half a story. And then, scrivener, you and I are going to discuss the price."

Freya's hand went, without his meaning it to, to his coat, where the copied pages lay against his ribs. "There's a boy," he said. "He came to our door barefoot in the snow. He speaks a language older than any kingdom. And the ledger says the guild sold him before he could walk."

Something moved behind Lady Vess's eyes—the flicker of a woman seeing her own nineteen-years-ago self standing barefoot at someone else's door.

"The moon turns thirteen times," Freya said. "We have until the thirteenth turn. And I need to know, my lady, what it cost you—*truly* cost you—so I can decide whether I can afford it."

Lady Vess looked out the window at the grey valley, at the road that gave out, at all the distance between here and the mountain.

"Sit down," she said. "It will take all night to tell you. And when I'm done, you may wish you had never found that mark in silver ink."

She pulled a chair from the wall. Outside, the first snow of the season had begun to fall, early and thick, as if the north itself had decided to count the moons down with them.
