## 9. The Court Beneath

The dream did not come to Ash the way dreams came to other children.

Other children, he had learned—watching Maren's face as she told stories by the kitchen fire, watching the way the guild-thieves laughed at their own memories in the evenings—other children sank into sleep the way one sinks into warm water. Slowly, and then all at once, and then not at all. Their dreams were made of the day behind them, bent and brightened, or of days they wished for ahead. They woke from nightmares with their hearts hammering and their mothers' names on their lips, and by morning the fear had gone soft at the edges, worn down like a stone in a stream.

Ash's dream was not like that. Ash's dream was a place.

It waited for him every night beneath his eyelids like a room beneath a floor. It did not change. It did not blur. It had corridors and ceilings and a smell—the smell of hot gold and cold stone and something older than either, something like the inside of a bell that has been ringing so long it has forgotten silence. In the waking world, Ash could not have described any of this, because the waking world had taken his words away before he ever learned to hold them. But in the dream, in the deep gold light of the court beneath the mountain, he understood everything, because understanding was the only currency they dealt in there.

He always arrived the same way: falling upward.

That was the trick of it, the joke the court never tired of telling. The mountain did not pull him down. It pulled the ground out from under everything else, until down became a direction he remembered rather than a direction he traveled. He fell through snow that hung motionless around him like a held breath, through the grey village that no longer existed, through the arms of a woman whose face he could not see but whose hands he knew better than his own heartbeat—and then the gold took him, and the falling stopped, and the doors opened.

They were very tall doors. Everything in the court was tall. Tallness here was not grandeur; it was patience given a shape. The doors were gilded from top to bottom, but the gold was thin, worn at the edges where centuries of hands had pushed them open, and beneath the gold the wood showed through like bone beneath skin. That was the first lesson of the court, the one it taught you before you could even stand: everything beautiful was something else wearing a coat.

Inside, the corridor stretched farther than any corridor should. Lamps burned along its length without flame, each one holding a small, slow light that moved the way breath moves in sleep. And along the walls, set into niches carved with perfect courtesy, stood the files.

Ash had no word for *files* in the old language, though the old language had a word for almost everything. What his people had called them was closer to *kept ones*—the things that were kept, the things that were being kept safe for someone, the way a knife is kept safe in its sheath, the way a debt is kept safe until it comes due. Each niche held a small shape of light, no bigger than a loaf of bread, no brighter than a coal at dusk. Each one breathed.

He walked among them because he could do nothing else. His feet knew the way the way a river knows its bed.

Some of the lights flickered when he passed. Some leaned toward him, straining against the walls of their niches, and though they had no mouths he could feel them trying to speak—not in words but in weather, in the pressure that builds before rain. A girl-light with a crack down its center, like a bowl that had been mended badly. An old man-light, dim and stubborn, still burning after what must have been sixty years of keeping. A child-light, smaller than the others, newer, and Ash always stopped there longest, because the child-light always stopped for him too, and for one moment the two of them simply looked at each other across the gilded dark, two kept things recognizing their own kind.

Then the footsteps would begin.

They came from everywhere and nowhere, unhurried, polished, patient. The clerks of the court did not walk so much as arrive, in increments, the way frost arrives on a window. They were beautiful the way the doors were beautiful: gold over bone. Their faces were smooth and kind and entirely without expression except for courtesy, which they wore the way other creatures wore fur. When they spoke—and they spoke rarely, and softly, and only when a thing needed saying—they spoke in the old language, the language the waking world had stripped out of Ash's mouth, and in the dream it poured back into him like water into a dry well.

*"The account remains open,"* they said, every night, the same words, in voices like pages turning. *"The account remains open. Payment is expected at the turn of the thirteenth moon."*

And then they would smile—which was to say, their faces would arrange themselves into the shape kindness makes when kindness is a procedure—and the tallest of them, the one whose gold coat fit worst, whose bone showed most at the wrists, would kneel down to Ash's height and look at him with eyes like ledger-lines, ruled and numbered and empty, and say:

*"You are not lost, little kept one. You are filed."*

Every night, the same. Every night, the gold, the breathing lights, the courteous footsteps, the words. And every night, at the end, just before the dream let him go—just before it released him gently, almost tenderly, back up into his small bed in the guild-house above—it showed him the vault.

The vault was where the paid accounts went. He knew this the way he knew the smell of hot gold: not learned but remembered, some knowledge older than his knowing. The vault door was iron, and the iron was honest—no gilt, no polish, only rust and rivets and the plain blunt truth of metal that expects to be shut forever. Behind it, in rows deeper than the corridor, deeper than the mountain, the settled accounts slept. Souls fully paid for. Souls that belonged to someone now, the way a chair belongs to whoever bought it, the way a field belongs to whoever holds the deed, the way—

The way Ash belonged to whoever held his page.

He never saw who opened the vault door. He only saw it standing open, once, at the very end of the dream, and beyond it a darkness that was not empty but *full*—full of the quiet of things that have stopped struggling—and a voice, not the clerks' voice, something lower and vaster, coming up out of that fullness like heat from a forge:

*"Bring me the thirteenth page."*

*

He woke the way he always woke: all at once, sitting up, hands already fisted in the blanket, breath sawing in and out of a throat too small for the size of the scream caught in it. The scream never came out. It never had. Even here, even in the waking world where his voice technically existed, it stayed locked behind his teeth, because some doors, once locked, stay locked from both sides.

The attic room Maren had given him was dark and cold and smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil, and it was nothing—*nothing*—like the corridor, and he made himself look at every corner of it twice to prove that. The crooked window with its frost ferns. The shelf where Maren kept her mending basket. The little stove she'd carried up three flights for him, dented and black-hearted and giving off its honest heat. His boots, mended, sitting side by side beneath the window like two small faithful animals asleep.

His boots. Someone had mended his boots. He touched them every morning. In the court beneath the mountain, nothing had ever been mended; things there were only kept, or spent.

He sat until his heart slowed. Then he did what he did every night, the ritual Maren had taught him without ever calling it a ritual: he pressed his palm flat against the cold window glass and counted the moons. One small silver curve hung over the rooftops, three-quarters gone, and below the horizon, invisible but known, its sisters were turning. Twelve had turned since the night he'd come barefoot to the hidden door. Thirteen remained—no. No. Twelve remained. He recounted, fingers against the frosted pane, and got twelve again, and the number sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Twelve turns left. Then the ground would tilt, and the falling would begin, and this time he would not wake up at the end of it.

*

In the mornings, the world was kind to him, and he had learned to accept its kindnesses the way one accepts gifts from someone dying: gratefully, and while looking away.

Maren fed him porridge with honey stirred through it, honey being expensive and Maren spending it anyway, her broad face daring anyone in the kitchen to comment. She talked to him constantly, a river of talk that asked nothing back—about the bread proving badly, about the state of the cellar stores, about the cook in some other guild-house years ago who'd watered the soup until even the rats complained. Ash didn't understand one word in twenty yet. It didn't matter. Her voice was a roof over him, and under a roof, even a storm is bearable.

Freya was different. Freya was quiet the way the vault door was quiet—honest metal, no polish—and he watched Ash with eyes that seemed to be reading something written just behind the boy's face. In the evenings, when the guild-house emptied and the lamps were trimmed low, Freya would sit across the table from him with paper and ink, and they would play the game they played.

It was not really a game. It was an excavation.

Freya would draw—a stick figure, a house, a moon, a mountain—and watch Ash's face. If the boy's eyes went flat and far away, wrong drawing. If they sharpened, if his breath changed, right drawing, keep digging. Then Ash would take the charcoal and draw back, and his drawings were terrible in technique and unbearable in content: the mountain with its mouth open. Rows of small lights in walls. Hands, many hands, reaching up out of the ground. And figures—always figures—small stick-children walking in a line toward the mountain's mouth, and behind them, larger, the whole village walking the same direction, not dragged but *walking*, heads bowed, and Freya had stared at that drawing a long time the first night, long enough that Ash had feared he'd broken something in the man.

Tonight the excavation went differently. Tonight it broke through.

Freya drew the mountain, as he always began, and Ash nodded, and Freya drew the corridor, the niches, the small breathing lights—and Ash's chest tightened, because yes, yes, exactly, how did this man know, has he *been* there?—and then Freya paused, quill hovering, and instead of drawing a clerk, he wrote.

A single word, in his careful thief's script. Ash knew it instantly, because Freya had written it a hundred times in front of him, had spoken it a hundred times in that low careful voice, tracing its letters in spilled flour on the kitchen table while Maren clattered pots overhead: the word for the great books, the books of debts, the books that ruled this whole house above and, he understood suddenly, the whole house below.

*Ledger.*

And Ash looked at the word, and something in him turned over like soil in spring.

Because here was the thing about the waking world: it had taken his language, but it had not taken his *understanding*, and understanding, denied a mouth, will find another door. For months the words of this new tongue had been gathering in him like snowfall on a roof—silent, weightless, accumulating. He understood Maren's river-talk more than he let show. He understood the thieves' jokes half the time. He understood Freya completely, or nearly, and had understood for weeks, and had said nothing, because saying nothing was the only power he had left and he hoarded it the way the court hoarded souls.

But the word *ledger* was not snowfall. The word *ledger* was the roof beam itself.

It was the word the court used most. It was the word the clerks' voices were shaped like. It was the word his whole existence hung from—one soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain—and hearing it in this warm kitchen, written in this kind man's hand, he felt both languages meet inside him at last, the old one flowing down and the new one rising up, and where they met, a door unlocked.

"Ledger," said Ash.

The word came out cracked and rusty and strange-shaped, a sound that hadn't been used in months, and it hurt his throat the way standing does a leg long asleep, and it was the most magnificent pain of his life.

Freya went absolutely still.

Not startled-still. Still the way a man goes still when a deer steps into a clearing—the stillness of not wanting to frighten a miracle. His eyes, those reading eyes, went wide and wet at the same time, and his hand, the one holding the quill, trembled so finely that a drop of ink fell and bloomed on the table like a small black moon.

"Ledger," Ash said again, steadier, tasting it, owning it. "Ledger."

"Ash." Freya's voice came out low and rough, barely working. "Ash, can you—do you understand me?"

He understood the question. He understood every word of it, and the joy of that, the *freedom* of it, was so enormous that it broke something loose, and to Freya's visible alarm the mute boy began to laugh—a silent laugh, because his voice only worked in single words, but laughing all the same, shoulders shaking, tears sliding down his face, pointing at the word on the page and then at himself and then at Freya and then at the ceiling, at the whole house, at the entire waking world that had finally, finally let him in.

Footsteps on the stairs. Maren, drawn by some cook's sixth sense, filled the doorway with flour on her apron and a wooden spoon still in hand, and took in the scene—Freya frozen, ink bleeding, Ash laughing and crying—and her face did something complicated and tender.

"What happened?" she said. "What's happened, love?"

"He spoke." Freya's voice cracked clean through on the second word. "Maren. He *spoke*. He said—" and here Freya stopped, because he had realized what Ash had realized, and the joy drained slowly out of his face and left something colder behind. "He said *ledger*."

The spoon lowered. Maren looked at the boy—at the sweet, silent, mended-boots-and-honey boy she'd folded into her kitchen like a stray cat—and Ash watched her arrive, a beat behind Freya, at the same understanding. Not *mama*. Not *home*. Not *help me*.

The word of the books. The word of the debt. Of everything that was being counted against him.

"The dream again?" Freya asked quietly, kneeling beside Ash's chair, and Ash nodded, and found—he marveled at this—that he could answer. One word. He had one word, and he would spend it like his last coin.

"Yes," Ash said.

Freya closed his eyes. When he opened them, they had gone hard and bright, the eyes of a man doing sums he doesn't want to finish.

"Ash," he said carefully, slowly, choosing each word like a lockpick. "In your dream. The place under the mountain. Did you hear them say anything about—about a page? About *your* page? About when they're coming for it?"

Ash understood. Oh, he understood, and the understanding was ice water, because he had hoped—childishly, stupidly, warmly—that speaking would bring him into the light, that words were the way out. But the first word out of his mouth had been the word of the debt, and the debt did not care that he could speak now. The debt had never needed him to speak.

Still. Freya had asked. Freya deserved the truth; Freya had mended his boots.

"Yes," Ash said. Then, because one word was not enough and he refused to stop at one, he reached for the quill, pulled the paper close, and drew: thirteen circles in a row, and crossed out twelve of them, one by one, pressing hard enough to tear.

One circle left unmarked.

Below it, he drew the iron door of the vault, standing open. And below that, because he could not say it and the picture was not enough, he took Freya's hand in both of his small ones, and pressed the man's palm flat against his own chest, over his heart, the way you press your hand to a wall to feel whether something is moving behind it.

Something was moving behind it. Something patient. Something hungry. Twelve turns from now, it intended to collect.

Freya stared at him for a long moment, feeling that small beating heart under his palm like a knock at a door—like a knock at the thirteenth hour—and when he stood, he was no longer the quiet archivist of the evening table. He was something older and harder, something Ash had only glimpsed in the man's rare silences.

"Maren," Freya said, and his voice was steady in a way that frightened Ash worse than the dream had, because it was the steadiness of a decision already made. "Get your coat. We need to go down to the ledger room."

* * *

The guild-house slept around them as they descended—Maren's heavy tread behind, Ash's hand swallowed in Freya's grip, past the kitchens, past the dormitory where thieves breathed in the dark like a tide. Down, and down, to the door Ash had never been allowed through, the door at the bottom of the stairs where the air changed and went cool and dry and smelled of ink and dust and time.

Freya lit a single lamp. Its light climbed the shelves, and the shelves rose into shadow, row upon row upon row, hundreds of ledgers, thousands, the whole accounting of the Ember's thirty stolen decades sleeping in their bindings.

Ash stood in the doorway, and the hair rose on his arms, because he knew this room.

Not the room itself—but the *shape* of it. The hush. The sense of things kept. It was a corridor, this room, and the ledgers in their shelves were lights in their niches, and somewhere in this gilded-dark house above the world, patient and courteous and utterly without hurry, the court beneath the mountain kept its mirror.

Somewhere on these shelves was a page with his name on it. Or whatever the court used instead of a name.

Freya set the lamp down, rolled up his sleeves, and looked at the shelves the way Ash had seen him look at locks.

"All right," he said softly—to Ash, to Maren, to the room, to whatever was listening from beneath the mountain. "Let's find out what we owe."

And far below them, deeper than cellars, deeper than foundations, in a corridor of gold where nothing ever hurried, something turned a page.
