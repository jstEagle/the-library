## 10. An Offer, Politely Made

Guildmaster Corvane had a theory about tea.

Anyone could pour it hot. Anyone could choose the leaves. The art of it—the whole architecture of power distilled into a single domestic act—was in the waiting. Tea that arrived the moment a guest sat down said *you are expected, you are managed*. Tea that arrived too late said *you are beneath notice*. But tea poured at precisely the moment the guest's hands began to fidget, at the moment silence first grew teeth—*that* tea said: *I have been watching you this entire time, and I am not troubled by what I've seen.*

He had Maren brew the black leaf from the southern trade roads, the kind with smoke underneath it, because Freya Reyes drank his tea like a man performing indifference and Corvane wanted the performance to cost him something.

The ledger room's upper office smelled of tallow, old vellum, and the faint mineral cold that seeped up through the floor from the vaults below. Corvane kept the lamps turned low. He had learned long ago that men told more truth in dim rooms; brightness made them rehearse.

Freya came when summoned, which was itself interesting. He came without hurrying, which was more interesting still. And he paused—one heartbeat, no longer—at the threshold of the office, eyes flicking to the corners, to the shelf behind the desk where the master ledgers lived under lock and warding-sigil, before he arranged his face into its usual mask of competent boredom.

Yes, Corvane thought, pouring. There it is.

"Sit," he said. "You'll want to hear all of it standing up, I imagine. Sit anyway."

Freya sat. He did not touch the tea.

Corvane let the quiet stretch. Outside, the tenth moon was two nights past full, and its light came thin through the leaded window, silvering the dust motes, laying pale bars across Freya's folded hands. A young man's hands, scarred at the knuckles from picking locks older than he was. Corvane had watched those hands for eleven years. He knew their tells better than Freya did. The right thumb pressed flat against the forefinger when he was calculating. Both hands went still as dead things when he was afraid.

Both hands were very still now.

"You copied pages," Corvane said pleasantly.

* * *

He watched the word land. Not like a knife—like a coin dropped down a well, falling a long way before the sound of it caught up. Freya's face did nothing. His breathing did nothing. But his weight shifted back onto his heels, half an inch, the body quietly preparing for a door that wasn't there yet.

"I don't know what you mean," Freya said.

"No," Corvane agreed. "You wouldn't." He turned his cup a quarter-rotation on its saucer, aligning the painted rim with the painted line. "Tuesday last. Third hour past midnight. You took the wax impressions of the locks on the ledger case—you used candle stub from the kitchen, which was sloppy; Maren counts her stubs—and you spent ninety-one minutes in this room with *Annals of Trade, Volume Seven*, and you left with the shape of forty-one entries inked into a notebook you keep strapped against your left ribs."

The lamp flame leaned. Somewhere below them, in the memory-vaults, a stored life murmured in its sleep, the way the deep shelves always murmured on cold nights. Corvane found it restful. Most people found it dreadful.

"You're wondering how I know," he continued. "Don't. It's my business to know what happens in this house. That is the whole of what a guildmaster is—a man who knows. You've been waiting eleven years for me to be careless. I admire your patience. Patience is the rarest thing we steal." He smiled, and made sure the smile reached only halfway, so Freya could see the machinery behind it. "But you've made a mistake, and the mistake isn't the copying. The mistake is that you stopped. Forty-one entries. If you were going to hang me, boy, you should have taken five hundred."

Freya's voice, when it finally came, was admirably level. "If you knew, why am I not already in the canal?"

"Because canals are for mistakes, and you haven't made one yet. You've made a *choice*. Those are different animals entirely." Corvane rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over the rooftops of the Warrens, where the guild's invisible hands had been at work for three generations. "I'm going to tell you a story. You'll want to interrupt it several times. Don't. Interruption is just fear wearing a clever coat."

* * *

"The year I took this desk, I was thirty-one years old and I believed exactly what you believe now—that the Ember traded in stolen things and nothing worse. Years. Grief. Memories pried out of sleeping heads. Ugly commerce, but honest ugliness. Nobody dies. That was the catechism. *Nobody dies.*"

He heard Freya shift. Held up one finger without turning around.

"In my fourth year, a woman came to the door at the thirteenth hour. You'll appreciate that detail. Barefoot, though it was autumn, not winter. She carried a child wrapped in a blanket, and the child was breathing wrong—each breath with a little hitch in it, like a song played by someone who'd forgotten the middle. She had no money. What she had instead was an offer from someone who claimed to represent a court beneath the mountain. They would take her son's soul—filed, sealed, held in trust—and in exchange they would leave her village alone. No wasting sickness. No dreams that walked. Her neighbors had already sold two daughters the same way, and their houses stood untouched while others rotted from the inside."

"And you believed that," Freya said. Flat. Not a question—an accusation with nowhere to land yet.

"I believed I was being tested," Corvane said. "I thought some rival guild had invented the mountain court to see if I'd flinch. So I sent the woman away. Politely. With bread." He turned from the window then, and let Freya look at him properly—let him see that whatever was in his face was not cruelty. "Thirty days later her village was empty. Not dead—empty. Every soul in it simply *gone*, the way a cup is gone after the tide takes it. Houses intact. Fires still lit. Meals on tables going grey. And in every hearth, a small gilded token, stamped with a seal I now know very well. Payment rendered. Receipt issued."

Silence. Below them, the vaults murmured.

"The court beneath the mountain is not a story, Freya. It is older than every kingdom whose coins we fence. It does not hate us. Hatred would be comprehensible, and we could fight hatred. It is *hungry*, and patient, and it keeps immaculate accounts. And here is the truth I have governed this guild by for thirty years: it can be fed, or it can hunt. Feeding is cheaper. Feeding is quieter. Feeding looks, from certain angles, almost like mercy."

"You call selling children mercy."

"I call arithmetic mercy," Corvane said, "when the alternative is subtraction." He returned to the desk, sat, laced his fingers. "One soul, sold lawfully, and a village stands. Refuse, and the court takes what it considers owed—with interest. I have kept the ledger balanced for thirty years. Do you know how many villages in these mountains still light their fires? More than should, given what walks beneath us. That is not cowardice. That is stewardship."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone careful, the way it went when he was cataloguing a theft—measuring everything, feeling nothing. Or trying to.

"The entries I copied. Forty-one souls in thirty years."

"Fifty-three, actually. You missed twelve; the early ones are filed under crop-tithe codes, deliberately. But yes."

"And the buyers?"

Corvane inclined his head, genuinely pleased. "There it is. You always were the best of them. Yes—the court doesn't eat everything it buys. Some souls it resells, to collectors, to the ambitious, to grieving parents with bottomless purses. Lady Vess bought her son back at a price that beggared three generations of her house. You found her mark. Of course you did."

"If it's mercy," Freya said softly, "why does it need to be secret? Why do the ledgers lie about themselves?"

And there it was—the question Corvane had been waiting for, the sharp edge of him, the reason Corvane had never once considered having the boy drowned despite eleven years of watching and waiting for cause. He spread his hands.

"Because mercy that announces itself gets murdered, Freya. Because if the city knew what the mountain wanted, the city would do what cities do—burn the wrong things, kill the wrong people, march into the dark with torches and come back as receipts. Secrecy isn't shame. Secrecy is load-bearing." He let that sit, then rose again and came around the desk, close enough that Freya had to tilt his chin up slightly to hold his gaze. Close enough for intimacy, or threat, or both. "Which brings me to the actual purpose of tonight."

* * *

"Partnership," Corvane said.

He set a second object on the desk between them as he said it: a slim ledger, bound in grey calfskin, unmarked except for a sigil pressed blind into the cover—no ink, no gilt, legible only to fingertips. The private book. The true one. He watched Freya's eyes go to it and stay there, hunger and horror fighting openly across his face for the first time all evening.

"The Ember has run for sixty years on the fiction that it steals things," Corvane continued. "Years, griefs, memories—small change. Beneath that, for thirty of those years, it has done the work that actually matters: keeping the accounts of the deep court balanced. Buying low, selling lawfully, feeding the mountain just enough to keep it sleeping. I have done this alone, Freya. Alone, with quills and arithmetic and a face I show no one, and I am fifty-nine years old, and my hands shake on cold mornings now, and I find myself needing—" he paused, chose the word with care— "*a successor.* Someone young enough to sit across from auditors without flinching. Someone meticulous enough to catch what I miss. Someone who already knows the worst of it and hasn't run."

"You haven't offered me a choice," Freya said. "You've offered me complicity."

"I've offered you the truth, which most men never get from anyone. Take it or don't." Corvane opened the grey book to its final page, where a single line waited, blank and ruled. "Sign as my partner-in-trust, and the notebook against your ribs becomes a working document. We'll need it. The court has called in a debt—there's a boy in my kitchens, barefoot in the snow, speaking the old language, and the ledgers say he was sold before he could walk. Thirteenth turn comes due in three moons. I intend to honor that sale, because the alternative is what happened to the woman's village multiplied by every valley from here to the sea. And I would rather not do the arithmetic alone."

He watched the name land on Freya like weather. Ash. The child Maren had named, the child Freya had been seen carrying on his shoulders through the yard last week, laughing—or making the shape of laughter, which for Freya amounted to the same thing.

"He's a child," Freya said. Quietly. Dangerously.

"He's a *debt*," Corvane answered, gentle as snowfall. "That is what the world made him before either of us met him. I didn't sell him. The ledger says otherwise, and the ledger, whatever else you think of me, does not lie—it merely records. Corvane sells what he never owned? No. Read your own stolen pages again, boy. The signature on Ash's sale predates me by nine years. My predecessor's hand. I inherited the account, as I inherited the desk, as you may inherit both."

It was even true. That was the beauty of thirty years of careful bookkeeping: the lies were all structural, and every individual fact remained load-bearing.

Freya stood. Slowly, without permission, but Corvane allowed it; there was no further use in pretending this was an interrogation. The boy looked down at the open page, at the blank rule awaiting a name, and Corvane saw the whole war happen behind his eyes—the loneliness that had lived in him since he'd sold his own childhood to this guild at fourteen, the found family he was only now admitting he'd begun to feel, the terrible clean logic of the arithmetic Corvane had laid before him. Corvane had built the offer out of exactly those materials, the way he built everything. People believed what their wounds told them to believe.

"I need time," Freya said.

"Of course." Corvane closed the grey book and slid it across. "Keep it. Read every entry. Verify my numbers—I insist on it; I've earned scrutiny. When you've finished, sign, or bring the book back and tell me to my face that you'd rather feed fifty-three villages to the tide than sully your conscience. Either answer, Freya, and I will respect it." A pause. "One thing only. While you decide—the boy stays inside the walls. If he leaves this house before the turn completes, the court will take him wherever he stands, and everyone within arm's reach besides. Keep him close. For his sake."

Freya took the book. Their fingers did not touch. He walked to the door, and Corvane spoke once more, softly, to the space between the boy's shoulder blades:

"For what it's worth—I know what you gave us at fourteen. I approved the purchase myself. Your father's debts were real, and the price was fair, and I have often wondered what it was you paid away." He let the lamp gutter once, deliberately, so the shadow crossed his face. "If you ever want it back, partner, I keep excellent records. Even of the things we took from you."

Freya went out without answering. The door clicked shut with the particular softness of a man refusing to slam it.

Corvane stood alone in the lamplight a long while. Then he retrieved the wax impression Maren's counting had uncovered, rolled it between finger and thumb, and dropped it into the fire, where the kitchen candle's ghost curled and vanished.

He was not worried. Worry was for men who hadn't yet read the ending. Freya Reyes would rage for a few nights, run his errands to Lady Vess—he'd marked the boy's second visit to the Vess townhouse yesterday, and had done nothing, because a conspiracy needed rope before it could be useful—and then, when the thirteenth turn came and the mountain stirred, the boy's conscience would do Corvane's signing for him. Conscience always did. It was the most reliable instrument in any thief's kit, and Corvane had never once had to steal it.

Below the floor, the vaults murmured. Fifty-three souls, filed and dreaming.

Fifty-four, soon enough, he thought, pouring himself the last of the cold tea. Unless the boy surprised him.

Corvane rather hoped he would. It had been thirty years since anything had.
