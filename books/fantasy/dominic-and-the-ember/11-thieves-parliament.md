## 11. Thieves' Parliament

The meeting place was a dead man's house, which was fitting, because everyone in it had spent years pretending to be someone else.

It stood at the end of Candlewick Row, behind a shuttered chandler's shop that hadn't sold a candle in eleven years. The owner had been a forger named Pellam Wick, and when he died—quietly, in his sleep, with no debts anyone could collect—the thieves of the city had observed a superstition older than any guild: no one claimed the house. It sat there like an unspent coin, and coins left unspent, the saying went, belonged to luck. So they met there when they needed to meet without being seen, because even luck had to be paid sometimes.

Freya arrived last, by design. He had spent two hours that evening walking a route with more turns than a liar's confession—down through the tannery district where the stink covered all other scents, across the Weepers' Bridge where the fog was thick enough to drown a footprint, up again through the bone-yard of old ships along the river's edge. He had counted his shadows twice and found three too many, then found them gone, and decided he could not afford to decide whether that was better or worse.

The copied pages were against his chest, sewn into the lining of his coat in oilcloth. Thirty years of Corvane's handwriting. Dozens of names. *One soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain.* Over and over, in ink so patient it might have been written by the mountain itself.

He let himself in through the coal chute, as instructed, and climbed the stairs into a room lit by a single lamp turned down to a bruise-colored glow.

They were already there.

* * *

He knew two of them by reputation and one by scar.

The reputation belonged first to Lady Vess Amaranthe, who sat in the room's only intact chair as though she had brought it with her, which perhaps she had. She wore mourning grey without looking mournful—a tall woman with silver at her temples and hands that stayed perfectly still in her lap, the stillness of someone who had learned that moving hands invited watching eyes. Her son's soul had been bought back from the deep court at a price that had ruined two merchant houses and one barony. She was, as far as Freya could determine, the only person alive who had ever made the mountain give something back.

The second reputation was Sable Quill—or just Sable, since no one used surnames in rooms like this—who ran the lock-and-key trade on the east side and was rumored to have picked the Cardinal's confessional while the Cardinal was still in it. Sable was small, quick-eyed, and currently eating an apple with the concentration of someone who did not trust food they hadn't prepared themselves.

The scar belonged to a broad, gray-bearded man called Harrow, who had been a mine captain before he'd been a thief, before the mountain took his crew and gave back nothing but silence and a widow's pension fund he'd stolen to build. He sat against the wall with his arms crossed, as if the room owed him money.

And in the corner, half in shadow, a fourth figure: a thin young man with ink-stained fingers who introduced himself only as Tally, and who, Freya understood within moments, was there because someone needed to write things down. Every conspiracy, however criminal, apparently required its clerk. Freya felt an odd kinship and an immediate distrust.

"You brought them," Vess said. Not a question. Her voice was low and dry, like paper turning.

"I said I would."

"Show me."

Freya unpicked the stitches in his coat lining with fingers that did not shake, though he noted distantly that they wanted to. He laid the oilcloth packet on the table between them and unwrapped it the way one unwraps a wound.

Sable put down the apple. Even Harrow leaned forward.

The pages caught the lamplight—thirty years of ledger entries in Corvane's precise, unhurried hand. Dates. Names reduced to lot numbers. Prices paid in currencies most people didn't know existed: a decade of someone's courage; the memory of a mother's face; a soul entire, sold, *payable beneath the mountain.*

"How many?" Harrow asked. His voice sounded like gravel settling.

"Forty-one that I've found," Freya said. "There may be more. The early ones are in a cipher I haven't broken yet."

"Forty-one souls." Sable turned a page with one finger, not quite touching the ink. "And the Ember's been feeding them to the court for thirty years, and nobody noticed."

"Somebody noticed," Vess said quietly. "I did. When they took my son." She looked up at Freya, and her eyes were the color of a winter sky over ice—beautiful, and nothing you'd want to fall through. "But noticing is not the same as proving, Master Reyes. And proving is not the same as stopping. The mountain does not accept petitions. It accepts payment."

"Then we don't petition," Freya said. "We steal."

* * *

For a long moment, nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, in the dead shop, a shutter creaked in wind that may or may not have been wind.

Harrow laughed first—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it. "Steal from the deep court. That's what you're proposing. Walk into the mountain that has eaten forty-one souls and pick its pocket."

"Not its pocket," Freya said. "Its ledgers."

That got their attention. Thieves understood ledgers. A ledger was a claim, and a claim was a chain, and every thief in the city had built a life on the knowledge that chains could be broken if you found the right link.

"The sale of the child—of Ash—is recorded in the court's own books," Freya went on. "Corvane sold him. Lawfully, by their accounting. But here's what I've learned reading thirty years of my guildmaster's handwriting: the court is bureaucratic. Gilded. Patient. They keep accounts the way a miser keeps coins—counted, weighed, sealed. And accounts can be disputed."

"On what grounds?" Vess asked. "You don't dispute the mountain. People have tried. There's a chapel full of candles for them."

"On the grounds that Corvane sold what he never owned." Freya tapped the page. "Look at this entry. Look at the seller's mark. This is the Ember's seal—but read the attestation beneath it. *Seller warrants clear title.* Clear title, Lady Vess. Which means the court requires the seller to actually possess what they sell. And Corvane never possessed Ash's soul. It was sold before he ever touched the boy—sold by Ash's own village, a generation of debt stacked on a generation of debt. Corvane bought a claim that was already encumbered and resold it as clean. That's fraud. That's fraud by the court's own rules."

"And you know their rules?" Sable said, one eyebrow arching.

"I know ledgers," Freya said. "All ledgers are the same animal. They want to balance. Give me access to their books and I'll find the imbalance."

Silence again, but a different quality of it now. Freya had watched this happen a hundred times in the guild's counting room: the moment when numbers stopped being numbers and started being a door.

"And your proof," Vess said slowly, "is pages you stole from your own guildmaster. Pages whose existence you cannot explain without confessing your own theft. Pages that, if this fails, hang every person in this room."

"Yes," Freya said.

"You understand," she continued, and her voice did not rise at all, which somehow made it worse, "that mutual destruction is the only honest foundation for an alliance among thieves. You are offering us yours. I want you to understand that we will use it."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

It went on until well past midnight, and what it became, piece by piece, was this:

Vess would provide the way in. Her son's rescue had cost her more than money—the court had required a bond, a memory given as surety, and inside that memory lived the road beneath the mountain: an abandoned mine shaft on the north face, the descent road the court had used when it still bothered to walk among men. She could lead them partway. Only partway; the bond blurred past a certain depth, like a painting left out in rain.

Harrow would get them down the shaft. He had mined these hills for twenty years before the mountain ate his crew, and he knew the old workings the way a priest knows scripture—including which tunnels the court had sealed, and which seals were newer than they pretended to be.

Sable would handle everything that locked. "Everything locks," she said, with the serenity of a woman stating the weather. "Doors. Chains. Oaths. Especially oaths. An oath is just a promise with a padlock on it, and I've never met a padlock I respected."

And Tally would record everything—every agreement, every threat, every price—in a book that would be copied four ways and hidden in four places, so that if any one of them betrayed the rest, the others could burn him with the truth of it.

"That's the real contract," Sable said, nodding at Tally's book. "Not whatever pretty words we say tonight. The book. We each hold a copy of each other's sins. That's trust, thief-style."

"Blackmail," Harrow corrected.

"Trust," Sable repeated, "thief-style."

Freya looked around the lamplit room—at the noblewoman spending her grief like coin, the miner spending his dead, the lockpick spending her freedom, the clerk spending his anonymity—and felt something twist in his chest that was not quite hope and not quite shame. It was the feeling of a ledger balancing, he realized. Everything entered. Nothing forgiven. All accounts open.

It was, he thought, the most honest family he'd ever been offered.

"One more thing," Vess said, as the copies were being made. She drew Freya aside, toward the dead hearth, out of the others' hearing. Up close he could see the fine cracks in her composure, hairline fractures around her eyes like glaze on old porcelain. "Your guildmaster. Corvane. When this is done—if it is done—he will not simply be exposed. Men like him do not survive exposure. Do you understand what you're setting in motion?"

"He sold forty-one souls."

"He fed a god," she said, "and gods remember their feeders. If the court loses its supply, it will look for another. And if it cannot find one—" she paused, and for the first time her stillness looked less like discipline and more like exhaustion, "—then it will come up. My son is alive because I paid them. I intend to keep paying whatever keeps them below. Your little war risks waking something that has been very politely asleep."

"Then we'd best be quick," Freya said, "and thorough."

She studied him for a long moment. "You remind me of the man who brokered my son's return. He also believed the court's rules would protect him." She turned away. "The court's rules protect the court."

* * *

They dispersed before dawn, one and two at a time, each taking a different door into a different dark. Last terms were set: they would move within two moons, before the thirteenth turn came due and the debt collected itself. Until then, silence. Until then, strangers.

Freya went out through the coal chute and stood a moment in the alley behind Candlewick Row, breathing air that tasted of tallow and river rot, watching his breath cloud in the cold.

Two moons. He thought of Ash asleep in the loft above Maren's kitchen, boots mended, belly full, learning words one at a time as though each one were a gift he was afraid to break. He thought of the sigils the boy drew in spilled flour, and how the ledger had recognized them, and how Corvane had watched the boy across the supper table with the mild, measuring gaze of a man checking on an investment.

He thought about what Vess had said—*gods remember their feeders*—and about what he had not told the others: that the cipher in the oldest pages was beginning to yield, letter by letter, and that the earliest entry, thirty-two years old, bore a name he had hoped never to see in Corvane's hand.

His own father's name.

Freya pulled his collar up and walked home through the sleeping city, carrying the one secret he could not yet put in anyone's book—not even Tally's. Some entries, he was learning, you carried yourself.

Behind him, in the dead man's house, the lamp guttered and went out, and luck closed its hand around what remained.
