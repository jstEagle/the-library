## 12. The Turn of the Tenth Moon

The tenth moon came in thin as a coin worn smooth by a hundred hands, and Maren felt its turn the way she had once felt a labor begin—low and certain, a tightening in the bones before anything showed on the surface. She was salting the morning's porridge when the first of them stopped speaking to her.

It was Doss. Big Doss, who had eaten at her table for eleven years and cried into her shoulder when his sister died of the fever, who took his bread always from the left end of the loaf because he believed the right end was luckier and he would not spend his luck on something so ordinary as breakfast. He came into the kitchen that morning, saw Ash sitting on the flour barrel with his heels drumming against the wood, and turned around and walked out without taking bread at all.

Maren stood with the salt spoon in her hand and watched the door swing shut behind him.

"Well," she said to the empty room. "There it is."

Ash looked up at her. He could speak now—a little, in their tongue, words gathered like a magpie gathers buttons, bright and mismatched. He had begun with *ledger*, which Maren thought was the most damning first word any child had ever spoken in that house, and since then he had added *bread* and *Maren* and *cold* and *again*, this last one deployed whenever Freya read aloud to him by the stove. Now he said nothing. He had learned already, in his few moons among thieves, that silence is also a language, and often the honest one.

"Eat your porridge," Maren told him, and he did, and she salted hers too though she no longer tasted anything.

She had been a midwife for thirty years before Corvane bought out her debts and set her to keeping the Ember's kitchen, and thirty years of catching babies teaches you how to read a room the way other people read weather. The guild was turning. Not all at once—nothing in that house turned all at once but the spits and the seasons—but turning nonetheless, the way a child turns in the womb in the last month, restless and inevitable, everyone in the house able to feel it coming.

Doss went first. Then the Pell brothers, who had never agreed on anything in their lives and chose this to agree upon. Then quiet Sef, who kept the memory-vaults and said perhaps eleven words a year, and whose defection hurt worst of all because Sef had carried Ash up those vault stairs himself on the boy's first night, barefoot and snow-blind, and had not once complained of the weight.

By the time the tenth moon stood full over the chimney pots, nine of the guild's twenty-three had taken Corvane's coin or Corvane's argument—Maren wasn't sure which, and suspected it made no difference, since with Corvane they arrived together, the argument wrapped in the coin like poison wrapped in honeycomb.

"He says the mountain must be fed." Freya told her this at night, in the kitchen, after Ash was asleep in the alcove off the pantry where Maren had made him a bed of quilts that smelled of lavender and tallow and home. Freya sat with his hands wrapped around a cup he wasn't drinking. "That's the speech. That it's mercy. Feed the deep court one soul a decade and it sleeps; starve it and it wakes and takes a city. Thirty years of souls, and he's dressed it as a shepherd's work."

"And folk believe him?"

"Some want to. Believing him means they've been living above a slaughterhouse for years and calling it a guild. Believing me means they helped build the pen." Freya's thumb moved along the cup's rim, back and forth, a habit Maren knew meant he was counting something grim. "Corvane understands people, Maren. It's the only ledger he keeps honestly."

She looked through the pantry door at the small shape under the quilts, the slow rise and fall of his breathing. Three moons left. Nine weeks, if the moon-count held, until the debt came due and the court beneath sent its collectors—not men, the old midwife Wren had told her, not men and not anything that could be argued with, only a courtesy like an opened door, and the boy walking through it because the law of the sale compelled him, body and soul walking together down into the dark.

Over my dead body, Maren had said, and Wren, old and dry as a seed husk, had looked at her with terrible gentleness and said: They'd take that too, dear, and count it a bonus.

"Freya," Maren said. "When it comes to choosing—and it's coming, we both know it's coming—you tell me plain which side you're standing on. I'm too old for riddles and I've raised enough children to know that a man who won't name his side has already picked it."

Freya was quiet a long moment. The fire ticked. Above them, somewhere in the warren of the guildhouse, a door closed softly—someone keeping watch, someone keeping secrets, impossible anymore to say which.

"I stole from the guild," he said at last. "Copied pages. Corvane knows. He offered me partnership anyway." A sound that wanted to be a laugh and didn't get there. "He offered me my own childhood, Maren. The memories I sold when I was young. As payment. If I stand aside."

Maren set down her cup. She crossed the kitchen, and she took the boy-man's face in both her hands the way she had once taken the faces of birthing mothers, when the pain was at its peak and they needed someone to hold still and be held.

"Listen to me, love. I caught four hundred babies and I buried eleven of them, and I'll tell you what I learned from every single one: you don't get to be born twice. What you sold, you sold. Grieving it is honest. Buying it back with a child's soul—" She shook her head slowly. "That's just selling yourself again, only this time you know the price."

Freya closed his eyes. When he opened them, something had settled in them, some stone finding the bottom of a pool.

"Ash stays," he said. "Whatever it costs. Ash stays."

"There," Maren said, and kissed his forehead like he was five years old and feverish. "Now I know which side of the bed the sun rises on. Go to sleep. Tomorrow I've got to teach a boy to hide."

* * *

Sef came to her on the last night of the tenth moon, and he did not knock, because Sef had never knocked in his life; he simply appeared in doorways the way dust appears in light.

"They're coming for him," Sef said. Eleven words, spent at once, like a man emptying his purse. "Not the mountain. Them. Corvane's lot. Before the turn completes. They mean to deliver him themselves, walk him down the descent road, collect the finder's fee."

Maren's hands went still in the bread dough. "How many?"

"Four. Maybe six. Doss will lead them. He thinks it's mercy too." Sef's face did something complicated and painful. "I sided wrong, Maren. Or I stood nowhere, which is the same thing but slower. Let me spend what's left of me right."

She wiped her hands and looked at him—old Sef, gray as ash, keeper of the vaults, a man who had spent forty years among bottled lives and had somehow kept his own unspilled.

"The vaults," she said. "Can you hide him there?"

"No one hides in the vaults. Nothing hides in the vaults. That's the point of them." His mouth twitched, the ghost of a joke forty years dead. "But a life stored properly reads as inventory. Catalogued, sealed, shelved. Corvane can walk those rows till doomsday and see nothing but the guild's property." He paused. "Which is the trick of it, isn't it. We make the boy into property to save him from being treated as one."

"It's a lie," Maren said.

"It's a cellar," Sef said. "Lies are what we keep in them. Come."

They woke Ash gently, and Maren packed him a satchel while Sef doused the kitchen fire, and the boy—sharp-eyed now, quick, having learned in four moons what took other children years, that adults' soft voices at night are the truest alarm bells—asked no questions. He only took Maren's hand as they went up the back stairs, his small fingers laced through hers, gripping hard.

The memory-vaults lived beneath the guildhouse proper, deeper than the cellars, cut into the old foundation rock that predated the city and possibly the kingdom. To reach them you passed three locked doors, each keyed to Sef alone, and then a corridor where the air changed—the way air changes near a church, or a grave, thickened with attention. Maren had delivered babies in plague houses and famine winters and once, memorably, in a burning barn, and still her skin crawled going down those stairs.

The vault itself was a long gallery hewn from black stone, and it was full of shelves.

Floor to ceiling, row upon row receding into dark, and every shelf crowded with vessels: glass bottles stoppered with wax, lead-sealed jars, slender vials wrapped in silk, boxes banded with iron. And they were not silent. That was the thing no one warned you about, the thing the guild paid Sef to endure. Stored lives whisper. A stolen year murmurs in its bottle like wind in a chimney. Bottled grief weeps audibly if you pass too close, a fine continuous dripping that leaves no water. Memories pried from sleeping minds talk to each other in the dark, low and ceaseless, in voices that are almost familiar, almost yours.

Ash stopped on the threshold. His grip on Maren's hand tightened until it hurt.

Around him the whispering rose—or seemed to rise, or perhaps the boy simply heard it better than anyone ever had. A thousand small voices, layered like sediment: a woman laughing at something unseen, a man repeating a stranger's name over and over with increasing despair, a child counting, counting, always counting. Somewhere a bottle of stolen courage rattled faintly on its shelf like a trapped moth. Somewhere else something older than the rest sang three notes over and over, in a language that made Ash go rigid from heel to crown.

"That song," Maren whispered. "Do you know it?"

Ash nodded. Then, in his careful, button-bright way with their tongue, he answered: "Before. Home-before. Under." He pointed down, past the floor, past the rock, down and down toward the mountain's roots. Then he touched his own chest.

Maren knelt and took him by the shoulders. "Listen to me, little ember. You're going to stay here a few nights, maybe more. Sef will bring you food. I'll come when I can—I'll find a way, I always find a way, I found ways before you were born and I'll find them after. These whispers, they're just old sorrows with nowhere to go. They can't hurt you. But you—" she tapped his chest, right over the heart, where Wren said the soul sits like a coal in a banked fire— "you keep yourself quiet. Can you do that? Keep your flame low. Low and quiet, like embers under ash."

At the word *ash* the boy almost smiled. Almost.

"Low," he repeated. "Quiet."

Sef showed him a cot in a niche behind the oldest shelf-row, showed him the water jug, showed him which bottles never to touch (all of them, effectively, but Sef marked six with red string and said *these especially*, with the gravity of a man handing down scripture). Then the old man did something Maren had never seen him do. He put his hand flat on Ash's head, briefly, the way you'd steady a candle in wind, and said: "I kept these things safe for forty years. I'll keep you safer."

Then he walked Maren out, and locked the three doors behind them, and stood in the corridor with his keys hanging from his fist like a man holding a heart on a string.

"Doss comes Thursday," Sef said. "Market day. House is half-empty. They'll search everywhere but here, because here is mine, and even Corvane trusts my silence—it's the only thing I've ever sold him." He looked back at the locked doors. "Forty years I told myself I was a librarian. Keeper of things, not taker of them." His voice dropped to gravel. "I shelved a child tonight, Maren. Whatever happens Thursday, I needed you to hear me say it plainly. I shelved a child."

"You saved one," Maren said. "The shelving's just the shape of the saving. Most saving is. Ask any mother."

Sef nodded once and went, and his footsteps faded, and Maren climbed back up through the sleeping guildhouse past doors behind which her family of twenty years lay dreaming—some of them soon to be enemies, all of them, even now, still hers—and she understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that this was what Corvane had truly done, worse than the souls, worse than the ledgers. He had taught them all to shelve each other. To catalogue, seal, and store away whatever was inconvenient, whatever was costly, whatever loved them back.

Well. Two could play at inventory.

Thursday was two days off. Maren went down to the kitchen, built up the fire, and began baking—for market day, for the whole house, for Doss most of all. If treachery was coming through her door, it would come fed and floured and soft-bellied, and it would find her standing in front of the oven with her sleeves rolled, and it would have to look her in the eye.

And downstairs, in the dark beneath the dark, a thousand stolen lives whispered around a living boy, and one voice among them—the old one, the singing one, three notes over and over—began, very softly, to sing in harmony.
