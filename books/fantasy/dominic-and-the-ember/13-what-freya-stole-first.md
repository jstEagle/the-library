## 13. What Freya Stole First

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width of tallow when Freya finally set down the quill.

Three nights he had worked in the ledger room after the house slept, copying page after page into a cheap notebook bought from a stationer who never asked questions. Three nights of Corvane's elegant hand sliding past under his eyes—dates, sums, the small cold notation *one soul, sold* repeated like a refrain across thirty years of accounting. He had thought that by now the horror would have gone dull with repetition. It had not. Each entry landed fresh, like a stone dropped into still water.

But it was not Corvane's hand that kept him awake tonight. It was his own.

He had found it an hour past midnight, tucked between a record of bottled grief sold to a widow in Marrowgate and an inventory of seven stolen years belonging to a fisherman who had drowned the following spring. A single entry, dated twenty-two years ago, written in a boyish, careful script he did not recognize until he saw the signature line.

*F. Reyes. One lot: childhood memories, miscellaneous, quality unassessed. Received in full payment against debts outstanding.*

His own name. His own handwriting—or close enough to it that there was no pretending otherwise. He had been eleven years old when he signed that line. Eleven, and he could not remember doing it.

Freya sat very still in the pool of candlelight and listened to the guild breathe around him. Somewhere above, Maren's cat turned over on a windowsill. Somewhere below, Ash slept in the narrow bed beside Maren's room, breathing the slow breath of a child who did not yet know what the ledgers said about him. And here in the ledger room, in the oldest book on the lowest shelf, Freya Reyes had recorded the first theft he had ever committed—the theft of himself—and felt nothing at all when he read it back.

That was the frightening part. Not the entry. The nothing.

* * *

He remembered his father's hands. That much survived, though whether memory or invention he could no longer say. Big hands, knuckles scarred, always smelling faintly of tar and copper coin. His father had been a salvage-man on the river docks, hauling drowned cargo out of the brown water and selling it to whoever didn't ask where it came from. A thief before thieves had a guild to teach them how.

Freya had been small then. Small and quick, the kind of child who could slip through a warehouse window left open a finger's width, who could hold a lie in his mouth without it souring. His father had seen the gift early and spent it freely, sending him through windows and over fences, keeping half the take and drinking most of the rest.

The debts had come the winter Freya turned eleven. He knew this only because the ledgers told him so—he had checked, years ago, tracing the family name through the Ember's records the way you might probe a tooth that ached. His father had borrowed against everything: the boat, the tools, the rooms they slept in. And when all of that was gone, someone—a broker with a soft voice, the records implied—had told him about a different kind of collateral.

They had brought the boy to a door that wasn't there.

That was the part Freya could almost feel, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing limb. A door in a wall with no door in it, somewhere off Coopers' Row, and a knock made in a rhythm he must once have known by heart—three, pause, two—and a woman with grey eyes opening onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of ink and beeswax. He could not see her face. Twenty-two years, and every face from before the sale was a smudge, a portrait left out in the rain. But the lamplight he could almost see. The warmth. The sense of having arrived somewhere that, for one terrible moment, had felt like safety.

And then—

Nothing. A clean cut, stitched closed, no scar he could find. Whatever had happened in that room between the moment he signed and the moment he walked out again belonged to the Ember now. It sat in their vaults, perhaps, in one of the long drawers where memories were kept in wax-sealed glass, labeled in his own careful hand: *childhood, miscellaneous.* Miscellaneous. As if the whole of being eleven years old were a drawer of unmatched buttons.

He had gone back once, early in his apprenticeship, and asked the vault-keeper—an old man named Hollis, long dead now—whether a person could buy back what they'd sold. Hollis had looked at him with something like pity and said, "Everything in this house is for sale, lad. The question is always the price." Freya had never asked again. The price of a childhood, he suspected, was more than a lifetime of thieving could raise, and besides—what would he do with it? Grief keeps badly. It curdles.

Except that tonight, sitting alone with the candle guttering, Freya understood for the first time that the grief had not been sold away cleanly. Something of it remained, the way smoke remains in a coat. He could not remember why he had wept—but he knew that he had wept. There was a hollow place behind his breastbone shaped exactly like crying, worn smooth as a river stone by some old and repeated sorrow, and no amount of competence or craft had ever filled it.

He had told himself for years that he felt nothing because he was disciplined. Because the code demanded detachment—you steal the thing, you log the thing, you do not wonder whose life you are holding in your hands. He had recited the code to apprentices with real conviction. *We are invisible. We are impartial. What passes through our hands does not pass through our hearts.*

Now, reading his own name in the debt column, he understood the truth: he felt nothing because the feeling had been taken. They had weighed him at eleven years old, found him wanting, and purchased the part of him that would have objected. Every memory he had catalogued since with steady hands and an empty chest—that emptiness was not discipline. It was inventory. He was a man walking around with a hole in him shaped like himself, and the guild had cut the hole, and charged his father for the privilege.

* * *

Sleep would not come, so he climbed instead.

The Ember's safehouse had been a merchant's townhouse once, before the street sank and the merchants moved uphill, and its attic ran the length of the building under rafters furred with dust. Freya went up the hidden stair on legs that moved without consulting him and stood at the round window that looked out over the sleeping city, over the dark seam of the river, over the far black shoulder of the mountain where Ash's soul was filed like an asset in a court that gilded its rot.

Snow was falling again. It fell the way it had fallen the night Ash arrived—softly, indifferently, covering everything equally, the way the ledgers covered everything equally. A stolen spoon. A stolen year. A stolen soul. All entries in the same book, all weighed on the same scale, and the scale did not care. That was the genius of the Ember, Freya thought, and the evil of it too: it had made theft boring. It had taught a whole city to stop flinching.

He pressed his forehead against the cold glass and let the memories come—not the sold ones, but the ones that remained, the scraps too worthless to purchase. Learning to pick a lock at nine with a bent nail while his father watched and laughed. The taste of river water. The specific grey of the sky over the docks in November. Small change. The coins left in his pocket after the brokers took the treasure.

And threaded through them, faint as watermark, the feeling he could not source: the shape of weeping. He tried to follow it the way he'd follow a buyer's mark through three false names, patient, methodical, letting the trail narrow. Why had the boy wept? Before the signing or after? In fear, or in relief? Had his father held him? Had anyone?

There was nothing. Only the shape.

Freya realized he was gripping the windowsill hard enough to ache, and made himself let go, finger by finger.

This was what Corvane had done to dozens of families, he thought. This was what sat behind those elegant entries in thirty years of books: children grown into adults with holes where their histories should be, parents grown old wondering why their sons and daughters looked at them like strangers, whole households amputated and invoiced. And Corvane called it mercy. *The court must be fed or it rises.* Perhaps that was even true. Freya had spent three nights proving Corvane a monster and had not yet needed to lie to himself about the alternative. The mountain was hungry. Someone had fed it for thirty years, politely, in installments.

But hunger explained the trade. It did not explain the boy in the ledger room, asleep upstairs, who had walked out of the snow speaking a language older than kingdoms because his village had traded its children for a winter that never ended. It did not explain the notation in the margin of the oldest book, in Corvane's hand: *asset recovered, re-listed.* Recovered. As if Ash were a spoon that had wandered out of the drawer.

Freya had spent his life believing that what passed through his hands did not pass through his heart. Standing in the dark attic with snow falling on the mountain, he finally understood that his heart had been the first thing to pass through his hands, and that everything since—all the flawless cataloguing, all the immaculate invisibility—had been the sound of a man pretending the wound was a talent.

No more. Tomorrow the parliament of thieves would meet in the salt cellar under the Tanner's Bridge, and Lady Vess would come with her ruinously expensive hope, and they would plan the descent into the mountain's throat. Freya would bring the copied pages. He would bring the proof. And he would carry with him, folded small behind his ribs, the entry in his own childish hand—not as evidence, but as a reason.

One soul, sold. He knew now exactly what that cost, because he had paid it himself, at eleven, in the only currency he had.

He was not going to let them weigh Ash on that scale. Whatever it took. Whatever it broke—including, if it came to it, the man he had been for twenty-two years, the competent hollow thing the guild had made of him. Let that go into the fire too, if fire was what it wanted.

Below him, faint through the floors, he heard a sound that stopped his breath: a child's voice, half-asleep, murmuring in the old language. Then Maren's low reply, soothing, wordless. Then quiet.

Freya stayed at the window a long time, watching snow bury the city, and in his mind he composed the sentence he would speak tomorrow when the thieves asked him why. He would not mention justice. He would not mention the court beneath the mountain, or the moon counting down its thirteen turns, or Corvane's reasonable, rotting face.

He would say only this: "Because I know what the receipt looks like. I've been carrying mine since I was eleven years old."

And then he would show them the way down.
