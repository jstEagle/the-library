## 14. The Descent Road

The mine had three names, which was how Freya knew it had been abandoned a long time. The carters called it the Old Throat. The miners who had once worked it called it Saint Verity's Cut, though no saint had ever blessed anything that came out of it. And in the ledgers of the Ember—the old ones, the ones Freya had copied by candlelight with his hands shaking—it appeared only as *the approach*, underlined twice, as if some clerk thirty years dead had wanted to make certain no one skimmed past it.

They gathered at the minehead two hours before dawn, on the last night of the tenth moon's turn, and Freya counted them the way he counted everything: like entries awaiting an audit.

Lady Vess stood at the mouth of the cut with a lantern held high, her face lit from below so that she looked like a woman carved for a cathedral's doom-lintel—noble, grief-stricken, immovable. Beside her was her man-at-arms, Halloway, a broad silent creature who had not spoken since Freya met him and whom Freya trusted precisely to the degree that he distrusted everyone else. The thieves' parliament had sent two: Sef, a wiry lockwoman with burn-scarred fingers, and Odo, whose smile never reached his eyes and whose debts were famous enough that Freya had personally recorded two of them in the Ember's books. And there was Maren, who should not have come, who had been told not to come, who had looked at Freya with the flat patience of a woman who had raised children through plague winters and said, *You'll want someone along who knows how to keep a body alive when the mountain tries to stop it breathing.* He had not argued. He was learning, slowly and against his nature, that some arguments were lost before they began.

And there was Ash.

That had not been negotiable at all. That had been the whole point.

* * *

Vess unrolled her map on a crate of rusted pit-props, weighting the corners with stones, and Freya watched her hands as she did it. They were steady. He knew what that steadiness had cost. Her knowledge of the descent road came from her son—the soul she had bought back from the deep court at a price that had hollowed out her house and left her title a shell around a grieving woman. The boy had been beneath the mountain for six years before she clawed him home. Whatever he remembered, he remembered in fragments, and Vess had spent two years stitching those fragments into a map, the way one might reassemble a shattered vase and learn its shape by its missing pieces.

"The road goes down in threes," she said. "Three galleries, then a gate. Three gates, then the threshold hall. My son drew me this much." Her finger traced the ink. "Past the threshold hall, nothing. He would not speak of it. He wept when I asked, and I stopped asking."

"And the gates?" Sef asked. She crouched over the map with a thief's economy of movement, cataloguing exits that weren't drawn. "Locked? Warded? Guarded?"

"Polite," Vess said, and something in her voice made them all go still. "That is what you must understand about the court below. Nothing down there is locked. Nothing is guarded. Everything is *invited*. The first gate opens when you knock. The second asks your name and your business. The third—" she paused, and Freya saw her hand tighten on the lantern until the light shivered— "the third asks what you have brought."

"An offering?" Maren asked.

"A payment," Vess corrected softly. "Or a claim. They are a court, Mistress Maren. Courts run on claims. We are going down there to file one."

Freya felt the weight of his satchel against his hip, where the copied pages rode wrapped in oilcloth. Forty-one souls sold across thirty years, each entry in Corvane's own meticulous hand. One of those pages bore Ash's name—or rather, bore the sigil-language that translated, when Vess's son's fragments were laid against it, to *one child, mute, sold by his village, purchased for the winter court, payable upon the thirteenth turn of the moon.*

Two turns remained.

"We move quietly and quickly," Freya said, and heard the guildmaster's cadence in his own voice and hated it. He pushed past it. "No fires beyond what the lanterns allow. No names spoken aloud below the second gate. And no one touches anything. Down there, everything is either property or evidence, and I'd rather we didn't become the former."

Odo smiled his smile. "Spoken like a ledger-keeper."

"I am a ledger-keeper," Freya said. "It's why you're all still alive. Let's keep it that way."

* * *

The first gallery was wide enough for four abreast, and the rails of the old ore-carts still ran down its center, furred with a moss that glowed faintly green when their boots disturbed it. Freya found the glow beautiful and then hated himself for finding it beautiful; beauty down here, Vess had warned, was bait. The mountain court dressed itself in loveliness the way a fisher dressed a hook.

Ash walked beside him, small hand hooked into the strap of Freya's satchel—not holding Freya's hand, because Ash had decided somewhere along the road that he was too old for that, but holding something attached to Freya, which amounted to the same thing. The boy had not spoken since they entered the cut. His silence now was different from the silence he'd arrived in, barefoot in the snow. Then, his muteness had been an absence. Now it was a held breath.

"You know this place," Freya said quietly. Not a question.

Ash nodded. He lifted his free hand and made the gesture he used for the old language's most terrible word—the one that meant, as near as anyone could render it, *owed*. Then he pointed forward, down into the dark where the rails converged.

"Yes," Freya said. "We're going to pay it. But not the way they think."

Ash looked up at him then, and in the green-glowing dark his eyes caught the light strangely, the way a cat's do, and Freya thought—not for the first time, and with more force each time—that whatever the village had sold, they had sold something vast and living, and the mountain had been keeping it in a child-sized jar for thirteen years.

Maren caught up on Ash's other side. "How is he?"

"He remembers the road."

"That's not what I asked."

Freya exhaled. "I don't know how he is. I don't know how to ask it in any language either of us owns." He glanced at her. "You shouldn't have come, Maren."

"Mm." She adjusted the pack on her shoulders, which contained more poultices and bone-splints than any sensible person carried into a mine. "You told Corvane the same thing once, I expect. About things you shouldn't have done. How did that go?"

He had no answer, and she hadn't expected one. That was the trouble with Maren: she armed her silences better than most people armed their knives.

* * *

The first gate was exactly as Vess had promised: unlocked, unguarded, and waiting.

It stood across the gallery where the tunnel narrowed—a door of pale wood, silvered with age, set into the rock as if the rock had grown around it deliberately, a jaw fitted with a single tooth. There was no handle. There was a knocker of black iron shaped like an open hand, palm up.

"It wants a knock," Vess said. "My son was very clear. It opens for anyone who knocks. That's not mercy. That's bookkeeping. Everyone who enters is entered."

"Then we enter together," Freya said, "and we leave together, or not at all." He raised his fist—and hesitated, because the thief in him, the part trained since boyhood to read every lock as a question, saw clearly that this was no lock at all. A lock resists. This thing *welcomed*, and welcome was infinitely harder to defeat.

He knocked. Once.

The sound went down into the mountain like a stone dropped into a well, and kept falling far longer than the depth of the passage could account for. Then the door swung inward without a whisper of hinges, revealing stairs cut in smooth descending spirals, and air rose up to meet them that smelled of honey and cold iron.

"Everyone breathe normally," Maren murmured. "Air that smells that good is trying to sell you something."

* * *

Between the first and second gates, Odo began to talk.

He was good at it—Freya had to grant him that. He talked the way water finds cracks. Little observations, dropped casually: how far the stairs went, how the glowing moss thickened, how Lady Vess seemed to know the way awfully well for someone whose knowledge came secondhand from a traumatized child. He said nothing actionable. He said nothing treasonous. He simply seeded doubt, grain by grain, the way a farmer seeds a field, and Freya listened with the back of his neck prickling because he had spent twenty years reading ledgers and he knew what a falsified column looked like. It looked exactly like this: plausible, tidy, and wrong in a way you couldn't prove until the totals failed.

At the rest halt, while Sef checked her tools and Halloway stood watch with the patience of furniture, Odo drifted close to Freya and lowered his voice.

"Questions, keeper. Just questions." His smile was warm as a coin left in a beggar's cup. "Vess bought her son back, yes? Paid ruinously, the story goes. So the court let one soul go—for a price. Now we march down expecting them to void forty-one sales outright, including the one they've been waiting thirteen years to collect. You've read the ledgers closer than anyone. Does that sound like how a creditor behaves?"

"It sounds like how a fraud behaves," Freya said evenly. "Corvane sold what he never owned. The court's whole power rests on clean accounts. We give them dirty ones."

"Or," Odo said pleasantly, "we give them an excuse to take all of us, and the parliament loses five members and gains nothing but a story." He clapped Freya's shoulder. "I'm sure it'll be fine. You seem very sure, and sure men are always right."

He walked away whistling, softly, a carter's tune, and Freya stood in the moss-light doing arithmetic he didn't like. Odo's debts. Odo's debts were famous. And who, in the Ember's books, held the paper on half of them?

Corvane.

Freya closed his eyes. *He knows about the copied pages,* Corvane had said, in that reasonable voice, offering partnership like a man offering a seat by his fire. *Did you imagine I wouldn't?* And Freya had walked out, and had gone to the parliament, and had built his conspiracy—and had never once asked himself whether the guildmaster had built it first, seeding it with a debtor, letting the conspiracy walk itself down into the mountain carrying its evidence straight to the court that wanted it.

*No,* said another voice in him, colder and older, the voice that had survived his father's creditors and the Ember's initiation both. *If Corvane wanted the pages, he'd have taken them off your corpse weeks ago. This is something else. Think.*

But there wasn't time to think. The second gate was ahead.

* * *

The second gate was a doorway of standing stone, and it asked their names.

Not aloud. As they approached, each of them felt it—a pressure behind the eyes, patient as a clerk waiting for a signature—and one by one, answers surfaced. Sef gave hers flatly. Halloway spoke for the first time in Freya's presence, a single word, his name, in a voice like a door closing. Vess gave hers with a curtsy that managed to be both sincere and contemptuous. Maren gave hers and added, defiantly, her mother's name too, as if daring the stone to object.

Ash stepped up last. The pressure settled on him, and Freya saw the boy's small hands curl into fists.

Then Ash opened his mouth and spoke—not the trade-tongue, not the fragmentary words Freya had coaxed from him over the months, but the old language, full and fluent and resonant in the stone throat of the world, syllables that made the moss-light gutter and made Halloway take an involuntary step backward. It lasted perhaps four heartbeats.

When he finished, the pressure withdrew, almost gently. Almost respectfully.

"What did you say?" Freya whispered.

Ash looked up at him, and for the first time since the snow, since the door, since everything, the boy smiled—a small, fierce, terrible smile.

Later, at the halt, Ash traced it in the dust with his finger, and Freya copied the shapes into his memory the way he copied everything: *I am not the debt. I am the creditor. Open the books.*

The gate ground open.

* * *

The third gate took their guide.

It happened between one step and the next, on the long stair above the threshold hall, in a stretch of passage where the glowing moss gave out entirely and Vess's lantern was the only light. Later, Freya would replay it a thousand times and never find the moment it began. Odo had fallen back—naturally, casually, the way he did everything—to walk beside Vess. There had been the scrape of a boot. A word from Odo, low and urgent, pitched to carry just to her: *your son—the drawing—he got the third gate wrong, I'm sure of it, look—*

And Vess, who had spent two years rebuilding a shattered vase piece by piece, who had staked everything on the fragments, turned her head. Turned the lantern with it. For one heartbeat the light left the path ahead.

Halloway shouted—but Halloway was ten paces back with the rear, and the shout was already too late. The floor of the stair did not collapse; it *opened*, a section pivoting on some ancient counterweight with a sound like a book closing, and Vess dropped into the dark with the lantern, and the light spun away below them and went out.

What followed was chaos rendered in lantern-shadows. Sef flinging herself flat, hooking her burn-scarred fingers into the gap. Halloway's arms plunging in to the shoulder, finding cloth. Freya hauling at Halloway's belt. And below—below, in the shaft, Vess's voice, not screaming, which was somehow worse: calm, furious, *my arm, mind my arm, pull, damn you, PULL—*

They pulled. The trapdoor section hung open on its counterweight, and Freya could see, in the instant before Sef jammed a pit-prop into the mechanism, that it was designed to reset itself. Designed to swallow one, and wait politely for the next. Bookkeeping.

They got her onto solid stone. Her left arm was broken, badly, mid-shaft, the bone tenting skin, and Maren was already kneeling with splints and that terrifying midwife calm, talking to Vess in a low stream of ordinary words—*there now, grip my wrist, you'll want to bite something, here*—while the noblewoman's face went grey as ash and her breath came in whistles.

Odo stood apart, hands visible, face arranged into horror. "She turned—I called to her about the map and she turned—gods, it was an accident, you all saw it was an accident—"

"I saw," Freya said.

His voice came out level. He was surprised by that. Inside, something had gone very quiet and very bright, the way a blade looks when you finally take it out of its wrapping.

"Sef. Check his wrists."

Sef moved without being told twice, and Odo—quick as he was—wasn't quick enough for a lockwoman at arm's length with suspicion already drawn. From inside his coat she brought out a slip of folded paper, and even by the dimming lantern-light Freya knew the seal before it was fully unfolded. Grey wax. A feather pressed into it. The guildmaster's mark, the one Freya had seen on a thousand routine orders, the one that had signed away forty-one souls in a meticulous hand.

Odo didn't bother denying it. That was the strangest part. He looked at the circle of them—at Sef holding the letter, at Halloway looming, at Maren with blood on her hands from Vess's splint—and he shrugged, almost apologetic.

"He has my debts," Odo said simply. "All of them. Every marker I ever signed. He says the court will honor them as assets, once the collection comes due." He spread his hands. "You'd have done the same. Any of you. Don't look at me like saints—we're thieves. It's the one honest trade there is."

"Where does it say?" Freya asked. "In his letter. What does he tell you to do?"

Odo's eyes flicked—just flicked—toward Ash.

Freya felt the world narrow to a point. "Read it," he said. "Aloud. All of it."

And Odo, because he was a coward but not quite a liar, read it: instructions to separate the child from the party at the third gate. To deliver him alone to the threshold hall, where the court's stewards would receive him. Whereupon the debtor Odo's obligations would be marked *paid in kind*.

Silence, except for Vess's controlled breathing and the drip of water somewhere far below.

"Here's what happens now," Freya said. He heard his own voice as if from a distance—cold, precise, every word an entry in a ledger. "Halloway binds him. We leave him somewhere the court will find him—with the letter pinned to his chest, so they can read whose errand he ran. If Corvane wants to negotiate with the deep court, he can do it himself, in person, on their terms instead of ours." He turned to the others. "And we keep moving. Because we have two turns of the moon, one broken guide, and one map in pieces—and the only person left who carries the rest of the road"—he looked down, and Ash looked up, and the boy's cat-bright eyes held no fear at all— "is the one who lived it."

Ash stepped forward. He tugged Freya's sleeve, and pointed down the dark stair, toward the threshold hall and whatever waited past the place Vess's son would not speak of.

Then he spoke aloud, in the trade-tongue, in a voice rusty and small and absolutely certain, the longest sentence Freya had ever heard him string together:

"I remember the way home. Follow me."
