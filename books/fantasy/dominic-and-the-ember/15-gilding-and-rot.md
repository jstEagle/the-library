## 15. Gilding and Rot

Lady Vess had prepared herself for monsters.

She had dressed for them, in fact—chosen the grey silk deliberately, the color of ash and mourning, because she had assumed the court beneath the mountain would appreciate grief. She had rehearsed her courtesies in the dark of the mine road while Freya Reyes slept sitting up against his pack and the mute child dreamed whatever dreams a sold soul dreams. She had imagined thrones of bone, wardens with iron voices, chains. Thirty years of rumor had furnished her imagination generously: the deep court was a devouring thing, a mouth, a hunger wearing a crown.

What she found instead was a waiting room.

It was, she had to admit, a very fine waiting room.

The corridor opened without warning from the black throat of the abandoned mine into a hall of white marble veined with gold, as though someone had poured sunlight into stone and let it set. Lanterns burned with a light that cast no shadows—or rather, that cast shadows in the wrong directions, each of them leaning politely away from the people they belonged to. The ceiling was painted with a sky: not any sky Vess knew, but an idealized one, cloudless, the blue of a memory no one had actually lived. Benches lined the walls, upholstered in velvet the color of deep water. On each bench sat a small brass plaque engraved with a number.

Vess counted forty-one benches. Forty-one numbers. She did not let herself think about what the numbers might count.

"Your ticket, madam."

The voice came from beside her, though nothing had approached; she simply became aware of a clerk standing at her elbow as if he had always been there, the way one becomes aware of a draft. He was slender and grey-eyed and utterly unremarkable, which she understood at once to be a kind of uniform. His skin was very pale, but not corpse-pale—rather the pale of things kept from daylight on purpose, like mushrooms, like pearls. He held out a small rectangle of hammered gold.

Upon it was stamped: **VESSEL HALLORAN-VESS — ONE (1) VISITOR — PURPOSE: NEGOTIATION — QUEUE POSITION: NINE.**

"I didn't take a ticket," Vess said.

"No one takes a ticket," the clerk agreed pleasantly. "Tickets are issued. Yours was issued the moment you crossed the threshold. It is terribly efficient." He smiled, and the smile reached every part of his face except anywhere that mattered. "Do have a seat. Position nine is quite good, actually. Position twelve has been waiting since before your grandmother's wedding."

Vess looked down the hall. On bench twelve sat a man in the fashion of two generations past, his coat cut long and his boots polished to mirrors, his hands folded in his lap with the patience of furniture. He did not appear to be suffering. That was somehow worse. He appeared to be *comfortable*, the way one is comfortable in a dream one cannot wake from.

"And what," she asked carefully, "is he waiting for?"

"Resolution," said the clerk. "Isn't everyone?"

* * *

She took position nine, because there was nothing else to do and because she suspected that doing nothing else was precisely the point. The bench was warm. She chose not to wonder why.

Freya sat beside her, and the boy—Ash—sat between them with his knees drawn up, watching the painted sky with an expression Vess could not read and did not want to. Since entering the court's halls the child had changed. He walked straighter. He looked at the clerks—not fearfully, but the way a debtor looks at a bank, or perhaps the way a bank looks at a debtor—and once, passing a doorway hung with velvet, he had reached out and touched the fabric with one finger, gently, the way one touches the coffin of someone one loved.

"They're real," Ash had said. It was the first time Vess had heard him speak more than a handful of words in their tongue. His voice was small and level and it had frightened her more than any monster would have. "The chairs are real. The sky isn't. They make you comfortable so you'll forget what you came for. My mother told me. Before."

Before, Vess thought. Before what? Before the sale? Before the village traded its children for a winter that never ended? She had read the pages Freya copied from the Ember's ledgers by lantern-light, over and over, until she knew the entries better than her own marriage lines: *one soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain,* again and again and again, decades of them, in Guildmaster Corvane's neat hand. But somewhere in those same ledgers—one entry only, thirty years old—was the record of a soul bought *back*. Her son's. Paid for at a price that had hollowed out three generations of Halloran wealth and left her a rich woman with nothing to be rich *for*.

That was why she was here. Not for passage, exactly, though passage was what she had promised the conspiracy. For the proof. Somewhere in this beautiful bureaucracy was the file of Tam Vess—the boy whose rescue was the only one on record—and inside that file, if the court kept records the way she suspected it did, was everything: the terms, the seller's mark, the name of the guildmaster who had signed away a child he had no right to sign away. Corvane had fed the mountain for thirty years. Vess intended to hand the mountain its own accounting and watch it eat him instead.

If the mountain could be made to eat anyone. That remained to be seen.

"Position five," announced a second clerk who had not been there a moment before.

On bench five, a woman stood up, smoothed her skirts, and followed the clerk through a door that had not been visible until it opened. Vess noticed that the woman did not look back. She noticed, too, the small golden ticket left behind on the velvet, already tarnishing, as though the metal itself were being spent.

"What happens to them?" she asked quietly.

"That depends on their business," said the first clerk, materializing again with a tray of something steaming. Tea. He offered it around; Freya took a cup with the wariness of a man who trusted no beverage he hadn't stolen himself; Vess declined. "Some come to sell. Some come to redeem. Some come to dispute. Disputes are my favorite." His grey eyes brightened with something almost human. "So rare. So *ambitious*. Everyone else accepts that accounts must be settled. A disputant believes accounts can be *wrong*."

"Can they?" Freya asked.

"Oh, certainly," the clerk said cheerfully. "Frequently, even. Fraud is a perennial problem. Sellers misrepresent their goods. Sellers sell what was never theirs to sell." He sipped his own tea. "Of course, the court does not concern itself with the *morality* of a transaction. Only its validity. Morality is upstairs. We deal in validity." He paused, and added, with the air of a man imparting a great courtesy, "You may find that distinction useful, madam. You strike me as a disputant."

Vess met his eyes. "And what strikes you about my boy?"

The clerk looked at Ash for a long moment. Something moved behind the grey eyes—recognition, appetite, or merely filing—and then was filed away.

"The child is not on my ledger today," he said. "Which means he is either already accounted for, or not yet due. Either way—" another smile, perfect and empty, "—welcome to the Court Beneath. Position four will be called shortly. Do finish your tea. It helps with the waiting. The waiting is designed to help with everything else."

* * *

Position four took six hours.

Vess learned the waiting room the way she had once learned the etiquette of the capital, by observation and ruthless inference. She learned that the tea never cooled. She learned that the benches were numbered in order of *debt*, not arrival—the plaque on her own bench had quietly re-engraved itself sometime in the third hour, and now read QUEUE POSITION: FOUR, and beneath it, in smaller letters she was certain had not been there before: **ASSETS DECLARED: ONE ESTATE, THREE HOUSES, ONE (1) REDEEMED SOUL (REDEEMED, NOT RETURNED).** She learned that the painted sky changed when no one watched it: clouds formed and dissolved in patterns too slow and too deliberate to be weather, and once—only once—she could have sworn the clouds arranged themselves into a ledger line, columns and sums, before relaxing back into blue.

She learned, above all, that the court was not hungry the way rumors said. Hunger was animal. This was worse. This was *appetite with paperwork*.

When the door finally opened for them, it opened onto a gallery.

The auditor's hall was a colonnade of gold-shot marble a quarter mile long, and along both walls, from floor to vaulted ceiling, stretched shelves. Not bookshelves—though there were books, thousands of them, chained and labeled—but niches, small alcoves of glass and gilt, and in each niche something glowed. Some glowed white. Some glowed the warm gold of candlelight through honey. One or two flickered a sullen red. And between the shelves walked clerks with ladders and long silver tongs, lifting the glowing things down, examining them against little brass instruments, recording figures in books, and setting them back—all with the unhurried tenderness of gardeners turning bulbs.

Souls. Filed like assets. Audited like assets.

Ash stopped walking. Freya put a hand on his shoulder and the boy shook it off and stood there in the middle of that gorgeous colonnade, staring up at a niche near the ceiling where something glowed the particular green-gold of new leaves, and his face did something terrible and quiet.

"That one's from my village," he said. No one had told him which shelf. No one had told him anything. "That one's Ilset. She used to braid my hair when my mother's hands were full." His voice did not rise. That was the horror of it—it went flatter. "They keep them where they can see them. They keep them *lit*. Like lamps."

"They are well maintained," said their escort—a senior clerk, this one, with silver at his temples and a chain of office made of linked brass tags, hundreds of tiny tickets worn as jewelry. He said it sincerely. That was the worst thing yet: he meant it. "Deterioration is a form of loss, and the court abhors loss. Your friend is in excellent condition. Better than most. Rural souls keep beautifully—less wear, you understand. Fewer compromises." He gestured graciously onward. "The auditor will see you now. Do wipe your feet; the marble remembers."

Vess wiped her feet. She would remember later, with shame, that she wiped her feet.

* * *

The auditor was not a monster either. That was the final cruelty of the place.

He sat behind a desk of black glass at the end of the colonnade, and he looked like somebody's grandfather—broad, comfortable, silver-bearded, with spectacles perched on his nose and ink-stained fingers and an expression of inexhaustible patience. He could have presided over a village fair. He could have judged preserves at a harvest festival. When they were seated—he gestured to chairs that adjusted themselves to their bodies with a soft sigh of upholstery—he folded his hands and regarded them over the spectacles with genuine, courteous attention.

"Lady Vessel Halloran-Vess," he said. His voice was warm. It was the warmth of a hearth in a house where something is buried under the floorboards. "An honor. Your family has done business with this court before. Excellent record. Full payment, early settlement, no disputes." He turned the great ledger on his desk toward himself. "Until today, I am given to understand. Today you wish to dispute?"

"Not dispute," Vess said. "Amend."

The auditor's eyebrows rose a fraction—an entire conversation in that fraction. "Amend. Do go on."

"You keep meticulous accounts," Vess said. "I have seen your shelves. I know quality when I see it—I married into trade before I inherited title, and I audited my husband's houses myself for eleven years. So I will not waste your time with appeals or weeping. You don't deal in those. I have a proposition concerning an entry in your files: a redemption, some thirty years past. A soul bought back. The only redemption on record from the guild called the Ember."

"Tam Halloran-Vess," the auditor said. No hesitation. He turned to a side table, drew a slim volume from a drawer with two fingers, and opened it to a marked page as though he had been expecting this meeting for years—which, Vess thought coldly, he probably had. "Redeemed at ruinous premium. The court noted the anomaly at the time. Redemptions distort our projections, madam. We do not like anomalies. What of it?"

"Inside that file," Vess said, "is the mark of the seller. The party who sold my son's soul to you. And inside your general ledgers, under that same mark, are dozens of other sales—children, mostly, sold by a man named Corvane, guildmaster of the Ember, who sold souls he had *no right to sell*. Souls entrusted to his guild's care. Souls of children whose families believed the Ember kept them safe." She leaned forward. "You pride yourselves on validity. Then hear me: every one of those transactions is tainted. He sold stolen property. And you—" she let the pause stretch, polite as a drawn blade, "—you are holding stolen goods."

The silence that followed was long and absolutely still. Even the distant clerks among the glowing shelves seemed to move more softly.

Freya, beside her, had gone very carefully blank—the blankness of a thief watching a mark take bait. Ash stared at the auditor with his hands flat on his knees, and Vess saw that the boy was trembling, and that he was holding the trembling the way soldiers hold a wall.

The auditor removed his spectacles. Polished them on his sleeve. Replaced them. All of it unhurried, courteous, dreadful.

"Madam," he said at last, warmly, "do you know what happens to a court that admits its holdings are stolen?"

"I imagine," Vess said, "it returns them."

"It *collapses*," the auditor said, with the mild regret of a man describing weather. "Every asset called into question becomes unsellable, unleverageable, unownable. Our whole economy rests on the assumption that a valid sale is permanent. You are proposing we void sixty years of commerce on the testimony of a grieving mother and a"—his gaze slid, briefly, appraisingly, to Freya—"professional thief. Do you begin to see our difficulty?"

"I begin to see yours," Vess said. "Now let me show you mine." She drew from her breast the copy Freya had made of the Ember's ledgers—the pages she had slept with beneath her pillow since the thieves' parliament, wrapped in oilcloth, worth more to her than her name. She laid it on the black glass desk. "Seller's marks. Dates. Terms. Cross-referenced against your own redemption file, which I am asking you—no, inviting you—to open and compare. If even one entry proves Corvane sold what he never owned, then your shelves are full of frauds. And if you will not look, then every soul down here, every lamp on your lovely walls, is lit by theft you *chose* not to see." She folded her hands, mirroring his. "I'm told the court abhors loss. Consider what a scandal abhors."

For the first time, the auditor's grandfatherly calm flickered—not into anger, but into something far more interesting: *interest*. He looked at the pages. He looked at Ash. And then he did something that made Vess's blood run cold in that warm, shadowless light.

He laughed—a short, genuine, delighted bark, like a merchant finding a coin behind a cushion.

"A dispute after all," he said. "How rare. How *refreshing*." He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself, dipped his pen, and wrote a single line in a hand as neat as Corvane's. "Very well, Lady Vess. The court will convene a Reading of Accounts. You will present your evidence. The seller—if he has the wit to come—will answer. And we shall discover together whether your guildmaster sold what was never his." The pen paused. He looked up, and behind the spectacles his eyes were old, and patient, and hungry in the way of ledgers rather than teeth. "One condition. The child attends. The account in question concerns him most closely, and this court does not transact in the absence of the goods."

Beside her, Ash spoke without trembling now, in his small level voice, in the old language first and then, deliberately, in theirs:

"He is not goods."

The auditor regarded him for a long moment. Then he inclined his head, gracious as a host.

"We shall see," he said, "what the accounts say he is."

Behind them, far down the golden colonnade, among the forty-one glowing niches of a village that sold its winter dearly, one small green-gold light flickered—as if something filed there had heard its name spoken, and remembered it.
