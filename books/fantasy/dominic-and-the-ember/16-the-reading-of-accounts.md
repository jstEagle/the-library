## 16. The Reading of Accounts

The courtroom beneath the mountain was not what Freya had expected, and he had expected a great deal. He had imagined a cavern of teeth and torchlight, a throne of femurs, a judge with too many eyes. What he found instead was a room like a countinghouse, if a countinghouse had been carved by patient hands out of living stone and then upholstered by someone with exquisite taste and no soul at all.

The walls were veined with gold—real gold, or something that behaved like gold, running through the rock in slow bright rivers that moved when you weren't looking at them. The benches were black wood polished to a mirror shine. There were no windows, of course, but the ceiling gave off a soft amber light, even and unblinking, like the glow behind a banker's counter. Everything smelled faintly of ink and warm wax and, beneath it, something older and sweeter that Freya could not name and did not want to.

At the center of the room stood a desk. Not a bench, not a throne—a desk, broad and immaculate, with a brass lamp and a blotter and an inkwell of smoked glass. Behind the desk sat the auditor.

It looked like a man. That was the first horror of it. It had a face like a man's, gray and smooth as paper, with small neat features arranged in an expression of permanent mild attention. It wore robes of deep green, buttoned to the throat, and its hands—long, clean, unhurried—rested on the desk beside a stack of ledgers so tall they cast their own shadow.

"State your name for the record," the auditor said. Its voice was dry and pleasant, the voice of a man who had said the same sentence ten thousand times and had never once grown tired of it.

"Freya," he said. His voice came out smaller than he intended. He cleared his throat. "Freya Reyes. Keeper of ledgers, formerly of the Ember guild."

"Formerly." The auditor made a small mark in a slim volume before it. The scratch of its pen was the only sound in the room, and Freya felt it in his teeth. "And the subject of this reading?"

"Ash." The name still felt new in his mouth, though Maren had given it months ago. "A child. Eight years old, or near enough. Sold to this court by Corvane, guildmaster of the Ember, under a contract dated—" He stopped. Steadied himself. "Under a contract I intend to prove fraudulent."

Something happened to the auditor's face. It was not a smile. It was the place where a smile would go on a face that had forgotten how.

"Fraud," the auditor repeated, with something that might have been appetite. "Do you know how long it has been since anyone stood in this room and used that word?"

"No," Freya admitted.

"Neither do we," said the auditor, and made another note. "That is what records are for. Proceed."

* * *

They had come down through the mountain's throat for six days—through the abandoned mine, past the place where Vess's guide had turned on them and been lost to the dark—and Freya had spent every one of those days rehearsing. In the lamplight of their camp, with Ash asleep against Maren's side and Vess sharpening a knife she had no intention of using, he had rehearsed. He had argued with the dark. He had argued with himself.

But standing here, in the amber hush of the court, he found the argument arranging itself the way a lock arranges itself under a skilled hand. Not because he had forced it. Because it had always had a shape, and he had finally listened for it.

"The contract," Freya said, "purports to convey one soul, held in trust by the Ember guild, sold with full title and right of transfer. Is that the court's understanding of the document?"

The auditor turned a page. The ledger before it was ancient—Freya could see the foxing on the edges, the brown bloom of centuries—and the auditor touched the pages the way a priest touches scripture.

"It is the court's understanding," it said, "that the document is what it says it is. Documents are what they say they are. That is their nature."

"Then your nature is to be deceived," Freya said, "because a document is only what its writer says it is. And the writer of this one had no right to write it."

He heard Vess shift behind him, on the visitors' bench. He did not turn. In the thieves' parliament, weeks ago, she had told him: *They are not monsters down there. That's the trap. Monsters you can fight. Bureaucrats you have to out-file.* He had laughed at the time. He was not laughing now.

"Explain," said the auditor.

"The Ember guild trades in stolen things," Freya said. "That is its trade and its pride. Stolen years. Stolen grief. Memories taken from sleeping minds. Every item in our inventory was taken from someone who did not consent to the taking. That is the whole of our business. I kept the ledgers for eleven years. I know what we sold, and I know how we came by it." He drew a breath. "A soul is not a memory. A soul is not a year. A soul cannot be taken, because it is not property. It is the *holder* of the property—the one to whom every stolen thing, every memory, every grief, every year, ultimately belongs. Corvane sold a soul to this court. But you cannot sell a soul, because a soul is not something a man *has*. It is something a man *is*."

The auditor's pen had stopped moving.

"The contract," Freya pressed on, "says Corvane held full title. Title to what? Title is a claim of ownership, and ownership requires a lawful origin. Show me the lawful origin. Show me the day Ash's soul was lawfully Corvane's. There is no such day. There is no such entry. What there is—" and here Freya reached into his coat, slowly, the way one moves in the presence of a strange dog, "—is this."

He laid the copied pages on the desk. Thirty years of the Ember's secret ledger, in Freya's own careful hand: every soul Corvane had sold, every payment received, every entry marked with the deep court's own sigil. He had copied them in the dead of night, breaking the only rule he had never broken, and the ink of his copies still felt warm, still felt like betrayal. He set them down now and they felt like something else entirely.

"Corvane did not sell one soul," Freya said. "He sold dozens. Over thirty years. And here is the fraud, auditor, and I give it to you plainly: he did not sell them because the court demanded it. He sold them because the court *paid*. He found a buyer with deep pockets and no questions, and he fed it for three decades, and every soul he sold was a soul he never owned, taken from children who never consented, by a man who told himself he was keeping the peace."

The amber light did not flicker. The gold in the walls did not stop its slow crawling. But the silence in the room changed quality—thickened, the way air thickens before a storm.

The auditor looked down at the copied pages for a long time. When it looked up, its small gray face was as mild as ever, and that mildness was now the most frightening thing Freya had ever seen.

"These are copies," the auditor said.

"They are true copies," Freya said. "I made them myself. I am the guild's keeper of ledgers. My hand is known. My hand can be verified against thirty years of original entries."

"Copies," the auditor said again, gently, as if explaining to a child, "are claims. Originals are facts. The court deals in facts." It folded its long hands. "You are asking this court to void a sale on the basis of documents that could have been written last night by anyone with a grudge and a steady hand."

Freya's stomach dropped. He heard Vess's breath catch behind him.

"And yet," the auditor continued, before Freya could speak, "the court is not without curiosity. In thirty years, no one has come before us to contest a sale. Not once. The sellers are always satisfied. The buyers are always satisfied. The sold—" it paused, and for the first time its pen moved without being written with, a small unconscious twitch, "—the sold do not come. They cannot come. They are *here*." It looked at Freya, and its eyes were the color of old paper. "You came. That is without precedent. The court is fond of precedent. It is the only food we trust."

* * *

They adjourned—Freya did not learn until later that the court *could* adjourn, that the word had meaning down here, that the amber light itself dimmed and brightened on a schedule older than any kingdom above—and he was led to a waiting room that was, like everything else in the deep court, almost right and therefore entirely wrong. Comfortable chairs. A fire that burned without wood. A pitcher of water that tasted faintly of iron and, when he wasn't thinking about it, of something sweet.

Vess sat across from him, her hands folded, her face composed the way only a woman who had bought back her own son's soul at ruinous price could compose it.

"It's toying with us," she said quietly.

"No," Freya said. "It's *reading* us. There's a difference. Toying is for creatures. Reading is for accountants." He rubbed his eyes. "It wants the originals. Of course it wants the originals. It's a court of documents. It won't void a sale on copies any more than we'd pay out a claim on a forged note."

"Then we've come all this way to be told the ledger isn't in the room."

"The ledger is never in the room," Freya said. "That's the first thing they teach you. The ledger is the *room*." He looked up. "Corvane has the originals. Thirty years of them, in the vault beneath the Ember, behind a door that opens to my hand and no other. He knows we've come. He's had time to burn them."

Vess was quiet a moment. "Has he?"

Freya thought about it honestly, the way he thought about locks. Corvane was many things—a seller of children, a keeper of comfortable lies—but he was, above all, a man who believed in records. He believed that what was written was true, and that what was true should be written. He had built an empire on ink. Men like that did not burn their ink. Men like that *hid* it, and told themselves they were preserving history.

"No," Freya said slowly. "He hasn't. He can't. Those ledgers are the only proof that he was ever more than a thief. He'll bring them himself, if it comes to that. He'll walk them down the mountain in an iron case and offer them as evidence of his own good faith, because to Corvane the books are innocence itself." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "He'll hand us the knife and ask us to admire the handle."

* * *

They called him back at what might have been dawn, if dawn meant anything a mile beneath the earth. The auditor sat behind its desk with the copied pages squared neatly before it, and beside it now stood a second figure—taller, robed in the same green, its face smooth and featureless as an egg, holding a sealed writ of some kind.

"The court has considered your petition," the auditor said. "The court finds it irregular, unprecedented, and—"

Here it paused, and Freya understood that the pause was the most expensive thing in the room.

"—*interesting*."

The auditor rose. It was not tall, but rising, it seemed to take up more of the room than it had, the way a ledger takes up more of a shelf once you know what's written in it.

"The court agrees to hear evidence," it said. "A formal reading of accounts will be convened. You will produce your originals. The seller will be summoned to answer. The contract will be weighed against the claims of fraud, and the court will rule as the accounts require." Its pen scratched once, twice. "Be advised, Freya Reyes, keeper of ledgers: the court will rule *as the accounts require*. If your originals prove your claim, the sale is void, and the child's soul returns to the child, and the seller answers for thirty years of false conveyance. But if your originals fail—if a single entry is false, a single figure altered, a single line forged to serve your purpose—then the sale stands, and you will answer in the seller's place, for the crime of wasting this court's time is the only crime we punish without mercy."

Freya felt the trap close around him, elegant and invisible, and he almost admired it. His copies were true—every line of them, copied in his own hand with a forger's precision and an honest man's terror. But the originals were a hundred miles above, in a vault behind a door that opened to his hand, in the keeping of a man who had just learned what Freya had stolen.

"I'll produce them," Freya said.

"You have until the moon completes its thirteenth turn," the auditor said. "The debt is already called. The reading will be held on that night, or not at all." It sat, and folded its hands, and its gray face returned to its permanent mild attention. "The court is adjourned. Do not be late, keeper. The court so dislikes waiting."

Freya turned to go. At the door he paused, because there was one thing left, one thing that had been sitting in his chest since they'd entered the mountain, and he was tired of carrying it.

"Auditor," he said. "The other souls. The dozens Corvane sold before Ash. When the sale is voided—what happens to them?"

The auditor looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere in the walls, the gold ran on, patient and bright, carrying its slow cargo through the dark.

"That," it said, "depends entirely on what your ledgers say."

Freya climbed back up toward the light and the cold and the waiting faces of the only family he had left, and he did not tell them, not yet, that the court had given him exactly what he'd asked for: a chance at justice, on the enemy's terms, with a deadline measured in moonlight and a price measured in every entry he had ever written.

He did tell them one thing, that night, over a fire no larger than a cupped hand, while Ash slept and the mountain breathed around them like something dreaming.

"He's coming," Freya said. "Corvane is coming down to answer for it. And when he does, he'll bring the ledgers himself—because to him, the books *are* his innocence." He stared into the flames. "We just have to make sure the books are still telling the truth when he arrives."

Above them, far beyond the stone, the moon turned. Eleven days remained.
