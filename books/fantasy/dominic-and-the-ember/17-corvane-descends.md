## 17. Corvane Descends

The road down was older than the guild.

Corvane had walked it once before, forty years ago, when he was a young man with ink-stained fingers and a master who taught him that everything could be priced if you were brave enough to name the figure aloud. He remembered the way the mountain's throat swallowed sound—the crunch of his boots going thin and far away, as though the dark itself were eating the noise before it reached his ears. He had been afraid then. He had wept in the antechamber of the court, quietly, like a child at a funeral, and the auditor had waited with perfect courtesy until he finished before asking for his signature.

He did not weep now.

Now he carried a lantern of green glass, and behind him came six of his own—Brannic and Sella and the twins, men and women who had eaten at his table for decades and who still believed, because he had told them so and they wanted it to be true, that they were descending to negotiate. He had not told them they were descending to buy back a soul with stolen goods. Some truths were ledger entries; others were simply weight, and you carried them yourself or you broke your people's backs with them.

*You carry too much alone,* Maren had said to him once, years ago, pressing a cup of cider into his hands at some midwinter feast he could no longer clearly picture. *Even a guildmaster is only a man.*

He had liked her then. He wondered, distantly, whether she would be the one to spit on him when this was over, or whether she would simply turn away, which would be worse.

The mine shaft narrowed. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a clock that measured centuries. Corvane counted the drips the way he counted everything—habit older than memory—and by the four-hundredth he felt the change in the air: warmth without source, a sweetness like overripe fruit, and beneath it something mineral and old, the smell of a room that had never known weather.

They were close.

"Master." Brannic's voice, low, at his shoulder. "The mark on the wall. The one Vess described."

Corvane raised the lantern. There it was: a sigil burned into stone, the same sigil that had appeared in his ledgers thirty years ago in an entry he had signed himself, the ink brown now with age. *One soul, sold, payable beneath the mountain.* He had thought, signing it, that he was being clever. That he had found the one buyer in the world who paid in gold that never tarnished and asked questions that never needed answering.

He had been clever for thirty years. Cleverness, he understood now—standing in a dead mine with a lantern and six liars and a debt come due—was just another word for a slow fall you hadn't noticed beginning.

"Keep moving," he said.

* * *

The antechamber had not changed.

Gilded doors tall as cathedral spires, worked with scenes of harvest and accounting—wheat sheaves bound with ledgers' ribbons, scales weighing grapes against coins, a crowned figure seated at a desk, writing. The floor was black marble polished to the depth of standing water. Their reflections looked up at them from below, pale and slightly wrong, as though the floor were showing them the versions of themselves that had made different choices.

Corvane did not look down. He knew what lived under there.

"Wait here," he said to his six.

Sella hesitated. "Master—"

"You heard me." He softened it, because a guildmaster's voice was a tool like any other, and fear was a poor whetstone. "What happens next happens between me and the court. If I'm not out by the time the lantern burns through its oil—" he set the green glass lamp on the floor between them, where its light pooled like something spilled, "—then go home, tell the truth about what I've done, and let the parliament do what parliaments do."

He watched them absorb it. Watched Brannic's jaw tighten. Watched Sella's eyes flick toward the door and back, calculating—not whether to obey, but what obedience would cost her later, when the story was told in taverns. Good. His people were thieves to the marrow. Even their loyalty was appraised.

He left them with the lantern and pushed the doors.

They opened without weight, without sound, onto the court.

* * *

It was beautiful. That was the thing no survivor ever managed to convey properly, the thing that made drunkards in dockside taverns wave their hands and give up: it was beautiful, and it was polite, and those two facts together were the whole of the horror.

The hall stretched farther than the mountain should have allowed. Columns of veined silver rose into a ceiling that might have been night sky or might have been paint; it was impossible to say where one ended. Courtiers moved through the space in gowns and coats of colors that did not exist above ground—colors Corvane had seen only in bottled memories, the shades of dreams people sold to pay debts. They glanced at him as he passed, and each glance was a small, courteous appraisal, the look of a clerk estimating what a stranger might be worth.

And at the end of the hall, at a desk of pale wood, sat the auditor.

She was not what she had been forty years ago—or perhaps she was exactly what she had always been, and shape was simply another entry she amended as needed. Tonight she wore the form of a woman past middle age, gray-streaked hair pinned with quills, spectacles perched low on a nose that had probably never existed. She was writing. She did not stop writing when he approached. She finished her line, blotted it, set down her pen, aligned it precisely with the desk's edge, and looked up.

"Guildmaster Corvane." Her voice was dry paper and warm tea. "You've come early. The account isn't due for another turn and a half."

"The moon lies," Corvane said. "I've read the sky my whole life. It hurries when it wants something."

A smile touched her mouth without arriving anywhere near her eyes. "Sit."

There was a chair opposite the desk. He had not seen it arrive. He sat, and the chair fit him perfectly, which he chose not to think about.

"I'll be direct," he said. "I want to renegotiate."

"There is nothing to renegotiate. A sale was made. The sale stands."

"The sale was fraudulent." He kept his voice level, kept his hands loose on his knees. "That's why I'm here. Not to argue—I know better than to argue with you—but to offer settlement. Full settlement. Before your reading of accounts concludes and makes all this"—he gestured faintly at the impossible hall, the courteous horror of it—"unnecessary."

The auditor folded her hands. Around them, the courtiers had drifted closer, forming a loose crescent at a respectful distance, the way spectators gather at an execution or a wedding. Corvane could not decide which this was. Perhaps the court saw no difference.

"Settlement," the auditor repeated. "In what?"

"In something better than coin." And here it was—the moment forty years of careful bookkeeping had been building toward, whether he'd known it or not. He reached inside his coat and drew out the case: lacquered black, iron-bound, sealed with the Ember's mark. He set it on the desk between them with the care of a man laying down a winning hand he hated having been dealt.

"Do you know what this is?"

"I know what you keep in your vaults," the auditor said. "We receive copies. We have for decades. Your guildmaster before you sent us our first shipment the winter I took this office." Something moved behind the spectacles—amusement, or its fossil record. "Did you imagine you invented the arrangement?"

Corvane's stomach turned over, slowly, like a body in water. He set the feeling aside. There would be time to feel things later, or never.

"Then you know what's in this case," he said. "Memories. Twenty-three years of them, decanted and preserved. A complete childhood, purchased from a boy of eleven to settle his father's debts. Every entry documented. Every vial dated." He slid the case forward another inch. "I am offering them as payment for the Reyes boy's soul. Full and final settlement of the account. You get twenty-three years of a life, cleanly stored, legally acquired—which is more than you'd get from Ash, whose soul is damaged goods anyway, half-spoken-for already, tangled up in a thief who won't let go of it."

The hall had gone quiet. Not silent—quiet, the way a room goes quiet when everyone in it has leaned in.

The auditor removed her spectacles. She polished them on her sleeve, unhurried, and Corvane understood that the gesture was not for cleaning but for timing—that she was letting him sit in the silence long enough to hear his own pulse, long enough to doubt.

"You are offering," she said at last, replacing the spectacles, "to sell me a childhood."

"I'm offering to return property to its rightful market."

"And the rightful owner? The boy who grew out of that childhood?" She tilted her head. "Does he consent to the sale?"

"He doesn't remember any of it," Corvane said. "That's rather the point of storage. You can't miss what isn't in you." The words came out smooth, rehearsed, true—and hearing them aloud, in this hall, in front of this woman, they curdled into something else. Something that sounded, to his own ears, exactly like what it was.

The auditor studied him for a long moment. Then she opened the case.

* * *

She lifted the first vial to the light, and Corvane saw it.

Not imagined it—saw it, the way you see a thing through another's eyes when the other's eyes are not human and do not blink. The vial held a summer afternoon: dust motes, a kitchen window, bread on a board, a woman humming. A boy's view of the world, low to the ground, everything enormous and safe. Freya at seven. Freya at nine. Freya at eleven, the last vial in the row, the seal still bright, the contents still warm.

The auditor held that one longest.

"Tell me," she said, without looking up. "When you bought these, did the boy cry?"

"Yes." The word left him before he could choose a better one. Forty years of craft, and one honest answer, dropped like a dropped glass.

"Why?"

"Because children cry at everything. Because his father had sold him and someone had to make the transaction feel like anything other than what it was, and crying was the cheapest way to do it." He heard his own voice flatten into the register he used for haggling, and hated it, and could not stop it. "Because it doesn't matter. Grief is not a legal consideration. The contract was valid. The payment was accepted. What I am proposing now is simply—"

"A trade." She set the last vial down, aligned it with the others, and closed the case. Her hands rested on the lid. "One child's stored years against another child's unspent ones. Do you know how many times I have heard this exact proposal, guildmaster? In this hall, across these centuries, in every language that has ever had the word *owe* in it?" She looked up, and behind the spectacles her eyes were the gray of ash, of old snow, of the space after a fire. "It is always offered by a man who believes himself reasonable. Who has a system. Who tells himself the court must be fed or it rises, the debt must be paid or worse follows, the arithmetic is cruel but the arithmetic is not *his*. And every one of them, when I open the books, turns out to have been skimming."

"That's not—"

"Thirty years," the auditor said. "Shall I read them? I have the entries. Souls sold by Guildmaster Corvane of the Ember, payable beneath the mountain. Fourteen souls, of which eleven were collected and three remain outstanding—including the child called Ash, including the girl from Harrow Fen whose village believed she drowned, including a ferryman whose wife still lights a lamp in the window." She recited it without malice, without emphasis, the way one reads a shipping manifest. "Do you know what strikes me most, reviewing the accounts? Not the number. Numbers are numbers. What strikes me is the handwriting. It improves, year over year. Whatever else this work has cost you, guildmaster, your penmanship has flourished."

Someone laughed—a courtier in the crescent, a sound like coins spilled on marble. Corvane did not look at them. He kept his eyes on the auditor and on the closed case between them, and he made himself do the hardest thing he had ever done in a negotiation, harder than any lie:

He made himself listen to the silence after her words, and hear it accurately.

*Fourteen souls.* He had stopped counting at ten, somewhere in the second decade, in the professional way one stops counting anything that becomes routine. But hearing them named—the girl from Harrow Fen, the ferryman, the lamp in the window—he understood that he had not stopped counting at all. He had merely moved the count somewhere he didn't have to look. Somewhere dark and deep and well-organized. Somewhere rather like this hall.

"You think I don't know what I am," he said quietly.

"I think," the auditor said, "that you know exactly what you are, and you have constructed a life so intricate and so well-kept that you will never have to stand still long enough to look at it. Until now. So. Let us conduct business, since business is the only language you have left." She drew a fresh sheet of paper toward her. "Your offer is noted. Stored memories of one F. Reyes, twenty-three years, condition excellent, provenance documented. Against: one soul, the child Ash, currently claimed by this court under a valid instrument of sale." Her pen hovered. "Before I enter it into the record, I am required to inform you of two complications."

"Complications."

"First: the claimant Freya has filed a formal contest of the original sale, on grounds of fraud. The court has agreed to hear evidence at the reading of accounts. If his argument prevails, your offer becomes moot—the account voids, and the memories in that case become, legally speaking, stolen goods offered for sale by a thief to thieves." She smiled thinly. "An irony I would enjoy more if I had not encountered it four hundred times."

"And the second complication?"

Her pen touched the page. She wrote one line, unhurried, and turned the sheet so he could read it upside down, as clerks do.

*"The memories may be entered as evidence."*

Corvane stared at it. The words did not rearrange themselves. They stayed exactly as terrible as they were.

"If Freya contests the sale," the auditor said, "he may submit anything as proof. Documents. Witnesses. Testimony." She tapped the case with one finger, gently, the way one taps a coffin. "And a contested soul-sale, guildmaster, is best evidenced by the seller's own records. Your vaults. Your entries. Your hand. Everything you copied, everything you hid, everything you built your case with—it cuts both ways. Ink does not take sides." She paused. "Though it does remember."

For a moment the hall tilted. Corvane gripped the arms of the chair, which was warm, which was always warm, which had perhaps been waiting for him to need holding.

Freya. His Freya—no, not his, never his, that had been the whole sickness of it, the boy had come to him half-made, hollowed out, and Corvane had filled the hollow with purpose and called it mentorship and called it family and called it anything except what it was, which was ownership, which was the only trade Corvane had ever truly known. And Freya had gone into the vaults at night with a copying pen and a heart that apparently, despite everything, still beat.

He could hate him for it. He tried. The hatred assembled itself and stood there for a moment, wearing the right clothes, saying the right lines—and then it collapsed, because underneath it, plainly visible now in this light that came from nowhere, was something worse. Pride. A cold, clear, unbearable pride. *Look at him,* the pride whispered. *Look what I made. He learned the ledgers. He learned to steal from me. He learned it all.*

"You're wondering," the auditor said, "what I will do with your offer."

"I'm wondering what you'll do with me."

"Nothing yet." She slid the untouched contract aside. "The reading of accounts convenes when the moon completes its turn. Bring your case, if you still wish to. Bring your defense, if you can construct one. Bring your penmanship." She picked up her pen again, and the audience was, with perfect courtesy, over. "Oh—one more item, since you've come all this way. The claimant Freya does not know what you brought. He knows you descended; he does not know with what. When he learns that you offered to sell his childhood a second time—"

"He already knows what was taken from him," Corvane said. "He signed the sale himself."

"Mm." The auditor wrote, and did not look up. "But he doesn't *remember* signing it. That is the difference between knowing a thing and carrying it. And in my experience, guildmaster, men like you survive on that difference. Men like him—" she blotted the line, precise as a knife laid down, "—do not."

Corvane rose. The chair released him reluctantly. At the far end of the hall the doors stood open on darkness, and somewhere beyond them, six of his people waited with a lantern burning down, and beyond them the long climb, and the cold air, and the guild, and the ledgers, and all the intricate machinery of a life built on the assumption that nothing down here would ever call his number.

"Will he win?" he asked, before he could stop himself. "The reading. Will Freya win?"

The auditor considered the question with the gravity it deserved, which was none.

"That depends," she said, "entirely on what you do next."

He walked the length of the beautiful hall. The courtiers watched him go with their patient, hungry courtesy, and their faces, he realized with a clarity that felt like falling, were all slightly familiar—versions of sellers who had come before him, worn smooth by centuries of sitting in this crescent, waiting for the next negotiation to teach them what they were.

Behind him, the auditor began to write.

Ahead, the doors waited, and the dark, and the climb, and the choice she had left him like a coin on a table: walk away and lose everything honestly, or stay and fight a boy he had hollowed out himself, using the very memories that proved the crime—

—and somewhere in the dark above, faint as breath, he could almost hear the sound of ledgers opening.
