## 18. The Price of Remembering

The auditor's chamber had no ceiling. That was the first thing Freya understood when they were brought before it—no ceiling, only a darkness so deep and so perfectly black that it might have been stone or might have been the underside of the world, and somewhere in that darkness hung lights like coins dropped into a well. They did not flicker. They did not move. They simply waited, as everything in this place waited.

The chamber itself was gilded in the way of old churches: gold laid over rot, gold pressed into seams, gold worked into the whorls of pillars that rose from a floor of black glass. Freya's boots left prints on the glass, and the prints did not fade. The court would keep them, he realized. It kept everything. Every step was an entry in a ledger no one could burn.

He stood at the center of a circle of pale light with Ash at his side and Vess a pace behind them both. Maren had been made to wait above, at the threshold where the mine opened into the court's antechambers; the deep court did not admit midwives to its readings of accounts. Ash's hand was small and cold in Freya's own, and he gripped it the way he had once gripped rope over a long drop—not because it would hold him, but because letting go was unthinkable.

Across the circle stood Corvane.

He had arrived two days after them, descending the mountain's throat in a litter carried by things that wore livery and had once, perhaps, worn faces. He looked immaculate. He looked rested. He looked, Freya thought with a surge of hatred that surprised him by its heat, like a man arriving at a reading of his own accounts confident that he had already bought the auditor.

And perhaps he had. The auditor stood between them—a figure too tall for its robes, its face a smooth expanse of pale material like unmarked vellum, featureless except where eyes should have been. There, two slits of soft silver light burned steadily, and Freya understood with sick certainty that those eyes had read every page ever written about every soul ever sold, and had forgotten none of them.

"The account is open," the auditor said. Its voice came from everywhere at once, patient as dripping water. "One soul, juvenile, designated by sigil." It turned those silver eyes on Ash, and Ash did not flinch. Freya felt the boy's grip tighten, but he did not flinch, and something in Freya's chest cracked open a little wider with pride. "Presented for sale thirty years past by the intermediary called Ember. Claimed by the intermediary called Ember. Now contested." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down. "The claimant will speak."

Freya stepped forward. His mouth was dry. In the guild's ledger room he had argued prices across tables with men who could have ruined him with a word, and his voice had never shaken. Here, before a thing that measured souls in columns, his voice shook.

"I contest the sale," he said. "Not on mercy. I know better than to bring mercy to this court—you deal in law, and law is what I've brought."

* * *

He had spent three nights preparing, hunched in the cell the court had assigned them—it was a comfortable cell, which was somehow worse—with the copied pages spread around him by candlelight while Vess drilled him on procedure she had learned the hard way, buying back her son's soul piece by piece across seven ruinous years.

"They respect one thing," she had told him, her fine face hollowed by the mountain's air. "Ownership. Provenance. You cannot appeal to what is right. You can only prove that their books are wrong—and then show them whose fault the wrongness is. Make the error someone else's. Auditors forgive errors. They never forgive debts."

So now he spoke the language of provenance.

"Corvane sold this soul," Freya said, "but he did not own it. Ownership requires acquisition. Show me the record of acquisition. Show me how a child from a village four hundred miles north of here came into the Ember's possession as property. There is no such record. There cannot be. Because there was no purchase, no trade, no gift, no forfeiture. What there was—" and here he drew out the copied page, the one with the sigils matching Ash's birthmarks, the ink gone brown with age, "—was a theft dressed as a transaction. Your court respects lawful title. This title is not lawful. It is not even competent forgery. It is a lie entered into your ledger by a man who has been lying to you for thirty years."

The auditor did not move. The silver eyes did not waver. Somewhere in the dark above, one of the coin-lights shifted, very slightly, like something turning over in sleep.

"Corvane," the auditor said, "will answer."

Corvane smiled. Freya knew that smile—he had seen it a thousand times across the guildmaster's desk, warm as a hearth, and he had spent most of his adult life forgetting that warmth could be a weapon.

"Honored auditor," Corvane said, and his voice filled the chamber without effort, the way it filled any room he chose to stand in. "You've heard a thief's arithmetic. Let me offer you the truth, which is simpler. The court beneath must be fed—that is the covenant older than any kingdom, older than your ledgers, honored auditor, if you'll pardon the phrase. Souls given freely keep the deep places sleeping. I have fed this court for thirty years. Not one kingdom has fallen. Not one famine has come up out of the mountain. I have done what kings lack the stomach to do, and I have done it cleanly, quietly, with ledgers kept in perfect order." He spread his hands, and the gold light caught them. "Yes, I acquired some souls imperfectly. Show me a merchant who hasn't. But the debt was paid. The court ate. The world turned. And this—" his gaze moved to Ash, and it was gentle, horribly gentle, "—this child would have starved in a village that traded him away. I gave him a roof. A name. A family." His eyes found Freya's. "I gave *him* a family."

"You gave him a due date," Freya said.

"Every life has one."

The words landed in the chamber and lay there. Freya heard Ash breathe beside him, small and steady, and clung to that breath.

"Enough," said the auditor.

* * *

What followed took hours. Freya would remember it afterward as a kind of terrible music—the reading of accounts, performed in full.

The auditor produced the master ledgers. Not copies; the originals, drawn up out of the dark in vessels of black iron, and when they were opened Freya saw that the pages were not paper but something thin and translucent, and that each page bore not ink but names—actual names, suspended in the substance of the page like insects in amber, faintly moving. Thirty years of Corvane's transactions, read aloud.

"One soul, male, aged six. Acquired: village of Halden's Ford. Consideration: none recorded."

"One soul, female, aged nine. Acquired: debtor's forfeit, city of Carreth. Forfeiture coerced. Coercion noted."

"One soul, male, aged eleven. Acquired: payment in kind for passage rights, the Weepwater mines. Seller deceased before delivery. Delivery executed regardless."

On and on. Dozens. Freya had known the number from his stolen pages—had counted them alone in the night with shaking hands—but hearing them read aloud in that patient, water-dripping voice was different. Each name was a child. Each child had been somebody's whole world, and the world had gone on turning because a reasonable man with a warm smile had decided the mountain needed feeding more than anyone needed remembering.

Vess wept silently through all of it, her gloved hand pressed to her mouth. When the reader reached the entry marked *rescued*—her son, bought back at a price that had stripped her house to its foundations—the auditor paused.

"This account," it said, "is closed. Paid in full. It does not concern these proceedings." And it moved on, and Lady Vess bowed her head, and Freya saw that even here, even now, having paid everything, her son's freedom remained a line item in someone else's book. That was the horror of this place. Not cruelty. Cruelty would at least be honest. This was bookkeeping.

Then the reading reached the last entry, and the auditor's silver eyes turned fully upon Ash for the first time, and the whole chamber seemed to lean inward.

"One soul, juvenile. Sigil-borne. Acquired: village of the winter-that-never-ended." A pause, longer than any before it. "Acquisition method: *unrecorded.*"

"There," Freya said quietly. "There it is."

"The seller claims the debt stands regardless," the auditor said. "The claimant disputes the title. The court will now weigh the evidence." Those eyeless eyes moved to Corvane. "Intermediary Ember. Produce your proof of ownership."

Corvane reached into his coat and drew out a ledger of his own—small, bound in green leather, the twin of a hundred ledgers Freya had catalogued in the vault above. He opened it with the reverence of a priest and held it up, and Freya's heart stopped.

Because the pages showed exactly what Corvane claimed. A record of acquisition. A consideration paid. Signatures. Dates. A clean chain of title running back thirty years, seamless as silk.

"That's forged," Freya said, and heard how thin his own voice sounded.

"All ledgers are forged," Corvane said mildly. "Some are merely forged earlier than others. Yours, Freya—I know about yours, boy, I've known since the night you broke into my vault, and I let you walk out because a thief who steals proof against me is still a thief who trusts paper. You think truth lives in ink?" He closed the green ledger gently. "Truth lives in whoever holds the book."

But the auditor had extended one long, pale finger, and the green ledger floated from Corvane's grasp and crossed the chamber and hung open in the air beside the original records, and the court began to compare them.

Freya watched the silver eyes move from page to page. Watched the silence stretch. Beside him Ash tugged his sleeve and pointed—not at the floating ledger but at the auditor's face, at the smooth vellum skin, and whispered a single syllable in the old language, the one that meant, as near as Freya could translate it, *reading.*

"It sees the ink," Ash whispered again, in Freya's tongue now, halting and new. "Under the ink. Ink remembers."

And Freya understood. Of course it did. This was a court of souls; these were ledgers written in souls; and the dead and the taken remembered what had been done to them, whether or not any living hand recorded it. Corvane's forgery was beautiful. It was also, under the ink, empty—pages with no names suspended in them, no faint movement, nothing alive at all.

"The presented instrument contains no souls," the auditor announced. "It is therefore not a ledger. It is decoration." A pause, and then, with what Freya would swear was distaste: "The court finds the acquisition of the sigil-borne soul to be fraudulent. Title void. The sale is dissolved. The debt is discharged."

For one heartbeat, Freya felt nothing at all—just white silence, the way he felt when he catalogued a stolen memory, that awful professional blankness—and then Ash's hand squeezed his, hard, and the feeling broke over him all at once like a wave over a seawall, and he had to stand very still for a long moment with his eyes fixed on the middle distance because if he looked down at the boy he was going to come apart in front of a court of things that fed on souls.

They had won. They had actually—

"The matter is not concluded," the auditor said.

The wave went cold.

"A voided sale returns the asset to its origin," the auditor continued, inexorable. "The origin is the village of the winter-that-never-ended. The village no longer exists. The parents who sold are dead. There is, therefore, no rightful owner to whom the soul can be restored." The silver eyes regarded Freya, and then Ash, and then Freya again. "An ownerless soul in the deep places becomes court property within one turn of the moon. Unless—" and here the auditor's voice changed, and Freya heard in it, unmistakably, the sound of a contract being drafted, "—a keeper's bond is sworn."

"What does that mean?" Freya asked. "Exactly. I deal in exact."

"So do we. It means this: a living soul of standing binds itself as trustee. Not owner—trustee. The bond is permanent, non-transferable, and enforceable. Whatever the ward's soul requires—protection, provision, presence—the keeper provides, or the bond breaks, and the soul reverts to this court, together with a portion of the keeper's own." The auditor tilted its great smooth head. "You would hold the child's soul in trust until he comes of age and speaks his own claim. It is not freedom, thief. It is custody with love in it. It is the nearest thing to freedom this court knows how to write."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in one turn of the moon, the mountain keeps him. Forever is our specialty."

Freya looked down at Ash. The boy was watching him with those strange, old eyes, and Freya thought of the vaults above, of all the bottled griefs and stolen years shelved in the dark like preserves, of every soul Corvane had filed away because filing was easier than loving. He thought: *this court and my guild are the same animal. One just wears better gold.*

He thought of Maren waiting at the threshold with soup she'd carry down anyway, against all rules, because rules had never once stopped her from feeding someone.

He knelt. On the black glass floor, before the auditor, before Corvane whose face had gone carefully, dangerously blank, before the whole patient hungry gold of the deep court, Freya Reyes knelt and took both of Ash's hands in his own.

"Ask me," he said to the boy. "In whatever language you like. Ask me, and I'll swear it."

Ash looked at him for a long moment. Then he spoke—one word of the old tongue first, low, deliberate, a word Freya didn't know yet but would learn within the year, a word that meant *home*. Then, in Freya's own language, learned in a kitchen over spilled soup and mended boots:

"Keep it. Keep me. Both. Same thing."

"Yes," Freya said. "Both. Same thing."

He swore the bond in front of the court, and the court wrote it down—in a ledger that was not paper, in ink that was not ink—and when it was done the auditor inclined its head with the terrible courtesy of a creditor closing an account.

"The bond is entered. The ward is released to the keeper's custody. You may ascend." A pause. The silver eyes dimmed, almost kindly. "Be advised, keeper. Bonds are audited. We will be watching. We always watch."

They turned toward the ascent road, Vess already moving, Ash's hand in Freya's, and behind them Freya heard Corvane's voice, quiet and pleasant, addressed to the auditor:

"Now then. About payment for *my* services rendered. I'm prepared to discuss a settlement. I have certain assets—memories, mostly. High quality. One owner in particular—"

Freya did not stop walking. But he memorized the voice, and the words, and the shape of the man's desperation, because he had been a thief for thirty years and he knew better than anyone alive what Corvane was about to try to spend.

Above them, through the mountain's throat, the moon was rising toward its thirteenth turn.
