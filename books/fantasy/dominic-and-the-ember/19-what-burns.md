## 19. What Burns

The mountain began to die at the moment the moon completed its turn, and Freya ran through its death with a child's hand in his and a stolen soul pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

Behind them, the deep court was coming apart in layers. That was the terrible elegance of it—the auditor had not screamed, had not raged. She had simply closed her ledger, inclined her head to Freya as though he were a colleague closing a long meeting, and said, *The accounts are voided. The structure will follow.* As if a civilization built on filed souls could do nothing else but file itself away.

Now the gilded corridors were shedding their gilt. It flaked from the walls in long golden ribbons that curled as they fell, and where each ribbon touched the floor it burned without flame, leaving only a smell like coins left too long in a dead man's fist. The patient lamps along the corridor guttered one by one, not going dark but going *out of date*, their light turning the grey of old paper. Somewhere far above—or perhaps far below; distance meant nothing here—the sound of the mountain clearing its throat, a grinding groan that Freya felt in his teeth.

Ash ran beside him, barefoot still—Maren's mended boots had been lost somewhere in the descent road, in the collapse that took Vess's guide—and the boy did not stumble once. His feet found the stone the way water finds a channel. He was speaking now, had been speaking since the reading of accounts, small words in Freya's language dropped between words of the old tongue like coins into a well.

"*Run*," Ash said, testing it. Then, looking back over his shoulder at the collapsing splendor: something longer, older, sadder, that Freya could not translate but understood anyway. A word for a house that has forgotten it is a house.

"Don't look back," Freya said, and hated himself for it, because he was looking back. Because he had spent thirty years in a guild whose first rule was *see everything*, and he could not stop seeing: the alcoves where souls had been shelved like vintage wine, now emptying, the pale lights of them drifting up through cracks in the ceiling like sparks returning to some fire they remembered. The clerks' desks with their immaculate instruments of patience. The great filing hall where Lady Vess had bargained with her son's story held out like a blade made of grief—all of it folding inward, politely, the way a ledger closes when the last entry is made.

Vess was behind them somewhere. He had heard her voice in the chaos after the voiding, sharp and commanding, organizing the court's own functionaries into an evacuation line because apparently even the end of an empire required someone to count heads. She had looked at Freya across the ruin of the auditor's chamber—one glance, no more—and given him a single nod. *We are even,* the nod said. *We were never friends. Go.*

He went.

* * *

They came up through the throat of the mountain the same way they had come down, through the abandoned mine, except that the mine was abandoning itself now. Timbers groaned and split overhead. Dust fell in curtains, gold-flecked—the mountain bleeding its ornament down onto them. Twice the passage ahead simply wasn't there anymore, and twice Ash stopped, tilted his head as if listening to the stone, and led them sideways through gaps Freya would have sworn were solid rock. The old language again, spoken to things that were not people. The mountain, dying, seemed willing to listen to a boy it had almost owned.

"You're talking to it," Freya said, breathless, hauling himself over a fallen beam.

Ash considered this. "It is talking to *me*," he said carefully, each word of Freya's language placed like a stone in a wall. "It says—" He frowned, searching. "It says it is sorry. About the paperwork."

Freya laughed—a raw, cracked sound, the first laugh in longer than he could measure, which was itself a kind of wound, because he knew exactly why he couldn't measure time before the guild. "Tell it the apology is noted," he said. "Tell it we keep records too."

But even as he said it, the laugh died in his chest, and what replaced it was cold and clear and had been waiting there since the auditor's chamber, growing sharper with every step toward the surface:

*Voided is not undone.*

The court had agreed. The sale was fraudulent; Corvane had sold what he never owned; every claim on Ash was struck from the deep record. But records lived above ground too. Records lived in a ledger room in a city three days' ride from this mountain, in a vault behind three locks, written in Corvane's neat, patient hand. And Corvane was still alive. Still clever. Still possessed of thirty years of practice at making monstrous things look like mercy.

The court beneath was dying, yes. But the Ember would endure. Guilds always endured—that was the whole point of them, that was what Corvane had taught him in Freya's first year, tapping the master ledger with one finger: *Names burn, Freya. Empires fall. Paper survives. We are not thieves. We are librarians of the impossible.* And with the deep court gone, the Ember would not even need a buyer anymore. There would be others. There were always others—Lady Vess had proven that, poor woman, desperate enough to buy her own son back at ruinous price. Grief was an infinite market. The guild would simply find new depths to sell into, new courts, new hungers, or it would hoard, and trade among itself, and every entry in those books would remain a living chain around some sleeping soul somewhere.

Dozens of names. He had copied the pages himself, by candlelight, breaking his own rule, his hands steady and his heart not. He had thought he was stealing evidence. He understood now that evidence was not enough. Evidence could be dismissed as clerical error. Evidence could be bought, as Corvane had tried to buy *him*, offering back the erased years of Freya's childhood like a man offering a beggar his own severed hand.

Paper survived. So paper had to burn.

The thought arrived not as a decision but as a temperature—as heat behind his sternum, as rage finally finding its true shape. Not rage at Corvane alone. Rage at the whole architecture of it: the idea that anything in a person could be entered in a book and thereby owned. That he himself existed in those books—his childhood, sold at nine years old to escape his father's debts, weeping over something he could no longer remember weeping about. Somewhere in the vaults above was the page with his name on it. Somewhere was the memory of why he had cried, filed under assets, gathering dust in the dark while he walked around all these years with a hole shaped like a boy where his heart should have been.

He was going to burn all of it. Every page. Every name. Including his own.

* * *

They surfaced near dawn, through the mine's collapsed mouth, into snow and grey light and air so clean it hurt. The thirteenth moon was setting in the west, fat and white and finished, sliding down behind the hills like a coin paid at last into a closed account. Ash stood beside him in the snow and watched it go, and when it touched the horizon the boy shivered once, deeply, like a bird settling its feathers, and then turned to Freya with an expression of enormous, astonished calm.

"It let go," Ash said. "The moon. It was holding the debt and now it—" he made a motion with both hands, opening them, letting something invisible fall. "Done."

"Done," Freya echoed, and did not believe it yet, and pulled the boy close anyway, pressing his lips to the cold crown of his head. Maren was waiting at the safehouse Vess had arranged—Maren, who had named him Ash, who had mended his boots, who had hidden him in the memory-vaults when the guild fractured and whispered to the stored lives until they whispered back and kept watch over her boy. Three days' ride. He sent word by hawk from a village whose name he never learned, four words only: *Coming. Both of us. Burn ready?*

Her reply came back within the day, a single scrap: *Kerosene's been waiting a year.*

* * *

The Ember's hidden door opened to him at midnight two days later, because the door did not know yet that he was a traitor, or perhaps because doors are loyal to whoever knocks correctly. Inside, the corridors were quiet in the way of a house holding its breath. The guild had fractured after the tenth moon—half its members gone to Corvane's banner, half scattered, a few, like Maren, choosing the boy over the books. But the vaults remained. The vaults always remained. Whatever happened to the people, the paper survived.

That was the doctrine. He was about to become a heretic.

He found Maren in the kitchen, as he'd known he would, sitting at the long table with a lamp turned low and a knife she was not using, just holding, the way other people hold rosaries. She took one look at Ash—whole, breathing, *unowned*—and made a sound Freya had never heard from her in fifteen years, something between a sob and a laugh, and then she had them both crushed against her flour-dusted apron and none of them spoke for a long time.

Then she pulled back, held Ash's face in her rough hands, studied him like a midwife counting fingers, and said, "Right. You're eating first. Rescued children eat. House rule." And over the boy's head her eyes met Freya's, and read him the way she read everyone, instantly and without mercy, and her expression shifted. "You're not staying, are you. You've got that look. The ledger-room look."

"I need oil," Freya said. "Lamps. Everything flammable. And I need you to take Ash and be very far from here by the time I start."

"No," Maren said.

"Maren—"

"You'll drown in smoke, you stubborn man, or Corvane's people will find you standing in the ashes and cut you down, and I'll have buried two people I love inside a month." She said *love* the way she said everything, flat and practical, which somehow made it heavier. "So we do it together, fast, and we all leave together. Besides." A grim twist of her mouth. "You'll need someone to carry the oil. And I've been wanting to burn something in that building for thirty years. The porridge alone deserves it."

* * *

The ledger room at night was exactly as it had always been, and that was the obscenity of it. Rows of shelves rising into shadow. The smell of vellum and iron-gall ink and lamp oil waiting patiently in the corner stores. The master ledgers on their lecterns, open to current entries, Corvane's handwriting marching across the pages neat as fence posts. Thirty years of sales. Stolen years. Bottled grief. Memories pried from sleepers. Souls, sold, payable beneath the mountain—dozens of entries in Corvane's hand, each one initialed, each one witnessed, each one lawful according to rules the guild had written for itself so that it would never have to call anything theft.

Freya walked the rows slowly, lamp raised, and let himself see it all one last time. Here was the shelf where his own sale had been recorded—he knew the section the way you know the location of a scar. Volume eleven, third shelf, entries for the northern districts, the year of the bad harvest. Somewhere in that row was a page describing a nine-year-old boy's childhood, itemized. His father's debts crossed out, settled. The boy's tears, if they were mentioned at all, mentioned as consideration received.

He had planned, in the mountain, to search for it. To pull volume eleven and read, finally, what he had paid. He had told himself it was thoroughness.

He stood before the shelf now for a long moment, lamp trembling slightly in his grip.

Then he set the lamp down, unopened, and walked away.

"Not going to look?" Maren asked quietly, from the doorway, Ash's hand in hers.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because whatever's on that page isn't mine anymore either way." His voice came out steadier than he expected. "If I read it, I'm buying myself back. And I'm done shopping in this store."

Maren looked at him for a moment, and something in her weathered face softened into something that was not quite pity and not quite pride. "All right," she said. "Burn it all, then?"

"Burn it all."

They worked fast and silently, the way the guild had taught them—irony so complete it was nearly holy. Oil sloshed across shelves, soaked into vellum, pooled in the aisles between the records of every impossible thing the Ember had ever taken from a sleeping world. Ash helped, solemn and fierce, carrying the small cans Maren filled for him, tipping them out with the gravity of a boy performing a sacrament. When the last aisle was slicked and reeking, Freya climbed to the highest gallery where the oldest volumes sat—the founding ledgers, the ones older than Corvane, older than the guild's current name, the ones that proved the rot went all the way down to the roots.

He struck the match there, in the high dark, alone with the first book ever kept.

For one heartbeat he hesitated—not from doubt, but from grief. This room had been the only home his mind could verify. Every certainty he owned lived on these shelves. Burning it would leave him a man with no past and no proof, standing in ash.

Good, he thought. Let the next thing I build be honest.

He dropped the match.

* * *

Fire moved through the ledger room the way truth moves through a family—slowly at first, then all at once, and nothing afterward is the same. It went up the oiled shelves in blue whispers and came down in orange banners. It found the master ledgers and ate Corvane's neat handwriting line by line, initial by initial, and if fire makes any sound as it destroys ink, Freya would swear he heard the pages sigh.

They went out through the kitchen, the three of them, and did not run. Maren locked the door behind them out of pure habit and then laughed at herself, a real laugh, wet-eyed. In the street outside, in the falling snow, they stood and watched the hidden windows of the Ember begin to glow from within, gold light bleeding through the seams of a building that had taught him everything he knew about taking, and nothing at all about giving back.

Ash slipped his small cold hand into Freya's.

"What does it mean?" the boy asked, watching the fire. "Burning it. In the old language there is a word. It means burning, but also—" he searched, frowning, "—also *setting free*. Also *waking up*. Same word."

Freya watched the roof timbers catch, watched thirty years of lawful theft climb into the sky as sparks, and thought of pale soul-lights drifting upward through cracks in a dying court, going home to some fire they remembered.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "Same word."

The heat washed over their faces, and for the first time in as long as he could remember—which was, precisely, as long as the ledgers had allowed him to remember—Freya felt warm without owing anyone for it.

It was Maren who saw them first. She caught his sleeve, nodded across the street, toward the mouth of the alley where the lamplight didn't reach.

Three figures stood there, watching the fire. Not fleeing it. Not fighting it. Watching with the stillness of people who had come to see exactly this.

And the tallest of them stepped forward into the light, and Freya's stomach dropped, because he knew the walk, he knew the coat, he knew the patient, reasonable face of Guildmaster Corvane, who looked at the burning vaults, and then at Freya, and smiled.

"Well," Corvane said. "I see we need to talk about what you've just destroyed."
