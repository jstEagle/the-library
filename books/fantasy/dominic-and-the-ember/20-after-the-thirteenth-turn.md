## 20. After the Thirteenth Turn

The snow came down soft as sifted flour, and Maren stood in the kitchen doorway of the Ember and watched it bury the year.

Thirteen turns. She had counted every one of them, marked them in chalk on the lintel above her stove the way she had once marked the days of a difficult labor—because some births, she had learned long ago, were not of children but of endings, and you did well to keep track of when the pain would crest. The thirteenth chalk mark was still there, half-smudged by Ash's sleeve where he'd reached up on his toes to touch it that morning, solemn as a little priest at an altar.

It was done now. The moon had turned. And Ash was still here, still breathing, still asking for honey in his porridge with the particular stubborn silence he used when words felt too heavy for his mouth.

Maren wiped her hands on her apron and let herself feel it—not joy, not yet, but something quieter and more durable. Relief, maybe, the kind that settles into the bones like heat from a banked fire. The kind you don't trust until it's been there a full day.

Behind her, the guild house creaked and settled around its own emptiness.

* * *

They had begun leaving three nights after Freya came down out of the mountain, ash still in the creases of his knuckles and a look in his eyes that Maren recognized from women who had just given birth to dead children: the look of someone who has carried something to term and lost it and gained it all in the same hour.

He had burned the ledgers. Everyone knew it by morning, though no one had seen it happen. You could smell it—the whole vault level smelled of it, ink and vellum and old glue rendered down into smoke that had seeped up through the floorboards like a confession. Corvane's master ledgers, thirty years of sales, every soul entered in a neat clerk's hand: *one, sold; payable beneath the mountain.* All of it gone to fire while the deep court collapsed behind them like a held breath finally released.

Some of the guild had cheered. Maren remembered that with a strange, sour clarity—Doss cheering, actually cheering, raising a cup of raw spirits at breakfast and declaring the Ember free. Others had not cheered. Others had gone very quiet, the way men go quiet when they realize the thing that made them rich was also the thing that made them damned, and they cannot decide which fact to grieve.

By the fifth day, the quiet ones had started packing.

By the ninth, Doss himself was gone, taking two of the younger thieves with him, off to whatever work waited for hands that could pick locks but had never learned anything else. He hadn't said goodbye. He'd left his room swept clean, which Maren thought was either an apology or an accusation, and she decided she didn't care which.

Now, on the morning after the thirteenth turn, the guild house held exactly five souls: Maren in her kitchen; Freya somewhere upstairs, sleeping for the first time in what she suspected was days; Ash, who was currently sitting on her counter swinging his legs and supervising the bread dough with the gravity of a foreman; old Tam, who had been with the Ember longer than anyone and who had announced, the night the ledgers burned, that he was too old to start over and too stubborn to leave; and Wren, the youngest of them, who had come to Corvane's guild as a starving pickpocket of eleven and who now, at sixteen, kept looking around the empty rooms as if expecting the walls to confess something.

Five souls. Once there had been forty.

Maren kneaded the dough and counted them again, the way you count fingers on a newborn. Five. It was enough. It would have to be enough.

* * *

Corvane they had delivered to the parliament on the second night after the burning, and Maren had gone along because someone had to, because Freya's hands were shaking too badly to tie the knots and Ash could not be allowed to watch, and because—in a way she did not examine too closely—she wanted to see it.

The thieves' parliament met in a disused counting-house under the tanneries, where the air smelled of lime and old blood and money that had changed hands dishonestly for a hundred years. There were no laws there. That was rather the point. The lawful courts of the city would have taken Corvane, patted him on the head for paying his taxes, and asked whether the mountain court might be persuaded to sell souls back at a discount. Justice, among thieves, was a different animal: older, uglier, and honest about its appetite.

They had stripped him first. Not of clothes—of records. Or rather, of the lack of them. Freya had brought what remained: fragments, scorched edges, pages he had copied before the burning precisely so that this night could happen. The copies survived. The originals did not. Corvane stood in the lamplight of the counting-house with nothing left to prove his innocence and everything left to prove his guilt, and Maren watched a man who had ruled forty thieves for thirty years discover what it was to be an entry in someone else's ledger.

He had not begged. She would give him that. He had stood very straight, very pale, and when the parliament's speaker—a broad woman with a dyer's stains on her hands and a voice like a door slamming—had read aloud the names of the sold, Corvane had closed his eyes at each one, as if in prayer, or arithmetic.

Forty-one names. Maren had not known there were forty-one. She had known about Ash, and she had suspected others—the way you suspect rot behind a wall, from the smell alone—but hearing them read out, one after another, each with a date and a price and a buyer's mark, had been like watching a grave open slowly over the course of an evening.

The parliament debated until nearly dawn. Some wanted him dead. Some wanted him sold—there was a bitter poetry in that which several voices clearly savored. In the end, they chose the punishment thieves reserve for their worst crimes, worse than death in its way: they unmade him. They declared his name forfeit. Every thief in every city would know his face and refuse it. No guild would hold him, no fence would buy from him, no door that opened to the underworld's knock would open to his. They took his guild ring—he surrendered it, Maren noticed, almost gratefully—and they wrote his name on a scrap of ledger paper and burned it in a dish, and that was that. Corvane walked out of the counting-house into the snow a free man with no name, which is to say no man at all.

Freya had watched the whole thing without expression. But afterward, in the street outside the tanneries, with the snow coming down and the lamps guttering, he had stopped walking and stood very still, and Maren had put her hand on his arm and felt him trembling like a wire.

"You did right," she told him.

"Did I?" he said. His voice sounded scraped. "I spent thirty years learning his trade. I sat at his table. I ate his bread." A pause. "I stole memories, Maren. For wages."

"Aye," she said. "And then you burned the book. Both things are true. That's what being a person is."

He hadn't answered. But he had started walking again, and he had slept that night without screaming, which was more than could be said for the nights before.

* * *

Now the kitchen was warm and the bread was rising and Ash was drawing in the flour dust on the counter—sigils, always sigils, the old language spilling out of his fingers even when his mouth stayed shut. Maren had stopped fearing the marks. Somewhere in the last month they had become simply his handwriting, the way another child scrawled lopsided houses and suns. These were houses and suns too, probably. Houses and suns from before any kingdom she knew.

"Tell me what that one says," she said, nodding at a looping mark near his elbow.

Ash considered it seriously. Then he looked up at her, and his mouth worked, and out came—halting, careful, an accent like wind through a canyon—"Home."

Maren's hands went still in the dough.

Not the word itself—though the word itself was enough to stop a heart, a word she had heard him say only once before, to Freya, in a whisper, on a night neither of them spoke of. What stopped her was the language. He was speaking hers. Freya's tongue, the common tongue, the tongue of the city and the guild and the kitchen—clumsy and new in his mouth, but real, growing, like green shoots through ash.

Since the mountain, the words had been coming. Slowly at first—a word here, a phrase there, usually at odd hours, usually when he thought no one was listening. Wren claimed he'd said *bread* in his sleep. Tam swore he'd heard *no thank you*, delivered with impeccable manners, when offered a second helping of kidneys. And now this, awake, deliberate, offered to her across a counter dusted with flour like the first snow of a kinder year.

"That's right," Maren said, and her voice cracked in the middle, and she didn't care. "That's exactly what it says. Clever lad."

Ash smiled—the small, rare smile, the one that showed one crooked tooth—and went back to his drawing, and Maren turned to the window so he wouldn't see her wipe her eyes, though she suspected he saw anyway. Children always saw. Especially children who had been property, who had learned to read faces the way other people read weather.

* * *

In the afternoon she took stock.

It was a midwife's habit, this inventory-taking: how much food, how much fuel, how many hands, how much strength. She walked the guild house room by room with a stub of pencil and an old receipt's back for paper, and the house told her its truths.

The pantry was thin but not desperate; Maren had been salting and smoking against disaster since the sixth moon, and the stores would carry five mouths through winter if five mouths was all there was. The vaults below—the memory-vaults, the shelves of bottled grief and stolen years—were intact, untouched by the fire, and that was a problem wearing the face of a treasure. Hundreds of stolen things, sitting in the dark with nowhere to go home to, now that the book that recorded their theft was ash.

"We'll have to return them," Freya had said, three nights ago, staring down into the vault with the particular grimness of a man assigning himself a life sentence. "Every one. Find the owners, or their kin, or their kin's kin. Give it all back."

"It'll take years," Maren had said.

"I know," he'd said, and something in his face had loosened even as he said it, as if the sentence were also a pardon.

Upstairs, Tam was in the map room doing what Tam did, which was mending things nobody had asked him to mend: a chair, a shutter, a lockbox whose lock had been broken since before the war. He'd taken Corvane's old chair and sawn a hand's width off the legs, and when Maren raised an eyebrow he'd shrugged and said, "Was too big for the room anyway," and that was the entire eulogy the guildmaster received from the man who had served him longest.

Wren was in the stable loft with the account books—not Corvane's ledgers, those were smoke, but the ordinary books, the ones tracking grain and lamp oil and boot leather. She was teaching herself the numbers, frowning at them like enemies, and Maren paused at the foot of the ladder and watched her for a moment: sixteen years old, orphaned twice over, once by plague and once by a guildmaster's kindness that had been a trap. Wren had chosen to stay. Had said, in the flat voice she used for important things, "Someone has to learn where the money goes, so it can't go there again." Sixteen years old, and already a keeper of accounts. Maren intended to teach her everything she knew, and then to teach her better.

And Freya—Freya was asleep, truly asleep, in the narrow bed in the attic room, with Ash's boots—mended, resoled, warmed by the fire—set beside the door where he'd see them first thing. Maren had crept up past noon with broth and found him like that: on his side, one hand curled loose, looking young. Younger than his years, younger than his sins. She'd set the broth down without waking him and stood a moment in the doorway, and had thought, with the fierce unsentimental tenderness she'd spent on a hundred mothers and a hundred babies: *There. That one I'm keeping.*

She had lost children before. Not her own—she'd never had her own, her life having gone another road—but she had caught them, nursed them, buried a few, and loved more than was sensible. She knew the arithmetic of a household the way she knew the arithmetic of a birth: what you have, what you owe, what you can bear. Five souls. One kitchen. One long debt of returned memories stretching out ahead like a winter road.

It was not the Ember anymore. The Ember was dead, and good riddance, and she'd spit on its grave. Whatever this was, it was smaller and warmer and named nothing yet.

But it was theirs.

* * *

Evening came early, the way it did in the deep of winter, and the four of them plus one sleeping gathered in the kitchen because the kitchen was where the warmth was, and warmth draws people the way rivers draw towns.

Tam carved. Wren read aloud from a penny dreadful she'd bought off a peddler, doing all the voices badly, which everyone pretended not to love. Ash sat pressed against Maren's side, drowsy, full of bread and honey, and every few minutes another word surfaced from him—*light,* *spoon,* *stay*—each one laid down like a coin in a collection plate, and each one received with the same exaggerated delight, as though he'd performed a miracle, because he had.

Outside, the snow kept falling on the guild house, on the tanneries, on the mountain whose court had emptied like a lung exhaling, on the city where forty-one families would someday receive strange parcels containing bottled grief and stolen years and no note at all except, perhaps, eventually, a knock at the door and a tired man saying, "I believe this belongs to you."

Maren looked around her kitchen—at Tam's scarred hands, at Wren's bent head, at the child breathing slow against her ribs—and let the exhaustion have her, fully, for one honest minute. Thirteen moons of fear, paid off. A monster unmade. A boy saved. And tomorrow, and the day after, and the year after that: work. Returning, repairing, rebuilding. Feeding five mouths on a salted ham and stubbornness.

It was, she thought, the best bargain she'd ever struck.

She was reaching for the lamp to trim the wick when the knock came.

Three raps, soft, deliberate, at the hidden door—the door that no one found unless the guild wanted finding, the door that had admitted a barefoot child thirteen moons ago and had been silent ever since the burning.

The kitchen went still. Wren lowered her book. Tam's knife paused mid-carving. Ash lifted his head from Maren's shoulder, wide awake now, and looked toward the sound with an expression she couldn't read—fear, or recognition, or both.

From the attic, footsteps: Freya, rising, coming down the stairs with the unhurried tread of a man who had already decided how he would answer.

Maren drew Ash closer and waited, her heart keeping time against his ear, as the bolt slid back.
