## 1. The Date in the Margin

The cellar smelled of tallow, brine, and old paper — the three smells of Xanthe's whole life. He crouched among the crates with a lantern turned low, counting bolts of Thornfell blue that were not, strictly speaking, Thornfell blue, and telling himself the flutter under his ribs was only the usual pre-run nerves.

Twenty-five tomorrow. The guild said a man wasn't whole until he'd run the coast road alone, and tomorrow night he would. Tonight was packing, checking knots, oiling boots. Routine.

The crate at the back of the cellar was not routine.

He'd passed it a thousand times — black oak, iron bands, no lock, only a seal of grey wax stamped with the Eastwick knot. His grandmother had told him once, when he was small enough to be fobbed off with stories: *That one stays shut until it opens.* He'd laughed then. Tonight his fingers found the wax already cracked, a hairline fracture running through the guild mark like a scar through an eye.

He hadn't done it. He was almost sure he hadn't done it.

The lid came up without a sound, which was somehow worse than a scream. Inside lay a single book, bound in leather gone dark as dried blood, its spine unlettered. No ledger he knew. The guild kept its accounts upstairs, in ink that could be audited by men who could be bribed. This book smelled of something older than bribery.

He opened it to the first page and read:

*What is sold below shall be collected below.*

Then dates. Columns of them, marching back a century, each beside a name. He recognized some — names from gravestones, names from songs the old-timers sang when they thought the young ones weren't listening. Beside each name, a few lines in a hand that changed over the years but never softened.

His lantern guttered though there was no draft.

He told himself he was looking for his grandmother's name. He told himself that was all. But his thumb moved down the columns as if it had read this book before, past 1919, past 1887, past entries where the ink had browned to rust, and stopped on a date written in ink still wet enough to shine.

Tonight's date.

Beside it: *Xanthe Eastwick.*

And beneath his name, in that same glistening script:

*Comes down of his own will. Does not come up. The mountain keeps what walks into it keeping something else.*

Xanthe sat back on his heels. The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above him, floorboards creaked as his grandmother moved through her evening rituals — kettle, latch, lamp — the small domestic liturgy that had raised him. He looked at the words again and felt nothing for a long moment, the way you feel nothing when a wave knocks you flat before the cold arrives.

*Does not come up.*

"That's not me," he said aloud, and his voice sounded like a boy's. "I haven't come down anywhere. I'm going *up* the coast road tomorrow."

But the prophecy didn't say tomorrow. It said tonight. And beneath the mountain — well. There was nothing beneath the mountain. Everyone knew that. Everyone also knew the guild's oldest roads ran *through* the mountain, roads no living person remembered, because remembering them had been forbidden since before anyone now breathing was born.

He should burn the book. That was the sensible smuggler's move: destroy the evidence, keep the run clean. His hands were actually moving toward the lantern when the door at the top of the stairs opened and his grandmother came down them without hurrying, the way she did everything, as if time were a river she owned the tolls on.

She was seventy-one and could still out-walk him up a cliff face. She looked at the open crate, at the book in his lap, and something crossed her face that he had never seen there in twenty-five years — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman meeting a debt she'd been pretending had lapsed.

"You weren't meant to see that yet," she said.

"Yet." His voice came out flat. "There was a *yet* planned?"

"There was always a yet, love. There's a yet for everything." She held out her hand. "Give it here."

He didn't give it. That was the first order he'd ever refused her, and they both felt the ground shift under it. "What is this? Whose handwriting?"

"The first warden's," she said, "and every warden's after, adding their lines. It's the guild's true ledger, Xanthe. Not what we took and sold. What we *paid*. What we're still paying."

"Paid to who?"

"To the Undercourt." She said it the way you'd say a creditor's name in front of the creditor's man. "And tonight the account comes due against you."

"It says I die." He heard how young his voice was and hated it. "Under the mountain. Tonight."

"I know what it says." She knelt — his grandmother, kneeling, on the cellar flags — and took the book from him gently, the way you take a blade from a child. From her apron pocket she drew the stub of candle she used for sealing letters, lit it from his lantern, and touched flame to the page with his name on it.

The paper caught. Burned quick and clean, curling to black lace. She let the ash fall into the crate and closed the lid, and for one breath the cellar smelled only of smoke, and Xanthe thought — absurdly, desperately — *that's done then, prophecies are just paper, paper burns.*

Then the itching started.

It began at his left wrist, a prickle like sunburn coming on, and spread. He shoved back his sleeve. There, rising through the skin in fine dark strokes, letter by letter, as if written by a pen moving underneath: *Comes down of his own will.* Across his forearm: *Does not come up.* He tore at his collar and found more, crawling up his throat toward his jaw, and he understood with a sick lurch that the rest of it was somewhere he couldn't read, being written on him line by line, patient as tide.

"Grandmother."

"I know." Her face had gone grey. "I know, love. Sit down before you fall down."

He didn't sit. "You *knew*. You've known my whole life. You named me — did you name me out of this book?" The words were climbing his collarbone. "Was I ever anything but a line in a ledger?"

She flinched. Good, some vicious part of him noted. Let her flinch.

"You were everything," she said quietly. "That's why I burned the page the first time too, the night your mother signed. Paper doesn't hold it. Skin holds it. Blood holds it. The court writes in what can't be torn out." She reached up and gripped his wrist hard, over the fresh ink, as if pressure could stop the writing. "Listen to me. Tomorrow you run the coast road. You do not go near the mountain. You do not take the low tunnels. Whatever Wren tells you—"

The name went through him like a hook.

"What does Wren have to do with it?"

His grandmother's mouth closed. Opened. Closed again, and he watched twenty-five years of her composure fail her all at once, watched her decide whether to lie to him one last time, and watched her lose.

"She comes due at moonset," she said. "Her soul. Her mother sold it years ago to cover the guild's debts, and moonset tonight is the collection." Her grip tightened on his wrist. "The prophecy wants you down there, Xanthe. It has wanted you down there since before you could walk. Every word on your skin is a trap dressed as a fate. So whatever you're thinking right now — and I know exactly what you're thinking, because you have your grandfather's face and his stupid brave heart — you think it *after* the run. After moonset. Do you hear me?"

Above them, faint through the floorboards, the harbor bell rang the hour. Four hours to moonset.

Xanthe looked down at his own arm, at the fine dark letters still surfacing, and felt the dread settle into something harder and colder and more useful. Because his grandmother had made one mistake, the same mistake the book had made: she'd told him what was being taken, and when.

*Comes down of his own will,* the skin said.

"Well," Xanthe said, reaching for his pack, "then it's lucky I'm so good at doing things of my own will."

His grandmother closed her eyes. "*Xanthe.*"

But he was already counting rope, already checking the lantern, already turning toward the stairs and the woman he loved, whose soul had four hours left in the world above.

Behind him, in the sealed crate, the ash of the burned page stirred once, though there was no draft, and settled into the shape of a road leading down.
