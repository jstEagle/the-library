## 2. What the Guild Sold

The words were still on his skin when Wren came to him.

Xanthe had scrubbed at his forearm with lye and a horsehair brush until the skin went raw and pink, and the ink of the prophecy had only settled deeper, the letters gone from black to something like bruise-purple, as if they'd been there all his life. *XANTHE EASTWICK, THIEF OF THE UNDERCOURT, DIES BENEATH THORNFELL.* Tonight's date sat underneath it like a signature.

He was standing in the drying loft above the harbour house with a crate of unbranded brandy open at his feet — the run he was supposed to be making tonight, before everything, before the ledger — when the door creaked and Wren slipped in, smelling of woodsmoke and river mud, her hedge-witch's satchel banging against her hip.

"You look like a man who's seen a ghost," she said.

"Worse." He turned his arm toward her. "I've seen my own handwriting."

She read it the way she read everything — fast, then slow, then again as if the words might rearrange themselves under attention. Her face did something complicated. He'd have called it fear, except that fear didn't usually come with guilt.

"You knew," he said.

* * *

Three hours earlier, Wren had been kneeling in her mother's kitchen grinding feverfew, and her mother had said, without looking up from the fire, "It comes due tonight."

Wren's hands had stopped. The mortar rocked. A little heap of green spilled across the table like spilt emeralds.

"Say it plain," she said.

"Your soul, girl. The one I sold. Moonset." Her mother poked the fire hard enough to make it spit. "The guild's debts came due the winter you were nine, and I paid them the only way the Undercourt takes payment. You've eaten my bread for sixteen years off the back of it. Don't look at me like that."

"I'm not looking at you any way," Wren said, which was a lie; she was looking at her mother as if she were a stranger wearing her mother's face, which in a sense she had been all along. "I'm going out."

"You'll go to the Eastwick boy." Her mother's voice was flat as slate. "You will not. He can't help. No one above ground can help."

But Wren was already pulling on her boots, because her whole life she had done the opposite of what that voice told her, and it had kept her alive this long.

* * *

Now, in the drying loft, Xanthe listened to Wren tell it — the sale, the debt, the moonset — and felt the floor tilt under a building that had stood four hundred years.

"My parents," he heard himself say. "There's an entry in the ledger about my parents. I thought it was a debt they owed. Coin. Goods."

"It wasn't coin," Wren said quietly.

"No," he said. "It wasn't, was it."

He went down to the cellar. She followed. He took the great ledger down from its chain — the ordinary one, the one every apprentice learned to keep — and instead of turning to the current year he turned back. Back past his own apprenticeship. Back past his grandmother's iron rule. To the year his parents died beneath Thornfell, officially drowned in the estuary, mourned with a stone that had no body under it.

There it was, in his father's neat accounting hand: *Received of the Undercourt: passage and protection, in perpetuity. Paid: two souls, freely surrendered.*

Freely surrendered. His father's handwriting. His father's word for whatever had actually happened in the dark below the mountain.

"There are more," Wren said. She'd taken the other ledger, the sealed one, and was turning its vellum pages with a hedge-witch's care, the way you handle something that might bite. "Look. Page after page. The guild didn't smuggle brandy, Xanthe. Brandy was pocket money. The guild trafficked souls. Forty years of it. Sixty. Every fortune the Eastwicks ever built—"

"—was built on this." He closed the ordinary ledger gently, the way you close a coffin.

His first thought, honestly, shamefully, was flight. There were boats. He knew every creek on the coast; by dawn he could be forty miles away with Wren beside him and the prophecy would simply fail, unwritten, its thief vanished. That was the sensible smuggler's arithmetic. Run, live, lose nothing but honour, and he had never been sure he had any of that to begin with.

Then he looked at Wren, and Wren looked at the entry for her own soul — *Paid: one soul, age nine, freely surrendered by M. Corvane* — and her chin came up in exactly the way it did when she was about to do something reckless and kind.

"They'll take me at moonset whether I walk down or not," she said. "My mother made sure of that. It's written into the bargain. But—" she hesitated, and he saw how much the next words cost her, "—the old laws say a soul can be *redeemed*. Bought back. If someone pays the price."

"What price?"

"No one knows. Everyone who's tried is dead or worse." She smiled, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen, bright and cracked. "So. Sensible option. We run."

"Sensible," he agreed.

They looked at each other over the ledgers, over sixty years of sold souls, over the purple words on his arm.

"The gate behind the salt cellar," he said. "Under the third stair. Grandmother thinks I don't know about it. The roads no one remembers."

"You're choosing descent," Wren said. Not a question. Her hand found his, and her grip was cold and steady.

"I'm choosing you," Xanthe said. "Duty can stand in the queue."

Above them, through the floorboards, the guild bells began to ring the evening hour, and outside the last slice of sun went into the sea. Somewhere beneath Thornfell, he understood now, something old and patient had opened its ledger too, and put a fresh date against a fresh name, and begun to wait for moonset.

The ink on his arm itched. He scratched it, idly, and when he looked at his fingernails they were stained black, and the words had moved — shifted half an inch toward his heart, like something walking in its sleep.

* * *

They went down as the moon rose.

At the mouth of the hidden gate, Wren pressed a sprig of rowan into his hand and kissed him once, hard, like sealing a letter.

"If the mountain offers you anything," she whispered, "anything at all — don't sign."

Too late for that, he almost said. My name is already in the book.

Behind them the tide turned. Ahead, the dark under Thornfell breathed out, cold and mineral and old, and somewhere far below, faint as a moth against glass, he could already hear — or imagined he heard — a small light flickering. Waiting for him.

Waiting to be stolen back.
