## 3. The Roads No One Remembers

The door behind the salt cellar was exactly where the old rhyme said it would be, which was the first thing that frightened him. Guild lore lived in songs, and songs were meant to be sung wrong on purpose so the roads stayed safe. But *three steps past the brine, knock where the wall forgets its face* had opened a seam in the cellar stone without a single lie in it, and Xanthe stood in the dark beyond it holding a lantern he no longer trusted to tell the truth about depth.

The stairs went down the way a throat goes down.

He counted them out of habit — smugglers counted everything, tides and turns and debts — and lost the number somewhere past four hundred, when the walls stopped being cut stone and started being something older, ribbed like the inside of a shell. The air tasted of iron and cold water. Once, far above, he thought he heard his grandmother's voice calling his name, but it was probably just the mountain repeating him to himself, the way it repeated everything.

*Thief,* it whispered back at him, in his own voice, every time his boots rang on the stone.

"I know what I am," he told it.

The mountain laughed, which was worse.

* * *

The first road was called the Meridian, and no living person remembered it, but his hands did. That was the horror of guild blood: you could burn every ledger, drown every elder, and still your fingers knew the knots. His left hand kept making a sign against the dark — thumb over knuckle, twice — that he'd never been taught, or had been taught before he could remember, by someone whose name wasn't in any book because books were the first thing they'd sold.

At the third turning there was a niche with an old lamp in it, and the lamp was lit.

Nobody had come this way in a hundred years. The dust on the road lay thick as flour, unbroken except for one line of small bare footprints leading down, and none coming back. But the lamp burned steady, and beside it sat a wooden token, the kind smugglers used to leave as toll. He turned it over. On the back, scratched crude: *PAID.*

His parents' token. He knew it the way he knew the hand-sign. He put it in his pocket next to the burnt page's ash, which he'd scooped from the grate like an idiot, like a boy keeping a dead bird, and walked on.

* * *

The gatekeeper met him at the fifth mile, where the road forked around a black pool that reflected no lantern light.

It looked, at first, like nothing at all — a tall absence shaped vaguely like a customs officer, which was the most frightening thing Xanthe had ever seen. It wore the guild's old colors, faded to the grey of drowned cloth.

"Name," it said.

"Xanthe Eastwick."

"Trade."

"Thief."

"Good." Something that might have been a mouth moved. "Honesty is rare down here. The last one who came through lied about both and we kept the lies. They're still screaming in the vault, if you'd like to hear them."

"I'd like to pass."

"Everything costs. Even passing. So: a riddle, thief, since you carry a prophecy like a knife in your belt." The gatekeeper drifted closer, and up close it smelled of ledger-ink and pennies. "*What is taken freely that was never owned? What is given back that was never kept? Answer true and pass. Answer false and join the lies.*"

Xanthe stood very still. The pool didn't reflect him. That was the detail his mind kept snagging on — the black water held the lantern, the gatekeeper, the ribbed walls, and where he stood there was only road.

"A soul," he said slowly. "Sold by someone who never owned it. Given back by someone who can't keep it."

The silence went on long enough for him to count his own heartbeats, and lose that number too.

"Close," the gatekeeper said, almost gently. "Wrong, but close. The answer is *love*. But souls are close enough; they travel together." It stepped aside. "Pass, thief. And since you answered nearly true, I'll give you nearly truth for free — the one coin I'm permitted to spend."

"What?"

"The prophecy you carry. The book in your cellar, the page your grandmother burned, the words crawling up your arm right now like ivy." His sleeve felt suddenly hot. "Who do you think wrote it?"

"The guild."

"The guild *kept* it. The first warden wrote it. Every word, every date, every name — mine among them, once." The gatekeeper's voice dropped into something almost like pity. "A warden cannot leave. A warden can only be replaced. So he wrote a beautiful trap: a prophecy of a thief who descends and takes the crown, because power is the bait every thief bites. He has caught nine. Their bones are arranged decoratively past the vault. You'll recognize the craft."

Xanthe's mouth was dry. "And if I don't take it?"

"Then you'll do something no one has done," the gatekeeper said, "and I genuinely do not know what happens, and I have stood at this pool since before your guild had a name." It turned back toward the dark. "I find I am looking forward to finding out. Go. Moonset is not patient, and neither is the vault."

* * *

The last road was not a road. It was a stair of debts.

Each step was a slab of pale stone with a name cut into it, and each name glowed faintly under his boots as he trod it — Harrow Eastwick, paid. Maren Eastwick, paid. Dozens more, whole generations, the guild's fortune spelled out in the currency he now understood. He climbed down his own ancestors like a ladder into a grave, and near the bottom, fresh-cut, edges still sharp:

**WREN OF THE HEDGE. DUE AT MOONSET.**

Below her, one blank slab, waiting for its chisel.

The vault opened off the final landing like a wound. It was smaller than he'd imagined — a low stone room, ceiling close enough to touch, and every surface set with niches, thousands of them, each holding a small flame pressed under glass like a moth pinned in a case. They weren't flames, quite. They flickered the way living things flicker. Some of them fluttered against the glass when he passed, and he made himself not look at their colors, because some of them were the exact gold of eyes he loved.

Wren's niche was center, front, newly fitted, the glass still bright.

Her soul hung inside it like a moth in a jar — small, silver-green, beating itself softly against the barrier over and over, patient the way she was patient, stubborn the way she was stubborn, and when he pressed his palm flat to the glass it stilled, and turned, and he would have sworn on every burned ledger in Thornfell that it *knew* him.

"Wren," he said, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "I'm here. I found the roads. I'm sorry it took me so long."

The flame flared once, hard, and settled.

Behind him, deeper in the vault, something vast shifted its weight — the sound of a ledger opening, of a pen being dipped, of a crown being lifted from its hook in anticipation of a head.

"You came for her," said a voice like closing accounts. "Or you came for what she's worth. Decide quickly, thief. The moon is setting, and the court keeps excellent time."

Xanthe looked at the little silver-green flame, and then at the blank slab with his name waiting to be cut, and understood, finally, what the mountain had been testing all the way down.

Not whether he could steal.

Whether he would *pay*.
