## 4. The Coin the Court Cannot Keep

The crown floated above the ledger like a thing drowned.

It was iron, or something older than iron, and it turned slowly in the dark of the vault as if it had been waiting a century for a head to settle on. Around it, the souls hung in their glass bells — hundreds of them, thousands, each one a small green light pulsing at its own rhythm. Wren's was third from the end of the nearest row, beating fast, the way she breathed when she was frightened and pretending not to be.

*Take her place willingly,* the warden's voice said, from everywhere and nowhere, *and this is yours.*

The first warden stood at the edge of the lamplight — or the shape that had been the first warden, tall and worn thin as a page read too many times. His eyes were ledger-lines. His smile was an entry awaiting ink.

"You came down the roads no one remembers," he said. "You read my book. You followed every word I wrote a hundred years before your birth. You are already mine, Xanthe Eastwick. The prophecy only asks you to finish."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the girl burns out at moonset, and you climb home alone, and the book waits for the next name." He lifted one hand toward the crown. "Or. Take it. Wear it. Keep her soul in a bell where it will never age, never ache, never leave you. Every soul in this mountain becomes yours to command. Every road, every door, every debt. You will be the most powerful thing between Thornfell and the sea."

Moonset was close. I could feel it in my teeth, a pressure like a held breath. Somewhere above us, Wren was sitting on the cold hearthstone of her mother's cottage, waiting for whatever came up out of the dark to collect her.

I thought about running. Let's be honest: I thought about it for three full heartbeats. The guild had taught me every kind of exit, and there were four from this vault, and two of them weren't watched.

But I'd read the ledgers. All of them, last night, by tallow light, while my grandmother wept in the doorway like a woman at a funeral she'd been planning for years. Page after page of names. My parents'. Wren's. Forty-one souls sold to keep a smuggling guild fat and warm through two famines and a war. Every coin in the strongroom upstairs had a person underneath it.

The court hadn't taken those souls. We'd *sold* them. That was the part no prophecy needed to tell me. The trap had never been the mountain. The trap was the ledger, and I was born inside it.

"You want me to take her place," I said. "Willingly. That's the price."

"That is the price," the warden agreed. "Freely offered, freely bound. Love given under duress is worthless to us. Love surrendered is the finest coin there is. It spends forever."

I looked at Wren's soul flickering in its bell. A trapped moth. A hedge-witch who mended bones for farmers and never once asked what anything cost her.

And I understood — not cleverly, not like reading a riddle, but the way you understand a current when you're already in the river — that the warden was telling the truth, and that was the trick. Everything he wanted me to offer was real. The crown was real. The binding would be real. If I knelt and gave myself in Wren's place, I would spend eternity exactly like him: a tall thin shape at the edge of someone else's lamplight, smiling with ledger-lines, waiting for the next name in the book.

He wasn't offering me a choice between power and love. He was offering me a choice between becoming him and becoming nothing. Those were the only entries in his ledger.

So I refused both lines.

"I'm not here to trade," I said.

The vault went very quiet. Even the soul-bells dimmed, listening.

"The court deals in debts," I said. "Debts, bargains, prices. Everything owned, everything owed. But there's one thing you can't hold, because you can't make anyone give it, and you can't take it, and it doesn't transfer." I reached out and put my palm flat against the cold glass over Wren's soul. "Love freely given isn't a coin. It stays with the giver. It can't be spent because it was never for sale."

"It *will* be for sale," the warden said softly, "the moment you offer it in exchange for her."

"No," I said. "Because I'm not offering it in exchange for anything. I'm just giving it. Here. Now. To her, not to you. You're standing in the wrong room to collect."

I didn't have a spell. I didn't have guild lore or prophecy or a drop of witch-blood. What I had was twenty-five years of loving a girl who mended broken wings on the kitchen table, and I did the only thing left in me: I stopped calculating. I stopped weighing exits. For the first time since I found my name in that book, I let go of the outcome entirely — her freedom, my freedom, all of it — and simply loved her, out loud, into the dark, asking nothing back.

The glass cracked like river ice in spring.

The sound ran down the rows — crack, crack, crack — bell after bell splitting along seams no one had ever seen. Green light spilled out across the floor and didn't pool; it *rose*, drifting upward like seeds off a dandelion, and where it passed the ledger-pages blackened and curled. Debts burned. Names unwrote themselves. The first warden made a sound I will hear until I die — not a scream, a *balancing*, the sound of an account closing that had been open for a hundred years — and he thinned, and paled, and became a line of ash on the air, and then not even that.

The crown hit the stone and rang like a dropped pan. It rolled. It stopped at my boot, ordinary now, just rusted iron, worth less than a farthing.

Wren's soul came to rest in my cupped hands, weightless and warm, and then it sank through my palms like water through fingers — going home, going up, going to her.

The mountain began, very quietly, to let go of everything it was holding.

* * *

We climbed out of Thornfell as the sky went grey, then pearl, then the soft bruised gold of a working dawn.

The smugglers' roads were collapsing behind us — gently, without malice, the way a fist unclenches. I felt every passage I'd memorized going dark, the lore dying in my head passage by passage until there was only one road left, the common road, the one anyone could walk. When we came up out of the last cut and onto the moor, I looked back once. Thornfell stood against the sunrise like any other hill. Just a hill.

Wren walked beside me with her shawl pulled tight, and she kept glancing at me sideways the way she did when she was checking whether I was real.

"Say something," she said finally. "You've been silent since the vault."

"I'm trying to work out what I am," I said. "No guild. No prophecy. No inheritance but debts that burned." I flexed my hands, half expecting to see ink rising on my skin again. There was nothing. Clean skin, morning-cold, mine. "I spent my whole life being the last apprentice of something. I don't know how to be the first apprentice of nothing."

"You're not nothing," Wren said. She stopped on the path, and the dawn caught her face, and her eyes were hers — fully, entirely hers, no green flicker, no moth-flicker, just Wren looking at Wren's own future. "You're mine," she said. "And I'm yours. Not traded. Not sold. Given." Her mouth curved. "Turns out that's the one thing nobody can ledger."

She took my hand, and we came down off the moor toward the smoke of the town waking up, guildless and unowned, belonging to each other and to the long ordinary day ahead —

— and pinned to the door of the empty guildhouse, fluttering in the morning wind, was a single sheet of paper. Old paper. Century-old paper, torn cleanly from a sealed book, and on it, in fresh ink, in a hand I knew better than my own, a new date.

Tomorrow's.

And beside it, two names.
