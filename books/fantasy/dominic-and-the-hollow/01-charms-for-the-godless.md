## 1. Charms for the Godless

The night market of Vess began, as it always did, with someone lying about the weather.

"Fair skies tonight," called Old Brannick from his fish stall, though the clouds overhead were the color of wet slate and had been threatening rain since noon. Nobody corrected him. In Vess, optimism was a kind of currency, and everyone was poor enough to spend it freely.

Lior Eastwick set up her stall in the crook between the tallow-chandler's cart and the wall of the old granary, which leaned just enough to give her a sliver of shelter if the sky made good on its threats. She laid out her wares on a scrap of oilcloth: luck-knots braided from red thread and river-grass, fever-wards stitched into squares of undyed linen, a small tray of "blessed" buttons that were blessed only in the sense that she had said something encouraging over them while sewing.

None of it was magic. That was the point. That was the law.

The Purge had ended real magic in Vess forty years before Lior was born — burned it out of the city like damp out of a chimney, or so the temple preachers said every seventh day, loudly and at length. What remained was craft: knots and stitches, herbs and habits, the small domestic superstitions that even the Inquisition's censors had eventually decided weren't worth the candle-oil it would take to hunt them down. A luck-knot couldn't change your fortune. It could only remind you that you'd once hoped to.

Lior made them anyway, and sold them cheap, and told herself every night that this was honest work.

The sigil beneath her left sleeve disagreed. It was inked there in guild-ink that no scrubbing would fade — a small circle broken by a vertical line, the mark of the Hollow Guild — and some nights, when the market lamps guttered low, she could have sworn it warmed against her skin like a cat settling in for attention. She kept it covered. She always kept it covered. The last apprentice of the Hollow Guild knew better than most what happened to people the Inquisition caught wearing the old marks.

"Charm-woman!" A boy of maybe ten skidded around the chandler's cart, all elbows and urgency. "Mistress Ilsa wants you. Her cow's off its feed."

"Tell Mistress Ilsa that cows are not my department," Lior said, "and that if she'd stop naming them all after dead husbands, they might stop pining."

The boy grinned and ran off with the message, and Lior allowed herself a small smile of her own. This was the part of the work she liked — not the charms themselves, but the small economy of being useful. In a city that had outlawed wonder, she dealt in the next best thing: attention. Someone remembered your cow's name. Someone asked after your mother's cough. Someone sewed a knot while listening to your troubles, and by the time the knot was done, half the trouble had worked itself loose just from being spoken aloud.

It wasn't magic. But it wasn't nothing, either.

* * *

Business was steady in the way it always was on a thirdday: slow until the lamplighters finished their rounds, then a rush of dockworkers wanting luck-knots before their shift on the night boats, then the long thin hours when only the desperate came out.

The desperate were Lior's real customers, though she hated thinking of them that way. They came quietly, hats low, and asked for things her wares couldn't honestly promise. A man whose wife had been coughing blood since midwinter bought three fever-wards and paid with coins he must have dug out of his floorboards. A girl who smelled of the tanneries asked whether a charm could make a certain young man write back. Lior sold her a luck-knot and told her, gently, that no charm ever written could make a man pick up a pen, but that she deserved someone who wrote first without being charmed into it. The girl laughed — a surprised, cracked little laugh — and Lior counted that as the best sale of the night.

She was rethreading her needle when the mother arrived.

Lior noticed the shoes first. They were good shoes, or had been — clerk's shoes, maybe a schoolteacher's, resoled twice and polished within an inch of their life. People who wore shoes like that in the night market were people who had somewhere better to be and had run out of places better to go.

The woman stopped at the edge of the oilcloth and looked at the wares the way a drowning woman looks at driftwood: with hope and resentment in equal measure.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. Not a question. Word traveled through Vess's poor quarters faster than fire through a hay barn.

"I'm the knot-and-stitch woman," Lior said. "Charms are a strong word for what I do. I'd hate to oversell."

"My son is sick." The woman's hands were clasped so tightly her knuckles had gone white. "Fever. Four days now. The physician came — the cheap one, from the charity house — and he looked at Tam and said 'nourishing broths' and charged me two days' wages for the looking." Her voice didn't rise; it compressed instead, like coal under weight. "The broths aren't working. He's seven years old and he's so hot he doesn't know me anymore, and the temple says prayer, and I have prayed, mistress, I have prayed until my knees won't hold me, and nothing answers. Nothing has answered for forty years. Everyone knows nothing answers."

Lior's needle stilled.

Because that was true, and everyone in Vess knew it was true, and yet here was a woman standing in front of her saying it out loud in the middle of the night market, where an Inquisitor's ear could be anywhere. You didn't say *nothing answers*. You said *the gods rest*, or *the Purge saved us*, or you said nothing at all and bought a fever-ward and went home.

"What's your name?" Lior asked.

"Maret. Maret Sollow." The woman blinked, thrown by the ordinary question. "Why does it matter?"

"Because I'm going to sell you a fever-ward, Mistress Sollow, and I want you to understand exactly what you're buying." Lior held up one of the linen squares, its stitching neat and unremarkable. "This is thread and cloth. I've stitched it in the old pattern, the pattern my teacher taught me, and the pattern is meant to give a sick person something to hold onto. Something warm. Something that says *someone tried*. It will not break your son's fever. If anyone tells you otherwise, they're selling you something worse than cloth — they're selling you false hope, and false hope costs more than it looks."

Maret Sollow stared at her. Then, horribly, her eyes filled.

"That's it, then," she whispered. "That's everything. There's nothing else. I've been to everyone and there's nothing else, and you're telling me the truth, and the truth is nothing." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I don't want truth. I want my boy."

The market noise went on around them — Brannick hawking his fish, the lamplighters' poles tapping, a fiddler tuning up two stalls away — but for Lior the sounds seemed to come from very far away, through water.

Here was the thing Lior never said aloud, the thing she barely let herself think: the Hollow Guild hadn't only done knots and stitches. Once — before the Purge, before the burning, before Aldous had hidden her in a root cellar with a bag of bread and a sigil freshly inked on a nine-year-old's arm — the guild had done *work*. Real work. Fever-wards that actually turned fevers. Luck that actually bent. And Aldous, who had taught her the patterns, had also taught her the rule that governed all of them:

*A charm is a question. Magic is whatever answers.*

And nothing had answered in forty years. Nothing answered anywhere. The gods were ash, the Door was sealed, the guild was a name on a list of traitors, and Lior Eastwick sold thread and cloth and called it honesty because the alternative was to admit she was the last ember of a fire everyone else had agreed was out.

"Two coppers," she heard herself say. "For the ward. And—" Her mouth kept moving without consulting the sensible part of her brain. "And I'll stitch your name into the border. Yours and Tam's. It's an old custom. It means the ward knows who it's for."

"It doesn't do anything," Maret said flatly.

"No. It doesn't do anything."

Maret put four coppers on the oilcloth anyway — double the price, her chin up, refusing charity the way only the truly broke can refuse it — and stood there shaking slightly while Lior threaded fresh silk and stitched two names into the linen border in the small looping script of the Hollow Guild. *Maret. Tam.* The needle moved. The lamplight flickered. Somewhere behind her ribs, beneath the sleeve that covered the sigil, Lior felt a warmth she decided firmly was her own imagination.

"There," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Hold it near him. Not on his skin — near. Tell him stories. Broths, too. The physician was right about the broths, even if he charged you like an emperor for knowing it."

Maret took the ward in both hands, the way you take something breakable, and looked at Lior for a long moment.

"You have kind hands," she said finally. "My grandmother used to say you can tell a liar by the hands. Liars keep them busy." She tucked the ward into her shawl, against her heart, and walked away into the market crowd without looking back.

Lior sat very still for a moment.

Then she packed up her tray of blessed buttons, pulled her sleeve down over the sigil, and told herself, in the firm internal voice she used for such occasions, that she had done nothing wrong, sold nothing but cloth, promised nothing but company, and that the strange tight feeling in her chest was simply hunger, because she hadn't eaten since morning.

* * *

She was wrong, of course. She found out how wrong at a little past midnight, when the market was packing down and the fiddler had gone home and the clouds had finally made good on their threats, sending down a fine cold drizzle that turned the cobbles slick and dark.

Lior was folding her oilcloth when she heard running.

Not the ordinary running of the night market — the dockworkers sprinting for the last boat, children chasing each other between stalls. This was the other kind, the ragged, sobbing kind, and it resolved out of the drizzle into Maret Sollow, shoes ruined, shawl soaked, holding the linen ward out in both hands like an accusation or a miracle.

"You lied to me," Maret gasped, stopping in front of the stall. Water ran off her chin. Her eyes were enormous. "You looked me in the face and you lied."

Lior's stomach dropped through the cobblestones and kept going. *They've arrested her. They've taken the boy. They've traced the stitching—*

"Mistress Sollow—"

"The fever broke." Maret's voice cracked clean down the middle. "An hour ago. An hour after I hung the ward above his bed. He was burning — I've watched fevers kill before, I know what the end looks like, I was sitting there deciding which of us would watch the other die — and then he sighed. Like a child falling asleep on a summer day. Just sighed, and the heat went out of him like a banked fire, and he opened his eyes and asked for bread." She thrust the ward forward. It was dry. In the drizzle, in the soaking rain, the linen square was perfectly, impossibly dry. "You told me it was thread and cloth. You told me it does *nothing*."

Lior took the ward. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, everything was ringing like a struck bell.

The stitching was unchanged. The names were unchanged. And yet the linen hummed faintly against her fingers, the way a plucked string hums after the note is done, and beneath her sleeve the sigil had gone from warm to hot — hot as a hand clamping over her wrist, hot as attention, hot as an answer.

*A charm is a question,* Aldous had said, forty years dead and eleven years gone. *Magic is whatever answers.*

Nothing had answered in forty years.

"Say it again," Lior heard herself whisper. "What you just said. About the fever."

"Broke. It broke. He's eating, mistress, he ate a whole crust and complained about the butter, he *complained*, thank all the—" Maret stopped. Blinked. "Thank whoever. Thank *something*." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Something answered. Didn't it. After forty years of nothing — something answered *my* boy."

The drizzle fell. The last stall-lamps guttered. Somewhere across the dark market, Old Brannick was lying to someone about tomorrow's weather, fair skies, fair skies, and Lior Eastwick stood holding a dry square of linen in the rain and felt the ground of her whole careful, hidden, honest little life tilt beneath her like a deck in a storm.

Because she had stitched that ward herself, and she knew — she *knew*, down to the last knot — that there was nothing in it. No guild-work, no craft beyond comfort, no power she had touched since the night the soldiers came to the guildhall and the world agreed to forget. She had made a hundred wards exactly like it. She had sold one that evening to a tannery girl with a hopeless heart, and that one was doing exactly what it should: nothing, kindly.

But this one had been stitched for a named child, by the last apprentice of the Hollow Guild, in the hour when a mother said aloud, in a public street, that nothing answers anymore.

And something had heard.

"Mistress?" Maret was watching her face. "You've gone white as your own linen. Is something wrong? Should the ward not have—"

"No," Lior said quickly. Too quickly. "No, it's — I'm glad. Glad is the word. I'm glad for Tam." She pressed the ward back into Maret's hands and closed the woman's fingers over it. "Keep it. Keep it safe, and keep it quiet — you understand me? Quiet. Don't tell the physician. Don't tell the temple. Don't tell anyone at all, especially not anyone in a grey coat."

Maret frowned. "The Inquisition? Why would I tell the Inquisition my son's fever broke?"

"Because in Vess," Lior said, and heard how strange her own voice sounded, "miracles are contraband."

Maret left, slower than she'd come, glancing back twice. Lior stood alone in the emptying market with the rain coming down and the sigil burning under her sleeve like a second heartbeat, and did the arithmetic she had spent eleven years avoiding.

Forty years of silence. Forty years of thread and cloth and honest nothing. And tonight — tonight of all nights, in her hands, from her stitching — an answer.

Either the world had changed while she wasn't looking.

Or it had been waiting for her specifically, the way a trap waits, patient and hungry, for the last foot to fall.

Far off toward the temple district, a bell began to ring. Not the hour bell — the alarm. One toll, two, three, urgent and thin through the rain, and voices rising in the dark streets where it came from, and a smell on the wind that didn't belong to any kitchen in Vess.

Smoke.

Lior pulled her sleeve down hard over the sigil, grabbed her basket, and ran toward the light.
