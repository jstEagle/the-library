## 2. The Law of Ash

The Inquisitor came to the night market on the first cold morning of autumn, when the braziers were lit and the luck-knots hung heavy with dew.

Lior knew before she saw her. The market changed around an Inquisitor the way water changed around a dropped stone — ripples moving outward through the stalls, vendors going quiet in a wave, the fishmonger's wife suddenly finding urgent business with her scales. Someone at the far end of Tanner's Row had spotted the grey cloaks and the word traveled faster than any charm Lior could sell: *Vane. Vane is here.*

She kept her hands steady and re-tied the knot she was working on. A fever-ward, small, honest work — three cords, seven turns, no guild sigil anywhere near it. She'd learned to braid without the sigil years ago, learned to let her fingers do the old patterns while her mind counted something else entirely, like the number of exits from the market or the price of bread.

*Four exits,* she thought now. *Bread's up another copper.*

"Inquisitor's burning again," said Old Nettie from the next stall over, not looking up from her pile of dried yarrow. Nettie was seventy if she was a day, sold nothing but tea and gossip, and had survived every Purge sweep since Lior was in swaddling clothes by the simple method of being too poor to bother with and too sharp to be caught.

"I heard," Lior said.

"You heard nothing. You're braiding."

"I'm braiding *and* hearing."

Nettie snorted. "Then hear this: she took Marlo's whole tray last week. Every ward he had. Made him watch." The old woman's hands paused over her yarrow for just a heartbeat — the only tell she ever allowed herself. "Marlo cried, you know. Big man like that."

Lior's fingers went still on the cord.

She knew Marlo's wards. Half of them were patterned off Aldous's work, the way half the market's work was, because Aldous Eastwick had been generous with his patterns back when generosity wasn't a crime, and the market had soaked them up like rain into dry ground. When you burned Marlo's wards, you were burning echoes of her master's hands.

*When you burn anything down here,* she thought, *you're probably burning him.*

The grey cloaks came into view.

There were six of them, walking in formation down the center lane of the market like they owned the cobblestones, which in every way that mattered, they did. Four were just acolytes — young, nervous, carrying iron pails of lamp-oil and long tongs. But at their head walked Sarel Vane, and the market didn't just quiet around her; it seemed to hold its breath.

She was younger than Lior had expected, the first time she'd seen her. Younger than Aldous would have been now, certainly. Tall, straight-backed, dark hair pinned with severe precision under a hood the color of wet slate. She carried a ledger under one arm — a fat, bound thing with brass corners — and she carried it the way other people carried weapons. Her face was not cruel. That was the worst part, Lior had decided. Cruelty you could hate cleanly. Sarel Vane's face was the face of someone doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic always came out the same way: everything burns.

"Morning," Vane said to the fishmonger's wife, pleasantly enough, as though greeting a neighbor about a fence. "New stock since the last inspection?"

"No, Honored One. Same as ever. Fish."

"Fish," Vane agreed, and moved on.

* * *

They worked the row methodically. That was the thing people got wrong about the Inquisition, Lior thought, watching from behind her stall with her heart tapping against her ribs like a moth against glass. People imagined raids as chaos — doors kicked in, screaming, fire loose in the streets. But Vane did it like an accountant doing inventory. Stall by stall. Seller by seller. Questions asked softly, answers written in the ledger, judgments made with the unhurried certainty of a woman who had never once doubted what the numbers added up to.

At Tessin's apothecary cart she stopped longest.

Tessin was legal. Licensed, stamped, paid up, his permits hanging from the cart in a laminated fan of official seals. He sold willow bark and honey salves and cough syrup, and if he sometimes slipped a luck-charm into the paper wrapping of a regular customer, well, that was between him and whatever gods weren't listening anymore.

"Master Tessin." Vane consulted her ledger. "Your license is current."

"It is, Honored One."

"Your sales records?"

"Filed quarterly. As required."

"And yet." She turned a page. Lior couldn't hear it from where she stood, but she could see the ledger open in Vane's hands, could see the neat columns of ink, and she thought: *she remembers everything. She keeps accounts of all of us.* "A customer reported purchasing from you a knotted cord. Red thread. Three strands."

"That would have been a package tie, Honored One."

"A package tie."

"I'm told they're traditional."

Something flickered at the corner of Vane's mouth. It might have been the ghost of amusement; it might have been indigestion. "Show me your ties, Master Tessin."

He showed her his ties. Plain hemp, every one. And Lior, watching, felt a small hot coal of admiration settle in her chest, because Tessin had burned or hidden every red thread in his stock two days ago — she knew because she'd seen him do it — and now he stood there presenting hemp string to an Inquisitor with the serene face of a man who had never tied anything more complicated than his own boots.

Vane looked at the hemp for a long moment. Then she closed the ledger.

"The law requires me to inform you," she said, "that possession of guild-patterned craftwork carries a sentence of branding. Sale of such work carries the collar. I trust your package ties remain... traditional."

"They're very boring, Honored One."

"Mm." She turned away, then paused. "How is your daughter? Recovering from the winter fever, I hope."

Lior saw it hit Tessin like a fist he refused to flinch from. His smile stayed exactly where it was. "Growing like a weed, Honored One. Thank you for asking."

"Good health to her." Vane moved on.

Lior's hands had gone cold around her braid. Because that hadn't been a question about a child. That had been a ledger entry with legs — *I know what you love, and I can find it.* No cruelty in it. Just arithmetic.

* * *

They reached the burning ground at midmorning.

It was a square of flagstones behind the old tithe-house, swept clean, with an iron brazier big enough to roast an ox. Confiscated goods came there to die: charms and knots, prayer-flags and painted stones, bundles of dried herbs cut in the old patterns, children's rattles with hollow cores full of grain blessed by nobody living. The acolytes piled it all with tongs while the sellers whose goods these had been stood at the rope line and watched.

Lior stood among them. She hadn't been able to make herself leave.

She recognized pieces in the pile. That was the trap of standing there, and some part of her knew it was a trap and stood anyway, because grief is stupid and loyal. There was a fever-ward with seven turns done in blue cord — Aldous's pattern, Aldous's exact tension, probably sold by Marlo. There was a set of divination bones in a worn leather cup, and she knew that cup, she had drunk soup out of that cup when she was nine years old because Aldous said the leather gave the broth character. There was a luck-knot so badly made it could only have been a child's practice piece, all lumps and crossed threads, and something in Lior's chest folded in on itself at the sight of it.

And there, near the top of the pile, laid carefully by some acolyte who hadn't known what he was handling:

Her master's tools.

His knotting board, oak, worn smooth as river stone by forty years of hands. His bone needles in their roll of faded green cloth. His measuring cord, knotted at intervals only guild-trained fingers could read. The little brass balance he'd used to weigh out charm-components, dented on one pan because Lior, age eleven, had dropped it and lied about it and finally confessed through tears, and Aldous had laughed until he wheezed and said the dent improved the accuracy, which was nonsense, and she had believed him for a year.

Someone had taken these from his workshop after the raid. After the arrest. They should have been destroyed then, but things slip through inventories, things get pocketed and pawned and forgotten, and somehow, impossibly, his tools had made their way through eight years of the Purge to end up here, in a pile, waiting for oil and flame, and Lior could not move and could not look away.

"Step back from the rope, please," said one of the acolytes, not unkindly.

She stepped back.

Sarel Vane stood beside the brazier with her ledger open. This was the part Lior had heard described by others who'd watched previous burnings: the reading. Vane read out the charges against each item in a clear, level voice, like a priest reciting a litany, like a clerk reading names off a manifest. Not mockery. Worse than mockery — ceremony. The law wanted its destructions to mean something.

"Confiscated lot forty-one," Vane read. "Assorted knotted cords, guild pattern, count thirty-two. Charged under Statute Nine: unauthorized invocation. Sentence: fire."

Oil went into the brazier. The acolytes began feeding the pile in with the tongs.

The luck-knots went first, catching fast, thread and cord flashing to nothing. Then the herbs, which went up in a sweet grey smoke that made several watchers cough and made Lior think, absurdly, of Aldous's kitchen, of rosemary on roasted turnips, of ordinary mornings in a house that no longer existed.

Then the tools.

She watched the green cloth roll catch. Watched the bone needles blacken. Watched the knotting board — his board, his smooth-worn, patient board that had held every lesson of her childhood — begin to char along its edges, flame crawling up the oak like ivy in fast-forward, and the heat of the brazier reached her face and her eyes burned and she told herself it was the smoke.

*You're watching him die again,* said some cold clear voice in her. *This is just the second funeral. You missed the first.*

The brass balance didn't burn. Brass doesn't burn. It sat in the flames glowing dull orange, the dent visible even through the shimmer of heat, and when the acolyte raked the ashes at the end it tipped out of the wreckage and rolled across the flagstones and stopped against the toe of Sarel Vane's boot.

Everything in the square went quiet.

Vane looked down at it. She bent, picked it up with two fingers, and examined it — turning it over, thumb brushing the dent — and for a moment, one strange suspended moment, she held a dead man's tool in her hand and the ledger was closed and her face did something Lior had never seen it do.

It hesitated.

Then the moment passed, the way moments do, and Vane placed the balance on the flagstones, raised her boot, and brought her heel down. Once. Twice. The brass pan crumpled with a sound like a struck coin, small and bright and final.

"Lot forty-one," she said to the acolyte holding the ledger-pen, "is concluded. Note it."

*Note it.* As if the law needed a receipt for killing a memory.

Lior turned and walked away before her face could betray her, and behind her she heard Vane begin the next litany, calm as morning: "Confiscated lot forty-two. Painted prayer-stones, count sixty..."

* * *

She made it four stalls before Nettie caught her arm.

The old woman pressed a tin cup into her hands. Chamomile, from the smell of it. "Drink. You've gone the color of tallow."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine the way a wet roof is fine. Drink."

Lior drank. The tea was terrible, over-steeped and bitter, and it was exactly what she needed, and she hated how well Nettie read her.

"He had a board," Lior said, before she could stop herself. "Oak. He taught me on it. I watched it burn just now and I —" Her voice snagged. She took another sip to cover it. "Eight years. Eight years I've been careful. Sigil covered, patterns disguised, selling charms so weak they barely qualify as superstition, and today I stood there and realized it doesn't matter how careful I am. She'll find it. All of it. Everything he left in the world, she'll feed to that brazier one lot at a time and *note it*, and the law will call that justice."

Nettie said nothing for a while. Around them the market was trying to resume itself, voices climbing cautiously back to normal volume, the fishwife hawking again, life doing what life does around fresh graves.

"You want my advice?" Nettie said at last. "No, you don't, but here it is. Hate her if you must, girl, but hate her accurately."

"Accurately?"

"She's not the fire. She's the hand that holds the match. There's a difference, and someday the difference might matter." Nettie took back the empty cup and squinted down the row toward the tithe-house, where Vane and her grey cloaks had moved on to questioning the candle-seller. "That one reads her ledger like scripture. And I've known scripture-readers my whole long life, and the dangerous ones aren't the ones who believe hard. The dangerous ones are the ones who start noticing what the book leaves out."

"That sounds dangerously close to sympathy, Nettie."

"That sounds like seventy years of watching people," Nettie said. "Sympathy's for saints. I'm talking strategy." She shuffled back toward her yarrow, then paused. "Oh — and the widow Anthea was asking after you earlier. Says her boy's fever broke in the night. Whole fever, gone by dawn. Wants to thank whoever made the charm she bought."

Lior felt the world tilt slightly, very gently, like a boat meeting a swell.

"She bought the fever-ward," Lior said slowly. "The one from Tuesday. Blue cord."

"That's the one. Seven turns, wasn't it?" Nettie frowned. "Funny thing, though. She says it worked too well. Boy's healthy as a spring lamb — but he woke up talking nonsense, she says. Some gibberish tongue, won't stop chattering in it. Doctor can't place it." The old woman shrugged. "Fever does odd things to heads. Probably nothing."

*Probably nothing,* Lior thought.

But her hands had gone cold around the memory of that charm — the one she'd made tired, at the end of a long night, the one that had come out of her fingers smoother and truer than anything she'd made in years, the one that shouldn't have worked, that had no business working, that she had sold cheap and thought about twice since.

Because she knew a language no doctor in Vess could place.

She'd known it since she was six years old. It was the language Aldous had prayed in, alone, behind the sealed inner door of the guildhall, thinking she couldn't hear.

Old Hollowtongue.

And somewhere in the widow Anthea's house, a child who had never heard a word of it was speaking it fluently, and the god under Vess had just answered its first prayer in eight years, and it had used *her* charm to do it.

Lior set down Nettie's cup with a hand that almost didn't shake.

"I have to go somewhere," she said.

"Now? Market's barely started—"

"Family emergency," Lior said, already gathering her satchel, already calculating — not exits from the market this time, but streets, routes, the fastest path across the city to a house where a fever-broken boy was chattering in the language of the dead. "I need to check on a patient."

Nettie looked at her for a long moment, seventy years of watching people narrowed into two sharp eyes.

"You be careful," she said. "Grey cloaks are still walking the rows."

"They are," Lior agreed, pulling her sleeve down over the sigil scarred faint into her wrist, feeling the fear in her chest curdle into something hotter, harder, more useful. "So I'd better be quick."
