## 3. What the Mother Prayed For

The knock came at the third hour past dawn, which was suspicious in itself. Nobody knocked on Lior's door at a reasonable hour unless they wanted something unreasonable, and nobody knocked at all unless they'd already tried shouting.

She had been asleep on her cot with her boots on — a habit Aldous had called "the posture of a man expecting to be robbed" and she called "efficiency" — so she was upright before the second knock finished, one hand already closing around the iron poker that served as her weapon of choice, her alarm system, and occasionally her back-scratcher.

"Lady Eastwick?" said the door.

Nobody had ever called her *lady* in her life. She opened it.

The mother from the night market stood in the stairwell of Lior's tenement, holding a covered basket and wearing an expression Lior had learned to dread more than any Inquisitor's badge: gratitude.

"Mistress," Lior said carefully. "You shouldn't be here."

"I know." The woman — Sella, her name was Sella, Lior remembered now, Sella Broadstairs, washerwoman, third landing up in the cooper's row — stepped inside anyway, because grateful people always did. "I waited two days to be sure. I watched the street. There's no graycoats."

"There are never graycoats until there are," Lior muttered, but she shut the door and threw the bolt, because she was a hypocrite and also curious, and curiosity had been killing her slowly for three days like a cat with principles.

Sella set the basket on Lior's only table, which was also her only chair, which was also where she dried her socks. She lifted the cloth. Inside: half a loaf of good bread, still warm. A twist of salt. Six eggs. A jar of honey with a wax seal, real honey, not the chalk-and-clover substitute sold at the corner.

Lior looked at it the way a soldier looks at a gift horse with a saddle full of dynamite.

"That's a week of wages," she said.

"It's nothing," Sella said. "It's less than nothing. My boy is alive."

And there it was. Lior sat down on her cot because her knees had opinions.

"Tell me," she said.

"He slept," Sella said. "That was the first thing. He hadn't slept in four days — just burned and thrashed and cried, and then he went quiet, which frightened me worse, and I thought—" Her hands did something complicated in the air, a gesture that needed no translation. "I put your charm under his pillow and I prayed. Not to anything. Just *please*, into the dark, the way you do when you've got nothing left. And he slept. All night. And in the morning the fever was gone. Gone, mistress. Not broken, not easing. *Gone*, like it had never been invited."

Lior nodded slowly, the way you nod at a story about a dog that sang. Charming. Impossible. Keep smiling until you can get to a window.

"Fever-wards do that sometimes," she said. "The body turns. It looks like a miracle and it's really just—"

"He talks," Sella said.

Lior stopped nodding.

"Wakes up talking," Sella went on, and her voice had changed now, the gratitude thinning out over something underneath it, the way ice thins over a river. "Not fever-talk. He's clear-eyed, eating, strong as a goat. But the words aren't words. It's a language, mistress. Whole sentences. He gets angry when we don't answer him right. He asked my husband a question yesterday and when Tam couldn't understand, Wren—" she swallowed, "*Wren* got frustrated with *him*. Like a scholar with a slow pupil. My son is six years old."

"What kind of words?" Lior asked. Her mouth had gone dry. "Say some. Say what he says."

Sella frowned, working at it. "It's all... round sounds. Old-sounding. Like church talk, but not the temple's talk. There's one bit he says over and over, morning and night, like a grace before meals." She closed her eyes, and haltingly, syllable by careful syllable, she gave it back:

"*Hal ven dorren iss. Hal ven dorren iss.*"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog started barking at a cart, and the sound seemed to come from another country.

Lior knew the words. Of course she knew the words. She'd known them since she was seven years old, kneeling on cold stone while Aldous paced the length of the guildhall chapel with his hands behind his back, making her repeat the liturgy until her voice cracked: *hal ven dorren iss* — *let the door remember us*. The opening line of the Hollow Guild's binding hymn. A language with maybe four hundred living speakers a decade ago, and every one of them either dead, scattered, or very carefully pretending otherwise.

A six-year-old washerwoman's son should not know it. No one should know it. It was supposed to be ash and memory, like the guildhall, like Aldous, like the version of the world where Lior had a family and a trade worth the name.

"Mistress?" Sella said. "You've gone white as milk."

"I'm fine," Lior lied, with the fluency of long practice. "Eat — no, wait, don't eat, sit — no, actually, you should go."

"You have to help us." Sella leaned forward, and now Lior saw the other thing underneath the gratitude, the thing that had made her climb four flights to a charm-seller's garret at dawn: fear. "People are saying things. Tam's mother says he's *choked by a spirit*. The temple deacon came by yesterday, just to look at Wren, just to look, and he wrote something in a little book. Mistress, if the deacon writes to the Inquisition—"

"He won't have to," Lior said grimly. "They'll come on their own. Answered prayers leave marks."

"Marks?"

"Holes," Lior said. "Burned hollows. You'll see. Or you won't, if you're lucky." She pressed the basket back toward Sella. "Take this home. Feed it to your boy. And listen to me very carefully: whatever he says in that language, write it down. Every word. Don't correct him, don't teach him temple prayers, don't let anyone else hear more than they must. Can you do that?"

"Why? What is it? What did you *sell* me?"

Nothing, Lior wanted to say. That's the whole horror of it. I sold you string and knotwork and a mumbled blessing I barely believed in, and something out there in the dark heard you anyway, and answered, and the bill hasn't even arrived yet.

"A luck-knot," she said instead. "Fever-ward. Standard work." She managed a smile, and it must have been a decent one, because Sella gathered up the basket, thanked her twice more, and left — though at the door she paused and said, quietly, "You knew him. The way you said those words back just now, in your head. You knew them."

Lior didn't answer, and after a moment the stairs took the woman away, footsteps fading down through the building's bones.

Then Lior sat alone with the honey she'd refused and the words she couldn't refuse, and did the arithmetic of catastrophe.

*Hal ven dorren iss.*

Three days ago a fever-ward had worked that shouldn't have worked. Last night, according to the market gossip she'd pretended not to collect, old Cobb Tanner's prayer for rain had been answered with a downpour that flooded his own cellar — and the cellar wall, when he'd gone down with a lamp, had a scorched hollow in it shaped like nothing at all and exactly like a doorway. Two days ago, a dockhand's prayer for his brother lost at sea had been answered with the brother walking out of the surf, alive, dry, and unable to say where he'd been. The city called it coincidence, or contraband charms, or the start of a new smuggling ring. Vess always preferred crime to miracles; crime could be taxed and arrested.

But Lior had grown up on the other side of that particular wall of silence. She knew what it meant when the gods started answering.

It meant they were awake.

And it meant someone was paying for every answer, because that was the oldest law her guild had kept, older than its oaths, older than its seals: *nothing is given; everything is moved*. A prayer answered here was a hole burned there. The gods didn't create. They only carried things across, and the crossing left scars.

She'd felt it, too. She hadn't told anyone — there was no one to tell — but for three nights running she'd woken with an ache behind her ribs, deep and specific, like a fishhook set in the meat under her sternum. Each dawn it was a little worse. She'd told herself it was the damp, the cheap bread, the onset of the melancholy appropriate to her station.

It wasn't the damp. It was the hook. Something had hold of her, or of what she was, and was reeling.

"Oh, Aldous," she said aloud, to the empty room, because talking to dead masters was only slightly less dignified than answering them. "What did you people *do*?"

The room, being a room, said nothing. But the ache behind her ribs pulsed once, gently, almost companionably, and Lior decided not to think about that.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best, which was pretending to be harmless in public while gathering information like a magpie with a grudge.

The night market didn't open till dusk, but the sellers gathered early in the tallow-lit warrens behind the fishmongers' row, and gossip moved faster than goods. Lior bought a cup of hot spiced wine she couldn't afford and stood at the edge of Brannick's stall, listening.

"—flooded clean to the second step, I'm telling you, and the hole in the wall big enough to put your fist in, edges smooth as glass—"

"—my cousin's wife prayed for her mother's hip and the mother danced at the wedding, *danced*, and now the family won't let her near the hearth—"

"—graycoats were at the Tanner house this morning. Took the burnt brick away in a box. A *box*, like relics—"

"They'd love that," Brannick said darkly. He was a big, sad-eyed man who sold warding-ribbons of honest uselessness, and he was one of perhaps three people in Vess who suspected what Lior was and had decided, charitably, not to know it. "Give the Inquisition a holy hole and they'll build a doctrine on it by spring. Burn a few sellers for kindling. Business as usual."

"Business is usual," Lior said. "That's the problem."

Brannick glanced at her. "You look like a woman doing sums she doesn't like."

"I'm always doing sums I don't like. It's the whole trade." She wrapped both hands around the wine cup. "Brannick. Hypothetically. If a child woke up speaking a language nobody knew—"

"Temple would call it possession. Inquisition would call it evidence." He shrugged his enormous shoulders. "Either way the child doesn't win. Why? Whose child?"

"No one's. Hypothetical child."

"Your hypotheticals have faces lately," Brannick said, not unkindly. "Watch yourself, little sister. Whatever's loose in this city, it's pulling threads, and threads pull back."

Lior was still turning that over — *little sister*, a market nickname she tolerated because correcting it would require explanations — when she felt the hook behind her ribs pull.

Not metaphorically. Actually pull. A short, sharp tug, like a line going taut, and across the market she heard a woman cry out — joy, at first, high and bright, someone's prayer answered, someone's luck turned — and beneath the joy, from somewhere in the walls themselves, a low groan of stone taking heat. Everyone heard it. Everyone went quiet, the way animals go quiet, and then everyone pretended they hadn't, and the market noise resumed at a slightly higher pitch of fear.

Lior set down the untouched wine and walked home fast, one hand pressed flat against her sternum like a woman holding a door shut.

* * *

Night. Candle stub. The flat of her blade across her knees, and on the tabletop, a sheet of cheap paper she'd bought from the stationer who didn't ask questions because his prices depended on not asking them.

She was writing down everything she remembered of Old Hollowtongue. It wasn't much. Liturgies, mostly — the guild had guarded its tongue the way it guarded everything, jealously and pointlessly. She'd been taught the hymns, the oath-formulas, the names-of-address used in sealing-work. Aldous had drilled her on grammar for two years and she'd hated every minute, and now she sat reconstructing it like a woman counting coins after a fire, furious at herself for every word she'd let fall out of her head.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said to Sella, the thing she could barely say to herself:

Children didn't learn languages in fevers. And the guild's liturgical tongue wasn't just a language. It was a *handle*. The old texts said the Hollowtongue was the speech the gods understood before there were temples — the first grammar of asking. The guild had used it to bind, to seal, to command. It was not a thing you spoke. It was a thing you *did*.

So either Wren Broadstairs had somehow absorbed four hundred years of forbidden liturgy through a pillow stuffed with knotwork —

— or something was using his mouth. Something that knew the old words better than any living speaker. Something that had reached through a six-year-old's fever because a washerwoman prayed into the dark with nothing left, and the dark, for the first time in a generation, had answered.

*Let the door remember us,* the hymn began.

Lior put down the knife, pinched out the candle, and sat in the dark with her hand over the ache in her ribs, which pulsed under her palm like a second heartbeat, patient and enormous and waiting.

There was one place in Vess where the answers might be. One place she had sworn, at nineteen, at a funeral, over a grave with no body in it, never to enter again.

The guildhall was sealed. It had been sealed for eleven years — by the Inquisition's order, with wax and lead and posted writs, but also, she'd always suspected, by something older and less official. Nobody squatted there. Nobody even looted it, and in Vess that was a miracle with no prayer attached.

She'd walked past it maybe a thousand times and never once looked directly at the door, the way you don't look at the sun or at grief.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I'll walk past it. Tomorrow I'll decide.

Behind her ribs, the hook pulled again — gentle, insistent, unmistakably a summons — and from the direction of the river, faint but perfectly clear, a child's voice rose in a hymn Lior had last heard sung by the dead.

She was moving for her coat before the second verse ended.
