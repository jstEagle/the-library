## 4. Voices in the Sealed Room

The guildhall on Chandler's Row had been empty for nine years, which in Vess meant it had been picked clean twice, condemned once, and was currently owned by nobody who wanted to admit it. The city had a way of swallowing buildings whole. It swallowed people faster.

Lior stood across the street in the shadow of a tallow-chandler's awning, hood up, hands jammed in her pockets, and had a long argument with herself.

*Go home,* said the sensible part of her, the part that kept her alive on charm-money and caution. *You have three luck-knots to finish, a landlady who wants rent, and a face that Inquisitors sketch onto paper.*

*But he answered her,* said the other part. *The boy prayed and something answered, and it came through you, and it spoke Hollowtongue.*

She had spent two days trying not to think about it, which is to say she had thought about nothing else. She had laid out the fever-ward she'd sold the mother — a copy of it, made from memory — on her table forty times. It was bad work. She knew it was bad work. Three strands of cheap cord, a knot pattern she'd learned at eleven, no true binding, no guild sigil burned into it because burning the sigil took heat and heat drew eyes. It shouldn't have worked. Charms like that didn't work. Charms like that were honest lies, comfort shaped like cordage, and Lior had built six years of survival on the difference between a lie that soothed and a spell that did anything at all.

Except this one had cured a boy's fever and left him speaking the liturgical tongue of a guild that officially did not exist, in a city where magic had been banned since before the boy was born.

So here she was, arguing with herself in front of a dead building, losing.

"Fine," she muttered, and crossed the street before the sensible part could file a formal objection.

The guildhall's front door had been boarded, and the boards nailed, and the nails rusted, which told her everything about how much the Inquisition actually cared anymore. They cared about sellers in markets. They cared about smoke and rumor. They did not care about a hollow building where nothing had happened for nine years. Nobody guarded what they believed was already dead.

Lior went around the back, through the gap between the hall and the cooper's yard where she used to squeeze through as an apprentice when she was late for evening lessons, and found — absurdly — that she still fit. Barely. She left a strip of her coat behind as a toll and dropped into the old wash-yard, where the rain barrel had rotted into a ring of black staves and the herb beds Aldous had tended were a wilderness of nettle and bindweed.

Something in her chest folded up small at the sight of those beds.

Inside, the entry hall still smelled faintly of ash and tallow and, beneath both, of the particular dust of a place that had been loved and then abandoned. The Inquisition had stripped it, of course. The great workbenches were gone, the charm-racks gone, the copper bowls gone — melted down or locked in some confiscated vault, catalogued by men who thought a fever-ward was a knife. But they hadn't taken the floors, or the walls, or the staircase with its worn hollows in each tread where four generations of apprentices had climbed.

Lior put her hand on the banister and stood very still until she could breathe again.

*You came all this way,* she told herself sternly. *Don't fall apart in the doorway.*

She climbed.

* * *

The upper floor was where the guild had lived, and living leaves traces that confiscation can't inventory. A candle stub fused to a shelf. A mug with someone's teeth marks in the rim. On the wall of what had been the apprentices' room, faint charcoal ghosts remained where the whitewash had been patched over them instead of scrubbed: a drawing of a dog, a lopsided sun, and — Lior touched it with one finger — the tally marks of a card game she had lost catastrophically at age thirteen, in which the stakes had been dishwashing duty for a month and Aldous had laughed until he coughed.

"You're being sentimental," she informed the empty room. "He sold you out to no one, taught you badly, and died of a fever that a real charm might have—"

She stopped. Her own voice sounded wrong in there. Not loud. Just present, in a place where presence had been missing a long time.

The sealed door was at the end of the corridor.

It had always been sealed, even before the Purge. The inner sanctum — the Hollow, the older members called it, though never in plain speech — was behind it: a room the guild's founders had built around whatever it was the guild actually kept. Apprentices weren't allowed. Journeymen weren't allowed. Lior had been a full member for exactly eleven days when the Inquisition came, and Aldous had said, "Next month, I'll show you," and next month had never arrived.

The door itself was ordinary-looking, which was the first strange thing about it. Oak, iron-banded, with a keyhole filled in with lead and the lead stamped with the guild sigil: a circle broken by a doorway shape, like a moon with a bite taken out. After the Purge the Inquisition had plastered warning strips across every wall of the building — *CONDEMNED BY ORDER OF THE HOLY OFFICE* — and the strips had fallen everywhere else but had somehow never adhered to this door. As if the door declined to be warned about.

Lior had come with a plan, technically. It involved a pry-bar, an hour of privacy, and a firm intention to look at whatever was behind the door and then run. She set down her bag. She got out the pry-bar. She fitted it to the gap in the frame.

And then, from behind the door, quite clearly, someone said her name.

"Lior."

Not Hollowtongue. Plain Vessic, slightly roughened, pitched low, with that dry half-smile in it that her master had used whenever she was being dense and he was too fond to say so directly.

Lior did not scream, which she considered later to be the finest achievement of her life. What she did was go backward over her own bag, sit down hard on nine years of dust, and skid until she hit the far wall, pry-bar brandished before her like a sword, breathing in the shallow fast way of small animals.

The corridor was empty. Of course it was empty. There was only her, and the light through the landing window going amber, and the door.

"Aldous Wren—" she started, and stopped, because using his name aloud felt like prying up a floorboard in her own chest. "No. No, you're dead. I watched the pyre. I paid for the pyre, half a crown, which you'd have called extravagant—"

"Lior." Patient now. Fond. "You're talking to a door."

"I know I'm talking to a door!" Her voice cracked upward. "That's the entire problem!"

Silence. Then, gently, in the tone he'd used when she was sick as a child and he'd sat by her cot reading audit ledgers aloud because he claimed exciting stories gave children nightmares: "Breathe, girl. In through the nose."

She breathed. It was humiliating how well it still worked, being told.

"What are you?" she whispered. And then, because she had inherited his habit of asking the second question first: "Where are you?"

"Behind the door," he said, "in the manner of a man standing on the other side of one. And also somewhere much larger than that, which we'll come to. Are you going to use the bar?"

"Yes," Lior lied.

"Don't." A pause. "Please. If you open it from your side, before things are ready, it will be very loud, and loud things draw the Holy Office, and you've met them."

"How do you know I've—" Lior closed her eyes. "Of course. You know everything. Dead masters are terribly well-informed."

"We get the newsletters," Aldous said dryly, and Lior laughed — one short, shocked bark of laughter, entirely against her will — and then pressed her sleeve against her mouth because laughing in this corridor felt like dancing on something sacred.

She got her feet under her. Stood. Kept the bar, because holding it was the only thing keeping her knees honest.

"The boy," she said. "Wren. The mother's son. That was you? Your voice — coming out of him?"

"That was me, wearing him like an ill-fitting coat, and I am sorry for it, and it won't harm him. He's a strong little vessel and he'll forget most of it, though perhaps not the oaths. Those stick." A rueful note. "I didn't choose to frighten anyone. But the god needed a mouth, and I am — nearby. Proximity has privileges."

"The god." Lior's grip tightened on the bar. "Whose god? The ones the Purge killed? Because I've seen the records, master, I've read the histories — the gods are dead, the Inquisition says so in very large print—"

"The Inquisition also says I was a smuggler of charms, which is true, but they say I was caught, which is not." The humor thinned. "Lior. The gods are not dead. They were starved. There's a difference, and it matters more than you can yet know. Something lives under this city, under the Door, and it has slept a hungry sleep for a hundred years, and lately — lately it has begun to dream. And when it dreams, prayers get answered. Badly. Greedily. Like a starving man handed a loaf who eats the hand that offers it."

The fever-ward. The impossible charm. Lior felt cold walk up her spine. "The mother. She prayed. Her son nearly died, and she prayed, and my stupid, worthless charm—"

"—was the nearest open window," Aldous finished quietly. "Your worthless charm was a crack in the wall, and the dream poured through it. Yes."

"So it's my fault."

"It's the guild's fault," he said, sharp enough that she flinched, "and mine, and my masters' before me, and their masters' before them — a debt a hundred years deep, and you, apprentice-mine, happen to be the last person alive whose name is on the account book. Which is why I called you here. Which is why I have been calling you for days, if you'd stop sleeping with wax in your ears."

She stared at the door. The sigil stamp in the lead seemed darker than she remembered; the broken-circle mark caught the amber light and held it.

"The Door," Aldous said. "You remember the word from your lessons. You thought it was a metaphor. Everyone thinks it's a metaphor. It isn't. It is a thing with hinges, girl, built by our founders, and it is the reason the god under Vess is starving instead of feeding, and it must be found, and opened, and — managed. Before the dreaming gets worse. Before the burns spread."

"Burns?" Lior said. "What burns?"

But he had gone quiet in the way he went quiet when he'd said more than intended and was deciding whether to say the rest. She knew that silence. She had spent six years learning its exact length.

"Master." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. Terror and longing had gotten tangled somewhere around her heart, and she could no longer tell which one was speaking. "Nine years. Nine years I knocked on the inside of every quiet moment and got nothing. I lit your death-day candle. I said the closing words of the rite over an empty grave marker because the Holy Office dumped your ashes in a pit with sixty others. I kept your knot patterns alive by selling them as *luck bracelets* at a night market like a woman selling buttons." Her breath hitched. "And now you're here, and you're here behind a *door*, which means you're not really anywhere, which means I should be running, and instead I'm standing here asking — asking what?"

"Asking what happens next," Aldous said softly. "Which is the right question. It was always your best one."

The light through the landing window had gone from amber to copper. Somewhere below, the nettle-beds moved without wind.

"Listen to me, Lior Eastwick, last apprentice of the Hollow Guild." Every trace of humor had left his voice, and underneath it she heard something vast and tired shifting its weight, the way you hear the sea behind a cliff. "The gods are waking. All of them, everywhere, wherever the seal has thinned — but ours first, because ours is hungriest and because you exist. You carry the sigil. You carry the inheritance, such as it is, and it is heavier than you think, and I am sorry for that, truly. Find the Door. Find the others — because you are not as alone as you believe, and some of them hold pieces. Trust the mapmaker more than the merchant. Distrust everyone in an official coat. And when you find the Door —"

He stopped.

"When I find the Door *what*?" Lior stepped closer to the oak. The iron bands were warm. That was wrong; iron bands in a dead hallway in autumn should be cold as river stones, and hers were warm as a sleeping animal's flank. "Aldous. When I find the Door *what*?"

For a long moment, nothing. Then, very close to the wood, almost a whisper:

"Be careful whom you send for help. There are eyes on the market now. Sketches on walls. Someone is asking questions about a seller of luck-knots, and she has your nose drawn better than you'd like."

The warmth drained out of the bands so suddenly that Lior staggered.

"Master! Master, come back — you don't get to say *be careful* and *find the Door* and just *stop*, you don't get—" She hammered the flat of the pry-bar against the oak, twice, three times, and the sound fell dead in the corridor as if the air itself refused to carry it. "ALDOUS!"

From very far away — farther than the other side of a door, farther than the other side of death — she heard him one last time, and he was smiling; she could hear the smile, and it broke her.

"Good girl. You always did knock loudest when you knew no one would answer. Go east, Lior. Follow the unwritten roads."

Then silence, the ordinary kind, and the copper light, and the smell of dust.

* * *

She sat on the stairs for a while afterward. There was no shame in it; there was no one to see.

Her mind kept snagging on small things. The teeth-marked mug. The card game tally. *Dishwashing duty for a month.* The fact that she had heard his voice again after nine years and the conversation had lasted less time than it took to drink tea. The fact that he had known about the sketches — which meant he could see things outside this building, which meant he had probably watched her selling charms, watched her struggling, watched her light his death-day candle, and said nothing for nine years, and she didn't know whether to forgive him or—

Or maybe he hadn't been able to. Maybe proximity had limits. Maybe he'd been calling her for days, he'd said, calling, and she'd been sleeping with wax in her ears — she hadn't, literally, but how many nights had she pulled the blanket over her head against dreams of corridors?

Lior wiped her face with her heel of her palm, thoroughly and impatiently, the way Aldous had hated.

"East," she said aloud, testing it. "Unwritten roads. Mapmaker good, merchant bad." A wet, exhausted laugh escaped her. "That's not instructions, that's a riddle wrapped in a debt note."

But she was already moving. Down the stairs, through the entry hall, past the wilderness of his herb beds — she paused there, snapped off a sprig of nettle like a thief taking a keepsake, and immediately felt ridiculous, and kept it anyway.

At the gap in the wall she stopped, because her mind finally finished the sentence he'd ended on.

*Sketches on walls. Someone asking questions about a seller of luck-knots.*

Sarel Vane toured the market every third day. Lior had watched her burn confiscated charms from behind a fishmonger's stall, had memorized her face the way prey memorizes hawks. And the market had eyes, and the eyes had mouths, and Lior had been the only seller whose charm had *worked* — and if the Inquisitor had a sketch with Lior's actual nose on it, then the sensible part of her life, the careful part, the six-years-surviving part, was already over. It simply hadn't sent her the notice yet.

She couldn't go home. Or she could, briefly, for the luck-knots and the coin and the one bag worth packing, and then—

"East," she said again, and squeezed through the gap, tearing another strip of coat as toll, and dropped into the cooper's yard as the last light failed over Chandler's Row.

Behind her, in the sealed room at the end of a dead corridor, something turned over in its long hunger and began, softly, to dream of her.
