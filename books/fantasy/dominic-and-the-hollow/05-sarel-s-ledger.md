## 5. Sarel's Ledger

The report on Inquisitor Sarel Vane's desk was one page long, which by the standards of the Vessan Inquisition made it practically an epic poem.

*Boy, age six. Fever of nine days. Cured overnight. Mother: Dama Osk, laundress, Candlewick Row. No physician attended. No licensed remedy recorded.*

Sarel read it three times, then set it down and looked out her window at the ash-grey morning over Vess, where somewhere a bell was ringing the hour and somewhere else — she would have bet her badge on it — somebody was already breaking a law they hadn't bothered to learn.

"Overnight," she said to the empty office.

Her assistant, a gangly young man named Pellin whose chief talents were transcription and an unshakeable belief that every problem could be solved with better filing, looked up from his stack of forms. "Sorry, Inquisitor?"

"Cures don't happen overnight, Pellin. Fevers break, but they break messily. They sweat and wander and take their time about it. This one just—" She snapped her fingers. "Stopped. Like someone reached into the boy and turned the fever off."

"That does sound irregular."

"It sounds impossible." Sarel stood and took her coat off its hook. "Which is my favorite kind of thing, because impossible things always have a person standing next to them looking guilty."

Pellin hesitated, form in hand. "Shall I note that in the ledger?"

"Pellin, if you ever manage to write down what I actually say instead of what a sensible person would have said, I will promote you on the spot." She pulled on her gloves. "Candlewick Row. Bring the warrant book and try not to enjoy yourself."

He enjoyed himself enormously. He always did. Sarel had long ago stopped holding it against him; in a city where her job mostly meant burning old women's knotwork and confiscating love charms that worked about as well as kind words, a little enjoyment wherever it could be found was nearly a virtue.

* * *

Candlewick Row smelled of tallow and wet stone, as it had every day of its existence, and Dama Osk answered the door with a child balanced on one hip and a look on her face that Sarel recognized from a hundred doors before it: the flat, braced expression of a woman deciding how much trouble she was in.

"Inquisitor," Osk said. "If this is about the laundry tax, I paid—"

"It's not about the laundry tax." Sarel held up the report. "Your son. Wren. He had a fever. Nine days."

Something flickered behind the woman's eyes — fear, quick and bright, like a fish turning in shallow water. "He's better now."

"He is. That's rather the point. Who cured him?"

"Nobody cured him. Fevers break."

"Not like this one." Sarel kept her voice gentle. It wasn't hard; she'd never much cared for the theatrical growl some of the older inquisitors affected. Fear closed mouths, and she needed this one open. "Dama Osk. I'm not here for your boy. A fever that breaks clean overnight is either a miracle or a crime, and my order doesn't believe in miracles anymore. So you see the problem."

For a long moment the laundress said nothing. The boy on her hip — Wren, small and solemn, with the enormous eyes children develop after illness has burned everything else out of them — stared at Sarel with an attention that made the skin of her neck prickle. Not the stare of a shy child. The stare of something taking inventory.

Then Osk sighed, all at once, like a rope going slack. "There was a charm," she said quietly. "Night market. Off Chandler's Stair, third row past the pie cart. A woman sold me a fever-ward for two coppers." Her mouth twisted. "Two coppers. I've bought wards before, real ones, before the ban got tight — they cost a month's wages and half of those were swindles anyway. This one cost less than a pie."

"And it worked."

"It worked." Osk laughed, and there was no joy in it. "My boy was grey, Inquisitor. Grey like dishwater. I sat up with him and I prayed — not to anything particular, mind, there's nothing particular left to pray to, but you pray anyway when it's bad enough — and then I remembered the charm in my pocket and I felt like a fool, because it's just knots and spit, isn't it, that's what they tell us. And I put it under his pillow because a fool will do anything." She shifted Wren's weight. "By morning he was asking for breakfast. Asking for *porridge*, like nothing had happened."

Sarel wrote it down. "The seller. Describe her."

"Young. Hood up. Thin — thin like she forgets meals, not like she's performing it. Kept her sleeve pulled down over her hand when she gave me the ward, like she didn't want skin showing." Osk paused. "She had nice hands, though. Careful ones. She tied the knots right there in front of me and she counted under her breath while she did it, like she was checking her work. Swindlers don't check their work."

"No," Sarel agreed. "They don't." She closed the notebook. "You'll hear from the office. If anyone comes asking about the boy who isn't from the Inquisition, you'll send for me first. Not the temple watch. Me."

"Why?" Osk asked. And then, sharper: "What happens to him? To us?"

"Nothing," Sarel said, "if I can help it," and walked back down Candlewick Row feeling the weight of the sentence like a stone she'd picked up and couldn't put down.

Behind her, she could have sworn she heard the boy say something in a voice too low and too old for a child of six. She didn't turn around. Later, she would make herself remember that she hadn't.

* * *

The confiscated records were kept in the subvaults beneath the Inquisition's headquarters, three floors down behind two locks and a door that Pellin swore groaned in E minor, though Sarel had never had the heart to ask how he knew his modes well enough to be sure.

Officially, the subvaults held everything the Purge had gathered: forty years of charms, grimoires, prayer-cloths, god-stones, liturgical wheels, and the ledgers of the guilds that had once kept such things — catalogued, cross-referenced, and sealed, because the war against the gods had been won and nobody read the accounts of a finished war.

Unofficially, Sarel had learned early in her career that the fastest way to be reassigned to a provincial office was to be caught reading them. So she read them carefully. Slowly. On nights when the duty rosters put her name near the vault door.

Tonight she told herself it was procedure. The healed boy was procedure. Tracing a working to its source required understanding the tradition it came from, and the night-market charms were guild-work, however degraded — everyone knew that, it was in the training manuals, the Hollow Guild's junk magic leaking into the gutters of Vess like dye from a cut purse. Procedure demanded she review the guild files.

Procedure demanded a lamp, apparently, and a chair, and four hours.

She started with the inventory ledgers, because inventories are honest in a way doctrine isn't. And it was there, in a courier log from the last year before the Purge, that she found the thing that would sit in the back of her skull for the rest of her life, patient as a splinter.

An entry, in a clerk's tidy hand:

*Fever-wards, standard pattern, dispatched to Vess district: 400 units. Note: these are not workings. These are reminders. The ward holds no power of its own; it marks the bearer as heard. The god attends to those marked. Effect ceases when attention ceases.*

Sarel read it twice.

*Not workings. Reminders.*

She had spent eleven years confiscating fever-wards. She had burned them by the sackful. Every manual, every instructor, every confession extracted from every frightened hedge-witch agreed: the things were tricks, placebos, frauds dressed in knots. At worst they were frauds *plus* sedition. Nobody had ever suggested — not once, in eleven years, in any document she'd been permitted to see — that the charm might simply be a *label*, and the actual cure might come from somewhere else entirely.

From something doing the attending.

She pulled another ledger. Then another. The clerks of the old guilds had been compulsive recorders, bless them forever; they wrote down everything, including the questions, and it was the questions that undid her, slowly, in the lamplight, floor three below the sleeping world.

*Query from the Ashfield chapter: when the god attends ten marked households in one night, the wards do not dim. Where does the increase come from?*

*Query: the god has refused attendance for the household of a man who beat his daughters, despite marking. Is this judgment? Whose?*

*Query, unsigned, in different ink:* *We keep saying "the god hears." Has anyone asked what it costs the god to listen?*

Sarel sat back in the chair. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, the great building settled and ticked like any old house, and she thought, with sudden vertigo, that she had spent her entire adult life guarding a victory, and victories were supposed to feel like something.

Here was what she had been taught, in the seminary schoolrooms with their scrubbed floors and their careful maps: the gods of Vess had been tyrants. Old, hungry, arbitrary tyrants who demanded sacrifice and hoarded worship, and the Purge had broken them and given the world back to people. Clean story. Load-bearing story. She had recited it at her own induction, hand on the ash-iron badge, voice steady, and she had believed it the way you believe a wall — not because you inspect it but because you lean on it daily.

And here were the guild records, forty years older than her faith, and the picture in them was of a god who answered fevers for two coppers' worth of string, refused to attend child-beaters, worried its own priests with questions about *cost* — and a guild that administered the arrangement like tax collectors, keeping their ledgers neat, keeping their percentages.

Tyrants. She turned a page. *These* were the monsters the Purge had died fighting?

She found the Purge-era material near midnight, in a box marked with the seal of the Third Commission, and she should have stopped there. The seal meant high doctrine; the seal meant other people's business; eleven years of instinct told her exactly where the edge of the cliff was.

She opened the box.

Inside were the commission's field reports, and unlike the guild ledgers they were not tidy. They were the notes of men doing something ugly and trying to write around it. *Resistance minimal.* *Chapter hall cleared; materials burned; three members fled, two taken.* And then, on the ninth report, in handwriting gone hurried:

*Interrogation of guild elder (name struck through). Subject states the guild did not banish nor destroy the object of veneration. Subject states this was never possible. Subject states the founders SEALED it, generations past, and the guild's function since has been maintenance of the seal and collection of offerings to keep the sealed thing quiet. When asked what happens without the offerings, subject declined further comment. Recommend reclassification of operation objectives pending theological review.*

Pending theological review. Sarel almost laughed. She knew how reviews ended in wartime — with the uncomfortable answer filed and the operation proceeding, because the operation had momentum and budgets and careers attached to it, and because *reclassification* is a word that dies in committee.

So the Purge hadn't killed a god. Fine. Perhaps that was doctrine-by-another-road: they'd sealed the prison doors and called it a tomb. But the reports kept going, and the last one — the one she read standing up, because sitting felt suddenly too much like being caught — was worse.

*Final summary, Third Commission. Sealed site located beneath the city, extent unknown. Seal integrity: failing, per subject's estimates, within two generations without full offering schedule. Commission recommendation: disperse the maintaining guild, confiscate records, terminate offering schedule, and station the Order as custodians of the surface sites. Note appended in different hand: this course guarantees eventual failure and transfers responsibility to an order with no knowledge of the mechanism. Dissenting opinion recorded and dismissed.*

Dismissed. Somebody had known. Somebody had stood up in whatever room commissions meet in and said *this will fail, and we are handing the failure to people who don't know what they're guarding* — and had been noted, and dismissed, and forty years later Sarel Vane was reading the note by lamplight while a six-year-old boy across the city spoke in a dead language, because the offerings had stopped, and the seal was failing, and *the fever-wards were starting to work again.*

The lamp guttered. Sarel became aware, gradually, of several facts arriving in her mind in single file, like mourners.

First: the impossible cures weren't returning. They were *leaking*. Something sealed and starving under Vess had begun, in its extremity, to answer whatever prayers slipped through the cracks — and each answer was a crack widening.

Second: her order was not the victor of a holy war. It was the unwitting janitor of a failing prison, guarding doors it didn't know existed, burning the very charms that had once been part of the maintenance schedule. She had spent eleven years tearing holes in the roof and calling it housekeeping.

Third: somewhere in the night market was a young woman with careful hands who tied fever-wards like she was checking her work, and who wore her sleeve down over her sigil, and until tonight Sarel would have called her a criminal. Tonight she couldn't decide between *criminal* and *prophet*, and neither answer was comfortable, and only one of them could be arrested.

She copied three pages by hand — quickly, in her own cipher, an offense so severe they'd burn her for it alone — and put the originals back exactly as they'd lain. Then she climbed the three flights to her office, where Pellin had left a note: *Ledger updated per your instructions. Also a message — Dama Osk sent word her son is speaking strangely. Some foreign tongue. Neighbor says it sounds "old."*

Sarel stood in her dark office holding the note, and outside the window Vess lay under its curfew lamps, ten thousand chimneys, a hundred thousand sleepers, and beneath all of it — if three pages of condemned paper were to be believed — something hungry turning over in its sleep, dreaming of being heard.

She took out her personal ledger, the little black book where she recorded cases, and instead of writing she flipped back through it, weeks of neat entries, and saw for the first time what the entries added up to: a scatter of impossibilities across the city, growing denser toward the river, toward the old districts, toward the low ground.

Toward, if the maps in her memory served, the oldest quarter of all.

She sharpened her pen and wrote one line: *Find the charm-seller first. Before the Order does. Before whatever is under us finishes waking.*

Then she blew out the lamp and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening — absurdly, she knew — for the sound of something below the city, turning in its sleep.

It was very quiet. But quiet, she thought, pulling on her coat, had never once in her life meant empty.
