## 6. The Guild That Wasn't

Tessin's shop sat on Cordwainer Lane, which was the sort of street that pretended very hard to be respectable. Brass plaques. Window boxes with obedient geraniums. A boot-boy who swept the same three feet of pavement over and over because standing still was a firing offense for boys on respectable streets.

Above the door hung a sign: *T. Marrowby, Licensed Apothecary. Remedies Approved by the College of Physicians.* Below that, in smaller letters someone had scratched and someone else had tried to buff out: *No charms. No fortune-telling. No exceptions.*

Lior stood across the lane for the better part of an hour, pretending to examine a fountain she had walked past a thousand times. The fountain featured a stone dolphin strangling a stone fisherman, which said something about Vess's civic pride that Lior had never wanted to hear.

She hadn't slept. She'd spent the night with her mother's prayer-ward spread on the table, turning it over and over, sniffing it like a suspicious housewife checking milk. It was good work. It was her work — fever-bark, ash-of-oak, a knot tied in the old pattern, the one Aldous had taught her with his big clumsy fingers guiding hers. There was no reason in the world it should have worked.

And yet the boy Wren had woken up cool-skinned and speaking a language that had been dead since before the Purge. Since before *Aldous*. Old Hollowtongue, the guild's liturgical tongue, the language of oaths and sealed doors. Lior knew exactly four words of it — enough to recognize it, not enough to understand why a six-year-old was dreaming in it.

There were perhaps nine people alive who could tell her more. Most were dead. One was an Inquisitor. And one, as far as she knew, was currently mixing cough syrup behind a brass plaque on Cordwainer Lane while pretending the last twenty years hadn't happened.

"Right," Lior told the dolphin. "Worse things than being thrown out of a shop."

The dolphin did not argue.

* * *

A bell chimed when she entered, a bright little bell that belonged in a nicer story. Inside, the shop smelled of camphor and dried lavender and, underneath both, faintly — so faintly most people would have missed it — of oak ash. Fever-bark. The smell of guild work.

Tessin stood at the counter weighing out blue pills with the delicacy of a man defusing something. He'd gone soft around the middle and gray at the temples, but his hands hadn't changed: long-fingered, precise, knotted with old small scars from wire and knotwork. Hands Lior had watched tie a luck-knot in under four seconds once, drunk, on a dare.

He looked up. His face did something complicated — a whole weather system passing through in the space of a breath — and then settled into polite blankness, the way a pond settles after you throw a brick in it.

"Help you?"

"I've got a cough," Lior said. "Terrible one. Keeps me up all night."

"We have an excellent syrup."

"The kind that works even if I don't believe in it?"

The scale in his hands trembled, just barely. Two blue pills tipped gently against each other, *tick*, like tiny bells.

"Young woman," Tessin said, "I sell remedies approved by the College of Physicians. If you want superstition, there's a night market down by the river full of criminals selling knotted string to desperate people. I'd thank you not to bring that filth into my shop."

"That's funny," Lior said, walking her fingers along the counter, "because this filth smells your shelves. Oak ash under the lavender. Third jar on the second shelf — the one labeled 'willow extract' — is fever-bark prepared the old way. I'd know. I learned the preparation from the same man you did."

For a moment Tessin looked at her with the expression of a man watching his own house catch fire in slow motion. Then he moved — fast, for a soft man — to the window and pulled the curtain half-shut.

"Liar," he said quietly. "You're nobody. I don't know you."

"Tessin." She pushed back her sleeve.

The sigil on her inner wrist caught the lamplight: a circle broken by a keyhole, inked in the gray-black ink the guild used, the ink mixed with oak ash so it never quite healed flat. She watched him look at it. Watched the weather come back to his face — grief, fear, and under both, horribly, relief, the look of a man finding another survivor of a shipwreck and hating them for it.

"Don't," he whispered. "Don't show me that. Not here."

"A boy nearly died of fever last week. His mother bought a charm from my stall. It shouldn't have worked, Tessin. It *couldn't* have worked — I checked the knots twice, they were duds, I sold them as decoration—"

"Then it didn't work," he said sharply. "Coincidence. Fevers break."

"He woke up speaking Hollowtongue."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere in the back of the shop, a pestle rolled off a mortar and clinked against a shelf, and Tessin flinched like it was a gunshot.

"You're mistaken," he said. "It's a dead language. Nobody speaks it. Nobody has spoken it since—" He stopped himself.

"Since the Purge," Lior finished. "I know what you were going to say. I also know you keep fever-bark in a mislabeled jar, and I know only two kinds of people do that: people who still believe, and people who can't stop believing but are too much of a coward to admit it."

Something in Tessin's face closed like a fist.

"Get out of my shop."

* * *

He threw her out. Literally — well, as literally as a soft apothecary can throw out anyone; it was more of a firm steering, hand between her shoulder blades, murmured apologies to the neighbors' curtains. She let herself be steered, because she'd expected it, and because she'd counted the steps between the counter and the door and knew there was a back room beyond the bead curtain, and knew that men who steer you firmly toward the front door are often making sure you see exactly how normal everything is.

She stood on the step, adjusting her collar with wounded dignity, and heard, clearly, through the gap under the door: the scrape of a bolt. Then footsteps — away from the front of the shop. Toward the back room.

*Not toward the front,* she thought. *Away.*

Well. That was either very good or very bad, and there was one way to find out, and its name was loitering.

She loitered magnificently. She examined the boot-boy's technique (deficient). She read the physician's college certificate in the window twice, including the part where the seal was slightly crooked, which told her Tessin had mounted it himself, angrily, probably the day he got it. She bought a meat skewer from a cart and ate it slowly, which in Vess granted a citizen near-permanent right to occupy any given patch of pavement.

Forty minutes later the back door of the shop opened a hand's width and Tessin's voice hissed through it: "Are you planning to stand there all afternoon?"

"It's a nice wall," Lior said. "Good mortar work."

"In the alley. Quickly. Before Widow Prentiss sees you; she reports everything to the parish, including weather."

* * *

The back room smelled honestly of herbs instead of dishonestly. Drying racks hung from every rafter — yarrow, boneset, vervain — and among them, tucked between legitimate bundles where only a guild-trained eye would notice, three sprigs of ash-key and a twist of rowan. Tessin lit a lamp, saw how close it hung to the drying racks, swore, and moved it.

"You're sloppy now," Lior said. "Aldous would have had you scrubbing mortars for a week."

"Don't." The word came out cracked down the middle. He lowered himself onto a stool with the care of an older man than he was. "You don't get to use his name at me. You weren't—"

"Weren't what? There? You're right, I wasn't there. I wasn't there the night they came for the hall, because I was eleven years old and he'd packed me off to an aunt in the river district with a lie about needing lamp oil." Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. "I wasn't there when they burned his tools, either. But I watched them burn some more of them yesterday, on the market green. An Inquisitor named Sarel Vane. She has a list, Tessin. She has names. And now there's a child speaking Hollowtongue in a fever-ward in Salt Row, and she will find him, and then she'll find the charm, and then she'll find *me*, and then she'll come down Cordwainer Lane asking why a licensed apothecary keeps ash-keys hidden in his rafters like love letters."

Tessin stared at the floor. His thumb was moving over the back of his other hand — over a spot on the skin, rubbing it, over and over. Lior realized with a small cold shock that she was watching him rub at a scar where a sigil used to be. Burned off, or scraped, or acid-taken. The guild had taught three ways to remove the mark. All of them hurt. They'd never imagined anyone would want to.

"They offered us licenses," he said finally. "After. Anyone who renounced. Sign the abjuration, swear the oath, and you get to keep your life and open a legal trade. Half took it. Half ran. I—" He laughed without any humor in it whatsoever. "I did both. Signed first, ran second. Couldn't decide which cowardice suited me."

"Tessin—"

"Do you know what the abjuration says? Word for word?" He wasn't looking at her anymore. "'I renounce all commerce with the unseen, acknowledging that the gods are dead, that magic is fraud, and that the Guild of the Hollow Door was a conspiracy of charlatans and worse.'" His thumb kept circling the scar. "I signed it in my own blood. They liked that touch. Very theatrical, the early Inquisition. And the ink took strangely, because guild blood — our blood, we used ash-ink so long it's *in* us — doesn't sign away clean. My line went crooked. The clerk made me do it twice."

"Tessin, I didn't come about your conscience."

"No. You came about a miracle." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly awake. "A charm that couldn't work, working. A child speaking the liturgy. You want me to tell you what it means, because Aldous is dead and I'm the next-worst thing." He stood, abruptly, and began pacing between the drying racks, agitated, alive in a way the shop-front man had never been. "You want to know what I think? I think we should all be on a boat. I think every person who ever tied a luck-knot should be on a boat, far away, before whatever is waking up finishes waking. Because I remember what the guild was actually for, Lior. Do you?"

"The guild protected people."

"The guild *sealed* things." He rounded on her. "That's the part they burned the records to hide, and it's the part Aldous only told the inner circle, and I only know it because I eavesdropped like the little rat I've always been. We weren't priests. We were *locksmiths*. And you don't need locksmiths unless there's a door."

The lamp guttered. Outside, a church bell rang three o'clock, and Lior noticed — filed away, in the cold drawer of her mind where she kept things too large to feel yet — that the bell rang wrong somehow, a half-tone low, as if the metal itself were tired.

"There's no door," she said. "There's nothing under Vess but cellars and smuggler tunnels."

"Then why does the liturgy exist? Why did we have *oaths* in a dead language, binding oaths, if there was nothing to bind?" Tessin dragged a hand down his face. "Child. Listen to me. I have spent nineteen years being nobody, and it has been the safest and worst thing I have ever done, and I am begging you — take nothing I'm about to give you to any authority, any priest, any friend. Especially no friends. Friends get people killed."

"You just called yourself a rat and you're about to help me anyway," Lior said. "Why?"

"Because you showed me the sigil." His voice dropped. "Because for nineteen years I have poured honest syrup for dishonest customers and gone to bed grateful and hollow, and I thought I'd killed the part of me that minded. And then you stood in my shop and showed me a broken circle and a keyhole, and it turns out I mind *very much*." He smiled, and it was Aldous's smile suddenly, crooked and sad, and Lior had to look away from it. "Loyalty is a disease, girl. It goes into remission. It doesn't cure."

He turned to the far wall, behind the rack of boneset, and pried loose a brick with his thumbnail — the motion practiced, the hiding place old. From the cavity he drew a flat packet wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the worktable with the reverence of a man uncovering a relic.

It was a scrap of vellum, no bigger than a hymnal page, scorched brown at one corner. On it, drawn in ash-ink faded almost to rumor, was a map fragment — or the ghost of one. A few lines. A symbol at their center that Lior knew from nowhere and everywhere: a keyhole inside a broken circle, larger than a coin, ringed by script in Old Hollowtongue.

"This is all that's left of the guild's survey maps," Tessin said. "We each memorized a piece, in case. Aldous gave me mine the winter before the end. Made me copy it until I could draw it blind." He tapped the central symbol. "This mark appears on none of the others the same way twice — it shifts position relative to the lines. Which drove the cartographers mad, and means the Door isn't in a *place*, exactly. It's in a relation between places. Understand? You can't find it with a map alone. You need pieces held by different hands, and you need to bring them together."

"How many pieces?"

"That survived the Purge? I know of two besides mine. Maybe three." He wrapped it again, quickly, as if the vellum had begun to smolder. "One belonged to Maren Cole. You won't like where she ended up."

"Maren Cole informed," Lior said slowly. "Maren Cole is the reason they found the hall."

"And she kept her piece anyway." Tessin's mouth twisted. "Sentiment, or insurance, or guilt. With Maren it was always all three, in rotating order." He pushed the packet across the table to Lior, then — as her fingers touched it — pressed down, holding it, holding her eyes.

"Understand what you're carrying. Nineteen years I've told myself the guild was a fraud and a fairy story, because the alternative was admitting I helped bury something living. If that child speaks the liturgy, then the seal is thinning, and everything Aldous died believing — that the lock would hold, that the sacrifice meant something — comes apart in our hands." His grip tightened. "So. Last chance to walk out of here an apothecary's customer with a cough."

Lior thought about Wren, small and flushed and speaking words older than the Inquisition. Thought about Aldous's voice, which she hadn't heard in nineteen years except in dreams, and how the mother had described her son's new sleep-talk: *like an old man explaining something patiently to a child.*

"Someone's answering prayers," she said quietly. "Badly. Every answer leaves a burn. I think the lock isn't just thinning, Tessin. I think something on the other side is knocking, and it doesn't know its own strength."

Tessin released the packet. Sat back down on his stool, all at once, like a man whose strings had been cut.

"Then you'll need the other pieces," he said. "And you'll need them fast, because if the Inquisition hears 'Hollowtongue' attached to anything, they won't send a clerk. They'll send Vane." He rubbed his scarred hand again, absently. "Take the river road east when you go. And Lior — if you find whoever's answering those prayers—"

He stopped. Outside, the crooked bell was ringing four o'clock, though it had rung three less than a quarter hour ago, and neither of them remarked on it, because both of them had already decided not to remark on it.

"Yes?"

"Tell it we kept the faith badly," Tessin said, "but we kept it."

Lior slipped the oilcloth packet inside her coat, against her heart, where contraband and hope go. At the back door she paused.

"Tessin. The license. Would you — if it all changed, if the ban ended — would you come back to the hall?"

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed, wetly, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand.

"Girl, I've been back for nineteen years. I just charge for the syrup."

* * *

She took the river road east as the light went long and coppery, and at the first bridge she stopped under the arch where no one could see, and unwrapped the fragment, and held it up against the sunset.

The lines on the vellum pointed east-northeast, out past the walls, past the border provinces — toward country she'd always assumed was empty. And as the sun sank, the ash-ink seemed to drink the last light and hold it, the broken circle glowing faintly warm against her fingertips, like a knot tied by hands that had been waiting a long time to be found.

Behind her, distantly, Vess's tired bell tolled five o'clock.

It had tolled five once already today.
