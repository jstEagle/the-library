## 7. Prayer Burns

The first hollow appeared on a Tuesday, in the tannery district, and everyone agreed it was the smugglers' fault.

Lior heard about it from Dossi, who ran the pie stall two down from her corner in the night market and heard about everything before it happened, sometimes before it was true. "Burned right through the wall," Dossi said, leaning over a tray of eel pasties that smelled like regret and vinegar. "Clean circle, smooth as an egg. Like something reached through and took a bite of the world."

"Smugglers," Lior said, in the flat tone that meant *stop talking to me*.

"That's what the criers said." Dossi wiped his hands on his apron, which had stopped being white sometime during the last reign. "Though I don't know what they'd be smuggling that burns holes in walls. Contraband pepper? Illicit cheese?"

"Distilled spirits. Bad ones. They eat through masonry."

"They don't, though, do they."

"No," Lior admitted. "They don't."

She'd known it wasn't smugglers the moment she'd heard the description. She'd known it in her teeth, in the old scar along her palm where she'd once gripped a sigil-stamp too hard, in the place behind her ribs that had been quietly aching since the night a fever-ward had worked when it shouldn't have. A clean circle, smooth as an egg. She knew exactly what made marks like that. Guild doctrine called them *expenditure wounds*. Her master Aldous had called them, in one of his darker moods, *the world's receipts*. Every answered prayer cost something. And now somebody — something — was paying the bill in Vess's walls.

The trouble with knowing things was that you couldn't unknow them, and the further trouble was that knowledge, in Vess, was a hanging offense with extra steps.

So she did what any sensible person would do: nothing. She sold her luck-knots and fever-wards, which were honestly just knots and wards with good stitching, and she kept her head down and her sleeve down over her sigil, and she told herself the ache behind her ribs was a chill.

Then came Thursday's hollow, and Friday's, and by Sunday there were four, and by the next market night there were nine, and the city had stopped blaming smugglers only because the criers couldn't keep up with the new theory, which was plague. Plague of burning. The temples were selling protective medallions at three coppers each, made of tin, blessed by men who hadn't felt anything holy since the Purge, and people were buying them because people will always buy the shape of safety even when the substance is gone.

Lior stood at her corner under the paper lanterns and felt each new burn arrive.

That was the part she didn't tell anyone, couldn't tell anyone, wouldn't have known how to say if she'd tried. It started the week after the fever broke for the cooper's son — Wren, the mother had said his name was, little Wren with his eyes like a startled owl. Each time a prayer got answered somewhere in Vess, Lior felt it first: a tug behind her sternum, sharp and specific, like a fishhook set in wool and then in flesh. And then, minutes later or hours later, word would come down the market of another scorched circle, another hole in the world, and the location always matched the ache, though she couldn't have explained how she knew that either. She just knew. The way you know someone is standing behind you. The way you know your own name is being said across a crowded room.

Nine burns. Nine hooks. She counted them at night lying in her rented room above the chandler's, staring at the ceiling, pressing her palm flat against her breastbone as if she could hold whatever it was in place.

"This is fine," she informed the ceiling. "This is completely fine."

The ceiling, being a ceiling, declined to argue.

* * *

On Monday she went to look at one.

It wasn't difficult. The burns drew crowds the way executions used to, back before the Inquisition decided crowds were seditious. This one was in Candlemaker's Row, low on the wall of a shuttered dye-house, and by midmorning there were forty people around it, and by noon there were eighty and a man selling roasted chestnuts, because grief and wonder both empty pockets if you stand close enough to the heat.

The hollow was smaller than the stories claimed. Perhaps the width of a dinner plate, perfectly round, its edges fused glassy-smooth, and inside it — nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. Lior had seen the guild's oldest records describe this, in diagrams drawn by hands three centuries dead: *a local absence, non-spatial, non-temporal.* Aldous had annotated that page in his cramped left-slanting hand: *Nonsense. It's a bite mark. Something ate here.*

She looked into it and her stomach dropped the way it does on stairs that aren't where your foot expects them.

And behind her ribs, faintly, patiently, the hook shifted.

"You shouldn't get so close," said a voice beside her, and she nearly jumped out of her skin, because the voice belonged to a small girl, maybe six, holding a rag doll by one leg. "Mam says the burn eats prayers. If you pray near it, it takes yours too."

"Does she," Lior managed.

"Mam prays all the time anyway. For Da's lungs. She says it's worth the risk." The girl swung the doll. "Da says it's rubbish and the burn is from smugglers."

"What do you say?"

The girl considered this with the gravity of a judge. "I say it's a door," she said. "A little door. For a little thing to come through." Then she wandered off, trailing her doll, leaving Lior standing in the cold with her heart hammering and every instinct she owned screaming that a six-year-old had just casually diagnosed the apocalypse between bites of chestnut.

Because that was it. That was exactly it. Not a wound. Not a weapon-mark. A door — small, temporary, punched through from the other side whenever something answered hard enough. The guild's founders hadn't sealed the old gods behind a wall. They'd sealed them behind a *door*, and doors had two sides, and something on the far side was now reaching through every time some desperate soul in Vess asked hard enough for help.

And every reach cost. The burns weren't damage. They were *toll*.

Lior bought chestnuts she didn't want, just to have something to do with her hands, and walked home the long way, counting hooks. Ten now. She'd felt number ten come in while she stood at the wall — somewhere south, near the river, she'd have sworn it. Near the poor streets. The prayers of the poor were always answered first; the god, whatever was left of it, had never lost its taste for the desperate.

* * *

Tuesday, the Inquisition posted notices.

Lior read one off the chandler's door while pretending to examine his candles, which were terrible. The notice was printed in heavy black type, the kind the Inquisition reserved for edicts and funerals:

*BY ORDER OF THE HOLY OFFICE OF THE PURGE: The scorch-marks afflicting the city are the work of smuggled charm-craft, forbidden under Article One. Any person found possessing, making, selling, or PRAYING OVER such devices shall be subject to examination. Rewards offered for information leading to arrest. The Purge protects. The Purge endures.*

"Praying over," Lior murmured. "They're arresting people for praying now."

"They're arresting people for everything," said the chandler, not looking up from his wicks. "My cousin got examined for owning a blue cloak. Said it was seditious. Blue, he said. The color of the old temple banners." He snorted. "They gave the cloak to the fire and let my cousin go. Took him three weeks to stop flinching at ovens."

Lior drifted back toward the market with the notice's words stuck in her like a splinter. *Praying over.* Which meant the Inquisition had figured out the connection too — or suspected it — which meant Sarel Vane was somewhere in this city connecting the same dots Lior was, except Sarel had a warrant book and forty trained readers of guilt, and Lior had a corner stall and a sleeve she needed to keep pulled down.

And sure enough, at the far end of the market row, under the lanterns, stood a figure in ash-grey, talking to Dossi.

Lior's whole body went cold and then very busy pretending to rearrange her charms.

Sarel Vane didn't look like the stories. The stories had her tall and terrible, with a voice like a closing door. In person she was ordinary height, narrow, with ink stains on two fingers and the patient expression of someone doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to. That was worse, somehow. Terrible you could run from. Arithmetic followed you home.

"—nine burns in eleven days," Sarel was saying, not unkindly. Dossi had gone pale as his pastry flour. "Each one within a street of where a charm-seller works. You see the pattern."

"I see a lot of patterns," Dossi said. "I sell pies. Patterns everywhere."

"Humor me. What do you notice about the sellers?"

Dossi's eyes flicked — just once, just briefly — down the row, and Lior watched the flick travel and understood, with sick clarity, exactly how much danger one friendly glance could carry. She turned to her stall and began, with tremendous focus, re-sorting charms that were already sorted.

"The usual lot," Dossi said loudly. "Luck-knots. Fever-wards. Nothing that works, obviously. You'd have better luck buying a button."

"Obviously," Sarel said. And then her footsteps came closer, unhurried, and stopped at Lior's corner.

Lior looked up with the carefully blank face of a tired woman interrupted at work. "Charms are two coppers. Fever-wards three. No refunds, the luck doesn't take to everybody."

"Doesn't it," Sarel said. She looked at the display without touching it — Lior noticed that, noted it the way you note a cat watching a birdcage. "Your stitching is very good."

"I practice."

"Where did you learn?"

"My grandmother. She was a seamstress. Everything I know about knots, she knew about buttons." Lior smiled the smile that had gotten her through ten years of markets: warm, vacant, forgettable. "You'll want the grey knot, if it's for travel. Green's for money. Red's—" she lowered her voice conspiratorially, "—red's mostly for hopeful people. I sell a lot of red."

Something moved at the corner of Sarel's mouth. Not quite a smile. A ledger entry. "Mostly for hopeful people," she repeated. "Yes. I imagine business is good lately."

"Lately?"

"People are frightened. Frightened people buy hope." Sarel picked up a luck-knot at last, turned it over in her ink-stained fingers, and Lior felt the hook behind her ribs pull — gently, curiously, like the thing on the far side of the door had noticed the inspection. She kept her face still through sheer spite. "No sigil work. No guild marks. Just stitching."

"Just stitching," Lior agreed.

"Mm." Sarel set the knot down precisely where it had been. "There was a woman in this market three weeks ago. Bought a fever-ward for her son. The ward worked. Do you know anything about that?"

"I know wards are three coppers and hope is free, but only if you can afford the ward first."

For a moment — one long moment — Sarel Vane looked at her. Really looked, the way a reader reads a difficult page, and Lior stood inside the look and thought about running and knew running was confession and stayed exactly, perfectly, boredly still.

"Three coppers," Sarel said finally. "I'll take two red ones. For hopeful people."

Lior wrapped them, took the coins, and watched the ash-grey figure walk away down the lantern-lit row, pausing once to glance back — not at her, she realized. At the crowd. Counting. Always counting.

Dossi appeared at her elbow the moment the Inquisitor rounded the corner, gray-faced. "She knows something."

"She knows something's happening. Different thing." But Lior's hands weren't steady tying the coin-string, and they both pretended they were.

* * *

That night, the eleventh hook came in while she was eating cold rice, and it came wrong.

The others had been tugs. Sharp, distant, bearable. This one landed behind her ribs and *stayed*, a slow drag, like a hook catching in cloth and pulling thread loose, and Lior set down her bowl and pressed both hands to her sternum and breathed through it, and outside her window, three streets over, something glowed orange against the underside of the clouds.

Not a lamp-glow. Too big, too low, too hungry-colored.

By the time she reached the street — coat over her nightshirt, boots unlaced, because priorities — half the neighborhood was out in the cold, faces tipped up toward the glow. From the roofline she could see it: fire in the weaver's quarter, climbing a warehouse wall in sheets, and threaded through the smoke, impossibly, a shape. Round. Smooth-edged. A circle of light burned into the air itself, wider than a wagon wheel, wide as a doorway, and through it — through the nothing at its center — Lior felt a pull so strong her knees buckled against the gutter.

Not a bite mark this time. Not a plate-sized toll.

A door. Standing open. Getting bigger.

"Prayer," whispered the woman next to her, an old weaver with smoke-smudged cheeks, gripping Lior's arm without knowing whose arm it was. "Someone prayed so hard it opened. My granddaughter's up there — the fire took the loom-house — she'll have prayed, she prays like breathing—"

And Lior, hanging onto the gutter with both hands, feeling the hook behind her ribs pull taut enough to sing, understood the whole shape of it at once, the way you understand a fall has begun only after your feet leave the ledge.

Every answered prayer opened the door a little. Enough small prayers, enough desperation, enough of Vess's poor begging the dark for help — and the door would stand open all the way. And whatever had been paying in scorched circles until now would no longer need to pay in circles at all.

The glow held for eleven heartbeats. Twelve. Then it folded inward like a closing eye, and the fire settled back into ordinary fire, and the hook behind Lior's ribs eased — slightly, temporarily, unsatisfyingly — and around her the crowd began to murmur: smugglers, plague, punishment, the old words, the safe words.

Only the old weaver was crying, quietly, holding Lior's arm. "It answered her," the woman said. "I know it answered her. Whatever it costs — it answered."

"Yes," Lior said. Her voice came out rough. "It does that."

She walked home through streets that smelled of smoke, past the chandler's dark window, up her narrow stair, and sat on her bed with a lamp lit and her sleeve pushed back and the guild sigil bare on her forearm for the first time in years — the interlocked ring and key, faded ink gone silver-grey, the mark of a dead order on a living liar.

"Aldous," she said to the empty room. Her master three years dead, whose tools she'd watched Sarel Vane feed to a brazier, whose voice she sometimes still heard in the creak of the guildhall door. "If you're anywhere, if any of you are anywhere — what am I supposed to do? Warn people? Warn them of what? That their prayers are working?" She laughed, and it came out cracked. "They'll burn me for saying it, and the burns will keep coming anyway."

The lamp flickered. Draft, she told herself. Old building, bad windows.

But behind her ribs, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, the hook tugged once — twice — like a knock.

Like something on the far side of a door, answering.
