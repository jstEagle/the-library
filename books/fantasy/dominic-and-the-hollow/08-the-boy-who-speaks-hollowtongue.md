## 8. The Boy Who Speaks Hollowtongue

Lior stood in the alley outside the widow Maren's tenement for the better part of an hour, working up the courage to knock on a door she'd been invited to knock on.

It was absurd. She had faced down cutpurses in the night market. She had watched Inquisitor Sarel Vane feed her master's life's work into a brazier without flinching — well, without flinching *visibly*. She had broken into her own dead guildhall and spoken with a voice behind a sealed door that should not have been able to speak at all. And here she was, frozen on a stoop because a seven-year-old might say something frightening.

But that was precisely it. Cutpurses could be outrun. Inquisitors could be dodged. A seven-year-old who spoke in his sleep in a language that had been dead for forty years could not be reasoned with, bribed, or escaped, because he lived three flights up in a room that smelled of boiled turnips, and he kept asking for her by name.

*The charm-seller comes again,* the mother had told the neighbors, loudly enough that Lior had heard it from the street. *She fixed my Wren.*

*I didn't fix him,* Lior wanted to shout back down the stairwell. *That is exactly the problem.*

She climbed.

* * *

Wren was sitting cross-legged on the windowsill when she came in, which was against every rule his mother had ever set, watching the street below with the intense, unblinking attention of a cat at a birdfeeder. He turned when the door opened, and his face did something complicated — delight and recognition and something older underneath, something that made Lior's stomach drop.

"You came," he said. "He said you would."

"Who said?" Lior asked, though she knew. She set her basket down by the door like a peace offering, like a shield. She had brought candied ginger. It seemed important, somehow, to arrive armed with something sweet.

"The man behind your eyes," Wren said simply, and hopped off the sill. "Also your bootlace is undone."

Lior looked down. Her bootlace was, in fact, undone. She could not remember it coming undone. She knelt to tie it mostly so she would not have to look at him while her hands shook.

Up close, Wren looked healthy. That was the cruelest part. The fever that should have killed him — the fever her too-strong charm had burned out of him at a cost nobody had yet tallied — had left him rosy and bright-eyed, sturdier than before, as if sickness had been wrung out of him and replaced with something else entirely. His mother hovered in the corner, wringing a cloth that had been wrung so many times it had achieved a state of permanent dampness.

"He sleeps two hours a night now," the mother whispered. "The rest of the time he's... busy."

"Busy doing what?"

"Reciting." The mother's voice cracked on the word. "Things. Words I don't know. Lists of names. He says them like a prayer but we don't pray anymore, miss, nobody prays, that's the law—"

"I know," Lior said gently. "I'll talk to him."

* * *

They sat on the floor because Wren insisted the floor was where conversations happened. He took the candied ginger with grave courtesy, chewed one piece thoughtfully, and said, "You're frightened of me."

"A little," Lior admitted. Gentle honesty had been Aldous's method with difficult clients; she was only now discovering how much of her master lived in her habits.

"That's silly," Wren said. "I'm just me. He's the one you should be frightened of." He considered. "But he says you shouldn't be frightened of him either. He says fear is a door held shut from the inside. Whatever that means." He shrugged with the magnificent dismissiveness of a child quoting scripture. "He talks like that all the time. Like a book nobody finished."

Lior's heart was doing something painful against her ribs. "Wren. Can you tell me what he says? The things you recite at night. Can you say any of them now?"

Wren put down the ginger. He folded his small hands in his lap, and his face changed — not into anything sinister, which would have been easier to bear, but into attentiveness, the look of a boy listening hard to a teacher only he could hear. When he spoke again, the words came out in a rhythm that raised every hair on Lior's arms.

*"Ash-kevar, ash-dolen, ash-veth."*

Old Hollowtongue. The guild's liturgical tongue, taught only to sworn members, dead since the Purge, and this child pronounced it better than Lior ever had. She knew the words. Every apprentice knew those words. They were the opening of the Oath of the Hollow — *from ash we came, from ash we hold, from ash we turn.*

"What comes next?" she whispered.

*"Vess-ah dorren, Vess-ah keel"* — what wakes beneath, what hungers below — and then, in plain Vessian, in Wren's own clear seven-year-old voice: "I bind myself to the hollow place, until the last door is opened or the last hand falls away from it."

Lior stopped breathing. It was the oath. All of it. The full Oath of the Hollow Guild, the one she had taken at sixteen with Aldous's hand heavy on her shoulder, the one whose final lines were sealed — sealed, secret, known only to masters. And Wren was speaking them like a nursery rhyme.

"Wren," she said carefully. "Where did you learn that?"

He blinked, and was a boy again, reaching for the ginger. "I didn't learn it. You know things by learning them. This is just *in* me. Like the words for colors." He popped a piece of candy in his mouth. "He says you took the oath wrong, by the way. Your pronunciation of *dolen*. Very flat. He winced."

Despite everything — despite the dread sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone — Lior laughed. It came out wet and startled. "He always did wince."

"He says you always did laugh when you were scared. He says—" Wren paused, listening, and his brow furrowed with an effort that made him look much younger. "He says to tell you: *the god under Vess is waking, and it is not waking kindly.*"

The room went very quiet. Downstairs, someone was arguing about the price of fish. The ordinary world carried on, obstinately.

"It's hungry," Wren said, and now his voice had a strange doubled quality, as if the words were passing through him rather than from him. "It has been hungry for forty years. It ate nothing for forty years, do you understand, girl, and now every answered prayer is a meal, and it does not know the difference between a mother praying for her son's fever to break and a king praying for his rival's death. It will answer everything. It will answer *everything*, and it cannot tell mercy from appetite, because hunger never could." Wren's small hands gripped his knees. "And it is half-mad from starving. A mad thing that answers prayers, Lior. Think what that means. Think what it will make of this city."

Lior thought of the charm that shouldn't have worked. Of the prayer-burns people were already muttering about — scorched hollows appearing in walls and cobbles across Vess, each one shaped like nothing, each one humming faintly if you put your palm near. She had felt them for days now, a hook-tug behind her ribs, one tug for each burn, as if something vast had noticed her existence and found it interesting.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why warn me? Aldous is dead. I'm the last of a guild of two hundred, and most of those two hundred are informers and corpses."

Wren tilted his head, listening. Then he smiled — Aldous's smile, dry and crooked, arriving on a child's face like sunlight through the wrong window. "Because," he said, "you are the heir. Because the Door must be found before the hunger finds its own way out. And because—" the smile faded, and the boy's own voice returned, small and honest, "—he says he's sorry. For leaving you the mess. His words. He says he was always leaving you the mess. He says the dishes especially."

"That's true," Lior managed. Her eyes were burning. "The dishes especially."

* * *

Wren's side of the day, as he would later retell it to anyone who would listen:

The woman with the sad coat came back. Good. He had liked her even before the man arrived, because she had sold his mother the luck-knot that saved him, and also because she had the kind of face that tried very hard not to hope and failed constantly.

Now she sat on the floor with him and asked careful questions, and he answered them, and between his answers the man answered too, using his mouth the way you use a doorway — just walking through it, no trouble at all. It wasn't scary, exactly. It was like sharing bread. Except the bread was words and the other eater was enormous.

Wren could feel the enormity of him the way you feel thunder through a floor. Most of the time the man stayed behind Wren's eyes, quiet, a weight and a warmth, humming with thoughts too big to fit. But when the woman asked her questions — her scared questions, her brave ones — the man leaned forward, and Wren let him, because the man was sad in a deep old way, and because he called Wren *little door* affectionately, and because he promised, in the formal old tongue that tasted like candle smoke, never to take more than he was given.

Wren believed him. Mostly. There was one thing he hadn't told anyone, not his mother, not the coat-woman: sometimes, at the bottom of the man's presence, there was something else. Something vaster and colder that the man himself was holding shut, the way you lean your whole body against a door. When Wren pressed his ear against that inner door in the night, he heard a sound like a city chewing.

He decided the coat-woman should probably know that eventually. But not today. Today there was candy, and she laughed when he teased her about her flat vowels, and for a minute everyone in the room — the woman, the man, even the mother pretending to fold laundry — sounded almost happy.

Today was good. Wren hoarded good days now. He had a feeling they were going to be rationed.

* * *

By late afternoon, Lior had filled six pages of her notebook in a cipher of her own devising, and Wren had fallen asleep mid-sentence, sprawled against her arm like it was the most natural thing in the world. She sat very still under his weight and watched the light move across the floor and let herself feel, for one measured minute, the full strangeness of her life.

Aldous was alive, or something wearing his voice was. It had taken a dead language and a living child to reach her. The god under Vess was starving and insane and beginning, bite by answered bite, to wake. And somewhere out there, Inquisitor Sarel Vane was asking pointed questions about a healed boy, because Inquisitors always asked pointed questions about miracles. It was only a matter of time before the pointed questions arrived at this door.

"She can't find him," Lior said softly, to the sleeping boy, to the man behind the boy's eyes. "If the Inquisition learns what he's carrying—"

*They will burn him,* the voice said, directly into her mind this time, and she flinched at the intimacy of it. *Or worse. Sarel Vane is not cruel, which makes her more dangerous, not less. Cruel people can be predicted. Believers act from love.*

"You sound like you knew her."

*I burned her seminary thesis,* the voice said, with something horribly like nostalgia. *It was very good. That was the problem.*

Lior closed her notebook. "Then we move him tonight. There's a woman in the Guild Ward — Tessin — he was guild, before. He denies it now, but he owes Aldous, and Aldous was never once in his life without a ledger of debts owed to him." She paused. "Was he."

*No,* said the voice in her head, sounding proud and ashamed at once. *He was not.*

Downstairs, a bell began ringing — the ward bell, three quick strokes, the pattern that meant officials in the street. The mother appeared in the doorway, white-faced, cloth finally dropped.

"There's a wagon at the corner," she whispered. "Gray livery. Someone's asking after the boy who recovered."

Lior was already moving, gathering Wren up out of his sleep; he woke instantly and silently, which children of the poor always could, and wrapped his arms around her neck.

Through the window, three stories down, she saw them: two gray-cloaked assistants and, standing apart from them in the lamplight with a ledger open in her hands, reading something with a slight frown of concentration, the tall figure of Inquisitor Sarel Vane.

And beside Vane, pointing up — not at the window, not at the building, but at some middle distance only she could see, with the absolute confidence of a bloodhound — stood a young assistant holding a charcoal sketch.

Even upside-down and distant, Lior recognized her own jawline.

"Hold the door behind us," she breathed — to the mother, to the room, to whatever was listening — and slipped out the back stair with another person's child in her arms and her whole life, apparently, about to become a map fragment and a rumor.
