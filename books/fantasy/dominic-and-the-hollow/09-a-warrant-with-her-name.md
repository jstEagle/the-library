## 9. A Warrant with Her Name

The trouble with hiding in a city you'd grown up in was that everyone knew your face — and the trouble with the night market specifically was that everyone who knew your face also owed you money.

Lior learned this within an hour of Tessin's warning note arriving, folded inside a delivery of dried feverfew she hadn't ordered. *They're making sketches,* it said, in his cramped apothecary's hand. *Stay off the lamps' row.* Which meant, naturally, that by dusk she had to be on the lamps' row, because her luck-knots sold best under the third lantern where the light came down amber and forgiving, and because rent on her room was due, and because — if she was honest, which she tried not to be — a part of her wanted to see the sketch.

She wanted to know what they thought she looked like.

So there she was, hood up, standing at her old pitch with a fresh batch of charms laid out on a cloth that had once been white, when Dalla the fishwife stopped mid-stride, squinted at her, and said, "You look terrible, Eastwick."

"Thank you, Dalla."

"No, I mean it. Like something that got left out overnight." Dalla bought two luck-knots anyway, because fishwives were practical people and luck was luck regardless of how the seller looked. "There's soldiers at the fountain gate. Inquisitor's people. They've got papers with drawings on them."

Lior's hands went very still over her cloth. "Drawings of smugglers?"

"Drawings of everyone." Dalla leaned in, lowering her voice to the register fishwives used for gossip of genuine quality. "My cousin saw one. Said it was a woman, young, dark-haired, with a scar—" Lior touched her own jaw, where the scar lived, thin as a thread, before she could stop herself. Dalla's eyes went from the gesture to Lior's face and back, and something kind and terrible happened in them. "Ah," said Dalla.

"Dalla—"

"I never saw you." She pocketed her knots. "And if anyone asks, you were here earlier selling me a charm against gout, which is a lie because nothing works against gout, but it's the sort of lie people believe." She patted Lior's shoulder with a hand that smelled honestly of fish and dishonestly of nothing else, and moved off into the crowd like a boat putting out to sea.

Lior should have packed up then. She knew that the way you know a stair has a missing step: intellectually, thoroughly, and only in retrospect usefully. But the coins in her pouch were few, the fever-wards took another hour to set properly, and some stubborn guild-trained corner of her brain kept insisting that a charm finished was a charm finished, and half-made wards were worse than none. So she stayed. She tied the last knot. She wrapped the last ward in waxed paper. And that was precisely how long it took for the market to go quiet in the particular way markets do when authority walks into them — not silent, never silent, Vess's poor were incapable of true silence, but quiet in the joints, a hush that traveled faster than boots.

She heard the boots next. Then the voice, carrying over the stalls with the easy projection of someone who had done this many times and enjoyed it exactly as much as she pretended to: "Good evening. Lovely evening for honesty."

Sarel Vane.

* * *

Lior did not run. Running was the first thing they looked for; Aldous had taught her that in her second year, along with a hundred other lessons she'd resented at the time and now recited to herself like scripture. *Walk slower than you want to. Look bored. Boredom is invisible.* So she walked, slow and bored, toward the tanner's alley with her cloth bundled under one arm like laundry, while behind her Sarel Vane's voice moved through the market like a comb through tangled hair.

"Papers, please. Stall registration? No? How interesting. And what do you call this?" A pause. "A knot."

"It's a luck-knot," said a vendor whose name Lior didn't catch, in a voice wobbling between defiance and apology.

"A knot," Sarel repeated, and there was something in her tone that wasn't mockery, exactly. It was almost gentle, which was worse. "Do you know what we used to hang people for, in my grandmother's time? Less than this. You're lucky to live in kinder days." Paper rustled. "Sign here acknowledging confiscation. You'll get a receipt. The receipt is worth nothing, but you'll get one."

Lior reached the mouth of the tanner's alley, and that was when she saw the other thing: the sketch itself, nailed to the notice board by the fountain gate, flanked by two of Vane's gray-cloaked questioners. She couldn't help it. She looked.

It was a good likeness. That was the horror of it. Not flattering, not cruel — just accurate. The artist had caught the set of her mouth, the wariness around the eyes, the scar drawn as a single confident stroke. Underneath, in official block letters: *WANTED FOR QUESTIONING. LAST SEEN SELLING UNLICENSED CHARMS, NIGHT MARKET. ANSWERS TO THE NAME LIOR EASTWICK.*

*Tessin,* she thought, with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear. *Oh, Tessin, they went through your records.*

Which meant he was already being questioned. Which meant every hour she spent admiring her own portrait was an hour Tessin spent paying for her.

She cut down the tanner's alley at a pace just short of running, boredom abandoned, and came out behind the chandlers' row where Tessin's shop crouched between a candle-maker and a dyer, its windows dark, its door — she noted with a spike of alarm — ajar.

Inside, by the light of a single stub of candle, Tessin was methodically destroying his inventory.

Not burning it. Not yet. He was grinding it. Mortar and pestle, working with the calm of a man kneading bread, reducing months of licensed, legal, meticulously documented apothecary stock into a fine gray powder that he swept into a bucket of water. Feverfew, valerian, willowbark, comfrey — all of it perfectly legal, all of it useless to an Inquisitor, all of it going into the bucket anyway.

"Tessin."

"You're supposed to be east of the river by now." He didn't look up. He was older than she remembered him being, though he couldn't have aged more than three years since the funeral — grayer at the temples, softer in the middle, with the resigned stoop of a man who had spent a decade pretending very hard to be nobody.

"They have a sketch of me."

"I know. I described you." Now he looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Under questioning. Before they asked. I told them everything I could remember about your face so they wouldn't dig further into anything else. It seemed like a fair trade at the time." He went back to the mortar. "I'm revising my opinion."

"The shop, Tessin. Your license. Your whole life—"

"My whole life," he agreed pleasantly, "is about to become a pile of ash, so let's not sentimentalize it in advance. Sit down. Have a lozenge. They're licorice; they were going to be evidence in about ten minutes anyway."

Lior sat on a stool surrounded by the smell of a hundred ordinary remedies, and watched a careful man unmake himself, and understood — with the cold, settling clarity of ice forming — why he was doing it.

"They'll trace the charm," she said. "The mother's charm. Through me to the guildhall, and from the guildhall to anyone who ever—"

"To anyone who ever trained beside me, yes." The pestle scraped. "Vane pulled the confiscated ledgers from the Purge years. My name is in them. Yours is in them. Aldous's whole roster is in them, and until this week those ledgers were dust in a basement. Now they're a hunting map." He tipped the powder into the bucket. Stirred. "I made a bargain with myself when the guild fell, Lior. I said: keep your head down, sell cough syrup to respectable people, and survive. And it worked. For eleven years it worked beautifully, right up until a woman prayed in a night market and the world answered her."

"You don't know that it was my charm."

"Lior." He set down the pestle and finally, fully, looked at her, and there was no anger in his face at all, which was somehow the worst thing yet. "I taught you binding knots. I know your work the way a mother knows handwriting. Whatever answered that prayer, it answered through you. And whatever is waking up because of it — Vane can feel it too, even if she calls it something else. Everyone can. The city's been holding its breath for a month." He wiped his hands on his apron. "The question isn't whether they come for us. It's whether they come for both of us at once, or whether we give them a reason to be satisfied with one."

"No." Lior stood up so fast the stool rocked. "Absolutely not. I am not letting you—"

"You're not *letting* me anything." He smiled, and it was Aldous's smile, or near enough that her throat closed. "That's the trouble with apprentices. They think the guild ran on hierarchy. It ran on debt. You pay forward what was paid for you. Someone paid for me once, at cost I never learned. Tonight I'm paying for you, and you'll hate it, and someday — gods willing, or god, whichever of them is listening — you'll pay for someone else, and they'll hate it too, and that's the entire secret of the Hollow Guild, passed down free of charge."

"That's not the entire secret. There were also the oaths, and the liturgy, and forty volumes of—"

"Lior."

"—and honestly the accounting practices alone took me two years to—"

"*Lior.*" He took her face in both flour-soft hands, the way Aldous used to when she was small and furious. "Listen to me once without arguing. It's the last professional courtesy I'll ever ask of you."

She listened.

"There's a back way out through the dye-house. The dyer owes me for his wife's lungs and won't ask questions. Take the cloth bundle, take nothing else, and don't stop moving until you're past the tanneries. When the fire starts — and it will start, because I've already soaked the storeroom and I'm very thorough — you keep walking. You don't look back. Looking back is how they identify accomplices." He pressed something into her palm: small, wrapped in waxed paper, heavier than a lozenge had any right to be. "For later. Don't open it in front of anyone."

"What is it?"

"Sentiment, probably. Open it when you're somewhere safe and cry if you need to; it's medicinal." He turned back to his bucket of ruined medicine, calm as Sunday. "Now go, before I lose my nerve, because unlike you I was never any good at the brave parts."

* * *

She made it four steps into the dye-house passage before the first shout went up from the direction of the fountain gate.

They'd found the sketch-witnesses, or the witnesses had found courage, or simply the hour had come; it didn't matter. What mattered was the sound of gray cloaks moving through the night market in formation, and Sarel Vane's voice cutting clean above it: "Chandler's row. The apothecary's. Move."

Tessin had perhaps two minutes. Maybe less.

Lior stood in the dark passage with the waxed paper biting into her palm and did the arithmetic every coward and every hero does at moments like this, and discovered to her fury that the sums came out the same either way: one fugitive saved, one shopkeeper lost, and no version of the ledger where she changed the entries without erasing herself.

*Walk away. Walk away. Walk away.*

She walked back.

"Tessin—" she started, and stopped, because he was already looking at her with an expression of profound, weary resignation, the expression of a man watching a student fail the easiest exam of her life.

"I told you not to look back."

"I'm not looking back. I'm walking forward in the wrong direction, which is different." Her mind was sprinting ahead of her mouth, hauling up scraps — the guild's contingency drills, the ones they'd practiced as children as a game, hide-and-seek with consequences. "The storeroom. Where did you soak it?"

"Lamp oil and spirits, floor to shelf, why—"

"The chimney draws north. If you light it from the south wall low and let it climb, the flash goes *up*, not out — the front room stays intact thirty seconds longer, long enough for anyone coming through the door to see nothing but smoke and believe the rest. And the wind tonight—" she'd felt it on the walk over, off the river, gusting east — "the wind carries the smoke across the square, which means their eyes water and their lanterns gutter, and nobody identifies anybody in a smoke column." She was already moving as she talked, dragging the spirit keg to the south wall, because her hands had decided before her fear finished objecting. "It buys you minutes. Minutes are how the guild always survived. Not power. Not magic. Minutes and spite."

Tessin stared at her. And then, slowly, he began to laugh — a real laugh, cracked and helpless, the first one she'd heard from him in three years. "Eleven years I kept that drill locked up in my head," he said, "telling myself it was a children's game, telling myself I was a chemist now, a licensed man with receipts—" He struck flint to steel, once, twice, and the tinder in the south-wall soak caught with a soft blue bloom. "Minutes and spite. Gods, Aldous would have loved you tonight."

"Tessin, come *with* me. The dye-house passage—"

"And confirm to every questioner on chandler's row that the apothecary fled with the fugitive?" He shook his head, stepping back from the growing fire with the serenity of a man who had already argued himself through every exit and closed each one deliberately. "No. I stay. I answer questions. Badly, slowly, with many complaints about my knees. I am a frightened old tradesman whose shop caught fire, possibly arson by smugglers, certainly nothing to do with me, and I know *nothing*, Lior, because I spent eleven years becoming a man who knows nothing, and I intend to give them excellent value for it." The heat was climbing now, orange light crawling up the shelves. "Go."

"Tell me where to find you after. Tell me you'll—"

"Eastgate prison, probably. Or a cell under the Chapterhouse." He said it lightly, the way you'd discuss the weather, and then not lightly at all: "But listen — listen — if you get out, if you find whatever it is you're finding, the Door or the god or the bottom of this whole rotten business — finish it. Don't avenge me. *Finish it.* Avenge me and you'll just make more Tessins. Finish it and maybe there aren't any more Tessins to make." He pushed her toward the passage, firelight gilding one side of his tired face. "Now walk. And Lior — the waxed paper. When you open it. I want to know if you cry."

The last thing she saw of him was his silhouette against his own burning shop, hands folded, waiting with the patience of a craftsman for the knock at his door.

* * *

She kept her word. She didn't look back — not at the shop, not at the crowd gathering in the square with buckets they were too slow to fill, not at the small gray figure being escorted out through the smoke with his wrists bound and his spine, even now, straight as a plumb line. She walked. Past the tanneries, past the last lamps, out onto the river road where the east wind carried the smell of burning rosemary and valerian and eleven careful years, and the city behind her glowed like a coal someone had breathed on.

Only when Vess was a smudge against the stars did she stop, under a dead elm at the road's edge, and unwrap the waxed paper with fingers that refused, at first, to be steady.

Inside was a lozenge. One licorice lozenge, slightly crushed.

And beneath it, folded eight times, a scrap of paper in Tessin's cramped hand — written, by the smudge of ink transfer, weeks ago and kept ready, the way careful men keep things:

*If you're reading this, I gambled and lost, which means you won, which means it was worth it. The map fragment I gave you shows the Door's approach but not its lock. The lock is sung, not drawn — three keys, and the songs to wake them. Find Maren Cole first. She'll lie. She always lies; it's how she lives with herself. But she lies *around* the truth, and if you're patient, the shape of the hole tells you the shape of the thing.*

*One more thing. The night the guild fell, Aldous sent me away with the same speech I gave you tonight — debt paid forward, someone always pays. I thought it was comfort. It wasn't. It was instruction.*

*You're the last one holding the ledger, Lior. Balance it well.*

*— T.*

She ate the lozenge. It tasted of licorice and smoke and, faintly, of a shop that no longer existed.

Then Lior Eastwick wiped her eyes with her sleeve — crying counted as medicinal, Tessin had said so, and he'd been right, it loosened something in her chest that had been seizing since the fountain gate — and turned her face east, where the road ran out of Vess toward villages that weren't on maps anymore.

Behind her, the city burned bright with answered prayers and confiscated paper, and somewhere in its heart a woman named Sarel Vane was asking her questions of a man who knew nothing, patiently, thoroughly, and getting, for the first time in her career, answers that frightened her.

Lior tightened her grip on the cloth bundle and the scrap of paper, and started walking.

Maren Cole. Who would lie.

Fine. Lior had been raised by liars, saints, and accountants, and she knew exactly how to wait out a lie until it showed its shape.
