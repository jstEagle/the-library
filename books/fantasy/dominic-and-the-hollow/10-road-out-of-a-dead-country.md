## 10. Road Out of a Dead Country

The road east out of Vess smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and for the first six miles Lior kept waiting for the sound of hoofbeats behind her.

They never came. That was somehow worse.

She had left before dawn, when the night market's last sellers were dousing their lamps and the ash-sellers were arriving to sweep up the morning's trade. She'd taken nothing but her satchel of charms — the ones that didn't work, mostly, though after everything she was no longer sure *mostly* meant what it used to — a change of clothes, the map fragment Tessin had pressed into her palm like contraband, and three days' worth of hard bread that tasted the way an apology looks.

Tessin was in an Inquisition cell because of her. Or rather, he was in a cell because he had chosen to be, which was worse, because now she owed him not just gratitude but the particular crushing debt of someone who had *decided* to pay your price for you.

"Keep walking," she told herself out loud, because the road was empty and nobody could hear a woman talking to herself except crows, and crows were famously discreet.

The countryside around Vess flattened into farmland, then rose again into scrubby hills where sheep watched her pass with the flat judgment only sheep can manage. She walked until her feet complained, then walked further, because complaint was a luxury and Inquisitors did not stop for sore heels.

By late afternoon she reached a crossroads with a leaning post and a sign nailed to it at an angle that suggested either haste or contempt. The sign read:

**EASTMAR PROVINCE — 40 LEAGUES**

Below it, someone had nailed a second board over the first, official-looking, stamped with a seal she recognized from tax documents and wanted posters: the crowned hand of the Chancery.

**PROVINCE DISSOLVED BY DECREE OF THE CHANCELLOR. THIS ROAD ADMINISTERED DIRECTLY FROM VESS. NO LOCAL AUTHORITY RECOGNIZED.**

Lior stared at it for a long moment.

"Dissolved," she said to the nearest sheep. "You can't dissolve a province. It has *hills*."

The sheep chewed.

But of course they could. The Chancery dissolved things all the time — guilds, parishes, names, people. Eastmar had been a border province once, back when there had been a border worth guarding, back before the Purge had made the whole country one gray administrative body with Vess as its head and heart and increasingly its entire nervous system. She remembered Aldous grumbling about it, years ago, sharpening his knife on a whetstone worn to a smile: *"Used to be you couldn't cross into Eastmar without a stamp and a bribe, girl. Now you can't cross into it at all, because officially it isn't anywhere."*

Officially. Which meant the maps would show nothing. Which meant —

She pulled out Tessin's fragment with hands that were colder than the evening justified. It was old vellum, cracked along one edge, drawn in the careful faded ink of a guild cartographer. It showed a stretch of country east of Vess, hills and a river and small neat marks for villages, and at its center, circled twice and annotated in a cramped hand she didn't recognize, a symbol she did: the hollow square. The Door.

Except the fragment showed the Door sitting squarely inside Eastmar Province. And Eastmar Province, according to the crowned hand, did not exist.

"Well," Lior said to the sheep, tucking the fragment away. "That simplifies things."

It did not simplify anything.

* * *

She slept that night in a haybarn whose owner she never saw, and dreamed of Aldous standing behind a door, saying something she couldn't hear, his voice muffled the way voices are muffled through stone. When she woke, her fingers had been tracing the guild sigil under her sleeve without her permission, over and over, like a rosary, or a tic.

Three more days east. The land changed subtly — the fields grew narrower, hedgerows taller, the farms older, their stones fitted in patterns that predated any decree. And the churches changed too.

That was how she noticed it first. In the villages near Vess, the temples stood tall and bare, Purge-white, their niches empty, their altars holding nothing but the Chancery's approved image of the Unseen Order — which was to say, nothing at all, represented tastefully by an empty frame. But here, the little whitewashed chapels had something *off* about them. A lintel carved with wheat sheaves instead of the crown. A weathervane shaped like no bird she knew. A doorstep worn strangely smooth in front, as if generations of knees had rested there.

And in the third village — a place called Brant's Hollow, if the crooked sign could be trusted, which was already more than most places deserved — she saw the old woman.

It was evening, lamplighting time, and the woman came out of her cottage carrying a covered dish, walked to the edge of her garden where a fieldstone stood half-buried in the grass, set the dish down, uncovered it — bread and honey, Lior saw, steam still rising — spoke a few words too low to catch, and went back inside.

An offering. An honest-to-gods offering, laid out in the open, in a country where the penalty for open worship was confiscation first and questions later.

Lior should have walked past. Every survival instinct she owned — and she owned a modest but functional collection — said walk past, eyes down, charm-seller's shuffle, nothing to see here.

Instead she knocked on the woman's door.

The woman who opened it was small and round and looked at Lior the way farmers look at weather: assessing, unimpressed, prepared for disappointment. "If you're selling, I've got no coin. If you're collecting taxes, I've got less than that. If you're the law—" she squinted, "—you're too short and too dirty. So which is it?"

"I'm none of those," Lior said. "I'm... traveling east."

"Everyone's traveling somewhere. Doesn't answer my question." But the woman stepped aside anyway, in the manner of people who have decided something and see no reason to announce it. "Come in before the damp does. You look like a soup situation."

Her name was Dalla. Her cottage smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and, underneath both, something faintly sweet and old, like the inside of a chapel that hadn't been whitewashed recently enough. On the mantel sat a row of small stones, each painted with a mark.

Lior stopped dead.

They weren't guild sigils. They weren't quite anything she knew. But they were *adjacent* — the same grammar, different language. Binding-marks. Ward-shapes. One of them, near the end, was almost exactly the hollow square, if the hollow square had been drawn by someone working from a description given secondhand by a drunk.

"You know what those are," Dalla said. Not a question.

"I know what they're *like*," Lior said carefully.

"Mm." Dalla ladled soup into a bowl and pushed it across the table with the authority of a woman who had fed reluctant eaters for sixty years and would not be argued with. "My grandmother's grandmother's grandmother learned them from a walker who came through before the Purge. A guildsman, she said, though she said it the way you'd say 'a ghost.' He stayed a season, taught the village the shapes, told them: *when the burning comes, hide them in bread and stones and doorsteps, and keep the words alive in the mouths of children.*" She shrugged. "So we did. Four generations. Nobody's been burned yet. Mind you, nobody's been *saved* much either. The shapes mostly don't do anything anymore."

"They don't," Lior agreed, and then, because the soup was excellent and honesty felt affordable for once: "Mostly."

Dalla's eyebrows climbed. "Mostly."

"There's a fever-ward shape there, third from the left. You've got the angle wrong — the cross-stroke should lean east, not south. As drawn, it's just decoration. Corrected..." Lior turned her spoon over. "I honestly don't know anymore. A month ago I'd have said nothing. Charms are sympathy and suggestion and a fair bit of theater. But lately things I sell have been... overperforming."

"Overperforming," Dalla repeated, with the relish of a woman filing away a word she intended to use on her neighbors.

"A boy's fever broke off one of my luck-knots. It shouldn't have. And now—" She stopped. She had been not-talking-about-Wren for four days with tremendous discipline, and the discipline broke all at once, like a dam made of tissue paper. "Now there's a child who wakes up speaking a dead language, and prayers are being answered all over Vess, and every answered prayer burns a hole in the world, and the woman hunting me is starting to doubt the thing she burned people for believing, and apparently the province I'm walking into was *dissolved*, which means the place I'm looking for is officially nowhere, and my dead master talks to me through doors."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"Well," said Dalla at last. "You *do* look like a soup situation."

* * *

She stayed two nights, because Dalla fed her and asked nothing and because, though she'd never have admitted it, the cottage was the first place since Tessin's arrest where she'd slept without listening for hoofbeats.

On the second evening, Dalla took her to the fieldstone at dusk, along with half the village, it seemed — a dozen quiet figures drifting out of cottages with covered dishes, moving with the unselfconscious choreography of long practice. They laid offerings on the stone and on two others Lior hadn't noticed, half-hidden in the hedge line. No words were spoken above a murmur. No god was named. The whole rite took less time than boiling water.

"What do you actually say?" Lior whispered. "I heard shapes, not words."

"Old words. Half of them we don't understand anymore." Dalla's murmur was unbothered. "Grandmother always said understanding wasn't the point. *Faithfulness is the point,* she said. *The gods can tell the difference between a prayer that understands and a prayer that stays.*"

"And which do they prefer?"

"If tonight's anything to go by," Dalla said dryly, nodding at the untouched honey bread, "they prefer dinner."

But as the villagers drifted home and the dark settled properly, Lior lingered by the stone, and put her palm flat against it, and felt — faintly, distantly, the way you feel a train through the ground before you hear it — something answering.

Not hungry. Not the vast pulling ache she'd felt in Vess, the hook behind her ribs that tightened with each new burn-mark in the city walls. This was smaller. Sleepier. A warmth like a banked coal that had been tended faithfully by people who didn't remember why.

"*Stay,*" she whispered, in Old Hollowtongue, without deciding to — the word simply arrived in her mouth, tasting of Aldous's lessons.

The warmth pulsed once, gently, like something rolling over in its sleep.

She walked back to the cottage with her heart hammering and her face wet and couldn't have told anyone, least of all herself, whether it was terror or homesickness. Possibly the two had stopped being different things somewhere on the road.

* * *

East of Brant's Hollow, the world got less cooperative.

The roads thinned to tracks, the tracks to suggestions. Twice she asked directions from farmwives and both times got the same unsettling answer: a blank look, followed by, "East? There's nothing east. There's the old province, or what was, but the surveyors came through years back and struck it off. No roads kept up past the river. No record. You can walk it, mind, folk do, but you'll be walking through nowhere, and nowhere doesn't feed travelers."

One old man put it more plainly, spitting into his fire: "Chancery says Eastmar's dissolved. Dissolved means the maps show white. White means if you break your leg out there, you never happened."

Lior thanked him and lay awake that night doing arithmetic she didn't like.

Tessin's fragment placed the Door in the middle of that white. Which meant either the fragment was wrong, or the guild had built its greatest secret in a place the Chancery had erased so thoroughly that even finding it required finding someone who mapped the unmappable. And the fragment was only a fragment. Somewhere there was more — a whole map, or the man who'd drawn it.

She turned the vellum over for the hundredth time and noticed, for the hundred-and-first time, the tiny notation in the corner she'd read a dozen times and never really *heard*: a cartographer's signature mark, half worn away — *I—t*, and beneath it, in pencil added later, almost apologetically, a single word: *exiled.*

"Exiled," she murmured. "Well. Exiles find each other. Everyone knows that."

Everyone did not, in fact, know that, but it sounded true at midnight, alone in a barn in a dissolved province, and truth at midnight is the kind you act on.

* * *

The villages kept coming, thinner and stranger as she went. Worship in secret became worship in half-light became — as the Chancery's reach stretched and thinned — worship that barely bothered hiding. A chapel with the empty frame shoved in a corner and a plain bowl of river water on the altar, which everyone understood was not *nothing*, whatever the law said nothing was. A milestone painted with warding marks. Children skipping rope to a counting rhyme that was, Lior realized with a jolt, a garbled scrap of a binding hymn — the same hymn she'd sung at Aldous's funeral, stripped of its tongue but keeping its bones.

In one village an innkeeper refused her coin for supper and asked instead that she look at his daughter's chapped hands, and when Lior admitted she couldn't heal so much as a headache, the man laughed and said, "Didn't think so. But you've got the look. Haven't seen the look since I was a boy." He wouldn't explain what the look was. He gave her extra bread anyway.

The loneliness, which had been a cold constant since Vess, began to change texture. She was alone — truly alone, no master, no guild, no Tessin, and Wren was somewhere behind her in custody and the thought of that was a stone she carried in her chest and set down only to pick up again — and yet the road itself seemed to know her. Every mile east, the country hummed a little louder under her boots. Old faiths, half-asleep. Waiting, maybe. Or maybe just enduring, the way Dalla's family had endured, keeping the words alive in the mouths of children without knowing what the words were *for*.

On the ninth day, at a ford where the map fragment's river should have been — and was, though no map in Vess would admit it — she met trouble.

Two men on the far bank, dressed in the mismatched gear of men who rob travelers for a living and have stopped pretending otherwise. One had a crossbow held in the confident grip of a man who had never actually fired one at anything that could complain.

"Road toll," the confident one called across the water.

"It's a *ford*," Lior called back. "Fords don't have tolls. That's the entire point of a ford."

"New policy."

"Dissolved province," the other muttered, in the tone of a man citing scripture. "No law past the river. Means tolls are legal."

"That is *spectacularly* not how law works," Lior said, and then thought, with distant hysteria, that this was precisely how law worked now, everywhere, and that Sarel Vane would probably agree with the bandits on principle while arresting them for vagrancy.

What saved her was neither wit nor magic. What saved her was the miller.

He appeared out of his mill upstream like a boulder developing opinions — a huge grey-bearded man carrying a millstone's worth of presence and an actual blacksmith's hammer, and behind him, absurdly, seven villagers armed with farming tools and the grim enthusiasm of people who had been waiting years for a legitimate excuse.

"River's free," the miller said. "Always has been. Always will be."

The bandits assessed the mathematics of seven scythes versus one crossbow and concluded, correctly, that the crossbow had been optimistic from the start. They withdrew with the dignity of the deeply outranked.

The miller looked at Lior across the water. Looked at her travel-worn satchel. At her sleeve, where the sigil hid. Something in his face shifted — recognition, or its ancestor.

"Guild," he said. Not loudly.

"I'm the last of it," Lior said.

"Huh." He nodded slowly, the way men nod at funerals and good harvests. "Then you'd best get where you're going. Before there aren't any last-ofs left." He pointed east, upriver, to where the hills folded together around a valley that no map bothered naming. "Old road runs through the pass. Surveyors skipped it — too steep to bother striking off. There's a town past it that isn't on any list. Folk there do ink work. Maps, charts, records." His eyes narrowed slightly. "Man there came from Vess some years back. Exile. Keeps to himself. Draws places that aren't supposed to exist anymore."

Lior's heart performed a maneuver her ribs objected to.

"He wouldn't be named Ivet, would he?"

The miller's eyebrows rose. "You *have* met."

"No," Lior said, and found herself smiling for the first time in nine days — tired, disbelieving, but real. "But I'm about to."

She crossed the ford on stones the villagers steadied for her, took the old road into the pass as the light went gold and then blue, and walked toward the one person alive who might map the country the Chancery had murdered — carrying a dead guild's name, a fragment of vellum, and the growing, terrifying suspicion that exile was not a punishment the road dealt out at random.

Exiles, she thought, climbing. Exiles get sent where they're needed.

The pass swallowed the sunset behind her, and ahead, somewhere past the dark, a man who drew places that didn't exist was waiting to learn why.
