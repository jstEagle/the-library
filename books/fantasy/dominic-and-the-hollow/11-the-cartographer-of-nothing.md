## 11. The Cartographer of Nothing

The trouble with mapping a place that officially does not exist, Ivet Sarn had learned over eleven years of exile, is that you have to be very careful about who sees your ink.

He had built his life on that principle. His cottage outside the drowned hamlet of Pell's Ford — itself erased from the records two generations back, which was why he could afford it — had one room for living and one room for lying. The lying room was where he kept the maps. Not the licensed ones, the gray-and-gold surveys of roads and rivers that paid his bread, but the others: charts of villages struck from the rolls, forests renamed into nothing by decree, an entire eastern province dissolved so thoroughly that even the word for it had been pulled from schoolbooks like a bad tooth. He drew them from memory, from rumor, from the accounts of smugglers and pilgrims and one memorable deaf shepherd who communicated entirely in gestures and was, Ivet suspected, the finest geographer alive.

Tonight the lying room smelled of tallow and wet wool, because it was raining the way it only rained in the borderlands — sideways, insulted, as though the sky held a grudge against the concept of roofs.

Ivet heard the knock at half past midnight and knew before he opened the door that it would change something. Nobody knocked at half past midnight out of politeness.

"Are you the cartographer?" said the girl on his step.

She was soaked through, thin as a winter branch, and she had the particular exhausted alertness of someone who had been walking for days while listening behind herself. Mud to the knees. A satchel worn to shine at the strap. And beneath her left sleeve — he caught only a glimpse when she shifted her weight, but he had spent a lifetime noticing what officialdom tried to hide — the ghost of a mark. Inked over, maybe burned over, but there.

"I'm a surveyor of record," Ivet said, which was the standard lie, delivered with the standard apologetic shrug. "Roads and bridges mostly. Nothing exciting."

"Then you won't mind if I don't believe you," she said, "because nobody walks three days off the pilgrim road to ask a bridge-surveyor a question."

He liked her immediately. That was going to be a problem.

"And what question," he said, stepping aside, "would you have asked a bridge-surveyor?"

The girl — Lior, she said, giving only the name and no more, which he respected — stood dripping on his good floor mat and looked around the living room with the flat professional assessment he usually saw in tax assessors. Then her eyes snagged on the wall above his hearth, where he'd hung, as a joke only he understood, an authentic Surveyor-General's certificate of competency, awarded to Ivet Sarn, and beside it, deliberately crooked, a child's charcoal drawing of a house with too many windows.

"That's not a child's drawing," she said quietly.

"No."

"It's Errow."

He turned to look at it properly. Eleven years, and he still felt the small cold lift in his chest whenever someone else saw it. "It's a village that doesn't exist," he said. "You're standing in the cottage of a man whose entire career consists of drawing things that don't exist. Would you like tea? It's terrible tea, but there's a great deal of it."

* * *

Her name came up eventually, the way names do when two people sit across a small table long enough — not all at once but in pieces, offered and withheld in careful alternation, like traders haggling over goods neither fully trusts. She gave him Lior Eastwick and nothing else. He gave her Ivet Sarn and, after a decent interval, ex-cartographer of the Second Eastern Survey, and watched her go very still.

"The Second Eastern Survey," she repeated.

"You've heard of it."

"Nobody's heard of it. That's rather the point, isn't it? There is no second eastern province. There never was, according to every map printed in the last twenty years." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "My master used to say the Inquisition didn't just burn books. They burned *places*."

"Aldous Wren," Ivet said, and had the satisfaction of watching her flinch. "Yes, I know the name. You don't survive eleven years in exile without learning which guild names were struck hardest. Your master's was struck deepest. They didn't just erase him, they erased the hall he taught in, the street it stood on, and the well it drew water from. There are people in Vess today who walk past the spot every morning and feel a vague sadness they can't explain. That's what thorough looks like."

She set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way people do when they're afraid their hands will do something else. "Why?"

"Why were they erased? Or why did I keep drawing them?"

"Either. Both. Start anywhere."

So he told her, because it had been eleven years and the story had gone soft with disuse, like a coin kept too long in a pocket.

* * *

"I wasn't a dissident," Ivet said. "That's the shameful part. I want to be very clear that I was a *good* cartographer. I was praised. I had a stipend and a green cloak and a desk by a window, and I believed — this is important — I believed the surveys were honest. Places get dissolved sometimes. Provinces merge, borders move, hamlets drown or burn or empty out. The map has to match the world. I genuinely thought that was all it was."

Lior said nothing. She had the listening posture of someone raised by a teacher — elbows in, spine straight, eyes patient — and it made him want to confess things he hadn't planned to.

"My last assignment was routine. Resurvey of the eastern borderlands after the administrative reforms. Walk the ground, compare it to the record, note discrepancies. Standard work. Except the discrepancies started adding up wrong. I'd find a bridge where no road should lead. Boundary stones older than any charter, carved with symbols the archive called decorative. And once — once — a mile of hedgerow running dead straight to nowhere, the kind farmers plant along a road, except there was no road, and the villagers nearby looked at me strangely when I asked about it. Not hostile. *Frightened.* Frightened the way you'd be if a stranger asked you about a family member you'd been told to forget."

He got up and fetched the first map, the one he showed almost no one. It was drawn in his own careful hand, but the hand shook slightly on the eastern edge, and he'd never redrawn it, because the shake seemed honest.

"This is what I found when I compared my field notes to the master archive. The reforms didn't remove places that had stopped existing. They removed places that had *stopped being allowed to exist*. Villages with shrines. Hamlets where the tithe records showed irregular offerings — candles, grain, first-born lambs left at crossroads. Whole valleys where the parish registers simply ended mid-sentence, one generation to the next, like a book dropped in water." He laid it flat between them. "And in the middle of the largest blank space — a blank space the size of a county, Lior, blank on every map for twenty years, which is geographically impossible unless you make it so — I found one thing the erasers missed."

She leaned over it. Rain ticked against the shutters. Her finger stopped above a tiny annotation, half a line of cramped field-hand:

*Errow (name recovered from boundary stone). See attached.*

"There was no attachment," Ivet said. "There never is. But the stone was real. I went looking for it. Took me four months and I nearly died twice, and when I finally found the village the stone described — the shrine, the straight hedgerows, the well with the carved rim — the village was *there*, Lior. Smoke from chimneys. Dogs barking. An old woman waved at me. A village of perhaps two hundred souls, farming, singing, burying their dead, worshipping whatever they worship — and it appears on no document in the entire country. It has been unwritten. Not destroyed. *Unwritten.* Do you understand the difference?"

"Yes," Lior said softly. "I think I'm beginning to."

"They could have burned it. The Purge burned plenty. But burning leaves ash, and ash leaves questions, and questions leave *maps*. So instead they did something cleverer. They made it so that no official person could ever find it, because officially there was nothing there to find. The roads to it aren't closed; they're unrecorded, so no licensed traveler ever goes down them. The name isn't banned; it's absent, so no scholar ever searches for it." He tapped the little annotation. "Errow exists in the gap. And I have spent eleven years asking myself one question."

"Which is?"

"What else is in the gaps?" He sat back down. "Because here is what haunts me, apprentice Eastwick. When they unwrote Errow, they also unwrote everything *about* it. Whatever that village guarded, or hid, or prayed to — it went into the gap too. And I have spent a decade mapping the gaps, and I will tell you what I've learned: the gaps are not empty. The gaps are *full*. Every place they erased, something stayed behind in the erasing. My shepherds hear singing in the dissolved valleys. My smugglers swear the old province roads are warmer than they should be at night. And every year, the blank spaces on my maps get — " he searched for the word, embarrassed by it — "*hungrier*."

* * *

Lior was quiet for a long moment, turning her cup again, and then she asked the question he'd been waiting for since she'd seen the charcoal drawing.

"Do you have a map of Errow? A real one. With the approaches."

"I have three," Ivet said. "One is wrong because I drew it drunk on grief. One is wrong because I drew it sober and cautious, which is worse. The third one I've never shown anyone, because showing it means deciding, and I've spent eleven years not deciding."

"Not deciding what?"

"Whether I'm a mapmaker or a guide." He laughed, without much humor in it. "A mapmaker draws the world. A guide walks you into it. The moment I put this chart in your hands and point you east, I stop being a man who documents crimes and become a man who assists them. The Inquisition draws that distinction very sharply, I assure you. Documenters lose their licenses. Assistants lose other things."

"Then why show me at all?"

"Because of what you haven't told me yet." He leaned forward. "You walked three days in the rain to find a man who maps erased places. You're the last apprentice of a burned guild. Your sleeve has a sigil under it that you keep touching when you think I'm not looking, the way people touch a wound. And you said — about ten minutes ago, right after I mentioned Aldous Wren — you said 'he told me to find the Door,' and then went white as whey and drank your tea like it was an order. So either you tell me what the Door is, or I decide you're mad, and frankly, mad is easier."

She almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and he understood suddenly that she didn't do it often.

"He told me to find the Door," she said. "Aldous. My master. Who has been dead for six years." She took a breath. "Except he hasn't stopped talking. And the Door is sealed under a village that doesn't exist, and the god behind it is waking up hungry, and I need a map to a place that has been unwritten from every record in Vess. So." She spread her empty hands. "Mad might be easier. But here we are."

Here we are indeed, Ivet thought, and felt the strange lightness that comes when a decision you've been dodging for eleven years finally lands on you and turns out, unexpectedly, to be a relief.

* * *

He fetched the third map.

He unrolled it on the table between the teapot and the lamp, weighting the corners with his ink pots and a fossil he kept for no reason he could defend, and watched her read it the way he imagined a priest watches someone read scripture for the first time — hoping, fearing, ready to be disappointed.

"It's beautiful," she said at last, and meant it, and he had to look away for a moment because eleven years alone with a guilty vocation makes praise land harder than it should.

"It's accurate," he corrected. "Beauty is incidental. Look at the approaches. Three ways in, and only one of them real anymore. The north road was rerouted in the reforms — it now bends fifteen miles south for no engineering reason whatsoever, a perfectly absurd detour that every surveyor has documented and none has questioned. That's the trap. The west track floods, but only in memory — the drainage works were unwritten too, and they still function, which means the flood is a fiction maintained by absence of maintenance. The third way is a shepherd's path along the dry ridge, unmarked, unmapped, and therefore — " he tapped it — "the only road in the country the Inquisition cannot watch, because to watch a road you must first admit it exists."

"And the village itself?"

"Here. On the shelf below the ridge. Two hundred souls, a chapel older than the Inquisition, a priestess who — if my informant is reliable, and she communicates in gestures, so reliability is a generous term — has been waiting for something." He hesitated. "For someone, possibly. There's a carving on the well. I rubbed it once, years ago, and I've shown it to exactly one person, a linguist, who went pale, paid me double, and told me never to show it to anyone again. It's guild script, Lior. Old Hollowtongue. And the phrase translates roughly as *the heir will come from the city that forgot*."

The silence that followed had weight. Outside, the rain had gentled into something almost polite. Lior put her fingers to her sleeve again, over the sigil, and this time she didn't pretend she wasn't doing it.

"You said you weren't a dissident," she said quietly. "But you kept all this. Eleven years. Why? What's your guilt in it, Ivet? Everyone I meet who helps me has one. Tessin had one. I have several large ones of my own. What's yours?"

He looked at his maps. All of them. The gray licensed ones and the true forbidden ones, the whole double life of lines and ink.

"My guilt," he said, "is that I was good at my job. When the reforms came through, I verified them. I signed the certificates. I redrew the borders that made Errow impossible, and my hand was steady, and my work was praised, and somewhere in a drawer in the Surveyor-General's office there is a map with my signature on it that unmade two hundred lives as neatly as a sponge takes paint off slate." He met her eyes. "They didn't need to threaten me. I *believed*. That's what the Purge actually was, I think — not an army, an agreement. Thousands of ordinary competent people agreeing, all at once, to see less. I unmade a village with a pen. It seems only fair that I spend the rest of my life putting it back."

Lior nodded slowly. Then, with the air of someone making a formal decision she'd probably made days ago: "Come with me."

Ivet blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"To Errow. I need the map, yes, but maps get lost, misread, captured. I need the man who made it. Someone who knows which shepherd's paths are real and which are wishful. And — " she hesitated, and he watched her decide to be honest, watched it cost her something — "I've been alone with a dead man's voice for weeks, and I would very much like to travel with someone who argues about tea."

"The tea argument is that it's terrible," Ivet said. "There's no arguing it. It's a settled matter."

"Then you'll come."

He looked at the third map. At eleven careful, cowardly, magnificent years of documenting crimes instead of facing them. At the charcoal house with too many windows. At the rain, which was stopping, because the sky apparently held grudges but not indefinitely.

"I'll need an hour to pack," he said. "And I warn you, I talk when I'm nervous. Constantly. About map projections, mostly. It's been described as a punishment."

"Wren will love you," Lior said, and when he raised an eyebrow: "The boy who speaks Hollowtongue. He's waiting for me at a shrine two miles south. Eight years old. Possessed by my dead master, more or less. He collects interesting rocks and asks unsettling questions."

"Two miles south," Ivet repeated faintly, already reaching for his satchel, his good pens, the oilskin that had kept the third map safe through a decade of cowardice. "In this weather."

"She's eight," Lior said. "And the god under Vess is waking up, and every day we wait it eats another hole in the world. Weather is the least of it."

He slung the satchel over his shoulder and paused at the door, one hand on the frame, taking a last look at the cottage where he had hidden for eleven years among documents of nothing.

"You understand," he said, "that the moment I walk out that door, I stop being a mapmaker."

Lior smiled the rare smile again.

"No," she said. "You start being one. Come on, Ivet. Let's go draw the erased back onto the world."
