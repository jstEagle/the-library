## 12. Sarel Interrogates Tessin

The holding cells beneath the Inquisitorium smelled of wet stone and old smoke, and Tessin had been in them for three days without once looking frightened.

Sarel Vane noticed that first, before she even reached the bars. Most prisoners performed fear — it was a currency, something to spend or withhold. This man sat on his cot with his hands folded in his lap like a shopkeeper waiting for a customer who was late, and when she stopped at the cell door he looked up at her with mild, apologetic eyes and said:

"You'll want a chair. These interrogations run long."

"I've done this before," Sarel said.

"Of course you have." He gestured at the corridor behind her. "But you'll want a chair anyway. Trust me. I sold remedies for twenty years; I know how people's backs are built, and yours is already complaining about standing."

She should have ordered him to be silent. Instead she sent for a chair.

It arrived — a plain wooden thing dragged down from the clerks' floor by a nervous acolyte who would not meet Tessin's eye — and Sarel sat, and opened her ledger, and began.

"Name."

"Tessin Marlowe. Though you know that. You wrote it yourself, there, at the top of your page. Lovely penmanship, by the way. The Inquisition teaches its people to write beautifully, which has always struck me as an odd priority for an organization so fond of burning things."

"Occupation."

"Licensed apothecary. Third district. Fully registered, taxes paid, and I have the receipts if anyone here can read numbers as well as they read handwriting."

"Prior occupation."

A pause. Small, but Sarel had been trained to hear pauses the way musicians heard rests, and this one had weight to it.

"Apprentice," he said finally. "In another life. Before the Purge made everything clean."

"Guild affiliation."

"No such organization exists," Tessin said pleasantly. "That's official doctrine. You wrote it into law yourselves. The Hollow Guild never existed, its members were never prosecuted, and its records were never burned, because there were no records and nothing to burn. You can't ask me about a thing that never happened, Inquisitor. It would be like asking me about my third arm."

Sarel turned a page. "You were seen, two nights ago, speaking with a young woman in the night market. Dark hair. Left sleeve worn long. She bought nothing from your stall, yet you spoke for the length of a candle."

"Did we? I talk to everyone. It's terrible for business — customers flee from me — but I've never learned to stop." He leaned forward slightly. "Was she pretty?"

"That is not relevant."

"It's always relevant. It's the only relevant thing, most days." He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and Sarel understood suddenly why the acolyte wouldn't look at him. There was something unbearable about a cheerful man in a cell. "But go on. Ask me what you actually came to ask me."

Sarel set down her pen.

"The boy," she said. "Wren Callow. Fevered for nine days. His mother bought a charm in the night market — a fever-ward, cheap twine and river-clay, the kind of trinket your stall sells by the basket. And the fever broke. Not eased. *Broke*, in an hour, in a way the physicians at Saint Verren's called impossible. And the boy woke up speaking a language no scholar in this city can identify."

"What a remarkable story," said Tessin.

"There's more. Since then, across Vess, walls have been scorched from the inside. Hollows burned into stone where people knelt to pray. Forty-one incidents in six days. The temples say smugglers. My colleagues say smugglers." She watched his face. "I don't think either of us believes that."

Tessin was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, the pleasantness had thinned, though not gone — it was more like pleasantness stretched over something else, the way canvas stretches over a frame.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said, "how long have you served?"

"Eleven years."

"And in eleven years, how many witches have you burned?"

"That number is in the public record."

"I know. I've read it. That's why I'm asking *you* — not the record. The record says one hundred and six. But you were there for some of them. So: how many did *you* burn?"

"Nine."

"And how many of those nine could do what happened to that boy?"

Sarel did not answer.

"Because I knew some of the people your order caught," Tessin went on, softly now. "I knew what our work actually was. Luck-knots. Fever-wards. Charms to keep moths out of wool and arguments out of marriages. We were cobblers of small things, Inquisitor. If any of us could have cracked a nine-day death-fever in an hour, we'd have been rich instead of hiding in a market at midnight." He spread his hands. "So you tell me. What did the Purge save anyone from?"

"The Purge saved Vess from—"

"From what?" He wasn't smiling anymore. "Say the words. The ones from the training texts. *Unchecked sorcery. Corruption of the faithful. The tyranny of the old gods.* Say them and mean them, and then explain to me the scorch-marks on the temple of Saint Aldric, which appeared after a widow prayed there for her son's safe return from the coast road. Explain the boy. Explain why the gods you burned out of the world seem to be answering prayers again — and doing it badly, clumsily, like hands remembering their shape after years asleep." He leaned back against the wall. "I'll give you whatever you want, Inquisitor. Names, dates, the whole sad history. The moment you can answer my question honestly. Not the doctrinal answer. Yours."

For a long moment the only sound was water dripping somewhere in the dark behind the cells.

"I didn't come here to be interrogated," Sarel said at last.

"No," Tessin agreed. "But isn't it restful, being asked a question you're allowed to think about?"

She stood. The chair scraped. At the mouth of the corridor she paused, because fairness was a discipline she practiced like any other, and she owed him one true thing.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the food here is worse than you deserve."

"Oh, I know," said Tessin cheerfully. "I've been complaining since the first bowl. Your kitchen staff are very patient."

* * *

She did not sleep that night.

This was not unusual — Sarel slept the way soldiers slept, in shallow watchful increments — but tonight the waking hours had a different texture. She sat at her desk with a single lamp lit and the confiscated-charms inventory open in front of her, and she read it not as a clerk reads but as a woman reading letters from someone she'd wronged.

Item 44: luck-knot, red cord, seven knots. Confiscated from a girl of perhaps fourteen. Burned.

Item 51: fever-ward, clay and twine. Burned.

Item 58: a marriage-blessing ribbon, embroidered with a pattern the confiscation clerk had described as "possibly meaningless." Burned.

Possibly meaningless. She thought of the widow at Saint Aldric's. She thought of the boy Wren, whom she had visited that afternoon under pretense of a health inspection, and who had looked at her with clear gray eyes and said, in a voice that was not entirely a child's voice, *"You kept the ledgers well, little ash-daughter. Even the false entries."*

She had written that off as fever-dream. She was finding it harder to write things off lately.

There was a knock. Her junior, Pellam — earnest, freckled, constitutionally incapable of disapproval — put his head around the door.

"You're still up, Inquisitor."

"So are you."

"I brought tea. It's the good kind, from the Provost's own tin, which technically makes it theft, so drink it fast and don't thank me." He set the cup down, hovered, and then — because Pellam had never in his life managed to leave a room when he wanted to say something — said: "You went to see the Callow boy today."

"I did."

"Pellam's report says—" He stopped, corrected himself. "*My* report says the interview lasted four minutes and produced nothing."

"It produced nothing," Sarel agreed.

Pellam shifted his weight. "Only — Corwin was on the door, and he says the boy spoke to you. And that afterward you stood in the courtyard for a quarter hour without moving, and when he asked if you wanted supper you said—" He winced. "You said, 'What did we burn it all *for*, Corwin,' and then you apologized to him, which he found upsetting, because you've never apologized to anyone, including that time you fell down the archive stairs."

Sarel looked at her junior over the rim of the teacup. "Is there a question buried in all that?"

"Yes." Pellam took a breath. He was twenty-three and brave in the way of people who hadn't yet learned what bravery cost. "Are we the good guys, Inquisitor? Because I joined up because of the stories. The Purge saving the city. And lately the stories don't... fit. The scorch-marks. The boy. And Master Tessin in the cells keeps asking the guards questions, and none of them can answer, and last night Brother Hask came off duty shaking because Tessin asked him about his mother, who's ill, and offered to pray for her, and Hask didn't know whether to hit him or say thank you."

"What did Hask do?"

"He said thank you," Pellam admitted. "And then reported himself for unorthodox sympathy. Which I'm supposed to log tomorrow." He hesitated. "Am I going to log it?"

Sarel held his gaze. Eleven years she had walked the straight line of doctrine, and the line had been comfortable, and comfort was its own kind of blindness.

"Log it," she said. "Hask thanked a prisoner for kindness. Write it exactly like that. Let whoever reads it sit with it the way we're sitting with it."

Pellam nodded slowly, and left, and the tea went cold beside her while she stared at the wall of her office — behind which, three floors down and two wings over, stood the Sealed Archive.

Every Inquisitor knew about it the way children knew about the locked room in a grandparent's house. It held the original records of the Purge: the trial transcripts, the god-censuses, the guild registries. Access required the Provost's seal and two witnesses and a stated doctrinal purpose. Opening it without those things was heresy — not metaphorically, but formally, in writing, with her name attached.

The order forbidding access sat in her top drawer. She had signed it herself, four years ago, as part of her elevation to full Inquisitor. *No record of the pre-Purge era shall be consulted except in defense of the faith.*

Except in defense of the faith.

Well. She rose, took her lamp, and went to find out what her faith was actually defending.

* * *

The Sealed Archive door was iron, and the lock was older than the Inquisitorium around it, and it opened to her key with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: dust, and cold, and shelves upon shelves of documents that no one had disturbed in decades. A saint's candle burned in a niche by the door — someone, sometime, had thought to leave a light for whatever was kept here. It had burned down to a stub and guttered long ago. Sarel relit it. It seemed important, somehow, that the room not be entirely dark.

She started with the census.

It was bound in gray leather and titled, in a clerk's careful hand, *Enumeration of the Old Powers, Final Reckoning.* She had expected myth — she had braced for myth, for the wild theological exaggerations that justified any purge — and instead she found bookkeeping.

The gods had been counted. There were thirty-one of them in the Vess region alone, each with an entry: name, domain, observed behaviors, number of active shrines, disposition. The handwriting grew shakier toward the end, as though the census-takers had begun to hurry.

*Entry 19: The Under-Keeper. Domain: hearth, debt, mercy between strangers. Shrines: 214. Disposition: quiet, attentive, answers most prayers within a season. Note: does not demand worship. Note appended by second hand: then what is it FOR?*

Sarel read that note twice. Someone, sixty years ago, had stood in front of evidence of a gentle god and been *bothered* by its gentleness.

She moved to the trial transcripts. Here the tone changed. The early trials were almost embarrassed — magistrates asking healers to demonstrate their charms, frowning at results that were modest, local, harmless. A woman who could keep milk sweet an extra day. A man whose knots genuinely did hold luck, in the sense that people carrying them made marginally better decisions, or believed they did, which amounted to the same thing.

Then, midway through the volume, the transcripts changed again. New language crept in. *Contamination. Sedition of the soul. The Door.* References to a guild operation — sealed, expunged, discussed only in fragments — something the founders had done *to* the gods rather than merely *about* them. One transcript fragment, torn at the edge, preserved a single line from an unnamed witness:

*"They didn't banish anything. They shut it all in one room and lost the key, and now every prayer falls down a well and something down there is learning to climb."*

Sarel sat down on the cold floor, because her legs had opinions about standing.

She read until the candle was half gone. She read the final report of the Purge commission, and found that its conclusion section — the page that would have explained what threat had been neutralized, what victory had been won — was missing. Cut out. Neatly, with a blade, long ago. The binding showed the scar of it.

Someone inside the Inquisition had removed the answer to Tessin's question before Sarel was born.

She closed the census. She sat in the dust with her back against the shelf, and she thought about nine witches, and one hundred and six names, and a boy speaking a dead tongue, and forty-one scorch-marks shaped like kneeling people, and a cheerful man in a cell who had asked her one question and refused, with perfect courtesy, to accept a lie.

Then she did the thing that could not be undone.

She took out her notebook, and by the light of a saint's guttering candle, in her beautiful Inquisition-trained hand, she began to copy. Entry nineteen first. Then the witness fragment. Then the list of the missing pages, and the date of the excision, and the seal-mark of whoever had authorized it — a mark she did not recognize, which meant it belonged to someone above her, which meant it belonged to nearly everyone above her.

When the candle died she copied the last lines by memory and lamplight carried in from the corridor, and she locked the archive behind her, and she stood a moment in the dark listening to her own heart.

Above her, faint through floors of stone, the city murmured in its sleep. Somewhere out there, another prayer was being answered, and another hollow was opening in the world, and the thing beneath everything was climbing.

In her pocket, against her ribs, the copied pages felt warm as a live coal.

Tomorrow she would go back to Tessin's cell. Tomorrow she would sit down in the chair he'd told her to get, and she would give him the only honest answer she had:

*I don't know yet. But I'm going to find out — and I think someone made very sure we never could.*
