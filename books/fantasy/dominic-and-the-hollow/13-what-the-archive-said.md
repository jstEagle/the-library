## 13. What the Archive Said

The archive beneath the Bastion of the Word was cold in the way that only places built to outlast people are cold. Stone drank the warmth from everything that entered it — torches, breath, faith. Sarel Vane had spent eleven years walking its corridors and had never once thought of it as a prison. It was a library. It was a fortress of truth. It was where lies went to be catalogued and forgotten.

Tonight she came down the stair with Tessin's question folded into her breast pocket like a knife.

*What did the Purge actually save anyone from?*

She had asked herself the same thing at two in the morning, standing over the boy's empty sickbed, and had hated herself for asking it. Inquisitors did not ask. Inquisitors *knew*. The gods were dead or fled; the Purge had burned away the rot; the world since was cleaner, poorer in wonders perhaps, but honest. That was the catechism. She had recited it so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and she had never noticed that a stone worn smooth is also a stone worn *smaller*.

"Archivist," she said to the man behind the desk at the bottom of the stairs.

Brother Hallow was seventy if he was a day, with ink-stained fingers and the mild expression of a man who had long ago decided that whatever happened above ground was not his department. He looked up at her without alarm. Everyone below a certain rank looked up at Sarel Vane with alarm. Archivists did not. She had always found this restful.

"Inquisitor." He marked his place with a ribbon. "You'll want the Purge holdings, I expect. Third gallery."

"I want the original records," she said. "Not the catechism copies. Not the sanctioned histories. The *firsthand* accounts. Sealed materials."

Hallow's pen stopped moving.

It was a small thing, the stopping of a pen. But Sarel Vane had spent eleven years reading faces across interrogation tables, and she knew what she was looking at. Not fear. Something older and sadder than fear — the look of a man who has been waiting years for someone to finally ask.

"You understand," he said mildly, "that sealed materials require a writ from the First Voice himself."

"I have a warrant under the Emergency Statutes. Unanswered prayer-work in the lower city. Two cases. One child speaking a dead language." She set the warrant on the desk. "That makes this an active investigation, and active investigations supersede archival seal per Article Twelve."

"Article Twelve," Hallow repeated, tasting it. "Nobody has used Article Twelve in my lifetime. I'd begun to think it was decorative."

"Hallow."

"Inquisitor." He rose, unhurried, and took a ring of keys from a hook behind him. As he passed her, he said, very quietly, without turning his head: "Third gallery, far wall, behind the map cabinets. The lock sticks. Lift, then turn. And Inquisitor — there's no lamp down there that works. Bring your own light."

Then he sat back down and resumed copying whatever harmless thing he had been copying, and Sarel understood that she had just been given something she had never once been given in eleven years of service: help.

* * *

The space behind the map cabinets was not on any floor plan she had ever seen, and she had seen all of them. The door was iron, unmarked, set into stone so old the chisel-marks had softened like skin. The lock stuck, as promised. She lifted, turned, and felt something give with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: dust, darkness, and paper. So much paper. Rows of shelving cut directly into the rock, and on them, crates and ledgers and bundles tied with cord gone brittle as autumn grass. No lamps, as promised. She lit her own hand-lamp and held it high.

The first crate was labeled in a clerk's careful hand: **VESSEL DISTRICT — CONFESSIONS, MONTH OF EMBERS, YEAR ONE**. Confessions. She pulled one free. A standard Purge-era confession, sworn testimony extracted before burning — she had read a hundred of these in training, the grammar of terror unchanged across decades. *I confess I kept charms. I confess I spoke to what I believed dwelt in the well.* Nothing unusual.

Except that this one continued past the point where they all ended. There was a second page. Second pages did not exist. Every confession in the sanctioned record ended at its confession, because a confession was an ending — you confessed, you burned, the ledger closed. But this one had an addendum in a different hand, an examiner's hand:

*Subject states repeatedly, under no coercion, that the entity in the well did not ask for worship. It asked whether the children had enough to eat. Recommend further investigation.*

Sarel stood very still in the cold.

There should have been a further investigation. There was no further investigation. She checked the next confession, and the next. Ten of them, twenty. A woman who said the river-god had warned her village of a flood three days early — *recommend investigation*. A man who said the field-god had mended his lame ox for nothing, for nothing at all, asking nothing back — *recommend investigation*. An entire district of small, strange, gentle testimonies, each one ending in the same bureaucratic dead end.

*Recommend investigation.*

And none ever conducted.

Her lamp trembled slightly. She steadied it with both hands, the way she'd been taught, the way you steady anything when your body knows a truth before your mind consents to it.

"They weren't taking confessions," she said aloud, and her voice sounded wrong in the stone room. "They were taking *reports*."

She began to work properly then, the way she worked best — methodically, furiously, pulling crates, cross-referencing dates. The picture assembled itself the way a mosaic assembles: one tile means nothing, a hundred mean everything. The first year of the Purge was full of reports of gods who fed people, healed people, warned people. The second year the reports changed tone. Fewer gentlenesses. More hunger. By year three the examiners' notes had shifted too — less *recommend investigation*, more *containment feasible*, more *the guild claims it can be done*, and finally, near the end, a single bound folio whose cover read:

**THE HOLLOW ACCORD — FINAL SESSION — DO NOT COPY — DO NOT READ ALOUD**

Sarel Vane had broken rules before. Small ones. She had once given a starving charm-seller a week's head start instead of three days, and told herself mercy was a form of law. She had never broken *this* kind of rule. This rule existed so that things like the Hollow Accord stayed unread.

Tessin's question sat in her pocket like an ember.

She broke the folio's seal.

* * *

It was not long. That was the worst part, she thought later. Eleven years of dread built atop a document of nine pages.

The Accord was a treaty, drafted between the Hollow Guild and the Inquisition in the last month of the Purge. She recognized the Inquisitorial signatory — Grand Inquisitor Casserly, founder of the order, the marble saint in the Bastion's atrium whose eyes every recruit learned to meet. His hand was unmistakable, the letters tall and severe.

The guild's signatory made her go cold: *Aldous Wren, First Voice of the Hollow Guild.*

Aldous. The name the boy spoke. The name the fugitive Eastwick carried like a wound. She had assumed it was grief, or guilt, or some cultist's delusion. She wrote it down now in her own notebook, and her handwriting, which interrogators complimented for its iron neatness, wobbled like a drunkard's.

The terms, in summary, which she copied out twice to be certain she was not mad:

One. The old gods could not be killed. Every rite of destruction attempted during the Purge had failed; the entities absorbed destruction the way fire absorbs wood, growing by it. *(Casserly's marginal note here: "We have been feeding them for two years. God forgive us.")*

Two. The gods could be *sealed* — not destroyed, not banished, but closed behind a Door, their hunger shut away from the world. The guild claimed the knowledge. The guild demanded, in exchange, immunity for its members and the preservation of its texts.

Three. In return, the Inquisition would assume perpetual custodianship of the seal. Not victory. Custody. Casserly had written the word himself, underlined twice: *"We do not end this. We hold it. Our successors must be told, generation upon generation, that we hold it, or the holding fails."*

And four — the clause that made Sarel sit down on a crate because her legs declined to hold her —

Four. The final disposition of the Accord, the section describing *how* the Door was made and *what sustained it*, had been removed. Physically cut from the folio, the stub of the page still visible, and replaced with a slip in a later hand, official Inquisitorial script:

**SECTION FOUR EXPUNGED BY ORDER OF THE FIRST VOICE. THE PURGE IS COMPLETE. THE GODS ARE ASH. LET NO RECORD SUGGEST OTHERWISE.**

They had been guarding a prison for sixty years and calling it a graveyard. Worse — they had been ordered to forget it was a prison at all. Somewhere in the generations after Casserly, some First Voice had looked at the truth and decided the order couldn't survive it, and had amputated the record instead, and every Inquisitor since had marched proudly around a dungeon believing themselves victors in a war that had never ended, only been locked.

Sarel laughed. It came out of her sideways, one dry bark that echoed off the stone, and it frightened her more than weeping would have.

"All those burnings," she said to the dark. "All those poor bastards with their fever-wards and their luck-knots." Burning charms that were too weak to be called magic, while beneath the city — beneath *everything* — the real thing sat sealed and patient, and the Inquisition stood on the lid and called itself a triumph.

She thought of the mother in the night market, the one whose son's fever had broken. She thought of the burns appearing in walls across Vess, each one a prayer answered, each one a crack in a lid she now understood she had been standing on her whole career.

She thought of Tessin in his cell, refusing food, refusing talk, giving her nothing but the question. He had known. Not the details — but the shape of it. They had all known, the old ones. They had watched the Inquisition guard a prison and call it a purge, and they had paid for the knowing with fire, and the ones who survived had decided the new keepers deserved to stay stupid, because stupid keepers were safe keepers.

Could she blame them? She tried, professionally, the way she'd been trained to weigh culpability, and found the scales wouldn't tip.

*What did the Purge actually save anyone from?*

Nothing. That was the answer. It had saved everyone from nothing and imprisoned everyone with something, and the bill was coming due in scorched hollows and a boy who spoke the language of the jailers.

* * *

She worked until her lamp guttered. She copied everything worth copying into her own notebooks — the Accord, the examiner's marginalia, the list of districts where the gentle gods had lived, cross-referenced against her memory of where the prayer-burns were appearing now. They matched. Of course they matched. The god waking under Vess was waking in the old neighborhoods, the poor ones, the ones where people had never entirely stopped whispering to wells.

Near dawn she climbed back up through the galleries and paused at Brother Hallow's desk. The old man was asleep sitting up, pen still in hand, a half-copied inventory drifting under his chin. Sarel stood over him a moment.

"You knew," she said softly.

Hallow opened one eye. "Knew what, Inquisitor?" Mild as milk. "I copy inventories."

"Sixty years," she said. "Your predecessors copied inventories too. Right outside that door."

"Mm." He closed the eye again. Then: "Inquisitor. A piece of archival advice, freely given. When a record has been expunged by order of the First Voice, the expunging is itself recorded. In triplicate. With the reason, sometimes, if the First Voice of the day was careless." He sighed. "Ours was careless. The removal order references *where the missing section went*. Some things, once written, cannot be unwritten. Only moved."

"Where did it go?"

"The private vault of whoever holds the office," Hallow said. "Which means it is currently in the keeping of First Voice Ambrose. Who believes, sincerely, in the ash." A pause. "Or says he does. At his level, Inquisitor, it stops mattering which."

Sarel straightened. Outside, somewhere far above, the Bastion bells rang the hour, and beneath the bells, beneath the stone, beneath sixty years of comfortable lies — something turned over in its sleep, and a wall in the Tanner's Ward grew another black flower of burnt hollow, and some desperate soul got exactly what they prayed for.

She had come down the stairs hunting a witch. A fugitive named Lior Eastwick, a dead master's voice, a child speaking forbidden words. That hunt had seemed simple. Righteous, even.

Now she stood in the cold with the truth in her notebook and understood that the witch-hunt had been the smallest part of it all along. The boy wasn't the anomaly. The burns weren't the crime. The crime was the lid, and the lie about the lid, and she was one of the liars by inheritance, whether she'd ever spoken the words or not.

"Hallow," she said. "If I request access to the First Voice's private registry, how long before he hears of it?"

"Oh, minutes," the archivist said comfortably. "Hours at most."

"Good." She tucked her notebook inside her coat, over her heart, where warrants went. "Then I won't request it. I'm going to find another way in. And when I do—" She stopped, hearing herself, hearing what she was about to say, and said it anyway. "When I do, I'm going to finish the investigation somebody recommended sixty years ago and nobody ever conducted."

Brother Hallow smiled, just slightly, into his inventory.

"About time," he said. "Someone left a lamp burning for you down there. Figured it might take another thirty years."

Above them the bells finished ringing. Below them, patiently, the prisoner waited — and Sarel Vane climbed toward the daylight already planning how to steal from the men who had taught her everything she knew.
