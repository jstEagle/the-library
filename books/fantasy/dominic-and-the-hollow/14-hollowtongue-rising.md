## 14. Hollowtongue Rising

The cell smelled of damp stone and someone else's despair, and Sarel Vane had been sitting in it for three hours pretending to be a piece of furniture.

Tessin the apothecary had been in Inquisition custody for six days now, and he had done nothing but be pleasant about it. He thanked the guards who brought his bread. He complimented the quality of the straw pallet. He had once — and this was the moment that had brought Sarel down into the cells with a lantern and a notebook, determined to break him before supper — asked a young interrogator named Pell whether his cough was a dry one or a wet one, because it made all the difference in what one ought to take for it, and then recited three remedies from memory, free of charge.

Pell had been charmed. Pell was twenty-two and would have followed Tessin into a fire if the man had told him it was medicinal.

Sarel was not charmed. Sarel sat on the stool in the corner with her notebook open on her knee and her badge face-down beside her, because she had found in recent weeks that she could not look at the thing straight on without feeling something she refused to name.

"You're not eating," she said.

"I ate." Tessin sat cross-legged on the pallet, hands loose in his lap, an old man gone soft at the edges like a book left in the rain. "It's good bread. Whoever bakes for the Inquisition is wasted here."

"Master Tessin."

"Inquisitor Vane." He smiled at her, mild as milk. "You've been sitting there long enough that I'm starting to worry about your back. There's a salve —"

"The boy," Sarel said. "Wren. The mother's son. The fever-ward child. You knew the woman who bought the charm. You pointed her toward the night market. Three witnesses put you within speaking distance of Lior Eastwick on four separate occasions over two years. And you are going to tell me that you did none of it, knew none of them, and sold licorice root to pilgrims until the day you were arrested. That is your story. I want to know why it doesn't hold water."

"It holds plenty of water," Tessin said comfortably. "I'm an apothecary. Water is half my trade."

Sarel closed the notebook. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something she had never once done in eleven years of service: she set the notebook aside entirely, folded her hands, and said, quietly:

"My father prayed, the winter my mother was dying. Did you know that? Not loudly. Not in a temple. He knelt by her bed when he thought I was asleep and he prayed to whatever was left to pray to after the Purge, which was nothing, which was ash, which was the Inquisition telling him from every street corner that prayer was a door and behind doors were teeth. She died anyway. And I grew up believing we had burned the right things. That the silence after the Purge was peace." Her voice did not waver. She had trained it not to, years ago, for reading sentences of judgment aloud. "Then a fever breaks in a slum room off Tanner's Row and a seven-year-old starts speaking a language that was supposed to be dead, and I read the confiscated records, Master Tessin. The old ones. The ones with the seals still on them. Do you know what I found?"

Something shifted in the old man's face. Not much. A crack in plaster, thin enough to miss if you weren't looking for it. Sarel was looking for it.

"I found," she went on, "that the guild didn't die fighting the gods. They died sealing them. And that my order has spent two hundred years standing guard on a prison and calling it a victory." She leaned forward. "So here is my offer, and understand that no one else in this building can make it and I will deny making it by morning. Tell me where Lior Eastwick is going. Tell me what the Door is. And when this ends — however it ends — I will see that your name is struck from the convict rolls. Not pardoned. *Erased.* As if you were never arrested at all."

The cell was very quiet. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the yard.

Tessin studied her the way he might study a patient — kindly, thoroughly, with a professional sadness about what he found.

"You've been reading the sealed archive," he said at last. "All of it?"

"All of it."

"Then you already know more than Eastwick does. More than the girl knows about her own inheritance." He sighed, and something in him seemed finally to settle, like silt in still water. "You want to know where she's going? Inquisitor, I'll tell you honestly: I don't know. I gave her a map fragment because she was going to get herself killed poking at the guildhall alone, and a map fragment was the only way to point her somewhere safer than where she was. I don't know where the fragment leads. I only know who drew it, and when."

"Who?"

"Aldous Wren." At the name of the dead master, Sarel felt the hair rise along her arms. "Drawn forty years ago, when Aldous still believed the guild could fix what it broke. And here is the part you actually want, since you've offered me erasure for it: the boy."

"Wren?"

"Wren," Tessin said, and there was no comfort in his voice now at all. "You have him locked in a room on the third floor with a guard outside the door and chalk marks on the threshold. You've been recording what he says in his sleep. You've heard it yourself — you just won't write it down in any report anyone will ever read."

Sarel said nothing. Because it was true. She had stood outside that door twice, listening through the gap, hearing a child's voice come out in cadences no child should own, deep and liturgical and *old*, reciting oaths in a tongue that predated the Inquisition's founding charter. She had written in her private notes only: *the subject speaks. The subject is spoken through.*

"He isn't possessed," Tessin said. "I need you to understand that, because everything depends on it. Possession pushes. This pulls. Whatever is in that child under Vess — it isn't driving him anywhere. It's *calling* him. Calling him toward the girl. Toward the heir. And a called thing, Inquisitor, cannot be held by chalk marks and a bored guard. It can barely be held by its own body." He met her eyes. "Move him tonight. Or better, let him go tonight, gently, before he goes through the wall."

"That's superstition."

"That's apothecary's observation," Tessin said. "Two hundred years of watching what happens around your order's prisons. Things under pressure come out sideways."

---

They came for the boy at moonrise, and they came too late, and Sarel knew it before she reached the top of the stairs because the guard — Pell, sweet doomed Pell — was standing at his post with the door open and both hands pressed flat against his own chest, and he was crying.

"Inquisitor," he said, stricken. "Inquisitor, I only looked away for — he asked me for water. He asked so politely. And then —"

The cell beyond was empty. The window was barred; the bars were intact; the chalk threshold was unbroken. But the bed's blanket lay folded — *folded*, neatly, corners squared, the way a child folds a blanket when he has been taught manners by a voice older than manners — and on the floor where the boy had slept there was a scorch mark.

Not a burn. Sarel had seen burns, hundreds of them, had lit most herself in the market squares. Burns were ragged and angry. This was clean. A hollow, smooth-sided, exactly the shape of a small sleeping body, pressed into the stone as though the boy had sunk an inch into the world and then been lifted out. The edges were cool when she touched them. Cooler than the surrounding stone, in fact, as though the mark had drunk a little heat out of the room and kept it.

"Pell," she said. "What did you hear? Before you opened the door. What did you hear him say?"

Pell wiped his face with his sleeve. "It wasn't — Inquisitor, it wasn't words, exactly. It was singing. Low. Like a hymn but wrong, like a hymn sung backward, except it was the most —" His voice cracked with embarrassment and wonder in equal measure. "It was the most beautiful thing I've ever heard. I wanted to go in. I wanted to kneel down. I had my hand on the latch before I knew I'd moved."

Backward hymns and beautiful things. Sarel stood in the empty cell with the folded blanket and the cool hollow and understood, with the clarity that had been ruining her faith all season, that Tessin had been right, that the pressure had come out sideways, and that somewhere in Vess or beyond it a seven-year-old was walking toward Lior Eastwick with the whole weight of a starving god leaning on his small shoulders.

She took the sketch of Lior from her coat — she had drawn it herself, weeks ago, from witness descriptions: sharp-faced girl, sleeve pulled low over her wrist — and looked at it, and looked at the window, and made the second decision of her career that she would deny by morning.

She was not going to report the escape. Not yet. Not first. First she was going to follow the same trail the god was following, because the god, whatever else it was, had never once in its long hungry existence needed to fill out a requisition form, and therefore moved faster than her order ever would.

"Pell," she said. "The record shows I questioned the prisoner alone and found the cell empty at second bell. You discovered it with me. Say nothing else, to anyone, until I return."

"Return from where, Inquisitor?"

Sarel looked east, through the barred window, toward the dark hills beyond the walls, where the border provinces used to be before the maps were ordered blank.

"Toward the nothing," she said.

* * *

Wren ran.

He had never run like this before — not from the butcher's dog on Cordwainer Lane, not from the bigger boys at the tannery sluice, not ever, not with his legs doing the running while some other part of him steered. His feet knew the way. That was the frightening part and also, if he was honest, the wonderful part. He hadn't known where anything was in the Inquisition fortress, and then the voice had said *left at the cold room, mind the step, the drain grate lifts if you push with your shoulder*, and it had all worked, every word of it, as though the fortress itself were a body and the voice had grown up inside it.

He was sorry about the guard. He hoped the guard wasn't in trouble. He'd meant to say thank you for the water and somehow the singing had gotten ahead of him again.

The voice didn't sing anymore, now that they were out. Now it talked, low and even, the way his ma talked when the fever was bad and she was sitting up all night with him, saying nonsense to keep him calm. Except the voice's nonsense was true.

*East,* the voice said. *Follow the east road past the tanneries, and don't look back at the tower, little wren. If you look at the tower, the ones in the tower will feel you looking.*

"Why?" Wren whispered. It was full dark and he was crouched in the shadow of a woodpile behind a chandler's shop, breathing hard, his heart going like a drum at a wedding. "Why can't I look?"

*Because you carry a lit candle, and moans and moths and worse things all turn toward light. Keep moving.*

"I want my ma."

There was a pause. The voice did pauses sometimes, and Wren had learned that the pauses were when it was being careful, and the careful was when it was most like his ma.

*Your mother is safe. I have seen to it that she sleeps deeply and wakes believing you were ransomed by kin from the eastern villages. It isn't the truth. I regret that. Truth is a luxury of things that aren't dying.*

"You're dying?"

Another pause. Longer.

*I am very old,* the voice said at last, *and very hungry, and I have been shut in the dark for longer than your city has had a name, and I am trying very hard to be gentle with you, because you are the only door I have. So let us not ask each other sad questions tonight. Let us walk.*

So they walked. Out through the tannery district, where the smell covered everything including fear, and onto the east road, which began as a real road with milestones and ended, three miles out, as a rumor — a track worn through gorse with no marker, because the provinces it led to had been struck from every map and milestone in the country, and roads that lead nowhere official stop being maintained.

Wren didn't care. Roads were roads. His feet were shoes his ma had resoled twice, and the stitching held, and the stars came out thick overhead the way they only did away from city smoke, and the voice hummed to him as he walked, old tunes in the backward-beautiful language, and after a while Wren realized he was humming too, and that he knew all the words, and he could not remember learning a single one.

Once, near midnight, he stopped at a stream to drink and saw, downstream, a farmhouse with a scorched hollow burned clean through its barn door — round-edged, smooth, exactly the shape of a kneeling man. The family had hung ribbons on it. Red ones. Someone had left bread and salt on the ground beneath it, arranged carefully, like an offering on a table.

*They pray here,* Wren said. *Out loud. Nobody burned them for it.*

*Nobody official,* the voice said. *But look closer, little wren. Look at the ribbons' colors. Red for hunger. They leave bread because they're afraid of me, and they leave salt because they love me, and both are correct, and I have never once decided which one to be.*

Wren looked at the offering a long time. Then he took the heel of the loaf, because he was seven and starving and the voice laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of it, warm as a banked fire — and said *yes, take it, the faithful may as well be fed*, and they walked on together into the east.

Near dawn, stiff-legged and bleary, Wren crested a hill and saw below him a shrine. Just a roadside shrine, a leaning post with a wooden hood, the kind that dotted the old roads before the Purge tore most of them down. Someone had recently re-carved the symbol at its crown: a circle with a keyhole, and inside the keyhole, a smaller mark.

A sigil. Pulled low on a wrist, hidden under a sleeve. Sharp-faced girl. He'd never seen her, but the voice showed him — showed him a night market stall, luck-knots swinging on strings, and a pair of quick brown hands tying a fever-ward, and a sleeve slipping back just far enough.

"There," Wren said, pointing with a finger that shook with tiredness and certainty both. "She went that way. Didn't she. Through there."

*Through there,* the voice agreed, soft and vast and pleased. *You have a compass in you, little wren, and it points at her. Walk.*

Behind them, miles back on the dark road, a single rider crested the same hill — a lean figure on a gray horse, no escort, no banner, a badge turned face-down in her pocket like a thing she was ashamed of. Sarel Vane reined in at the shrine, dismounted, and stood for a moment studying the freshly carved keyhole with an expression the Purge's founders would have burned her for.

Then she looked east, at the small footprints in the dew, heading toward a village that was not on any map, following a girl who was not supposed to exist, carrying a god that was not supposed to wake — and spurred the horse on, because whatever waited at the end of that trail, she had decided she would rather meet it than file a report about it.

The compass pointed east. All three of them followed it.
