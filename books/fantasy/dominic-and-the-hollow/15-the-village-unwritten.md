## 15. The Village Unwritten

The road forgot them three days before Errow did.

Lior noticed it first in the way the ruts disappeared — not worn smooth, not washed away by rain, simply *absent*, as if no cart had ever rolled this way and the grass had never been bothered to grow back. Then the milestones stopped. Then, somewhere past a leaning stone that might once have marked a boundary, the trees began to look embarrassed about something.

"Are we lost?" Ivet asked, for the eleventh time since dawn. He said it cheerfully each time, the way other men said *good morning*. He was a broad, soft-spoken man with ink-stained fingers and the permanent squint of someone who had spent thirty years arguing with maps, and he carried his satchel of instruments the way a priest carries relics — which, Lior supposed, in his case they were.

"No," she said.

"You're certain."

"I'm following the feeling behind my ribs."

"Ah," Ivet said. "Cartography's loss is medicine's gain."

She almost smiled. Almost. The hook behind her ribs — because that was what it was, there was no point pretending otherwise anymore, some patient fisherman's hook set deep under her sternum with a line running east — had been pulling steadily for two days now, and every mile it pulled, the ache eased a little, like a splinter working its way toward the skin. Whatever waited at the end of this road wanted her to arrive. That was either a comfort or a trap, and Lior had learned the hard way that the two were often the same thing dressed differently.

They camped that night in the hollow of a hill where an old waystone stood, and Lior dreamed of Aldous's voice saying her name from behind a sealed door, and woke with frost on her eyelashes and Wren's absence sitting on her chest like a stone. The boy was still out there somewhere, walking toward her through a country that wanted him dead or worse, drawn by the same voice that drew her. Every day she didn't find him was a day the voice got to keep him.

"We'll send word ahead when we can," Ivet said, reading her face over the fire. He was good at that. It was unnerving. "There will be people in Errow. There are always people."

"There's no Errow," Lior said. "You told me yourself. You've mapped half the borderlands twice over and there's nothing there but forest and a river bend."

"Yes." Ivet turned his hands palm-up, apologetic. "And yet here we are, walking toward it. In my experience, the places that aren't on maps are the ones most insistent about existing. A map is just a record of what someone was willing to admit to. Errow has apparently decided against being admitted to."

"That's not how geography works."

"My dear girl, I have made my living for fifteen years mapping things that officially do not exist. Geography works exactly however it likes, and then men with seals write down the parts that suit them." He poked the fire. "The Purge didn't just burn charms. It burned *records*. Whole villages that kept the old rites were struck from the rolls — not destroyed, mind you, *unwritten*. Their taxes reassigned, their roads rerouted on paper, their names given to other places. As far as the Inquisition's ledgers are concerned, those villages never existed, so nothing that happens in them can be a crime."

Lior stared at him. "That's monstrous."

"It's efficient," Ivet said mildly. "Monstrous and efficient are old friends. They drink together."

* * *

They smelled Errow before they saw it: woodsmoke and bread and, underneath both, something green and strange — a scent like crushed herbs and rain on hot stone that Lior hadn't smelled since she was small enough to sit under Aldous's workbench while he ground dried vervain. Her eyes stung before she could stop them.

The village appeared around a bend in the river as if it had been waiting for permission. Thatched roofs, slate roofs, one tower with a bell. Geese. An unreasonable number of geese. Laundry strung between houses. And everywhere — everywhere — the signs.

A charm-knot hung above every door. Not the weak, pretty things Lior sold in Vess's night market for two coppers and a lie; these were *real*, woven in patterns she knew by heart, guild patterns, the fever-ward and the hearth-blessing and the traveler's knot done properly, with the right number of turns and the ends left loose the way Aldous had taught her — *"you leave room for the god to get a finger in, Lior-girl, or what's the point of asking."*

An old woman was braiding one at her doorstep as they passed, humming. She looked up, nodded pleasantly, and went back to her humming, as if a stranger walking into her village were the least remarkable thing that had happened all week.

"They're not hiding it," Lior whispered.

"Why would they?" Ivet murmured back. "As far as any ledger in Vess is concerned, we're standing in unclaimed forest."

"But the Inquisition—"

"The Inquisition," said a voice behind them, warm and dry as autumn, "has never once found Errow. We find that suits everyone."

Lior turned. The woman standing in the lane was perhaps sixty, tall and straight-backed, with grey hair cropped short and eyes the deep brown of river stones. She wore plain wool, undyed, and around her wrist — casually, the way another woman might wear a ribbon — a cord bearing a sigil stamped in lead.

Lior's hand went to her own sleeve before she could stop it.

The woman's gaze followed the gesture. Something moved through her face — slow, careful, like weather changing — and she said, very quietly:

"Well," she said. "Look what the road brought us."

* * *

Her name was Sera Voss, and she was, she explained as she walked them through the village toward the chapel, the priestess — though priestess, she admitted, was a generous word for a job that mostly involved settling disputes about geese and remembering which prayers went with which seasons.

"Geese disputes," Lior repeated.

"You'd be amazed how much theology is required to divide a flock fairly between two households who both claim the birds were theirs to begin with." Sera Voss didn't smile, quite, but the corner of her mouth suggested she had once known how. "The god doesn't care about geese. But the god cares about how we treat each other over geese, which amounts to the same thing in practice."

"And the sigil," Lior said. Her heart was doing something complicated and painful. "On your wrist. Where did you learn it?"

Sera Voss stopped in the middle of the lane. Around them the village went on — children chasing a dog, a man repairing a roof, the geese conducting their eternal parliament — and none of them looked twice at the three of them standing still, which somehow made it worse.

"Every child in Errow learns the knots," she said. "We learn them the way you'd learn your letters. But the sigil—" she touched the lead stamp gently, the way you'd touch a scar, "—the sigil belongs to the Hollow Guild. We've kept it alive the way you keep a fire alive in a house whose owners have gone away. Not because it's ours. Because someone should be home when they come back."

Lior couldn't speak.

Thirty years. Thirty years since the Purge had scattered the guild like ash on wind — masters burned or fled or bought, the halls sealed, the name itself a crime in eleven provinces. Thirty years of Lior selling luck-knots too weak to be called magic, hiding her birthright under her sleeve like a wound she was ashamed of. And all this time, in a village that wasn't on any map, strangers had been keeping the light on.

"You knew," she managed finally. "You knew the guild survived. Someone survived."

"One or two, here and there, over the years. They came up the same road you did, usually half-starved, always surprised." Sera Voss resumed walking. "We fed them. We listened. Some stayed. Most went on, because the ones who carry that sigil seem constitutionally incapable of staying anywhere safe." She glanced sideways at Lior. "You'll want to argue with me about that. Don't bother. I've buried enough of your kind to know how the argument goes."

Ivet made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

* * *

The chapel was small and old and wrong in a way Lior couldn't immediately name until she did: there was no altar to the Nine of the official faith, no gilded icon of the Concordant God the Inquisition enforced. Instead the single room held a great stone well — sunk into the floor itself, ancient, its rim worn smooth by generations of hands — and around the well, carved into the flagstones in a spiral, ran words in Old Hollowtongue.

Lior read them without meaning to. She'd learned the liturgical tongue at her master's knee, the way he had, the way every apprentice had, and the words rose in her like water finding its level:

*What was closed may open. What was given may return. What was hungered may be fed.*

Her blood went cold.

"Sera Voss," she said slowly, "what exactly does your village pray to?"

The priestess stood beside the well with her hands folded, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet.

"The same thing your guild prayed to, heir," she said. "The god under the world. The one your founders bound beneath Vess and called it protection. The one whose Door your order sealed and guarded and lied about for three hundred years." She turned, and her river-stone eyes were kind and pitiless at once. "We never believed the Purge's story. You don't live next door to a prison and mistake it for a graveyard. We heard it breathing."

Lior sat down. There was a bench; her legs found it more or less on their own.

Outside, the geese argued on. Somewhere a hammer rang on a roof. The ordinary world continued its business while the floor of Lior's understanding quietly gave way.

"Aldous told me the gods were waking," she said at last. "Through — through a boy. Through Wren. He said the Door must be found, that the guild had to be gathered. He didn't say—" She stopped. Started again. "He never said the guild *built* the prison. He talked like we were the wardens."

"Wardens, jailers, thieves — the titles blur after three centuries." Sera Voss crouched in front of her, unhurried. "But here is what matters, and why the road pulled you here instead of letting you die quietly in a ditch, which frankly was the likelier outcome. The binding is failing. Your master was right about that much. The god is stirring, and it is hungry, and it has begun answering prayers through cracks in the seal — badly, hungrily, the way a starving thing eats. Those burns in Vess? The hollows in the walls?"

Lior's hand pressed involuntarily against her ribs. "You know about those."

"We know about everything the god touches. It touches us too, heir. It has always touched us. We are the village it whispers to when it dreams." Sera Voss reached out and, with two fingers, drew back Lior's sleeve — gently, asking permission in the motion even as she did it — and exposed the sigil branded into Lior's forearm: the hollow circle, the broken line, the mark of the last apprentice of the last guild.

The priestess looked at it for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — dropped its dryness, its irony, all its comfortable armor, and underneath it was something old and reverent and terribly tired.

"Heir of the Hollow," she said formally, in Old Hollowtongue, and the words rang in the chapel like the bell they didn't ring. "The house has been waiting for you. The fire has been kept. Will you come home?"

And oh, it would have been so easy to say yes. That was the treacherous part, Lior thought later — that the offer was genuine, that the warmth was real, that every tired bone in her body leaned toward it like a plant toward sun. A village that sang the guild's songs. A fire kept burning thirty years for her sake. A chair at a table, a name spoken without fear, a place where the sigil on her arm was a welcome instead of a death warrant.

Belonging. Offered freely, on a plate, in a language she'd thought dead.

"I—" Lior's voice cracked. She steadied it. "Before I answer anything, I need to ask you something, and I need you not to soften it."

"Ask."

"The god. The one under the world. When it answers prayers — what does it cost? Not the people praying. *It.* What does it lose, or take, or break, every time it reaches through the seal?" She held the priestess's gaze. "Because I've felt it. Every answered prayer in Vess, I feel it here, like a hook behind my ribs, pulling me east. And I don't think a god pulls its heir across a country out of affection."

For the first time, Sera Voss looked away.

"Ah," she said softly. "So you've already paid something. I wondered." She rose, walked to the well, and rested her hand on its ancient rim. "No, heir. I won't soften it. The god is starving, and every miracle it performs costs it strength it cannot replace, and when the seal finally fails — soon, within a season, perhaps less — it will not go quietly. It will tear free, and it will feed, and it will not distinguish between the guilty and the geese." She looked back at Lior. "Unless the Door is opened properly. Unless the keys are gathered. Unless the heir comes home and pays the inheritance that's owed."

"What inheritance?" Lior asked. "I have nothing. I sell two-copper charms. My whole estate fits in this bag."

Sera Voss smiled then, and it was the saddest thing Lior had seen since Tessin's shop went up in flames behind her.

"That," the priestess said, "is what you think. Come. Eat first. The dead questions keep; the living shouldn't. And tomorrow—" she paused at the chapel door, silhouetted against the evening gold, "—tomorrow we must talk about the boy. Because he's coming here, heir. He's been coming here since the day the god first spoke through him. And he is not coming alone."

Lior was on her feet before the sentence ended. "Sarel Vane."

"An Inquisitor walks your trail like a shadow," Sera Voss agreed calmly. "Half of Errow wants to bar the gates against her. Half wants to throw them wide and beg her forgiveness. And the god—" the priestess's hand tightened on the doorframe, and for just a moment Lior saw fear move across her face like a fish under ice, "—the god is whispering to my village too. Promising miracles. Promising *anything*. And I am no longer entirely sure which half of my flock is listening to me, and which half is listening to it."

Outside, the geese fell suddenly, completely silent.

All of them. At once.

Then, from the direction of the well, faint and far beneath the flagstones, something sighed — long and low and satisfied, like a guest who has finally heard the front door open.

Sera Voss closed her eyes. "It knows you're here," she said. "Welcome home, heir. Gods help us both."
