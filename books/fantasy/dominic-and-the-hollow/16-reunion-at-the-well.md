## 16. Reunion at the Well

The bell in Errow's chapel tower rang wrong, and Lior knew before anyone told her why.

She had spent four days in the village learning its rhythms — the way the baker's wife sang to her ovens, the way old men argued dice in the shade of the tannery, the way the priestess, Ysolde, moved through morning like a woman setting a table for guests who might arrive at any hour. The bells were part of it: dawn, noon, dusk, three notes each, plain and unadorned. Guild-bells, almost. Lior had almost stopped flinching at them.

But this one rang ragged, hurried, over and over without counting, and Ysolde came down the chapel steps two at a time with her sleeves still wet from washing.

"The well," she said. "Someone's coming up the east road, and Tam says it's a child walking alone."

Lior's heart did something complicated and painful. "How old?"

"Small. Ten, maybe. Carrying nothing." Ysolde paused at the bottom of the steps and looked at her with those clear grey eyes that seemed to weigh everything and forgive most of it. "You know him."

It wasn't a question, so Lior didn't insult either of them with a lie. "His name is Wren."

"And the Inquisitor?"

"Behind him. Days behind, maybe hours. I don't know anymore."

Ysolde nodded once, as if Lior had confirmed something she'd already written down somewhere, and started across the square. "Then we'd best get the boy indoors before his shadow arrives."

* * *

They found Wren at the well itself, standing at its lip with both hands flat on the stones as though he'd been holding himself upright by will alone. He was thinner than Lior remembered, dusty to the elbows, one shoe missing entirely, and when he looked up at her his face went through relief so fast and so complete that it made her chest ache.

"Lior," he said. Then, in Hollowtongue, soft and precise as liturgy: *"The door remembers your name."*

"Don't do that," she said gently, kneeling to his level. "Don't let it talk through you right now. Talk as you."

"I'm tired of talking as me," Wren said, with the terrible honesty of children. "It's loud in here. It's been getting louder since Vess. It wanted to go north and I wanted to go east and we fought about it the whole way like an old married couple." He rubbed his nose with the back of his hand, leaving a streak of dust. "We compromised on east because there was bread smell."

Despite everything, despite the dread sitting in her stomach like a swallowed stone, Lior laughed. "A sound strategic mind."

"There was also a dog," Wren added seriously. "For two days. It left when I did."

Ivet appeared at the edge of the crowd that had gathered — half of Errow, it seemed, drawn by the bell and the spectacle of a stranger child at their well. He had a cartographer's habit of materializing precisely when geography became interesting. He took one look at Wren, one look at Lior's face, and said, "I'll watch the road."

"Sarel won't ride up waving a warrant," Lior said. "She'll walk in quiet, ask questions, be polite. That's worse."

"I know," Ivet said. "I mapped her type years ago, back when mapping things was legal." He squeezed her shoulder once and went.

* * *

They put Wren in the chapel's back room, which Ysolde explained was where they kept the sick, the visiting, and the occasionally possessed, "in descending order of frequency." He fell asleep mid-sentence, mid-chew, halfway through describing a cheese he'd been given by a farmer two villages back, and Lior sat beside him listening to him breathe and trying not to think about how easily a child could become a vessel and how little anyone had asked him about it.

Ysolde brought tea and sat across from her, unhurried.

"You're waiting for me to tell you what the sigil means," Lior said.

"I'm waiting for tea to steep," Ysolde said. "The other thing can happen after."

Lior wrapped her hands around the cup. Outside, Errow carried on — hammering from the smithy, someone singing badly, the ordinary music of a place that had never been written down and therefore never been burned. "When the priestess called me heir—"

"I meant it."

"It's not an honor. It's a debt with legs. Everything the guild hoarded, everything it broke to keep — I'm the last one standing when the bill comes due. You should give the title to someone with better prospects."

Ysolde sipped her tea. "In Errow we have a saying: you don't choose the river that runs through your land. You just decide whether to dam it or drink." She set the cup down. "The god under Vess has been whispering to my people too, Lior. Promising miracles. Curing a lame knee here, finding a lost ring there. Small mercies, priced high later. Half my congregation dreams of it every night now, and the other half has started sleeping with iron nails under their pillows." Her mouth twisted wryly. "Last week Mrs. Hallow the miller accused Mrs. Hallow's own sister of witchcraft over a disputed fence line. The nails are going to everyone's heads."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Nothing yet. Be here. An heir is proof of something my people have only known as rumor — that the guild was real, that the sealing was real, that this isn't just a hungry wind blowing through." Ysolde looked toward the back room, where Wren slept. "And that child is proof of something else. Proof the god is already loose enough to speak."

That was when Tam burst in — the boy who'd rung the bell, all elbows and urgency — and said, breathless: "There's a woman at the gate. Grey coat. Asking about pilgrim roads and whether we've seen a child pass through." He swallowed. "She showed a badge."

* * *

Lior stood at the chapel window and watched Sarel Vane come up the village street, and hated how much she respected the sight.

Sarel walked the way water freezes — gradually, inevitably, no wasted motion. She had left her horse outside the gate. She carried no visible weapon. Her badge was tucked away again, offered once and withdrawn, a courtesy rather than a threat. Two villagers trailed her at a cautious distance the way cats follow something dangerous to learn its habits.

"She'll ask for the headman first," Ivet murmured beside her. "Establish legitimacy. Then the priestess — clergy respects clergy, even the kind it burns. Then she'll start on the children, because children don't lie well."

"You've thought about this a lot."

"I've hidden from people like her a lot." Ivet's voice was light; his knuckles on the windowsill weren't. "Question is whether you meet her or hide."

Lior watched Sarel stop at the well. Watched her look down into it for a long moment — too long, reading something there — and then look up, directly at the chapel window, directly at Lior.

Of course. She'd known already. She always knew.

"If I hide," Lior said, "she tears the village apart looking. If I run, she follows me and leaves Errow intact but Wren—" Her stomach turned. "If I run, Wren runs too, and he's so tired, Ivet. He's ten and the god rides him like a borrowed horse."

"So you meet her."

"I meet her." Lior straightened her sleeve over her sigil out of pure habit, then deliberately pushed the fabric back. No more hiding. Not here. Not today. "Where does Ysolde keep the good tea?"

* * *

They met in the chapel, which was either Ysolde's masterstroke or her madness — neutral ground that belonged to neither of them, holy ground that belonged to neither of their orders. Sarel entered and stopped three steps inside, and Lior watched her take in the room: no altar to the sanctioned faith, none of Vess's austere whitewash. Instead the walls held carvings — spirals and doorways and figures with seed-shaped eyes, worn smooth by centuries of touching hands. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the rafters. Nine small clay lamps burned in a semicircle around an empty stone basin.

Sarel stared at the lamps for a long moment.

"We light them at dusk," Ysolde said mildly, from her place by the basin. "One for each of the nine who sealed themselves in. Old custom. Older than your Purge, Inquisitor. Older than the guild, if you want the truth of it."

"This is worship of proscribed entities," Sarel said. Her voice was flat, recitation-flat, and Lior heard the effort it cost. "Under statute—"

"Yes," Ysolde agreed pleasantly. "Under statute. Would you like to sit? The benches are older than the statute too."

For a moment Lio thought Sarel would turn and leave. Then she crossed the room and sat on the bench, spine straight, hands folded, a woman attending a service she intended to disapprove of thoroughly and completely.

Lior sat opposite. Up close, Sarel looked worse than she had at the market raid — thinner, shadowed under the eyes, her grey coat travel-worn and spotted with mud. There was a new burn scar along her jaw she hadn't earned in any fight Lior had witnessed. Something had happened to her between Vess and here. Something that had cost her.

"You're harboring a fugitive," Sarel said. "Two fugitives. And an accomplice whose maps are technically evidence."

"Technically," Ivet agreed from the doorway, "everything is evidence. That's the trouble with evidence."

"Inquisitor." Lior leaned forward. "Why are you really here?"

"To arrest you." Sarel said it simply, without heat. "That is my function. I traced the healed child. I traced the escaped child. Both roads led east, and both roads led to you. I have a warrant with your name on it, Eastwick, and I have carried it across half a dead country." Her hands tightened in her lap. "What I don't have—"

She stopped.

"Say it," Lior said quietly.

"What I don't have," Sarel said, "is an answer. I opened a sealed archive, Eastwick. Against orders. What I read in it — the Purge wasn't a victory. It was a prison transfer. We didn't kill the gods. Your guild locked them away, and my order has spent sixty years guarding the lock and calling it salvation." Her eyes came up, and they were burning, and Lior realized with a lurch that Sarel Vane was not here as an enemy. Or not only. "I burned charms in a market square for eleven years. I need to know what I actually burned."

Ysolde rose, went to the stone basin, and touched its rim. "Then ask," she said. "This is Errow, Inquisitor. Here, asking is permitted."

* * *

What followed was not a truce. Lior would remember it later as something stranger — two languages being spoken at once, neither fully understood.

Sarel questioned them for two hours, and Ysolde answered, and the answers were rites. When Sarel asked what the god under Vess wanted, Ysolde poured water into the basin and showed her how the ripples spread outward and returned. When Sarel asked why the guild had sealed the Door instead of destroying it, Ysolde lit the ninth lamp and said, "Because destruction requires a power the guild stole and could not hold," and Sarel wrote nothing down, which Lior understood was the loudest thing she'd done all day.

Wren woke near dusk. Lior heard him before she saw him — his footsteps in the corridor, hesitant, then the small body appearing in the doorway, blinking at the lamplight. He saw Sarel and froze.

Every muscle in Lior's body went tight. *Run*, she thought uselessly. *Hide him. Hide him now.*

But Wren didn't run. He walked into the chapel, past Sarel's bench, close enough to touch her, and studied her face with the unnerving directness of the very young and the very old.

"You're sad," he told her. "Not angry-sad. Empty-sad. Like a house where the furniture got sold."

Sarel went very still. "Child—"

"He says your name is Sarel," Wren continued, tilting his head, "and that you used to pray to something before you learned to burn it. He says—" and here Wren's face crumpled slightly, because the voice coming through him gentled into something ancient and grieving, "—he says he remembers your prayers, little clerk. Every one. He kept them. Even after you stopped sending them."

The silence in the chapel was total. Somewhere outside, the dusk bell began to ring.

Sarel Vane, Inquisitor of the Third Chair, veteran of eleven years of burnings, put her face in her hands and wept — silently, horribly, the way people cry who have forgotten how, each breath a controlled disaster. Lior sat frozen, unsure whether to move, whether this was mercy or the cruelest manipulation the god had yet attempted.

Ysolde, practical as rain, simply put a hand on Sarel's shoulder and waited.

It took a long time. When Sarel finally lowered her hands, her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"I'm not arresting anyone tonight," she said. Her voice was rough gravel. "Tomorrow I don't know. But tonight—" She looked at the nine lamps, at the basin, at Wren standing small and solemn with a god's grief still fading from his face. "Tonight I'd like to know what those lamps are for."

So Ysolde told her. About the nine who sealed themselves in willingly, before the guild grew greedy. About the covenant that predated the theft. About the difference, as Errow understood it, between a prison and a promise.

And Lior watched Sarel listen — really listen, the way scholars listen, hungry and horrified — and understood that something irreversible had happened in this room. Not forgiveness. Nothing so clean. But the Inquisitor had asked her first honest question in eleven years, and honest questions, once asked, cannot be unasked.

* * *

Later, past midnight, Lior found Sarel standing at the well in the dark, looking down into it.

"They say it's deep," Lior offered. "The well. Ivet asked. He can't help himself."

"Everything is deep if you're honest about it," Sarel said. She didn't look up. "Your master. Aldous. The archive named him. First among the sealers."

Lior's throat closed. "Yes."

"The archive also named the ones he informed on." Now Sarel looked up, and her expression was terrible and gentle at once. "Eastwick, whatever you believe he was — I've read the ledger entries. I know what the guild did to its own. Before you make him a saint, make sure you can survive meeting the man underneath."

"He's dead," Lior said. "Whatever he was, it's finished."

"Is it?" Sarel glanced meaningfully toward the chapel, where Wren slept. "Dead men have been very talkative lately."

Lior wanted to argue. Wanted badly to defend the hollow-eyed kindness of the only father she'd ever had. But Sarel had read ledgers Lior had never seen, and the worst part — the part that kept her awake after — was that Lior had already heard it, hadn't she? In the sealed guildhall. Behind the inner door. *The Door must be found.* And Aldous had never once said *why.*

"I should arrest you tomorrow," Sarel said abruptly. "It's what the law demands. It's what I am."

"But?"

"But I read a report today that says the law was built on a lie I helped maintain." Sarel's laugh was short and bitter. "Do you know what happens to an Inquisitor who discovers her whole life was a prison guard's uniform? She becomes very interested in doors, Eastwick. All of them. Especially the ones her superiors don't want opened."

"That sounds like treason."

"It is." Sarel said it plainly, without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "I've decided to commit it carefully. Which means I need to know what you know. All of it. The map fragments, the keys, the heir business, the child. Because in three days, maybe less, my order notices I've stopped sending reports. And when they notice, they send people considerably less thoughtful than me."

Lior looked at her — this woman who had burned her master's tools in the market square, who had hunted her across a country, who had wept at a god's grief and now stood offering treason like a trade agreement.

"Why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't," Sarel said. "Trust is earned in installments. Consider this the first payment." She reached into her coat and set something on the well's stone lip: her badge, the brass dull in the moonlight. "When my order comes, show them this at the checkpoint. They won't question a Third Chair credential. It'll buy you an hour, maybe two."

"You're giving me your authority."

"I'm giving you time," Sarel corrected. "Authority was never mine. That was rather the point of the whole rotten system." She turned to go, then paused. "Eastwick. The boy — Wren. When the god speaks through him... does it hurt him?"

"Sometimes. More lately."

Something moved across Sarel's face — guilt, or its cousin. "Then we find your Door quickly," she said. "Before it eats him alive."

* * *

Dawn came grey and cold. Lior stood at the chapel door with tea going cold in her hands, watching Sarel saddle her horse at the village gate. Not leaving — riding out to meet her own order's scouts on the east road, to feed them false directions, to buy the days she'd promised. A double agent in a grey coat, committing treason in installments.

Wren appeared beside her, hair sleep-wild, one shoe still missing.

"Is the sad lady our friend now?" he asked.

"I don't know," Lior admitted. "Ask me in three days."

Wren considered this with great seriousness. Then he pointed at the well, where the water had gone perfectly still — unnaturally still, mirror-still, though there was no wind to blame.

"It likes her," he said. "The god. It doesn't like many people. Mostly it just wants and wants." His small face clouded. "Lior — it's starting to say my name instead of Aldous's. At night. When I'm asleep, it practices saying my name."

Lior crouched and took his shoulders. "Then we don't wait three days," she said. "We find the first key. Today."

Behind them, unnoticed, the well water trembled — once, softly, like something turning over in its sleep — and settled again into silence.

Far to the west, in a city that still believed itself saved, a temple bell cracked down the middle, and no one could explain why.
