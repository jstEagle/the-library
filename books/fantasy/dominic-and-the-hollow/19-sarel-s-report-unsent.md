## 19. Sarel's Report, Unsent

The candle had burned down to a knuckle of wax by the time Sarel Vane admitted she was writing the report wrong.

She had started it four times. Four sheets of good Inquisition paper lay crumpled in the wastebasket like the shed skins of small, failed serpents. The first draft had opened with *It is my duty to inform the Office of Inquiry that* — and then stopped, because her hand refused to write the next word. The second began with an apology, which was worse. The third was so hedged with qualifying language that by the third paragraph it said nothing at all, a document shaped like fog.

The fourth sat before her now:

*I have found evidence that contradicts the founding account of the Purge.*

There. It was out on paper, where things became real and dangerous. She looked at the sentence for a long moment, the way one looks at a dog that has just knocked over something expensive — with a mixture of anger and a strange, helpless affection.

"You're going to be the death of me," she told the sentence.

The sentence did not reply. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, in the courtyard of the Chapterhouse, a novice was practicing bell-calls badly, and the sound drifted up through the frost-furred window like a bird learning to sing by falling off branches repeatedly.

Sarel pulled the wastebasket onto her desk and read through her own failures. Draft two, the apologetic one, actually wasn't bad. There was a line in it — *"I write this with fear, and I have decided to be honest about the fear rather than pretend it away"* — that she suspected would get her burned faster than any heresy, because the Inquisition could forgive doubt but never the admission of it.

She set the drafts aside and did what she always did when a problem resisted her: she made tea, and she laid out the facts as if they were confiscated charms on an examination table, each one labeled and turned over and stared at until it confessed.

Fact the first: Tessin the apothecary had asked her a question under interrogation, and she had not been able to answer it. *What did the Purge actually save anyone from?* Eleven years of service, three hundred interrogations, forty-one burnings witnessed, and she could not produce an answer that didn't sound rehearsed. She knew all the liturgical responses. *From the hunger of the old gods. From the chaos of unlicensed power. From the age when prayer was a knife held to the world's throat.* She had recited them to frightened people in small rooms for a decade. But recitation, she was beginning to understand, was not the same thing as knowing, the way a parrot knows words without knowing weather.

Fact the second: the sealed archive beneath the Hall of Records. She had broken into it against orders, using her own credentials and a forged counter-signature she was not proud of, though she had executed it very well. Inside she had found the Purge ledgers — the true ones, not the sanitized chronicles every novice memorized. And the true ones said nothing about gods dying. They spoke in careful bureaucratic language about *containment*, about *the Sealing Action*, about *perimeter integrity*. One entry, in the cramped hand of First Inquisitor Halvard himself, read: *We do not slay what cannot be slain. We lock the door and post a watch and call the watching a victory.* Below that, someone — later, different ink — had written simply: *And who watches the watchers?* That question had been underlined twice, and then someone else had crossed it out so hard the paper tore.

Fact the third: the boy. Wren, eight years old, healed of a fever that should have killed him, now speaking a dead tongue fluently. Sarel had heard the recording-scribes' transcription. She did not know Old Hollowtongue — almost no one did; it had been purged from every curriculum — but she knew *cadence*, and the transcription moved in the cadence of liturgy. Call-and-response. A congregation of one.

Fact the fourth, the one she kept coming back to at night, lying awake in her narrow bed listening to the Chapterhouse settle: the prayer-burns. Hollows scorched into walls and cobbles wherever a desperate person had prayed hard enough and something on the other side had answered. Forty-one confirmed in Vess and rising daily. The official position was that smuggler-covens were causing them, and the official position required her to believe that smugglers had developed, in secret, in a city where the Inquisition watched everything, the ability to answer prayers. Which was not how magic worked. Magic, even the forbidden kind, took from the caster. These burns gave. Something was *giving*.

Sarel drank her tea. It had gone cold and bitter, which suited her mood, so she drank it anyway.

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud helped her think and she had long ago stopped being embarrassed about it. "You believe the archive. You believe the burns. You believe the boy. Now write it."

She pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began again, and this time she did not stop.

---

By the second bell after midnight she had nine pages.

They were good pages. She hated that they were good pages. She wrote the way she arrested — thoroughly, methodically, leaving no gap for escape — and what emerged was a document that laid out, step by evidentiary step, the following argument: that the Purge had never destroyed the old gods but had imprisoned them; that the Inquisition's founders had known this and encoded it in records they then sealed from their own successors; that the current spate of answered prayers indicated the prison was failing; and that the appropriate response was neither suppression nor denial but investigation, preparation, and possibly negotiation, since one did not burn one's way out of a prison breach, one either reinforced the wall or learned to speak to what was inside.

Nine pages of clean reasoning. Any archivist would call it exemplary work. Any Second Chair would call it treason.

She read it through once more, looking for the flaw, the soft place where a superior could puncture it and send her to the reflection cells. There wasn't one. That was the problem. A flawed report could be defended — *I was hasty, I was misled, forgive my zeal* — but a flawless heresy was just heresy wearing armor.

She tried imagining presenting it. She put herself across the desk from Second Chair Ondrel, that great gray monument of a man with his voice like a drawer of unsorted cutlery, and watched him read page one, and page two, and arrive at the phrase *the Sealing Action* and go very still. She imagined him looking up at her. She imagined the pause. She had seen that pause before, from the other side of the desk, given to others. It was not a pause of consideration. It was a pause of arithmetic — a man calculating what she cost weighed against what she'd contributed, and finding, with genuine regret, that the ledger tipped toward ash.

Eleven years, Sarel thought. Eleven years of dawn shifts and cold courtyards and the smell of burning parchment that gets into wool and never leaves. And it would end in the same fire as everyone else's, because the fire does not distinguish between the witch and the inquisitor who hesitates. She had written those words herself once, in a training manual revision. *The flame is impartial.* She had meant it as comfort. It had apparently been a threat all along, waiting its turn.

She got up, stretched until her shoulders cracked, and paced the length of her cell-room — six steps, turn, six steps — which she had furnished, over the years, with exactly one luxury: a chair by the window, stolen from a condemned office during a restructuring nobody missed it from. She sat in it now and looked out at Vess under curfew, the streets empty and blue-white with frost, the temple spires black against a sky that had no stars in it worth naming.

Somewhere down there, in the poor quarters, people were praying. She could feel it lately, the way you feel weather coming in a scar. Every answered prayer was another hollow burned into the city, and every hollow was proof that the thing under the world was waking, hungry, and — here was the part that kept her pacing — *listening*. The archive's final section had been a list. Not names. *Requests.* Things people had prayed for in the last months before the Sealing, recorded by some diligent clerk as data. *A widow asks for her son home from the war. A farmer asks for rain. A girl asks that her father stop drinking.* Page after page of small human wants, catalogued with the same dry precision as grain inventories. And in the margin beside them, in Halvard's hand again, one line that Sarel had copied out and now knew by heart:

*It answers kindly at first. That is how it learns what we cannot refuse.*

Kindly at first. The fever-broken boy speaking a dead language. Was that kindness? It had felt like kindness to the mother, surely — her son alive, warm, breathing. And yet. Something had come through with the mercy. Something had taken up residence behind a child's eyes and begun reciting oaths.

Sarel pressed her forehead against the cold glass. "You are losing your mind," she informed her reflection. Her reflection, which had dark circles under its eyes and a smudge of ink on its chin, declined to argue.

But here was the thought she could not shake, the one that had followed her out of the archive and up the stairs and into bed and through three days of interrogations she had conducted on autopilot: *if the prison is failing, then everything I have done for eleven years has been maintenance work on a wall that is already coming down.* Not evil. Not even misguided. Just — irrelevant. Sweeping a floor while the house burns from underneath. And if that were true, then the only question left, the only question with any weight at all, was: when the wall comes down, do you want to be standing on it, defending it, or on the far side, having at least tried to understand what was coming through?

She went back to the desk. She picked up the report.

And then she did the exercise that broke her heart, because she was thorough even in treachery: she read it one final time as her enemy would.

Page one. Fine. Page two. Fine. Page four — there. Page four, where she cited the archive's shelf-mark and accession date. Because that citation proved access, and access proved the forgery, and the forgery proved premeditation. A careful reader wouldn't even need the rest of the report. The citation was the confession; the other eight pages were just decoration around the gallows.

And even if she removed the citation — even if she scrubbed every traceable detail — the report itself would still arrive at the Second Chair's desk, and he would read it, and he would face exactly the choice she was facing now, except with more power and less honesty available to him. He would bury it. Of course he would. Burying it was the only act that preserved the Inquisition, and the Inquisition was the only thing he had ever been permitted to preserve. Or — and this possibility she turned over slowly, like a stone with something underneath it — he would forward it upward, to the High Office, and it would vanish into whatever sealed room handled *inconvenient truths about the Sealing*, and somewhere a clerk would note her name, and within the month she would find herself reassigned to some distant province, or invited to a reflection retreat, or asked to surrender her credentials *pending review*, and the review would never conclude.

Either way, the report died. Either way, she probably did too, just more slowly in one version.

"So," she said to the room. "You've written a beautiful document that can only be received as a bomb. Congratulations. Very thorough. Would you like a commendation?"

The novice below had finally gotten the bell-call right, or given up; either way, the silence that followed felt like applause.

Sarel sat down. She looked at the nine pages. Then she did something she had never done in eleven years of scrupulous, letter-perfect paperwork.

She folded the report in half, and in half again, and put it in the inner pocket of her coat, the one over her heart, where the badge usually rode.

The badge she set on the desk, face-up, glinting dully in the candlelight. She looked at it for a while. It was heavier than it looked, badges always were; you carried them for years before your shoulder noticed. She did not throw it in the wastebasket. She was not ready for theater. She simply moved it to the corner of the desk, the way you move a conversation you're not finished having.

Then she took out a fresh sheet, because a defector in secret still needed cover, and wrote, in her best dutiful hand:

*Report on night-market enforcement actions, week forty-two. Three vendors cautioned. Confiscated materials: seventeen luck-knots (inert), four fever-wards (degraded), one sigil-stamped cloth fragment (provenance under inquiry). No actionable intelligence recovered. Recommend continued surveillance of the east quarter.*

Every word of it true, except for the spirit, which had fled the document entirely. She signed it, sealed it, and set it atop the growing pile for morning delivery. Somewhere above her, the machinery of the Inquisition would ingest this small dishonesty and be satisfied, and no one would ever know that the woman who wrote it had the true report folded against her heart like a love letter from a country that didn't exist anymore.

She blew out the candle. In the dark, she rested her hand flat against her chest, feeling the crisp edges of nine pages through the wool.

*Tessin had asked her a question in that little room, hands cuffed, eyes tired and clear: what did the Purge actually save anyone from?*

"Not you," she murmured. "Not yet. But I'm working on the answer."

Outside, far off in the poor quarter, a faint orange light flickered against the low clouds — another prayer-burn, another hollow opening in the world, another small kindness leaking through the wall. She watched it for a moment. Then, quietly, deliberately, Sarel Vane began planning how to find a fugitive apprentice, a mad cartographer, and a child who spoke for a god — before anyone else in the Inquisition did.

* * *

Morning came gray and early. Sarel delivered her false report to the clerk's cage personally, watched it stamped and filed, and walked out into the courtyard feeling lighter than she had any right to feel, the way one feels after setting down something heavy in a place where no one saw you do it.

She was halfway across the frost-rimed flagstones when a runner caught up to her — young Brannic, all elbows and earnestness, breath steaming.

"Inquisitor Vane! Second Chair wants you. The boy's gone."

She turned slowly. "Gone?"

"Escaped custody in the night. Cell locked from outside, guards saw nothing." He swallowed. "Second Chair says — he says you traced the boy's healing to the market. He says whoever finds him first finds the smugglers. He's giving you the trail."

He was, was he. Giving her the leash and pointing her at the door, trusting her to bring back what the order needed — or to hang herself with it, whichever came first. Sarel looked past the boy's shoulder at the eastern sky, where the road out of Vess ran through country that officially contained nothing at all.

"Tell the Second Chair," she said, "that I'm already packed."

And she was. The report rode against her heart, and for the first time in eleven years, so did the truth.
