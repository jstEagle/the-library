## 20. The Second Key Is Buried

The fire in Errow's chapel had burned down to a red heart by the time Lior finished reading Maren Cole's confession for the third time.

She sat on the floor with her back against a pew, the paper trembling less now than it had an hour ago — not because the words had softened, but because she'd run out of shaking to do. Across the room, Ivet was pretending to work on his maps. He was very bad at pretending. Every thirty seconds or so he'd glance up at her over the top of his compass, look away quickly when she noticed, and then glance again, like a man watching a pot he was afraid might boil over.

"Say it," Lior said finally.

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You've looked at me eleven times in the last two minutes. I counted."

"That's cartography," Ivet said. "Triangulation."

"You're triangulating my face."

"It's a very informative face." He set down the compass and gave up the pretense entirely. "All right. Say what you're thinking, then, since you clearly want me to ask."

Lior looked down at the confession again. Maren's handwriting had gone wobbly toward the end, the letters of a woman writing fast before her courage evaporated. *The second key belonged to Dannel Foss. They took him in the third week of the Purge. He didn't give them anything — not his name even, they had that already — so they buried him whole, key and all, in the field outside Vess where they put everyone else. The convicts' field. Past the tanneries, past the ash heaps. There's no marker. There were never markers.*

"The second key is in a grave," Lior said. "A mass grave. Outside Vess."

Ivet was quiet for a moment. "The city we just spent three chapters of our lives escaping."

"I don't know about chapters. But yes. That city."

"With checkpoints. And curfews. And an Inquisition that has your sketch nailed to every market post from here to the river."

"And," Lior said, "a rioting population, martial law, and prayer-burns spreading through the walls like rot in a hull. Don't forget those. It makes it sound so much more inviting."

Ivet rubbed his eyes. Outside, somewhere across the village, Wren was laughing at something — one of the priestess's granddaughters had taught him a skipping game, and his laugh carried on the evening air, bright and ordinary and completely wrong for the conversation they were having. Lior found herself listening to it the way you listen to rain when you're trying to decide whether to bring the harvest in.

"So we go back," Ivet said. It wasn't a question. She could hear him arriving at it the same way she had, step by reluctant step, like walking into cold water.

"We go back."

"For a key. In a grave. With two hundred other graves around it, probably."

"Maren says three hundred and forty-one." Lior folded the confession along its worn creases. "She counted. She would have been the one keeping the ledgers, back then. Informants always count things. It's how they make themselves feel precise instead of complicit."

"That's bitter."

"That's accurate. There's a difference, but it's small."

* * *

The priestess found them there an hour later, when the chapel had gone fully dark except for the banked coals. She moved quietly for an old woman; Lior hadn't heard the door. One moment they were alone with their dread, and the next Sefa was settling onto the pew beside Lior with the unhurried ease of someone who had been sitting in chapels longer than either of them had been alive.

"Dannel Foss," Sefa said.

Lior startled. "You read it too?"

"I read everything that comes into my chapel. Old habit. In the years when reading got people burned, you learned to read everything, quickly, and burn nothing." She folded her hands. "Dannel Foss. I knew him. Not well — he passed through here twice, maybe three times, carrying guild messages north. Big man. Terrible singer. Sang anyway, constantly, which tells you most of what you need to know about him."

"He sang?" Lior tried to picture it. Aldous had never spoken of the others much — or rather, he'd spoken of them constantly and somehow never told her anything true. Names without faces. A guild as a list.

"Badly," Sefa confirmed. "He used to say the gods didn't care if you hit the notes, only if you meant them. Which was either profound theology or an excuse, depending on the day." Her voice gentled. "He meant them. That's why they killed him and not just his name."

Lior looked at the coals. "The key is buried with him. In the convict field outside Vess."

"Yes."

"So we dig him up."

"Yes."

"That's—" Lior searched for the word and found several, none adequate. "That's sacrilege. That's desecration. That's the single worst thing I can imagine doing to a man who died refusing to talk."

"It is," Sefa agreed placidly. "And?"

Lior blinked at her. "And—"

"And the dead of the Purge have been waiting twenty years for someone to come back for them. All of them. Not just Dannel." The old woman's eyes caught the coal-light. "You think digging in that field is a violation? Child, that field is full of people who were thrown away by a state that wanted them forgotten. Nobody has visited. Nobody has mourned aloud. Their names aren't carved anywhere. You going back there with a shovel isn't the desecration. The field already is. You'd be the first person in two decades to treat those graves like they hold anyone worth finding."

Lior hadn't thought of it that way. She suspected she'd spend the rest of this whole affair being shown, repeatedly, all the ways her guilt had arranged itself into shapes that weren't quite the truth.

"He'll forgive me," she said, mostly to herself. "The terrible singing. He'll understand."

"Oh, he'll haunt you," Sefa said cheerfully. "But affectionately."

* * *

They told Wren the next morning, which went worse and better than expected.

Worse, because Wren refused outright to be left behind — stood in the middle of the priestess's kitchen with his arms crossed and his chin out, ten years old and immovable as a boundary stone. Better, because the reason he refused turned out to be Aldous.

"He says I have to come," Wren reported, in the flat, slightly distant voice he used when the voice in him spoke through his mouth. His eyes stayed his own, though. That was the part that frightened Lior least and most at once: the boy remained in there, watching, while something older borrowed the front of him. "He says the keys answer faster near me. He says—" Wren paused, listening to something only he could hear, and then his face did something complicated. "He says he's sorry about your childhood. He says it wasn't supposed to cost that much."

Lior's hand went, without her permission, to the place behind her ribs where the missing memories lived — the soft gap where her mother's face should have been, where a house in the low town should have been, where a dog whose name she could no longer retrieve had slept at the foot of her bed. Using the guild's real magic to break Sarel out of the cells had taken those. She'd known the price going in. Knowing hadn't made the paying lighter.

"Tell him," Lior said carefully, "that apology is noted. And that he owes me a dog's name."

Wren tilted his head. Then he grinned suddenly, all boy again. "He says it was Bristle. A terrier. Ugly. Bit the postman."

The laugh came out of Lior before she could stop it — a real one, surprising her, the first in days. "Bristle. Yes. Yes, that's right, he—" and then the rest of the memory slid away again like water through fingers, because the magic hadn't left her a loan, it had left her a receipt. She knew the fact. She couldn't keep the feeling. It was the cruelest kind of bookkeeping.

Wren's grin faded too. "It comes back sometimes," he said quietly. "When I'm near you. Little pieces. He says the god holds what the magic took, and the god doesn't lose things."

"The god loses entire countries," Lior said.

"He says that was different."

"They always say that," Ivet muttered from the corner, packing his maps.

In the end Wren came, because there was no force in Errow — including the priestess, who had tried — that could stop him, and because Lior, lying awake after everyone had argued themselves hoarse, admitted the truth: the keys answered faster near him, and speed mattered, because every day the burns spread further through Vess and the thing under the city grew hungrier and half of Errow wanted the Door destroyed and the other half wanted it opened wide, and the ground between those halves was shrinking.

So: four of them. A fugitive heir, an exiled mapmaker, a haunted child, and whatever Aldous had become.

Going back into the lion's den.

* * *

Sefa prepared them the way villages like Errow had once prepared pilgrims, before such preparations had to happen in cellars. She fed them until they complained. She dressed them in the gray-brown wool of northern pilgrims, sun-faded and mended, clothes that said *we are poor and pious and boring, please ignore us*. She cut Lior's hair shorter with a knife against a bowl — "you have a criminal's face, dear, we must distract from it" — and taught Ivet to walk like a man with bad knees, which he took to with insulting naturalness.

"This is typecasting," Ivet said, hobbling a circuit of the kitchen.

"It's casting," Sefa said. "Type would require talent."

Even Sarel's badge came out of hiding — Lior had carried it since the graveyard wall, iron and heavy, the Inquisition's eye stamped deep. Sefa studied it without touching it.

"You'll wear this inside your coat," she said. "Not to show. To show is to die. But if a checkpoint goes wrong, you put it in a guard's hand and you let them think whatever thinking it buys you. Understand? It's not honor. It's a tool that happens to be shaped like a lie."

"Sarel gave it to us to pass the gates," Lior said.

"Sarel gave it to you because she couldn't give you anything else. That's different, and you know it, and it will still work." Sefa pressed the badge into Lior's palm and closed her fingers over it. "Now. The field. Tell me what you know of it."

Lior laid out what Maren's ledger had given them: the convict burial ground lay west of Vess, beyond the tannery district, past the ash heaps where the city dumped its burnt refuse — a deliberate geography, she understood now, so that the smoke of the executions' pyres and the smoke of the garbage would mingle and no one could say which smell was which. Unconsecrated ground. No wall. No markers. A fence, probably, and a watchman who drew wages for guarding nothing.

"There'll be a garrison nearby," Ivet said, tapping the map. "Martial law means patrols doubled. And the prayer-burns — if they've reached the tanneries, the whole quarter will be crawling with Inquisitors looking for causes."

"Then we don't go as ourselves," Lior said. "We go as what the field actually needs." She looked up, and felt the old guild logic settle over her like a coat that still fit. "Grief-workers. Mourner-hire. Three hundred and forty-one unmarked graves and a city full of families who lost people in the Purge and were never allowed to weep publicly. Pilgrims who've come to tend the dead. Nobody questions mourners. Nobody searches mourners. Everyone wants mourners gone as fast as possible, because they remind people of things."

Sefa smiled slowly. "There's the guild blood. Aldous trained you better than he knew."

"He trained me to sell luck-knots to people who needed them," Lior said. "This is the same trade. Just with a shovel."

* * *

They left at dusk two days later, and the road east was full of ghosts of the living kind.

Refugees, mostly — the first trickle of what rumor said would become a flood, folk leaving Vess ahead of the martial law crackdowns, carrying bundles and children and that particular stunned quiet of people who had left home faster than they could decide to. And among them, moving the other way, pilgrims. Always pilgrims. Thin, dusty, determined people walking toward the capital with their faces set, drawn by the same rumor that had reached Errow: that the Door existed, that the old god was waking, that prayers were being answered somewhere and might be answered anywhere.

One old man fell into step beside them for a mile. He had a wooden charm on a cord around his neck — crude, handmade, the kind Lior had sold a thousand of in the night market, the kind that shouldn't have worked.

"My wife's fever broke last month," he said, catching Lior looking. "Forty years of fevers, and it broke in a night. Nobody prayed that hard. We'd stopped praying, mostly." He touched the charm. "Something's listening again. I mean to find out what, and thank it properly."

"What if it wants something back?" Wren asked.

The old man considered this with the unhurried air of someone who had outlived several empires' worth of urgency. "Everything wants something back, lad. The trick is knowing whether what it wants is worth what you got." He nodded to them all and drifted on ahead, and Lior watched him go and thought: *that's the whole war, right there, walking on two legs.*

Ivet walked beside her as the light failed. "You're doing the face again."

"What face?"

"The one where you're triangulating the future and don't like the coordinates."

Lior huffed. Ahead of them, Vess was not yet visible — it wouldn't be for another day — but she could feel it, the way you feel a storm behind hills: a pressure, a weight, a faint wrongness in the air. The prayer-burns. Each one tugged at her, a hook behind the ribs, the guild's inheritance in her blood resonating with its stolen source. Some nights the hooks pulled in chorus.

"When we get there," she said, "we go in, we go to the field, we do the work, and we come out. No detours. No heroics. I'm done with heroics. Heroics are how Tessin ended up in a cell and my master ended up in a door."

"And how you ended up with a warrant and a sketch on every post."

"Especially that."

Ivet was quiet a few steps. Then: "For what it's worth — and I know exactly what it's worth, which is very little — I've dug graves before. Proper ones. My father's. My brother's. It's not work I ever thought I'd be grateful to know how to do." He adjusted his pack straps. "But I'd rather dig for Dannel Foss than for almost anyone. Sefa's stories helped. A man who sings badly on purpose, on principle — you want to get that one's key back. You want to tell him somebody kept the faith he kept."

"He'll haunt us affectionately," Lior said. "Sefa promised."

"Sefa promises a lot of things."

"She's never been wrong yet. It's deeply annoying."

The stars came out, hard and clean away from the city's smoke. Somewhere behind them Errow's factions were arguing by lamplight over the fate of the world; somewhere ahead Vess lay under curfew with its walls full of burning hollows and its temples full of soldiers. And somewhere between, in a field with no markers, three hundred and forty-one people waited under the dirt with their names unsaid and one of them holding a piece of a Door in his closed hand.

Lior walked on through the dark, toward the lion's den, rehearsing under her breath the words you say to the dead when you disturb them — the old courtesy, the guild courtesy, the one Aldous had taught her for opening sealed rooms:

*We come not to take but to carry. Forgive the shovel. Keep the song.*

Behind her, softly, in a voice that was a child's and much older than any child, Wren began to hum the binding hymn — badly, off-key, meaning every note.
