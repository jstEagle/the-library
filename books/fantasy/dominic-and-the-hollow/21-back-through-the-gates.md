## 21. Back Through the Gates

The road into Vess had three kinds of travelers on it these days: soldiers, smugglers, and pilgrims. Lior had chosen to be the third kind because it was the only one that didn't require lying to a checkpoint captain — only dressing like someone who would.

"Pilgrims," Ivet said, adjusting the rope belt of his borrowed robe for the fourth time in an hour. "We're pilgrims."

"We're pilgrims," Wren agreed solemnly. He had a bowl in his hands and a smudge of ash on his cheek that Lior had put there herself, and he was carrying the performance with the terrifying commitment of a nine-year-old who has discovered theater.

"You don't have to say it every time someone says it," Lior told him.

"I'm practicing. Ivet says if you practice a lie enough it stops feeling like one."

"Ivet," Lior said, "is a cartographer, not a philosopher, and should stop teaching children things."

Ivet smiled the small, crooked smile he saved for moments when he'd been caught enjoying himself. "It's true, though. Ask any priest. Half of faith is repetition." He squinted down the road at the walls of Vess, gray in the morning haze, and his smile faded the way it always did when the city came into view. "There she is. The old girl."

"The old girl is under martial law," Lior said.

"All the more reason to visit. She's lonely."

They joined the pilgrim road at the shrine of Saint Ovel, where a hundred-odd travelers were queued in the mud, murmuring prayers they mostly seemed to be making up as they went. That was the thing about the prayer-burns, Lior had learned on the road east: fear made believers faster than any sermon ever had. People who hadn't prayed in twenty years were praying now, carefully, quietly, the way you'd speak to a large dog you weren't sure was friendly.

She couldn't blame them. She'd felt the burns through her ribs all the way from Errow — faint hooks tugging behind her sternum whenever a prayer landed somewhere in Vess and something answered. Three days out, the tugs had been occasional. This morning, waiting in the queue, they came like rain on a tin roof.

*Someone's asking for rain,* she thought, and then felt it land, somewhere south of the river, and the answering pull behind her ribs like a stitch tearing loose.

"Stop doing that," she muttered at her own chest.

"Doing what?" Wren asked.

"Nothing. Ash check." She rubbed more soot onto his cheek before he could argue. Under the grime he looked like any other child of the road — which was the point. A boy who recited guild oaths in his sleep drew exactly the wrong kind of attention in a city where the Inquisition still had checkpoints, even if the Inquisition itself was currently being eaten alive by its own contradictions. Sarel Vane's badge hung inside Lior's shirt on a cord, cold as a coin from a dead country. *Pass freely. In the name of the law.* Whose law, anymore, was anyone's guess.

* * *

The gate called Saltgate had two lines: one for carts, one for everyone else, and a third unofficial line of people being pulled aside and questioned against the wall. Lior counted four Inquisitors in the gray-and-ash livery, which was four more than she wanted to see, and none of them was Sarel Vane, which was either very good or very bad depending on what Sarel was doing instead of standing at gates.

"Name?" said the Inquisitor at the head of the line. He was young, with the chapped hands of someone who'd been a soldier before the temples fell and hadn't yet learned how to stand like anything else.

"Marla Fenwick," Lior said. The name belonged to a woman she'd once sold a fever-ward to in Corrow; Marla wouldn't mind. "Pilgrim. These are my brother and my son."

"Coming or going?"

"Coming home. We went east when the burning started." True enough. Everyone was coming home now; half the city had fled during the riots and half of those were trickling back because cities are like bad debts — you can't stop thinking about them.

"Burning started," the young Inquisitor repeated. His eyes flicked up, quick and tired. "You mean the scorch-marks. The hollows."

"I mean whatever folks call them."

"We call them arson." He said it flatly, the way a man repeats an order he's been given too many times to believe. Behind him, on the gatehouse wall, someone had painted over an older slogan — *THE PURGE PROTECTS* — and the new paint hadn't taken; the old words bled through like a bruise. "State your business in Vess."

"Family graves," Lior said, and touched the ash-smudge on Wren's head like a blessing. "And thanks to be given. My son was ill this winter. We made vows."

Something moved behind the young man's face — hunger, maybe, or its cousin, hope. "Healed, then?"

"By the grace of—"

"Move along." He waved them through before she could finish, and Lior understood, walking under the arch with her heart tapping at her teeth, that she had just watched a man of the Purge let a confessed miracle-witness pass because he wanted, desperately, for the story to be true.

The city swallowed them.

* * *

Vess in curfew was a city holding its breath. The streets were full and silent at once — market stalls open but unattended, shutters half-drawn, people moving in the quick, hunched way of folk who've learned that lingering attracts questions. And everywhere, everywhere, the burns.

Lior had seen the first ones as small things: a scorched handprint on a doorpost, a circle of ash where a beggar had slept. Now they were architecture. A whole tenement on Tanner's Row wore a black bloom across its face like a burst inkwell, three stories of stone gone glassy and smooth where some family had prayed hard enough, long enough, for something to come through. Children had chalked outlines around the smaller burns and played hopscotch between them until their mothers dragged them indoors. Someone had left flowers in the biggest hollow on the street — brown, wilted, replaced by fresher ones. An altar nobody had decided to build.

"It's spreading along the poor quarters," Ivet murmured, mapping with his eyes the way he mapped everything. "Follows the water, look. Riverswell, Tanner's Row, the Docks. Prayer runs downhill same as everything else."

"The rich pray too."

"The rich pray to things that answer politely. Or they pay other people to do their hoping." He nodded at a walled compound up the hill, where the merchant houses stood clean and unmarked, not one scorch on their whitewash. "Not one burn up there. Either nothing hears them, or—" he paused, choosing the gentler phrasing, "—or nothing needs to."

Lior felt each new hollow as they passed. Not pain, exactly. More like fingers plucking a string strung somewhere behind her navel, each answered prayer a note in a song she couldn't stop hearing. By the time they reached the shrine district she had a headache sitting behind her eyes like a second skull.

Wren had stopped walking.

He was standing in front of a burned doorway — just a doorway, the house around it ordinary, the scorch shaped almost like a hand pressed flat against the wood — and his lips were moving.

"Wren." Lior crouched to his height. "What did I tell you about listening?"

"That I shouldn't do it without telling you first." His voice was thin. "I wasn't listening. It was already talking. It says—" his brow furrowed, translating, "—it says *the well remembers*. And then it said something about counting. It's counting, Lior. It counts the burns like I count stairs."

"How many?"

"A lot." He looked up at her, and for a moment the ash-smudge made him look older, or she wished it did. "It's not angry. That's the worst part. It's patient. Like it knows we're coming back."

Ivet glanced up and down the street. Two women were watching from a window across the lane, and a watchman's lantern bobbed at the corner. "Inside talk," he said quietly. "Shrine first. We look like pilgrims until dark, and then we're gravediggers."

* * *

The shrine of Saint Ovel had become, in the space of a season, the most crowded building in Vess. Pilgrims packed its nave shoulder to shoulder, kneeling on the stones, whispering. No priest led them — the priests had all been Inquisition men, and the Inquisition had withdrawn from the shrines after the third riot — so the faithful had organized themselves into a low, constant murmur, like bees keeping a hive warm.

Lior found a place near the back wall with Wren between her and Ivet, and did what pilgrims do: she knelt, bowed her head, and listened.

The murmur resolved into prayers. That was the strange mercy of crowds — anonymity — and the strange horror of them too, because here, in this room, she could hear what the whole city was asking for.

*Mend the roof. Mend the roof before winter.*

*Let Tam find work, any work, he's not been right since the mills closed.*

*I didn't mean the bad thing I said to her. Let her know it.*

Small prayers. Human-sized. A city of exhausted people asking for bread, for forgiveness, for their children. Nothing hungry. Nothing grand. And every few breaths, one of them *landed* — Lior felt the tug, heard somewhere across the city a soft intake of breath as someone realized the fever had broken, the coin had turned up, the letter had come — and each landing pulled at the string behind her ribs, and each pull fed the patient thing underneath everything, the thing counting its burns like stairs.

"They don't know," she whispered to Ivet. "They think it's grace."

"It is grace," Ivet said. "That's the trap of it. You can't warn people off grace. They'll just pray harder to prove you wrong." He was watching a woman near the pillar, young, hollow-cheeked, rocking slightly as she whispered the same phrase over and over: *enough, please, just enough.* "My mother prayed like that," he added, softer. "In Errow, before it was erased. I used to think the gods didn't answer because we were unworthy. Turns out they didn't answer because they were locked up. I still haven't decided which is worse."

"Both," said Wren, with a child's certainty. "It's both."

"Hush," Lior said, but gently, because he was right.

They stayed an hour, invisible in the crowd, learning the city's heartbeat through its wishes. Then the bells rang the curfew-warning — two hours of light left — and the crowd began to drain away toward homes and hiding places, and Lior rose on knees that had gone numb, and felt, for the first time since entering the walls, the specific tug she'd been dreading.

It came from the west. From the direction of the Inquisition's mass grave outside the Wallows, where the convicted of the Purge lay in trenches under unmarked sod.

Something down there had noticed them coming.

* * *

They spent the curfew hours in a room above a dyer's shop that Ivet swore he'd used before, which meant either he had contacts in Vess or a past, and Lior had decided months ago not to ask which. The room smelled of mordant and old wool. Through the shutter-slats they watched the patrol lanterns swing in their slow arcs, and Lior laid out on the floorboards the things they'd need: two shovels bought from a scrap-dealer who asked no questions, sacking for the tools, lamp-oil, Wren's bundle, her own.

"Grave-robbing," Ivet said, testing a shovel handle against his palm. "When I was exiled, I made a list of professions I might fall into. Cartographer, ferryman, tutor. Grave-robber didn't make the list."

"You're not robbing. You're retrieving."

"From the dead. Without asking." He set the shovel down. "Does it bother you? Genuinely. You've been quiet since the shrine."

Lior considered the question honestly, because Ivet was the sort of person you owed honesty. "It bothers me that they're there at all. Unmarked. Convicted of praying." She turned the spade over in her hands. "Digging them up feels less like theft and more like... finishing a sentence somebody else started badly."

"And the god stirring down there? Wren said it noticed us."

"Wren says a lot of things the god thinks. I'm trying to learn which ones to worry about." She looked at the boy, asleep already in the corner, curled around his bundle like it was a sibling. Even asleep his lips moved sometimes, shaping syllables of a language that had been dead longer than he'd been alive. "Aldous would have hated this plan."

"Your master? Why?"

"Because Aldous loved rules the way other men love wine. He'd have said there's a proper channel for recovering guild property." She smiled despite herself. "Then he'd have done it exactly this way, at night, with a shovel, and sworn afterward he'd followed every rule that mattered."

Ivet laughed — a real laugh, quickly smothered under a blanket because sound carried in curfew — and for a moment the room was warm, and Lior let herself have the moment, hoarded it like a coin.

Then the bells rang midnight, and it was time.

* * *

The Wallows after dark were worse than the market districts, because the Wallows had stopped pretending. Every surface wore its burns openly now — doors, drains, the faces of saints chiseled off church corners a generation ago, all of it blooming with fresh scorch like frost spreading across a window. The poor quarters had stopped painting over them. Some households had begun sweeping their thresholds *around* the marks, leaving them intact, the way you'd leave a guest's chair alone.

Nobody was in the streets except patrols, and the patrols had learned something too: they walked wide of the burns. Lior watched a squad of six cross to the far side of a lane rather than pass through a scorched archway, and thought: *they know. Whatever the officers believe, the boots on the ground know something is answering.*

The grave field lay beyond the last houses, past a collapsed boundary wall, on low ground the city had always used for the people it preferred not to think about. No fence. No marker. Just a field with grass growing in long ridges, row upon row upon row, like plow-furrows made of the forgotten.

Lior stood at the edge with her shovel and found, to her surprise, that she couldn't move.

Hundreds of people. Guild members among them — she knew the records, knew the names, knew that somewhere under this grass lay the second key sewn into the shroud of a man named Ellery Vance, who had taught her the binding hymn's second verse and laughed at her terrible handwriting. She had told herself the digging would be simple. It was not simple. It was standing on top of a congregation.

"Lior," Ivet said quietly.

"I know."

"He'd have liked the hymn sung first. If he was anything like you say."

So she sang it — the binding hymn, first verse and second, voice low, barely more than breath, the words in Hollowtongue rolling out over the furrowed grass. Wren joined in on the second verse without being asked, pitch-perfect, and the two voices braided together in the dark, and Lior felt the string behind her ribs go still — the whole city's tugging paused, as if the thing beneath everything had stopped counting to listen.

When the verse ended, the silence had a different texture. Attentive.

"Well," Ivet said. "Either that was beautiful or we've just knocked on the door."

"Both," said Wren.

They dug.

The ground gave easily, too easily, soil turned and re-turned by other diggers before them — mourners, probably, come secretly over the years, because the convicted had families too and families don't stop visiting just because the state forbids markers. Lior found proof of it two feet down: a small clay charm-bead, guild-work, cheap and cracked, buried deliberately beside a skull. Someone had given their dead a luck-knot for the road.

Her throat tightened. She put the bead in her pocket. It was still warm.

* * *

They found Ellery Vance's trench by the record-numbers Ivet had copied from a bribed registrar — trench nine, plot forty-one, *convicted: heretical practice, sentence: ash and earth* — and by the time the shovels struck cloth, the sky in the east had gone the color of dirty pewter, and Lior's arms were shaking, and Wren had gone very quiet in a way that made the hair on her neck rise.

"It's louder here," he whispered. "Underneath. It's not counting anymore."

"What's it doing?"

"Waiting." He hugged his bundle. "It says you brought the key. It says it can *hear* the key."

Lior lifted the rotted shroud with hands that wanted to tremble and didn't let them. Beneath the cloth, folded against bones that had once played a fiddle badly and sung worse, was a shape wrapped in oilcloth — and through the oilcloth, faint as a heartbeat, came a warmth that had no business surviving eleven years in cold ground.

The key. Warm as a living thing. Warm as the charm-bead in her pocket, warm as every answered prayer in a starving city.

And from the direction of Vess, across the dark fields, a bell began to ring — not the curfew bell, not any bell with a schedule. Alarm bells, several of them, overlapping, urgent, and beneath them, rising like smoke, a sound Lior took a moment to identify because she'd never heard it from a whole city at once.

Singing. Hundreds of voices, ragged and desperate and joyful, pouring out of the poor quarters into the night.

Ivet straightened, shovel in hand, listening. "That's not a riot," he said slowly. "Riots don't harmonize."

Wren's eyes had gone wide and bright in the dark. "It's the well," he breathed. "Lior — it says *come to the well*. It says everyone's coming to the well."

Lior wrapped the key against her chest, next to Sarel Vane's badge, two relics of two dead orders warming each other, and looked east toward the ringing, singing city she had grown up in and no longer recognized.

"Then we'd better get there," she said, "before someone decides what all those people are guilty of."
