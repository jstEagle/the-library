## 22. Graveyard of the Convicted

Ivet had spent his whole life believing that the worst thing a mapmaker could do was draw a line in the wrong place. He had been wrong about many things since leaving Vess — he was wrong about the road east, wrong about the innkeeper at Dossel Ford who had sworn there were no pilgrims on the roads (there were hundreds), and wrong, most grievously, about how heavy a shovel could feel when you knew what you were digging toward.

The convict graveyard lay outside the eastern wall of Vess, past the tanneries, where the city's smell gave way to something older and more honest: turned earth and river mud. It was not a cemetery in any sense Ivet understood. There were no stones, no rows, no names. Just a long field of low grass with a rusted iron fence around it that someone had once meant to be imposing and had instead become an apology.

"They buried them standing up," Lior said.

She stood at the fence with her hands through the bars, which seemed to Ivet both sentimental and tactically foolish, but he had learned in three weeks of travel that Lior Eastwick did not do things tactically when grief was involved. She did them honestly, and honesty was louder.

"Standing up?" Wren asked. The boy crouched at the gate, examining the lock with the particular intensity of a nine-year-old who had recently discovered that locks were just puzzles that had given up.

"Convicts," Lior said. "No coffin. No marker. They dig one trench, stand them in it, fill it in. Saves space." Her voice was flat in the way Ivet had come to dread — flat the way ice is flat before you hear it crack. "Saves dignity too, presumably. Two savings for the price of none."

Ossa said nothing. She had said very little since they'd left Errow, and what little she said came out in the tone of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. She stood apart from the fence, hood up despite the mild evening, looking at the field the way Ivet imagined a surgeon looks at a wound she'd stitched badly thirty years ago.

"You were here," Ivet said to her. Not a question. He'd gotten better at those — or worse, depending on whom you asked. "During the Purge."

"I filled in trenches," Ossa said. "It was considered penance. Cleansing work." A pause. "We called it cleansing because we were very good at calling things what they weren't. It was practically a guild skill."

The humor landed the way Ossa's humor always landed — like a coin dropped down a well; you heard it, but you never found out how deep it went.

* * *

They waited for full dark, because even a dissolved province has patrols, and Vess under martial law had patrols the way a dog has fleas. Ivet passed the time the only way he knew how: drawing. He sat against the fence with his knees up and sketched the graveyard from memory of the shape of it, then began annotating — trench lines estimated from the subtle swells in the grass, the drainage ditch, the tanner's wall, the patrol road.

"What are you doing?" Lior asked, sitting beside him. In the dark her face was mostly eyes.

"Mapping it."

"It's a graveyard, Ivet."

"Everything is mappable. That's rather my entire tragedy." He tapped the sketch. "If the executed were buried standing, in sequence, the records would have gone oldest at the north end. Bureaucracies are predictable. They're the one force in nature more reliable than gravity." He looked up. "Your guildsman — the one with the key. When was he executed?"

Lior was quiet a moment. "Late. Near the end. Aldous spoke of him once. Brannic Hale. He held the second key and refused to give it up even on the scaffold. The Inquisitors made a lesson of it."

"Then he'll be near the south end," Ivet said. "The end they finished last. And if I'm wrong, we will have desecrated several innocent graves for nothing, which — " he caught himself, winced, " — poor phrasing. We'll have disturbed several graves of people who were guilty of nothing but being inconvenient, which is somehow worse, and I apologize to all of them in advance."

Wren giggled. Then stopped, because Wren had been stopping his laughter lately, mid-sound, as though something behind his ribs reached up and pinched it quiet. He pressed two fingers to his sternum and stared south across the field.

"He's there," Wren said. His voice came out doubled, faintly, the way a bell sounds doubled if you strike it near another bell. "He says — *he* says — south corner. Under the crooked post."

Ivet felt the hair rise on his arms. Three weeks of traveling with the boy and he still hadn't acclimated to the moments when Wren's mouth got borrowed. He suspected he never would. He suspected that was healthy.

"The dead are chatty tonight," Ossa observed, coming over. "That's either good news or very bad news."

"When is it ever good news?" Lior asked.

"Never, in my experience. But I like to keep the categories current."

* * *

The fence had a gap in the southeast corner where the iron had rusted through, and they went through it single file, and Ivet — who had crossed borders illegally, forged charters, and once talked his way out of a Vesser customs house by claiming to be a tax collector's ghost — found his hands shaking anyway.

Because this was different. This wasn't lying to the living. This was disturbing the dead, and the dead of this particular field hadn't been disturbed by anyone in twenty years, because no one had wanted anything from them. That was the whole horror of a convict grave, Ivet thought, driving the first shovel into the earth: not that it was cruel, but that it was *efficient*. Somewhere in the Inquisition's ledgers there was probably a column for burial costs, and somewhere above that column a clerk had felt pleased at the savings.

"We should say something," Wren whispered.

"Yes," Lior said.

"Do you know something? To say?"

Lior knelt at the head of the trench they'd opened — four feet down already, the soil coming up dark and soft, easier than Ivet expected, as if the ground here had never been packed properly, as if even the earth had declined to commit fully to the Inquisition's arrangements.

"There's a hymn," she said. "Guild burial rite. We sang it for our own dead, in the hall, before—" She stopped. Before the Purge. Before the sealed door. Before everything. "Aldous taught me the first verse. I never learned the rest."

"Sing the first verse," Wren said. "He can hold the rest. He remembers all of it."

And so Lior Eastwick, kneeling in a field of unmarked graves outside the city that had outlawed her life's inheritance, sang six lines of Old Hollowtongue in a voice that cracked twice and steadied on the third try, and Ivet, holding the shovel like a man holding something he'd be questioned about later, found that his eyes had gone hot and unfocused, and that the words — which he didn't understand, which meant nothing to him in any dictionary sense — meant everything anyway, the way maps mean things to people who can't read them.

Ossa sang too. Low, from memory, the harmony line. Her voice was old and rough and absolutely certain, and hearing it, Ivet finally understood what she had been for twenty years inside the Inquisition: not hidden. *Buried.* Standing upright in a trench of her own, waiting.

* * *

They hit wood at five feet.

Not a coffin — a plank, crude, laid over the body lengthwise, and beneath it the soil was different, darker, and Ivet climbed down into the trench himself because someone had to and because Lior's hands had started trembling and because, frankly, he was the tallest and the trench was narrow and he'd spent forty years squeezing into archives and map rooms and one memorable smuggler's crawlspace, and if his life had prepared him for anything it was lying flat in uncomfortable dark places looking for truth.

"Brannic Hale," he murmured, brushing soil from the shape below. The body had gone to bone and leather. Boots still on. Hands folded — folded, deliberately, by whoever had laid him here, which meant someone had cared enough to arrange him and hadn't dared to do more. Around the neck, on a rotted cord: a shape.

Ivet lifted it free.

The key was iron, old iron, black with age except where his thumb wiped it clean — and along its shaft ran a script he recognized without being able to read it, because he had spent the last month staring at Tessin's map fragment, and the same script crawled along the fragment's border.

"Got it," he said, and his voice came out strange, and he cleared his throat and said it again, normal, human: "Got it. Hand me up."

Lior's hand came down into the dark and took the key, and the moment her skin touched it she made a small sound — not pain, exactly. Recognition. Like touching a doorknob in your childhood home after decades away.

* * *

It was Wren who noticed the charms.

The boy had lain down at the trench edge, chin on his folded arms, peering down into the hole with the lamp — because of course he had; children cannot be kept from holes, it is one of nature's fixed laws — and suddenly he went rigid.

"Ivet," he said. "Look at their hands."

Ivet looked. At first he thought it was bones — the finger bones of the other bodies in the row, visible where the plank had shifted. But no. Wrapped around the phalanges, tucked into palms, threaded through ribs: small objects. Knots of cord. Bits of stamped tin. Little sewn pouches the size of thumbnails.

Charms.

Luck-knots. Fever-wards. Cheap tin sigils stamped with the Hollow Guild's mark — the mark Lior kept hidden under her sleeve, the mark that had been worth hanging for twenty years ago and was worth hanging for now.

"They had charms," Ivet said stupidly. "Why would they have—"

"Because they were ours," Lior said above him. Her voice had gone very quiet. "The guild sold charms to everyone. The poor bought them because the poor buy hope wherever it's cheapest. And when the Inquisition arrested them — when they marched them out here — someone let them keep them. Or they hid them. In their fists. In their mouths, maybe." A breath. "They died holding us, Ivet. They died holding the guild in their hands, and nobody ever dug deep enough to take it away from them."

Ivet lay in the trench among the dead of the Purge and did not move for a long time. Above him the night sky hung over Vess, starless with the city's smoke, and below him the dead kept their small hopes wrapped in their fingers, and he thought: *I have mapped coastlines that moved less than my understanding of this country.*

Then he noticed the warmth.

At first he blamed the lamp, the close air, his own exertion. But he lifted one luck-knot — gently, gently, apologizing under his breath in three languages — and it was warm. Not body-warm. Not lamp-warm. Warm the way a stone is warm that has been in someone's pocket all day. Warm the way a thing is warm that is being *held*.

"Wren," he said. "Don't touch anything else. Get Lior. Get Ossa. Now, please."

* * *

They gathered at the trench edge, and Ossa lowered herself down beside him — old bones, deliberate movements — and picked up a fever-ward between two fingers, and held it, and closed her eyes.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you *fools*. You magnificent, doomed fools."

"What?" Lior demanded. "What is it?"

"They've been praying." Ossa opened her eyes. "Down here. For twenty years. Every one of them, in the dark, with their little charms in their hands — praying. Not to be freed. Not for revenge. Listen to me, girl, because this matters." She gripped Ivet's arm hard enough to bruise. "They prayed for the ones they left behind. For their children. For the neighbors who informed on them. For the Inquisitors themselves, some of them, I can feel it — there's a prayer here for an Inquisitor, a specific one, asking that his own children be kept well. Twenty years of answered-nothing prayers, soaked into these charms, stored up like rain in a cistern."

"And now?" Ivet asked, though he already knew. He had known since the warmth. He was a cartographer; he understood what happens when you breach a dam, even a small one, even a grave-sized one.

"Now we've broken the seal," Ossa said. "Grave-soil keeps things. That's half of what consecration is, anywhere, in any faith — it's not blessing, it's *containment*. And this field was never consecrated, but it was forgotten, and forgetting holds almost as well." She looked up, out of the trench, across the dark field, and Ivet followed her gaze and felt his stomach drop.

The grass was moving.

Not wind. There was no wind. But a slow shiver ran across the graveyard, row by row, trench by trench, from the north end to the south, like a hand smoothing a bedsheet — like something enormous turning over in its sleep and pulling the covers closer, except the covers were the ground itself and the sleeper was underneath all of it, underneath Vess, underneath everything, and it had just been handed twenty years of the gentlest, most stubborn, most forgiving prayers anyone had ever bothered to save.

Every charm in every fist in that field went warm at once. Ivet felt it through the soles of his boots. The whole graveyard, warming, like a banked fire remembering it was fire.

"It's not angry," Wren said. The boy stood at the trench edge with his arms wrapped around himself, and his voice was his own again, small and terrified and awed. "It's — it's *reading* them. It's reading all the prayers. Nobody ever read them before. Nobody ever—" His face crumpled. "It's crying. Gods can cry. Why can gods cry?"

"Because we made them in our image and then locked them in a cellar," Ossa said grimly, hauling herself up the trench wall. "Out. All of you, out, now, quietly. Fill it in as best we can. We are leaving."

"The key—" Lior began.

"Is in your hand, yes, and it cost us a hole in the world to get it, and now we run, because in about ten minutes every prayer-burn in the city of Vess is going to flare at once, and the Inquisition is going to come looking for whatever woke their prisoners' god, and I would prefer very much that they find empty ground and a tidy trench."

They filled it in. It took too long and not long enough. Ivet worked with his sleeves rolled and his heart hammering and his apology running on a loop — *forgive me, forgive me, forgive me, we needed what you kept, and we should have come sooner, and we should have come for you and not just for what you carried* — and when the last spade of soil went down, Lior knelt and pressed her palm flat against the fresh earth, over where folded hands held their small warm hopes, and said nothing at all, which Ivet thought was probably the correct liturgy.

* * *

They were halfway back through the gap in the fence when the sky over Vess lit up.

Not fire. Light — a soft gold rising from a hundred places at once, from the poor quarters, from the temple district, from the walls themselves, and Ivet realized he was seeing the prayer-burns, the scorched hollows that had pocked the city for weeks, all of them kindling together like lamps at festival. From the tannery road they could hear the first bells, then shouting, then the deep confused roar of a city waking to find its nightmares rearranged.

And underneath it all, faint, threading through the noise like a root through a wall, a sound Ivet would spend the rest of his life failing to describe accurately on paper: a voice, vast and cracked and gentle, saying — in Old Hollowtongue, in the tongue of the dead, in the language of the buried —

a name.

Lior froze with her hand on the broken fence. Wren's mouth moved with the syllables, translating without wanting to, tears running freely down his face.

"It says *thank you*," the boy whispered. "It says thank you, and then—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Then it says someone else's name. Someone it's been waiting to ask for. Someone still alive."

"Who?" Lior demanded. "Whose name?"

Wren turned and looked east, toward Errow, toward the Door, and his eyes were not entirely his own, and they were frightened.

"It says *come home*," he said. "It says it to all of us. But it says it loudest to the priestess."

Behind them, across the burning-lit fields, the gates of Vess opened, and riders came out.
