## 23. Aldous Speaks Plainly

The key was still cold in Lior's pocket when they made camp in the ruined granary outside Vess's western wall. It had been colder underground, pressed against the ribs of a man who had died for a lie — though she hadn't known it was a lie yet, and wouldn't until the boy opened his mouth.

Wren sat cross-legged on an overturned grain bin, swinging his feet the way eight-year-olds do when they are trying very hard to look like they are not afraid. Ivet had built a small fire from broken pallet boards, and Ossa — who had joined them two days ago with all the warmth of a filing cabinet learning to walk — was grinding something medicinal-smelling in a mortar, mostly, Lior suspected, to avoid conversation.

Lior knelt across from Wren and set her hands on her knees.

"You know why we're here," she said gently.

The boy nodded. Then he didn't nod. His chin lifted, his shoulders squared, and when he spoke again his voice was not a child's voice. It was dry as old paper and warm as a hearth remembered, and it went through Lior like a splinter finding its old wound.

"Hello, little wren of mine."

"Aldous." Her throat closed around the name. She had heard it through sealed doors and through this boy's dreams, always half-glimpsed, always interrupted. Tonight she had asked him to speak plainly, and plainly he would, because that was what Aldous did once he decided a thing needed saying: he said it all the way through, like pulling a stitch.

"You've been circling the question for three days," Aldous said, through Wren's mouth. "You keep asking me about the Door, about the keys, about where to dig. Clever questions. Practical questions. You learned that from me, which is one of the few things I don't regret teaching you." He paused. "But you haven't asked me the real question."

Lior looked at the fire. "I'm asking it now."

"Then ask."

She made herself look up. Made herself see her master's cadence sitting inside a child's face, the way a hand fits inside a glove that is far too small for it.

"Why did the guild seal the gods?"

* * *

For a long moment there was only the crackle of pallet-wood and the scrape of Ossa's mortar, which had gone very still.

"I wondered when you'd get there," Aldous said. "I hoped you'd get there angry. Anger keeps better than grief."

"I'm not angry yet."

"You will be. Sit down properly. This is a long confession and you'll want your legs under you."

Lior sat. Ivet quietly moved his cartography satchel off the bench beside her and settled next to it, elbows on knees, the look of a man who intended to take notes with his eyes if not his pen. Even Ossa stopped grinding.

"The story they taught you," Aldous began, "is the story every guild apprentice got, in every city, for three hundred years. The gods grew proud. The gods grew hungry. Mortals prayed themselves hollow feeding them, so the wise founders of the Hollow Guild bound the old powers behind a Door and gave magic back to the people, free and small and safe. Charms instead of miracles. Luck-knots instead of blood sacrifice." He smiled with Wren's mouth, and it was a terrible thing to watch, because it was Aldous's exact smile, the one he'd used over supper when he was about to ruin someone's comfortable evening. "It's a good story. I believed it for forty years. I taught it for twenty-five. I told it to you on your first night in the guildhall, do you remember? You were nine and you'd walked forty miles with a stolen apple in each pocket."

"I remember," Lior said. "You took the apples."

"I took *one* apple. The other was your dinner. I wasn't a monster." He let the smile fade. "Here is the true story, then. As plain as I can make it, because you asked, and because I have been dead long enough to run out of reasons to be a coward about it.

"The guild was founded by thieves."

Ossa's mortar clinked down onto stone.

"Not metaphorically," Aldous went on. "Literally. The first guildsmen were hedge-witches and temple-thieves and one disgraced priestess of the River Mother, and what they discovered — what they spent their whole lives discovering — was that prayer has weight. Every prayer whispered to a god adds a grain to that god's store. And gods, being vast, never counted their grains. They let the prayers pool at their feet like loose coin and never noticed when a few coins went missing.

"So the founders stole them. A pinch here. A handful there. Siphoned divinity out of the gods' own hoards and kept it in vessels — the first keys weren't keys, Lior, they were purses. That's why there are three. Three purses, split between three hands so no single thief could run off with the whole take."

Lior's hands had gone cold despite the fire. "The keys hold *prayer*?"

"The keys hold what prayer becomes. Which is power. Which is everything." He shifted Wren's shoulders, and the boy's face flickered — for half a heartbeat he looked simply like a tired child — before the old voice settled back in. "And for a generation it worked beautifully. The guild grew rich in secret. We could heal any wound, raise any harvest, quiet any storm — quietly, privately, for paying clients, while the public got our cast-off charms and thought themselves lucky to have them. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Every fever-ward you ever sold in that market was a crumb swept off a table the guild had robbed."

"And the Door?" Lior's voice came out flat. "The great sealing? The sacrifice?"

"The Door was the vault door." He said it simply, without decoration, which was somehow worse. "When the gods finally noticed their missing stores — and they did notice, eventually; hunger makes even gods attentive — the guild couldn't give the theft back. You can't unspend power, little wren. So they did what thieves do when the watch comes up the stairs. They barricaded the room and told everyone outside that they'd caught a monster."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a night bird called twice and thought better of it.

"They sealed the gods behind the Door," Aldous said, "not to protect the world from them. To protect the theft from them. And the world — the world got the story. The world got heroes. The world got a guild of humble charm-sellers keeping a lonely vigil against ancient hunger, and it loved us for it, and we let it love us, because love is excellent cover."

Lior found she was gripping her own wrists hard enough to leave marks. "The Purge—"

"Ah." He exhaled through Wren, a long, careful breath. "Yes. The Purge. Here is where my part gets ugly, so pay attention, because I won't say it twice.

"When the Inquisition rose — and they rose because some of the gods' surviving servants figured out what the guild had done and told the truth to anyone who'd listen — the guild faced a choice. Confess. Return what remained of the stolen power. Open the vault and let the creditors in." He shook Wren's head slowly. "We chose the vault. We locked it tighter. And when the Inquisition started burning hedge-witches by the dozen looking for us, we let them. We handed over names, Lior. Not all of us — but enough. Small practitioners, village cunning-folk, people whose only crime was knowing a rain-song. We fed them to the fire so the Inquisition would stop digging toward the Door. We sacrificed the innocent to protect the guilty, and called it strategy, and slept soundly, most of us."

"Maren Cole," Lior said.

"Maren Cole was the worst of us and also the most honest, which is a distinction I'll let you chew on later. But she wasn't alone. I signed the list myself." His voice didn't waver. Aldous never wavered; it was one of the things she'd loved him for, and it curdled now. "Aldous Fenwick, last Master of the Hollow Guild, protector of the small folk, put thirty-one names on an Inquisitor's desk to buy the guild another decade. Twenty-nine of those people burned."

The number landed in the granary like a dropped anvil. Ivet had stopped pretending to take notes; his pen was just resting on the page, making a slow ink blot. Ossa stared into the mortar as though the answer might be ground out of it.

"I stayed near the Door afterward," Aldous continued, quieter. "Someone had to. Not out of nobility — out of accounting. A thief who guards his loot isn't repentant, he's just careful. I told myself I was doing penance. I told myself the vigil mattered. And maybe some of that was even true. But when the god under Vess started whispering to me through the seal — and it did whisper, for years, it knows every thief by name — I never once considered opening the Door and giving it all back. Not once. I planned how to hold it. I trained you to hold it after me." He looked at her, and Wren's brown eyes were suddenly, unbearably direct. "I raised you to be the lock on my vault, little wren. That was your inheritance. That was the whole of it."

* * *

Lior stood up because sitting felt like agreeing, and walked to the granary's broken wall, and looked out at the dark fields between them and the distant smudge of Vess's lights.

Behind her, the body of a child said, in her dead master's voice: "Say something cruel. You'll feel better."

"Don't tell me how to feel."

"There she is." Warmth in the old voice. Genuine warmth, damn him. "I did teach you that too."

She turned around. "Why now?" she demanded. "Why tell me this *now*, tonight, after weeks of riddles? If you wanted me to open the Door — if that was the plan all along — why confess the theft first? You could have let me walk in there believing the pretty story. Believing in *you*. I would have done anything you asked if I still believed in you."

"Yes," Aldous said. "That's precisely why I couldn't."

He let that sit. Wren's feet had stopped swinging; the boy's hands lay flat on his knees, patient, the way Aldous used to fold his own hands when he was delivering a lesson he expected to hurt.

"Listen to me carefully, because this is the last piece and it matters more than all the rest. The plan failed. Understand? The theft failed. The vault failed. I failed. The god under Vess has been drinking answered prayers for months now — every fever broken, every luck-knot that worked, every burn in the walls — and it is waking up *starving*, because stolen power doesn't nourish, it only appetizes. When it wakes fully, it won't stay politely behind our Door. Doors are for things that respect boundaries, and we taught this one nothing but appetite for three hundred years." He paused. "There is exactly one way this ends well, and it requires the one thing the guild never once tried in its entire rotten history."

Lior crossed her arms. "Which is?"

"Giving it back. All of it. Freely." He said it lightly, almost cheerfully, the way he announced unpleasant chores. "Not a negotiated settlement. Not a guarded trickle. Everything the guild hoarded — every vessel, every key, every scrap of hoarded divinity, including whatever's left in the heir's own blood — returned to the god that it was stolen from. Voluntarily. With an apology, if you can manage one. Gods, it turns out, are like creditors: what ruins them isn't the debt, it's the insult."

"That would end the guild," Lior said. "All of it. The charms, the craft, the—" She stopped. Heard herself. "The power."

"Yes," Aldous said. "Every bit. You'd be an ordinary woman in a country full of ordinary women, and the guild would be a story children learn wrong, and you would carry nothing out of it but the name." He tilted Wren's head. "I want you to notice, right now, that your first instinct was to mourn the power and not the people we burned. That's mine too. That's the inheritance working as designed. Grieve it, little wren. Then put it down."

The silence stretched. Out in the dark, Vess glowed against the sky like a banked coal, and somewhere beneath it — or beneath Errow, or beneath wherever the Door truly stood — something vast turned over in a sleep that was ending.

"I hate you a little right now," Lior said at last.

"Good. Hate is honest. Love with a dead man is mostly memory wearing his coat." Aldous softened. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much — the apples were good apples. You picked well. Of everything I stole, teaching you was the one thing I took fair."

"Master." Her voice cracked on the word and she let it. "If I give it all back — if I do this — what happens to *you*? To the voice? To any of it?"

Wren's face went very still. When Aldous answered, the dry warmth had gone out of him, and what remained was older and tireder than she had ever heard him.

"A thief who returns the take doesn't get to keep the fence he hid behind. When the inheritance goes home, I go with it. All of me. Whatever I am now, I am made of guild-power, and guild-power is what you'll be unmaking." He smiled — the real smile this time, small and crooked, the one from the first night, the apple night. "So ask your questions while you can. I'm told the dead get one good conversation, and I intend to spend mine being useful. Now — the third key. Ossa has it, yes? Good. Then listen closely, all of you, because tomorrow you go back through those gates, and the Inquisition's graveyard is not a forgiving place to dig."

Lior wiped her face with her sleeve, the way she hadn't allowed herself to do since the night market, and sat back down by the fire.

"One question first," she said. "Just one."

"Ask."

"The boy. When you're done with him — when you're done borrowing him — does he come back whole?"

Wren blinked, and for a moment he was only Wren again, eight years old and exhausted, looking at her with his own eyes. "Mostly," the child said, in his own voice, small and hoarse. "He says mostly. He says... he says sorry a lot, actually. More than you'd think."

Lior laughed — a wet, cracked, surprised laugh — and reached over and ruffled the boy's hair, and Wren let her, leaning into it like a cat.

Then the old voice returned, brisk as a schoolmaster ringing the bell: "Right. Graves. Listen carefully, because the convict field lies east of the ash-pits, and the wardens change shift at—"

The words were still coming when the light reached them: a bobbing lantern-glow beyond the granary wall, two lanterns, three, moving along the road from Vess with the unhurried, terrible discipline of people who already knew exactly where they were going.

Ossa snuffed the fire with one practiced motion. In the sudden dark, Lior's hand found the cold shape of the key in her pocket, and Wren's small fingers found hers.

"They've followed the digging," Aldous murmured, quick and low. "Little wren — whoever's out there — *do not let them take the boy.*"
