## 24. Sarel Catches Them

Sarel Vane had been standing behind the same leaning gravestone for the better part of an hour, and her left boot had finally given up all pretense of waterproofness. The Graveyard of the Convicted sat in a shallow bowl east of the city walls, where the ground was too poor to farm and too shameful to build on, and the rain came off the hills sideways and found every seam a woman had.

She did not mind, particularly. She had spent the last three days doing arithmetic that no amount of wet feet could make worse.

The arithmetic went like this: she had watched four children dig up the executed dead. She had seen them kneel in the mud among the graves of people her own order had burned, and she had seen what they pulled out of the earth — small charms, hundreds of them, knotted cord and folded paper and cheap tin, buried by grieving hands in defiance of every law she had sworn to uphold — and those charms had been *warm*. Not metaphorically. She had held one. It had sat in her palm like a sparrow's heart, beating heat against her glove, while the boy who spoke Hollowtongue wept over the grave of a man he had never met.

And then there was the ledger. She had read it twice now, once by candlelight like a sensible person and once at noon in the archive stairwell like a woman losing her grip, and both times it said the same thing. *The Purge did not destroy the old gods. The guild sealed them. The Inquisition has been standing guard on a prison for two hundred years and calling it a victory.*

Her whole career was a wall, and someone had shown her the wall was painted on.

So when the voices came toward the graveyard wall — low, hurried, one of them a child's — Sarel did not draw her iron. She stood up slowly, so as not to startle anyone into running, and her knee cracked like a green branch, and everyone started anyway.

* * *

There were four of them silhouetted against the bruised evening sky: the Eastwick girl, mud to her elbows; the exiled cartographer, Ivet, clutching a shovel like a man who had never been introduced to one before tonight; the boy, Wren, pale and luminous-eyed; and an old woman Sarel did not know, upright as a fencepost, who looked at Sarel's badge and said, "Well. The prison has come to inspect the prisoners."

"Ossa," the Eastwick girl said, in a warning tone.

"What? I'm old. I've earned the right to be rude to uniformed men and women." The old woman peered closer. "You're Vane. You signed the destruction orders for the Ash Street collection. I catalogued what you burned. Some of it was irreplaceable."

"Some of it was fever-wards sold to slum children at extortionate prices," Sarel said.

"And some of it was two hundred years of memory," Ossa said, "burned at extortionate prices, if you follow me."

Sarel did follow her. That was rather the problem.

Lior Eastwick had shifted, subtly, until she stood between Wren and the wall — between the child and the Inquisitor — and Sarel found herself looking at a young woman with a smuggler's posture and a guild sigil half-hidden under a frayed sleeve, and thinking: *she is protecting him from me, and she has every reason to, and I am the one holding the sword.*

That image did more damage to her than any argument had. She put the thought away carefully, the way you pocket something breakable, and addressed the group.

"You've dug in the convict graves," she said. "That's sacrilege under statute nine. You've removed objects from Inquisition custody — yes, custody; the dead in this ground belong to the Order, which is a sentence I have just heard aloud for the first time and found wanting." She heard Ivet make a small involuntary sound, something between a laugh and a swallow. "You are, all of you, guilty of crimes carrying penalties I do not wish to think about. And I have been standing behind that stone listening to you talk about keys."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Lior's hand had found Wren's shoulder.

"How much," Lior said at last, "did you hear?"

"The word 'key' eleven times. The word 'Door' six. The phrase 'Aldous would have wanted' twice, spoken in a child's voice, which I will be thinking about for the rest of my life." Sarel stepped over the low wall, deliberately unhurried, and set her lantern down on the grass so its light fell upward and made them all look taller than they felt. "I also heard enough, these past weeks, to know that arresting you would be the easiest thing I ever do and the first wrong thing."

* * *

They did not believe her. Of course they didn't. Sarel had spent years cultivating a reputation — the market-burner, the charm-crusher, the woman whose questions came before the fire did — and reputations do not dissolve because the woman wearing one has had a difficult week.

"Sit," she said, and when nobody moved: "Stand, then. But listen. And you—" she pointed at Ossa— "stop looking at me like that. Whatever you were going to say, I've already read the sealed archive, so you'll have to be shocking in some entirely new way."

Ossa's eyebrows rose a fraction, which Sarel would later learn was the equivalent of another woman fainting.

"You read the archive," Ossa said. "Against orders."

"I opened a sealed record without authorization, transcribed portions of it, and then failed to file the transcription. As far as the Order is concerned, I am currently investigating a routine smuggling ring and making disappointingly slow progress." She paused. "I have never in my life made slow progress. My superiors will find it suspicious eventually. I have perhaps a month."

Lior had relaxed by perhaps one degree. Sarel could measure it; reading fear was her trade. "Why?" Lior asked. "Why any of it? Why let us go?"

"Because I want the truth," Sarel said, "in writing. From each of you. What you know, what you've seen, what the Door is, what the god under this city is, what happened in the Purge — everything, in your own hands, signed. Not testimony extracted in a cell. Testimony given freely, by people who chose to give it." She looked around at their faces, lit from below by her own lantern. "Do you understand what I'm asking? I have spent fifteen years taking truth from people who feared me, and not one word of it was worth the candle. I don't want your confessions under threat anymore. I want them the way you'd give them to a friend. Because I have discovered, late and at considerable personal cost, that I have been on the side of the fire, and I would very much like to die having once been on the side of the light."

The cartographer, Ivet, cleared his throat. "That's a very dramatic speech."

"I've been rehearsing it behind that gravestone for an hour. There wasn't much else to do. My boots flooded."

"It was good," Ivet conceded. "The bit about dying was maybe—"

"Ivet," Lior said.

"—a touch much. But sincere. I map things for a living; I know true lines when I see them."

Wren had come out from behind Lior sometime during all this. He was studying Sarel with the unnerving directness of a child and something older underneath it, and when he spoke, his voice doubled for a moment — the boy's clear treble and beneath it a resonance like a bell struck in an empty room — and said, in Hollowtongue:

"She means it."

Then he blinked, and shivered, and was only a tired boy in a graveyard again. "She means it," he repeated, in his own voice, sounding surprised.

Everyone looked at Sarel. Sarel looked at the sky, because a woman can only be appraised by an oracle so long before she requires a moment.

"Well," she managed. "Good."

* * *

They sat, in the end. The mud was against it but the mud was against everything that night. Ossa produced, from some hidden recess of her ancient coat, a flask of plum brandy and passed it around with the air of a sacrament; even Wren got a thimbleful and a look that dared him to comment. Ivet drank most of his and then apologized to it.

Sarel laid her notebook on a flat stone and wrote as they talked, and the picture assembled itself out of their pieces the way a map does — she caught herself thinking in Ivet's terms now, which was its own small alarm. The Door beneath Errow. The three keys. The truth Ossa carried like a stone in her chest: that the Door fed the god, that it demanded a keeper on the far side, that Aldous Renn had died before taking up the post and left the debt to an apprentice who sold luck-knots in a night market.

"That was the part I couldn't reconcile," Sarel said, tapping her pen against the stone. "Your master. By every account I have, Aldous Renn was the finest kind of criminal — brilliant, careful, beloved. Why would he leave the burden to a girl of nineteen instead of taking it himself?"

Nobody answered. Lior was looking at the graves.

"Because he was afraid," Ossa said quietly. "He was the bravest coward I ever knew. He spent forty years telling himself he'd take up the post when the guild found another way. There was never another way. And then we informed on him—" her eyes flicked, briefly, to nothing and no one, but Sarel saw Lior go still— "and he died at the stake still promising himself next year."

The brandy went around again. This time Sarel took the largest share, and did not apologize to it.

* * *

It was full dark when they finished, and the rain had stopped, and the clouds had torn open here and there to show indifferent stars. Sarel closed her notebook. Four testimonies, signed — Lior's hand quick and slanted, Ivet's precise as a survey line, Wren's enormous letters with a small drawn door beside the name, and Ossa's cramped and furious script, as though the pen resented every word.

"There," Sarel said. "Now it exists outside my head. If they burn me, it survives. If they bury it, I've hidden copies. If you fail—" she looked at Lior— "someone will at least know what was attempted, and why, and that an Inquisitor believed you."

"Why?" Lior asked again. She kept coming back to it, turning it over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. "You gave up your career tonight. For a story."

"For the truth, in writing. As negotiated." Sarel reached up to her collar. Her badge hung there on its chain — worn silver, stamped with the eye-and-flame, the thing that had opened every door in Vess for fifteen years and closed quite a few permanently. It came free more easily than she expected. Fifteen years, and one tug.

She held it out to Lior.

"Curfew starts at dusk bell now; martial law since the riots. Every gate, every checkpoint, every patrol answers to this. Show it and they'll wave you through and salute, probably — I'm told I frighten recruits." A gust moved through the graveyard, setting the dry grass whispering like a congregation. "Get to Errow. Open your Door or close it forever, whichever turns out to be right. And when someone eventually asks how you passed the gates of a city under martial law, tell them the truth. Tell them the woman who burned your charms handed you her own name on a chain. Let it cost me what it cost."

Lior didn't take it at first. She looked at the badge the way you look at a snake that has offered to be a belt.

"If this is a trap," she said.

"Then it's an extraordinarily elaborate one, and I've wasted a very good notebook on it."

"If you follow us—"

"I won't follow you." Sarel stepped closer and pressed the chain into Lior's muddy palm and closed the girl's fingers over it with her own two hands, and felt the tremor run through her — fear, exhaustion, something that might have been the god passing under the ground like a whale under a boat. "I'll walk ahead of you, in a sense. There's a thing I have to do in this city, and it doesn't happen in Errow. Someone has to say what's in that archive out loud, where the Order can hear it, on the temple steps if my nerve holds. You carry the truth east. I'll plant it here." She smiled, and it felt strange on her face, like wearing someone else's hat. "Between us we should cover the whole country. Such of it as exists."

* * *

They went over the wall one by one. Ivet first, with the grace of a man escaping a burning building he had started himself; then Ossa, who took Sarel's arm at the top of the wall and said, conversationally, "You burn collections well, Vane. It's a pity. You'd have made a fair archivist"; then Wren, who turned at the bottom and bowed, formally, in the manner of someone much older using a body much younger, and said something in Hollowtongue that Sarel did not understand and somehow agreed with completely.

Lior went last. She stood on the wall's low crest a moment, badge in one fist, silhouetted against the torn clouds, and Sarel saw her clearly for the first time — not a smuggler, not a fugitive, not even really an apprentice anymore. An heir. Heirs always look like that at thresholds, she supposed. Like people who have just read the will and found themselves named.

"Lady Vane," Lior said. "The report you never filed. The transcription of the archive."

"Yes?"

"When all this is done — read it aloud anyway. Even if I fix everything. People should hear it from you. You're the only one they'll believe."

Then she dropped down on the far side, and the grass swallowed her footsteps, and Sarel Vane stood alone in the Graveyard of the Convicted with a wet left boot, a lantern guttering low, and no badge at all — just a citizen of Vess now, subject to the same curfews and fires as anyone, free exactly insofar as she had made herself homeless.

She picked up her lantern and turned toward the city, where the temple steps waited, and the highest of them was very well lit and very public and would hold a woman, a page, and a great deal of truth quite nicely.

Behind her, in the graves at her back, several hundred small warm charms beat gently in the dark — like hearts, like hands applauding softly, like a city's stolen prayers learning, after two hundred years, the way home.
