## 25. Riots of the Answered

The bell in the Ashspire had not rung in Sarel Vane's lifetime.

She knew its sound only from the old manuals — *three strokes for fire, seven for heresy, one long unbroken toll for a state of emergency* — and she had assumed, in the comfortable way one assumes things, that it never would. The Purge had ended emergencies. That was the whole point. Her entire life had been built on the assurance that the bell would hang silent until it rusted through.

At the fourth hour past midnight, it rang one long unbroken toll, and did not stop.

Sarel stood at her window in the Inquisitors' Quarter with her report — the unsent report, the true one, folded into quarters and carried against her ribs like a stolen relic — and listened to the city scream.

Not with fear. She had heard Vess scream with fear before: the plague summer, the granary fire, the time a whale had washed up in the harbor and everyone had been briefly certain it was an omen. This was different. This was the sound of a city getting what it wanted and having no idea what to do about it.

She dressed quickly, pinned on the badge she no longer entirely believed in, and went down into the streets.

* * *

By dawn, half the Lantern District was standing in front of the Temple of the Sealed Name, which was not a temple anyone had prayed in for forty years. It had been converted to a granary, then a granary overflow, then mostly a place where pigeons held their own convocations. Its bells had been pulled and its altar smashed and its doors bricked over, because that was what the Reformation did to temples: it didn't tear them down, it just made them useful, which was crueler.

The crowd had torn the bricks out overnight.

Sarel watched from the mouth of Cooper's Lane as a fishwife with forearms like dock rope climbed through the empty doorframe holding a lantern, and came back out weeping, shouting that the inside was *warm*, that the stones were warm as bread, that something in there was listening. The crowd surged forward like water finding a hole in a ship.

"Back!" The temple watch — six men with staves and the terrified posture of men who had signed up to chase pickpockets — formed a line that lasted approximately as long as it takes to say *lasted*. "Inquisition orders! No entry to consecrated—"

A cabbage struck the watch-sergeant's helmet with a sound like a drumbeat. Then the whole harvest of the district followed it.

Sarel did not draw her iron. There were four hundred people and six cabbages' worth of order, and somewhere in the back of her skull a voice that sounded suspiciously like Tessin Cole asked, mildly, whether she truly planned to iron-scorch a fishwife for wanting to pray in her own neighborhood.

She turned down Cooper's Lane and walked fast, like a person with urgent business elsewhere.

* * *

The Ashspire command room smelled of tallow, wet wool, and panic, which Sarel thought of as the official incense of the Inquisition. Grand Inquisitor Halvern Roke stood at the head of the map table, and around it stood every senior officer in Vess, all talking at once, none listening, which was how meetings here had always worked but was worse now because they had actual news.

"—the Saltgate congregation—"

"—three hundred at the Chantry Steps singing something, some song, nobody could make out the words—"

"—prayer-burns in the Cistern Ward, forty of them, *forty*, and people are putting their hands in them like they're holy wells—"

"They ARE holy wells," said Brother Anselm, who was ninety years old and had been tolerated at these meetings for decades on the theory that he would soon die and stop being inconvenient. He had outlived three Grand Inquisitors and showed every sign of planning to outlive the institution itself out of spite. "That is precisely what they are. I have been telling you lot for thirty years that sealing a god is not the same as deleting one, and you put me on latrine inspection for it."

"Anselm," said Roke, in the voice of a man who has been awake too long to be angry properly.

"You put me on LATRINE inspection," Anselm repeated, with relish, "and now look."

Everyone looked. Out the tall window, across the rooftops of Vess, six separate fires were burning, and between them, in the streets, thousands of small lights moved — lanterns, candles, hand-lamps — converging on temples, chantries, shrines, and the bricked-up shells of everything the Purge had emptied. It looked, Sarel thought unwillingly, like the city was full of fireflies, all flying home.

Roke turned to the map. "Martial law," he said. "Curfew from sundown. Patrols doubled. All congregations dispersed. Anyone found at a sealed temple is to be arrested for sedition." He looked up. "Where is the girl?"

Sarel's stomach performed a maneuver she had not authorized. "Which girl, Grand Inquisitor?"

"The witch." Roke's finger came down on the sketch pinned to the corner of the map — Lior Eastwick, drawn from the night market raid, young and sharp-faced and looking, even in charcoal, like someone who apologized to furniture when she bumped into it. "Eastwick's last apprentice. She fled east with the cartographer and the child. Reports say Errow." He said the name the way other people said a curse. "The riots began within days of her escape. Coincidence, Brother Corvin thinks."

"Brother Corvin thinks everything is the witch," said Anselm. "Brother Corvin blamed the whale on witches."

"The whale," Corvin said with dignity, "was an omen."

"The whale was a whale, you absolute—" Roke pinched the bridge of his nose. "Vane. You've read the deep archive. You've been reading it." His eyes were bloodshot and very steady. "Tell me what's happening in my city."

It was the moment. Sarel felt it arrive and stand there, enormous, waiting. She had the report against her ribs. She had Ossa's warnings, the archive pages, the truth about the Door, the truth about the prison. One sentence — *the gods were never killed; we guard their cell* — and this room would crack like the temple bricks.

But she looked at Roke's face and saw not a tyrant but an exhausted man who had spent his whole life believing he was a wall between the city and the dark, and she understood, with a lurch, exactly what that truth would do to him if it arrived from her mouth instead of his own discovery. It would not free him. It would break him, and broken men with armies give worse orders than tired ones.

And there was Wren to think of. A child with a fugitive's face on a warrant, in a city about to declare martial law.

"The answered prayers are not her doing," Sarel said carefully. "They're not anyone's doing, in the sense you mean. The prayers are being answered because someone is finally home to answer them." She chose each word like stepping stones across a flooded street. "What I can tell you, Grand Inquisitor, is that arresting the poor for praying will not stop the praying. It will only teach them whom to hate."

"Then what do you propose?" Roke snapped. "A festival?"

"I propose," said Anselm, "that you let them into the temples, post guards to keep the peace rather than keep them out, and accept that forty years of pretending produced exactly this night. But nobody ever listens to me. Latrine inspection."

Roke stared at the window. The fires kept burning. Somewhere below, faintly, through glass and stone and distance, came singing — hundreds of voices, wordless or near enough, rising off the streets like smoke.

"Martial law," Roke said at last, quietly. "Curfew. Patrols. But no iron unless blades are drawn." He looked around the room. "We are keepers of the peace, not gravediggers. We have been gravediggers before. I won't have it again."

Sarel did not know, then, whether she admired him or pitied him or both. She settled, provisionally, on both.

* * *

The problem with martial law, Sarel discovered by noon, is that it requires the military, and the military of Vess consisted largely of the sons and nephews of the people currently storming the temples.

At the Chantry Steps she watched a pikeman — nineteen, maybe twenty, cheeks still soft — receive the order to disperse the crowd, look out at it, and find his own mother in the third row. The crowd recognized him too. Someone shouted "Tam! Tam Wexler, don't you dare point that at your nan!" and the entire line of pikes sagged like wet laundry while Tam Wexler underwent, publicly and in uniform, the complete moral collapse of the Vess Inquisition in miniature.

He grounded his pike. His sergeant screamed. Tam's mother waved. By evening, half the garrison had cousins in the crowds, and the curfew was enforced chiefly in the districts where nobody lived.

Sarel wrote it all down, because writing was the only thing left that obeyed her.

*Saltgate: patrol refused dispersal order. Chantry Steps: crowd sang until dawn; guards joined in on the parts they remembered from grandmothers. Cistern Ward: a prayer-burn in a baker's wall has become a shrine; queue of two hundred; the baker is selling rolls to the queue and has never been happier. Note: the baker's wife says the burn hums when children touch it. Note: I did not arrest the baker. Note: I am aware of what these notes add up to.*

She paused over that last line, then kept it. Honesty in a ledger was a kind of courage, even a private kind.

* * *

Near dusk she went, against every instinct of self-preservation and several explicit orders, to see Tessin Cole.

The apothecary's shop was ash. It had burned cleanly the night of the raid — too cleanly, Sarel had always thought, for an accident — and Tessin had been in the Inquisition cells since, refusing counsel, refusing food beyond what kept him upright, and refusing, most memorably, to confirm the existence of magic, guilds, gods, or, on one occasion, the sea.

She found him in the exercise yard, sitting on an upturned bucket with the serenity of a man watching a play he'd already seen twice. He brightened when he saw her, which unnerved her more than hostility would have.

"Inquisitor Vane! Come to enjoy the weather? It's rioting, apparently. Even the sky's joined in."

"You burned your own shop," Sarel said.

Tessin examined his fingernails. "Clumsy of me. Lamp fell. Very hot lamp."

"There were forty pounds of licensed salts in that cellar. They don't catch like that. And you stayed in the building until the market was clear." She crouched to his eye level. "You stayed so a girl could run."

"What girl? I know no girls. My acquaintance is exclusively with lamps."

"Tessin." Her voice came out lower than intended. "There are riots outside these walls. There is a child with a warrant on his head and a god in his mouth. There is a ledger in the deep archive that says everything either of us ever believed might be a lie told by frightened people to other frightened people, and I am standing in a prison yard asking the one man who might actually know something, and you are making lamp jokes."

For a moment — just a moment — the humor drained out of Tessin's face and Sarel saw underneath it: a craftsman who had watched forty years of his trade go into a fire and walked into the flames himself to buy a child ten minutes. It was the same look she'd seen in her own mirror lately, and it frightened her more than the riots.

"Lamp jokes are load-bearing," he said quietly. "Take them out and the whole structure comes down." He glanced at the walls, the guard tower, the barred windows. "You want to know something true, Inquisitor? Here's one. Whatever's waking up under this city — it isn't angry at the poor. Read your archive again. Ask yourself why the prayer-burns appear in tallow-shops and cisterns and never once, not one single time, in the Inquisitors' Quarter." He smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "Gods remember who fed them and who starved them. That's not magic. That's just memory."

"And the girl? Eastwick?"

Tessin stood, joints cracking, and stretched like an old dog in the sun. "Lior Eastwick," he said, "is the most decent person I have ever failed, and if your order catches her, I hope every lamp in Vess falls on your heads." He shuffled toward the yard door, then paused. "Also — the whale was definitely an omen. Tell Corvin I said so."

* * *

Night brought the worst of it.

The curfew held nowhere. By the second bell, the crowd around the old Temple of the Sealed Name had grown past counting, and they had done something no one had ordered and no one could have prevented: they had begun cleaning it. Sarel watched from a rooftop opposite — she had stopped pretending she was on patrol — as hundreds of people passed buckets of water and baskets of rubble hand over hand out of the broken doors, sweeping forty years of grain dust and pigeon rule out of the nave by candlelight. Nobody was preaching. Nobody was armed. Somebody had started singing the same wordless thing the Chantry crowd sang, and the sweepers moved to it like oarsmen, and the sound coming off that building was the sound the Inquisition had spent eighty years insisting could not exist: worship without permission, joyful, ordinary, everywhere.

Her badge felt very heavy on her chest. Below her, a watchman spotted the crowd, looked up at the rooftop, saw her, and — with the exquisite tact of a man deciding not to see something — turned his horse around and rode the other way.

That was when the first prayer-burn opened in front of her.

It happened on the rooftop itself, in the lead flashing at her feet: a soft pop, like a log settling in a fire, and a scorched hollow bloomed in the metal, edges glowing, shaped — she leaned closer, heart hammering — shaped like an open hand. Warmth rose off it. Not heat, exactly. Something warmer than heat, if warmth could have an opinion.

The reports called it hunger. The archive called it feeding. Aldous's ghost, via a child, had called it *half-mad*. But this small scorch at her feet felt less like hunger than like knocking. Like something vast, on the far side of the world's thin places, pressing one fingertip through and saying, gently, *I'm still here. Are you?*

Sarel knelt. She should have marked it, reported it, ordered it scraped and salted per procedure. Instead she pulled off her glove and laid her bare palm above it — not touching, hovering — and felt the warmth lean toward her skin like a plant toward light.

Eighty years of doctrine said this was abomination. Her knees on cold lead, her breath fogging, Sarel Vane found that what she actually felt was embarrassed — embarrassed for the institution that had taught her to fear this, and homesick for something she had never had.

"I can't stay," she told it quietly. Talking to a hole in a roof; her instructors would have had her on latrine inspection by sundown. "But I'm going to tell the truth about you. In public. On the temple steps, where everyone can hear. I don't know yet how I survive that. But it's going to happen."

The burn dimmed slowly, like a lamp accepting an answer. Whether it understood her, or simply guttered on its own, she couldn't say. She chose — deliberately, the way she was now choosing many things — to believe it understood.

* * *

She wrote until the third bell.

The report grew page by page beside the guttering candle: the granary-temple, warm as bread; Tam Wexler's grounded pike; the baker's humming wall; the queue of the poor at every hollow in the city, leaving offerings of bread and buttons and, in one documented case, a small embroidered cushion, because even miracles deserve comfort. She wrote down the archive citations. She wrote down Ossa's account. She wrote, in a paragraph she immediately recognized as the end of her career, the following:

*The Purge did not save us. It imprisoned, and we have been prison guards calling ourselves priests. What is loose in Vess tonight is not an invasion. It is a release. The question before this order is not how to put the god back, but whether we have the honesty to admit we never owned it.*

She read it over. She imagined Roke reading it. She imagined Roke's face doing what faces do.

Then she folded it into quarters, tucked it against her ribs next to the older copy, and blew out the candle.

Through the window, Vess glowed — a hundred little fires, a thousand candles, one entire city refusing to sleep. Somewhere out there, east of everywhere, a girl with a warrant and a child with a god were walking toward a Door, and Sarel had just realized, sitting in the dark with the truth in her shirt, that she intended to be standing on the temple steps reading aloud before they reached it. Someone had to crack the wall from this side. It might as well be the person holding the mallet.

She pinned her badge on — crooked, she noticed later; she never did fix it — and went to bed.

At dawn, a runner arrived at the Ashspire with dispatches from the eastern road: sightings of three travelers matching the warrants, last seen passing through a village that, when the clerk checked the maps, was not on any of them. The clerk checked again. He checked four atlases and the provincial registry and finally the great survey map on the command room wall, and found, between Millbrook and the fens, a blank space about the size of a parish, unmapped, unlabeled, as if the cartographers had agreed unanimously to look away.

The blank space had a name penciled faintly in an ancient margin: *Errow*.

Roke stared at it for a long time. Then he turned, found Sarel in the crowded room, and said, in front of everyone:

"Vane. You've been east. Pack for travel."

Sarel's heart did something complicated — part dread, part relief, part a wild, inappropriate urge to laugh. They were sending her after Lior Eastwick. They were handing her a horse, authority, and a straight line to the one place in the world she needed to be, and they had no idea they were pointing the key at the lock.

She bowed. "At once, Grand Inquisitor."

On her way out, she stopped by the great map and looked at the blank space where a country had been erased, and thought, with a smile she didn't bother hiding: *You're not blank. You're just unwritten. Hold on. We're coming.*
