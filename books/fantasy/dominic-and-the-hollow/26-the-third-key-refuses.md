## 26. The Third Key Refuses

The Inquisition's archive smelled of lye and old paper, and Ossa Vell had spent thirty-one years making sure it stayed that way.

She was sixty-eight years old, small as a folded letter, and officially she was nothing at all. The ledgers listed her as a contract laborer, a cleanser of documents, one of four women who scrubbed the great vault beneath the Bastion Hall where the Inquisition kept everything it had ever decided not to burn. Her hands were cracked from the lye solution. Her back ached in weather. She had cleaned so many confiscated charms out of so many confiscated books that her fingertips had gone permanently numb, which was, she supposed, a kind of mercy. It is difficult to feel the hum of magic when you have scalded the feeling out of yourself on purpose.

Every morning she descended the ninety-one steps with her bucket and her brushes, and every morning she erased another piece of the world.

Today there was a book waiting for her that she had been avoiding for eleven days. She knew it was there because she had catalogued it herself when it came in — *Hymnal, Hollow Guild liturgy, partial, water damage* — and then she had shelved it spine-in, behind a row of tax records from a province that no longer existed, and told herself she would deal with it after lunch.

She had dealt with it after lunch for eleven consecutive lunches.

"You're hiding from it," said Brother Calloway from the top of the stairs, his voice echoing down the vault in that cheerful way of his. He was the youngest archivist and the only one who talked to her like a person rather than a fixture. "I've watched you walk past shelf fourteen nine times this morning. You're doing laps."

"I'm organizing," Ossa said.

"You're doing laps."

"Boy, I was erasing heresies before your mother learned to read." She wrung out her rag with a snap. "Go count your incunabula."

He laughed and clattered away up the stairs, and Ossa stood alone in the lamplight among forty thousand books the world was not allowed to remember, and finally, because she was seventy percent stubborn and thirty percent tired, she took down the hymnal.

It fell open to the binding hymn. Of course it did. Water damage or no water damage, some things know where they belong. She looked at the notes — the strange old notation, the hollow-headed quavers the guild used — and her mouth did the thing it always did around the binding hymn, which was to try to sing it without her permission.

She pressed her lips shut.

Thirty-one years, she thought. Thirty-one years of keeping my mouth shut in this hole. You'd think it would have taken by now.

* * *

They came for her at dusk, which was wrong on two counts.

First, nobody came for Ossa at dusk. The cleansers were day workers; after the evening bell the vault was locked and everyone above stairs pretended she didn't exist, which suited all parties. Second, they didn't come through the front. There was a soft scrape at the coal chute — the one she'd reported as rusted shut for three decades precisely so it wouldn't be — and then a young woman dropped down into her cleaning alcove like a sack of laundry thrown by someone apologetic.

Ossa did not scream. Screaming brought soldiers, and soldiers brought questions, and questions were how people died. Instead she picked up her mop and held it the way you hold a spear when you have not held a spear in thirty years but your body remembers anyway.

"Don't," said the young woman, raising both hands. Ash-smudged face, hood pushed back, eyes too old for her years. On her inner wrist, where the sleeve had ridden up: a small inked sigil, a circle broken by a vertical line. "Please. I'm not here to hurt you."

"No," Ossa agreed. "You're here to ask me for something. That's worse."

The girl blinked. "You know who I am?"

"I know the sigil." Ossa set the mop down slowly. "Lior Eastwick. Aldous Rennick's last apprentice. You've been leaving scorched holes all over my city and getting an apothecary's shop burned down, and now you've crawled into the lion's den through my coal chute, which I will note was very rude, because I sealed that chute myself and I'll be the one who has to explain it."

"I—" Lior looked genuinely stricken. "I'm sorry about the chute."

"It's fine. I lied about it for thirty years hoping someone would use it. I just wanted to say it out loud once." Ossa pulled off her gloves, finger by finger. Behind Lior, two more heads appeared at the mouth of the chute — a lean man covered in chalk dust, and a boy of perhaps ten whose eyes were doing something unsettling, flickering between brown and a gray that belonged to no living person. Ossa felt the bottom drop out of her stomach and kept her face perfectly still, because that was the first thing the guild ever taught you: *the face is the first door, keep it shut.*

"Well," she said. "You'd better sit on the crates. And you—chalk man—stop dripping on my floor. This floor is the only honest thing in this building."

"That's Ivet," said Lior. "And Wren."

"Mm." Ossa looked at the boy a long moment. He looked back with Aldous's gray eyes, and her heart did something complicated and painful that she filed away for later, the way she filed everything away, the way she had been filing things away since the night the Bastion bells rang and the guild died screaming. "So. You found Maren's key, I take it. And dug up poor Fenner Brase out of the convict field, which I hope you were respectful about, because he hated being cold."

Lior went very still. "How do you know all that?"

"Because I'm the last elder of the Hollow Guild," Ossa said, "and I have been reading this order's mail for thirty-one years. Every report about prayer-burns crosses my desk before it crosses the Grand Inquisitor's. Every record of every name Maren Cole sold — I cleansed those documents myself. Do you know what cleansing a document means, child? It means I read every word. It means I copied out the parts worth keeping. It means I know exactly which names she sold, in what order, and how much she was paid for each one." She folded her cracked hands in her lap. "Aldous Rennick fetched forty crowns. Maren haggled. I read her haggling."

The chalk-dust man — Ivet — made a low sound. Lior sat down on the crate as though her legs had been taken away.

"So yes," Ossa continued, gently now, because cruelty served nothing. "I know why you've come. You have two keys. You need the third. And you've come to the one woman in Vess mad enough to hide it inside the Inquisition itself, because who would ever search for a Hollow key in the ash-heap of the Purge?" She reached up and unpinned her collar. Beneath it, on a cord around her neck, hung a small iron key worn smooth as a river stone, and stamped with the same broken-circle sigil. "Here it is. Third key of the Hollow Door. I've slept with it against my heart for three decades. And my answer is no."

* * *

The silence that followed had texture. Ivet stopped fidgeting. Even the boy-Wren went quiet, head tilting, listening to something under the floor.

"You haven't even heard us out," Lior said finally.

"I don't need to. I've heard the plan in my head for thirty years, because I wrote half of it. Find the keys. Open the Door. Free the god. End the ban. It's a good plan. It's even a righteous plan." Ossa stood, went to her bucket, and began — out of pure habit — scrubbing a stain off the flagstones while she talked. "It is also, every word of it, wrong. And I know it's wrong because I helped seal that Door, child. I was thirty-seven. I stood in the chapel dark with Aldous's hand on my shoulder, and I turned my key, and I heard what came up from underneath. So before you spend one more life on this road — Tessin's shop, Tessin himself sitting in a cell right now — you will listen to an old woman tell you what your beautiful plan actually does."

Lior's chin came up. "Then tell me."

"Gladly. But first, tea. You don't make moral decisions on an empty stomach. That's how the Inquisition got started." She shuffled to the little alcove stove, and if her hands trembled slightly as she measured the leaves, well, the room was dim and the others were polite enough not to mention it.

* * *

While the water heated, Ossa studied them properly.

The cartographer, Ivet, sat cross-legged and tried to look unobtrusive and failed, because men who draw maps for a living cannot stop their eyes from measuring rooms. He'd already located both exits, counted the lamp oil, and noted the weight-bearing shelf. She approved. Useful in a siege, terrible at dinner parties.

The boy worried her. Wren sat very straight, hands flat on his knees, and he was watching her with an attention no child owned. Twice his lips moved without sound. Once he whispered a word in Hollowtongue — *vessik*, *come*, or possibly *hollow*, the tongue was slippery — and shuddered as though the word had been poured into him from somewhere cold.

"He speaks it fluently," Ossa said quietly, handing Lior a tin cup.

"The god does. Through him."

"Mm." Ossa lowered herself onto her stool. "And does the god ask anything of him? Does it thank him?"

Lior hesitated. "It... uses him."

"Yes." Ossa blew on her tea. "That's the part nobody wants to hear. Everyone in this city who prays these days thinks the god is a generous grandfather waking from a nap. Answered prayers! Miracles for the poor! Nobody asks what the god gets out of the arrangement. Nobody asks why a being powerful enough to break a fever across a whole district needs to borrow a ten-year-old's mouth to say hello." She sipped. "I'll tell you why. Because it isn't powerful anymore. It's starving. It has been starving for three hundred years, sealed behind our Door, and it has learned to eat the only food that reaches it — prayer. And prayer, child, is not nourishment. Prayer is salt water. It drinks and drinks and gets thirstier, and the burns you've seen all over Vess are its teeth marks."

"We know it's hungry," Lior said. "That's why we're opening the Door. To free it. Let it feed properly, let it—"

"Let it go where?" Ossa set down her cup. "That is my question, and I want you to actually sit with it. Your plan assumes the Door is a cage and opening it is mercy. Fine. Open it. The god walks out into the world. And then what? Where does a starving god go to eat?"

Lior opened her mouth. Closed it.

"Into the world," Ossa answered for her. "Into the fields, the wells, the birthing rooms. Into every charm-seller and every desperate mother and every child with a fever. You want to end the ban so magic flows freely again — but magic isn't a well, dear. Magic is *it*. Every spell anyone ever cast in the old days was a sip of that thing under the chapel. The guild didn't guard the Door to hoard power. We guarded it the way you guard a wound — not because you love the bandage, but because you've seen what comes out otherwise." She tapped her own sternum, where the key hung. "Open the Door and you don't free the hunger. You relocate it. From a sealed room into a country. From one starving prisoner into five million feeding troughs. And it will be gentle at first. It will answer prayers. It will heal fevers and find lost rings and rain on dry farms, and everyone will weep with joy, and in twenty years it will be 1198 all over again, except this time there will be no guild left to seal it, because we'll have handed it the keys ourselves."

"You don't know that," Lior said. But her voice had lost its edge; it had gone thin and young.

"I was there for the last time," Ossa said. "I know exactly what it sounds like when it says thank you."

* * *

The stove ticked. Somewhere far above, the Bastion bells rang the ninth hour, and everyone flinched but Ossa.

"Tell me about the sealing," Lior said at last. "All of it. Not the version in the songs."

Ossa looked at her for a long moment. The girl had Aldous's stubbornness, if not his face. She had his habit of asking the question that costs the most.

"All right," Ossa said. "But I warn you, the true version has no heroes in it. We weren't saints, the founders. We weren't even particularly good people." She wrapped her cracked hands around her own cup. "We were thieves with a theology..."

But before she could go on, the boy spoke.

Not Wren's voice. Something older, layered under the child's soprano like a second stream running beneath a shallow one, and every hair on Ossa's arms stood up because she knew that voice, she had known it across forty years and one closed door—

*"Little sister,"* it said, gentle and vast and terribly sad. *"Don't tell them yet. Some doors must open from the inside."*

Wren swayed. Lior caught him. "Aldous?" she breathed. "Aldous, is that—"

The boy's head sagged. When he looked up again, his eyes were only a child's eyes, wet and frightened. "It talks to me even when I don't want it to," Wren whispered. "It doesn't like it when she says no."

Ossa sat very still, tea cooling in her hands, the key warm against her heart as it had not been warm in thirty years.

*It knows I'm here,* she thought. *After all this time. It remembers my voice.*

And beneath the fear, unwelcome as spring weeds through flagstones, another thought was already growing — the thought she had been drowning for eleven days behind a row of dead province tax records:

*If it can still speak, if it can still reach through a child's mouth to call me little sister — then maybe it isn't only hunger in there. Maybe there's something left worth saving. And maybe, just maybe, the old woman's refusal is the one thing standing between this child and a Door that wants him.*

She set down her cup.

"Girl," Ossa said quietly, "before you argue with me any further, there's something I have to show you. A document. It's on shelf fourteen, spine-in, behind the tax rolls — yes, I hid it from myself, we've established I'm a coward with excellent filing habits." She rose, joints cracking, and looked at Lior with thirty-one years of carefully kept silence pressing behind her eyes. "What's written in it changes what the Door is for. And once you've read it, neither of us gets to pretend the choice is simple ever again."

She took down the book, blew off the dust, and put it into Lior's ash-stained hands.

"Now," said the last elder of the Hollow Guild, "let's see whether you still want to open it."
