## 27. What the Door Actually Opens

Ossa made tea the way she did everything else in the Inquisition's shadow — carefully, quietly, and with an exit already planned.

The kettle was dented copper, older than Lior's guild sigil, and it sat on a brazier no bigger than a loaf of bread. The room itself was a cleanser's cell in the undercrofts of the Vess archive: stone walls, one lamp, three chairs that had clearly been stolen from three different offices over the course of thirty years. Ossa had lived here so long she had grown into the room the way a tree grows around a fence.

"Sit," she said. "All of you. Wren by the wall, where I can see him."

"He's not going to do anything," Lior said.

"Child, last week he recited the Rite of Unbinding backwards in his sleep and cracked a water main on Sable Street." Ossa poured tea into mismatched cups with the serenity of someone who had long since stopped being surprised by anything. "He's going to do something. The question is only what, when, and whether anyone drowns."

Wren, who was eleven and mostly exhausted these days, climbed onto the chair by the wall and wrapped both hands around his cup like it was the last warm thing in the world. "It wasn't backwards," he said, with the dignity of the very young correcting the very old. "It was *inside out*. There's a difference."

"There is," Ossa agreed gravely. "And the difference is why we're all still alive. Mostly."

Ivet took the third stolen chair. Sarel stood by the door — old habits, guarding the exit, though whose side she was guarding it for these days nobody wanted to examine too closely. She had given them her badge to get down here. That badge was currently the only legal thing about any of them.

Lior held her tea and waited. She had learned, in the past months, that Ossa spoke when she was good and ready and not one breath before, and that rushing her produced nothing but longer silences.

The silence now lasted through half a cup of tea.

"You came back from the grave," Ossa said at last, to no one in particular. "You dug up a dead man's key with your hands. You've got an ex-Inquisitor standing at my door like a lost dog, and a child who speaks Hollowtongue like he was born to it, and a cartographer who maps places that don't exist. And you think you're ready for me to tell you what the Door opens."

"I think we're as ready as we'll ever be," Lior said.

"No." Ossa set her cup down with a small click. "You think you're ready because you're young and frightened and frightened people always think knowing will make it better. Sometimes knowing makes it worse. But you've earned worse." She looked at Lior, and her eyes — pale, watery things that had spent decades reading documents meant to be destroyed — were suddenly not watery at all. "You've more than earned it. Aldous Eastwick's apprentice. He wrote about you, you know. In the margins. Where the censors wouldn't look."

Lior's throat tightened. "What did he write?"

"*She asks too many questions. Good. She'll need the habit.*" Ossa almost smiled. It was a strange thing to see on a face built for gravity. "Well then. Ask."

Lior asked the question she had carried up out of the graveyard, out of the convict dirt, out of Aldous's confession through Wren's mouth. "Why did the guild seal the gods?"

"We've been told this part," Sarel said quietly from the door. "Aldous confessed it. They sealed them to hoard magic for themselves. The Purge was — "

"The Purge was a lie with excellent paperwork," Ossa said. "Yes. And the guild sealing the gods to steal their power is also a lie, or rather, a half-truth wearing a nice coat. Your dead master told you what he could stomach telling. He did not tell you what the seal actually *was*." She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and went to the wall of her cell. She pressed her palm against a stone that looked identical to every other stone, and a section of the wall swung inward — a cleanser's hiding place, deep enough for a body, lined with oilcloth.

From it she drew a book.

Not an archive folio. Not a ledger. A book bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, its spine stitched twice, its corners worn round as river stones. Lior knew that kind of wear. That was the wear of a book carried against a chest for years. Of a book read every night.

"This was Aldous's," Lior breathed.

"This was the guild's. Before that, the founders'. One copy. There were never two — the founders understood that two copies means disagreement, and disagreement about *this* would end the world." Ossa laid it on the table between them with the tenderness of someone setting down an infant. "I cleansed archives for the Inquisition for thirty years. Do you know what cleansing actually is? Not burning. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. Cleansing is *replacing*. You take the true record out, and you put a false record in, and you make the false record old. I have written more history than any historian alive. Most of it wrong, on purpose."

"And this one?" Ivet asked softly. "Is it wrong?"

"It's the reason I'm still alive and the reason I'm a coward," Ossa said. "Read the third chapter. Aloud. Hollowtongue reads truer aloud."

Wren made a small sound. Everyone looked at him. He was staring at the book with an expression far too old for his face, and when he spoke, it was in two voices braided together — his own thin reed, and beneath it something vast and patient and terribly sad.

"*We did not build the cage,*" said the boy who was also a god. "*We built the table.*"

* * *

Ossa let the words sit while she refilled cups none of them had finished. Lior noticed her own hands were shaking and made them stop, the way Aldous had taught her — press the tremor flat, breathe it down, save it for later. Later had been accumulating for months now. Later was nearly full.

"Explain it plainly," Lior said. "No liturgy. No guild-speak. Plainly, like I'm stupid."

"You're not stupid," Ossa said. "But plainly, yes. Here it is." She opened the book to a ribbon marker and turned it toward them. The page was dense with Old Hollowtongue, but someone — Aldous, judging by the cramped hand — had annotated the margins in plain Vessian. Lior read the margin notes first. She couldn't help it. His handwriting still did something to her chest, like a hand pressing there gently.

"The gods are not what the temples taught," Ossa said, tapping each note as she went. "Not sky-fathers, not harvest-mothers, not patrons of smiths and sailors. Those were costumes they wore because people understand costumes. Underneath, every god is the same thing: a *hunger that answers*. That's all divinity ever was. Something that hears wanting and cannot bear to leave it unanswered. Prayer isn't worship. Prayer is feeding. Every answered prayer costs the god something, and every answered prayer takes something from the world — you've seen the burns, you've felt them behind your ribs, don't look surprised, girl, the guild marked you years ago, that's how Aldous found you."

"I know what prayer costs," Lior said. "I've been carrying it since the night market."

"Then you know the arithmetic. A god answers, the world pays. A god answers *justly*, carefully, taking payment willingly offered — the world barely notices. A god answers hungrily, madly, after centuries of starvation—" Ossa gestured vaguely at the city above them, where scorched hollows bloomed in walls and streets like a disease. "—you get Vess in autumn."

"So the seal," Sarel said from the door. Her voice was careful, controlled, the voice of a woman holding a railing. "The guild sealed them to stop the hunger."

"The guild *fed* them," Ossa said. "That's the seal. That's the whole secret. Sit down, Inquisitor, before you fall down."

Sarel didn't sit. But she did lean against the doorframe, which for Sarel Vane was practically collapsing.

Ossa turned pages with fingers that trembled slightly, though Lior suspected it was not age. "The founders discovered the hunger-arithmetics centuries ago. They tried everything. Starvation — the god goes mad, you saw that. Destruction — you cannot destroy a hunger, only the thing that hungers, and what remains when the thing is gone is *worse*, it's appetite without shape, it eats meaning instead of bread. Bargains — the bargains rot, because hungers grow. So the founders did the only thing that worked. They didn't lock the Door." She looked up. "They put a *keeper* on the other side of it."

"A keeper," Lior repeated. The word felt cold in her mouth.

"One person. Willing. Guild-trained, guild-sworn, carrying the guild's inheritance — the hoarded power, generations of it, a fortune in stored grace — to feed the hunger from their own hand, deliberately, measured, forever. On the far side. Alone. The Door doesn't release the gods, child. The Door is the *pantry door*. Open it without a keeper inside, and you don't free anything. You just open the pantry to a starving animal and step back."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

Wren had gone white. When he spoke, it was only his own voice, small and horrified. "That's what it dreams about. In my head. It dreams about a door opening and nobody coming in. It says—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It says the last time, somebody promised."

Lior's blood went slow and heavy. "Aldous."

"Aldous," Ossa confirmed. "He was chosen twenty-two years ago. Sworn, trained, prepared. The inheritance was passed to him — the whole of it, every scrap of hoarded power the guild had saved since the founding, poured into one man to carry through the Door. He was to enter the year the Purge began." Her voice flattened. "Maren Cole sold his name to the Inquisition eleven days before the rite. He died on a pyre with the entire inheritance locked inside him, unspent. And the god has been starving ever since. Twenty-two years. You've seen what twenty-two years of starving does to a dog. Now imagine it with the power to answer prayers."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, Vess was rioting — the poor storming temples, demanding their god back, demanding miracles, demanding the hunger be fed because fed was better than ignored, and the martial-law bells rang over it all like a fever pulse.

Lior looked down at the book, at Aldous's marginal hand, and read the note beside the keeper-clause. She read it three times before she could make the words mean anything.

*If I am taken before the rite, the debt passes to my heir. Forgive me. Forgive me. I could not choose anyone else and I could not choose her.*

"He knew," Lior said. Her voice came out strange, level, very quiet. "At the end. When he taught me the hymns. When he gave me the sigil. He wasn't training an apprentice. He was training a *replacement*."

"Lior," Ivet started gently.

"He *knew*." She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "Every lesson. Every song. 'Sing it true, Lior, the binding needs true voices.' He was fattening the calf. And then he died and left me the bill, and I've spent two years selling luck-knots that don't work thinking I was the *last* of something, and the whole time I was the *next* of something. The next meal." She heard herself and stopped, breathing hard.

Wren slid off his chair, crossed the room, and took her hand. Eleven years old, fever-scarred, speaking with a god's voice in his sleep, and he simply stood there holding her hand like it was the most obvious thing in the world to do.

"You're not a meal," he said firmly. "You're the lady who sings. The god likes you best. It told me. Well — it said you taste like morning, but I think that means it likes you."

"That's somehow worse, Wren."

"Yes," he agreed solemnly. "Most comforting things are."

Despite everything — despite the book, the bells, the shaking in her own ribs — Lior laughed. Just once, wet and short, but real. Ossa watched the laugh with something like approval, the way an examiner watches a student pass a hard question.

* * *

They sat with the truth for a long time after that. Truth, Lior was learning, needed sitting-with the way dough needed resting; rush it and it stayed raw.

It was Sarel who broke the silence, and she broke it like she did everything — precisely, at the weakest point. "The inheritance died with Aldous," she said. "On the pyre. If the keeper must carry the guild's hoarded power to feed the god, and that power burned with him twenty-two years ago, then there is no keeper possible. The Door stays shut. Or it opens onto nothing but teeth." She looked at Lior, and her expression was terrible with sympathy, because Sarel had already done the arithmetic and arrived where Lior was still traveling. "Unless the inheritance survived somewhere else."

The room went very quiet.

Ossa reached beneath her chair and brought up a small iron box, plain, unmarked, the kind of box that existed in ten thousand offices and held ten thousand boring things. She set it on top of Aldous's book.

"When Maren Cole named Aldous," Ossa said, "the guild panicked. Some ran. Some hid. Three of us — myself, Aldous, and the elder Hesper, rest her — did the only sensible cowardly thing imaginable. We split the inheritance. Hesper took a portion into the grave with the second key — you dug past it, by the way, it's still there, warming the dead man's charms; I hope that explains what you felt. I took a portion and hid it where no one in the history of the world would ever search." She tapped the Inquisition's floor with her heel. "Inside the censor's office. Thirty years. Filed under *misprints*."

"And the largest portion?" Ivet asked, though his voice said he already knew.

Ossa looked at Lior.

"The night Aldous was taken," she said, "he had hours' warning. Enough to do one thing. He poured the whole remaining inheritance into the deepest vault he had." Another pause. Another look, patient and merciless and kind.

"Me," Lior said. "He put it in *me*."

"You were eight. You thought the ritual was a game — he told you it was a blessing, a guild-blessing, children get them all the time. You've been carrying the guild's entire hoard since before you could write your own name. Every charm you ever sold that *shouldn't* have worked and did — that was leakage. That was a fortune in stored grace bleeding through the seams of a girl who thought she had no talent." Ossa's mouth twisted. "The mother in the night market bought a fever-ward worth more than her street. Her son woke up speaking the language of gods. You didn't fail at small magic, child. You've never done small magic in your life. You've only ever done *enormous* magic badly, because nobody told you what you were carrying."

Lior sat down. It seemed wise.

So that was it, then. All of it, assembled at last: the charm that worked, the burns behind her ribs, the hook she'd felt since the first impossible prayer — the god hadn't marked her as prey. It had marked her as *larder*. As heir. As the walking remainder of a promise her master had made and died keeping, the debt passed hand to hand down twenty-two dark years until it arrived, complete with interest, at a market stall in Vess where a woman bought a luck-knot for two coppers.

The full weight of the inheritance landed on her, and it landed the way Aldous's books used to land off high shelves — all at once, with a sound like judgment.

"If I go through the Door," she said slowly, testing each word, "I take it with me. I feed it. Measured. Forever. Alone."

"That is the office of heir," Ossa said. "I won't dress it up. I'm too old and you're too smart."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the Door must be sealed another way, and there is no other way, only worse ones. Or it stays shut and the god starves until it breaks its own prison, which it will, soon, hungry as it is — and then it feeds itself, on Vess, unmeasured, and there will be nothing left of this country but the name." Ossa folded her hands. "Those are your options. Refusal has a price. Acceptance has a price. Everything does. The founders' genius wasn't escaping cost. It was choosing *who paid*."

Lior looked around the little room. Ivet, watching her with his mapmaker's eyes, already grieving. Sarel at the door, straight-backed, jaw set, composing some argument Lior knew she'd never finish. Wren still holding her hand, humming the binding hymn under his breath, off-key, the way she'd taught him.

She thought of Aldous's margin note. *Forgive me. Forgive me. I could not choose anyone else and I could not choose her.*

"Well," Lior said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Before I decide who pays — I want to negotiate the terms."

Ossa's eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. "Terms? Child, this is not a market stall."

"Everything is a market stall," said the last apprentice of the Hollow Guild, who had once talked a customs officer out of a confiscation fee with a counterfeit luck-knot and a convincing cough. "And I'm told the god is listening. So it can hear me too."

She turned, deliberately, to where Wren sat — to the space behind the boy's eyes where something vast and starving and lonely had been living all this time, dreaming of a door and a promise.

"Here are my terms," Lior said.
