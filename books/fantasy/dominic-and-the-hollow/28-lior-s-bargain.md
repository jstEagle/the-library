## 28. Lior's Bargain

The chapel beneath Errow smelled of cold stone and older smoke. Three keys lay on the altar cloth — Maren's, tarnished gold; the grave-iron one, still carrying a faint chill of Vess's convict dirt; and Ossa's, which she had kept hidden inside the Inquisition for forty years, disguised as a paperweight.

Lior looked at them and felt nothing like triumph.

"So that's it," Ivet said from the doorway. He had his sketchbook out, because Ivet always had his sketchbook out, but he wasn't drawing. He was just holding it, the way a man holds something familiar when the unfamiliar has grown too large. "Three keys, one hand. We put them together and the Door opens."

"And then someone walks through it," Ossa said, "and does not walk back."

The old woman stood with her back to the altar, arms folded, watching Lior with the flat, patient gaze of someone who had spent her career erasing documents and had long since stopped being surprised by what was written in them. "That was the arrangement. Aldous knew it. He was meant to take the office himself, and he died before he could be fitted for it. The guild has been a suit with no one inside it for thirty years. The god has been starving the whole time." She paused. "You are the heir, Lior Eastwick. By blood, by oath, and by the sigil you were foolish enough to keep sewn into your sleeve."

"I kept it because it was pretty," Lior said.

"You kept it because you were guild-born and guild-bred and you'd have kept it if it were ugly as sin," Ossa said. "I did the same. Don't lie to a cleanser of archives. We are professionally good at it."

Wren sat on the chapel steps, swinging his legs. The boy had been quiet since they came down — quieter than a child should be, in the way of a house with someone else living in it. Every so often his head tilted, listening to a voice only he could hear, and his mouth moved around words in a language that had no business in a nine-year-old's mouth.

"He's asking," Wren said suddenly. "He wants to know if you're going to argue, or if you're just going to be sad about it. He says sad is fine. He says sad is honest. But arguing is more interesting."

Lior crouched down to the boy's level. Up close, Wren's eyes had a depth to them that no child's eyes should have — a stillness, like water over a very long fall.

"Tell him I'm going to argue," she said. "Tell him I'm going to argue for a very long time."

Wren's face did something complicated — the boy surfacing for a moment, frightened and relieved in equal measure — and then the stillness came back, and when he spoke again the voice behind the words was deeper. Older. Familiar in a way that made Lior's chest hurt.

"Then argue, little apprentice," said the voice of Aldous Eastwick through the mouth of a child. "I always liked you better when you argued. It was the only time you listened."

* * *

They argued for three days.

Not continuously — there were meals, and sleep, and one memorable afternoon when Ivet discovered the chapel's collection of pre-Purge hymnals and had to be physically restrained from copying all of them — but the shape of it never changed. Ossa laid out the terms as they had always been. Lior refused them as they had always been, and then began building new ones, the way she built charms: small, stubborn, stitched together from things that shouldn't hold.

"The Door opens," Ossa said, on the second morning. "You go through. The god is fed — fed properly, by an heir who gives herself willingly, and the hunger quiets. The prayers of Vess stop burning holes in the world. That is the bargain. It has always been the bargain."

"It's a terrible bargain," Lior said.

"It's the only bargain."

"No." Lior set down her cup of Ossa's terrible tea — the woman had been hiding inside the Inquisition for four decades and had somehow never learned to brew anything — and stood. "It's the bargain *you* were taught. It's the bargain the guild made when the guild thought it owned the gods. Well, I've heard what Aldous said through Wren in the graveyard. The guild didn't own the gods. The guild *robbed* them, and then built a door to keep the robbery from being noticed. I'm not signing my name to a thief's contract. I'm renegotiating."

Ossa's eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch, which Lior had learned was the equivalent of another woman standing on a chair and shouting.

"You are in no position to renegotiate."

"I'm in the *only* position to renegotiate," Lior said. "You need an heir willing to walk through that door. There's exactly one candidate in this room, and she is me, and I have terms."

Wren laughed — a bright, ordinary, nine-year-old laugh, and for a moment it was just a boy laughing at the grown-ups, which Lior thought might be the healthiest sound the god had made in thirty years.

"Name them," Ossa said.

* * *

The terms took shape slowly, the way a charm takes shape — not all at once, but loop by loop, each one tested against the others.

"First," Lior said, standing at the sealed inner door with its three keyholes, its surface worn smooth by centuries of hands that had hoped never to use it. "The god eats. I understand that. It's been starving since the Purge, and starving things eat whatever's nearest, and what's been nearest is the faith of Vess's poor, and it's been eating them *badly*. Answered prayers that scorch. Fever-wards that work but take something. A child who wakes up speaking a dead language because the god needed a mouth and didn't ask permission." She looked at Wren, who was drawing in the dust with a stick — drawing the guild sigil, over and over, with the unerring accuracy of someone who had never learned it. "That stops. If I go through that door, the god answers prayers *justly*. The way a guild should. The fever breaks and the child stays the child. The luck comes and it doesn't cost the neighbor her luck. No more hunger wearing the shape of mercy."

"The god is not a vending machine," Ossa said.

"No," Lior agreed. "It's worse. It's a *landlord*. And I'm negotiating the lease."

Ivet, from his corner, made a sound that was trying very hard not to be a laugh, and mostly failed.

"Second," Lior went on, before Ossa could recover. "The ban ends."

That landed harder. Ossa went still in the way of old soldiers.

"The Purge banned magic," Lior said, "because the guild told the Inquisition that magic was dangerous. And it was — the guild's magic, the hoarded kind, the inheritance locked in vaults while the city's charmsellers got hanged for fever-wards. But I've seen what happens when the poor can't have anything. They pray anyway. And now there's something awake under their prayers, and it's *answering*, and every answer is a burn because no one taught it restraint. You can't ban the need. You can only decide who gets to meet it." She spread her hands. "The guild's inheritance dies with me going through that door — or most of it does; I'll get to that. What's left of magic in the world should belong to everyone. Charm-sellers in the night market. Midwives. The woman I sold a luck-knot to who couldn't afford a real one. The ban was the guild's crime wearing the Inquisition's uniform, and I'm not leaving it standing behind me as my monument."

"And if the god says no?" Ossa asked. "If it would rather have an heir on its own terms?"

"Then it can have no heir at all," Lior said, "and it can starve, politely, for another thirty years, and the burns will keep spreading, and eventually it will eat through whatever's holding it and come out the way starving things come out — which is *badly*. You know that. Aldous knows that. That's why the Door exists." She held the old woman's gaze. "I'm not threatening you, Ossa. I'm describing the menu. There are two meals on it. One comes with my terms."

The silence stretched. Wren's stick had stopped moving in the dust.

"You sound like him," Ossa said at last, and her voice had gone strange. "Aldous. In the last year, when he understood what the guild had done and couldn't undo. He talked about renegotiating. He talked about giving it back." Her mouth twisted. "We called it his heresy. We were going to censure him. And then the Purge came, and the censure didn't matter, and he died with the heresy still in his mouth."

"He died with it in his mouth," Lior said, "and now it's in mine. That's what an inheritance is. It's not the vault. It's the argument you never finished."

* * *

On the third night, the god spoke plainly — through Wren, but also, faintly, through the walls themselves, through the stone of the chapel, so that everyone heard it and no one had to pretend otherwise.

It came as Aldous, because that was the shape Lior's grief had left, and the god was honest enough — or hungry enough — to use it.

"You understand what you're asking," the voice said. It filled the chapel without echoing. It came from everywhere the way cold comes from everywhere. "You're asking a starving thing to eat politely. Forever. You're asking it to be a *guild* again — the thing it was before it was a god, the thing it failed at, the thing that made it what it is."

"Yes," Lior said. She was standing in the center of the chapel floor, in her nightclothes, because the voice had come at the hour when even the devout are weakest, and she had come down anyway. "That's exactly what I'm asking. I'm asking you to fail better."

The silence had texture. Ivet appeared in the doorway with a lamp, hair wild, saying nothing, just standing witness. Ossa came down the stairs a moment later, fully dressed — Lior suspected the woman slept fully dressed, like a knife in a drawer.

"If I agree," the god said, "and you are lying — if you walk through that door intending to give me nothing but your body and your obedience —"

"I'm not giving you my obedience," Lior said. "I'm giving you my *inheritance*. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone, because it was stolen from you. The guild hoarded what was yours. Power, rites, the binding hymns, the true names — all of it, locked in vaults and bloodlines while you starved behind a door with your own name scratched off it. I'm the last heir. Everything the guild held, I hold. And I'm giving it back. All of it. The hoard, the rites, the songs — and yes, me, what's left over, whatever an apprentice is once you take the apprenticeship out of her." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I'm not staying to hold the Door, because the Door shouldn't need holding. It should need *feeding*, and it should be fed what was always yours. That's my whole offer. It's not obedience. It's restitution."

The lamp in Ivet's hand flickered. In the dust of the chapel floor, Wren — awake now, standing in the doorway in his nightshirt, drawn down the stairs by the voice in his bones — looked at the guild sigil he'd drawn, and it was fading, the lines going soft, like chalk in rain.

"You would be powerless," the god said. Aldous's voice, gentle now, the way it had been when Lior was small and had broken something and feared the breaking more than the scolding. "The last magic in you would go with the inheritance. You'd walk out of here a charm-seller with no charms. You'd be *ordinary*, Lior Eastwick. After all of this."

"I've been ordinary my whole life," Lior said. "I was just pretending otherwise to survive. There are worse fates than getting to stop pretending."

"And the ban?"

"Ends. Fully. Not the Inquisition's version where licensed apothecaries sell watered tinctures and call it medicine. The real thing. Anyone who wants to learn the songs can learn the songs. Anyone who wants to tie a luck-knot can tie one and know it might hold. Magic back in the open, where it can be argued with, taxed, taught to children, blamed when it fails — *alive*, in other words, instead of locked in a vault or burned in a market square." She paused. "And the guild's name goes with it. Not as a legend. Not as a warning. As a *history*. The good parts and the theft both. If people are going to inherit the songs, they should inherit the argument too."

Wren spoke then — the boy's own voice, small and clear in the god's vast quiet.

"He's crying," Wren said. "Not sad-crying. The other kind. The kind you do when someone brings you soup when you thought everyone forgot you were in the house."

And Lior, who had held herself together through a raid and a graveyard and a country that didn't exist, sat down on the chapel steps next to a nine-year-old boy and cried too, briefly and without dignity, while a god learned what it felt like to be remembered kindly.

* * *

Ossa accepted at dawn.

She did it formally, because she was Ossa, and forty years inside the Inquisition had taught her that the shape of a thing mattered as much as its substance. She stood before the sealed door with the three keys in a row on the altar cloth, and she spoke the old words of assent — guild words, liturgical words, words in Hollowtongue that Lior hadn't heard spoken aloud since Aldous died — and then she said, in plain Vessic, so there could be no mistake:

"On behalf of the god beneath, whose name was taken and whose hunger I helped guard for forty years without once asking what it was hunger *for* — I accept the heir's terms. The prayers of the poor will be answered justly or not at all. The ban on magic ends with the guild that caused it. The inheritance returns to its rightful owner, and the Door will need no keeper, because it will have no thief behind it." She looked at Lior. "Witnessed by the last cleanser of the archives, who is adding her own name to the list of debts."

"You weren't a thief," Lior said.

"I was a lock," Ossa said, "and I never once asked what I was locking. That's not innocence. That's just a smaller vault." She picked up the three keys and held them out. "They're yours to turn, heir. When you're ready. Not before. And Lior—" for a moment the flat archival gaze wavered, and underneath it was just an old woman, very tired, very much alone with what she knew. "Thank you for arguing. He was right. You do listen better that way."

* * *

They spent that day the way people spend the day before a thing that cannot be undone — badly, and warmly.

Ivet copied hymnals and made Lior sit for a portrait, "for the archive," he said, "because if you're going to dissolve an entire guild's worth of magic into a starving god, someone should draw you first, and I'm the only one here who can hold a pencil." The portrait was terrible. He gave it to her anyway, and she folded it into her pocket next to the luck-knot she'd never sold, the one from the first night, the mother's coin still knotted into it.

Wren taught Lior a game with knucklebones that the god apparently remembered from before it was a god — from when it had been something smaller, a river-spirit or a hearth-thing, the kind of god that plays games. Lior lost six times in a row and suspected divine cheating and said so, and Wren giggled and said, "He says a good craftsman blames her tools," which was such an Aldous thing to say that she had to leave the room for a moment.

And in the evening, Sarel Vane arrived.

She came down the chapel stairs still in her Inquisitor's coat, travel-worn, one hand bandaged from the road, and she stood in the doorway taking in the scene — the old woman, the cartographer, the child, the heir — with the expression of a woman who has walked a very long way to check on a fire and found everyone calmly roasting dinner over it.

"You're doing it, then," Sarel said. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Lior said.

Sarel nodded slowly. Then she reached into her coat and took out a folded sheaf of papers — worn at the creases, carried a long way, handled a great many times. Lior recognized it. The report. The truth of the Purge, written in an Inquisitor's careful hand, unsent.

"I've been carrying this for three hundred miles," Sarel said, "trying to decide what to do with it. If I send it up the hierarchy, it dies in a drawer. If I publish it, I die in a cell. And if I do nothing—" her jaw tightened, "—then everything I burned, everyone I questioned, everyone who was *right* while I was holding the torch — it stays a lie forever."

She looked at Lior, and then at the sealed door, and then at the papers in her hand.

"So I've decided," she said. "Tomorrow morning, before you turn the keys — I'm going to read it from the temple steps in Vess. All of it. To whoever will listen. Let them come for me while the truth is still in the air."

Lior stared at her. "Sarel. They'll burn you."

"Probably," Sarel said, and for the first time since Lior had known her, the Inquisitor smiled — small, tired, and absolutely unrepentant. "But I've read the archive, Eastwick. I know what my order actually saved everyone from. Nothing. We saved them from *nothing*, and charged them their gods for it. Somebody has to stand on the steps and say so." She tucked the report back inside her coat, against her heart, like a charm.

"And besides," she added, turning for the stairs, "if I'm going to be arrested by my own order, I'd rather do it the same week the magic ban dies. Let history at least get the *order* of events right."

She climbed out of the chapel into the dark, and Lior sat down on the steps beside Wren, and above them, faint and patient, the god beneath Errow waited for morning.
