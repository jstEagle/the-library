## 29. Sarel Reads the Truth Aloud

Sarel Vane had spent nineteen years learning how to stand in a room and make everyone in it afraid of her. She had not, until this morning, ever tried to make a room love her, and she discovered quickly that she had no talent for it whatsoever.

"You're pacing," said Tessin.

"I am reviewing my remarks."

"You're wearing a groove in the flagstones of your own cell." He sat on the cot she'd been given — the Inquisition's idea of hospitality for its own arrested members was a cot, a bucket, and a door that locked from the outside, which Sarel found darkly funny given everything else — and watched her with the patient amusement of a man who had already lost his shop, his license, and his freedom, and had decided that if the world insisted on taking things from him it could at least be entertaining about it. "The guards change at noon. If you mean to do something foolish, that's your window."

"I'm not doing anything foolish."

"Sarel." He said her name the way one might address a dog standing on a table. "You asked me to smuggle a message to Lior Eastwick through a child who speaks in dead languages. You gave me your badge to pass along to fugitives. You have been writing, by candlelight, on paper you stole from your own order's stores, a document you refuse to let me read. Whatever you are doing, it is either foolish or treasonous, and knowing you, I suspect it is both."

She stopped pacing. The cell was small — a cleanser's cell, deep in the archive wing, where they'd put her after the interrogation of Tessin had gone so strangely wrong. Wrong, in this case, meaning: she had opened the sealed archive against orders, read what was inside, and then stood in front of Tessin's chains and told him the truth, which he had received with the expression of a man being handed a bill he'd been expecting for twenty years.

"They'll burn me," she said. "If they find out what I know."

"Probably."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"It frightens me enormously," Tessin said cheerfully. "But I've noticed something about fear, these last few weeks in a cell. It gets smaller when you look at it directly. Like an eye infection. Or a god."

* * *

The document took her three nights to write, and she wrote it four times.

The first version was a report — proper, measured, addressed to the Grand Inquisitor, formatted in the approved style with numbered findings and citations. It read like a confession written by someone hoping to be forgiven. She burned it in the cell's little brazier and felt nothing but relief.

The second version was an accusation. It named names. It laid out, in cold archival prose, the truth she had pulled from the sealed vault: that the Purge had not destroyed the old gods; that the guild had sealed them beneath the earth; that the Inquisition's founding mandate — *guard the ash, lest anything rise from it* — had never been a victory cry but a prison roster. That every charm burned in every market square for two hundred years had been burned not because magic was dangerous, but because magic was *evidence*. Evidence that the prison still held, and that the prisoners were waking.

She burned that one too, but slower, and it hurt more, because it was true and because it was useless. A report to the Grand Inquisitor would go into a drawer. A report to the Grand Inquisitor would get her quietly disappeared, the way reports like that always had. She knew now why the archive's cleansers were chosen from among the most devout: devout people did not read what they were sent to destroy. They fed pages to the fire with clean hands and grateful hearts.

The third version she wrote for the citizens of Vess. Plain language. No citations. She imagined her mother reading it — her mother, who had knelt every morning of her life before the Ash Altar and thanked the Purge for keeping her children safe — and she tried to write something her mother could hear without closing her ears.

That version failed too. Too gentle. It apologized around the edges. It kept saying *we believed*, as though belief excused anything, as though her mother's prayers hadn't been fuel for a lie.

The fourth version she wrote in one sitting, starting at midnight, and when she finished it the bells were ringing dawn and her hand ached and she read it once and did not burn it.

It began:

*I am Inquisitor Second Rank Sarel Vane, and everything I have done for nineteen years was done in service of a lie.*

*Here is the lie. Here is where it came from. Here is who profited from it. And here is what I intend to do about it.*

* * *

Getting out of the cell was almost insultingly easy, which told her something depressing about the state of the Inquisition's discipline. Martial law had stretched everyone thin. The riots — the *Riots of the Answered*, the criers were calling them, though no one agreed on whether that meant riots caused by answered prayers or riots demanding more of them — had pulled half the garrison to the temple district. Her own arrest, such as it was, had been conducted by a boy of maybe twenty who had saluted her twice during the escort and once more through the closing door, out of pure habit.

Tessin picked the lock with a fever-ward pin he'd been keeping in his collar for exactly this purpose, which raised questions Sarel decided not to ask.

"You're coming with me?" she said.

"Absolutely not." He settled back onto the cot. "I'm going to sit here, very innocently, until someone notices my cell is empty of me and full of you. By then you'll be gone, and their confusion will buy you an hour."

"Tessin—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "You gave me your badge. You got Lior Eastwick out of my burning shop. You came to my cell and told me the thing I'd spent thirty years pretending I didn't suspect, and you told it to me *first*, before anyone else, because you thought I deserved to hear it from someone who wasn't holding a torch." His voice did something complicated on the last words, and he covered it with a shrug. "Let an old man commit one act of loyalty. It's the only luxury left in this city that isn't taxed."

She gripped his shoulder. He patted her hand twice, briskly, like a man shooing a goose, and turned to face the wall.

* * *

The temple steps of the Ash Altar were the largest public space in Vess — nine tiers of white stone rising above the plaza, built, according to the founding inscriptions, *in triumph over the silenced gods*. Every citizen passed them eventually. Every execution of the Purge era had been held there. Sarel had stood on those steps at seven years old, holding her mother's hand, watching a charm-seller kneel at the top while an Inquisitor read the charges, and she had felt the rightness of it settle into her bones like cold.

She climbed them at midday, alone, in full uniform, carrying a leather folio, and the crowd noise dipped and rippled the way water does when a large fish passes under it.

People recognized her. That was the strange part. Nineteen years of market inspections and confiscations and quiet interrogations had made her face known across six districts — known the way weather is known, or taxes. She saw mothers pull children closer. She saw a charm-seller — one of hers, one she'd cited twice last spring — go pale and start backing toward an alley.

And she saw, scattered through the crowd, the hollows. The prayer-burns. Scorched circles in the cobblestones and the plaster walls, dozens of them now, some fresh-edged and black, some weeks old and swept clean but never quite gone. The poor had taken to leaving flowers in some of them. One near the fountain had a whole shrine going: candles, a chipped cup of milk, a child's boot arranged with great care. Nobody official dared remove it anymore. There weren't enough officials left who believed in anything strongly enough to fight a crowd over a cup of milk.

She reached the seventh tier, where the Reading Stone stood — the lectern from which all public charges were proclaimed — and she set the folio down on it, and she waited.

The crowd didn't hush for her. Why would it? But a hush arrived anyway, the way it does, moving inward from the edges, drawn by the shape of a person standing still in a place built for proclamations. Somewhere a garrison horn sounded, distant, and was ignored.

"My name," she said, and her voice carried better than she expected, trained by years of market-square commands, "is Sarel Vane. I am — I was — an Inquisitor of the Second Rank. I have served the Order for nineteen years. I have burned charms. I have arrested healers. I have made a widow in the Tanner's Quarter watch me feed her husband's luck-knot into a fire, because the law required her to witness it, and I required her tears to prove her loyalty."

A murmur. Not hostile yet. Curious. Crowds are fair-minded creatures at the start; they give you rope and watch what you build with it.

"This morning I am going to read to you from the sealed archives of this Order. I am going to read you the account of the Purge, written by the Inquisitors who conducted it. Not the hymn. Not the lesson they teach children. The record." She lifted the folio. "Some of you will want to kill me for it. I ask only that you wait until I finish. I've earned at least that much rope."

Someone laughed — a short, startled bark — and the tension broke just enough.

She opened the folio and began.

* * *

She read for the better part of an hour.

She read the founding charter of the Inquisition, and let the crowd hear the phrase its authors had buried in clause eleven: *to maintain the seal upon that which the Guild of the Hollow Door has bound beneath the earth, until such time as the binding fails or is renewed.* She read the correspondence between the first Grand Inquisitor and the guild's surviving elders — letters that made it plain the two institutions had not been enemies but partners, jailers dividing shifts. She read the inventory of what had actually been destroyed in the Purge's first decade: not gods, not demons. Records. Names. Songs. The liturgical tongue itself, hunted nearly to extinction, so that no one living could read the binding-oaths or understand what was being kept, or why.

She read the passage that had undone her, the night she found it, sitting alone in the archive with a lamp:

*The people must never learn that the gods live. Let them believe we killed heaven. A grieving city prays to nothing and is safe. A hopeful city prays to something, and wakes it.*

Her voice caught on that line. She let it catch. She had learned, watching Lior Eastwick walk away from her at the graveyard wall, that a certain kind of honesty only works if it costs you visibly.

When she looked up from the page, the plaza had changed shape entirely. The crowd had grown — doubled, tripled, spilling off the steps and into the side streets, shopkeepers standing on their counters, a fruit-seller holding up her baby so the child could see. And the faces looking up at her were not angry.

They were hungry. That was the word that came to her, and it frightened her more than anger would have, because she understood it. Nineteen years she had mistaken the city's silence for peace. It had never been peace. It had been a hunger, held politely, generation after generation, the way you hold your breath.

"The ban on magic," she said, "was never protection. It was a lid. And the men who forged it knew — they wrote it down, signed it, sealed it, and built this altar on top of it so that no one would ever dig." She closed the folio. "I spent my life enforcing that lid. I believed. Belief was easier than reading. I am not asking forgiveness. I am telling you what I know, because the woman who taught me otherwise — a fugitive, a guild apprentice, a girl I hunted across half of Vess — she gave me the truth when she had every reason to keep it, and the only payment she asked was that it be spoken aloud."

She stepped back from the Reading Stone.

"So. Here it is, spoken aloud. Do with it what you will. The Inquisition's authority rests on a lie, and I am finished upholding it, and I would suggest—" and here, despite everything, despite the garrison horns sounding again, closer now, despite the column of white-cloaked figures emerging at the far end of the plaza, she smiled, "—that you all start praying loudly, and see what answers."

* * *

They came up the steps at a jog — six of them, then ten, Captain Doss in the lead, a man she'd shared a hundred dull suppers with, whose face now wore the particular misery of a dog ordered to bite its own kennel-mate.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said. "You are under arrest for sedition, theft of sealed materials, and—" he glanced at the folio, at the crowd, at the sheer unmanageable size of what was happening, and visibly gave up on the list, "—and this."

"Yes," Sarel said.

The crowd surged forward and Doss's soldiers, bless their exhausted hearts, formed a ring around her not to drag her off but to shield her from the citizens, several of whom were shouting her name in tones of alarming devotion. Someone threw a flower. It hit Doss in the ear.

"Walk," Doss muttered. "Please. Quickly. Before they decide to rescue you, because if this mob carries you off on their shoulders, the Grand Inquisitor will have my rank, and I have a daughter."

Sarel walked. At the bottom of the steps she paused, turned, and looked back once — at the Reading Stone, at the folio already passing hand to hand through the crowd like something holy, at the flower lying on the stone where she'd stood.

Nineteen years building a cage. One hour to open it. And the strange thing, the thing she would carry with her through everything that came after, was how light the stairs had felt going down.

They took her badge at the gate. They took her cloak. They put her in a real cell this time, deep under the temple, with a door that locked from both sides, and through the stone overhead she could hear, faintly, muffled but unmistakable, the sound of the city singing.

Not the hymns of the Ash Altar. Something older. Something with a melody she almost recognized — a binding-song, a guild-song, the kind of thing that had been illegal to hum for two hundred years and was suddenly, everywhere, on every tongue.

She lay back on the cot and laughed until the guard outside knocked and asked, uneasily, if she was well.

"No," she called back. "But I'm getting there."

And far to the east, though she couldn't have known it, a girl with a stolen inheritance was already turning toward Vess — because somewhere in that singing city, behind a door that locked from both sides, the last ally she never expected had just paid for the truth with her freedom, and debts like that, in the Hollow Guild, were always collected in person.
