## 30. Breaking Sarel Out

The cells beneath the Inquisition's citadel were built to hold witches, which meant they were built by people who had never actually met one. That was Lior's first observation as she crouched in the shadow of a drainage grate three streets from the citadel wall, watching the guard rotations with Ivet beside her and Wren asleep against her knee.

Her second observation was that the guards were terrified.

They kept touching their belt-knives the way sailors touch charms before a storm — quick, compulsive, useless. Above them, Vess burned with answered prayers. The temple district glowed orange through the smoke-haze, and every few minutes another hollow bloomed somewhere in the city: a scorched circle in a bakery wall where a widow had asked for her husband's pension; a black ring on a fountain's rim where a child had wished for rain. The god under the city was awake and generous and starving all at once, feeding itself on hope like a fire feeding on oil.

"They've tripled the watch since the riots," Ivet murmured. He had his map-satchel across his chest out of habit, though there was nothing left to map. "And doubled the locks. And — is that a second portcullis?"

"That's new," Lior agreed.

"Your master ever break into this place?"

"Aldous once got us *out* of it." She remembered it too well: fourteen years old, her wrists raw from manacles, Aldous walking them past four checkpoints on nothing but a stolen uniform, a forged writ, and a confidence so complete that the guards had saluted him. "He always said the Inquisition's walls were only strong because everyone believed they were."

"And do you believe it?"

Lior watched a patrol pass, lanterns swinging, boots splashing through gutter-water gone pink with ash. "I believe Sarel does. And right now she's sitting in her own cells waiting for them to decide she's a witch. So no. Tonight I don't."

Ivet was quiet for a moment. "You said you'd sworn off the real magic."

"I did."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to be very sorry about it later." She pulled up her sleeve, exposing the guild sigil inked into the skin of her forearm — the hollow circle, the nine spokes, faded almost to nothing after years of hiding it under wool and lies. "There are exactly two kinds of guild magic left in me, Ivet. The small kind, the charm-work, the luck-knots and fever-wards. That kind costs effort. And then there's the other kind. The inheritance kind. It doesn't cost effort."

Wren stirred against her knee, half-waking. "It costs *you*," the boy said, in his sleep-rough voice that sometimes wasn't entirely his own. "Aldous says—"

"Don't tell me what Aldous says tonight," Lior said gently. "Tonight I'd only argue with him."

* * *

They went in through the drainage system, because of course they did. Every old building in Vess had been built on top of older buildings, and the citadel was no exception — its foundations were laid over the bones of a pre-Purge chapel whose crypts still drained into the river. Ivet had mapped it years ago, back when mapping erased places was merely a hobby and not a calling.

The tunnels smelled of river-mud and old stone and something else underneath: a faint sweetness, like incense burned down to its last curl. Lior felt it against the sigil on her arm first, a warmth like a hand laid flat over the mark.

"She knows we're here," Wren whispered. He was wide awake now, and his eyes had that distant shine that Lior had learned to dread. "Not Sarel. The other one. She's listening through the stones."

"Then let's give her something worth hearing," Lior muttered, and pressed her palm flat against the tunnel wall. "We're coming for the prisoner in cell nine. Don't help."

The sweetness deepened, which Lior chose to interpret as agreement rather than appetite.

They came up through the crypt into a wine cellar that hadn't held wine since the Purge, and from there into a corridor where the plaster was scored with decades of tally marks — prisoners counting days that mostly didn't end well. Two guards at the stairhead, playing dice by lantern-light, both of them flinching at every distant shout from the streets above.

Lior could have waited. Could have found the patient way, the charm-work way, the way that cost nothing but time. But time was the one thing the city didn't have. Somewhere above them, the Inquisition's council was deciding what to do with an Inquisitor who had read forbidden archives aloud, pocketed unsent reports, and handed her badge to fugitives at a graveyard wall. They would not deliberate long. Orders like those were carried out at dawn, and dawn was perhaps two hours off.

So instead Lior did the thing she had sworn off, the thing she had promised a dying man she'd never touch again.

She reached into the sigil.

The guild's real magic was not like charm-work. Charm-work was sewing: patience and thread and small clever knots. This was more like opening a door in herself that she had nailed shut at seventeen, and behind the door was everything Aldous had poured into her apprenticeship — the hoarded inheritance of a guild that had stolen its power from gods and paid for it with its own existence. It didn't ask her permission. It simply rose, warm and vast and *hungry*, and waited to see what shape she would give it.

"*Quietly,*" she told it, through gritted teeth, and gave it the shape of forgetting.

That was the trade. That was the price written into the inheritance's fine print, the part Aldous had made her memorize and swear to: *the guild's power runs on memory, Lior. Every great working spends a piece of the caster. Small spells spend small things — a name of a street, the taste of a meal. Great workings spend great things. Never cast great workings. Promise me.*

She had promised. She was about to break it, and she knew roughly what it would take: a working strong enough to unmake iron locks and bend two men's attention elsewhere needed something load-bearing. Not a street name. Not a meal. Something structural. Something from the foundation of who she was.

She chose childhood.

It seemed fair, somehow. She was doing this so that a child in the present could keep his mother, and a woman who had chosen truth could keep her life. Childhood was the coin she had that matched. And she told herself — standing in the dark with the magic rising in her like water in a locked cellar — that she wouldn't miss what she couldn't remember. That was the arithmetic of sacrifice. Everyone in the guild's history had done this math wrong, but surely, surely, she could do it right.

"*Go,*" she breathed, and let it go.

* * *

Upstairs, the two guards at the stairhead were mid-game when the dice stopped feeling like dice.

Corporal Hesk — who had been winning, which was unusual, and should have been his first warning — looked down at the bone squares in his palm and realized he could not remember learning to play. Not just this game. Any game. His hands knew how to throw them; the knowledge behind his hands had been scooped out like pulp from fruit. He looked up at his partner and saw the same blankness arriving there, and both men experienced, simultaneously and without any explanation available to them, the absolute certainty that the stairwell behind them urgently required their attention elsewhere. They left. They did not question it. There was nothing in them left to question with.

Below, in the crypt-dark, Lior sat down hard against a wine rack as the working tore loose from her.

Memory didn't leave cleanly. It left like ice leaving a pond — cracking, groaning, going all at once. She felt the edges of it as it went: her mother's kitchen, gone. The smell of the tannery row where she'd grown up, gone. A dog — there had been a dog, she was nearly sure there had been a dog, some shaggy traitor who'd followed her everywhere — the dog was last, and it fought, and losing it hurt worse than all the rest together. She heard herself make a sound, small and animal, and Ivet's arms came around her shoulders, and Wren pressed close against her side, and she held onto them both because suddenly they were most of what she had.

"What did it take?" Ivet asked quietly. "What's missing?"

"I don't know," Lior said, and started laughing despite everything, wet and cracked. "That's rather the point. I have a hole shaped like being seven years old. I can feel the shape of it. I just can't fill it."

"The dog," Wren said softly. His eyes were shining again. "There was a dog. Brown. Bad ears."

"Don't tell me—" She stopped. Pressed her palms to her eyes. "No. Tell me. If you know it, tell me everything. I'd rather have it secondhand than not at all."

"Brown," Wren repeated. "Bad ears. Bit a tax collector once. You loved him."

"Good," Lior said. Her voice shook. "That's — good enough. Secondhand dog." She got her feet under her. The hole inside her was enormous and quiet, a cleared lot where a house used to stand, and around its edges she could feel the strangest lightness, as if grief and relief had gotten tangled together and refused to separate. She had paid for survival with herself, and the self that remained was lighter and stranger and would have to be enough.

"*Come on,*" she said. "Sarel's waiting. And so is dawn."

* * *

Cell nine was at the end of a corridor lined with wards — old ones, Purge-era, carved into the doorframes in Hollowtongue script. Lior stopped in front of them and felt her stomach drop, because she recognized the hand.

"These are Aldous's," she whispered. "He carved these. Before. When he still believed in them." The irony sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: the man who had taught her the magic now stood between her and using it, in the form of wards designed to smother it.

"He'd want you to go through them anyway," Wren said.

"You don't know that."

"I'm standing next to him right now, Lior. I know that."

She put her hand against the ward-script and pushed the inheritance through it — carefully this time, threading it rather than pouring it, spending a little here and a little there. A memory of learning the alphabet went. A memory of her first fever-ward, the pride of it, gone. Each scrap bought passage through a line of carving, and each loss was smaller than the childhood she'd already spent, but they added up, and by the time she reached the last ward-line she was breathing hard and her eyes were streaming and she genuinely could not say whether it was the magic or the crying.

The lock to cell nine was iron and honest and no match for a guild heir in a hurry. It opened like a flower.

Sarel Vane sat on the cell's cot with her back straight and her hands folded, as if she'd been expecting a tea visitor rather than a rescue. She looked terrible — three days in a cell, a bruise along her jaw from the arrest — and she looked, simultaneously, entirely unsurprised.

"You're late," Sarel said.

"Dawn's in an hour. We're early." Lior leaned on the doorframe, catching her breath. "Also, hello. Nice cell. Very atmospheric tally marks."

Sarel stood, and paused, and studied Lior with that awful Inquisitorial attention that used to make Lior feel like a specimen. Except it was different now, and they both knew it. "Something's different about you," Sarel said slowly. "You're — lighter. What did it cost?"

"Does it matter?"

"Yes," Sarel said. "It matters enormously. I've spent my career making people pay prices I never counted. I'd like to start counting."

Lior held her gaze. The old resentment was still there, banked coals of it — this woman had burned her master's tools, hunted her friends, built the machine that ate her world. But the woman standing in front of her had also opened sealed archives, pocketed the truth, and given away her badge in a graveyard. Debts ran both directions now, and neither of them could pretend otherwise.

"My childhood," Lior said finally. "Most of it. There was a dog. I'm told he bit a tax collector."

Something moved through Sarel's face — not quite guilt, not quite grief, something that lived in the territory between. "I'll carry that," she said. "However I can. Consider it entered in my ledger."

"You keep a ledger now?"

"I've always kept a ledger," Sarel said. "For the first time it's honest." She stepped out of the cell, rolled her shoulders, and surveyed the corridor with a professional eye that was almost funny coming from a prisoner. "Two guards at the stairhead. Portcullis—"

"Asleep at their posts, or soon will be. And the portcullis is handled. Mostly. Ivet has opinions about drainage."

"Of course he does." Sarel hesitated, then reached to her belt — empty of badge, but not of everything — and drew out a slim knife, offering it hilt-first. "You'll want this. The council posted a kill-order on all of you at sundown. Not capture. Kill."

"Charming." Lior took the knife. Then, because the moment demanded it and because gentle humor was the only armor she had left: "Any chance the kill-order specifies methods? I'd hate to die inelegantly."

"Lior." Sarel's voice was low and very serious. "Thank you isn't sufficient. I know that. But — thank you. You spent yourself to buy a stranger out of a cage. Your guild taught you better than that."

"No," Lior said. "My guild taught me the opposite. Everything they hoarded, everything they stole, every ward and seal and key — it was all about keeping. Keeping power, keeping doors shut, keeping accounts." She touched the faded sigil on her arm. "Spending it on someone else is the first thing I've ever done that they didn't teach me. Maybe that's the real inheritance. Maybe it skipped a few generations and landed on me sideways."

Behind her, Wren giggled, and for half a heartbeat the voice in the giggle was an old man's, warm and rueful, and Lior chose — generously, deliberately — to believe it was approval.

* * *

They went out through the crypt and the drains and the long dark throat of the city's buried history, and if the stones listened, they let them pass. At the mouth of the drainage grate, where the river-smell gave way to ash-smell and the burning sky of Vess arched overhead, Sarel stopped and turned back toward the citadel — toward the towers where she had spent twenty years, where her report sat unsent in a superior's drawer, where her order was even now deciding whether truth was treason.

"We go east," Lior said. "To Errow. To the Door. Ossa's waiting, and Ivet's maps, and a priestess who calls me heir, and a village that's half ready to tear itself apart over whether I open that Door or seal it forever. It's — honestly, it's a mess. You could stay. Try to fix your order from inside. Someone should."

Sarel was quiet a long moment, watching the fires.

"My order is a man with a leaking roof, insisting the rain isn't real," she said at last. "I've tried fixing it from inside for twenty years. I'd rather walk east and see what's actually under the world." She glanced sideways at Lior, and there — briefly, astonishingly — was the ghost of a smile. "Besides. You spent your childhood getting me out. The least I can do is walk with you and describe it back to you whenever you ask. The dog. The tax collector. All of it."

Lior laughed, and it hurt, and it helped.

Then Wren went rigid at her side, his small hand snapping around hers, his eyes flooding silver-white — and from everywhere and nowhere, through the boy's mouth and the burning stones and the ash-choked air, a voice that was half Aldous and half something far older spoke a single sentence that emptied the street of warmth:

"*They have found the third key. Ossa is taken. Come quickly, heir — come before Errow learns what the Inquisition does with elders.*"

The fires crackled. The river ran. And Lior Eastwick, lighter than she had ever been and poorer than she had ever known, turned east and began to run.
