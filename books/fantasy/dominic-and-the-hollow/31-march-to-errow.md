## 31. March to Errow

### Lior

The road east of Vess had never been wide enough for this.

Lior stood on the crest of a hill at dawn and watched the road fill. Not an army — armies were tidier. This was a river of people: farmers with their chickens in wicker baskets, a cooper hauling his tools on a handcart, three sisters from the tannery district walking barefoot because they'd given their shoes to a woman whose baby wouldn't stop crying. Somewhere behind them all, someone was singing. Badly. The song carried up the hill anyway, threadbare and stubborn, one of the old hymns that hadn't been sung aloud in Vess for twenty years — not properly, not in daylight.

"They're following us," Ivet said beside her. He said it the way he reported weather. "Specifically, they are following *you*. Or possibly the child. Or possibly a rumor of a door that most of them couldn't find if you drew it on their palms."

"Thank you, Ivet."

"You looked like you wanted comfort. I wanted to be accurate first. Accuracy is my form of affection."

She almost smiled. Almost. Behind her, Wren was asleep against Ossa's pack, his mouth open, drooling slightly onto the elder's spare cloak — a boy who spoke the liturgical tongue of a dying god in his sleep and woke up complaining about porridge. It was hard to be afraid while watching that. Lior made herself look anyway, because being afraid was part of the job now. She was the heir of the Hollow Guild. Fear came with the sigil.

"How many?" she asked.

Ivet squinted down the road. He had a cartographer's eye; he counted things the way other men breathed. "Behind us? Four hundred. Perhaps five hundred since yesterday. Ahead—" he nodded east, toward the hills where Errow hid itself off every map he'd ever been paid to make, "—more. They'll converge. Rumor walks faster than we do."

"And when we get there?"

"Then five hundred people will stand in front of a chapel in a village that officially does not exist," Ivet said, "and wait for a girl with a burnt-out sigil to open a door."

Lior watched the sun come up over the river of strangers and thought about her master's voice saying *the Door must be found*, and about how no one had ever mentioned what to do once it was found. Aldous had been many things — brilliant, kind, secretive as a locked drawer — but he had never once finished explaining anything. She suspected it was a guild tradition.

"We should walk," she said. "Ossa wants to reach the ridge before noon, and Ossa has opinions about ridges."

"Ossa has opinions about everything. It's why I like her. She's the only person here who argues like a proper academic." Ivet shouldered his map case. "Come on, heir. Your nation is waiting."

"It's not my nation."

"No," he agreed cheerfully. "But it's following you anyway. That's generally how nations start."

### Ivet

Ivet kept two maps in his head these days, and only one of them was drawn.

The drawn one showed the road to Errow: the ford at Millbeck, the burned waystation, the ridge road where the pines grew crooked from wind nobody had bothered to name. He'd mapped it twice already in his life — once for the Survey Office before his exile, once for smugglers after — and both times he had carefully left a blank space where the village sat, because that was the job. You erased what they told you to erase. He had erased so much of the country that some nights he wondered whether there was any of it left under his ink.

The second map was in his head, and it was getting crowded.

It held four hundred people, and growing. It held the exact spot where the cooper's cart axle cracked outside Millbeck and six strangers rebuilt it in an hour because no one wanted to leave him behind. It held the well at the burned waystation where Sarel Vane — former Inquisitor, current pariah, current *companion*, which still made Ivet's skin prickle in an interesting way — drew water for strangers who flinched from her face until they saw her hands, which were shaking too badly to work the bucket rope, and then didn't flinch anymore. It held the moment, two nights ago, when a farmer asked Ossa to bless his sick cow and Ossa, who had spent thirty years cleansing archives for the Inquisition, blessed the cow with the solemnity of a coronation and then spent the next mile muttering that she had absolutely no idea whether that worked.

That one went in the second map in gold ink.

He fell into step beside Sarel, who walked at the rear of the column like a rearguard out of habit. She still wore her Inquisitor's coat. She'd cut the badges off herself, carefully, with a knife, on the night they freed her from the cells — and then folded the coat instead of burning it, which told Ivet more about her than the badge-cutting did.

"You keep checking the horizon," he said.

"I keep checking the horizon."

"For riders?"

"For everyone." She glanced back down the long slope of road. The crowd stretched behind them like spilled grain. "Ivet. Do you understand what happens if the Inquisition decides we're leading an insurrection?"

"I suspect we'd be an insurrection very briefly."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't entirely a joke." He let a few steps pass. "Sarel. Look at them. Half of them don't know why they're walking. A man asked me yesterday if we were going to a festival."

"Aren't we?"

"I genuinely don't know. That may be the truest thing anyone's said on this road." He adjusted his map case. "They're not following a rebellion. Rebellions have banners. These people are following a rumor of a door, and doors mean something different than banners. A banner says *follow me*. A door says *there is another side*. Do you see the difference?"

Sarel was quiet for a while. The road creaked and murmured around them; somewhere ahead a child was learning to whistle, badly, with encouragement from three adults who were worse.

"I spent eleven years burning doors shut," she said finally. "Metaphorically. Mostly literally, actually. We sealed shrines. We bricked up prayer-niches. There was a chapel in the Tanner's Quarter we filled with quicklime." Her jaw worked. "And every single time, I believed I was protecting them. All of them. Everyone on this road." She looked at the crowd with an expression Ivet had never learned to map — grief and vertigo wearing each other's clothes. "Now they're all walking east because someone told them a door exists, and I can't decide if I've failed them or finally started helping."

"Both," Ivet said. "Frequently at the same time. Welcome to the company."

Ahead, Wren had woken up and was demanding breakfast from Ossa with the imperiousness of an emperor. Ossa was pretending to resist. Neither fooling anyone.

"One more thing," Ivet added, because accuracy demanded it. "The horizon. I checked it this morning while you slept. There are riders. Three. Far back, moving fast, Inquisitorial gray. They're not gaining yet."

Sarel's face went very still and very calm, the way stone is calm.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

### Wren

Wren had decided that being a compass was mostly boring.

Everyone treated him like he might shatter. Ossa fed him first at every meal. Lior checked his forehead for fever every morning and evening like he was still that feverish boy in a night-market hovel, even though he'd told her — twice — that the fever broke months ago and what was inside him now wasn't sick, exactly. It was more like a draft coming under a door. Cold, sometimes. Patient.

And loud, when it wanted to be.

Today it was quiet, though, so Wren got to do the good parts of marching: walking near the front, listening to the farmer-sisters argue about whether hens could count, and eating the honey-bread a baker had pressed on him in exchange for nothing, just because he was the boy from the stories. There were stories now. He'd heard two versions. In one, he'd died of fever and been sent back by the old god as a messenger. In the other, he'd never been sick at all and the whole thing was a trick by smugglers. Both versions agreed he had very nice hair, which Wren felt was the only accurate detail.

He was licking honey off his thumb when the draft under the door stirred.

It didn't hurt. That was the strange part people wouldn't believe. It felt like standing in a huge empty house and hearing, far off, someone humming a tune you half-remembered from childhood. The tune today was the binding hymn — the one Lior sang at the well in Errow, the one that pulled him out of the crowd's drowning fervor. Except the god hummed it wrong. Slow. Longing. Like someone who'd heard it through a wall for twenty years and never been taught the words.

*You know them,* Wren thought at it, the way he'd learned to. *You just can't say them.*

The humming paused. Then, faintly, amused: a feeling that was almost words — *almost* — and Wren laughed out loud, right in the middle of the road, startling the hen-sisters.

"What?" said the eldest sister.

"He thinks the god is funny," Wren said.

All three sisters stopped walking. One of them grabbed the other's arm. Word traveled backward through the column like fire through dry grass — *the boy talked to it, the boy talked to it* — and within a minute people were pressing close, asking questions, holding out charms and letters and a shoe, for some reason, one single shoe, as if the god did cobbling.

Wren stood in the middle of it, honey on his chin, and felt the cold draft curl around him almost apologetically.

*I didn't mean to gather a crowd,* it seemed to say.

*You never do,* Wren thought back. *You're bad at being small.*

The feeling it gave him then was so enormous and so sad that he had to sit down on the verge for a moment, and Lior came pushing through the crowd with her hand already glowing faintly at the knuckles — guild-light, the last of her power, hoarded like the last coins of a bankrupt kingdom — and Ossa came behind her saying *give the boy air, give him AIR, you people are worse than archivists* — and Wren waved them off, laughing again, because honestly.

Honestly. Five hundred people, a haunted boy, a disgraced inquisitor, a mapmaker of nowhere, an archive-cleaner turned cow-blesser, and a god that hummed.

His mother would never have believed it. His mother had sold fever-wards. Well — bought them. From Lior. Which meant, if you followed the thread far enough back, that all of this — the road, the crowd, the door, the whole unwritten country ahead — had started with one charm that shouldn't have worked.

Wren licked the last of the honey off his thumb and got up.

"Keep moving!" he shouted at the crowd, in a voice he absolutely had not learned from Ossa. "We're not there yet!"

And the whole column laughed, and moved, and the god hummed its broken hymn, and the road went on east.

### Ossa

Ossa had buried three careers in her life, and she was beginning to suspect she was about to bury a fourth, along with possibly the entire institutional memory of the Inquisition, several centuries of theology, and — if the mood struck her — organized religion as a concept.

She found the prospect surprisingly restful.

Her knees objected to the march. Her knees had objected to everything since she was fifty, and she was seventy-one now, and she had long ago negotiated a settlement with them: she ignored them, and they complained, and neither party escalated. She walked at the head of the column beside Lior Eastwick, last apprentice, unlikeliest heir, a girl who had once tried to pick Ossa's pocket in an archive reading room and been caught by the simple expedient of Ossa grabbing her wrist without looking up from her book.

"You're limping," Lior said.

"I am *pacing myself*. There's a difference, and it's dignity."

"You're limping with dignity, then."

"All limping is dignified if committed to fully." Ossa shifted her pack. Below them, the valley opened toward the ridge road, and the crowd spread across it like a spilled basket of yarn. "Girl. A question, and I want your honest answer, because I'm too old for reassurance and too tired for lies."

Lior's shoulders did the thing they always did — squared, wary. Twenty-two years old and built like a drawbridge. "Ask."

"When you were a child — before the guild, before Aldous — did you pray? Properly. To one of the old ones."

Lior considered it. She had that quality Ossa privately treasured, rare in the young and rarer in heroes: she actually thought about questions instead of performing answers.

"My mother prayed to Hollow-Mother," she said at last. "For rain, mostly. For my father to stop drinking, which never worked, and rain, which usually did. I used to think that proved the god liked weather better than people." A pause. "I prayed once. When I was eight. I prayed that my mother would get well." Her voice went flat and careful. "She didn't. So no. After that I stopped asking the sky for things. I started making charms instead. Small ones. Things that fit in a pocket. Things that couldn't promise more than they could give."

"And now?"

"Now I'm walking five hundred people to a door I intend to open, knowing full well what it costs, having promised a god I'd pay it." Lior smiled, and it was the old night-market smile, the one she used to sell luck-knots to people who couldn't afford luck. "So apparently I've learned nothing."

"On the contrary," Ossa said, "you've learned the single hardest lesson in theology, decades earlier than most theologians manage it. You've learned that prayer isn't asking the sky for things." She nodded back at the column — at the cooper, the sisters, the woman with the baby, all of them trudging east on rumor and hope. "It's deciding what you're willing to owe."

Ahead, the ridge rose against the afternoon, pine-dark, and beyond it — though no map admitted it, though no signpost confessed it, though the road itself seemed to grow shy and narrow as it approached — Errow waited, with its well and its chapel and its priestess who called Lior *heir* in a voice that expected obedience.

And behind them, far back on the road, three points of Inquisitorial gray had gained perhaps half a mile since morning.

Ossa noted them without alarm. Alarm was for people with time.

"Well," she said briskly. "Let's go meet our congregation. And girl — when the shouting starts, and it will — try to remember that I warned you about the half that wants the Door destroyed. I intend to be insufferable about it afterward."

"You're already insufferable."

"Yes, but I intend to be *right* as well. It's a rare vintage."

### Sarel

On the third night, Sarel took the watch alone, as she always did, because the others pretended to offer and she pretended not to notice their relief.

She sat on a fallen wall above the camp — five hundred fires scattered down the valley now, more than she could count, a city that moved — and did what she had done every night since the cells: she read her own report.

She'd written it seven times. The first version had been careful, hedged, addressed to her superiors with the correct forms. The second was less careful. By the fifth, she'd stopped addressing anyone at all, and the report had become simply the truth, laid out in her precise, clerk-trained hand: *the Purge killed no gods. The guild sealed them. We were guards who mistook ourselves for executioners.* Each version was shorter than the last. Truth, she was discovering, edited well.

Tomorrow they crossed the ridge. Tomorrow, Errow.

Behind her, boots on scree. She knew the tread — light, deliberate, the walk of someone raised to move unheard and who had mostly stopped bothering. Lior sat down on the wall a respectful arm's length away and said nothing for a while, which Sarel appreciated. Most people filled silence like it was a debt they owed.

"The riders stopped at dusk," Lior said eventually. "Made camp at Millbeck. They'll reach the ridge by midday tomorrow."

"I know. I counted their fires."

"Of course you did." A pause. "Are you going to tell me what you're going to do about it?"

"I'm going to stand in front of them," Sarel said, "wearing this coat with the badges cut off, and I'm going to show them my orders — the real ones, the sealed archive access, Ossa's testimony — and I'm going to ask them which law they serve. And then either they'll listen, or they won't."

"And if they won't?"

"Then I'll be arrested by my own order for the second time in a month, and frankly, the repetition is starting to feel undignified." Sarel folded the report closed. Down in the valley, someone's fire sparked, and a scatter of laughter drifted up — actual laughter, on a road full of refugees. "You should sleep, Eastwick. You open a door tomorrow."

"*We* open a door tomorrow."

"No," Sarel said quietly. "Five hundred people open a thousand doors, someday, because of what happens at that chapel. But the one that matters — the one that costs — opens under your hand. I've spent my life watching other people pay for my certainties. I know the shape of it from the outside." She looked at the girl beside her — the thief, the charm-seller, the heir, the child of a dead woman's unanswered prayer — and found, to her enduring surprise, that what she felt was trust. Earned the hard way. Paid for in memories and arrests and burnt shops. "Whatever's on the other side of that door will try to make it easy for you. It offered me my faith back, in the graveyard. My whole life returned, justified, clean. I want you to hear that from me before you hear it from *it*: the ease is the trap."

Lior was silent a long moment. When she answered, her voice had the night-market warmth in it, the gentle humor she used like armor, except tonight it wasn't armor. Tonight it sounded like gratitude.

"You know," she said, "Aldous used to say something like that. 'Nothing worth carrying is light.'"

"He sounds like he was a wise man."

"He sounds like he was a liar who occasionally told the truth by accident. But yes. Wise too, maybe. Both can be true." She slid off the wall, then paused. "Sarel. If the riders take you tomorrow—"

"They won't."

"If they do — I want you to know the company doesn't leave people behind. Not anymore. That rule's new. Tessin taught it to us the hard way." She hesitated, then, awkwardly, formally, held out her hand — the sigil-hand, the burnt-out one. "Goodnight, Inquisitor."

Sarel took it. The grip was warm and calloused and utterly without magic, and it was, she thought, the most honest thing anyone had offered her in eleven years.

"Goodnight, heir," she said. "Walk faster in the morning. I hate midday deadlines."

Below them the fires burned, hundreds of them, patient as stars. Above the ridge, past the pines, past the blank space on every map in the empire, Errow lay dark and waiting — and somewhere in its sleeping streets, in a chapel older than the Purge, Sarel would have sworn she could feel a door standing very still, the way a held breath stands still.

By midday, they would all learn why.
