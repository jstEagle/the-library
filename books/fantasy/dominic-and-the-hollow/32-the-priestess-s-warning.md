## 32. The Priestess's Warning

The morning after the banners appeared, Errow smelled of woodsmoke and cut apples.

Lior noticed the smell first because it was wrong. Villages under strain smelled of fear — sour sweat, boiled cabbage, the sharp ammonia of animals kept too close. Errow smelled like a harvest festival. Someone was baking. Someone had decided that whatever came next deserved honey cakes, and Lior found she couldn't decide whether that was faith or madness or simply what people did when they'd run out of other ways to be brave.

She stood in the doorway of the priestess's house with a cup of chicory going cold in her hands, watching the village square fill up below. The well sat at its center, old and squat, its stones worn smooth by three hundred years of rope and bucket. And on either side of it — hung from the shrine post and the tithe barn door like laundry nobody would claim — two banners.

The first was white, marked with an open door drawn in ochre. *Come in*, it seemed to say. *Everything you've lost is inside.*

The second was grey, marked with a single closed eye. *Sleep*, it said. *Let sleeping things lie.*

"Subtle as a hammer, my people," said Seryn from behind her.

The priestess of Errow was a small woman made mostly of elbows and certainty, with silver hair cropped close to her skull and hands that were never still — braiding, sorting, mending, always some small work between her fingers as though silence itself needed patching. She'd called Lior *heir* the first night and hadn't used her name since, which Lior was trying very hard not to find ominous.

"How many?" Lior asked.

"On which side?"

"Either."

Seryn came to stand beside her, working a length of blue thread through her fingers. "Forty for the Door, maybe fifty against. The rest are waiting to see which way the bread falls." She nodded at the square, where a woman was setting out loaves on trestle tables — one stack near the white banner, one near the grey. "My grandmother fed both sides of a feud for nine years that way. Nobody throws stones at the woman who feeds them."

"And you? Which side does the priestess stand on?"

Seryn's fingers paused on the thread. For the first time since Lior had known her, the little woman looked tired down to the bone.

"The priestess," she said, "is going to tell you something this morning that I should have told you the night you arrived. Come. Bring your cup if you must, but drink it walking."

* * *

The chapel beneath Errow was older than the village above it, older than anything Lior had seen outside the guildhall's sealed inner rooms. Its walls were not built but grown — pale stone ribbed like the inside of a shell, and everywhere, carved so deep the shadows themselves seemed carved, the sigils of the Hollow Guild.

Lior had spent her whole life hiding those marks beneath her sleeve. Here they covered every surface, worn smooth at shoulder height where generations of hands had touched them in passing, the way you'd touch a doorframe leaving home.

Ossa walked slowly among them, trailing her fingertips over the carvings like a woman reading letters from someone long dead. The old cleanser had barely slept since they'd arrived; Lior kept finding her here at odd hours, standing in the dark, mouthing words. Ivet had set up his drafting table in the corner and was drawing the vaulted ceiling by lamplight, because that was what Ivet did with wonder — he measured it, patiently, until it stopped being frightening.

Wren sat on the steps before the far wall, where the carvings gathered into a pattern Lior knew: the Door itself, rendered in stone, its three keyholes worn bright by touch.

The boy was humming.

He didn't know he was doing it. He'd been humming for two days — the same low phrase over and over, a melody that snagged in Lior's chest like a fishhook, because it was the second verse of the binding hymn, the part Aldous had never taught her. The part only elders knew. Wren had never learned it either. It was simply *in* him now, the way the god was in him, and every time he hummed it, the lamplight in the chapel guttered slightly, as if the flame were listening.

"Wren," Lior said gently.

He looked up. His eyes were clear this morning — Wren's own eyes, brown and eleven years old and tired of being a window. "I'm fine today. Mostly fine. It's quieter when there's bread." He shrugged, eleven years old again entirely. "I don't know why. It just likes the smell."

"Everything likes the smell of bread," said Ivet without looking up from his ceiling. "That is the most sensible thing a god has ever done."

Seryn led them to the center of the chapel, to a flat stone in the floor that Lior had taken for an altar. It wasn't. When the priestess pressed her palm to a worn hollow at its edge, the stone swung aside on a hinge older than memory, revealing a cavity lined in lead — the guild's trick, lead against listening — and inside lay a bundle of wax tablets, each one sealed with the sigil of an elder's hand.

"My predecessors' record," Seryn said. "Not the guild's. Ours." She drew out the topmost tablet, broke its seal with her thumb. "Errow has kept a covenant with the thing beneath this land for longer than Vess has existed. We do not worship it. We do not open to it. We *feed* it — a tithe, once a generation, freely given, freely taken. One voice. One keeper who walks in singing. That was the bargain, and for three hundred years it held." She turned the tablet toward the lamplight. Wax impressions, row upon row: names. "These are our keepers. My mother's mother is the fourth line from the top."

Lior's mouth had gone dry. "And now?"

"Now the covenant is breaking." Seryn set the tablet down with terrible care, as if it might bruise. "Because the god has stopped waiting to be fed. It has started *shopping*."

Ivet's pen stopped scratching.

"It whispers," Seryn went on. "Through wells. Through dreams. Through the ears of anyone who will listen, and grief makes such good ear-trumpets." Her voice flattened. "It came to Tam Brask last month, the cooper whose daughter drowned in the spring flood. Promised him the girl back. Whole. Warm. Laughing. All he had to do was swear the Door should open. Tam painted the white banner himself. He wept the whole time, and he believes — Lior, he *believes* — that somewhere past that Door his Marla is waiting, and that every day it stays shut is a day she waits alone."

"That's not how it works," Lior said. "What's behind the Door isn't — people don't—"

"I know what isn't behind the Door." Seryn's eyes, when they met Lior's, were fierce and wet. "You know what isn't behind the Door. But grief doesn't take instruction, child. Grief takes *offers*. And the thing under us has had three hundred years of practice making them."

Wren had gone quiet on the steps. Very quiet. Lior looked over and saw him sitting perfectly straight, hands folded, face smooth — and the lamplight bending toward him like grass toward wind.

"*Don't,*" Lior breathed, and crossed the floor, and took the boy's hands in hers. They were cold. "Wren. Stay with me. Say something about bread."

"Bread's good," he whispered. His eyes focused. "It's — it's loud right now. Sorry. It heard its own name." He swallowed. "Lior, it says — it says the lady is telling it true, but it says she left out the ending."

"What ending?"

Wren's voice dropped, and though it was still a boy's voice, something vast moved underneath it like a whale under a rowboat. "*The half that wants Me closed wants worse than closed.*"

The light steadied. Wren sagged, and Lior caught his shoulders and eased him back onto the step, and the whole chapel exhaled — or perhaps that was just everyone in it.

"What does that mean?" Ivet asked quietly, pen forgotten. "Closed is closed. What's worse than closed?"

Nobody answered, because the answer was already walking up the stairs.

* * *

They heard the crowd before they saw it — not shouting, not yet, but that particular dense murmur of many people deciding together, which is the most dangerous sound in any language. Lior reached the chapel's ground-floor door in time to see the square full.

Both banners had come down off their posts. People carried them now — the white held high by Tam Brask, huge and red-eyed, flanked by perhaps thirty neighbors; the grey borne by a cluster of farmers from the east fields, grim-faced, their leader a broad woman Lior recognized as the baker whose ovens had perfumed the morning. Between the two groups, the crowd churned and sorted itself, families visibly splitting, a boy holding his father's sleeve while pointing his chin at the white banner, an old man standing alone in the middle holding no banner at all, looking like a man at a funeral for something no one else believed was dead.

"They've been at it since dawn," said Ossa, arriving at Lior's shoulder with unsettling silence for a woman her age. "Arguing at the well. The well, of course. Where it whispers best."

"We have to talk to them," Lior said. "Explain what's actually behind the Door. Explain the covenant, the keys, the—"

"With what result?" Ossa's voice was dry as the archive dust she'd cleaned for forty years. "Child, I have watched crowds for a long life. A crowd is not convinced. A crowd is *caught*. You can pour truth into it like water into a sieve, and whichever side it finally tips toward, it will believe it thought of the idea itself." She folded her hands. "But go ahead. Speak. It's what heirs are for."

Lior descended the chapel steps into the noise.

It took a long moment for the square to notice her — a slim young woman in a patched traveling coat, sleeves pulled down over her wrists out of habit, though here in Errow there was no law to hide the sigil from. But then Tam Brask saw her, and pointed, and shouted something, and the sound rolled outward and came back changed:

*"The heir! The heir of the guild!"*

The white banner surged forward. The grey banner planted itself like a pike. And suddenly Lior stood at the center of a circle of several hundred faces, half lit with desperate hope, half dark with stubborn dread, and understood with perfect clarity that whatever she said next would be remembered as scripture by somebody.

She raised her hands. Waited. The murmuring thinned.

"People of Errow." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I've come a long way to open a Door. That much is true. But I need you to hear all of it, because the thing whispering to you — the thing that promised Tam Brask his daughter—"

"Don't you say her name!" Tam's voice cracked across the square. He pushed forward, the banner trembling on its pole. "You don't know her name. You weren't there when they pulled the water out of her. I prayed for nine days, girl. Nine days to every saint and none of them answered, and then Something answered, and you want me to hate It for that?"

"No," Lior said. "I want you to understand *what* answered."

"It said she's waiting!"

"It said what you needed to hear." She kept her voice low and even, the way Aldous had taught her to calm a spooked animal — or a customer who'd paid too much. "Tam. If Marla could speak to you herself — truly herself, not a promise wearing her shape — what would she tell you?"

For a moment, something flickered in the cooper's face — doubt, brief and agonized, a candle in a draft. Then he hardened it deliberately, the way a man nails a board over a broken window. "She'd say open the Door."

Behind him, half the square nodded. Behind *her*, the grey banner growled.

"You see?" said a voice at Lior's elbow — Seryn, pushing through to stand beside her, small and immovable. "This is what three weeks of whispering buys. Half my village would dig with their bare hands."

"Then let them hear the alternative," snapped the baker, hoisting the grey cloth higher. "Priestess, we love you, but your covenant fed one voice a generation while the rest of us got *nothing*. No rain-tithe answered. No fever-ward. Three hundred years of feeding a hungry thing scraps so it wouldn't eat the flock — and now it's hungry anyway and talking anyway, so tell me, what exactly has the covenant bought us?"

Silence. Real silence, the kind that has weight.

And Lior, standing in it, realized with a lurch that the baker was right — or right enough. That was the horror of it. The covenant had been a leash, and leashes only work until the animal grows, and the god under Errow had been growing for three centuries in the dark, patient as root systems, and now every honest answer Lior gave only proved the baker's point.

"The covenant bought you time," said Ossa, descending the steps with terrible dignity. "Time is not nothing, woman. Time is everything. Ask the dead of Vess what time would have been worth."

"And what will time buy us now?" The baker gestured at the white banner, at the whisper-well, at the whole fraying world. "Old woman, I bake bread. I know dough that's proofed too long. It doesn't wait politely. It *splits the bannock*." She stepped forward, and her voice dropped, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "So here's our word, and you can carry it to your keys and your heir. If the Door cannot be safely shut — if it must be fed forever, one keeper a generation, forever and ever — then we say burn it. Stone it. Collapse the hill on it and salt the field. Better a dead god than a fed one."

A roar went up from the grey side. An answering roar from the white. The two fronts of the crowd pressed toward each other like weather fronts, and Lior saw the boy from earlier lose his grip on his father's sleeve, saw an old woman stumble between banners, saw Seryn's knuckles whiten around her blue thread—

And Wren began to hum.

It rose above the square like smoke — the binding hymn's second verse, pouring out of an eleven-year-old throat, and the effect was instantaneous and horrible: every head turned. Every argument died mid-word. The crowd didn't fall silent; it fell *attentive*, hundreds of faces tilting toward the chapel steps with the soft, slack rapture of people hearing their own names spoken in sleep.

"No—" Lior shoved through the crowd, heart slamming. "Wren, stop—"

But he wasn't singing anymore. He was standing at the top of the steps with his arms slightly away from his sides, eyes gone silver-bright, and his voice — when it came — came doubled, a boy's voice with a cathedral behind it.

"*You argue over My door like children arguing over a pantry,*" it said, gentle as rot. "*Sweet ones. Beloved ones. I have loved this valley since before your oldest stone. Feed Me and I feed you. Open and be filled. Close Me away and starve — but know this, little bakers and little mourners:* the light guttered along the whole square, lantern after lantern, *the heir has already chosen. Ask her what she promised. Ask her what she'll give.*

Then the light snapped back, and Wren crumpled, and Lior caught him at the bottom of the stairs with the entire village staring at her — white banner and grey alike, hope and dread suddenly united in a single question.

"What promise?" Tam Brask whispered. "Girl. *Heir*. What did you promise it?"

Lior held the shaking boy against her shoulder and looked out at hundreds of waiting faces, and heard Ossa say, very softly, behind her:

"Well, child. It seems the god writes its own sermons after all."
