## 33. Night of Split Banners

Wren had learned to count the god's moods the way other children counted sheep.

There was the *hungry* mood, which felt like standing too close to an oven. There was the *sleepy* mood, which felt like wading in warm water up to his chin. And there was tonight's mood, which was new, and which he did not have a name for yet — a bright, ringing thing, like a bell struck inside his skull, that made the lanterns of Errow look doubled and the voices of the crowd sound like they were coming from under water.

*They are singing my name,* the god said, delighted, in Aldous's voice. *Listen, little wren. They are singing it wrong, but they are singing it.*

"They're not singing your name," Wren muttered. "They're arguing."

*Arguing is a kind of singing,* said the god, who had been saying increasingly strange things all evening.

Wren sat on the low wall behind the priestess's house, where Lior had told him — twice, in the voice she used when she meant it — to stay put. From there he could see the whole square around the well: the two crowds facing each other across it like weather fronts, torchlight making orange rivers on their faces. On one side, the villagers who wanted the Door opened, wearing blue ribbons knotted at their wrists. On the other, the ones who wanted it destroyed forever, wearing red ash smeared on their foreheads. Between them, the well itself, old and patient, its bucket rope creaking in the wind as if it too were nervous.

Lior stood near the well with Ivet and Ossa, talking fast with her hands. Sarel stood apart from everyone, arms crossed, watching the crowd the way a cat watches a bird that has made the mistake of landing nearby. The priestess moved between the two factions with a bowl of salted plums, offering them to both sides equally, which Wren thought was either very wise or completely useless.

He was supposed to be staying put. He was very good at staying put. He had practiced it his whole life, mostly against his will.

The problem was the bell in his head. It kept getting louder.

*Go down,* said the god. *They need to hear it plainly. They keep asking what I want, and everyone keeps answering for me. Even her.* A pause, and something almost gentle in the borrowed voice. *Even Aldous's heir.*

"She's not answering for you," Wren said. "She's negotiating with you. That's different. Ossa explained it. Negotiating means you get to say no back."

*I am so tired of no,* the god said, and the bell rang harder, and Wren's teeth buzzed. *I have been saying yes for three hundred years and look what it bought me. Holes in walls. Ash in streets. Go down, little wren. Let them see me. Let them choose with their eyes open.*

Wren looked at the crowd. He looked at the wall he'd been told to stay on.

He was eight years old, and he had died once already — or nearly, the fever that Hollowtongue had burned out of him — and since then he had noticed that grown-ups made a great many rules that amounted to *stay where we can't see you being strange.* He was tired of that rule. He suspected, in the small stubborn place at the center of him, that the god was tired of it too.

He slid off the wall and went down into the square.

* * *

The trouble with walking into a crowd while carrying a god is that the crowd notices.

It happened gradually, then all at once. The first few people just glanced at him — a small boy in an oversized coat, weaving between legs. But the god leaned out through him as he walked, the way a candle flame leans toward a draft, and where he passed, people turned. A woman with a blue ribbon stopped mid-sentence. A man with ash on his forehead dropped his torch-stick. Someone said, "That's the child," and someone else said, "the one who speaks it," and someone else said nothing at all, just fell silent in a way that spread outward from Wren like ripples, until he reached the well and turned around and found that several hundred people were looking at him.

The bell in his head was deafening now. He could feel the god pressing at the backs of his eyes.

"Say it," called a woman on the blue-ribbon side. Her voice cracked with hope. "Child, tell us what it wants. Tell us it forgives us. Tell us it will make the crops grow and the fevers stop and—"

"It doesn't want anything yet," Wren said. "Ossa said—"

"Liar!" roared a man from the ash side. "It wants everything! That's what hunger *is!* My grandfather helped seal it and his hands shook till the day he died, and you want to open the door and shake its hand?"

"I didn't help seal anything," Wren said, reasonably. "I'm eight."

But nobody was listening anymore, because that was the thing about crowds — they don't listen, they *catch*, like fire catching, and the god was leaning further and further out through him, drinking in all that attention the way dry ground drinks rain, and the bell became a roar, and Wren realized, with a cold drop in his stomach, that he could no longer feel where his own thoughts ended.

*Yes,* the god breathed. *Yes, this. This is what prayer feels like from the inside.*

"Stop," Wren whispered.

The nearest torches flared. Every lantern in the square burned suddenly white and tall, and the crowd gasped, and half of them knelt, and the kneeling made the other half angry, and the anger made the first half more devout, and the god drank it all, and grew, and Wren felt himself getting thinner, like a glove turning inside out.

Someone grabbed his arm. Ossa — the old elder, sharp-eyed, smelling of ink and dust. "Child," she said, very quietly, "you need to let go of it now."

"I'm trying," Wren said, and his voice came out wrong, layered, two voices in one throat, and Ossa's face went gray.

The crowd surged. He heard wood crack — someone had torn a rail from the well fence, or maybe it was a banner pole, blue cloth and red ash tangling together somewhere in the crush. The priestess was shouting something about peace and plums, which even in the moment struck Wren as unlikely to work. Sarel had drawn something — not a weapon; a badge, holding it up, shouting words about order that vanished into the noise. Lior was pushing through the crowd toward him, shouting his name, and the terrible thing, the thing Wren would remember afterward with shame and gratitude tangled together, was that part of him — the part already half the god's — thought:

*She can't reach me here. Nothing can reach me here. This is the safest I have ever been.*

And then the crowd closed over him like water.

* * *

Here is what drowning in fervor is like, according to Wren, who knows:

It does not hurt. That is the worst part. It is warm, and loud, and full of light, and every person pressed against him is pouring something into him — hope, fury, grief, the desperate wish for the fever to stop and the crops to grow and the dead to come back — and the god takes all of it and gives back power, raw and golden, and Wren is the cup, and nobody, including Wren, remembers to ask how much a cup can hold.

He is on his knees now. Or floating. The ground has opinions he can't verify. Lanterns burn overhead in a ring, impossibly bright, and faces swim above him, mouths moving, and the sounds they make are beautiful and meaningless, like birdsong heard through a wall.

*Look what they give,* says the god, drunk and vast, wearing Aldous's voice like a coat three sizes too big. *Three hundred years I starved, and one night of them loving me and hating me and needing me, and I could raise cathedrals. I could raise the dead. Little wren, I could give you back your mother—*

"My mother isn't dead," Wren tried to say. "My mother is in Vess. You're mixing me up with somebody else."

*Am I?* said the god, and for one flickering moment the vastness wavered, and underneath it Wren heard something old and lonely and frightened, and he understood, in the way children understand things sideways, that the god was not lying on purpose. It had been hungry so long it had forgotten whose mother was whose. It had forgotten most things. Hunger eats memory first; Ossa had said that; Ossa had said a lot of things that Wren was only now understanding.

The pressure built. The cup filled past the brim. Somewhere far away, Lior's voice was calling his name, closer now, breaking through — *Wren, Wren, look at me* — and he tried, he genuinely tried, to turn toward it, but he had no edges left to turn with.

Then, through the roar, he heard singing.

* * *

It was not a pretty song. That was the first thing Wren noticed, even half-drowned: it wasn't a lullaby or a hymn, nothing smooth. It was a working song, a rope-and-timber song, with a beat like feet on floorboards and notes that stepped on each other on purpose. Lior's voice was not especially good. She sang it anyway, loudly, badly, with total authority, the way you'd swing a hammer.

He knew it. That was the second thing. He knew every word, though he had never been taught it — the way he knew Hollowtongue, the way the oaths lived in him. It was the guild's binding hymn, the one Ossa hummed while cleaning her spectacles, the one Ivet claimed not to know and always got wrong in exactly the same places.

*Bind the door and bind the breath,*
*bind the hunger, bind the depth,*
*what we hoarded, what we stole,*
*bind it, bind it, make it whole—*

And the song did something the shouting couldn't. It gave the crowd a rhythm. And a crowd with a rhythm is no longer a wave; it is a line of people, and lines of people can think. Heads turned. Feet found the beat despite themselves. The priestess began to sing along — she knew it too, of course she did, she was Errow-born — and then Ossa, in a cracked alto, and then, glaring furiously at himself for doing it, Ivet, getting the same verse wrong in the same place he always got it wrong.

The god flinched.

*What is that,* it said, and for the first time all night it sounded small. *What is — I know that song. Why do I know that song?*

"Because we sang it to you," Wren said — or his mouth said, from somewhere deep underwater, in the layered voice. "Before. When you were new. Before we stole from you. It's yours. We took everything else but we never took the song."

The hymn wound through the square, verse by verse, and with each verse the golden pressure eased, notch by notch, like a winch paying out. The lanterns sank from white back to warm yellow. The crowd's kneeling softened into standing, standing into swaying, swaying into simply being people again — embarrassed, hoarse, blinking at each other as if waking from a shared dream. Blue ribbons and red ash stood shoulder to shoulder, humming.

And Lior reached him.

She dropped to her knees in the trampled mud, gathered him in, and held on — not gently, not carefully, but the way you hold something you have nearly lost, both arms, face buried in his hair, still singing into the crown of his head because she did not dare stop. Wren clutched her coat and shook. The bell in his skull faded to a chime, then a whisper, then a warmth in his chest like a banked fire — present, but home.

*Little wren,* the god murmured, quiet now, almost shy. *Tell her — tell the heir — the third verse. Ask her why we stopped singing the third verse.*

"There is no third verse," Wren mumbled into Lior's coat.

*There is,* said the god. *Aldous cut it. Ask her why.*

* * *

Later — much later, after the priestess had fed everyone salted plums with the grim satisfaction of a woman whose strategy had finally worked, after Sarel had quietly unclenched her jaw and re-sheathed whatever she had half-drawn, after the blue ribbons and red ash had drifted apart into small exhausted clusters instead of armies — Wren sat wrapped in a blanket by the well with his feet in Lior's lap, because she had checked his toes were still attached four times and seemed unwilling to stop checking.

"You stayed on the wall for almost a whole hour," she said. "That might be a record."

"The god asked me to go down," Wren said. "It said people should choose with their eyes open."

Lior was quiet for a moment. Above them, the last of the split banners — one blue, one red — hung from the chapel eaves where the wind had thrown them, tangled together, impossible to tell which was winning.

"It wasn't wrong," she admitted. "That's the annoying part." She squeezed his foot. "But next time it asks you to be a doorway, you tell it the door is closed for repairs. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Wren said. Then, because it had been sitting in him all evening, heavy as a stone in a shoe: "Lior. The hymn. Is there a third verse?"

Her hands went still.

"Yes," she said slowly. "There is. Aldous taught us it was lost. He said the Purge burned it." She stared at the tangled banners, and Wren watched her arrive somewhere she didn't want to go. "If the god remembers a verse that was supposedly destroyed before any of us were born... then it isn't remembering. It was *there*. It heard Aldous cut it out."

"What's in the third verse?" Wren asked.

Lior pulled the blanket higher around his shoulders and did not answer right away, and when she did, her voice was very careful, the way it got when she was afraid and refusing to show it.

"That's what I'm going to ask Ossa," she said. "Tonight. Before anyone sleeps."
