## 34. Three Keys, One Hand

The chapel at Errow had no roof worth the name — half its slates had gone to moss and wind over the centuries — so that dawn came down through the gaps in long grey columns, like light poured through a strainer. Lior stood in one of those columns of light and tried to make her hands stop shaking.

They wouldn't. She'd stopped expecting them to somewhere around midnight.

"Here." Ivet pressed a strip of dried apple into her palm. "Eat. You can't open an ancient door on an empty stomach. Probably. No one's ever written down the rules."

"Because no one's ever done it," Lior said, and ate the apple anyway. It was leathery and too sweet and it was the best thing she'd tasted in weeks.

Below them, beneath the chapel floor, the old crypt waited. Ossa had gone down first — of course she had; the woman moved like a debt collector entering a debtor's home, certain of her welcome and contemptuous of it both — and her voice floated up through the stairwell, dry as paper: "The mechanism is intact. Which is more than I can say for the masonry. If anyone has any final objections to waking something half-mad under a village of four hundred souls, now would be the moment."

Nobody objected. That was the terrible part, Lior thought. Not that no one wanted to stop — but that everyone had their own good reason not to, and all those reasons fit together like the keys themselves.

Wren sat on the third step of the stairwell, swinging his legs, humming. It was Hollowtongue he hummed in, the guild's liturgical rise-and-fall, and he didn't know he was doing it. He'd been doing it since they'd crossed into Errow's bounds, getting louder by inches, the way a kettle gets louder before it sings. The god underneath them knew its company was coming.

Lior crouched beside him. "How do you feel?"

"Full," Wren said, after considering the question seriously, the way eight-year-olds consider everything. "Like when you drink too much soup."

"That's a horrifying image and exactly what I needed to hear."

"Maren says the same thing," he added helpfully. Then, in a voice that wasn't quite his — older, roughened at the edges, familiar in a way that made Lior's chest ache — *"She does, actually. Every time she eats my cooking. I've never decided whether it's a compliment."*

Aldous. Even now, even here. Lior closed her eyes for one breath. "Later," she said quietly. "Talk later. Let the boy be a boy until we're at the door."

The voice withdrew like a tide, and Wren blinked, looked around, and said, "Can I have soup?"

* * *

They went down together: Lior, Ivet, Wren, Ossa, Sarel. Maren Cole stayed above with the priestess and half of Errow standing watch at the chapel doors — the half that wanted the Door opened. The other half was out in the square, praying loudly enough that the sound came faintly down through the broken roof, a low murmur like rain. Both halves were praying, Lior realized. They just prayed different words.

The crypt was small. That surprised her every time she thought about it. All this — the guild, the Purge, the ban, the graves, the burned charms, the years of fear laid down in Vess like sediment — and it had all rested on a room barely bigger than Tessin's shop had been. Stone walls without ornament. A floor of packed earth. And set into the far wall, where the earth met the stone, a door.

It wasn't grand. That was the second thing that surprised people, Ossa had said, and she'd been right. It was a plain door of black wood bound in dull iron, unremarkable as a pantry door, except that the air around it was wrong. Not cold, exactly. *Sparse.* Like standing near a window in winter without the glass. Lior could feel it pulling at her — gently, patiently, the way hunger pulls — from six feet away.

Three locks sat in the iron band across its middle. Three keyholes, each shaped differently: one round, one square, one a shape she had no name for, a kind of folded star.

Ossa held the first key — hers, kept thirty years inside the Inquisition itself, hidden in the spine of a ledger of confiscated hymnals. She wore it now on a cord around her neck like a penitent's token. Maren's key had come up from Vess in Lior's pocket, wrapped in cloth, warm as a held hand. And the third —

The third they'd dug out of the convict graves outside the walls, and Lior still dreamed about the warmth of it, and the hundreds of little charms buried with the dead, still working, still trying, after all those years, to grant some small mercy to the people who'd carried them.

"Keys," Ossa said. Her voice didn't shake. Ossa's voice hadn't shaken since before Lior was born, probably, possibly ever. "Before we do this, I want it said plainly, once, with witnesses. Lior Eastwick. You understand what happens when these turn?"

"I understand someone stays behind," Lior said. "Or pays. Or both. I've read the same archives you have."

"You've read the archives *I* cleaned," Ossa said. "I cleaned them well. But there are things I couldn't scrub out because I didn't find them until last month, and one of them is this: the Door doesn't only need a keeper. It needs a keeper who *wants* to stay. Willingness isn't sentiment. It's structural. A reluctant keeper is a cracked hinge. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you think I might be a cracked hinge."

"I think you're twenty-three years old and you've spent the whole road pretending you haven't decided," Ossa said. "Decide. Now. Out loud. In front of witnesses. Because once these keys turn, there is no deciding anymore. There's only what you already chose."

The crypt went quiet. Above them, faintly, the two halves of Errow prayed against each other. Wren had taken Lior's hand without asking, the way children do, and his grip was sticky and absolute.

Lior looked at the door. Behind it — if the archives were right, if Aldous was right, if the hollow country waited — everything the guild had stolen and hoarded for three hundred years. Everything her master had died holding shut. Everything she was heir to.

"I choose," she said, "to go in if I must. And I choose to try, first, to give it back instead. Both. Out loud. Witnessed."

Ossa studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled — a small, crooked, human smile, startling on that austere face. "That," she said, "is the first honest answer anyone's given these keys in three hundred years. It'll do."

She held out the first key. Round lock, square lock, star. "Together. On three. And Lior —"

"Yes?"

"When it opens, whatever you see — don't step forward. Not one inch. Not until you know what's asking you to."

"On three," Lior said, and took the keys.

* * *

They turned together. Ossa's hand steady, Ivet's hand careful — the cartographer handled the third key like a rare map, with reverence and terror in equal measure — and Lior's own hand, which shook, and turned anyway.

Three small sounds. Click. Click. Click. Almost gentle. Like a house being unlocked after a long absence.

Then the iron band slid, and the black wood swung inward without a sound at all, and the Door opened onto a country that no longer existed.

Lior had braced herself for darkness. What she got was morning.

Light spilled out — real light, or the memory of light, golden and slanted, the exact angle of a Vess sunrise over the Tanner's Row rooftops. Beyond the threshold stretched a street she knew. Knew *intimately*. Cobbles worn smooth in the same patterns. The leaning chimney stack of the chandler's shop. The awning of the fruit-seller's stall, faded red. The night market lane where she'd sold luck-knots to strangers for six years.

Vess. Perfect Vess. Every stone remembered, every shadow in its proper place.

And empty.

Not ruined — that was the horror of it. Nothing was broken. Nothing was burned or crumbled or rotted. The street simply had no one in it, and the silence had a texture, a pressure, like the whole country was holding one breath and had been holding it for a very long time. The fruit on the stall was bright and untouched and cast no smell. The light fell on the cobbles and warmed nothing. Somewhere — everywhere — faintly, she could hear it: the sound of a city breathing in and never breathing out.

"It's beautiful," Ivet whispered behind her. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. He'd spent his life mapping erased places; here was the erased thing itself, whole, and it was worse than any ruin. "Gods forgive me. It's beautiful and it's starving. You can see it starving. Look at the edges of things —"

She looked. At the far end of the street, where Vess should have gone on toward the river, the city just... thinned. Colors drained. Rooftops became suggestions became nothing, like ink washed off a page. The hollow country didn't end. It *ran out.*

"The prayers fed it," Ossa said quietly. "Every answered prayer, every burn-hole in your city's walls — that was this place eating. And it hasn't eaten properly in three hundred years. What you're looking at is a country living on crumbs."

Wren stepped up between them, small hand still in Lior's, and looked into the hollow Vess with the frank, unblinking gaze of a child assessing a strange dog.

"He's crying," Wren said.

"Who, love?" the priestess called softly from the stairs.

"The god." Wren pointed at the empty street. "He lives in all of it. He's crying because he can't remember anyone's name. There were millions of names and he can't remember any of them, and he's so *hungry*, and hungry is loud, and loud is all he has left."

And then, from deep within the hollow country — from everywhere, from the cobbles and the chimneys and the breathless air — something noticed them.

Lior felt it the way you feel a change in weather. The pressure shifted. The silence leaned. Far down the empty street, at its vanishing edge, the light gathered — drew itself up, the way fog draws up into a figure — and stood.

It was tall. It was made of morning. And it began, slowly, without hurry, without threat, to walk toward the open door.

"Don't move," Ossa breathed.

"I remember this street," the god said.

Its voice was not thunder. That was the worst discovery of Lior's life. Its voice was ordinary — worn and warm and slightly hoarse, a voice like a man who talks all day for a living, a voice she had heard every night of her apprenticeship giving her corrections over supper.

It sounded like Aldous.

"It was raining the day they sealed me in," the god said, coming closer, and each word landed in the crypt like a stone dropped in still water. "No — no, that's wrong. Was it? I had a name for the rain. I had names for everything. I had a million names and they took the door and—" The figure faltered, flickered, steadied. The hunger came up through its voice then, raw and vast, a whale-song of want under the words. "*So hungry. So long. You brought keys. You brought — what did you bring? What are you? Are you food? Are you mine?*"

Wren's grip tightened on Lior's hand. Through the boy, faintly, Aldous spoke: *"Steady, Lior. It won't take you by force. It can't. That was the covenant — the door swings both ways, but nothing crosses unwilling. It can only ask. Gods only ever ask. That's why we were able to seal it at all."*

"And when it asks?" Lior whispered.

*"You'll want to say yes. Whatever it offers, you'll want it more than you've ever wanted anything. That's how it fed, back before the guild — not with force. With yes."*

The figure stopped at the threshold. Up close it resolved, horribly, beautifully: it was not one face but many, layered like reflections in deep water, and Lior saw among them — she had to bite her tongue not to cry out — her mother's tired smile. Tessin's apologetic shrug rendered in light. The fever-mother's desperate eyes. Aldous, young, Aldous as she'd never known him, laughing at some joke three hundred years dead. Everyone she had lost and everyone she had failed, all of them wearing the same patient, starving expression.

"Lior Eastwick," the god said, and said it correctly, gently, the way no one had pronounced her name since Aldous died. "Heir of the Hollow Guild. Keeper of the keys. Little thief of my crumbs." The many faces softened. "Come in. Come home. The city remembers you. I will build you a house out of every prayer you ever granted. I will give you back your childhood — every memory you burned to free your enemy, I keep them, I keep *everything*, nothing is lost in me, nothing is ever lost — come and take them back. Come and stay. Stay, and eat. Stay, and rest. Stay, and never again sell charms too weak to be called magic, because with me you will never be weak—"

It was sweet. That was the trap of it. It wasn't temptation shaped like temptation; it was temptation shaped like *relief*, like setting down a weight carried so long she'd forgotten her own shoulders. Every word landed in a bruise she'd forgotten she had.

Her foot slid forward half an inch.

Ossa's hand closed on her shoulder — iron-firm, seventy years of stubbornness in five fingers — and Ivet said, very calmly, in the tone of a man reading a map legend, "The village of Errow, population four hundred and twelve, current location: behind us. Just for reference. Just noting where home is."

Lior laughed — a shaky, wet, ridiculous laugh — and the spell thinned.

"No," she said to the god.

The many faces stilled.

"No," she said again, stronger, and stepped back from the threshold, and took the guild's inheritance out of the pocket of her coat — the sigil, the old lead token of the Hollow Guild, warm as a heartbeat — though what she meant by *no* was larger than a coin, was everything, was the hoard itself, three hundred years of stolen power waiting to be given back. "You don't get a keeper. You don't get another prisoner on either side of this door. You get something better, if you can bear it. You get *paid*."

The god looked at her with her mother's eyes, and her master's, and hungered.

"What," it asked, in a thousand borrowed voices, "are you offering?"

Behind her, Ossa exhaled like a woman watching a blade fall and miss. Ahead of her, the hollow country leaned in, beautiful and starving, to hear the answer.
