## 35. The God's True Face

The Door did not open so much as admit them.

Lior had expected a grinding of ancient stone, a blast of stale air, maybe — if the old guild texts could be believed — a chorus of trumpets. Instead the three keys turned in her hand like a lock in a kitchen door, and the wall beneath Errow's chapel simply decided it had never been a wall at all. It became a doorway the way a sleeping man becomes awake: all at once, and slightly annoyed about it.

Beyond was a country.

Not a room. Not a corridor. A country — Vess as she had never seen it and somehow recognized instantly. The rooftops were right. The crooked bell tower of Saint Ellery's leaned at exactly the angle it leaned in the real city, except here it was whole, gilded, beautiful, its cracked bell ringing a note that made Lior's teeth ache with homesickness. Streets she had walked a thousand times stretched out under a sky the color of late honey. The night market was there, every stall lit, every seller smiling. The river ran silver.

And it was empty. That was the wrongness under the beauty. No smoke from chimneys. No dogs. No one shouting prices or cursing cart wheels. A whole city holding its breath.

"It's hungry," Wren said quietly beside her. The boy's voice came out doubled sometimes now, his own words braided with something older and vaster underneath. "It made this to look at. Like a beggar wearing a fine coat."

"Then let's not keep it waiting," Ossa said. The old woman's hands were steady on her walking stick, but Lior noticed she crossed the threshold first, deliberately, planting herself between the others and whatever waited. "A hungry thing left waiting only gets hungrier."

Ivet lingered last, one foot on the chapel stone, one in the hollow country. He looked back the way he always did when crossing into unmapped territory — over his shoulder, at the world behind him, checking that it still existed. Then he stepped through, and the doorway sealed itself behind them with a sound like a book closing.

* * *

They walked streets that remembered feet better than they remembered people. Lior kept expecting the illusion to break — for the market stalls to dissolve when touched, for the honeyed sky to tear. But when she brushed her fingers along a fruit seller's awning, the canvas was real canvas, sun-warmed, smelling faintly of dye. The peaches in the baskets were perfect and cold as river stones. She picked one up and it sat in her palm with the exact weight of nothing at all.

"Don't eat anything," Ossa said without turning around. "Nothing here is food."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were absolutely going to." There was, remarkably, gentle humor in the old woman's voice. Fifty years scrubbing inconvenient truths out of Inquisition archives had not, apparently, scoured it out of her. "You have your master's face when you're curious. He once licked a cursed amulet to see if it tasted like anything. It did. For three days he could only speak in rhymes."

"That sounds like him," Lior admitted. Her throat tightened around the name. She hadn't said *Aldous* aloud since the graveyard. Saying names here felt dangerous, like dropping crumbs.

The city shifted around them as they walked — subtly, helpfully. Every street they needed seemed to slope gently downhill toward the same destination. Doors stood open to show glimpses of interiors: her old garret above the chandler's shop, the guildhall's reading room with its chairs worn soft by generations of apprentices, her mother's kitchen, which Lior had not thought about in eleven years and which appeared anyway, complete with the blue crockery and the burn mark on the table where a pan had slipped when Lior was six.

She stopped looking in doorways after that.

Wren walked close to her side, small and solemn, holding the hem of her coat the way children hold things when they want to be anchored. Twice the god spoke through him unasked — a low murmuring in Hollowtongue that made the shutters rattle — and twice Lior put her hand on his head until the boy surfaced again, blinking, saying something ordinary and heartbreaking like *"I'm thirsty"* or *"Are we almost there?"*

"We're almost there," she told him, though she didn't know it was true. But the streets kept sloping down, and down, and down, toward the place every city keeps at its center whether it admits it or not: the well, the temple, the grave. The oldest rooms.

The hollow country's heart turned out to be the guildhall.

Of course it was. Of course the god had built its mask out of the place she loved most.

* * *

It stood whole and golden in the evening light that never quite finished becoming dusk. The great doors were open. Inside, the long hall was lit by candles that burned without melting, and the air smelled of beeswax and ink and Aldous's terrible pipe tobacco, and Lior stood on the threshold with tears running down her face before she understood why.

Because the hall was full of people.

Not crowds. Just — everyone. Everyone she had lost, arranged through the room like guests at a wake who had gotten bored of grieving and started telling stories. An old woman by the hearth with flour on her hands, laughing at something — her mother, younger than memory, younger than grief. The chandler's son Tam who had died of the grey fever when Lior was nine, alive and mid-argument about dice. Guild members whose faces she knew only from portraits on the stair, three generations of Hollow hands, nodding to her as she passed as if she were merely late.

And at the far end, in the master's chair by the window, smoking a pipe that would never need relighting —

"Aldous," she said. Or tried to. What came out was mostly air.

He looked exactly as he had on the last morning. Grey stubble, ink-stained fingers, the patched elbow she'd sewn badly and he'd refused to let anyone redo. He raised the pipe in greeting, casual as Sunday.

"You're late, Eastwick. I've been sitting here so long I've read the same page forty times, and it wasn't even a good page."

"You're dead," Lior said. Her voice sounded very young. "I watched them bury you. I dug the grave myself because nobody else would do it properly."

"Yes, that was a grim Tuesday." He puffed the pipe, and the smoke curled upward into shapes — no. She caught it. The smoke was trying to curl into shapes. Little comforting shapes. A bird. A hand. She looked away first.

"You're not him," she said, steadier now. "You're wearing him."

"I'm wearing everything I have," said the god, in her master's voice, with her master's shrug, and the honesty of it was somehow worse than deception would have been. "You gave me very little to work with, Lior Eastwick. Your guild hoarded its gods like misers and left me scraps. Scraps of voice. Scraps of face. Everything you ever prayed away, everything you buried with your charms and your dead — that's my wardrobe." The hall flickered, just once, and in the flicker Lior saw what was underneath: something vast and thin and starving, folded impossibly many times to fit inside a shape the size of a man. "But scraps can be tailored. Come here. Sit. Let's talk about the future."

Behind her, she heard Ossa murmur, "Steady," and Ivet say, very softly, "Oh, that's *clever*, that's horribly clever—" the way he talked about maps that lied beautifully.

Wren tugged her sleeve. "Don't sit in the chair," the boy whispered. "If you sit in the chair, part of you stays in the chair. That's how chairs work here."

Out of the mouths of babes and vessels. Lior stayed standing.

* * *

"So," the god-Aldous said. "You've come to feed me. Or seal me. Or argue with me — your guild was always fond of arguing, it's practically a sacrament. Before you choose, I'm authorized to make an offer."

"Authorized by whom?"

"By necessity. By arithmetic. By five hundred years of hunger, child." He set the pipe down, and the gesture was so precisely Aldous — the little tap-tap against the armrest — that Lior's knees nearly went. "Here is what I know about you. You lost your childhood to the Purge. All of it — the years before your mother died, before the guildhall, before me. Burned out of you when the Inquisition's cleansers took your first memories as evidence. You don't remember your mother's voice. You remember the *shape* of remembering her voice, which is crueler."

Lior's breath stopped. Only Ossa knew that. Only Ossa, who had spent decades filing away the records of what the Purge took — and who must have told no one, but the god had been listening to archives too, apparently, or to the holes archives leave.

"I can give it back," the god said gently. "Every morning. Her voice saying your name. The smell of that kitchen. The pan, the burn, the crying, all of it. Yours again, permanent, true. And that's the small offer."

"Don't," Ossa said quietly, at her shoulder. "Lior. Don't listen past the next sentence."

"The large offer," the god continued, and now the hall dimmed around them, the false guests receding like tide, until there was only the two of them and the chair and the window with its honeyed light, "is him."

Aldous stood up.

Not the god-wearing-him. Him — or so perfectly him that the difference stopped mattering, which Lior understood, dimly, was the entire point. Warmth came off him like a stove. She could smell the tobacco, the ink, the horse he'd ridden the year he taught her to bind a fever-ward, the particular smell of his coat collar that she had cried into more than once and never told anyone.

"He stays," the god said. "Really stays. As long as you stay. You hold the Door, Lior, and I will hold nothing back — I'll pour myself into that shape and keep it warm for as long as you keep faith. Fifty years. Sixty. A lifetime, together, in a country where nothing hurts and nothing ends and the tea is always the right temperature. You wanted justice? This is better than justice. This is *undoing*. Nobody has to have lost anything. Not you. Not him. Not the boy, not the cartographer, not even the Inquisitor camped outside our door deciding whether to arrest us or join us." The smile was Aldous's smile, wry and lopsided. "All you have to do is sit down."

And oh, it was intimate, that was the horror of it. Sarel Vane could have tortured her and gotten nothing. The Inquisition could have burned the guild's last books in front of her and she'd have spat ash and walked on. But this — this reached into the exact hollow space in her chest, measured it, and offered to fill it, custom-made, free of charge.

Her legs actually bent. She felt herself begin to fold toward the chair, toward him, toward the warm shape of the only father she'd ever had—

Wren made a small sound.

Just a small sound. The boy, standing in the doorway of the great hall with the candlelight making him look eight years old instead of ten, watching her with an expression she'd seen on market mothers counting coins that wouldn't stretch. Not accusing. Just *seeing*. And in his doubled voice, half-Wren and half-the-thing-they'd-come-to-feed, he said:

"She knows it isn't real. She knows, and she wants it anyway. Isn't wanting-anyway the whole disease?" A pause, and then just Wren, small and clear: "Lior. My mum sold her wedding ring to buy your charm. If you sit in that chair, I have to go home and tell her it was for nothing."

The warmth in the room guttered like a draft had come through.

Lior straightened up. It cost her — stars, it cost her, it felt like setting down a weight she'd carried so long her spine had grown around it, and standing without it she was lighter and rawer and more afraid than she'd been in years.

"Here's my counter," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "You want to make staying easy. That's the whole trick, isn't it? That's how the guild caught you in the first place. They didn't chain you — they *negotiated*, and then they starved you in a lovely room. Well. I'm the heir, and I negotiate differently."

The god-Aldous tilted its head. Around them, the false hall held very still, listening.

"You'll get fed," Lior said. "Truly fed, sated for the first time since the founders locked you in here — not with stolen prayers, not with burnt-out children's fevers, but with something real. But not because I sat down. Because I stand up. And the terms are coming, and they're not sweet, and you're going to hate at least two of them." She took one step forward, away from the chair, away from the warm dead shape of her master. "So before we talk terms — take off his face. Take off all their faces. If I'm going to bargain with you, I'll bargain with what's actually under the coat."

For a long moment the god wore Aldous's expression, hurt and proud and stubbornly fond, and Lior thought it might refuse, might press the temptation harder, might be exactly as cruel as the guild histories claimed.

Then the hall sighed — a sound like a city exhaling after curfew — and the candles went out one by one, and the false guests folded away like laundry, and the honeyed light drained from the windows until only the doorway stood lit, and in the doorway stood something that had never had a face at all and was, for the first time in five hundred years, about to need one.

It opened what it had instead of a mouth, and said, in a voice like the first word ever spoken:

"*Name your terms, heir.*"

Behind her, boots rang on stone — fast, familiar, entirely unwelcome. Sarel Vane's voice came down the chapel stairs ahead of her, furious and afraid and resolute:

"Lior Eastwick — wait. Before you answer it. You'll want to hear what *I* have to say first."
