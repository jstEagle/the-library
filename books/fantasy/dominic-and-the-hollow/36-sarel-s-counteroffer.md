## 36. Sarel's Counteroffer

The god's country smelled of bread that had never been baked.

That was the first thing Sarel Vane noticed, standing on the far side of the Door with ash still on her cuffs from the Inquisition cells and her badge — surrendered, useless, a piece of tin she'd carried for nineteen years — heavy in her pocket like a stone she couldn't put down. The air of the hollow Vess was warm and sweet and wrong, the way a hearth is warm after the house has burned. Somewhere out along streets that mimicked streets she had walked every day of her life, ovens were baking loaves from nothing, and the smell drifted through windows of houses where no one lived.

She hated it immediately, and completely, and without apology.

"Stop making that face," said Ivet, beside her. The cartographer had his satchel clutched to his chest the way a soldier holds a rifle, and his eyes kept darting to the horizon, where a sun hung in a sky that had no business holding one. "You look like you're about to arrest the scenery."

"I have considered it," Sarel said.

The others were ahead of them, small figures on the long avenue that in real Vess led to the Customs House and here led nowhere at all. Lior walked first, because Lior always walked first now, her sleeves pushed up, her bare forearms showing skin that had gone strangely smooth since the night she'd burned her childhood to break Sarel out of prison — magic leaves marks when it takes something from you, and what it had taken from Lior had left no scar at all, which Sarel thought was somehow worse. Wren trotted at her side, the boy's head tilted, listening always to the voice inside him. Ossa came behind, old and deliberate as a closing door. And the priestess of Errow — Sarel had learned her name was Yeva, though everyone called her simply *the priestess* as if there had only ever been the one, which, in a way, there had — brought up the rear with her lantern unlit.

The light here didn't need help.

They had come through the Door an hour ago. Perhaps two. Time behaved badly in this place, pooling like spilled wine, and Sarel had given up counting. They walked toward the heart of the hollow country because Aldous — speaking through Wren's mouth in a voice that made Sarel's teeth ache, familiar in ways she refused to examine — had told them the god would meet them there. At the center. Where the temple would be, if temples could dream.

"You're doing it again," Ivet said.

"Doing what?"

"The face."

Sarel exhaled. "Ivet. I spent my entire adult life guarding a prison while believing I was standing watch over a victory. I burned charms made by frightened women to keep children from dying of fever. I signed ledgers. I read names into the record of the condemned." She nodded down the avenue, at the sweet false warmth. "And now I'm walking through the inside of the thing I helped build, and it smells like my grandmother's kitchen. Forgive me if my face has opinions."

Ivet was quiet a moment. Then: "My grandmother baked too," he said. "Bread with caraway seed. She'd give me the heel end and tell me it was the mapmaker's portion." He smiled faintly. "This place doesn't get the caraway right. That's how I know it's lying."

"That," Sarel said, "is the most cartographer sentence anyone has ever spoken to me."

Ahead, the avenue opened into a square, and the square held a temple that Sarel recognized so violently she stopped walking.

It was the Grand Temple of Vess — or its echo, its memory, its wound. Every column, every stair, every weathered saint whose face the Purge had chiseled blank. Except here the faces weren't blank. Here, every statue wore the face of someone Sarel had known. A novice she'd trained. A seller from the night market whose charms she'd fed to the fire. Her own mother, dead forty years, gazing down from a niche with an expression of mild disappointment that was, Sarel had to admit, extremely accurate.

"Oh," said Ivet softly. "Oh, that's foul."

"It's not trying to hurt us," Ossa said, coming even with them. Her voice was flat as slate; it always was. "It's trying to be *welcome*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. Remember it." She looked at Sarel, and for a moment the cleanser of archives let something show through the slate — weariness, maybe, or its twin, hope. "Whatever it offers you, whatever shape it wears — remember that a cage can be furnished kindly. We built it a very kind cage."

"I know," Sarel said. "I dusted it."

* * *

The god met them in the nave.

It did not arrive. It was simply there when they entered, the way morning is simply there, the way debt is simply there. It stood among its borrowed faces and it was — Sarel searched for the word and found only the honest ones — *beautiful*. It had chosen, for its primary shape, a woman of middle years with grey coming in at the temples and hands that looked like they had done real work, and Sarel understood with a lurch that it had taken the shape from somewhere inside her own memory, some figure of comfort she hadn't known she still carried. Its eyes were the color of the moment before dawn.

"Lior Eastwick," it said, and its voice was Aldous's voice, and Wren flinched at hearing it come from somewhere other than his own chest, and Sarel saw Lior's hand move — just slightly — toward the boy, steadying him without touching him.

"We're here," Lior said. No preamble. No fear she was willing to show. "You know why."

"I know why." The god turned its beautiful borrowed face and regarded each of them in turn, and Sarel felt that regard like sunlight through glass — warm, and filtered, and keeping something out. "The heir comes to settle the inheritance. The elder comes to witness. The priestess comes because she promised. The child comes because I am in him, poor lamb, and I will give him back the moment I am able." Its gaze settled last on Sarel, and something moved behind the dawn-colored eyes — curiosity, perhaps, or recognition between professionals. "And the Inquisitor comes because—"

"Because she has a counteroffer," Sarel said.

Silence. Even the hollow city seemed to lean in — the smell of unbaked bread thickening, the light going attentive.

Lior turned. "Sarel—"

"You've had your say," Sarel said, and was surprised to find her voice steady. Nineteen years of reading charges aloud in a marble hall will do that; it turns out the training holds even when the marble hall turns out to have been a kennel. "You've all decided it's me who gets a vote when it's convenient and silence when it isn't. So. I'm taking the vote." She stepped forward, past Lior, past Ossa, until she stood in the open floor of the nave with the god's attention full upon her, and it was like standing in front of an open oven, and she had stood in front of worse.

"My whole life built this prison," she said. "Not the guild's. Mine. The guild sealed the Door and then lied about it for three hundred years and got rich hoarding what they stole — fine, that's their sin, and their heir can carry it. But the Purge? The burning? The ledgers?" Her hand went, without her permission, to the badge in her pocket. "Every charm confiscated. Every seller questioned. Every name written into the record of the condemned in my own handwriting — I wrote them *neatly*, god help me, I prided myself on my penmanship. Nineteen years I stood on your wall and called it faithfulness. I guarded the lock and called myself its servant." She lifted her chin. "So when a keeper is needed — and we all know a keeper is needed, don't we, Ossa has been honest enough for that much — then it should be me. My penance. My post. Let the heir walk free. She's twenty-three years old and she's already paid more than the whole guild ever paid for anything."

"Sarel." Lior's voice cracked across the nave like a whip made of thread. "Don't."

"Somebody has to stay," Sarel said, not turning. "That's the arithmetic. We've run the sums every way from Errow to here and the sum doesn't change. And I am telling you — I am *telling* the god, since it's listening through every statue of my dead mother in this room — that the person best suited to hold a door shut for eternity is the person who spent her life holding it shut and calling it holy work." She laughed, short and ugly. "I'm even good at it. Ask anyone. Sarel Vane never slept on watch. Not once. You can check the records."

"They burned the records," Ossa said quietly. "I burned some of them myself."

"Well," Sarel said, "you'll have to take my word."

* * *

The god was silent for a long moment. When it spoke, it had set aside Aldous's voice, and Sarel was grateful; hearing her argument weighed in the voice of the man Lior mourned felt like theft.

"You would stay willingly," the god said. "Knowing what staying means."

"Tell me what staying means."

"You know. Your elder has told you true, which must have pained her." Something flickered across Ossa's face — confirmation, and the pain of being confirmed. "The far side of this door is hunger with a floor plan. To keep it is to stand in the hunger and be the shape it pours itself against. It does not kill the keeper quickly. It learns you. It wears your comforts. It bakes your grandmother's bread forever and never puts in the caraway." The god's borrowed eyes were kind, which was the cruelest thing about them. "You would grow old alone in a city of echoes, Inquisitor, while everyone you might have loved grows old in the sun."

"I've read the file on eternity," Sarel said. "I'm aware of the terms."

"And you believe this settles your debt."

"I believe—" and here her voice finally did shake, one hairline fracture, and she let it, because she had spent nineteen years letting nothing shake and look where that had gotten everyone, "—I believe debts don't care whether we survive paying them. I built the wall. The wall wants a guard. It's not complicated. It was never complicated. What was complicated was pretending otherwise."

She heard Lior move before she saw her — heard boots on the echoing stone, quick and furious — and then Lior Eastwick planted herself directly in Sarel's line of sight, arms crossed, chin up, twenty-three years old and absolutely immovable, and Sarel thought: *ah. There it is. That's what Aldous saw in her.*

"No," Lior said.

"That's not an argument."

"It's the beginning of one. Sit down and listen to the rest." Lior jabbed a finger at a pew that had probably never existed and absolutely materialized anyway, because this place couldn't resist furnishing. "Sit. Down."

To her own considerable surprise, Sarel sat.

"Three things," Lior said, pacing now, the way — Sarel realized with a pang — Aldous must have paced, once, in a guildhall before it was abandoned, teaching an apprentice to think. "One. If you stay, the Inquisition loses its defector. The woman who read the truth off the temple steps goes into a hole, and the order that replaces her tells Vess she died a martyr to witchcraft, and my friend Tessin rots in a cell under that story, and every scared apothecary in the city keeps their head down for another generation. You staying doesn't pay a debt, Sarel. It buries the receipt."

Sarel opened her mouth.

"Two." Lior didn't stop. "You think your life built the prison. Fine. But whose key opens it? Whose name is on the inheritance? The guild stole the gods' power, hid it behind a Door, and then died rather than admit it, and the last of it — the whole hoard, every scrap of what they took — sits in *me*. That's not poetry. Ossa confirmed it. The god knows it. The debt is the guild's, and the guild's estate passes to its heir, and I'm the heir, and I don't get to hand my inheritance to someone else any more than you get to hand your sins to me. Believe me." Her voice caught, just slightly, on something — a childhood she no longer possessed, a mother whose face she'd traded for Sarel's freedom — "Believe me, I asked."

"And three?" Sarel said quietly.

Lior stopped pacing. When she turned around, the fury had burned down to something underneath it, something that looked, uncomfortably, like the exact expression Sarel had spent her career trying to stamp out of the condemned: a person deciding, freely, to pay.

"Three," Lior said, "is that you don't get to make this your penance, because penance is just pride wearing sackcloth. You want to suffer proportionately. You want the ledger balanced. Well, I've got news, Inquisitor: the ledger doesn't balance. It never balances. Someone burned my master's name into a list and someone else dug his grave and neither of those people can fix it by standing in a dark city smelling bread that isn't real. Grace isn't arithmetic. I learned that from a woman who broke me out of prison knowing it would cost her everything she had left of herself, and who hasn't once, *not once*, asked me to thank her for it properly." She swallowed. "You gave me your badge. You gave me your truth. Don't you dare try to give me your punishment too. I won't carry it. I refuse the delivery."

The nave was very quiet. Somewhere above, in a sky that wasn't a sky, the false sun moved without moving.

Sarel found, to her horror, that her eyes were wet. "You sound like him," she said. "Aldous. He argued like that. I arrested him twice for arguing like that."

"He mentioned," Lior said. "He said you let him go both times and filed it as 'insufficient evidence.'"

"He was insufferable."

"Yes," Lior said, and her voice went soft and wrecked and fond all at once. "He really was."

The god watched them the way rain watches a roof — patiently, from everywhere, finding every seam. Then it spoke, and it had chosen a new voice, one Sarel didn't recognize and suspected belonged to no one living: older, tireder, almost humble.

"She argues well, the heir," it said. "As her master argued. But understand me, Inquisitor Vane. The offer stands for any willing soul. It has stood for three hundred years. It is not a trap. It is a need." It spread its borrowed hands. "Someone must hold the door, or the door must be fed some other way. Those are the only shapes this has ever come in. Choose your sacrifice, but choose honestly — do not choose for her out of your guilt, and do not let her choose for you out of hers. Guilt makes a poor mortar. I have watched it crumble."

"I know," Sarel said. She stood. She straightened her coat with the automatic precision of nineteen years and then, deliberately, messed it up again, which felt enormously transgressive and slightly wonderful. "Which is why I withdraw the offer. Formally. On the record." She looked at Ossa. "Witness it. You're the archivist."

Ossa inclined her head. "Withdrawn and witnessed."

"But—" Sarel turned back to the god, and now the thing beneath her voice was not penance at all, and never had been, and she finally had the courage to name it. "But I'm not leaving either. Not yet. Whatever she decides — whatever the inheritance turns out to cost and whoever ends up carrying it — I'm standing next to her when it happens. Not as a guard. As a witness. Somebody should see it done rightly and tell the truth about it after, and apparently I'm constitutionally incapable of doing anything else." She glanced sideways at Lior. "Also I owe your master a debt I can't pay him, so I'll pay it forward at interest. He'd hate that. Good."

Lior made a sound that might have been laughter strangled at birth. "You're impossible."

"I've been told. By professionals."

Wren tugged at Lior's sleeve and pointed upward, and everyone followed the boy's finger, and the light in the hollow nave began — gently, terribly, like a tide — to gather around the god, and the god's borrowed faces turned toward Lior Eastwick with an expression of such naked, ancient longing that Sarel felt her breath go thin.

"He's going to offer her things now," Wren said, in his own voice, small and clear. "That's what he does. He offered me my mum. I said no. It was really hard." The boy's chin trembled and firmed. "It's going to be harder for her. He knows her whole list."

"What list?" Sarel asked.

Wren looked at her with the terrible directness of children and the recently haunted. "Everything she lost," he said. "Starting with the memories she spent to save you."

The light gathered. The god stepped forward, wearing the face of a man two decades dead, and opened his mouth to begin.

* * *

Later — hours later, or minutes; time pooled — Sarel would remember that she reached for Lior's arm and gripped it, hard, and whispered the only counsel she had left:

"Whatever he shows you — however real it looks — ask yourself one question. Does it have the caraway?"

And Lior, gray-faced, gripping back, breathed: "What?"

"Real things," Sarel said, "get the details right. Fakes get the feeling right and the details wrong. It's the only test I ever trusted." She held the young woman's eyes. "Now go hear what eternity thinks it can bribe you with. And Lior — whatever you decide —"

But the god had already begun to speak, and the nave filled with the sound of a dead man saying her name, and whatever Sarel meant to say went, for the moment, unspoken — folded away like a letter in a pocket, to be delivered later, or never, depending on what the heir chose to carry out of the dark.
