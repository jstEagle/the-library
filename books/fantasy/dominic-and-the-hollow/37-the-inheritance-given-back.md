## 37. The Inheritance Given Back

The god wore Aldous's face, and it was very good at wearing it.

That was the cruelest part, Lior thought. Not the hunger she could feel radiating off the hollow country like heat off a forge — the hunger was almost honest. But the way the god had gotten Aldous's smile exactly right, the crooked one, the one that said *I know you've done something foolish, child, and I'm deciding whether to be angry or amused* — that was cruelty of a very high order.

"You could have him back," the god said, in Aldous's voice, from a dozen mouths at once. The hollow Vess around them rippled — its streets, its rooftops, its pale imitation sun — and everywhere people stood who were not people, only echoes, only shapes the god remembered and wore like coats. "Not an echo. Him. His memories are here. His hands. I kept everything the guild ever gave me, Lior Eastwick. I keep *everything*."

"I know," Lior said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You kept me too."

Behind her, she heard Sarel shift her weight — the small sound of a woman who had spent her whole life learning to read rooms, reading this one and hating what she found. Ossa stood at the threshold with the three keys still joined in her old hands, saying nothing, because there was nothing left to say. The plan was made. The bargain was struck. All that remained was for Lior to walk through and become the Door's keeper, as Aldous should have been, as every heir before him.

Wren tugged at her sleeve.

"Lior." The boy's voice was his own again — mostly — though when he spoke too long, something older moved underneath it like a fish under ice. "It's lying. Partly."

"Wren," the god said gently, in a mother's voice now, in a voice Lior half-remembered from a market day twenty years gone, "don't spoil the gift."

"It does that," Wren said to Lior, ignoring it with the magnificent rudeness of nine-year-olds everywhere. "When it wants something. It tells you true things in a lying voice so you can't tell which is which." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You promised you'd teach me the rest of the songs."

Lior crouched down to his level. Her knees cracked. She was twenty-six years old and felt like Ossa.

"I promised," she agreed. "And I keep my promises. That's the whole problem with me. Ask anyone."

"Ask *me*," said the god in Aldous's voice, wounded. "I raised her. Ask me whether she keeps—"

"No," Lior said, standing. "You don't get to do that anymore."

She turned to face it fully — the god, the country, the beautiful starving echo of everything Vess had been before the maps forgot it. And she let herself feel, just for one breath, how much she wanted to say yes. To walk into those arms. To hear Aldous actually laugh, actually scold her, actually finish the sentence he'd died halfway through on the guildhall steps. The god had built this whole country out of longing; it knew exactly what bait to use, because it had been fishing in human wanting since before humans had words for it.

*That's the trick,* she told herself. *It isn't lying about any single thing. It's just offering you the one door, when there's a window.*

"Sarel," she said. "You still have my knife?"

Sarel frowned. "You gave it back to me at the wall. Why would I still—" She stopped. Her hand went to her belt anyway, the reflex of a soldier, and came away holding the little paring knife Lior used for cutting charm-cord. "You put it back in my sheath. When?"

"When you were busy arguing that your whole life built this prison and therefore your penance should hold the door." Lior held out her hand. "Which was a very noble argument, and also completely wrong, and I needed you distracted while I did some thinking. Sorry. Guild habit. We're terrible people. It's in the oaths."

Sarel stared at her. Then — and Lior would treasure this for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be — Sarel Vane, Inquisitor of the Purge, terror of the night markets, laughed. Once. A short, rusty, disbelieving sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years.

"You're going to do something insane," Sarel said.

"I'm going to do something *guild-shaped*," Lior corrected. "There's a difference. Insane is when you don't know the cost going in."

She took the knife. She rolled up her sleeve.

* * *

The sigil sat on the inside of her forearm where it had always lived — the Hollow Guild's mark, inked in ash-and-salt when she was nineteen, the last apprentice ever to bear it. Beneath her skin it had always been more than ink. Every charm she'd ever sold too cheap, every fever-ward and luck-knot, every scrap of craft she'd hoarded and rationed and hidden from the Inquisition for seven years — all of it drew from the same well, and the well had a name, and the name was *inheritance*. What the guild had taken. What the guild had sealed. What the guild had passed down hand to hand like stolen silver, generation after generation, each heir believing they were carrying a treasure when really they were carrying a debt.

Aldous had known. That was why he'd been walking toward Errow when he died. He'd been coming to pay.

"He never told you," the god said quietly. It had stopped smiling. Around them, the hollow country dimmed at its edges, like a fire settling. "In all those years. He never told you what you were carrying."

"He told me the only thing that mattered," Lior said. "'Take care of people first, and the magic will take care of itself.'" She swallowed. "I used to think that was just a nice thing masters say. It wasn't. It was instructions."

She pressed the point of the paring knife to the sigil.

And stopped.

Because here was the thing no one had warned her about — not Ossa, not the priestess, not even Wren-with-the-god-inside-him, who honestly should have mentioned it — renunciation *hurt*. Not the blade. The blade was nothing. But the moment the steel touched the mark, Lior felt the whole shape of what she was about to lose, all at once, the way you feel the shape of a room when the last lamp goes out.

Every charm she would never make. Every knot she would never tie. The specific pleasure of a fever breaking under a ward she'd woven right, the small smug joy of a luck-knot working, the language of thread and salt and ash that had been hers alone in all the world. Seven years of hiding it had made it precious. Precious things don't want to be given away. They cling.

Her hand shook.

"Lior." Ossa's voice, dry as old paper, from the doorway. "Child. Look at me."

Lior looked. The elder stood with the keys in her hands and forty years of archive-dust in her lungs, and her eyes were fierce and clear and absolutely without pity, which was exactly what Lior needed.

"The guild stole," Ossa said. "We told ourselves we were shepherds. We were thieves with good manners. Everything you ever made with that mark was made from stolen goods, and the only question left — the only question there has ever been — is whether you're the kind of thief who dies clutching the silver, or the kind who puts it back."

"That's a horrible speech," Lior said. Her eyes were burning. "It's perfect. Don't ever change."

She cut.

* * *

It did not feel like losing. That surprised her most of all. She had braced for amputation, for cold, for the specific grief of a musician going deaf. Instead it felt like opening a fist she hadn't known she was making — seven years of clenching releasing all at once, the power pouring out of her not as loss but as *return*, a river remembering its bed.

The sigil lifted off her arm like smoke drawn upward. It hung in the air between her and the god — a small gray-gold spiral, the mark of the Hollow, turning slowly — and then it began to *unravel*, thread by thread, year by year, and each unraveling thread flew into the dark of the hollow country and became light.

She felt each one leave. Not painfully. Honestly. Like being read aloud.

*The fever-ward from the night market, the one that shouldn't have worked.* Gone — and somewhere in the hollow country, a shape that might have been a sickroom brightened.

*The luck-knots, two hundred of them, sold for coppers to people who couldn't afford real charms.* Gone — and the gray streets flickered with small green sparks, like clover coming up through cobblestones.

*The binding hymn. The guild's liturgy, the Old Hollowtongue, every word she'd ever sung over salt and thread.* Gone — and the god made a sound that no one in that country had heard in a hundred years, because it was the sound of being *sung to*, properly, by someone who meant it.

And underneath all of it, deeper than the spells, the hoard itself — the raw hoarded power the founders had seized, that Aldous had carried, that had passed to her in blood and ink. It left her in a long slow tide, and with it went the weight. She hadn't known she was carrying it. You never know, until you set it down, how heavy the silver was.

The hollow country *filled*.

Not with people — it wasn't that kind of filling. With color. With warmth. The imitation sun climbed a degree brighter. The echo-Vess around them stopped flickering at its edges and simply... settled, like a house at evening when everyone is finally home. And the god —

The god changed.

It happened slowly enough to watch. The hunger didn't vanish — Lior doubted anything could make a god forget hunger entirely — but it *turned*, the way a river turns a millwheel instead of flooding the valley. The faces it wore stopped being weapons. They became, one by one, just faces: a mother, a market-woman, a priestess, a boy. And last of all, standing closest, it wore Aldous — but differently now. Smaller. Truer. An echo that knew it was an echo, and was at peace.

"Lior." Aldous's voice, and this time it was only memory speaking through borrowed air, and both of them knew it. "You clever, stubborn, impossible girl."

"Don't," she said, laughing and crying in the same breath. "Don't you dare say you're proud of me. I'll fall apart."

"I would never," said the echo gravely. "I'd say you finally learned to take care of people first." It smiled the crooked smile. "The magic took care of itself."

Then it bowed — the whole god bowed, every face at once, a field of bows like wheat in wind — and the hollow country began, gently, to let go.

* * *

They walked out together. There was no other way to describe it. The Door stood open behind them, and the hollow country poured past them like a tide going out — not dying, Lior understood somehow, but *going home*, dispersing into the world it had been cut off from, becoming weather and luck and the small warm feeling of a prayer answered kindly. The ban on magic had lived inside the guild's hoard, a lock on a stolen chest, and the chest was empty now, and the lock had nothing left to lock. Somewhere in Vess, at that moment, a thousand confiscated charms were waking up. Somewhere, a law written in ash was quietly ceasing to apply to anything.

Ossa closed the Door herself. It took all three keys and most of her strength, and when it was done she sat down hard on the chapel floor and announced, to no one in particular, "I am going to sleep for a week, and if the god needs anything else it can write."

Wren was crying. Lior gathered him up and he wrapped around her like ivy and said, muffled into her shoulder, "You're not magic anymore."

"Nope."

"None at all?"

"Not a spark. I tried to light a candle in there and the candle won."

He pulled back and looked at her with red-rimmed, deadly serious eyes. "Good," he said. "Now you'll have time for the songs."

And Lior — powerless, penniless, heir of nothing, keeper of no door — laughed until her ribs hurt, in a ruined chapel in a village that officially did not exist, while dawn came up ordinary and gold through windows that had waited a hundred years to see it.

Sarel stood apart, near the threshold, watching the last of the light go. She had her badge in her hand — the one she'd given Lior at the graveyard wall, the pass through the gates, the symbol of everything she'd been. She turned it over once, twice. Then she set it down on the altar, deliberately, like a woman closing an account.

"What will you do?" Lior asked.

Sarel considered the question with the seriousness she gave everything. "Vess has a great many records that need correcting," she said at last. "And I'm told I'm very thorough." A pause. "Also, someone owes an apothecary an apology and possibly a new shop. I'm told I'm very thorough at that sort of thing too."

"Tessin's going to yell at you for an hour."

"I know," Sarel said, and there it was again — the rusted hinge of a laugh. "I'm looking forward to it."

Outside, the road east filled with morning, and with pilgrims, and with the smell of bread from the priestess's ovens, and Lior Eastwick walked out of a country that no longer existed carrying nothing but its name — and found, to her astonishment, that it weighed nothing at all.

Behind them, deep beneath the chapel, something vast and fed and gentle turned over in its sleep, and dreamed — for the first time in a century — not of hunger, but of hands.

And far to the west, in a city waking to its first legal charm-market in living memory, a cartographer named Ivet unrolled a blank sheet of paper, dipped his pen, and wondered aloud to no one: *"How do you map a miracle?"*
