## 38. A Country That No Longer Exists

Ivet had spent his whole life drawing lines on paper and calling them true, so he supposed it was fitting that the last thing he ever mapped was a place that refused to hold still.

The hollow country was fading. He could see it happening the way a man sees frost leave a window — not all at once, but in patches, warmth eating inward from the edges. The street they had walked that morning, when the Door first opened onto it, still stood: a crooked lane of shuttered houses with roofs like folded hands. But the house at the end of the lane had gone soft at its corners, its chimney dissolving into a gray suggestion of a chimney, and when Ivet squinted at it he could see straight through the wall to the hillside beyond, which was not a hillside at all but the inside of Errow's chapel cellar, seen from underneath, like a memory of a room.

He sat down on a step that might or might not have been there an hour ago and got out his notebook.

"You're *drawing*?" Ossa said. She stood a few paces off with her arms crossed, watching the horizon of the hollow country unwrite itself. "Now?"

"I'm a cartographer," Ivet said. "This is what we do at the end of things. We take notes."

"It's disappearing."

"Then I'd best be quick." He wet his pencil stub against his tongue. "Ossa, do you know how many maps I've made in my life? Forty years of them. Coastlines, river fords, tax districts, the road from Vess to the salt marshes drawn eleven separate times because some magistrate kept insisting the river had moved." He gestured with the pencil at the dissolving lane. "Not one of them was this interesting."

The hollow country was Vess, or had been Vess once, or was everything Vess had been afraid to remember. Its streets matched the capital street for street, but older — no Inquisition towers, no ash-marked temples, just the city as it might have looked before the Purge, before anyone decided the gods were a problem to be solved with fire. And threaded through it, everywhere, a hunger. Ivet had felt it the moment they crossed the threshold: a pulling sensation behind his eyes, as if the country itself were looking at him and considering whether he'd be better eaten than greeted.

It wasn't looking anymore. Lior had seen to that.

* * *

She had done it an hour ago, though Ivet's sense of time had gone strange in here, the way it did in fog or grief.

They had stood in the god's hall — a great chamber built of every church the Purge had burned, their stones gathered up and stacked into something new — and the god had worn Aldous's face, and Wren's mother's face, and Sarel's father's face, and finally, mercifully, no face at all, just a shape like lamplight through a doorway. It had offered Lior everything. Her childhood back, memory by memory. Her master alive. A country restored, stone by stone, if only she would stay and keep it company. Keep it fed.

And Lior Eastwick — who Ivet had known for barely two months and had already watched lie to an Inquisitor, dig up a mass grave, and sing a binding hymn to a drowning child without once losing her composure — had looked at the thing wearing her dead master's face and said, "No. But I have something better."

Then she had opened her hands, and the guild's inheritance had come out of her.

Ivet didn't have words for what that looked like, and he resented it, because words were supposed to be his trade too. It was light, but not light. It was weight leaving a room. It was forty years of hoarded magic — everything the Hollow Guild had ever gathered and sealed and stolen and saved — pouring out of one tired woman in a red coat and going home to the thing that had been starved of it since before either of them were born. The hall had filled with it. The hunger had stopped. Ivet had felt it stop the way you feel a headache lift: suddenly aware of how long it had been there.

And then the ban on magic — the law written into the world itself, the reason his sister had been arrested over a fever-ward, the reason half the graves outside Vess existed — had simply ended. Not repealed. Ended, like a held breath released.

Lior had swayed. Sarel had caught her. And the god — faceless now, and quieter than Ivet had ever heard anything be quiet — had begun, very gently, to let its country go.

* * *

"You should rest," Ossa said now. She had lowered herself onto the step beside him with the care of a woman whose knees had opinions. "We all should. When we're out."

"When we're out," Ivet repeated. "You say that like it's certain."

"The Door is open. The god is letting go. What's uncertain about it?"

"What's uncertain," Ivet said, shading in the outline of a house that was currently only sixty percent committed to existing, "is whether the way out is still where we left it. Places that are being unmade don't always keep their doors." He glanced at her. "You knew about this place. You cleansed its records. Did you know it would fade?"

Ossa was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer.

"I knew it couldn't last," she said finally. "A prison needs a keeper, and the keeper was gone. I thought it would collapse the day Aldous died. It didn't. It just got hungrier." She rubbed her hands together, an old habit, scrubbing at something invisible. "Forty years I filed papers for the people who burned my guild, Ivet. Do you know why? Because someone had to remember what was actually in the archive. Someone had to sit inside the lie and keep the truth dusted." She snorted. "And then a girl from the night market walks in and does more in a week than I managed in four decades."

"That's not fair to you."

"No," Ossa agreed. "But it's true, and I'm too old to pretend otherwise." She nodded at his notebook. "What will you call it? On the map."

Ivet considered. The lane was nearly gone now; the houses had become suggestions, then impressions, then a faint texture in the air like breath on glass. Beyond them, the false skyline of old Vess was going the same way, tower by tower, gently, almost politely, the way snow leaves a field.

"A Country That No Longer Exists," he said. "That's the title. Underneath, in small letters: *population, one god, recently fed.*"

Ossa laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a door opening after years shut. "He'll haunt you for that."

"He can try. I'm told he's full now."

* * *

They found the others near the well.

Of course there was a well. Every place worth remembering had a well, and the hollow country remembered everything. This one was dry, its bucket long rotted, but the stones around its mouth were worn smooth in the pattern of generations of hands, and Ivet found himself wanting to draw it before it went, and hating himself a little for wanting it, and drawing it anyway.

Wren was standing at the well's edge, peering down into the dark.

"Don't," Sarel said, without heat. She sat on the mounting block with Lior's head on her shoulder. Lior was asleep, or close to it — gray-faced, hands empty, breathing the slow breath of someone who has set down something enormously heavy and is only now noticing how much it weighed. "There's nothing down there but the bottom of a chapel cellar, and you've had enough of falling into things."

"I wasn't going to fall in," Wren said, with the tremendous dignity of a nine-year-old. "I was listening."

"To what?"

"The quiet." He turned around. His face, which for weeks had been a mask other voices wore, was entirely his own again — freckled, stubborn, a scrape healing on one knee. "It's been shouting my whole life. Since the fever. And now it's just..." He searched for the word. "Full. Like after supper."

Ivet felt something tighten in his chest. "Do you miss it?"

Wren considered this seriously, the way he considered everything. "Sometimes it sang," he said. "When nobody was asking it for things. Nobody ever asked it for songs." He shrugged. "But I get to go home now. Mam says we can move out of the tanner's row. She says—" His voice wobbled, steadied. "She says the whole city heard what the lady read off the temple steps. About the Purge. Everybody knows now."

"Everybody knows," Sarel said quietly. From anyone else it would have been pride. From her it sounded like a debt being counted.

"And you," Ivet said to her. "What happens to the famous Inquisitor Sarel Vane, traitor to her own order, freed from the cells by illegal magic?"

"She testifies," Sarel said. "Before the citizen council, in front of whoever cares to come. All of it. The archive, the grave, the prison we were guarding and called a victory." She looked down at Lior, asleep against her shoulder, and her voice went softer than Ivet had ever heard it. "And then I find honest work, gods help me. I'm told the night market could use someone who knows which charms are real."

"It could use someone who knows which sellers to leave alone," Ivet said.

"That too."

* * *

The country thinned around them as they talked.

It went the way Ivet imagined a dream goes when the sleeper begins to wake: not violently, not collapsing, but loosening, its edges giving up their pretense. The burned churches that made the god's hall let go of their stones one by one, each stone drifting apart like leaves on slow water. The crooked lane un-crooked itself into nothing. Somewhere a bell that had never been rung rang once, far off, out of courtesy or habit, and did not ring again.

And through it all, the god's presence — vast, patient, newly gentle — moved among them like a host showing guests to the door.

It did not speak. Not in words. But Ivet felt it pass him, and in passing it left something in him, the way a draft leaves cold in a room: an image, brief and unbearably clear. A map. Not of the hollow country — of the real one, the waking one, Vess and Errow and the salt marshes and the eastern roads, every ford and footpath, accurate down to the width of a sheep track. It lasted three heartbeats and was gone, and Ivet understood it as what it was: a gift. A thank-you. *Here,* it said, without saying. *Since you love lines on paper. These ones are true.*

He was crying before he knew it, quietly, pencil still moving.

"Did you see it too?" Wren whispered beside him.

"See what?"

"The map." The boy's eyes were wide. "It showed me the road home. All the way to the tanner's row. There's a shortcut past the dyer's yard I never knew about."

Ivet laughed, wiped his face with his sleeve, and got out the notebook one last time. "Hold still," he told Wren. "Tell me about this shortcut. I'm making a map, and for once in my life, I intend to get everything right."

* * *

The Door stood open at the end of the last street that was still a street.

Beyond it: the chapel cellar of Errow, lamplit, smelling of dust and candlewax and ordinary stone. Ordinary. Ivet had never loved a word so much.

They went through in no particular order. Ossa first, because she claimed old women shouldn't be made to wait in vanishing countries on principle. Then Wren, who paused at the threshold, looked back once at the fading gold of the god's hall, and bowed — actually bowed, solemn as a little magistrate — before stepping through. Sarel carried Lior, who had woken enough to insist she could walk and been unanimously overruled. Ivet went last, because cartographers go last. Someone has to check the edges.

At the threshold he stopped and turned around.

The hollow country was almost gone. What remained was a single image hanging in the gold: the well, dry and smooth-stoned, with the ghost of a bucket rope swaying though there was no wind. And beside it, faint as breath on glass, a figure — no face, no name, just a shape like lamplight through a doorway — raising one hand. Not farewell exactly. More like the gesture of a man at a window, watching travelers he is fond of walk safely out of sight.

*I was here,* the gesture said. *I was hungry, and I was here, and now I am neither. Remember me accurately.*

"I will," Ivet said aloud. "Every line of you."

He stepped through, and the country that no longer exists closed behind him like a book whose story is finished, and the cellar of a small chapel in Errow received five tired people and smelled, blessedly, of absolutely nothing magical at all.

* * *

Dawn was coming up over Errow when they climbed out of the cellar into the priestess's garden.

It was, Ivet thought, aggressively ordinary dawn. Gray-gold light on wet grass. A rooster somewhere making its idiot announcement. Smoke from bread ovens. The smell of rain that had fallen in the night and meant to fall again. Two village women were already arguing cheerfully by the garden gate about whose turn it was to fetch water, and neither of them had any idea that the world had changed twice before breakfast.

Lior stood in the grass with her face tipped up to the light. She looked emptied out — Ivet kept thinking of wells, of rooms with the furniture gone — but standing. When she lowered her head, her eyes were wet and she was smiling, which seemed to Ivet like the correct response to surviving your own inheritance.

"Well," she said. Her voice came out rough. She tried again. "Well. That's that."

"That's that," Sarel agreed.

"I can't feel it anymore. Any of it. The guild's power — it's like a tooth that's been pulled. My tongue keeps going to the gap." She flexed her fingers, opened and closed them. "I keep waiting for it to hurt worse than it does."

"It might yet," Ossa said practically. "Grief usually bills you later."

"Thank you, Ossa. Truly, your comfort is a balm."

"I'm told honesty is a balm. People lie to me constantly about this."

Wren had spotted the women at the gate and was already halfway across the garden, shouting his mother's name before remembering his mother was in Vess, seventy miles east, and settling instead for telling the startled women, at volume, that the gods were awake and the ban was over and there was a shortcut past the dyer's yard. One of the women dropped her water jug. The other crossed herself with the wrong hand, which in Errow, Ivet was beginning to understand, was probably the right one.

The priestess appeared in the chapel doorway with a tray of bread and tea, took in the scene — the fugitives, the ex-Inquisitor, the archivist, the cartographer, the child evangelizing the water-carriers — and said, "Sit down, all of you, before you fall down. Whatever you've done, it will keep until you've eaten."

For once, Ivet reflected, taking the bench nearest the bread, someone in authority was completely correct.

He ate. It was good bread. The sun came up properly, gilding the wet garden, and the rooster kept on, and the two women at the gate had stopped arguing and started praying, quietly, experimentally, the way people test ice — and when one of them gasped, Ivet chose not to look. Some answers belonged to the asker.

Instead he opened his notebook to a fresh page and began, in careful strokes, the map of the road east: Errow to the ford, the ford to the high road, the high road home to Vess, with the shortcut past the dyer's yard marked in Wren's honor. At the top of the page he wrote the title, and under the title, small, the dedication:

*For the one who was hungry, and is full. Drawn from memory, and from a gift.*

Lior leaned over to look. "What will you do with it?" she asked. "Maps of a country that doesn't exist?"

"This one's of a country that does," Ivet said. "That's rather the point. Everything I drew before was missing something and I never knew what." He tapped the eastern edge of the page, where the road ran off toward home. "Besides — word travels fast when a nation finds out it's been lied to for a century. There'll be pilgrims on that road within the month. Pilgrims get lost. Somebody ought to draw them an honest map."

"Pilgrims also argue," Sarel said into her tea. "I've arrested hundreds."

"Then perhaps you'll come along and apologize to them individually."

Sarel choked. Even Lior laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her, bright and cracked and human — and the sound carried across the garden, and the rooster fell silent as if in respect, and the morning went on being ordinary, which was the whole miracle of it.

Only later, much later, when the bread was gone and the others dozing in the sun, did Ivet notice the last line his pencil had written without his quite meaning to — a small note in the margin of the map, in his own handwriting, though he had no memory of writing it:

*The road home is open. So is every other one. Choose kindly.*

He looked at it for a long time.

Then, in the distance, down the eastern road, he saw the first of the pilgrims coming — dozens of them, then scores, walking toward Errow out of a country that had just learned its own name — and he closed the notebook, and went to wake the others.
