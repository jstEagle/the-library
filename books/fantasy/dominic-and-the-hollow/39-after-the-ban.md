## 39. After the Ban

The first charm Lior sold after the ban ended was a luck-knot, and she charged four coppers for it, which was robbery, and she knew it, and the woman buying it knew it, and they both smiled about it anyway.

"Three," said the woman.

"Four. It's got real knotwork. Look at the tension on that third loop. That's guild-grade tension."

"It's got a hole in it."

"That's where the luck breathes."

The woman laughed, paid four coppers, and walked off into the morning market of Vess holding a piece of braided string like it was a crown jewel, which, in a manner of speaking, it was. Two years ago Lior would have been hanged for selling it. One year ago she'd have been hanged faster, because it would have worked. Now a constable strolled past her stall, glanced at the little table of fever-wards and road-charms and knots for safe childbirth, nodded at her like she was a fishmonger, and moved on.

Lior sat back on her stool and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since she was nine years old.

The stall itself was nothing. A plank across two barrels, a striped awning Tessin had insisted on — "You need color, Lior, you dress like a funeral" — and a hand-painted sign that read EASTWICK CHARMS, HONESTLY WEAK. Tessin had painted it himself, in his careful apothecary's lettering, and had refused to explain the joke until she pointed out that nothing on the table was weak at all anymore. Then he'd shrugged and said, "Honesty in advertising. The coppers will come back when they learn the difference."

They had come back. They came back every morning now.

The strange thing — the thing she still hadn't gotten used to, six weeks after the Door closed and the hollow country folded up like a letter — was that the charms worked without her. That was the whole shape of the new world, and she still stumbled over it daily. She braided a fever-ward out of red thread and dried yarrow, and it worked, not because Lior Eastwick put anything into it, but because the god under Vess — the god that was no longer under Vess, that was now simply *in* Vess, in the mortar and the gutters and the morning bread — heard the small hope of whoever tied it on and answered, gently, the way you'd answer a child asking for a lamp lit.

She had given away her inheritance to make that possible. Some mornings it felt like losing an arm. Some mornings it felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was carrying. Most mornings it was both before breakfast.

"Four coppers," she said to the next customer, an old man wanting a charm for his goat. "Goats are extra. Goats know what you did."

* * *

The council chamber had been a temple once, back when temples were places you were told what to think instead of places you argued about it. Now the Vess Citizen Council met beneath a ceiling where someone had chiseled out the old god's face and left the chisel marks, because the council's first official act had been to vote that the marks should stay. *So we remember we did it*, the resolution read. Lior had liked them ever since.

She sat in the public gallery on the day Sarel Vane testified, and the gallery was full, and the room smelled of wet wool and nervous sweat and someone's contraband breakfast sausage.

Sarel looked smaller than Lior remembered. That was the first thing. The Inquisitor's coat was gone, replaced by a plain gray citizen's jacket that fit her badly, as if she'd bought it in a hurry, which she had, because the Inquisition's property had been seized along with everything else. Her hair was cropped short — prison hair grown out to something practical — and her hands, those hands that had signed a hundred burn orders and then one badge pressed into a fugitive's palm at a graveyard wall, lay flat and still on the table before her.

The council's chair, a broad-voiced dyer named Halva Ott, read the charges of inquiry aloud. Not charges against Sarel. Charges the council wanted Sarel to answer *about*. What the Inquisition had known. When it had known it. What the Purge had actually been.

Sarel answered for four hours.

Lior had read the transcript afterward — everyone had; the council published it in installments and the installments sold faster than bread — but reading it was different from watching the woman say it. Watching her say: *We were not guarding the city from the gods. We were guarding the gods from the city, and calling the prison a victory so we would not have to look at the door.* Watching her say: *I burned charms for eleven years. I believed I was keeping a fire from spreading. I was keeping a wound from being tended.* Watching her pause, once, for a long time, when a councilor asked whether she regretted the executions, and watching her say, "No. Regret is for things you would not do again knowing what you know. I would do all of it again, believing what I believed then. That is what frightens me. That is why I am here."

The gallery had gone very quiet at that. Lior had found herself gripping the rail, thinking: *that's the most honest thing anyone's said in this room since it was a temple.*

And then, near the end, a young councilor with ink-stained fingers — Pellit, the one from the tanners' quarter who asked all the uncomfortable questions — leaned forward and said, "Inquisitor Vane. You held the door open for them. At the graveyard. You gave them your badge. Why?"

Sarel had looked at the gallery. Lior was fairly sure she'd looked at her, specifically, though the light was bad and Lior had been wearing a hood out of old habit.

"Because someone asked me for the truth in writing," Sarel said, "and I found I had already written it, and could not unwrite it, and did not want to. Once you have read what we did, you have two choices. You can spend the rest of your life keeping the reading a secret. Or you can spend it being read." A pause. "I was tired of being the secret."

The council had cleared her of criminal action — *coerced belief under institutional deception*, the finding read, a phrase that would be argued about in taverns for a decade — but had stripped her of any office and required her to submit to a public accounting every season for five years. She had agreed before the vote finished. Lior heard afterward that she'd said, "I'd have asked for ten."

Walking out of the chamber into the cold bright afternoon, Lior had caught up with her on the steps. They stood there a moment, two women who had once been the two most dangerous people in each other's lives, now squinting at each other in ordinary daylight.

"You look terrible," Sarel said.

"You're wearing a jacket that fits you like a sack of turnips."

"It was the only one they had. Everything else was Inquisitor gray." She looked down at it. "This is also Inquisitor gray. I have a type, apparently."

Lior laughed, and it surprised her, the laugh, coming up out of her like something uncorked. Sarel almost smiled. Almost. It was a start, and they both knew it was a start, and neither of them pretended it was anything finished.

"Teaching, they tell me," Sarel said. "The guild songs. As history."

"Who told you that?"

"Tessin. He buys my herbs now. He talks more than he used to. I think nearly being hanged loosened something in him." She adjusted the terrible jacket. "History. Not spellcraft."

"They're the same songs," Lior said. "That's the point. What made them spells was never the words. It was who was listening." She shrugged. "Now everyone's listening. So they're just songs. Good ones. Aldous had an ear."

Sarel was quiet a moment. "I attended one of your lessons. Last week. The children didn't see me."

"I saw you."

"I know. You were singing about a door."

"It's a song about a door. There are a lot of those."

"You were singing it like an apology," Sarel said, and then, before Lior could decide whether to be angry, she added: "It was a good door. Whatever it cost. I've read the accounts of what was behind it. I know what you fed it instead of a person." She looked out over the rooftops of Vess, all those chimneys, all that smoke, all those prayers rising with the smoke now, unanswered in the old hungry way and answered in the new gentle one. "I spent my life building a lock. You built a table and set a meal on it. I'm still deciding which of us was the more frightening."

"Both," Lior said. "Definitely both. We should start a club."

"I don't join clubs."

"You'd be the founding member. It's very exclusive."

Sarel looked at her for a long moment, and then — this was the thing Lior would tell Tessin later, and Tessin wouldn't believe her — Sarel Vane laughed. Once. A short, rusty, appalled sound, like a door that hadn't been opened in years.

"I have to go," she said. "I'm late for my accounting."

"You're always late for your accounting."

"It's the only appointment I enjoy being late to."

* * *

The school was a rented room above a cooper's shop, with a slate board Tessin had salvaged from somewhere and eleven children who came because their parents sent them and stayed because Lior fed them honey-bread at the half-mark of the lesson, which she considered not bribery but pedagogy.

"Again from the top," she said, "and Wren, if you sing the descant over everyone one more time I'm giving your part to Marta."

Wren grinned at her with the gap where his front tooth had come out, a gap he was enormously proud of, and put his hand down. He was nine now, or said he was nine; there had been some debate. He lived with the priestess's order in Errow half the year and with Lior in Vess the other half, an arrangement nobody had planned and everybody tolerated, least of all Wren, who considered both households insufficiently attentive to his opinions.

They sang the binding hymn — the one that had pulled him out of the crowd's fervor at the well, the one that had once been a spell and was now, on an ordinary gray afternoon above a cooper's shop, just seven lines of Old Hollowtongue that the children pronounced with the cheerful butchery of the young. *Hollow-tong*, Marta said it. Lior had stopped correcting her.

The song was about a door. It was about holding a door, actually, and about who holds it, and why, and what it costs, and the melody went up at the end like a question, because Aldous had written it and Aldous had never met a melody that didn't deserve to end in a question.

Lior sang the descant herself when Wren behaved, and when he misbehaved she sang it anyway under her breath, because the truth was the descant had been hers since she was their age, and some inheritances you give away and some you just keep in your mouth where no one can tax them.

The god remembered her. That was the other thing. She had no power — none, not a spark; she'd checked, in the early weeks, with the desperate thoroughness of a woman patting her pockets for a lost purse — but when she sang, the city listened in a way it didn't listen for anyone else. Candles steadied. Fevered children slept. Once, memorably, a whole street's worth of rain had held off for exactly the length of the hymn and then come down all at once, like an audience applauding. She never asked for anything. She was very careful never to ask. But the god remembered who had fed it, and it was, in its strange half-mad now-settled way, grateful, and its gratitude leaked out sideways in small weather and small mercies, and Lior had decided to accept this the way you accept a cat that insists on sleeping on your chest: with resigned affection and a certain amount of hair in your mouth.

The lesson ended. Honey-bread was distributed. The children clattered down the stairs, and Wren lingered, the way he did.

"He's not in the song," Wren said.

"Who isn't?"

"Aldous. The song's about the door and the holder, but not him. He wrote it and he's not in it."

Lior busied herself stacking the song-slates. "He didn't like being in songs. He said a song with yourself in it is a mirror, and a mirror is the worst place to keep your face."

"That's a weird thing to say."

"He said a lot of weird things. That's what masters are for."

Wren considered this, then said, with the terrifying directness of children: "Do you miss him more or less now?"

Lior sat down on the slate board's ledge. Outside, Vess went about its business — carts, gulls, a neighbor yelling about a fence, the ordinary noise of a city that had stopped being afraid of its own prayers.

"More," she said. "Which I didn't expect. When he was alive I missed who I thought he was. Now he's dead I get to miss who he actually was. It's a better miss. It's heavier, but it's better." She looked at the boy. "That probably doesn't make sense."

"It does," Wren said, and patted her hand twice, the way you'd comfort a horse, and left.

* * *

Tessin found her at the stall at closing, as he did most evenings, ostensibly to inspect her inventory and actually to eat her dinner.

"Road-charms are up," he reported, mouth full. "Everyone's traveling now that the provinces are on the maps again. Ivet sent a new one, by the way. The eastern sheets. He's marked the old guild roads in blue."

"Blue?"

"He says the roads that used to lead to guildhalls should be a color that means *something was here*. He's lobbying the cartographers' council to adopt it officially. They're resisting. He's delighted about the resisting. I think he'd have been disappointed if they'd just said yes."

Lior smiled. Ivet's maps hung in the council chamber now — the full set, the erased villages restored, Errow on the page at last with its true name and a small notation in the corner: *population stubborn*. The cartographers' guild had made him an honorary member, which he treated as a personal insult and wore with pride.

She looked at her table. Fever-wards, luck-knots, road-charms, a small jar of what she labeled *grief salve* and what was, honestly, just good lavender oil, because some things should stay just what they are. The sigil was stitched openly on her awning now — the hollow circle of the guild, the door seen edge-on. Children pointed at it. Old people crossed themselves in the old way and then, catching themselves, laughed at their own hands.

"Tessin," she said. "Do you ever think about what we were?"

He chewed. Swallowed. Took his time, the way he did when a question mattered.

"I think about what we were *for*," he said. "We were for hiding. That was the job, at the end. Hiding things well enough that hiding could go on forever." He picked up a luck-knot, turned it over. "I don't miss it. I miss the people. The hiding itself, I'd burn again, and I say that as a man who once burned his own shop, so I know my burns."

"You could come back. Teach. The guild's not — I mean, it's not a guild anymore, it's a curriculum, but still."

"Lior." He set the knot down and looked at her with the mild, immovable face he'd used to deny everything to an Inquisitor and to slip her a map fragment under the same breath. "I spent thirty years being a guildsman in secret. I am now a licensed apothecary *in the open*, with a shop, and a sign, and a cat who has opinions. I am never again going to be part of anything I have to explain to a constable." He stood, brushed crumbs from his coat. "But I'll audit your lessons. For the honey-bread."

"It's for the children."

"It's for the honey-bread, and you know it."

* * *

That night she walked home the long way, past the temple steps where Sarel had read the truth aloud, past the wall where the last prayer-burn had been — it had been filled with pale mortar, and someone had planted a crocus in it, and the crocus had come up, because the god had a sense of humor about its old scars — past the cooper's, past the market square where the night market was setting up in the blue dusk, sellers calling out charms and candles and hot chestnuts in the same breath, all of it legal, all of it ordinary, all of it the most extraordinary thing Lior Eastwick had ever seen, because she had crossed a country that didn't exist to make an evening market possible.

At her door there was a letter. No seal, no name, just her name on the front in a hand she knew — a careful, clerk's hand, the hand of a woman who had written a hundred reports and one unsent truth.

She opened it standing in the cold.

*Eastwick —*

*The council has approved the fifth season of public accounting, and I find I have nothing left to account for, which the council finds suspicious, which I find funny. Pellit has proposed a formal commission on the sealed archives — full publication, everything the Inquisition ever took. They want someone to lead it who was on the inside. They asked me. I said I would think about it, which is what I said about the jacket, and you remember how that went.*

*Also: the grave. The unmarked one. The council wants it marked. They want a name on it and they want it to be a grand name and I told them no, because I have read your songs, and the last verse is about a door with no name on it, and I think you would say the grave is the same kind of door.*

*Tell me if I'm wrong. You are the only person I know who is allowed to tell me I'm wrong.*

*— V.*

Lior read it twice, then stood a while in the dark with the letter in her hand, listening to the market sounds fading, the city settling, the small patient breathing of a god in the walls.

Then she went inside, found paper, and began the reply — and got exactly as far as *Vane —* before she heard, from the direction of the temple steps, clear across the evening quiet, a single voice singing the binding hymn's descant. A child's voice. Not Wren. Not any of her eleven.

A voice she had never heard before, singing a song that had been a spell, in a city that had stopped being afraid — singing it slightly wrong, the way all the best inheritances start.

Lior set down the pen, put on her coat, and went to find out who was listening.
