## 40. The Name We Carried Out

The road out of Vess had been widened twice since Lior last walked it, and both times the widening had been paid for by people who remembered being afraid.

She noticed such things now. She noticed everything, the way a person notices a room after the furniture has been sold — all the shapes on the floor where things used to stand. A year of being powerless had taught her that the world was mostly made of absences, and that some of them were kind.

The hill outside Vess still bore its old name, though no one said it the old way anymore. The Convicts' Field, the Inquisition had called it, in the years when calling it anything else was itself a crime. Now the city council had voted — three sessions, two shouting matches, one resignation — to call it the Field of the Answered. Lior thought that was too pretty. She had voted, in the small way a woman with no office and no magic could vote, which was to say she had told Ivet her opinion over dinner and let him paint it onto a map that would outlive every council.

*The Field of the Purge Dead*, his map said. Plain as a stone.

She climbed the hill in the gray-gold light of early morning, a year to the day since the Door had closed — or rather, since the Door had stopped needing to be anything but a door. Her boots knew the path. Her body remembered the weight of the spade even though her hands no longer remembered how to hold anything but chalk and thread and a child's small fingers learning a song.

The graves had no markers. That had been the Inquisition's final cruelty, or its final cowardice — the convicted were buried in rows so straight they might have been ruled with a string, and then left nameless, so that even grief would have nothing to hold. But grief is a stubborn craftsman. In the year since the ban had died, the families had come with thread and ribbon and scraps of tin, and now the field wore its names the way a coat wears patches: crooked, mismatched, unmistakably loved. *Aldous Eastwick*, said one, in Tessin's careful hand, though Tessin had been dead three years and someone else had copied it. *Maren Cole*, though Maren had died rich and afraid and forgiven, which is more than most of us get. *Ossa Venn*, who had never had a grave and had been given one anyway, because the living need somewhere to put their hands.

Lior walked the rows until she reached the patch of ground that had no marker at all — the hundreds who had no families left to name them, the ones whose names existed now only in a ledger Sarel had spent four months reconstructing from confiscated records, and in Ivet's maps, and in the songs.

She sat down in the grass. It was damp. She was fifty-one years old and her knees had opinions, and she sat down anyway, the way you do at a grave.

* * *

"You're early," said a voice behind her. "The ceremony's not till noon. The council will be insufferable about it. There's going to be a *ribbon*."

Lior didn't turn around. She didn't need to. "You climbed this whole hill to insult a ribbon."

"I climbed this whole hill because I knew you'd be here, and someone has to keep you from doing something sentimental and irreversible. It's the anniversary. Last year on this date you gave away the single largest hoard of magical power in recorded history because you *felt sorry for a god*. I live in terror of anniversaries now."

Sarel Vane came around and sat down beside her with the graceless efficiency of a woman who had once knelt in mud for a living and had never quite unlearned it. She was gray at the temples now, and she wore no badge — she'd surrendered the last one at the gates a year ago, the one she'd used as a pass, and the council had offered her a new one and she'd laughed at them. She testified instead. Fourteen months of testimony. The transcripts ran to eleven volumes, and Ivet had them all, and had read them all, and cried at volume six, though he denied it.

"The ribbon is Wren's idea," Lior said.

"Of course it is." Sarel's voice did the thing it always did around Wren's name — softened by a half-degree, guarded against anyone noticing. "He's sixteen. He wanted the field to look like it was *loved*. He said—" she paused, and Lior watched her choose honesty over armor, a habit she'd been practicing for a year and still found expensive, "—he said the dead here were the only ones who never got to hear the songs. So the ribbon is so they can find the field. From wherever they are."

Lior looked out over the rows. "That's the most theological thing anyone's said in this city since the Purge."

"Don't tell him. He'll want to write it down. He writes everything down now. He's going to be insufferable at university."

"He got in, then."

"First try. Hollowtongue counts as a classical language, apparently. There's an entire department now." Sarel picked at the grass. "They asked him who taught him. He said, 'a dead man and a woman who can't do magic anymore.' They thought it was a joke."

"It was a joke."

"It was *mostly* a joke," Sarel allowed.

They sat in the quiet that old friends earn and new friends can't afford. Below them, Vess was waking up in its new way — smoke from bakeries that sold openly charmed luck-bread, the night market setting up in daylight now because there was nothing left to hide from, a bell in the temple district that rang the hour and nothing else, because the Inquisition's bell had been recast into three smaller bells for three smaller purposes, none of which involved fear.

"You know what's strange," Sarel said. "A year ago I could have burned every charm in that market with a word. Now I can't light a candle without flint like everyone else, and I don't miss it. I keep waiting to miss it."

"Do you miss it?"

"I miss being *certain*." She said it plainly, the way she'd learned to say everything. "Certainty was warm. It was wrong, but it was warm. Some mornings I wake up and I'd trade the whole truth for a little warmth." She glanced sideways. "You don't get to say anything. You traded actual memories for a stranger's freedom and you've been cheerful about it ever since. It's obscene."

"I'm not cheerful," Lior said. "I'm *practiced*."

It was the truest thing she'd said in a month, and Sarel heard it, and did the other thing she'd learned this year, which was to put her hand briefly on Lior's shoulder and then pretend she hadn't.

* * *

The memories were not gone, exactly. That was the strangest part, the part she'd never found words for and had stopped trying to.

She knew she had had a childhood. She knew its shape the way you know a house you lived in before it burned: where the stairs were, which floorboard sang, which window caught the morning. She could not walk through it. She could not open the door of it. But the shape was there, and sometimes, at the edge of sleep, she could feel warmth coming off it like a wall that had held a fire.

Her mother's face: gone. The sound of her mother's voice: gone. But she knew her mother had hummed while cooking, because the knowing had survived even though the hearing hadn't, the way a melody survives in the body after the words are lost. She had told Ivet once, late, over the last of the wine, that it was like being given a book with the pages pressed flat — you could see where the words were, you could feel the weight of them, you just couldn't read them anymore.

And Ivet, who mapped erased places for a living, had said: "Then you and the villages have something in common. Some things stay on the map even after the map is burned. The shape holds."

She had cried, a little. She was not ashamed of it. Shame was another thing the year had burned off her, along with the magic.

* * *

The sun climbed. People began to come up the hill — not the council, not yet, the council with its ribbon would come at noon with trumpets and a speech Sarel had already offered to heckle — but the early ones, the quiet ones, the families who came at dawn the way they had come for a year now, before the crowds, when the field still belonged to the dead.

Lior watched them and did the thing she came to do.

She carried chalk in her pocket. Real chalk, guild chalk, though there was no guild anymore and the chalk was just chalk. She went down the rows to the nameless section — the long, straight rows where the markers were still only ribbons and river stones and one ambitious wooden sign someone had carved that read *THEY WERE OURS* — and she knelt, and she began to write names in the dirt.

Not real names. She didn't have them; Sarel's ledger had recovered four hundred and eleven, but there were more than that in the ground, and some would never come back. So Lior wrote the names the songs had given them. The guild had a song for every trade, every virtue, every kind of death, and in the old days the singers had used the names of the dead as verses. She wrote *Mender* and *Lantern* and *Keeper of Small Doors*. She wrote *The One Who Stayed* and *The One Who Ran and Came Back*. She wrote *Aldous* last, though Aldous had his own stone now, because she had never once visited that stone without also visiting the nameless rows, and she wasn't going to start dividing the dead into the remembered and the forgotten on her watch.

The chalk wore down. Her knees ached. A little girl — someone's daughter, maybe six, wearing the fever-ward charm that every child in Vess wore now, openly, on a red string — came and stood beside her and watched with the frank rudeness of the very young.

"What are the words?"

"Names," Lior said. "For the people who didn't get any."

"Are they their *real* names?"

"No. Their real names are lost. These are the names we carry out for them."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "My mama says you used to be able to do magic."

"I could."

"What happened?"

"I spent it," Lior said. "On something worth more."

The girl looked at her the way children look at adults who say things they can't yet price — with suspicion, and a little pity. Then she crouched down, took a stick, and scratched something into the dirt beside Lior's chalk names. Lior looked. It was a lopsided little sun, or a flower, or possibly a cat.

"What's that one called?"

"*Hope*," the girl said, in the tone of someone stating the obvious, and ran off.

Lior sat back on her heels and laughed until her eyes stung, and if a little of it was crying, the field was polite enough not to say.

* * *

Noon came, and the council, and the ribbon — a truly enormous red thing that took six people to unfurl and was strung between two poles at the field's gate, and which Sarel heckled exactly as promised, quietly, only to Lior, which was the only way she heckled anything anymore. Wren spoke. He was sixteen and tall and his voice was changing and he hated it, and he stood up in front of four hundred people and recited the guild's naming litany in Old Hollowtongue, the language that had come out of a fevered child's mouth and started everything, and the words went out over the field like seed.

Lior did not speak. She had spoken last year, and the year would have its own turn, and besides, she had learned from Aldous — the real Aldous, the false idol Aldous, the Aldous she had loved and grieved and finally, painfully, understood — that the best teachers say less every year until at the end they are mostly silence and their students are mostly the lesson.

She stood at the back. Ivet found her there, as he always did, a map tube over one shoulder and ink on three fingers, and stood beside her without asking why she wasn't at the front, because he had never once in twelve years asked her a question whose answer he could read off her face.

"They're voting on the Errow road next month," he murmured. "A real road. Pilgrims walk it in mud half the year."

"Errow doesn't want a real road."

"Errow wants a *choosable* road," Ivet corrected. "That's different. That's the whole difference, isn't it? Everything we did — it was all just trying to make things choosable."

Lior watched the crowd, the red string charms, the ribbons, Wren's ears going pink as the crowd applauded a dead language. "When did you get wise?"

"Professionally. It's in the cartographer's oath. *I shall not map what is not there, and I shall never again map away what is.*" He said it lightly, but she heard the guilt underneath, the old guilt, the maps he'd drawn for the Inquisition in his exile years, the villages he'd helped erase. He carried his own field of names. Everyone she loved did.

* * *

She stayed after they all went down the hill. She always did. The council folded its ribbon, the families drifted home to supper, the little girl with the stick was hoisted onto someone's shoulders, and the field emptied the way a church empties — reluctantly, in a hush.

The light went long and gold. Lior walked the rows one more time, touching the ribbons as she passed, and came at last to the far corner where the grass grew thickest, where the digging had been deepest, where the god under Vess had stirred in its sleep the night they'd opened the ground. There was nothing there now. There was nothing there now *everywhere* — the hollows in the walls had healed, the scorched prayer-marks had faded to faint shadows like old bruises, the hungry thing beneath the city had been fed not with stolen prayer but with a guild's whole hoarded inheritance, and had, in the manner of all well-fed creatures, gone quiet and almost kind.

Almost. She still felt it sometimes, at the edge of sleep. Not a hook behind her ribs anymore. More like a hand resting lightly between her shoulder blades. *I remember you*, it seemed to say. *You fed me. I keep what I'm given.*

She knelt in the grass at the field's far corner.

She had not prayed in a year. Not truly. She whispered things at the grave sometimes, the way everyone does, but a prayer is a different animal — a prayer is a request with a door in it, and she had closed every door in herself on purpose, and she did not regret it, and she did not open them.

But it was the anniversary. And the field was full of names. And the light was doing that thing it does at the end of hard years, when it makes even the ordinary ground look like it's holding its breath.

So she knelt, and she pressed her palm flat against the grass, against the earth, against whatever was underneath — the god, the dead, the country that no longer existed, the whole layered quiet of it — and she whispered, not a request, not a bargain, nothing with a price or a door in it. Just a name. Just the name the whole year had been about, the name they had carried out of a country that no longer exists, the name that was hers and the guild's and the city's and the child's, all at once:

"Hollow."

Not a summons. Not a spell. Just — a greeting. *We're still here. We kept it. We carried it out.*

The wind moved through the grass, the way wind does.

And then it moved *against* the way wind does — a soft, slow pass over the field, from the far corner to the gate, bending every ribbon in the rows in sequence, a long ripple like a hand smoothing a bedsheet, like a reader running a finger down a page of names. It touched the back of her neck, warm, brief, unmistakable.

Not power. She had none, and nothing came to her, and no voice spoke in a dead man's cadence, and no door opened anywhere. It was smaller than all that. It was the smallest possible answer to the smallest possible prayer: *heard*. *Known*. *Kept*.

Once. Gently. The way you answer someone you love who has finally, after a long hard year, asked for nothing at all.

Lior knelt in the grass and laughed and cried in the same breath, and the field held both without comment, and the sun went down over Vess like it had somewhere better to be and would come back anyway.

She got up. She brushed off her knees. She walked down the hill toward the lamplighting, toward the market noise, toward Wren's letters from university and Ivet's impossible maps and Sarel's eleven volumes and the ordinary, unmagical, hard-won morning waiting on the other side of the night.

At the gate she stopped, once, and looked back at the ribbons stirring in the dark.

"See you next year," she said.

And from somewhere — the grass, the wind, the warm hand between her shoulder blades, the country that no longer existed and had never stopped listening — came the feeling, faint and certain and enough, of a name being carried home.
