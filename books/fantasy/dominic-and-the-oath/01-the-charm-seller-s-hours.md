## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

The bell above the door of Galloway's Curiosities rang at half past eleven, and Wren knew before he looked up that it was trouble. Not the Wardens—Wardens didn't ring, they entered like weather—but the particular hesitant chime of a man who had stood outside for a long time working up his courage.

The fisherman who came in was still dripping, though it hadn't rained in Corvane for nine days. Sea water, then. He left dark crescents on the floorboards and stood wringing his cap as if the cap were responsible for everything.

"Closed in an hour," Wren said pleasantly. "But I've time for one more."

The man's eyes went to the shelves: bundles of dried lavender tied with red thread, copper bells polished to a shine they'd never earned, knots of braided cord hanging from hooks like little hanged men. Harmless things. Market-fair things. The kind of stock any shopkeeper on Copper Lane might sell to gullible sailors and nervous brides.

"I'm Aldous Fen," the man said, as if that should mean something.

"It's good to meet you, Master Fen." Wren set down the ledger he'd been pretending to study. His handwriting in it was deliberately dull—prices, quantities, a note about a delivery of candle wax. If the shop were ever raided, the ledger would read like the account book of the dullest man in Corvane, which was exactly what he wanted it to say about him.

"They say—" Fen stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "They say you sell luck."

Wren smiled the smile he'd practiced for twelve years, warm and faintly apologetic, the smile of a man who dealt in superstition and nothing stronger. "I sell knots that hold, bells that ring, and lavender that keeps moths from your winter coat. As for luck, I find it mostly comes down to keeping your knife sharp and your promises few."

The fisherman's face did something complicated. Hope curdling into embarrassment, embarrassment hardening into desperation. "My boy's out past the Teeth," he said. "The *Sable Jenny*. Storm came up out of nothing two nights back, black as pitch, and no star to steer by. No star. You understand? There hasn't been a star over the water since—" He stopped himself, because everyone in Corvane had stopped themselves at that point for three days now, and finished instead: "The harbor master says wait. The other wives say pray. I don't know how to do either anymore, so I come here."

*No star to steer by.* Wren's hands went very still on the counter. He had felt it too, of course—the wrongness pressing down on the city like a lid, the sky gone blank as unwritten paper. Three nights now. He had told himself it was weather. He had told himself a great many things in twelve years; lying to himself had become a kind of craft, practiced until it nearly passed for skill.

"Sit down, Master Fen," Wren said. "Let me make you some tea while you tell me about your boy."

He moved through the back curtain into the workroom, where Tam sat cross-legged on the floor surrounded by a drift of cord-ends, her fingers flying through a pattern she was supposed to be practicing and clearly wasn't. She was seventeen, all elbows and sharp opinions, with ink stains up both forearms and her hair hacked short in the style she insisted was practical and he suspected was rebellion wearing a practical costume.

"We have a customer," Wren said quietly.

"At this hour?" Tam was already on her feet, brushing cord-ends off her trousers. "What flavor?"

"The frightened kind. Boy lost at sea. He wants luck."

Tam's face fell into something older than seventeen. "And you'll sell him a knot."

"And I'll sell him a knot."

"Which does nothing."

"Which holds when everything else lets go," Wren said. "That's not nothing, Tam. Sometimes it's most of what there is."

She muttered something under her breath—he caught the shape of *coward's charity* and let it go, because she wasn't wrong, and because being angry at her for seeing him clearly was a luxury he couldn't afford. He filled the kettle, and while it heated, he took down the good cord. Not the market hemp from the front shelf but the waxed flax he kept hidden behind the tin of tea leaves, dyed the deep sea-green that fishermen swore by and would never explain to a landman.

His hands knew the work without him. That was the trap of it, the quiet horror he'd made his peace with or told himself he had: even stripped down to trinkets, even buried under twelve years of deliberate smallness, the gift lived in his fingers. Every knot he tied took hold of something. Most days he tied them shallow—a grip on the wearer's attention, a nudge of fortune so slight it could pass for coincidence. Enough to keep the shop's reputation alive among people who needed hope more than they needed honesty. Never enough to light a Warden's lantern.

But for Aldous Fen, kneeling alone in a shop at midnight with a son beyond the Teeth, Wren found his fingers wanting to go deeper. Wanting to reach out along the green thread and *find*—the way he once could, the way he'd sworn at a gate in the mountains never to do again.

He tied the knot shallow. It cost him something, the way it always cost, and he paid it without letting his face show the price.

* * *

Fen drank his tea in three swallows and held the luck-knot like it might bite or bless him. "How much?"

"For a father waiting on a son?" Wren named a number so small it was barely honest commerce. "Come back and pay me the rest when the *Jenny* makes harbor."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then you'll have enough troubles without owing me coin."

The fisherman stared at him. Something in his wet, wrecked face cracked open. "You're a kind man," he said, wondering, as if kindness were the strangest thing he'd encountered all night—and maybe it was, in Corvane, in this season, with the sky gone dark. Then, lower, leaning across the counter: "There's talk. Down at the docks. That the stars didn't go out on their own. That someone *took* them." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "That the mountain court is collecting."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere down the lane a dog started barking and thought better of it.

"That's dock-talk," Wren said lightly. "Sailors will blame the Undercourt for a bad catch and a bad wife alike. The stars are weather, Master Fen. Weather turns."

But his chest had gone tight around the place where the oath lived—that old scar-tissue knot beneath his breastbone that had been silent for twelve years and had begun, these past three nights, to *ache*, low and rhythmic, like a rope straining against its mooring. He pressed his palm flat against his sternum and kept smiling.

Fen left with his knot and his almost-hope. Through the window Wren watched him go, shoulders hunched against a sky with nothing in it, and thought: *if the mountain is collecting, then someone has already paid.*

* * *

"You felt it too," Tam said, when the door had shut. She'd followed him into the front room and now stood with her arms crossed, watching him bank the fire. "Don't lie to me, Wren. You've been touching your chest for three days like something in there is trying to get out."

He kept his eyes on the coals. "Old wound. It aches when the weather changes."

"There *is* no weather. That's the whole problem." She came closer, dropped her voice to the fierce whisper she saved for arguments she intended to win. "Twelve years I've lived above this shop and you've never once told me what you were before you were a charm-seller. Fine. Everyone's entitled to their locked rooms. But I know what I see. When the stars went out, you went white as milk, and you haven't slept since, and just now, tying that poor man's knot, your hands were shaking—not from fear of the Wardens. From *wanting*."

"Tam—"

"You could help them. All of them. The fishermen, the families on Ash Street whose kids are sick, the whole starving gray city—you could *fix* things, and you sit here selling lavender."

"And then what?" He turned and met her eyes, and let her see, just for a moment, the thing underneath the shopkeeper. "Then the Wardens burn us down, the way they burned Ossa's dye-house last spring, the way they burned the midwife's cottage on Tanner's Row with the midwife inside it. Magic is outlawed, girl. Not taxed. Not licensed. Outlawed. The punishment isn't a fine." He tapped the wall, and beneath the plaster, where he'd scratched it years ago in a moment of weakness, the old warning mark flared briefly visible: *small lives long, large ones short.* "I built this life small on purpose. Small enough to hide. It's not cowardice. It's arithmetic."

"It's arithmetic that leaves everyone else to drown," Tam said, and the words landed harder than she probably meant them to, or maybe exactly as hard as she meant them to.

Silence stretched between them, the kind of silence that had been accumulating interest for months. Wren looked at his apprentice—at the sharp jaw, the ink-stained hands, the stubbornness he'd helped raise and could no longer tell whether he admired or feared—and felt the old ache of a life built on smallness: that it fit him like a coat cut for a thinner man, and that he had taught her to live in its shadow, and that someday she would resent him for it with her whole heart.

"I'm going to bed," he said, which was a lie, and they both knew it.

* * *

He didn't go to bed. He went up the narrow stair to the loft above the shop and opened the shutter, and stood looking out at the starless sky over Corvane's rooftops.

It wasn't clouds. Clouds had edges, movement, the smell of coming rain. This was absence laid over the world like a sheet over furniture in a house whose owner never meant to return. Three nights without a single star, and the city below him had responded the way cities do: with rumors, prayers, extra patrols. The Warden lanterns moved along Copper Lane in their paired rhythm—two lights, pause, two lights—and every time they passed, Wren counted his heartbeat until they were gone.

Twelve years ago he had stood at the border gate in the high passes and sworn away everything: the wide country beyond, the name he'd been born to, the full reach of his gift. *Keeper,* the oath had called him. *Keeper of the seal, warden of the border between what is and what must not return.* He had spoken the words with blood in his mouth and grief in his throat, and the oath had sunk into him like a hook into fish-flesh, and ever since, his magic had been a river run through a straw: enough to tie knots, not enough to matter.

Except the oath had never ached before. Twelve years of silence, and now, with the stars gone, it throbbed like a rotten tooth.

*Tam's right,* he admitted, to the dark, to no one. *Something's happening. Something big enough to shake the seal itself.*

Below, the paired lanterns paused outside his shop. Held. Moved on.

He closed the shutter and began, methodically, to pack.

Not to flee—never quite that. But a charm-seller who survived twelve years in Corvane survived by keeping a bag ready, coin sewn into the lining, a second name folded in oilcloth, routes memorized. He packed the way he did everything: carefully, invisibly, small.

He was cinching the strap when he heard the curtain move downstairs, and Tam's voice floated up, soft and strange:

"Wren? There's someone at the door. They're wearing hoods, and they're not knocking—they're just *standing* there. And I think—" a pause, and when she spoke again her voice had gone thin as wire, "—I think they're waiting for the Wardens to arrive first."
