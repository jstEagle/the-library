## 2. Twelve Years of Shut Doors

The night Wren Galloway swore his oath, it was raining in a way that felt deliberate—like the sky had been asked to wash something away and had agreed without enthusiasm.

He remembered that detail later, when so much else had gone soft at the edges. The rain came down in long gray ropes off the mountainside, drumming on the flat stones of the border gate, and the forty wardens who had marched him there stood in two silent ranks with their hoods up, water streaming from their shoulders as if they were carved from the cliff itself.

He had not been called Wren then.

That was the first thing the oath took, before anything else. Before the gate, before the seal, before the twelve years of small careful life—he had to hand over his name, and the woman who received it held it in her cupped hands like a coal she meant to drown.

*Say it once,* she told him. *Only once. After tonight, no one living may speak it, including you.*

So he said it. He stood under the rain on the last stone before the gate and spoke his own name aloud into the dark, and the sound of it went out of him like breath leaving a body, and he understood—in that final unguarded instant before the words left—that he would never hear it again from any mouth, ever, for as long as he lived.

Then the warden closed her hands, and the name was hers, and he was no one.

* * *

Twelve years earlier than that, though—no. He corrected himself even now, in memory, because the habit of precision had been beaten into him by the oath's architecture. Not twelve years before the oath. On the same night. The same hour. Because the story everyone in Corvane believed, the story the Wardens told in their barracks and the magistrates wrote into their ledgers, was that the border beyond Corvane had always been sealed, that magic had always been outlawed, that the world past the gate was a dead country full of nothing worth mourning.

Wren knew better. Wren had walked out of it.

The country beyond the gate had a name too, and like his own, it had been drowned. But for one night—one long, terrible, luminous night—it still existed, and he carried its shape inside him the way a man carries the shape of a room he grew up in, even after the house has burned.

In that country, his mother had kept bees.

Not ordinary bees. He smiled now, remembering, a private crooked smile that no customer on Copper Lane had ever seen, because charm-sellers did not smile about bees. His mother's bees carried light instead of pollen. They moved through her orchard at dusk in slow golden rivers, and where they settled, things healed: blighted branches greened, sour wells sweetened, the fevered slept and woke hungry. She called them her little lanterns, and she talked to them the way other people talked to neighbors, complaining amiably about their moods, their politics, their tendency to swarm over the chapel roof during services.

His father had worked the gate road—a courier, riding letters and small mercies between villages that trusted each other because the roads were watched and watched well. Everyone in those hills had some small craft: a blessing for childbirth, a word that made bread rise twice, a knot tied three ways to hold a roof through storm. Magic wasn't power there. It was manners. It was how neighbors took care of neighbors, stitched invisibly into every kindness.

And the king—or whatever they had called him; Wren could no longer recall the title, only the weight of the crown in the room—had wanted more than manners.

That was how it began. That was how everything ended. A man on a throne, looking at a country full of small generous wonders, and asking the question that ruins worlds: *why should this be scattered among the poor, when it could be gathered up and owned?*

They called it the Gathering. It sounded gentle. It was not.

* * *

Wren had been nineteen the year the Gatherers came to his valley, and he had spent that year doing what young men do when they sense a flood coming: he built sandcastles. He argued. He organized. He rode to neighboring holdings with petitions, and his father rode beside him, and his mother stayed home with her bees and said, in her mild terrible way, that crowns were just hats, and hats could be taken.

She was right. That was the horror of it. She was entirely, reasonably, provably right—and being right saved nothing.

The Gatherers did not come with armies at first. They came with contracts, and clerks, and scales. They offered fair prices—genuinely fair, at the start—for charms and crafts and blessings. And people sold, because people are people, and silver is silver, and what harm in selling a luck-knot when you can tie another?

But a blessing is not a knot. A blessing is given, and giving is a door, and the court's clerks knew how to walk through open doors. Every charm purchased gave the buyers a thread, and every thread let them pull, and within two seasons the valley's small magics were arriving at the mountain court in lacquered chests—harvested, catalogued, *owned*—and the fields they'd come from were going quiet. Wells soured. Bread refused to rise. The fevered tossed all night, and no one could remember anymore how to sing them down.

Because the craft had never lived in the words or the knots or the songs. It had lived in the giving. And the court, buying it, had killed it the way you kill anything: by making it property.

By the third season, the Gatherers stopped offering prices and started offering choices. Give freely or be searched. Be searched or be bound. And those who were bound—the ones who fought, who hid their craft, whose gifts ran too deep to be scooped up in lacquered chests—were collected whole.

Soul and all.

Wren learned the term *soul-ledger* from a Gatherer clerk who had drunk too much and wept too honestly, a round soft man who kept saying *I only write the entries, I don't make them.* Souls, entered into the ledger, became currency. Fuel. The court had discovered that a soul, properly processed, could power workings no living craft could match—wards that never needed renewal, lights that never guttered, a dominion so complete it required no soldiers, only bookkeeping.

His mother's bees were in one of those chests. He had watched the lid close.

His parents' names were written in the ledger by midwinter. He knew this because the weeping clerk had shown him, in a moment of drunken mercy, thinking to warn him. *Run, boy. Your line runs deep. You're worth more than the whole valley, and they'll come for depth.*

So Wren ran. Nineteen years old, carrying nothing but his mother's oldest bee-smoker and a hatred so clean and total it frightened him, and he ran toward the one place in all the world the court's writ did not yet run: the free city of Corvane, on the far side of the mountains, where magic had been outlawed for a generation—not gathered, not owned, simply *forbidden*, banned outright, the whole practice starved of practitioners until it died of neglect.

Corvane's law was cruel. Corvane's law was blind. But Corvane's law, Wren understood with the cold clarity of the hunted, was also a wall. Behind an absolute prohibition, there were no ledgers. No entries. Nothing to buy, because everything was already buried.

It was the only shelter left. And it cost exactly everything.

* * *

The keeper's oath was Corvane's oldest law and its least known. Most citizens never heard of it; the Wardens who administered it numbered, that night, barely forty, standing in the rain at a gate most of them had never seen, because the gate sat high on the mountain pass behind triple checkpoints and a road that officially did not exist.

Here was the truth Wren learned that night, kneeling on wet stone while the First Warden read the terms aloud: the border wards of Corvane were not maintained by the state. They were maintained by a person. One person, at a time, generation after generation. A keeper, sworn at the gate, whose own soul became the anchor-stone of the seal. The wards did not run on machinery or ritual alone—they ran on a life. On consent, freely given, renewed nightly in sleep, unbreakable except by death or desertion.

And the price of the office was total. Not just the name. Everything that could be traced back across the border: memories of the sealed country, softened to fog; the ability to work magic in the old generous way, cauterized; the very language of the hills, scoured from the tongue until it sounded like wind. A keeper was permitted one craft, one narrow licensed sliver of working, enough to maintain the seal and nothing more. A keeper was permitted a life, but a small one, a quiet one, a life designed to attract no attention whatsoever.

*Why me?* Wren had asked, kneeling there, rain running off his chin. *Why does it have to be someone from beyond the wall?*

*Because,* the First Warden said, and her voice was not unkind, which somehow made it worse, *the seal must be anchored by a soul that knows what it is sealing. A Corvanite would be sealing an abstraction. You are sealing your own home. The lock holds best when the key remembers the door.*

He had laughed then. He couldn't help it—a short, cracked sound that startled the ranks of hooded wardens into stillness. *You want my grief,* he said. *You're building your wall out of my grief.*

*Yes,* she said. And then, the only honest sentence spoken that entire night: *We are not good people. We are desperate people, and this is the least monstrous thing we know how to do. I will not pretend otherwise to you.*

Later—much later, in the small hours of many small years—he would try to remember if he had hesitated. Whether some version of himself had stood up from the wet stone and walked away, back down the pass, back into the gathering dark, to fight or die or simply refuse. He liked to think there had been such a moment. He needed there to have been a moment.

There had not. There had been a valley full of the harvested, a chest full of his mother's bees, a ledger with his parents' names in it, and a wall being offered, and he had knelt on the stone and said *I swear it* before the First Warden finished describing the terms, because grief makes a very fast decision and calls it duty afterward.

* * *

The oath, when it came, did not feel like chains. That was the cruelest part, the part he never told anyone, because no one would believe it.

It felt like warmth.

The First Warden set her hand on the crown of his head, and the seal rose up around him out of the mountain itself—a pressure, a hum, a vast slow turning like a millstone the size of the sky—and it poured into him along every nerve he had. It didn't hurt. It *filled*. For one blazing moment he was the wall: he felt the length of the border, every league of it, the deep roots of the wards running down through rock into darkness, and beyond the wall he felt the sealed country pressing against the inside of the barrier like a face against frosted glass, and he felt—

He felt his own name go.

Not violently. Gently. The way snow leaves a branch. One moment the name was his, warm in his mouth where he'd spoken it into the rain; the next it was a smooth blank place in him, a polished absence, and he reached for it and found nothing to reach with, because the reaching-part had gone too. Along with it went the smell of beeswax and smoke, the sound of his mother calling the hives home at dusk, the particular creak of his father's saddle, the word for *home* in the old tongue—all of it sliding away down some interior slope, settling somewhere below memory, not destroyed but *deposited*, filed, kept.

Kept. He clung to that. In the years after, he clung to it like a man clinging to a raft: the oath did not erase. It *held*. Somewhere in the architecture of the seal, in whatever vault the wardens' order maintained beneath the gate, everything he surrendered was stored—his name, his tongue, his mother's face. Held as surety. Held as anchor. The wall was built out of him, and therefore he was not gone; he was load-bearing.

A man can live on that kind of arithmetic for years. Wren lived on it for twelve.

The gate closed behind him with a sound he had no word for—not a boom, not a clang, but a kind of *settling*, the sound of a great weight finding its rest, and the First Warden helped him to his feet and said, *Welcome to the rest of your life, Keeper. It will be small. Guard it well.*

* * *

He chose the name Wren on the walk down the mountain, in the gray hour before dawn, because wrens were small and brown and everywhere and nobody looked at them. He chose Galloway off a signpost at the foot of the pass—an inn, shuttered, long abandoned—and he wore both the way he would learn to wear everything: loosely, quietly, like clothes chosen not to be noticed in.

Corvane taught him the rest. Corvane taught him that fear has a daily rhythm, that the city's terror of magic was a tide with predictable hours, and that a man with one licensed sliver of craft—who was legally forbidden even that sliver outside the gate—could survive indefinitely if he stayed shallow. So he went shallow. Charm-seller. Luck-knots, traveler's tokens, birth-blessings stripped of anything the Wardens' instruments could taste, each one a masterpiece of harmlessness, each one a small betrayal of everything he actually was.

He got good at it. That was the shame he carried closest: not the fear, but the *competence*. Twelve years of shut doors, and he had become excellent at keeping them shut. He could walk past a Warden patrol with his heart slow as a sleeper's. He could sell a frightened fisherman a knot of braided cord and mean, underneath it, the true blessing he could not give, and sleep soundly after.

Smallness, he discovered, is a skill. And like all skills, it grows teeth. There came a day—he could no longer say which—when he caught himself genuinely preferring the small life. When the blank place where his name had been stopped aching and started feeling like peace. When he looked at the sealed border in his dreams and felt, beneath the grief, a terrible comfortable *rightness*, the seal humming in his chest like a second heartbeat, and understood that the wall was no longer just built out of him.

He was becoming it.

That was the night he went to the foundling step of the Lantern Row temple and, after six years of solitude, broke his own careful pattern, because a child was sitting there in the rain with her knees drawn up and her chin up and her eyes like struck flint, and something in the sealed-off country of Wren Galloway's chest knocked once, hard, against the inside of the wall.

Her name was Tam. She told him so herself, defiantly, as if daring him to write it in a ledger.

He took her home. He told himself it was practical—a shop needed hands, an old man needed an heir to the harmless trade of harmlessness. He told himself a great many practical things in the years that followed, while she learned knots and prices and the choreography of invisibility, while she grew from flint into fire, while the shop on Copper Lane filled, inch by inch, with a warmth he had sworn off and never stopped needing.

He almost believed the doors would stay shut forever. He had gotten so good at shutting them.

And then, on an ordinary evening thick with the smell of solder and dried lavender, his apprentice came in late with rain in her hair and a broker's card hidden badly in her sleeve, and said, too casually, the words that would crack every door he had ever closed:

*Wren. Can I ask you something about bargains?*
