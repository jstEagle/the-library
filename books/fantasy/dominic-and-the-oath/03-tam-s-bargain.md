## 3. Tam's Bargain

The luck-knots sold best in spring, when the fishing boats went out fat with hope and thin with sense. Wren tied another one—hemp twine, three loops, a river-stone bead he'd bleached to look like sea-glass—and hung it on the rack between the dream-salves and the honest, useless love charms. Through the shop's warped window, Copper Lane ran brown and busy with morning, and the smell of tar and frying onions drifted in like it owned the place.

Twelve years of mornings. Twelve years of hanging trinkets that did nothing while the thing that *did* something sat folded up inside his chest, wrapped in oath and silence.

He heard Tam before he saw her, because he always did—the particular rhythm of her boots on the cobbles, quick this morning, quicker than usual. The door banged open hard enough to swing the charm-rack.

"You're out of fever-tea," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"I need it. For a friend." She said it too fast, and she wasn't looking at the tea shelf, she was looking at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen since the night she'd turned up on his doorstep at fourteen, soaked through, furious, carrying nothing but a name she refused to say out loud.

Wren set down the twine. "What friend?"

"A girl from the harbor quarter. Her brothers have the sweating sickness." Tam moved past him into the back room, all elbows and avoidance, pulling jars from the shelf as if inventory were the point. "Doesn't matter. I'll make it myself if you won't sell it."

"The tea's yours, Tam. It's always been yours." He followed her, leaning in the doorway. "That's not what this is about."

She stopped, jar in hand. For a moment—a long one, the length of three heartbeats—he thought she'd tell him. Her mouth opened. The lamplight caught the fine bones of her face, the stubborn jaw, the scar on her brow from the year she'd fallen off the roof learning to bind wards, laughing the whole way down.

Then the door curtain dropped over her expression like a shutter.

"Nothing's about anything," she said, and paid for the tea with coins she didn't count, and left, and the bell above the door rang wrong behind her, flat and hurried, like a warning rung out of time.

* * *

Tam drank none of the fever-tea. She poured it down the privy two streets away, where no one who knew her could see, and then she walked, fast, toward the Salt Quarter, checking behind her every thirty steps the way you learn to when the thing you're carrying is worth more than your life.

The broker kept rooms above a chandler's shop, up a stair that smelled of tallow and old rope. He was already waiting. He was always already waiting, which should have frightened her more than it did.

"You've been crying," Master Corvess said. Not unkindly. That was the worst of him—that he could sound kind.

"I haven't."

"Your eyes say otherwise." He gestured to the chair across the desk. On it lay a document, heavy paper, wax seals the color of dried blood. "The Galloway claim. Three hundred acres in the Veldt valleys, orchards, a mill, water rights. Stolen under the Reclamation Edicts, twelve years ago. Restored to the rightful heir." He slid it toward her with one finger. "Signed by a magistrate. Unimpeachable."

Tam looked at the name on the deed—*Tamarind Vale*—a name she hadn't used since she was fourteen, since soldiers burned the farmstead and her father went into a Warden wagon and never came out, and she'd buried that name so deep even Wren only knew its shadow. Seeing it in ink made her dizzy.

"It's real?" Her voice came out small.

"It's real. It will hold in any court in the province." Corvess folded his hands. His rings caught the light. "Payment is due on delivery, as agreed."

"I know what we agreed."

"Do you?" He tilted his head. "You agreed to a price. You never asked what the price *was*. Most clients don't. Most clients are happier not knowing. You, Tamarind—" he said her stolen name gently, like setting down something fragile— "you keep asking. It's why I like you. And why I worry for you."

"What is it?" She had asked eleven times before. She asked anyway. Twelfth time. "Tell me what it costs."

Corvess smiled, and behind the smile, for half a heartbeat, she saw something tired. Ancient. Sad, almost.

"It costs," he said, "exactly what it looks like. Nothing now. Everything later. The Undercourt's arithmetic is not ours to question—but I'll tell you this much for free: they only ever take what you'd least expect them to reach for. Guard your surprises."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer the contract permits." He pushed the deed fully across. "Take it to the harbor magistrate by week's end and the land is yours. Your family's wrongs, righted. Everything they took from you, given back."

Everything they took. Tam picked up the paper and her hands were shaking. Orchards. A mill. Her mother's pear trees that she could still taste, still smell, twelve years gone. Justice—actual justice, in ink, in seals—in a city where justice cost more than anyone from the harbor quarter would earn in ten lifetimes.

There had to be a hook in it. Of course there was a hook. But her family had been hooked once already, gutted once already, and what remained of them was a girl with a false name selling luck-knots in a hiding-man's shop.

"Thank you," she said, and hated how grateful she sounded.

"One more thing." Corvess's eyes flicked to her, sharp as a fish hook itself. "Your employer. Your—what do you call him—your Wren. He doesn't approve of brokers."

Tam went cold. "You've been watching me."

"I watch everyone whose paperwork crosses my desk." The smile again, thinner. "Keepers especially. They have such interesting ledgers. Do be careful with him, Tamarind. Men who seal doors hate to see windows open."

She left without answering, the deed pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and told herself the cold feeling in her stomach was just the wind off the harbor.

* * *

Wren knew something was wrong by suppertime, because Tam set the table for three.

They'd been two for twelve years. Two bowls, two cups, the small economy of people who had learned that attachments were dangerous and had decided, carefully, secretly, to be dangerous together. Now there was a third place laid at the table, cloth napkin, the good cup, and Tam stood beside it with her chin up like a duelist.

"We have a guest coming," she said.

"No," Wren said quietly. "We don't. Sit down, Tam."

"He's coming at moonrise."

"*He?*" The word came out harder than he meant. He made himself unclench his hands. "Sit. Please. Tell me what you've done."

So she told him. All of it—the broker, the rooms above the chandler's, the deed with her true name on it, the land restored, the price unnamed. She told it standing up, arms crossed, watching his face the way she watched Wardens: for the flinch.

And Wren felt the flinch come and could not stop it. Because he knew that kind of broker. Everyone in Corvane's shadows knew the type—smooth-handed men who dealt in restoration, restitution, reversal, who could get you anything back that the world had taken, for a fee that was never money. Money was the cheap currency. He had spent twelve years teaching Tam that smallness was safety, that wanting big things got people collected, and she had gone out and wanted the biggest thing he could imagine: *everything back.*

"Break it off," he said.

"You don't get to—" She laughed, a short ugly sound. "You don't even get to *react* yet. I haven't finished."

"I've heard enough. Brokers of the Undercourt don't restore land, Tam. They buy claims on people. The deed is real, yes. The price is realer. Whatever he's taking as payment, it isn't coin—it's *you.* Some part of you. Maybe the best part."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way you know weather before a storm." He came around the table, slowly, palms open. "Twelve years I've kept us small. Kept us *boring.* Not because I'm cruel—because it works. Nobody collects what nobody notices. And you've walked into the one shop in Corvane where they *always* notice, and you've signed something, haven't you? There's a mark on you already."

Tam's hand went, involuntarily, to her wrist. That was answer enough.

"There's nothing wrong with wanting our land back!" Her voice cracked and she let it, let him hear all fourteen-to-twenty-six years of grief in it. "You talk about smallness like it's a virtue. It's a *tomb*, Wren. You buried us both in it and called it keeping. Well, I grew up in a grave of someone else's making and I am done being careful. I am done being *invisible.*"

"The land won't matter if there's no you to walk on it."

"And whose fault is it that the land needs buying back at all?" she shouted. "Where were the keepers? Where were you, with your grand sealed border and your sacred oath, when they took everything from everyone? Keeping the door shut on *your side*, that's where!"

The words landed exactly where she aimed them, in the oldest wound he had, and he felt his own temper rise to meet hers, rare and hot. "Don't speak of the oath. You don't know what it cost."

"Then tell me! For once, tell me anything!" She was crying now, furiously, wiping her face with the heel of her hand. "Twelve years and I don't know your true name or your history or why a man who clearly has power in both hands chooses to sell *luck-knots.* You ask me to trust you about the broker. Trust goes both ways, Wren. It always has."

He opened his mouth. And found—horribly—that he couldn't. The old reflex clamped down like a ward snapping shut: *say nothing, reveal nothing, stay small.* Even now. Even with her.

That pause was all the answer she needed.

"Right," Tam whispered. "Right. Fine." She pulled the deed from inside her coat and held it against her chest. "The meeting's at moonrise. I'm going. And when the orchards are ours—when I can stand on my mother's land and say my own name out loud—I'll decide whether there's anything here to come back to."

"Don't do this." His voice was low, urgent. "Tam. *Please.* If you go to him tonight, I can't protect you from what comes next."

"Maybe," she said, reaching for the door, "I'm done needing protection."

The bell rang behind her—flat, hurried, out of time—and this time Wren understood it perfectly.

It was the sound of a door closing on twelve years.

He stood alone in the shop among the harmless charms, in the small safe life he'd built like a wall around them both, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that walls work until they don't—and that somewhere out in the dark, his girl was walking toward the mountain's long shadow, wearing a mark on her wrist and a price on her soul, and he had no idea yet how high that price would climb.

Above Corvane, the first stars were coming out.

One by one, he would remember later, though he couldn't have said how he knew—like lights going out in a house, room by room, before the whole dark.
