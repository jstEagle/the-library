## 4. The Night Without Stars

The first thing Wren noticed was the silence of the gulls.

Corvane's harbor birds never slept—not truly. They wheeled all night above the fish-market in ragged gray spirals, complaining to one another about scraps and moonlight. Their noise was so constant that Wren had long ago stopped hearing it, the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat. But he heard it stop.

He was at the workbench in the back room of the shop on Copper Lane, a tallow candle guttering beside him, tying off the last knot of a luck-charm for old Maren's grandson who was shipping out on the *Kestrel's Tooth* come morning. The knot was simple work—hemp cord, three river-stones ground to powder, a whisper of intent no stronger than a prayer. Harmless. Legal-adjacent, if anyone squinted. The kind of magic that could pass for superstition, which was the only kind of magic that survived in Corvane.

The gulls went quiet all at once, like a door closing on them.

Wren set down the cord. His hands had gone still on their own, the way they did when his gift stirred—the small, unquiet sense that lived behind his sternum and told him when a storm was coming, when a lie was being told two rooms away, when the world had shifted its weight onto a different foot.

"Tam?" he called toward the loft stairs. "You awake?"

No answer. She'd been sulking behind her closed door since their argument three days past, speaking to him only in monosyllables and the language of slammed cupboard doors. He couldn't blame her. He'd forbidden her the broker on Fiddler's Row, and Tam did not forgive being forbidden anything. She was nineteen years old and carried her anger the way other people carried knives: close, sharpened, always within reach.

He rose and went to the window that looked out over the rooftops toward the harbor, and that was when he saw it.

There were no stars.

Not clouds—he knew Corvane's fogs the way he knew his own hands, and this was not fog. Fog blurred. This erased. The sky above the city was a smooth, absolute black, black as the inside of a closed box, and where the stars should have been there was nothing at all. Not dimness. Nothing. As if someone had reached up with an enormous hand and plucked each light out like a splinter.

The moon was gone too.

Wren's mouth went dry. Twelve years in this city, twelve years of careful smallness, and he had never once felt the particular cold that slid down his spine now. This was not weather. This was not omen. This was *power*—old power, vast power, the kind that got bought and sold in places he'd sworn never to look.

Behind his sternum, the oath stirred.

He felt it then for what it truly was: not a memory but a living thing, a thread of iron spun through his chest twelve years ago at the border gate, humming its low constant note of duty. *Keep the seal. Keep the border shut. Keep what lies beyond from spilling into the world of men.* For twelve years it had hummed, steady as a hearthfire, and he had built his whole shrunken life inside its sound.

Now the note changed.

It went sharp. Then thin. Then—

*Snap.*

Wren cried out and caught the windowsill with both hands as something tore loose inside him. It wasn't pain, exactly. It was the absence of pain—a pulling-out, an extraction, the way a tooth leaves behind its own ghost. The iron thread that had run through him since the night he knelt at the gate and gave away his name went suddenly, utterly slack, and he felt every mile of the border wards—two hundred leagues of woven light and law from the Salt Cliffs to the Thornwood—shudder, strain, and fall.

Like cut ropes. That was the image that came to him, unbidden and terrible: ropes cut, not frayed, not worn through by time or neglect but *severed*, snipped clean by something with the patience to wait twelve years and the reach to end it in a single stroke.

"No," he whispered. "No, no—"

The lamp on his workbench flickered. Every flame in the room leaned east, toward the mountains, as though bowing.

Then the screaming started.

* * *

It began in the harbor district, a single voice rising out of the dark, and then another, and then dozens, the sound swelling and spreading through the sleeping streets like water finding cracks. Wren threw open the shop door and stood in the mouth of Copper Lane and watched Corvane wake into panic.

Doors banged open along the lane. People stumbled out in their nightclothes, faces tipped up to the starless sky, and the looks on those faces were the worst part—not confusion but recognition, the animal certainty that something had ended. A woman clutched her children against the wall of the cooper's shop and recited the Litany of Fixed Things under her breath, faster and faster, the words crumbling halfway through. Old Cobb the cooper stood in the middle of the street in his shift, bare feet on the cold cobbles, shouting at the sky: "Bring them back! Bring them back, you thieving bastards, bring them back!"

Far to the north, beyond the rooftops, the mountain loomed. Wren could just make out its silhouette against the lightless sky—or rather, he could make out where the stars *should* have been around it, a mountain-shaped hole in nothing. And beneath that hole, faint as heat-shimmer, he saw—or thought he saw—a pulse of pale gold light, deep underground, rhythmic as a heartbeat.

Something down there was drinking the sky.

And the payment had already been made. He didn't know how he knew it; he only knew it the way he knew the knot-work, the way his hands knew things before his head caught up. A bargain had been struck tonight, somewhere beneath the mountain, and the price had been paid in the only coin the Undercourt accepted.

His stomach turned over. *Tam.* Where was Tam? He turned back toward the shop, toward the loft stairs—

—and stopped, because the bells had begun.

First the harbor chapel, then the Wardens' tower on Barricade Hill, then every bell in the city, ringing not the measured alarm of fire but the wild, arrhythmic clangor of total emergency. And under the bells came another sound, one that froze Wren where he stood: boots. Hundreds of boots, in formation, moving through the streets.

Lantern-light bloomed at the head of the lane. A Warden patrol—no, not a patrol, a *column*, twenty strong, armored in black-lacquered scale, torches held high, marching with the grim purpose of men who had been given orders they'd been waiting years to receive.

"All citizens indoors!" the column's sergeant bellowed. "By decree of the High Warden: curfew is absolute! Indoors, or be taken for collaborators!"

Collaborators. With what? With whom? The word made no sense, and it made perfect sense, because Wren had lived in Corvane long enough to know what happened to a frightened city: it needed someone to burn. And Corvane had spent two hundred years keeping a list.

The column passed the mouth of Copper Lane without stopping—but one lantern lagged. One figure in black scale slowed, half-turned, and for a heartbeat the torchlight caught the face beneath the helm.

Serah Vail.

Her eyes found Wren across the dark street, and he saw something flicker there—not recognition exactly; she had never once acknowledged knowing him, not in six years of passing his shop, not in six years of pointedly not searching it during inspections. What he saw was calculation. She looked at him, looked at the starless sky, looked back at him, and her jaw tightened around some decision she hadn't finished making.

Then the column swallowed her and moved on, and Wren stood alone in the lane with his heart hammering and the ghost of a snapped oath aching in his chest.

* * *

He should have stayed inside. Every instinct honed over twelve years said stay inside, bar the door, be small, be nothing, survive. Smallness had kept him alive through purges and pogroms and the slow grinding suspicion of a city that hated magic the way a scarred dog hates raised hands.

But Tam's window was dark, and Tam's bed, when he climbed to the loft, was empty—blankets thrown back, her second-best cloak gone from its peg, her boots missing. Gone before the sky went black. Gone, maybe, to Fiddler's Row. To the broker.

"To sell something," Wren said aloud, and his voice cracked on it. "You stubborn, brilliant, stupid girl."

He was down the stairs and out the door before he'd decided to move.

Fiddler's Row was a twenty-minute walk in daylight; he made it in ten through streets gone mad. The panic had organized itself now, which was worse than chaos. Bands of men moved through the lanes with torches and hammers, and they weren't looting—they were *purifying*. Wren saw them smash the painted eye above the fortune-teller's door on Rope Walk. Saw them drag the old hedge-witch Marta's herb racks into the street and feed them to the flames while she wept on her doorstep. Saw, on the corner of Bellows Street, three Wardens standing watch over a mob as it broke the windows of the apothecary, and understood with sick clarity that the burning had been sanctioned—that the Wardens' tower had been waiting for a night like this the way a dry forest waits for lightning.

The charm-sellers would go first. They always went first.

He smelled the smoke before he saw the fire, and when he rounded onto Fiddler's Row his heart stopped.

The broker's establishment—a narrow, crooked townhouse wedged between a chandler and a shuttered playhouse—was ablaze. Not burning accidentally. Burning *deliberately*, oil poured and lit, flames roaring from every window, and in the doorway a figure slumped against the frame with an arrow buried in his throat.

Wren knew him. The broker. The man Tam had been sneaking off to see, the silver-tongued stranger who promised stolen land restored. He was dying, drowning in his own blood, and when Wren dropped to his knees beside him the man's eyes rolled over and fixed on his face with terrible urgency.

"You," the broker rasped. Blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth. "The keeper. I know what you are—I've always—" A wet cough racked him. "Too late. It's done. The court collected tonight. Payment rendered."

"What payment?" Wren gripped the man's shoulder. "What did you sell them? Who?"

"Not me." The broker's laugh was a horrible, bubbling thing. "I only brokered. Found what the court wanted. Something young. Something *bright*. A soul with no debts yet—clean stock, they call it." His fingers scrabbled weakly at Wren's wrist. "The girl. Your girl. She came to me for her family's land. I told her the price. I told her, and she—"

The world went very quiet and very far away.

"She wouldn't," Wren said. "She wouldn't pay that. She didn't know what—"

"She signed." The broker's eyes were glazing. "Three nights past. Thought it was a formality. Thought the price was coin, or years, or—" Another cough, longer, finaler. "They took her tonight. Before the sky went dark. Hooded men, up the pilgrim road, into the mountain. Her soul's in the vaults now. Fueling the dark." His hand tightened, sudden strength flooding into a dying body, and he pulled Wren close enough to smell the blood on his breath. "Hear me, keeper. You can't steal a soul back. The law won't allow it. No one has ever—only exchange. Only trade. Remember that, if you're fool enough to follow."

"Where," Wren said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Which road. How far."

But the broker's eyes had gone fixed on the burning sky, and whatever answer he might have given died with him, there on the threshold of his own ruined house, while the fire roared and the smoke rose straight up into a darkness with no stars left to mark it.

* * *

Wren walked back to Copper Lane because his feet knew no other direction, and because the shop was the last place Tam might have returned to, and because if he stopped moving he would have to feel what was tearing open in his chest, and he could not afford to feel it yet.

The lane was empty when he reached it. The mobs had moved on to richer hunting grounds. His own little shop stood untouched—for now—with its painted sign swinging gently in the windless dark: *GALLOWAY'S KNOTS & CHARMS. LUCK FOR SALE. NOTHING STRONGER.*

Twelve years of careful smallness, summed up in one honest lie.

He stepped inside and barred the door and stood in the dark workroom, surrounded by the trinkets of his hidden life—luck-knots and traveler's blessings, fever-easing charms disguised as folk medicine, love-tokens stitched with intent so faint it barely counted as magic at all. Twelve years of building a life so small the world would overlook it. Twelve years of the oath humming in his chest, telling him this was enough, this was duty, this was the shape of penance for whatever he'd sealed behind the gate.

And now the oath was gone. Snapped like a cut rope. He reached inward, the way he had every night for twelve years, and found only silence where the iron thread had been—an absence shaped exactly like himself.

He had expected grief. Instead, standing there in the wrecked dark of the night without stars, Wren felt something else unfurling slowly through him, terrifying and vast and unmistakable:

Freedom.

Every ward he'd ever woven was fallen. Every law he'd bound himself to had burned with the broker's house. There was nothing left to be small for. The sky itself had been stolen, and the girl he loved better than his own life had been sold to fuel the theft, and the careful, cramped, invisible existence he had built brick by patient brick meant *nothing*—had perhaps always meant nothing, had been a candle cupped in a fist while the whole house burned.

His hands were shaking. He looked down at them and saw, for the first time in twelve years, faint light gathering in his palms—his true gift, unbound, answering him without knots or powders or disguises. It hurt to hold. It felt like opening a door onto a room he'd bricked shut so long ago he'd forgotten the room existed.

*Terror,* he thought. *And underneath it, terrible freedom.*

He crossed to the hearth and pulled aside the loose stone at the back of it, and took out the bundle wrapped in oilcloth that he had sworn, twelve years ago, never to touch again: his old traveling cloak, his keeper's badge with its gate-and-key sigil, and a knife forged of star-metal that had once cut the threads of wards far stronger than any this city had ever known.

He laid them on the workbench. Outside, the bells were still ringing, and somewhere northward the mountain pulsed with its stolen light, slow and satisfied as a fed heart.

"They took her soul," Wren said to the empty room, to the dead sky, to the ghost of his own broken oath. "They think that's the end of it."

He picked up the knife, and the light in his palms flared brighter, and for the first time since the night at the border gate, Wren Galloway spoke his own name aloud—just the name, nothing more, a man testing whether he still existed.

"I'm coming for her," he said. "Whatever it costs. Whatever I have to trade."

He wrapped the bundle under his arm, blew out the candle, and opened the door onto the starless night—and found Serah Vail standing in the lane, helmet under her arm, torch abandoned in a puddle, looking at him with the expression of a woman who had finally finished making her decision.

"We need to talk, charm-seller," she said. "Before either of us does something we can't take back."
