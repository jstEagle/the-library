## 5. Ashes on Copper Lane

The stars were still gone in the morning, and Corvane had begun to pretend otherwise.

Wren walked through streets where shutters hung crooked and doors had been barred from the outside—people leaving their homes and locking them behind themselves as if the dark might follow them indoors. A milk cart stood abandoned at the corner of Bellows Street, its horse gone, its cans tipped and pooling white in the gutter. Somewhere a crier was shouting that the Wardens had everything under control, that the darkness was a temporary phenomenon, an atmospheric disturbance, a trick of the enemy across the border. His voice cracked on every third word. Nobody believed him. You could tell by the way no one stopped to listen.

Wren kept his head down and his hands in his pockets, and he told himself the same thing he'd been telling himself since he woke on the floor of his ransacked bedroom with a hole in his chest where his oath used to live: *Tam went to her aunt's in Lowmarsh. Tam is fine. Tam knew the patrols would come and she got out.*

He did not believe it. He had not believed it since dawn. But the lie was load-bearing, and if he set it down he was afraid he would not be able to stand up again.

Copper Lane began at the mouth of the underpass and climbed in a crooked spine toward the old customs house. From three streets away, Wren smelled it.

Not smoke. Smoke he knew—the sweet reek of pine kindling, tallow, the ordinary stink of a city warming itself without sunlight. This was different. Burnt sugar and scorched iron and something underneath like struck flint, like the moment before lightning. The smell of charms dying badly.

He ran.

His body made the decision before his mind caught up, and for half a block he was simply a man running uphill through a frightened crowd, elbowing past a woman carrying a birdcage with no bird in it, past two boys arguing over a sack of oranges scattered across the cobbles, past a Warden patrol that shouted after him and did not bother to chase because there was too much city and too few of them. Then the lane bent, and the shop came into view, and Wren stopped so abruptly that a man behind him walked straight into his back and swore.

*The Gilded Knot* had been many things in twelve years: a joke, a disguise, a coffin he built for himself one plank at a time. It had been a narrow shopfront with a hand-painted sign, a window display of luck-knots and river-glass pendants and love-charms that did nothing at all, exactly as advertised. It had been the place where Tam first knocked on his door at fourteen with hollow cheeks and a stolen apple and asked, in a voice that expected refusal, whether he needed an apprentice, because she was very good at sweeping and better at pretending not to be hungry.

Now it was a wound in the street.

The door hung off one hinge, splintered inward. The window was gone—not broken but *gone*, frame and all, as if someone had unscrewed the city's hold on it. Inside, every shelf had been pulled down and stamped flat. His workbench lay on its back with its legs in the air like a dead beetle. The little brass bell over the door, which Tam had polished every morning and named *Captain*, was missing.

And everything wooden was burning. Not blazing—burning the way things burn when they have been *touched* by something that hates them: slow, sullen, eating inward from the edges without heat enough to warm the air. The fire gave off light and no comfort. Wren had seen wardfire once, from a distance, on the night he swore his oath, and he had hoped never to see its twin again.

"Tam," he said, and his voice came out small, a boy's voice, and the shop swallowed it whole.

He crossed the threshold into ash. It lay everywhere, fine and grey and glittering faintly, and he understood with a lurch that it was not only ash—it was residue. Charms ground to powder. Twelve years of careful, harmless, beautiful nothing-work, every knot and charm and painted prayer-stone he had ever made or sold or hidden under the floorboards, all of it reduced to dust that shimmered with the last ghosts of their intent. Someone had known exactly where to look. The loose plank behind the counter was pried up and lying askew; the cache beneath it was empty.

They had found everything. Which meant they had known what to find. Which meant—

"They took her at moonset."

Wren spun. The voice came from behind the fallen counter, low and wet, the voice of a man talking around blood.

The broker lay crumpled against the wall with his back to the shelving, and Wren knew him even before he saw the face: the smooth-cheeked man from the night market with the rings and the easy smile, the one whose stall Tam had been seen leaving too many times, the one who promised impossible things to desperate people at reasonable rates. His smile was gone now. Most of his color was gone too. He was holding his own side together with both hands, and between his fingers Wren could see the wound was wrong—not cut but *branded*, a sigil seared into flesh, some legal instrument of the Undercourt written in a language of pain.

"You," Wren said. The word had edges.

"Wren Galloway." The broker laughed, which cost him; the laugh ended in a cough that flecked his lips red. "The famous invisible man. I wondered—" He winced, gathered himself. "I wondered when the mountain would remember your face."

"I don't have a face for the mountain. I gave it away." Wren knelt beside him despite himself, despite the rage climbing his throat like floodwater, because the man was dying and dying men deserve more than we give them. "Where is she? Where is Tam?"

"Gone." The broker's eyes were strange—not afraid, not anymore, but glassy with a terrible professional clarity, a man reading terms and conditions written on the inside of his own skull. "Collected at moonset. Four of them, hooded, no faces under the hoods—I do this work thirty years, boy, and I know collection when I see it. They didn't break your door. They *unmade* it. Paperwork." He coughed again, weaker. "Your apprentice signed. That's the thing nobody tells you about the Undercourt. Everything is signed."

"She didn't sign anything." Wren heard his own voice shake. "She's sixteen years old. She wanted her family's land back, that's all, that's *all*—"

"That's never all." The broker's hand found Wren's wrist with surprising strength, cold fingers closing like a trap. His eyes fixed on Wren's with the urgency of a man spending his last coin. "Listen. Listen to me, keeper, because I am owed nothing now and I want to pay a debt. Her soul was collected as payment—but payment runs *both ways* in that court. Nothing is taken that isn't owed, and nothing is owed that can't be discharged." Blood bubbled at the corner of his mouth. "Souls may be traded. Never stolen back. Only *exchanged*. Do you understand? Exchanged. Like for like. Value for value."

"Then I'll buy her back. I'll pay whatever—"

"You have nothing they want." The broker smiled then, a ruin of his market smile, and there was something almost tender in it. "Nothing you're willing to show them. But you'll think of it. On the road. Everyone thinks of it eventually." His grip tightened. "Mountain road. North postern. They left four hours ago, and collections travel slow because the cargo is heavy with grief. You could catch them. No one catches them. But you—you still have an oath-shaped hole in you, don't you? I can see it from here. Things fall *into* men like you."

His grip loosened. Slid away. His head tipped back against the shelf, and the strange clarity drained out of his eyes, and Wren sat in the ashes of his life holding the wrist of a dead man who had, in the end, told the truth.

For a long moment there was only the sound of the sullen fire eating the shelves, and the distant city, and his own heart.

Then Wren got up.

* * *

He worked quickly and without ceremony, because grief is a luxury and the road north does not wait.

From the wreckage behind the counter he dug the strongbox he'd buried under the hearthstone—the one place, he realized with a bitter twist, they hadn't looked, because it wasn't hidden by craft but by ordinariness, buried in the one corner of the shop that smelled only of old bread and burnt porridge, of breakfasts shared, of Tam complaining about the porridge every single morning. They had catalogued his magic and ignored his life. There was a lesson in that. He filed it away.

In the box: a pouch of silver and a few gold bits saved across twelve years of smallness. A knife. A map of the northern reaches, out of date by a decade. And wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, because he had never quite managed to destroy them—because a keeper who seals a gate should not keep keys, and he had kept them anyway—three charms of his true making. Not trinkets. Not luck-knots. Working magic, old and quiet and dangerous, each one a small confession of what he really was.

He weighed them in his palm and felt the freed oath churn in the space they'd left inside him. For twelve years those charms had been nearly inert, muffled by the seal, safe as sleeping snakes. Now they warmed at his touch like things waking. Like things *hungry*.

He took them anyway.

He stood once more in the middle of the ruined shop, and let himself look—really look—at what remained. Captain the bell was gone. Tam's sweeping brush lay split in two on the floorboards. On the back wall, where she had chalked the tally of days since her family lost their land (a number she updated every morning, whispering it like a grudge), someone had wiped the slate clean.

That, more than anything, more than the fire and the ash and the broker's cooling blood, broke something open in Wren Galloway.

Because he understood now, with the awful arithmetic of hindsight, how it had happened. Tam had gone to the broker to undo an injustice. The broker had gone to the mountain to sell what she carried. And the mountain—the court beneath it, the pale bench, the ledger-keepers of every sealed and starless bargain—had looked at a clever, angry, grieving girl and seen not a person but a *denomination*. They had bought her soul the way merchants buy grain: in bulk, at market rate, to fuel the dark that kept their power absolute. And Wren had spent twelve years teaching her to be small. Teaching her that hiding was safety. Teaching her, by example, that the right response to injustice was to knot your fingers around a luck-charm and look away.

She had learned one lesson too well and none of the important ones. She had stopped looking away—and she had done it alone, in the dark, at moonset, while the man who called himself her family slept ten feet above her head.

"No more," Wren said to the empty shop. His voice didn't shake this time. It was low and even and it sounded like someone he used to be. "You hear me? No more looking away."

He wrote no note—there was no one left in Corvane to receive one. He banked the wardfire as best he could with sand from the alley, though it fought him, sullen, and he suspected it would smolder for days out of spite. At the door he paused, reached up to the empty bracket where Captain used to hang, and touched the cold metal where the bell had been.

"When I come back," he said quietly, "we're going to need a louder bell."

* * *

The north postern gate was chaos, and chaos suited him.

Half of Corvane seemed to be trying to leave—carts stacked with children and chickens, a merchant bribing a gatewarden with a silver candlestick, a priest of the Sealed Order standing on a barrel preaching that the vanished stars were a test of faith and that faith, conveniently, could be expressed in coin. The other half of the city was trying to get *in*, refugees from the villages near the border, where the falling wards had reportedly let in things that walked out of the old dark on legs people refused to describe. The Warden at the postern was checking papers with the frantic diligence of a man who had decided that if he just checked enough papers the sky would fix itself.

Wren had no papers worth the name. What he had was a hood, a slouched shoulder, a pocket of silver, and twelve years of practice at being unremarkable. He joined the queue of laborers, passed two coppers to the right palm, and was through the gate before the priest finished his sermon.

Beyond the walls, the world had changed color.

The fields were grey under a starless noon—not night-dark but shadow-dark, as if the sun had been demoted to a rumor. The road north wound through orchards heavy with fruit nobody dared pick, past shrines where travelers had left offerings to gods who presumably also could not see them anymore. And far ahead, half a day's walk at least, the mountain stood up out of the plain like a raised fist.

It had no name on any honest map. Locals called it the Quiet One, or sometimes just *it*, with a gesture that apologized for pointing. It was too symmetrical to be natural and too worn to be new, and no snow rested on it no matter the season. From this distance it looked asleep. Wren knew better. Courts that buy souls do not sleep. They *wait*, patiently, behind paperwork, and they let the desperate come to them, because desperation is the only currency they respect.

Four hours' lead. Collections traveling slow, heavy with grief. If he pushed—if he walked through the grey noon and the darker dusk and did not stop—he might catch the hooded figures before the mountain's mouth swallowed them. And then what? He had a knife, some silver, three waking charms, and a hole in his chest where his identity used to be. Against four collectors of the Undercourt, it was not a plan. It was barely a gesture.

But the broker's words were still turning over in him, gathering weight like a stone rolling downhill. *Souls may be traded. Never stolen back. Only exchanged.* Exchanged, like for like. Value for value.

*You have nothing they want,* the broker had said. *Nothing you're willing to show them.*

Wren looked down at his own hands—charm-seller's hands, small, careful, hands that had built a life on being harmless—and for the first time in twelve years he wondered, honestly, what they might be worth to a court that priced everything.

He was still wondering when he saw the checkpoint.

It crouched where the road narrowed between hedgerows half a mile on: a Warden toll-post, lantern-lit despite the grey, two wardens turning back travelers, a third checking a ledger with the grim thoroughness of a man working from a list. And Wren might have slipped it—might have gone over the fields, through the ditches, the way he'd slipped everything for twelve years—except that he saw who stood beside the brazier feeding it documents to burn.

Tall. Upright. Grey cloak, grey eyes, a scar through one eyebrow that he'd have known anywhere, because he'd watched her earn it the same night he'd earned his oath, on the border, twelve years ago, from opposite sides of a gate.

Serah Vail. The Warden who had found his illegal charms a hundred times and looked away a hundred times, and who had never once said why.

She looked up from the fire. Across half a mile of starless road, her grey eyes found him in the crowd of stragglers as if she'd been waiting for exactly him—and Wren felt the quest settle onto his shoulders with its true weight, because he was going to need more than silver and sleeping snakes to get past her.

He was going to need blackmail. And he had, at long last, absolutely nothing left to lose.

He started walking toward the checkpoint.
