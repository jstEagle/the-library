## 6. The Warden Who Looked Away

Serah Vail had burned eleven charm-shops in her career as a Warden of Corvane, and she could name every one of them the way other soldiers named battles.

The first had been a candle-maker's on Tallow Row, an old man who wept while his stock went up and thanked her afterward for the mercy of a quick fire. The last had been three nights ago, a luck-seller's stall near the fish market, where a woman with salt-cracked hands had thrown herself over a crate of knotted cords until Serah's squad pried her away. The woman had screamed one word over and over—*my daughter's fever*—and Serah had stood there with her torch dripping flame onto wet cobbles and felt nothing at all, which was the worst part. Not guilt. Nothing. Twelve years in the gray cloak, and the order had finally sanded her down to something smooth and hollow, like sea glass.

She was thinking about that woman when the knock came at her door in the Warden barracks, two hours past midnight, soft as a moth against glass.

No one knocked softly at a Warden's door unless they wanted something or meant harm, and Serah had learned long ago to prefer harm. Harm you could answer with steel. Wanting was more dangerous.

She opened it with her hand already on her knife.

He was thinner than she remembered. That was the first thing. The charm-seller from Copper Lane with the careful eyes and the ink-stained fingers—the one whose shop she had walked past four years ago, whose table of harmless trinkets she had inspected with deliberate slowness while he stood very still, breathing like a man underwater, and then she had said *nothing here but knots and tin* and turned around and left him whole.

She had told herself it was oversight. She had told herself a lot of things.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said quietly. "Or gone. The list came down this morning. Every registered charm-seller in Corvane. Detain or burn."

"I know." His voice was rougher than she remembered too, scraped raw, like he'd been shouting into wind for days. "May I come in? Please. Before someone sees."

Every training she had ever taken said: call it in. Two words down the speaking-tube and six Wardens would be climbing these stairs with irons and fire. Every oath she'd sworn at nineteen with her hand on the gray banner said the same.

She stepped aside and let him in.

* * *

His name was Wren Galloway. She knew it from the ledgers, though he'd never given it to her—that day on Copper Lane he'd been only *the charm-seller*, a category, a line item. Now he sat across from her in the narrow room with its regulation cot and regulation desk, hands flat on his knees like a man trying not to fidget, and looked at her with those careful eyes and said:

"Four years ago you searched my shop. You found a luck-knot under the counter with real working in it. River-cord, triple-braided, keyed to a fisherman's true name. You held it in your hand for ten full seconds."

"I don't recall—"

"You do." No anger in it. Just tired certainty. "You put it down, you said the shop was clean, and you wrote in your report that I sold tin and thread. I've spent four years wondering why. I've decided I don't care why anymore. I only need you to know that I know."

Serah kept her face still. It was a skill the cloak taught you early—your face was the last thing they let you keep.

"So you've come to blackmail me," she said.

"Yes."

"At least you're honest about it."

"It's the only honest thing I have left." He leaned forward slightly. "Here is what I want. There are checkpoints on every road out of the city since the stars went dark. Wardens at all of them, and orders to hold anyone traveling toward the mountain. I need to get through the eastern cordon at Grayford Bridge by tomorrow night. You're stationed for rotation there in—" he glanced at the hour-candle on her desk— "eleven hours. I want to walk through your checkpoint unmarked, unwritten, unseen."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I walk to the commandant's office and tell them Serah Vail, twelve years' service, knowingly passed a working charm in inspection and falsified her report. They'll take your cloak. After the stars went out, they're not taking cloaks anymore. They're taking heads. You've seen the squares."

She had seen the squares. She had stood guard at two of the burnings herself, holding back crowds that no longer needed much holding, because fear did the work now. The city had gone quiet in the way of places where everyone is listening for their own name.

"They'll kill me," she said. "Not dismiss me. Kill me. You know that."

"Yes," Wren Galloway said. "I know that. And I'm asking anyway, because I have no one else, and because I think—" he stopped, and something moved behind the careful eyes, some grief so large it had worn itself smooth, "—I think you already looked away once. I'm betting there's a reason. People don't look away for no reason, Warden. They look away because looking straight would cost them something they can't pay."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

Serah thought about the woman at the fish-market stall, screaming her daughter's fever into the smoke. She thought about the old candle-maker thanking her. She thought about a sealed letter in the bottom of her footlocker, written in her sister's hand, that she had read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

"You're right," she said. "There is a reason. And no, I won't tell you what it is. If you're going to hang me with my own rope, you can hang me without knowing everything."

"That's fair."

"It isn't. Nothing about this is fair." She stood, crossed to the window, looked down at the barracks yard where the night watch was assembling with torches, little orange constellations moving through the dark—a dark that had no stars above it anymore, and wouldn't, ever again, if the rumors were true. "Grayford Bridge. Tomorrow at dusk. You'll be carrying a delivery crate. Fish-paste, the kind the garrison eats. Nobody inspects fish-paste; they just make faces about the smell. You'll walk behind the supply cart and I will log you as cart-hand, second shift, name of... Merrin. Merrin Doss. He deserted last month, so the number's free."

Wren blinked. "You've thought about this."

"I've had four years to think about what I'd do if you ever came back," Serah said, before she could stop herself. Then she closed her mouth hard, because that was more than she'd meant to give him.

But he heard it. She watched him hear it—watched the careful eyes go wide and then careful again, filing it away, and she hated him a little for being exactly as sharp as she'd suspected that day on Copper Lane.

"Why did you look away?" he asked softly. "That day. Tell me that much."

"No."

"Warden—"

"*Vail.* If we're doing this, you don't get my rank and I don't get your gratitude. We're a cart and a crate and a lie, and when the bridge is behind us we're strangers." She picked up her cloak off its hook. The gray wool felt heavier than usual, or maybe she was just finally noticing the weight. "Go. Out the yard door, left, down the drainage stair. Don't let the sentry see your face. Come at dusk with a crate and smell like herring, and pray to whatever outlawed god you keep that nobody at Grayford is having a curious day."

He rose. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and looked back at her.

"For whatever it's worth," he said, "the woman at the fish market—the one who threw herself over the luck-knots. Her daughter's fever broke two days later. I didn't do it. But I know who did, and they're safe, and they're sorry about the crate."

Serah stared at him.

Then he was gone, and the door clicked shut, and she stood alone in the regulation room with her heart going like a drum in a parade, and understood that somewhere between the moth-soft knock and the latch clicking home, she had already decided.

She had decided four years ago, probably. On Copper Lane, with a working charm in her hand and a frightened man's whole life balanced on her next sentence. Everything since had just been the long slow fall toward landing.

* * *

Dusk came up red over the eastern walls, the color of the torches they liked to use, and Serah met the supply cart at the Grayford road fork with her squad of four and her ledger and her face like a locked door.

The cart smelled exactly as promised. The driver, a fat garrison contractor named Pell, complained about the paste, the road, the weather, the absence of stars, and the general ingratitude of Wardens, in that order, while Serah checked his manifest with one eye and swept the cart-bed with the other.

The crate-hand was hunched and reeking of herring, face shadowed under a laborer's hood, hands deliberately filthy. He didn't look at her. Good. Looking was how people died at checkpoints.

"Herring again," said young Corporat Fen, wrinkling his nose. He was nineteen and earnest and still believed the cloak meant something, and Serah both pitied and envied him. "Third load this week."

"The garrison loves what it loves," Serah said, and stamped the manifest. "Wave it through."

And that should have been the end of it. That should have been the whole story: a crate, a stamp, a man walking east into the dark, and Serah Vail going back to her ledger and her hollow smoothness until the day the court beneath the mountain finally called in whatever it was they called in.

Except that as the cart rolled across Grayford Bridge, a rider came up the western road at a gallop, gray-cloaked, mud to the thighs, and shouted her name.

"Sergeant Vail! Order from the commandant!" He hauled the horse to a lathered stop, thrust out a folded paper banded with black wax—the new wax, star-black, that had appeared the night the sky went empty. "All posts: detain any traveler bound for the mountain. Double inspections. And—" he caught his breath, and his young face went pale in the torchlight, "—there's a description attached. A man, alone, heading east. Charm-seller. Considered extremely dangerous. Shoot first, report after."

Serah took the paper. Unfolded it. Read it by torchlight.

It was not Wren Galloway's face. The sketch was wrong—the jaw too heavy, the eyes too wide—but the description underneath was close enough, and the seal at the bottom was not the commandant's. It was older. It was a sigil she had seen exactly once before, stamped in the margin of a letter she kept folded in the bottom of her footlocker, in handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her sister's handwriting. Under that same seal.

The rider was watching her. Fen was watching her. The cart was already halfway across the bridge, the herring-smelling crate-hand walking steadily east, and the order in her hand said *shoot first*, and the river below the bridge ran black and starless, and somewhere under the mountain a court full of pale clerks was counting souls like coin.

Serah Vail folded the paper along its soft old creases, which matched the creases in her own heart almost exactly.

"Fen," she said. "Log the order received, dusk, Grayford post. I'm taking a fast horse."

"Where to, Sergeant?"

She swung up into the saddle and looked east, at the road, at the dark, at the place where a man with a crate was about to learn that his blackmailer had become his shadow.

"To correct an inspection error," she said. "Four years overdue."
