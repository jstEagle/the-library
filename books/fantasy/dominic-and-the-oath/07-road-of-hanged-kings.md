## 7. Road of Hanged Kings

The road was called the King's Way once, before the kings stopped being allowed to use it.

Brannoc knew this because he had walked it in both directions—first as a soldier of the Ninth Banner, marching north under a sky that still bothered to be blue, and now as an exile walking south with nothing but a cook-pot on his back and a name that got him spat at in every market town from the border to the sea. They called it the Road of Hanged Kings now, though no king had ever swung from its trees. Only healers. Only oath-breakers of the useful kind.

He passed the first gibbet an hour after dawn. The cage hanging from the oak was empty, rusted through at the hinges, but someone had carved into the wood beneath it, in letters as tall as his hand: *I only made the fever leave.*

Brannoc touched two fingers to the carving, the way soldiers touched the standards before a battle. Old habit. Then he kept walking, because stopping too long at gibbets was how you ended up in one.

His shoulder ached where the brand sat. He did not look at it. Twelve years of not looking at it had made him very good at it, the way Wren Galloway—that thin, careful charm-seller trudging twenty paces behind him—was good at not looking at the wardens' patrols, and the warden woman, Serah Vail, was good at not looking at anyone at all. Strange company, the three of them. A healer who couldn't heal, a warden who'd burned her own colors off her sleeve, and a charm-monger who walked like a man carrying a lit coal in his chest and daring the world to notice the smoke.

Brannoc carried the big pot. It was the least he could do. Also the pot was enormous and nobody else could.

"You walk loud," Serah said, falling into step beside him. She said little, but when she spoke it was always something like this—an observation shaped like a complaint. "For a man your size you should move quieter."

"For a man my size," Brannoc said, "I move like smoke."

"You move like a granary falling over."

"Smoke made of grain."

Behind them, Wren snorted—the first sound resembling laughter Brannoc had heard from him since Copper Lane burned. Good. A man who wouldn't laugh wouldn't eat, and a man who wouldn't eat couldn't walk to whatever mountain court had swallowed Tam Rell. Brannoc didn't know the whole story yet. He knew enough: girl taken, soul sold, stars gone dark, and this small frightened merchant setting out anyway, which told Brannoc more about Wren Galloway than any confession would have.

The road bent south around a hill crowned with dead elms, and there Brannoc smelled it before he saw it.

Blood. Old blood, days old, baked into dust. And under it, sharper: the smell he had spent twelve years trying to forget. Curse-stink. Like copper left in milk, like a room where something had been spoken over a person until the person stopped mattering.

He set down the pot.

"Don't," Serah said immediately.

"I haven't done anything."

"You've done the thing with your face. The thing before you do the other thing." Her hand drifted toward her belt, where she wore no weapon anymore but kept reaching anyway. "We can't afford entanglements. We have six days to reach the mountain's mouth before the next caravan goes up, and every hour we stop is—"

"There's someone in the culvert," Brannoc said. "Under the bridge. Alive. Barely." He was already moving. "You two go on if you like. I'll catch up."

It was a lie and everyone knew it. He heard Serah curse in the flat voice of a woman who had expected exactly this, and then their footsteps followed his anyway, which warmed something in him he hadn't let get warm in a long time.

* * *

The boy was maybe sixteen, curled in the concrete throat of the drainage channel beneath the old bridge, wrapped in a horse blanket gone gray with road dirt. His breathing came in a rhythm Brannoc recognized and hated: the long drag, the wet rattle, the pause too long between exhales, as if his body kept forgetting the argument for continuing.

Curse-work. Not the crude kind—a hex slapped on by some drunk hedge-witch. This was laid in deep, stitched through the boy like roots through soil. Brannoc knelt and pulled back the blanket and saw the marks: black filaments running under the skin of the boy's forearms like veins of ore, branching, spreading upward toward the heart. Every few seconds the filaments pulsed faintly, and with each pulse the boy flinched even in sleep.

"Healer's marks," Serah said quietly behind him. She'd seen them before; her tone said so. "Someone cursed a healer."

"Worse than cursed." Brannoc turned the boy's wrist gently. At the base of the thumb, scarred into the skin by cautery rather than ink, was the sigil of the field-surgeons' guild—a hand holding a lantern. "This is a *binding*. Someone caught him healing and wanted it to be the last thing he ever did. See how the lines branch? It's grown into him. If you cut it out—" he traced one black thread with a fingernail, not touching, "—you cut everything it's rooted through. Nerves, breath, the part of a person that decides to keep going."

Wren crouched on the other side, and Brannoc watched him do the same arithmetic he'd done himself. Watched the charm-seller's face go carefully blank, the way faces went blank in Corvane when the wardens knocked.

"How long has he been here?" Wren asked.

The boy's eyes opened. Gray eyes, fever-bright, and he looked at the three of them with the exhausted cunning of an animal calculating whether these were rescuers or the other kind.

"If you're from the Banner," he rasped, "I'll say I attacked you first. Save you the paperwork."

"We're not the Banner," Brannoc said.

"The Banner doesn't travel with giants and women without badges." The boy coughed, and the cough tore him; Brannoc saw fresh black spread a finger's width up his throat from the effort. "Then what are you?"

"Hungry," Brannoc said. "Traveling south. Bad at leaving people in ditches." He settled his bulk against the culvert wall, making himself small, which for Brannoc meant merely enormous instead of mountainous. "My name is Brannoc. I was a healer too. Before."

Something moved in the boy's face—hope trying to lift weight it couldn't carry. "Before they exiled you."

"Before they exiled me."

"They said you healed forty soldiers at Redford Crossing. After the order came down to let them die." The boy's voice cracked. "They said that's why they hang healers now. Because of you."

Brannoc looked at the dead elms overhead, at the empty gibbet swaying slightly in no wind at all. "They hang healers because it's cheaper than paying them," he said. "I just gave them an excuse with a face on it." Then, because the boy's breathing had gone worse and there was no more time for history: "What's your name?"

"...Pell."

"All right, Pell. I'm going to look at the work that's in you. I won't lie to you about what I find."

He took the boy's hand, and Brannoc let his own gift rise for the first time in weeks—the slow warm tide of it, the thing that had been worth more than gold and cost him everything—and followed the curse down.

It was beautiful, in the way that cruelty is sometimes beautiful when it is thorough. Whoever had made this binding had been skilled. It wasn't a knot; it was a *weave*, anchored at the heart, each black thread feeding on the boy's own healing gift, growing fat whenever Pell tried to mend anything—even himself. That was the elegance of it. Every instinct of a healer's body, every reflexive reach toward mending, fed the thing strangling him. He had been dying faster the harder he tried to live.

Brannoc withdrew, sweating, his hands shaking.

"It can't be cut," he said. "It can't be starved—he's too weak. It can't be smothered, because it lives in his own light." He looked up at Wren, and the words came out of him before he could weigh them, heavy as the pot on his back. "But knots can be retied. Weaves can be rewoven. I need someone who works charms."

"I sell luck-knots," Wren said. His voice was very careful. "Trinkets. Knickknacks. Nothing that would interest a warden."

Serah laughed once, short and sharp. "Wren. The man is dying."

"I know what he is." Wren's jaw worked. "And I know what happens to me if I touch real working in the open, with a warden standing over me, even a retired one. I have spent twelve years being small, Serah. Small is how I stayed alive. Small is how *Tam* stayed alive." He stared at the black threads pulsing under Pell's skin, and Brannoc watched something war in him—fear against the other thing, the thing that had dragged him out of Corvane with a stolen horse and a plan no saner man would have attempted.

"You already broke it," Brannoc said quietly. "Whatever oath kept you sealed. The night the stars went out, you felt it snap. You told me so yourself, on the first night, when you thought I was asleep." He leaned forward. "You're not small anymore, Wren Galloway. You're just still acting like it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, on the bridge, a cart rolled past, and none of the three of them so much as glanced up, which Brannoc thought later was perhaps the moment the company became a company: three outlaws who had stopped hiding from the road because they were busy with each other.

Wren knelt. He rolled up his sleeves.

"What do you need?" he said.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and Brannoc learned things about charm-craft that twelve years of exile had never shown him, because Wren Galloway worked the way water works—not striking at the curse but flowing around it, finding the seams, persuading where Brannoc would have pried.

The theory was simple enough that Brannoc felt foolish for not seeing it. The curse fed on healing; therefore the answer was not to fight it with healing but to *feed it a lie*. Wren spun a charm of false mending—a knot of light shaped exactly like the warmth of a healer's gift, indistinguishable to anything that hungered for it—and Brannoc guided it in along the curse's own channels, the two of them working in tandem, giant's hands holding the weave steady while the charm-seller's clever fingers walked it into place.

The curse took the bait. Of course it took the bait. It had been starving for days.

And as it swallowed the false light, Wren cinched the knot.

What happened then, Brannoc would remember all his life. The black weave, gorged and fooled, contracted—and Wren *turned it inside out*, the way you turn a glove inside out, so that every anchoring thread that had been rooted in Pell's heart suddenly pointed outward, into the air, into nothing. For one terrible heartbeat the whole structure hung inverted, screaming in whatever language curses scream in, and then it had nothing left to hold and it came away from the boy in a single sheet of darkness that Wren caught in his cupped hands like a net full of smoke, and crushed, and crushed, until it was no bigger than a coal, and then it was no bigger than ash, and then it was nothing at all.

Pell breathed. Really breathed—the whole lung, the easy rhythm, the gasp of a swimmer surfacing.

Then he wept, silently, staring at the underside of the bridge, and Brannoc put a hand on the boy's chest, gently, and let the last of his own warmth settle the storm the curse had left in its bed. Mending a body after the sickness was easy. It was the easiest thing in the world, and it had been outlawed anyway, and Brannoc did it anyway, and did not care.

Wren sat back on his heels, gray-faced, sweat-soaked, hands trembling. Serah crouched beside him and, without a word, pressed her waterskin into his hands.

"That," she said, "was the most illegal thing I have ever witnessed."

"Retired wardens shouldn't witness things," Wren managed.

"I'm saying if you can do *that*, you can do whatever needs doing at that mountain. Whatever's waiting for us." She looked at him a long moment. "You've been hiding a sun under a bushel basket for twelve years, charm-seller."

Wren didn't answer. But he drank the water, and his hands slowly stopped shaking, and Brannoc thought: *there*. There is the man who will break into a court of souls. He was there the whole time. He just needed someone to need him.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the bridge, a proper fire for once because Serah judged them far enough from any patrol, and Brannoc cooked in the big pot because the big pot existed to be cooked in. Barley and salt pork and a fistful of dried mushrooms Pell gathered from the bridge's shadowed banks, moving stiffly but moving, alive, his color coming back with every hour.

Pell told them his story in pieces, between bites. An apprentice field-surgeon, attached to the garrison at Marle. He'd treated a captain's son for fever after the order came that the boy was to be left to it—some quarrel, some punishment, Brannoc didn't learn whose. The boy lived. Three nights later men in plain clothes took Pell from his bunk, and a surgeon he'd never met laid the binding into him with great care, explaining each step as he went, the way surgeons explain things, and then they put Pell on a cart south and dumped him at the Road of Hanged Kings with a blanket and the instruction to die somewhere instructive.

"They wanted other healers to find you," Serah said. Her firelight face was very hard. "A warning that walks."

"It worked," Pell said simply. "Nobody's come near this bridge in four days except crows."

Brannoc passed him the pot. "Eat, crow-food. You're done being a lesson."

Pell ate. Then, quietly, not looking up: "I heard what you said to him. Before. About the Banner orders at Redford." He turned the spoon in his fingers. "Is it true? Forty men?"

"Thirty-eight," Brannoc said. "Two were beyond anyone."

"And they exiled you for it. Branded you. Put your face on every gallows from here to the coast." Pell's gray eyes came up, burning. "Doesn't that make you angry?"

Brannoc considered the question honestly, because the boy deserved honesty. "I was angry for years. Anger's a coal—you can warm yourself on it or you can burn your house down, and mostly you do both. These days—" he tapped his own chest, over the brand he never looked at, "—these days I just keep walking south and cooking for whoever's in the ditch. It's not justice. But it's *against* the injustice, consistently, and I've decided that counts."

"It doesn't count," Pell said. "It has to count for more than that."

"It does," Wren said, surprising all of them. He was sitting apart, sharpening a knife he had no obvious use for, and he spoke without looking up. "The court we're walking into—this Undercourt—they've built a system where nothing counts unless it's written in their ledger, weighed on their scale, stamped by their bench. Where justice is a currency and they hold the mint." The knife whispered along the stone. "A man who feeds people in ditches anyway, without permission, without ledger—that's the one thing their whole system can't price. Believe me. I've been thinking about little else for six days."

Silence again, but a different kind. Brannoc looked at Wren—at this small, tired, dangerous man walking toward a mountain to steal back a soul—and understood that he had already decided something, and that the decision had been made sometime around the moment Wren knelt in the culvert dust and rolled up his sleeves.

"Well," Brannoc said, and stood, and his shadow fell across the whole campfire like evening coming early. "If you're walking south to rob a court of the dead, you'll want someone who can carry things. Hold doors. Be large at strategic moments." He picked up the pot and slung it across his back. "I'm told I qualify."

Serah raised an eyebrow. "Just like that? No terms? You don't even know what waits under that mountain."

"I know what waits on this road," Brannoc said. "Gibbets and ditches and boys in culverts, forever, until somebody goes upstream and breaks the thing at its source." He nodded at Wren. "Your mountain is upstream. So. I'm coming."

Pell scrambled up too, blanket and all, swaying with the effort and refusing to acknowledge it. "Then I'm coming as well."

"You can barely walk," Serah said.

"I can walk *south*. And I owe a debt I intend to see paid, and besides—" the boy's chin came up, and Brannoc saw the field-surgeon in him for the first time, the steadiness under the ruin, "—you're walking into a place where people get hurt. You'll want a surgeon. Even a cursed one. *Especially* a cursed one. I know what those bindings feel like from the inside now. I'll know them when I see them."

Wren looked around the fire: the warden without a badge, the healer without a country, the boy without a future until this afternoon. Brannoc watched him do it—watched him count them, and flinch from counting them, because every person added to this company was another person the mountain could take.

"You understand," Wren said at last, quietly, "that I can't promise any of you come back."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "You can't. That's rather the point. Nobody promised me anything at Redford either, and I went in anyway." He held out his hand, enormous, palm up, over the fire. "Company of the road, then. Until the mountain or the grave, whichever bills us first."

One by one they put their hands in his—Serah's calloused grip, Pell's fever-warm fingers, and last Wren's small strong hand, which hesitated over the pile like a man at the edge of cold water, and then committed.

Far to the north, over the shoulder of the hills, the sky was wrong. It had been wrong for six days—starless, moonless, a lid of featureless dark pressing down on the world—but tonight Brannoc noticed something new, something that raised every hair he owned.

At the horizon, where the mountain stood, the darkness was *moving*. Slowly. Inward. Like a slow breath drawn through teeth.

Pell saw it too. "What is that?"

"The mountain," Serah said, "breathing." She stood, and her face in the last of the firelight looked ten years older than it had at dawn. "Caravan masters say the dark gets thicker the closer you climb. That petitioners bring torches up the switchbacks and the flames bend toward the peak, like it's inhaling them." She kicked dirt over the coals. "Sleep fast, all of you. We make the foothills by tomorrow night, and I want eyes on that caravan road before the next load of desperate souls queues up to get in."

Brannoc lay back with the pot for a pillow and did not sleep fast. He lay watching the dark breathe on the horizon, listening to the small sounds of three strangers sleeping near enough to touch, and thought about ditches, and gibbets, and the strange arithmetic by which a man exiled for saving thirty-eight lives had somehow, on a dead king's road, begun to save a fifth.

Toward morning, he finally slept—and dreamed of a door, deep underground, and behind it something whispering a name he almost recognized.

The name was *Tam*.
