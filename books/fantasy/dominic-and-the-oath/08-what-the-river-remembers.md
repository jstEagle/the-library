## 8. What the River Remembers

The river found them before they found it.

Wren heard it first as a wrongness in the birdsong—a hush where there should have been water-sound, though the ground sloped down and damp rose through the loam like breath from a sleeping mouth. Brannoc stopped so abruptly that Tam's borrowed pack swung against his back, and the giant tilted his head the way he did when listening to something below the range of ordinary ears.

"She's awake," he said.

"Who?" Serah's hand had already gone to her blade. Twelve years of patrols had taught Wren that reflex; he recognized it now for what it was—fear wearing armor.

"The river." Brannoc rolled his shoulders, and the old soldier's scars on his forearms caught the grey morning light. "They told stories in my village. The Sarrow. It remembers everything that ever fell into it. Everything that ever crossed it. And it likes to talk."

* * *

The Sarrow was not wide. That was the first lie of it—a traveler might have leapt it with a running start, thirty feet of brown-green water sliding between alder roots, so quiet it seemed apologetic. But the air above it bent, like heat-shimmer in winter, and when Wren looked at the surface too long the water looked back.

Not with eyes. With recognition.

Faces moved under the current. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Faint as coins at the bottom of a well, rising and sinking, mouths open as if mid-sentence. He saw an old woman's face dissolve into a young man's; he saw a child's features swirl apart into silt. None of them screamed. That was the worst part. They all looked like they were trying to say something they'd forgotten how to say.

"We don't cross here," Serah said flatly. "We walk to the ford at Marrow Bridge."

"Marrow Bridge has a Warden garrison," Wren said. "You told us yourself. Two checkpoints between here and the mountain road, and you're not walking through either one wearing a burned-out badge and three wanted fugitives."

"I'd rather fight Wardens than whatever that is."

"You don't have to fight anything," Brannoc rumbled. "You pay. Everyone pays. It's fair—that's the thing about her. She doesn't take what you won't give. That's more than can be said for most powers I've met."

Serah stared at him. "You've crossed before."

"Once. Coming south, after the exile." His jaw worked. "I paid a memory. Can't tell you which one, because—" he tapped his temple, and something bleak flickered behind his eyes, "—that's rather the point, isn't it."

Tam shifted at Wren's side. She'd been quiet since the ash fields, quiet since Copper Lane, and every silence of hers landed on him like a debt. Her soul was a coin in a vault beneath the mountain, and the girl beside him was—what? A vessel. A woman shaped by the absence of herself. She looked at the river now with a strange, naked curiosity, as if she hoped it held something of hers.

"What does she take?" Tam asked softly. "Exactly?"

"Memories," Wren said. "True ones. Ones that cost." He'd heard the stories too, back in Corvane's charm-stalls and smuggler dens: the Sarrow didn't want trivia. It wouldn't take your mother's maiden name or the color of your first coat. It took the memories that were load-bearing. The ones a person was built on.

"And if you refuse?"

Brannoc gestured at the faces beneath the water. "Then you join the conversation. Forever. She's not cruel. She's just lonely, and hungry, and very, very patient."

* * *

They walked the bank looking for another crossing for half the day, and the river walked with them—or rather, kept pace, appearing through the trees again and again, curving to intercept, until Wren understood that the Sarrow was not a place but a choice. There was no going around it. There was only across, or back.

Back meant Copper Lane's ashes. Back meant Tam hollow-eyed and fading a little more each day, her edges blurring like a drawing left in rain. Whatever the court was doing with her soul, distance wasn't slowing it.

"We cross," Wren said.

He felt Serah's stare on the back of his neck. "And what do you plan to pay, charm-seller? You've spent twelve years building a life out of small things. Small kindnesses, small lies, small magics. If she takes a load-bearing memory out of you—" Serah paused, choosing the words like stepping stones, "—what's left standing?"

That was the question he'd been turning over since dawn, turning like a stone in the pocket, worrying its smoothness. He didn't know. That was the truth of it. He didn't know what he was without the fear, without the hiding, without the careful smallness—and he was about to hand an ancient hunger a knife and ask it to find out.

"Whatever she asks," he said. "It's my toll first. I'm the one who broke the wards. I'm the reason the sky is dark and the Wardens are burning shops and Tam—" His voice snagged. He pushed through it. "I go first."

"No," Serah said, and it surprised them both, the sharpness of it. "We cross together or not at all. That's not sentiment. That's tactics. If she takes something from each of us while the others watch, we'll be four strangers on the far bank. We cross in a line, fast, no lingering. And nobody tells anyone what they paid." She glanced at Brannoc. "You knew this already. Didn't you. Why you said nothing about which memory you gave."

Brannoc smiled, slow and sad. "River rules, warden. Some mercies are just secrets wearing gloves."

* * *

Dusk came violet and cold, and the Sarrow's surface went glassy, reflecting a sky with no stars in it—which meant, Wren realized with a lurch, that for once the river showed them exactly what was real. A dark sky. Nothing missing that wasn't already gone.

She rose at full dark.

Not from the water—from the water's memory of itself. She came up as a figure made of current, tall as two women, her body a braid of every flood this valley had ever suffered, and where a face should be there was a slow whirlpool, turning, turning, and in its depths faces surfaced and sank—the same faces from the shallows, but close now, intimate, like family seen through ice.

"Keeper," the river said.

Her voice was many voices layered, the way sediment is many years. Wren's whole body went rigid. *Keeper.* No one had called him that in twelve years. No one alive should know the word.

"I'm not—" he started.

"You are." The whirlpool-face turned toward him, and he felt the current of her attention move through his chest like cold fingers finding the scar the oath had left. "You carry a broken seal in you, little keeper. It sings. It sings of gates and borders and a name folded up and put away. I remember names. I remember everything that washes past me." A ripple ran the length of her, almost like a shiver of pleasure. "You will make such a fine payment. But not yet. Not first. First we set the price."

Behind him, Serah's sword whispered an inch from its sheath. Brannoc's hand closed gently on her wrist: *no.*

"Name it," Wren said. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. Small steadiness. Small courage. The currency of a small life—but it would have to do.

"The face," the river said.

The word dropped into the clearing and sat there.

"Your face. The one you wore before the gate, before the oath, before you buried your name and grew this careful stranger over the grave." The whirlpool turned, slowly, considering him. "You look at reflections sideways. You flinch from windows. You have spent twelve years not knowing what you see. Give me the face you refuse, and the memory of it—all of it, the shape of your own jaw, the sound of your own laugh from before—and the crossing is yours. All four of yours. Fairly given. Freely given."

"That's—" Tam began.

"It's not possible," Serah finished. "A face? You can't take a man's face. That's his body. That's—"

"It is not flesh I take," the river said, with the mild patience of something that had watched mountains erode. "It is his knowing of it. Every man carries a portrait of himself painted in memory. His. Hers." The whirlpool inclined toward Tam, and Wren's heart clenched. "Hers was taken crudely, by clerks with ledgers. Crude work. Leaves a person ragged at the edges. I am not crude. What I take, I take whole and clean, and what remains stands straighter than you'd think. Cleaner than you'd think." A pause, currents folding. "Lighter."

"Lighter," Wren echoed. The word tasted like a trap.

"There is a version of you," the river said, "who has been waiting twelve years to stop carrying that face. You've hated it. It belongs to a man who failed everyone he loved. You keep it because grief keeps what guilt cannot bury." The whirlpool slowed. "I am offering mercy, keeper. Do not mistake it for theft. Theft is what the mountain does."

The wind moved through the alders. Somewhere downstream, water chuckled over stones, and every stone, Wren thought suddenly, had once been part of something larger, worn down by patience into something unrecognizable. That was the Sarrow's whole nature. Not violence. Erosion.

He thought about the mirror in the back room of his shop on Copper Lane, the one he'd hung facing the wall. He thought about twelve years of catching his reflection in shopfront glass and looking away, quick, like avoiding an old creditor. He thought about the night at the gate—the oath-light crawling up his arms, the moment before it sealed, when he'd had one last breath of being whoever he had been, and he remembered thinking, absurdly, *remember this face,* and then he hadn't. Couldn't. The oath had taken care of that.

Maybe the river was right. Maybe some part of him had been waiting to put it down.

"Wren." Serah stepped up beside him, low and urgent. "Don't. Whatever she's showing you, it's bait. Spirits bargain in the currency of despair. You pay her this and you won't know why any of this matters to you. You won't know why *she*" —a nod at Tam— "matters."

"I'll know," Wren said quietly. "Because I'll still be walking toward the mountain, won't I? That's the proof. Love isn't the memory of loving. It's the direction you keep moving." He heard the words come out of himself and marveled a little—they were braver than he felt, and truer. "Besides. You said it yourself. We cross together."

"This is madness."

"Probably." He managed a crooked smile. "Keep count of my steps. If I start wandering, hit me."

* * *

He waded in to his knees before he let himself think about it properly, because thinking about it properly was exactly how he'd lose his nerve. The water was warm. That unsettled him more than cold would have—warm as bathwater, warm as a held hand.

The river rose around him without wetting him, a column of current, and the whirlpool-face descended until it filled his whole horizon. Faces turned in its depths. For one vertiginous instant he saw one that looked—almost—like his own. Younger. Unlined. Laughing at something out of frame.

*Oh,* he thought. *There you are.*

"Say it freely," the river murmured, everywhere, inside the water and inside his skull. "Freely given is the only coin I honor."

"Freely given," Wren said.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it. It felt like exhaling after holding a breath for twelve years.

The memory unfolded out of him in long bright ribbons: his mother's thumb tracing the line of his nose and saying *you have your grandfather's brow*; the first time he'd seen himself grown in a polished shield and thought, startled, *so that's the man I am*; the specific weight of his own grin, the gap in it, the way his old name sounded in his own mouth. The face itself—cheekbones, jaw, the particular brown of his eyes—he surrendered last, and it went like a lantern being carried away down a long corridor, smaller and smaller, and then a corner of light, and then nothing.

Then he was kneeling in shallow water, and the river was a river again, and three people were staring at him from the bank as if he were a ghost.

Because to them, he supposed, he nearly was.

"He looks—" Tam's voice cracked. She pressed both hands over her mouth. "His face is the same. It's the same face, I can see it, but it's—it's *wrong*, it's like looking at someone through the wrong end of a spyglass, like the face doesn't belong to—"

"Don't," Wren said.

The word came out strange to him. Flat. He reached up and touched his own cheek and felt skin, warmth, the stubble of four days' road—and underneath the touch, nothing. No recognition. No flicker of *mine*. He was a man wearing an accurate mask of himself.

He tried to remember what he'd paid and found the hole where it had been, smooth-edged, painless, like a tooth lost long ago. He knew he had given something enormous—he could read it in their faces, in Serah's stricken eyes, in the way Brannoc bowed his head as if at a graveside. He knew it mattered. He simply couldn't feel *why.*

And yet.

He climbed the bank, dripping, and looked at Tam—at this woman whose own self had been scooped out by clerks and sold to buy a starless sky—and the ache in his chest arrived right on schedule, vast and specific and unmistakable. He didn't need the memory of his face to know her. He didn't even, apparently, need much of anything except the direction of his feet.

"Told you," he said, hoarse. "Love's the direction you keep moving. Come on. We're losing the night."

* * *

They crossed in single file behind him, and the river let them pass in silence, though Wren felt her attention trail each of them like fingertips. When Serah reached the far bank she stopped beside him, and for a long moment neither spoke. Across the water, the faces beneath the surface had gone still, watching, or perhaps just resting.

"You didn't hesitate," she said finally. It wasn't admiration, exactly. It was something rawer—the sound of a worldview cracking along a seam. "Twelve years I've hunted people like you. Charm-mongers, oath-breakers, smugglers of the forbidden. And every one of them, when it came time to pay, they paid with someone else. Always someone else. That's the whole trade, isn't it? Moving the cost off your own ledger onto whoever's nearest." She looked at him, and in the starless dark her eyes were very bright. "You paid with the only thing that was actually yours."

"It was the cheapest thing I had," Wren said. "Turns out."

"That's not true."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

She unslung her canteen, drank, offered it to him. A small gesture. An enormous one, from her. He drank.

"When we get there," Serah said, "to the mountain—to the court—if there's a moment where a badge matters, or a uniform opens a door that shouldn't open..." She turned the canteen in her hands. "I'll spend what's mine. Just so you know. I haven't decided yet what that is. But it's mine, and it's on the table."

Before Wren could answer, Brannoc made a sound low in his chest—not quite a growl, not quite a gasp—and pointed north.

Above the black shoulder of the world, where the mountain stood against the sky, the darkness was moving. Not cloud. Not weather. A slow spiral of absence turning above the peak like water circling a drain, faintly edged in green, the way deep water glows with things that have never seen the sun.

And threaded through it—thin, high, almost sweet—came a sound none of them could name and all of them felt in their teeth: the sound, Wren understood with a certainty that bypassed his stolen face entirely, of a court that had noticed them coming.

It was singing the way ledgers sing.

It was adding their names.
