## 9. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Serah Vail had walked the pilgrim roads of three provinces, had stood post at sieges and funerals and one coronation that ended in blood, and she had never seen anything like the queue.

It spilled out of the mountain's mouth like a tongue of grey cloth, winding down the switchback trail, across the scree field, along the frozen riverbank, and out of sight beyond a ridge where the morning fog still clung. Thousands of people. Tens of thousands, maybe. They stood in lines so old that the ground beneath their feet was worn into channels, knee-deep grooves in the stone where generations of waiting bodies had slowly carved their patience into the mountain's skin.

"They're not moving," Brannoc said. His voice came out hushed, which from a man his size sounded like an avalanche trying to be polite. "Look at them. Nobody's moving."

He was right. The line breathed—people shifted weight, passed water skins hand to hand, wrapped children in blankets—but it did not advance. Somewhere far up the trail, at the black archway where the mountain swallowed the road, nothing emerged. Nothing went in either, as far as Serah could tell, though the archway itself was too dark to read.

"Petitioners," Wren said quietly. He stood at the edge of the trail with his hood back, staring up at the mountain with an expression Serah was beginning to catalog: the tight jaw he wore when he was afraid, the steady hands he kept anyway. "The broker mentioned them once, when Tam—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "People who want justice from the Undercourt. Wrongs too big for any magistrate above ground. Stolen inheritances. Murdered kin. Curses with no cure." A pause. "Souls."

"Centuries," said a voice near their feet.

An old woman sat on a folding stool beside the trail, positioned as if she'd been planted there like a fencepost. She was knitting something long and shapeless out of grey wool, and her eyes were milky but fixed on them with unsettling precision. "You're new. I can smell new. Centuries, I've waited. My grandfather waited before me, and died in this line, and I took his place because the claim passes down." She held up her knitting. It had no beginning and no end; Serah realized with a cold turn of the stomach that the woman was knitting the same row over and over, unraveling each pass before starting again. "The Undercourt does not forget a petition. But it does not hurry one, either. Justice here is patient. Justice here is *eternal*. That is not the same thing as fair, whatever the clerks tell you."

"How long until you reach the door?" Serah asked.

The old woman smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "I was forty-three when I sat down. I'm ninety-one now. The line has moved eleven feet." She returned to her endless row. "But go on ahead, soldiers. You look like people in a hurry. Hurry is what they feed on."

*They*, Serah thought. Not *it*. As though the court were plural. As though it were hungry.

Wren was already climbing.

---

The trail rose through a landscape that grew stranger with every switchback. First the trees stopped—not thinned, simply ceased, as if drawn along a chalk line—and were replaced by white stone pillars rising from the scree like the ribs of some buried god. Then the sound changed. Wind still moved through the pillars, but it made no noise against them, and after an hour Serah realized the silence wasn't absence. It was attention. The whole mountain felt like a held breath.

Brannoc walked with his massive shoulders hunched, not from the cold but from something older. Twice he glanced back down the trail at the endless queue, and twice his hands curled into fists.

"My village sent a petition here once," he said, unprompted, somewhere past the third ridge. "After the plague. We asked the court to return the souls the fever took—there was a rumor, then, that the Undercourt could bargain them back. Four families sold everything they had to fund the journey. The petitioner was a girl of nineteen named Maren Ostwyck." He was quiet for several steps. "She never came home. Her mother walked into the sea the following winter. And the plague year passed out of memory, and the mountain stayed shut, and nobody spoke of Maren again except me, because remembering was my punishment for being the one who survived."

"You think she's in that line," Serah said. It wasn't a question.

"I think she's in that line or she's inside, or she's a name on some ledger in a vault, worth exactly as much as the ink." Brannoc looked up at the black archway, closer now, close enough that its darkness had texture—a depth that light refused to enter rather than failed to reach. "If she's in that line, Wren, I'm walking past her. Do you understand? I'm going to walk past eighty years of her waiting to save one soul, and I don't know how to carry that."

Wren didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was low and even. "Then we don't walk past her. We walk *through* what's keeping her standing there. There's a difference."

It was a fine distinction, the kind of sentence a man tells himself at night, but Serah found herself holding onto it anyway, the way you hold a rope over a drop.

---

They reached the archway at dusk—or what would have been dusk, if the starless sky allowed such things to be distinguished. Above the mountain, the heavens were a flat, dead grey, the same shade at noon as at midnight, and Serah had begun to understand why the old texts called this era the Dimming. Without stars, time lost its edges. Days blurred. Only hunger and tiredness marked the hours, and both felt like insults rather than information.

The archway itself was granite, thirty feet high, carved with script so ancient that the letters had eroded into suggestions. But the dark inside it was not empty. As Serah approached, she saw that the doorway was hung with chains—hundreds of them, thousands, thin as hair and black as ink, descending from some unseen height into the passage beyond. They swayed without wind. Some bore small tags of lead, stamped with numbers. One, near the bottom, close enough to touch, bore a tag stamped with a word instead: *PAID*.

"Don't," Wren said sharply, catching Serah's wrist an inch before her fingers met the chain.

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were. Everyone does. It looks like a boundary marker. It isn't." He released her wrist gently, but his eyes stayed hard. "It's an offer. Touch a chain, and you've made a petition. Touch it asking about someone, and you've told the court who matters to you." His voice dropped. "Every piece of leverage we have left, we keep. All of us. No names. No connections. Nothing given freely."

Serah pulled her hand back and flexed her fingers. Her sister's name sat behind her teeth like a stone—*Liss, Liss, Liss*—and she clenched her jaw around it and said nothing, and hated how easily Wren had read her, and hated more that he was right.

Beyond the archway, the passage opened after a hundred paces into a cavern that should not have existed.

---

The Undercourt's antechamber was vast enough to have its own weather. Serah's breath fogged; somewhere overhead, moisture gathered and fell in a fine, cold rain that never seemed to reach the floor. The walls were lined with desks—tier upon tier of them, rising like terraced fields into the gloom—and at each desk sat a clerk.

There must have been ten thousand of them. Grey-robed, grey-faced, each bent over a ledger, quills scratching in a rhythm so perfectly synchronized that the sound became a single instrument: *shhhk-shhhk-shhhk*, the heartbeat of the mountain. None of them looked up.

Between the desks ran aisles, and in the aisles moved other figures—petitioners who had somehow gotten this far, clutching documents, led by clerks-in-training with ink-stained fingers. And everywhere, everywhere, the shelves. They climbed the walls and vanished into the dark ceiling, miles of shelving, and on the shelves sat vessels. Jars of smoked glass. Urns of lead. Bottles sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Each one faintly, terribly luminous, glowing with a light that had no source—the light of the things inside them.

"Oh," Brannoc said softly. "Oh, no."

"Souls," Wren whispered. "That's what happened to the sky." He turned in a slow circle, and Serah watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes—the shelves, the thousands of shelves, the stolen light of all those lives banked here like grain in a granary while the world above went dark. "They didn't extinguish the stars. They *outbid* them. Every soul in this room is fuel, and the court burned enough to snuff out heaven, because heaven's light doesn't answer to them and theirs does."

A bell rang somewhere—once, a clear silver note that cut through the quill-scratch—and the nearest aisle emptied and refilled with mechanical smoothness.

"That's our cue, apparently," said a voice at Serah's elbow.

She had not heard him approach. That alone should have been impossible; Serah Vail had spent fifteen years training herself against ambush. But the clerk standing beside them was slight and neat, with spectacles perched on a narrow nose and ink to his second knuckle, and he smiled at them with the courteous emptiness of a man reading from a script he'd delivered ten thousand times.

"Clerk Ivo, Office of Intake, Third Terrace," he said, bowing slightly. "You are unregistered arrivals, which is unusual, and armed, which is irregular, and four of you, which is unlucky—we do prefer odd numbers, the paperwork balances better." He produced a slate and stylus from his robe. "Names?"

"No," said Wren.

Ivo's smile didn't waver. "Refusals are also recordable. Very well. Purpose of visit?"

"We're here about a collected soul," Serah said, before Wren could stop her. "Collected recently. Illegally."

Something flickered across the clerk's face—not offense, not anger. Interest. It lasted less than a heartbeat, but Serah had interrogated prisoners for a decade, and she knew the difference between a mask slipping and a mask adjusting. This man adjusted his mask.

"Illegally," Ivo repeated, tasting the word. "Sergeant—or former sergeant, judging by the coat you've tried to disguise—Vail, isn't it? Of the Corvane Wardens?" He tilted his head at her frozen expression. "We keep records too. Yours is quite thick. Most commendable arrests." He turned to Wren. "And you. The charm-seller of Copper Lane. Unlicensed practitioner, twelve years under notice, three warrants outstanding." His gaze moved to Brannoc and softened with something almost like pity. "And the healer of Harrow Fen. Exiled for saving the wrong soldiers. Yes. I believe I have all your files memorized."

"Then you know why we've come," Wren said.

"I know why you *think* you've come." Ivo tucked the slate away and folded his hands. Around them, the quill-scratch continued, patient as erosion. "You believe the Undercourt took a soul improperly, and that you have come to take it back. So let me save you a century in that queue outside and explain the law of this place, freely given, no charge—which, I assure you, is the last free thing you will receive here."

He began to walk, and they followed, because there was nowhere else to go.

"The Undercourt recognizes two forms of transfer," Ivo said, his voice carrying in the cavernous dark without effort, as though the mountain itself amplified it. "The first is *trade*: freely consented exchange between parties, witnessed and recorded. The second is *payment*: a debt discharged, likewise consented, likewise recorded. What the court does not recognize—" he raised one ink-stained finger, "—is theft. A soul may be bought, bargained, wagered, inherited, or owed. It may never be stolen. Consequently—" the finger rotated, pointing back at them, "—it may never be *re*-stolen. Retrieval without consent is theft. Theft is the one crime this court punishes instantly and absolutely, and the punishment is collection." He smiled again, mild as milk. "You would become inventory. Shelves are available. We are always building more shelves."

"And if the consent was coerced?" Wren's voice cracked like a whip. "If the person didn't understand what they were paying? If they were lied to, manipulated—"

"Then the contract is flawed," Ivo agreed pleasantly. "And may be contested."

Hope flared in Brannoc's face. Serah felt it try to kindle in her own chest—

"—through the proper process," Ivo continued, and the hope died. "A formal petition, filed in the queue, heard in due order. The current average wait for a contested-contract hearing is two hundred and sixty years. The claimant must remain present, personally, for the duration. Absence voids the petition." He spread his small neat hands. "You see the design. Souls may be traded but never taken back. Grievances may be voiced but never answered. The system is not broken, friends. The system is *perfect*. It converts outrage into patience, patience into queue-position, and queue-position into nothing at all. Everyone who enters believes they will be the exception. That belief is the mortar that holds these walls together."

They had stopped before a counter of black stone, taller than a man, its surface worn concave by centuries of pleading hands. Behind it, shelves rose into darkness, and the little lights of the vessels pulsed like a tide.

"So what you're telling us," Serah said slowly, "is that the only way out of a contract is another contract."

Ivo's eyes gleamed behind his spectacles. "*Exchange*," he corrected. "Yes. Soul for soul, value for value, consented on both sides, witnessed and recorded. It has been done. Rarely. The mathematics are unforgiving—one soul rarely balances another, and the court's appraisers are thorough." He leaned forward slightly, and for just a moment, beneath the script and the smile, Serah glimpsed something exhausted and human, quickly buried. "Now. You'll want to know whether the soul you seek can be appraised, and I am permitted to tell you that it can. Collected nine days ago. Payment rendered to a broker of Copper Lane, on behalf of a client whose seal I am not permitted to name." He straightened. "I am also permitted to inform you that the client in question has an account with this court, and accounts may be settled, and settlements may be discussed, in the office of Intake, Third Terrace, during regular hours."

"Which are?" Wren asked.

"Now," said Ivo, and rung a small silver bell.

Somewhere deep in the cavern, ten thousand quills paused mid-scratch—a silence so sudden and total that Serah's ears rang. In that silence, Clerk Ivo looked at each of them in turn, and when his gaze reached Serah, he held it a moment longer than the others, and touched two fingers briefly to his breast pocket, where a folded paper crackled faintly against his robes.

Then the quills resumed, and Ivo turned, and beckoned them toward the terraces with the courteous emptiness of a man who had just done something either very small or very enormous, and was not yet sure which.

"Come along," he said. "The paperwork won't balance itself."
