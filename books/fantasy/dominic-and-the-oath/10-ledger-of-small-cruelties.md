## 10. Ledger of Small Cruelties

Ivo had kept the ledgers for four hundred and six years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: everyone who came down the mountain believed themselves the exception.

They arrived at the Pale Bench with their griefs arranged like offerings—mothers with swaddled bundles of ash, soldiers with medals they'd bitten to test for gold, lovers clutching contracts signed in blood or worse. They all believed the court would see them plainly, weigh their case fairly, and hand back what had been taken. And the court did see them, precisely, completely, and then it charged them for the seeing.

Ivo recorded it all. That was his function. Not judgment—the Bench judged—but *record*. He sat at his desk of black basalt with its lamp of cold blue flame, and he wrote each petition into the Ledger of Small Cruelties in ink mixed from the petitioner's own breath, so that every entry carried the exact weight of the voice that made it. He knew the ledger by heart. All of it. Every line of grief since the founding. Some nights, when the mountain was quiet and the lamps burned low, he read it aloud to himself just to remember what the words sounded like outside his own skull.

Tonight there would be a new entry, and Ivo already knew how it would end, because he had written ten thousand entries and they all ended the same way.

The keeper had come down.

He'd been expected, of course. The moment Wren Galloway crossed the threshold of the mountain's mouth, the wards had tasted him—the old oath still clinging to him like smoke to wool—and word had gone up through the dark veins of the Undercourt faster than any runner. *The gatekeeper walks among us.* The Bench had convened in its slow, grinding way, three magistrates pale as drowned men, and issued instructions: show him the ledger. Show him the price. Then make him the offer.

It was always the offer. The court preferred purchase to force; forced things broke, but bought things stayed bought.

* * *

They brought the keeper to Ivo's chamber at the hour the court called dusk, though no dusk had touched this place in twelve years. Two bailiffs of grey stone walked him between them, and Ivo studied him over the rim of his spectacles as they came.

Thinner than the records suggested. The oath had eaten him from the inside—Ivo could see its architecture still wrapped around the man's heart, a cage of his own vows, though the bars were broken now, snapped on the night the stars went out. Interesting. Broken oaths were dangerous things. A broken oath still wanted to be an oath; it reached for whatever vow was nearest and tried to become it. The Bench found such men useful. Or dangerous. With the Bench it was often both.

"You are Wren Galloway," Ivo said. It was not a question. "Keeper of the western gate. Sworn twelve years past." He turned the great ledger toward himself and opened it to a ribbon of grey silk. "You will forgive me if I do not rise. My knees gave out sometime in the third century."

Wren said nothing. His eyes went, as all eyes went, to the ledger—to the sheer mass of it, forty volumes stacked like masonry, each one thick as a gravestone. Then to the lamp, the desk, the narrow door behind Ivo through which the sounds of the petitioners' queue seeped: shuffling feet, low weeping, someone reciting a name over and over like a prayer that had forgotten its god.

"You have questions," Ivo said. "Everyone does. Ask them."

"Where is she?" Wren's voice was rougher than Ivo had expected. Twelve years of silence should have polished a man smooth. This one had been worn rough instead, like river stone.

"Tamlin Ashgrave. Called Tam. Apprentice charm-seller of Copper Lane." Ivo let the name sit in the air between them. "She is not harmed. I want you to understand that before anything else, because everything else will be difficult enough without you spending your attention on fear."

"Where. Is. She."

"In the vaults. Sleeping, mostly. Souls sleep when they're newly housed—it eases the transition." Ivo gestured to the chair opposite his desk. It was a plain chair, deliberately; the Bench had learned long ago that comfort made petitioners angry and discomfort made them reckless, but neutrality made them listen. "Sit, Keeper. What I am about to show you, you may stand or sit for. It changes nothing. But my experience says sitting helps."

To his mild surprise, the man sat. He sat like a man at a bedside, though—leaning forward, hands knotted, as though the ledger were a dying friend.

* * *

Ivo opened volume thirty-one and turned to the entry.

He watched the keeper's face as he read it aloud, because the face was part of the record too, in a way. Ivo had watched ten thousand faces meet their own names in these pages, and each one did something different. This one went very still, the way ice goes still before it cracks.

*"Entry 31,447. Petition filed by the Undercourt of Corvane, acting under the Provision of Necessary Dark. Subject: one soul, unencumbered, young, unbargained elsewhere. Consideration rendered to the broker Halvard Moss: restoration of title to the Ashgrave freehold, three acres riverfront, plus interest accrued across two generations of seizure. Soul received into the common fund and allocated—"*

"Allocated," Wren repeated. The word came out flat.

"To the sky," Ivo said. He closed the volume gently. "You've seen the night, of course. You stood under it. No stars over Corvane, none anywhere within the ward-line. Do you know why the stars went out, Keeper? Not metaphor. Mechanism. Your border-wards and our sky-work are cut from the same loom—the old compact, the one your oath sealed. When the wards fell, the sky needed new thread to hold the pattern, or everything unravels at once: the wards, the dark, the quiet the city has enjoyed for twelve years. Souls hold light better than anything else we have. One soul, well-woven, keeps a whole horizon dim." He folded his hands. "Hers was well-woven. Young souls usually are."

"You're telling me," Wren said slowly, "that her soul is holding up the dark."

"I'm telling you her soul is holding up *the peace*. The Wardens keep order in the streets, but the dark keeps order everywhere else. No lights means no gatherings. No gatherings means no riots. The merchants hate it and pay anyway, because the alternative to our arrangement is a city that remembers what it lost." Ivo permitted himself the smallest shrug. "Every system runs on fuel, Keeper. Yours ran on silence. Ours runs on this."

"And the broker. Moss. She paid him in land."

"The court pays brokers in exactly the currencies brokers value. Land, titles, immunity. The soul itself comes from somewhere further upstream." Ivo did not enjoy lying, precisely, but neither did he enjoy the truth entire; the truth entire was that Tam had signed nothing she understood, and that the court counted on exactly that. "The point is this: the transaction was lawful. Under our law, which is the only law beneath this mountain, the transaction is *binding*. Souls may be traded. They may be exchanged. They may not be stolen back. If you attempt theft, the alarm woven into your own old oath will sound, and the vaults will seal, and you will spend a very long time learning what the inside of a contract feels like."

Something moved behind the keeper's eyes—not fear. Calculation, maybe. Or the particular stubbornness Ivo had seen in petitioners who refused, against all evidence, to believe the arithmetic of the place.

"So that's the law," Wren said. "Souls can't be stolen back."

"They can be *exchanged*. Equivalent value, freely offered. That is the single mercy this court permits, and even the mercy has a fee." Ivo drew a second book from the drawer—a slimmer one, unmarked, its cover soft with handling. "But before we speak of exchange, the Bench has instructed me to make you an offer. I would ask you to hear all of it before you answer. Most don't. Most start shouting around the fourth sentence, and then I must begin again, and I confess my patience is not what the Bench believes it is."

Wren leaned back. "Say it."

"Your companions." Ivo laid the slim book open. Inside were sketches—precise, unlovely, done by ward-sight rather than any artist's hand. Serah Vail, her badge still visible at her collar in the image. Brannoc Haleth, huge even in miniature, his exile-brand a dark star on his throat. "A warden who abandoned her post. An exile under sentence of death in three jurisdictions. A keeper whose broken oath makes him, technically, the most legally interesting man in the kingdom. The court could take all three of you tonight. There would be no trial worth the name—your friend Serah would simply be returned to the Order, where her fate would be brief. Brannoc would go to the deep cells, where exiles wait out eternity. And you—"

"I'd stand trial."

"You would stand trial," Ivo agreed, "and the Bench would find you complicit in twelve years of sealed suffering, because your oath *is* the seal, Keeper. Everything your gate locked away—all the magic, all the exiled, all the desperate who died at the border because the crossing was shut—you swore the lock. The Bench will argue, correctly under our law, that every cruelty committed beyond your gate bears your signature." He let that settle. "Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the keeper chooses, freely, to serve the court that his oath served all along." Ivo slid a sheet of vellum across the desk. It was blank except for a single line of waiting script at the bottom, and above it, in plain language, the terms:

*The undersigned agrees to depart the mountain with his companions and report thereafter upon all movements of unlawful magic within the ward-line. In consideration: the matter of Tamlin Ashgrave shall be reviewed by the Bench at its discretion; the undersigned shall bear no trial; the companions named herein shall be released without charge.*

"Walk out," Ivo said quietly. "All of you. Tonight. No cells, no trial, no brands. And in exchange, you watch. You go back to your little shop on Copper Lane and you sell your luck-knots and you tell us what moves in the dark, the way a keeper always has. That is all the court asks. It asked it of you once before, and you said yes, and twelve years of quiet came of it. Say yes again. Keep the small peace. Go home."

He watched the man read it. Watched his thumb press white against the edge of the vellum. Watched him arrive, as they all arrived, at the clause buried in the middle like a stone in bread: *reviewed at its discretion.*

"There's no promise in here," Wren said. "'At its discretion.' That means she stays."

"It means the Bench will consider her case," Ivo said. "Which is more consideration than any soul in the queue below us has received in a century. Keeper, look at what you hold. You can walk out of this mountain with your people alive and free—something no petitioner down that corridor has managed in living memory—or you can stay, and fight a court that cannot lose, on ground where the law itself is against you, and the most likely outcome is that Tam sleeps in the vaults until the sun burns out and you stand trial beside her empty shell." He softened his voice, because the moment called for it, because mercy was only mercy if it sounded like mercy. "I have watched ten thousand people come through that door believing love was a legal argument. It is not. Love does not move the Bench. Signatures move the Bench. Sign the paper. Take her chances home with you, thin as they are. A thin chance beats a beautiful ruin."

For a long moment there was only the sound of the blue flame, and far off, the endless shuffle of the queue.

Then Wren looked up, and his eyes were dry and terribly clear, and Ivo felt, for the first time in four hundred and six years, the strange sensation of being *read* rather than reading.

"How many," Wren said, "have signed?"

Ivo hesitated. It was a small hesitation, half a heartbeat, but the keeper caught it the way a fisherman catches the twitch of a line.

"In four hundred years," Ivo said, "eleven."

"And how many of those eleven ever saw their person again?"

Ivo did not answer. He did not need to. The ledger answered for him; it answered for everyone. He had written the follow-up entries himself. *Petition lapsed. Petition lapsed. Petition withdrawn by death of petitioner. Petition lapsed.*

"That's what I thought," Wren said.

He stood. He did not touch the pen.

"Thank you for showing me the ledger," he said. "I needed to know it was real—that she's really in there, in your numbers. I needed to know the price." He was moving toward the door now, and the grey bailiffs shifted uncertainly, awaiting instruction that Ivo had not yet given. "Here's what your discretion can do with your review. Tell the Bench this: I'm not signing. And I'm not stealing her back, either, since that's against your law. So think hard about what that leaves, magistrate, because I've spent twelve years learning what oaths cost, and I've got one left to break."

He stopped at the door. Turned halfway.

"One question. The broker—Moss. Where is he now?"

Ivo consulted nothing; he remembered. "Dead. Three nights past. Heart-failure, according to the record." According to the record, yes. "Why?"

"Because dead men don't collect on land grants," Wren said, "and a girl who bargains away her soul doesn't walk out of a room where the broker's been silenced. Somebody cleaned up after the sale. Somebody in this court decided loose ends were cheaper than witnesses." He pushed the door open. Down the corridor, the queue's murmur rose like weather. "Your law is binding, you said. Fine. But laws built on murdered brokers have seams, magistrate. I'm going to find the seam."

The door closed behind him.

* * *

Ivo sat alone for a long while, the lamp burning its cold blue, the ledger open to entry 31,447.

Slowly, he took up his pen. He dipped it in the breath-dark ink, and beneath the record of the sale, in the margin where annotations lived, he wrote the day's entry in his small precise hand:

*The keeper declined terms. Unremarkable. Eleven of eleven thousand decline, eventually.*

He paused. The pen hovered.

*Note: he asked about the broker. None have asked about the broker.*

Below that, after another pause, smaller, almost invisible, squeezed against the margin's edge as though hoping not to be read:

*Note to self: check the disposition of the Moss estate. Check who signed the silencing order. Check whether the Bench has begun cleaning up before the reckoning instead of after.*

Ivo blotted the page. Four hundred and six years, and he had never once written a note to himself in the ledger. Notes were how clerks began to become something other than clerks. He knew the histories. He knew what happened to magistrates who started noticing seams.

He closed the book, and told himself the slight tremor in his hand was only the hour, and rang the bell for the bailiffs to take the keeper to his cell—because of course the man would not simply be allowed to walk to the vaults, whatever he believed he had negotiated. The Bench's mercy ended at the door of this office.

And down in the corridor, faint through the stone, the queue went on shuffling, and someone began, very softly, to sing a song about the stars coming back—which was treason, under the Provision of Necessary Dark, and no one stopped her, and Ivo listened until the guards made her stop.
