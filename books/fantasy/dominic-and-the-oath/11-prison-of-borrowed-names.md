## 11. Prison of Borrowed Names

There was a girl once who had a name.

Tam knew this the way you know the shape of a room with your eyes closed—not by seeing it, but by the way the dark fit against her skin. She had a name. It had been warm in her mouth, worn smooth like a river stone from saying it to herself on bad nights. *Tam.* She turned the sound over now and it came up blank on one side, a coin rubbed faceless.

She sat in a niche carved into grey stone, because sitting was what her body remembered how to do. The vault around her went on forever in every direction—shelves cut into the rock, and on each shelf a vessel: glass, clay, iron boxes, jars of smoked crystal, things that might have been urns or might have been lanterns or might have been skulls if you looked too long and were foolish enough to look at all. Each one held a soft light. None of them flickered. The light in this place did not know how to flicker; it had never been alive enough for that.

*You are vessel number eleven thousand four hundred and six,* said the voice that lived behind her eyes. It had been saying things like that since they brought her down. It did not sound cruel. That was the worst part. It sounded like a clerk reading a shipping manifest. *Your contents have been assessed. Your contents are sufficient.*

"My name," Tam said. Her voice came out rusty. How long since she'd used it? Days. Weeks. Time down here moved like syrup poured uphill.

*Irrelevant,* said the voice.

"No," she said, and clung to the word because it was the only thing she owned. "No. I had one. I *had* one."

The vessels around her pulsed faintly, each holding its little stolen dawn, and Tam understood—with a knowledge that lived below thought, below memory, somewhere in the marrow—that she was looking at people. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every jar a someone. Every shelf a street's worth of lives, decanted, labeled, shelved.

And she was going to be one of them.

Not yet. Not quite yet. They had taken pieces of her already—the broker's hooded men with their cold hands and their ledger-scented breath—but the last of her, the bright core of her, was still walking around inside her chest, waiting to be poured out when the court needed fuel for its starless sky. She could feel it there. A coal. A seed. Hers.

*Hold still,* said the voice, bored as rain. *Assessment continues.*

"I'm not still," Tam told it. "I've never been still in my life."

But she couldn't remember what she'd been instead. That was the trick of it, the cruelty folded into the mercy: they hadn't erased everything. If they'd taken it all she would have been nothing, an empty jar already, no pain in it at all. Instead they'd left her the shape of a life without any of the life. She knew she had laughed before. She could feel the ghost of laughter in her ribs like an old break aching before weather. She knew she had loved something—or someone—and the knowing was a hand pressed flat against a door that opened onto smoke.

Whoever it was, they had made her laugh until her sides hurt. She was almost sure of that. The ache said so.

*Assessment,* said the voice. *Residual attachments detected. Recommend further pruning.*

"Leave it," Tam whispered, and put both hands over her chest as if she could shield the coal with her palms. "Leave me *that.*"

The voice did not answer, which meant it was considering, which meant it could be argued with, which meant—somewhere under all the grey—a girl who bargained with brokers over stolen land was still in there, still fighting over price. Tam held onto that too.

* * *

They came for her between one endless hour and the next. Two figures in robes the color of wet slate, faces smooth as uncarved wax. They did not touch her. They simply stood at the mouth of her niche, and the light bent toward them, and the voice behind her eyes said, *Walk,* and her legs walked.

Through the shelves, past rows upon rows upon rows. Tam counted as they went, because counting was a rope she could hold. Eleven thousand four hundred and six was her number but the shelves kept going past it, past twenty thousand, past numbers with more digits than she had patience for. Somewhere in these ranks was a sister she couldn't picture. Somewhere was a mother whose voice she'd lost before the hooded men ever came. Grief kept surfacing in her like bubbles in tar—she felt the feeling without the reason for it, wept twice without knowing what for, and wiped her face with hands that felt borrowed.

They stopped at last in a long gallery where the ceiling rose into darkness and the vessels grew larger, grander, older. Here the lights were not small and stubborn but vast and dim, like suns seen through fog. The slate-robed figures halted. One gestured, a movement like a page turning.

And there—at the end of the gallery, in a cage of black glass that drank every ray that touched it—was a boy.

He was maybe seventeen, gaunt as a winter branch, and he was pressing his palm flat against the inside of the glass the way Tam had pressed hers against her own chest. He wasn't lit like the others. His light was being siphoned away through channels cut into the floor, thin silver threads running off into the dark, feeding something far above.

Tam stopped. Her feet stopped without her telling them to.

*I know him,* said the hollow place where her memories used to live. And then, contradicting itself in a way that felt like a crack spreading through glass: *I don't know him.*

She stared. He stared back. And slowly, horribly, the boy's face changed—recognition dawning in him like sunrise in a country that had forgotten the sun. His mouth formed a word. Through the glass, muffled, warped, she heard it:

"*Tam?*"

Her heart slammed. The coal in her chest flared hot and painful and glad, all at once, and she hated it, because how could she be glad about a stranger?

"That's not my name," she said.

The boy's face broke. Not dramatically, not all at once—it broke the way ice breaks on a pond in spring, one hairline fracture after another, quiet and total. "It is," he said. "Tam. Tam Vess. You stole apples from the market on Copper Lane because you said paying full price was a personality flaw. You burned the porridge every single morning and blamed the stove. You—" His voice cracked clean through. "You're my apprentice. I'm Wren. I came to get you."

Every word landed on her like snow falling on a locked house. Some of it melted through the cracks. Most of it didn't.

"I don't—" Tam's throat worked. "I don't know you."

"You do." He pressed closer to the glass. Up close she could see he was wrecked—cut knuckles, a bruise going yellow along his jaw, eyes rimmed red with more than the vault's dust. "You do, you do, you made me a luck-knot when I was scared of the dark and you were nine years old and you told me it was crooked so I wouldn't feel bad about my knot-work. Tam. Please."

Somewhere very deep, a door strained against its hinges. Something in her wanted to say *yes, that's mine, give it back*—the luck-knot, the stove, the crooked stitches, all of it, the whole small warm inventory of a life. But reaching for it was like grabbing at fish in dark water. Her hands closed on nothing. Worse than nothing: on the *absence* shaped exactly like him, a Wren-shaped hole, and she understood suddenly that the hole was proof. They had cut him out of her, and the wound remembered his outline even if she didn't.

"They took it," she heard herself say. "Whatever you're looking for in my face—they took it first."

"I know." His forehead came to rest against the glass. From this near, she could see his breath fogging it, the only moving thing in all that stillness. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I was slow. I'm sorry I forbade you—I should have helped you find another way instead of just slamming doors. This is my fault. All twelve years of hiding, and it still found us, and I—"

"Twelve years?" Something snagged. "I haven't known you twelve years. Have I?"

He went very still. Then, carefully, gently, the way you speak to someone standing on ice: "How much do you remember? Of anything?"

Tam reached inward, cautiously, the way you probe a tooth. Market stalls. A city built on hills, copper roofs going green. Rain. The smell of solder and herbs. A shop with a crooked sign. A man's hands tying knots—no. No face. No name. Just hands, patient hands, guiding hers.

"A shop," she said slowly. "Charm-seller's. There was someone who taught me. I can't see him. I can't—" Frustration scalded her eyes. "Why can't I see him? Why can't I see *anything* whole?"

"Because they fed your soul to the sky," Wren said, and his voice had gone flat and terrible, a blade laid on a table. "Because the court beneath this mountain bought you, and memory comes out with the soul when they pour. Because I'm going to burn this place down to its foundation stones and take every piece of you back."

"That's not possible," said one of the slate-robed figures, in a voice like a ledger closing. "Souls may be traded. Never reclaimed. It is the law of the Undercourt."

Wren looked at the figure, and Tam watched something pass over his face—fear, yes, but underneath it a hard bright thing she recognized without remembering why she recognized it. Determination. Stubbornness. *His*, some buried grain of her insisted. *That's his. I know that look. I've rolled my eyes at that look a thousand times.*

A thousand times. She held onto that number like a rope.

"Then your law has a flaw in it," Wren said quietly. "Everything does. Even mountains."

* * *

They took him away before he could say anything else—two more figures materializing out of the dark, his name turning to shouts turning to silence down the gallery—and Tam was returned to her niche, to her shelf, to her number.

She sat in the grey and pressed her hands to the coal in her chest and did the only arithmetic left to her.

One stranger with patient hands who knew her laugh. One name, *Tam*, which fit her mouth better than any word had since the hoods came down. One promise of fire, absurd and impossible and warm.

They had left her the wound. The wound was shaped like him. Which meant that somewhere—maybe not in her, maybe in whatever vault they kept the poured-out parts of girls who bargained with brokers—there was still a self that loved this Wren, whole and specific and loud, and it could be gotten back. Things that exist can be found. That was the first law of thieves, and Tam had been a thief of small things her whole remembered life.

*I'll learn you again,* she promised the Wren-shaped hole, in the dark, to nobody. *Even if I have to start from the beginning. Even if the beginning is all we get. You came down here for me. So I'll walk out of here beside you, and you can tell me the apple story, and the porridge story, and every story I lost, and I'll steal them all back the way I steal everything—*

The voice behind her eyes stirred.

*Attention,* it said. *Anomaly in Gallery Nine. Unauthorized access. Vault protocols escalating.*

Far away, through miles of stone, came a sound Tam felt rather than heard—a boom, deep and rhythmic, like a giant knocking on a door. Then shouting. Then a grinding roar of stone that set every vessel on her shelf trembling, ten thousand small lights shivering at once like startled birds.

And in the trembling, for half a heartbeat, every jar on her shelf flared bright together—and Tam saw, reflected across all those curved surfaces, a hundred distorted copies of herself, and in one of them, just one, the girl looking back was *smiling*. Crooked. Defiant. Familiar.

Then the light settled, and the girl was gone, and the booming grew louder, and somewhere above them a door that should never open was opening.

Tam stood up.

*Remain seated,* said the voice.

"No," said Tam, and walked toward the sound.
