## 13. The Vault of Unlit Things

The Long Reckoning began with bells.

Not the bells Wren knew from Corvane—market bells, alarm bells, the cracked bell of the fish-temple that tolled only for drowned men. These came from beneath the stone itself, deep and patient, like a heart that had been counting for a thousand years and had finally lost interest in what it counted. The sound moved through the floor of the Undercourt's antechamber and up through the soles of Wren's boots and settled somewhere behind his ribs, where his oath used to live.

"They empty the benches," Serah murmured beside him. She stood in the shadow of a pillar carved to look like a column of chained petitioners—because of course they would carve that here, of course they would make their cruelty into decoration. "Every clerk, every magistrate, every pale-robed son of a ledger. They go down into the Reckoning Hall and count what they own."

"And while they count?"

"While they count," Brannoc said, "nobody is watching the vaults."

He filled the alcove the way he filled every space, shoulders brushing stone on either side, his exiled healer's hands—hands that had mended soldiers on battlefields until the crown decided mending was treason—folded gently as a man waiting in a queue. Which, Wren supposed, they all were. Everyone under the mountain was in a queue of one kind or another.

The plan was simple, and Wren distrusted it for exactly that reason. During the Long Reckoning, when the entire court descended to tally its holdings, the vaults would be held by a skeleton watch—two wardens at the iron door, no more, because the court had never imagined anyone would be foolish enough to come down here on the one night it emptied. Brannoc would hold the door once they were through. Ivo's stolen key-ring would get them past the locks. Serah's old badge, still smelling faintly of the oaths she'd burned off it, would get them past the wardens' suspicion long enough for Wren to do the rest.

And Wren would do the rest.

That was the part he kept turning over like a charm he couldn't quite read. Twelve years of selling luck-knots and fever-salves and harmless little lies stitched in copper wire, and now the whole weight of it—the whole hidden architecture of who he'd been before he made himself small—was supposed to come out of hiding and pick a lock woven from living law.

"You're doing the thing," Tam had said to him once, back on Copper Lane, when he caught himself staring at nothing with his lips moving. "The thinking-too-hard thing. You get a crease right there." She'd poked the spot between his brows. He could still feel it. He was holding on to things like that—small, useless, warm—because under the mountain everything else felt like it was being weighed against him.

*She doesn't remember you.* Ivo had said it almost kindly, which was somehow worse. *We took the memory along with the soul. It's cleaner that way. A vessel without longing holds its light better.*

Wren flexed his hands. Somewhere below them, behind iron and law older than the mountain's name, Tam's soul sat in a vessel shaped like whatever the clerks had decided a soul should look like—a jar, a lantern, a stoppered phial—and Tam herself walked these halls hollow-eyed and polite, answering to a number.

"Positions," Serah breathed.

* * *

They went down through the service galleries, where the walls wept mineral water and the carvings gave way to bare stone scored with tallies—centuries of petitioners scratching marks for every year they'd waited for a hearing that never came. Wren counted some of them without meaning to. Four hundred. Six hundred. One wall, in a careful, educated hand, stopped abruptly at nine hundred and eleven, and beneath it someone had written *enough*.

Ivo's key-ring was heavy in Wren's pocket, each key labeled in the clerk-magistrate's small precise script. *Vault of Unlit Things. Vault of Forfeited Names. Vault of Interest Accrued.* That last one made Wren's skin crawl. Even grief, down here, accrued interest.

The iron door of the Unlit Things vault stood at the end of a gallery lit by lamps that burned without flickering—court-light, fed on something Wren didn't want to name. Two wardens flanked it, spears upright, faces hidden behind visors worked into expressions of perfect bureaucratic neutrality.

Serah stepped out first, badge held high, spine straight in the way that only career soldiers and liars can manage.

"Warden Vail. Ninth Bench audit. The court requires verification of holdings during the Reckoning. Open the door."

The left warden tilted his head. "Verification wasn't scheduled for this vault."

"Then your schedule's wrong," Serah said, with just enough boredom. "Take it up with the Bench after. Or open the door now and spend your night drinking instead of writing reports." She let a pause stretch. "Your choice. But the Reckoning won't wait, and neither will I."

Wren, watching from the gallery's mouth, felt something twist in his chest that wasn't quite admiration and wasn't quite shame. She wore the lie like a uniform because she'd worn true ones so long they fit the same.

The wardens looked at each other. Looked at her badge. And the door groaned open on hinges that sounded like a confession dragged out syllable by syllable.

Brannoc moved before the gap was wide enough for a man. It shouldn't have worked—he was the least subtle object in the world—but he came through the lamplight like weather, and the two wardens went down against opposite walls with a sound like dropped furniture, and then his great hands were gentle again, checking pulse-points, easing heads so they wouldn't crack stone.

"Asleep," he rumbled. "An hour, maybe two. The door stays shut behind you and my shoulder stays against it. Go."

Wren paused at the threshold. Below, faint through miles of rock, the bells of the Long Reckoning went on counting.

"Brannoc. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't." The giant smiled, and there was something in it of the man who'd knelt in the mud of the outlaw road and asked Wren, politely, desperately, to mend a curse three kingdoms' worth of healers had refused to touch. "You fixed what nobody else could fix. Do it again."

* * *

Inside, the vault was cold in a way cold wasn't supposed to work.

It wasn't temperature. It was absence—the specific, directional absence of something that should have been there, the way a room feels wrong after a body has been carried out of it. Rows upon rows of shelves climbed into darkness, and on the shelves, vessels. Thousands of them. Clay jars sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Lanterns with their wicks unlit but glowing faintly anyway, from inside, from underneath the glass. Phials no bigger than a finger. Birdcages with the doors soldered shut. A child's shoe, laced tight, humming very softly.

Wren stopped breathing for a moment.

*Unlit things.* He'd thought it meant dark. Now he understood: it meant *waiting to be lit*. Every vessel here was fuel, banked and stored, the court's hoarded treasury of souls bought from brokers and widows and desperate fathers, all of it stacked in the dark like cordwood, waiting to be spent keeping the sky starless and the Benches fat.

"There are so many," Serah whispered. She'd insisted on coming the first stretch—her sister's soul was in these vaults somewhere too, though not this wing; Wren had seen her memorize the gallery signs with an expression he recognized from mirrors. "Gods above. There are *so many*."

"Not gods," Wren said quietly. "Clerks."

He found the index at the gallery's head: a lectern with a ledger chained to it, entries in Ivo's tidy hand even here, even for this. He ran his finger down columns of numbers and names—or rather numbers where names should be, because the court recorded souls the way merchants record grain. *Lot 4471. Acquired via Broker Hesh, Copper Lane. Consideration: land title, disputed. Grade: young, unspoiled. Storage: Tier Nine.*

Tier Nine. He climbed. The stairs spiraled up through the shelving, and with every tier the vessels grew quieter—not louder, *quieter*, as though the deeper holdings had been drained closer to nothing. On Tier Seven he passed a lantern and heard, or imagined he heard, a woman singing two notes of a lullaby over and over, never reaching the third.

On Tier Nine, the air changed.

It took him three steps to understand why, and when he did, he had to put his hand against a shelf to stay standing. The vessels here didn't hum or sing or glow. They *waited*. And one of them waited in a way that reached through twelve years and a broken oath and found the exact bruised place where Tam lived in him.

She stood on a shelf at eye level, if you could call it standing: a clay jar, small enough to hold with two hands, sealed with black wax stamped with the court's seal—an open hand closing. And through the clay, faint as warmth through a winter wall, came something Wren had spent twelve years pretending he didn't need: the particular stubborn brightness of her. Not memory. Memory was gone; Ivo had been thorough. This was older than memory. This was the thing that had made a starving kid on Copper Lane look at a charm-seller's crooked shop and decide, unilaterally, that she lived there now.

*Tam.*

His hand rose toward the jar.

And the oath woke up.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The oath was supposed to be dead—he'd felt it snap the night the stars vanished, felt it tear loose in his chest like a rope parting under load. But a snapped rope leaves scars, and scars remember, and what rose in him now was not the oath itself but its *shadow*: the shape of the prohibition burned into the deepest layer of him during that night at the border gate twelve years ago, when he'd sworn himself keeper and sealed the magic beyond the wall and his old name along with it.

*What is sealed shall not be taken. What is owed shall not be reclaimed by force. The keeper does not steal from the ledger of heaven.*

He'd sworn those words thinking they bound him to the border. He'd never imagined they'd been written into the same universal grammar the Undercourt wrote its contracts in. He'd never imagined that when the court drafted its purchase of Tam's soul—*acquired, owed, entered in the ledger*—his own ancient vow would stand ready, like a loyal hound, to defend any ledger anywhere against any hand that reached for it.

Light kindled along his forearm. Old light, gate-light, the white-gold radiance of the wards he'd tended for twelve years, crawling out of his skin in lines and sigils he hadn't spoken since the night he swore them. The whole tier brightened. Down the spiral stairs, alarms began—not bells, something worse: a voice, vast and dry as parchment, reciting.

*Intrusion. Breach of covenant. The keeper reaches for the ledger. The keeper reaches for the ledger. Let the record show—*

"No," Wren said. His voice cracked in the middle. "No, no, I *broke* it, I broke the oath, it's dead, it's—"

But his hand was still rising toward the jar, drawn like a compass needle, and the light along his arm was hardening into a bar between his fingers and the clay, a gate-bar, a border, the last reflex of a man who had spent half his life saying *no* to magic on the other side of a line. His own body was the wall now. His own vow was the lock.

Twelve years of hiding, and the hiding had grown into him like roots into a foundation. He could tear the building down. He couldn't tear out the roots.

*The keeper does not steal from the ledger of heaven.*

"I'm not stealing," he snarled at the voice, at the light, at himself. "She's not yours. Nothing here is *yours*—you bought grief from people who couldn't read your contracts, you've been running a kingdom on other people's—"

The voice recited on, patient, terrible, and Wren understood with a clarity that felt like falling: arguing wouldn't help. Force wouldn't help. Every instinct he had—the smuggler's cleverness, the keeper's sealing power, the charm-seller's sleight of hand—all of it belonged to the categories the oath knew how to fight. Take. Steal. Break. Seal.

The court had built its vaults knowing that someday a keeper might come. Maybe it had built them *hoping*.

Down below, iron boomed. Once. Twice. Brannoc's voice rolled up the stairwell, wordless, enormous, the sound of a door being held by a mountain pretending to be a man—and then a third boom that wasn't Brannoc, that came from *outside* the door, rhythmic, deliberate, a battering ram finding its pace.

Serah appeared on the stairs two at a time, sword drawn, face grey in the oath-light. "Reckoning's ended early—they know. Wren, we have minutes, maybe less. Tell me you have her."

He looked at the jar. At the bar of his own light holding his hand a finger's width from the clay. Close enough to feel her warmth. Close enough to ache.

Hope ambushed. Betrayed from within. And the battering ram coming up the dark.

"I—" Wren said. "I don't know yet."
