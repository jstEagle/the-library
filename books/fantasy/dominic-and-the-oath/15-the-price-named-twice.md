## 15. The Price Named Twice

They gave him a cell with a view.

That was the first cruelty of it—Wren understood that within an hour of being escorted there by two bailiffs whose faces were smooth as river stones. The chamber sat high in the mountain's throat, one wall open to the great descending shaft where the Undercourt's petitioners queued in their thousands, a river of the patient damned winding down into lamplight and murmur. From here he could see the whole machinery of grief: the benches, the clerks, the ledgers stacked like headstones.

*They want me comfortable,* he thought. *Comfort is what they sell.*

The second cruelty was the bed. It was soft. He hadn't slept on anything soft in twelve years; his cot above the shop had been stuffed with wood-shavings because Tam liked to say it smelled like honesty. He lay down on the court's kindness and stared at the ceiling, which was carved with stars—the old constellations, every one of them, rendered in pale stone that seemed to drink light rather than give it. A sky's worth of stars, hoarded underground while the real ones went dark over Corvane.

Somewhere below, in the vaults of unlit things, Tam's soul sat in its vessel like a candle in a locked room.

Wren pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw sparks.

*Think,* he told himself. *You've thought your way out of worse.*

But he hadn't, really. He'd hidden his way out of worse. That was the difference, and the mountain knew it, and it had built this whole beautiful trap around the shape of a man who survived by keeping small.

* * *

Ivo came at what might have been morning. There was no way to tell time down here except by the clerk himself, who arrived with the punctuality of a man who had been counting hours for centuries and could not stop.

He carried no ledger today. Just a single sheet of vellum and a pen, and he set them on the little table between the bed and the window as if laying out instruments for surgery.

"You've been awake all night," Ivo said. Not a question. His voice had the dry patience of paper.

"I've been thinking."

"That's what they all say." He sat, folding his hands. "The ones who scream, we sedate. The ones who think—" he gestured faintly at the carved ceiling, "—we let them think. It makes the eventual answer easier for everyone."

"Whose answer?"

"The court's. Yours. They're usually the same thing by the end." Ivo uncapped the ink bottle. "You argued well yesterday. Before the Pale Bench. Justice outside the law is still justice—I'll grant you, it was well said. The Bench granted it too. That's why you're in this room instead of a deeper one."

"And?"

"And so the court has done what courts do when argument fails them." Ivo slid the vellum across. "It has made your justice legal."

Wren didn't touch the page. He read it from where he sat, the way he'd once read charm-contracts across a counter, scanning for the hook hidden in the generous clause. The script was old court-hand, but the terms were simple enough to be obscene:

*One soul, freely given, in exchange for one soul held. The vessel of Tamrin Ashe shall be released unbroken upon receipt. No further claim shall attach to the giver or his companions. All debts dissolved. All hearings concluded.*

"They're offering her back," Wren said slowly. "For a price."

"They're offering her back *legally*. There is a difference, and you of all people should feel it." Ivo's eyes were kind in the way that old documents are kind—they record everything and forgive nothing. "A theft can be pursued forever. An exchange cannot. If you take her by force, the court's claim survives you, survives her, survives every star you relight. Some future clerk finds the ledger entry, and the debt reattaches, and she is collected again. Perhaps not tomorrow. Perhaps in thirty years, when she has a life worth taking."

"And if I trade?"

"Then the entry closes. Ink dries. The court's law is satisfied, and no power in this world or under it can reopen it." Ivo turned the pen toward him, handle first. "The court requires a soul of equivalent weight. Hers was taken as payment for the dark sky—a keeper's-weight payment, they judged, though she was no keeper. So the exchange must be a keeper's-weight soul."

The words landed in Wren's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard the splash a long time later.

"There's only one of those," he said.

"I believe," said Ivo, "that is why they wrote it this way."

* * *

After the clerk left, Wren stood at the open wall for a long time, watching the queue of petitioners wind downward. Faces turned up toward him occasionally—hopeful, hollow, hungry. They saw a man standing in a high place and wondered if he was someone who could help them. Twelve years ago he would have shrunk from their gaze. Now he let them look, because looking away had never saved anyone he loved.

*A soul for a soul.*

It was almost funny. Almost clean. One life weighed against another, and the scales tipped whichever way the heart leaned, and his heart had never been a fair judge. He thought of Tam at nine years old, standing in his doorway with rain in her hair and her family's deed burned to ash in her fist, asking not for charity but for work—*I can sweep, I can grind, I can learn, don't send me back out there.* He thought of her at fifteen, arguing with him about the ethics of luck-knots. At seventeen, laughing at him across the counter with flour on her nose from grinding bone-meal.

Twelve years of found family, weighed against however many years he had left in him.

There was no scale in any world where that balanced the other direction.

And yet—

And yet the lie was already forming in his mouth, and he hated himself for how easily it came. Because the plan that assembled itself in his mind, piece by piece, with the cold competence of a man who had spent twelve years hiding things, required deception. Not of the court. The court expected deception; it priced it into every contract.

His companions. Serah, who had burned her badge for a sister she might now never buy back. Brannoc, who had held a vault door against the court's own wardens and paid for it in blood. They would stand in front of him and refuse. He knew it the way he knew the weight of his own hands. Serah would call it madness and then offer herself instead—her soul was lighter, yes, but she'd argue some technicality, some loophole, and the court would smile and take the lesser price and keep the difference, because that was what courts did. And Brannoc—Brannoc would simply pick Wren up and carry him out of the mountain, gentle as moving furniture, and the moment would pass and Tam would rot in her vessel for another century while they regrouped.

No. This was the one door, and it opened once, and he would walk through it alone.

*The loner's arithmetic,* whispered something in him that sounded like Tam. *Always rounding everyone else down to zero.*

He told the whisper to be quiet. It sounded like her. That was the worst part—it sounded exactly like her, and she wasn't here to say it, and maybe that was why he could lie so easily. The living would try to stop him. The memory couldn't.

* * *

Serah found him at midday—or what passed for it. She came up the shaft-stairs two at a time, armor gone, wearing a plain traveler's coat, her hair tied back with the rough efficiency of a woman who had stopped performing soldierhood. She looked at the vellum on the table, then at Wren, and her jaw tightened.

"They offered you the trade."

"They offered everyone the trade," Wren said. "It's how the machine works. You know that better than I do—you served it."

"Don't." She said it quietly, without heat. "Don't do that thing where you push us away by making us the enemy. I've watched you do it since the road. You did it to Brannoc in the ferry town, you did it to me at the river, and it worked both times because we let it. I'm not letting it this time."

Wren picked up the contract and held it out to her. Let her read it. Better she learned the terms from the page than guessed them from his face—he'd had twelve years of practice keeping his face shut, and he needed every hour of it now.

She read fast, the way soldiers read orders. Her eyes stopped at the same line his had. *Equivalent weight.* He watched her arrive at the meaning, watched her go pale beneath the road-tan, watched her look up at him with something terrible dawning.

"No," she said.

"It's my choice."

"It's a *trap*, Wren. Don't you see what they've done? They've written a contract only you can sign, and they've dressed it as mercy, and you're going to walk in there grateful." She flung the vellum down. "This is what they did to me for six years. They held my sister and let me earn toward her, coin by coin, year by year, always one payment short. The debt was designed never to close. Why would this one be different? Why would *your* sacrifice be the one that finally satisfies them?"

"Because Ivo explained the law. Exchange closes the entry. Theft doesn't."

"Ivo." She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Ivo, who has served the Pale Bench since before your grandmother was born. You trust the clerk's reading of the court's own rules?"

"I trust that he hates them."

Something flickered across her face at that—she'd seen it too, whatever moved behind Ivo's dry eyes when he touched those ledger pages. But she shook it off. "Even if it's true. Even if the trade works exactly as written. You'd be giving them everything. Your magic, your years, your name—what's left of it after the river took your face. You'd be giving them the man who argued justice in front of the Pale Bench and made them flinch. Do you think they want your soul because it weighs much? They want it because it *matters*, and souls that matter are the only currency a dying court can still spend."

Wren turned to the window. Below, the queue shifted and murmured like the sea.

"If you have a better way to get her out," he said, "say it."

Silence. He hated how much relief he felt in it—and how much shame.

"We get Brannoc," Serah said finally. "We get the petitioners. We burn the Long Reckoning wide open and we take her vessel in the chaos, and we run, and we spend the rest of our lives outrunning the claim together. That's the better way. It's worse odds and it keeps everyone breathing."

"Together," Wren repeated. The word sat strangely in his mouth, like a charm in a language he'd half forgotten. "Serah. When the wards fell and the city burned, where was I? Hiding in a cellar on Copper Lane while Wardens torched shops I could have shielded. When Tam was taken, where was I? Following the trail a day behind. My whole life is a list of doors I got to too late because I insisted on going through them alone. I'm not doing it again."

"You're not doing it alone *now*. That's the entire point of—"

"You don't understand." He made his voice gentle, and gentleness was the deepest lie of all, because it was true feeling bent into a weapon. "If you and Brannoc are bound into this contract—if your names are anywhere near that altar—the court will find a way to weigh you in. It always does. The only safe distance is distance itself. Stay away from the Reckoning. Take Brannoc and go back up the shaft. Promise me."

"I don't make promises to men who are lying to me."

"I'm not lying."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he bore it, bore her eyes the way he'd borne the petitioner's gazes, and inside he recited the truth like a warding-charm: *I am lying about everything that matters, and it is the most loving thing I have ever done.*

"Brannoc won't leave," she said at last. "Whatever you tell him."

"Then I'll tell him something else."

* * *

Brannoc came at dusk, filling the doorway the way weather fills a valley. Someone had bandaged his arm—Serah's work, neat as drill-field stitching—and he carried a chipped clay cup of the court's gray tea in one enormous hand, as if the cell were a tavern and they were old friends, which after the road and the river and the vault door, perhaps they were.

"Ivo talks to me too," Brannoc rumbled, settling onto the floor because the chair would not hold him. "Thinks I don't notice. Talks to everyone, that one. Court's full of people talking instead of doing." He drank. "So. What's the plan?"

Wren had prepared the lie on three levels, the way he built charms: surface, structure, and secret heart. The surface was simple. The structure was sound. The secret heart was the part that would damn him.

"The trade happens at the Long Reckoning," he said. "Tomorrow night, when the whole court's attention turns inward for the audit. That's when the vaults are thinnest-guarded—that's when we move. Not for the trade. For Tam's vessel. While the Bench is busy weighing its own books, we take her and run."

Brannoc nodded slowly. "And the contract?" He nodded at the vellum.

"A decoy. I signed a copy this afternoon—well, I let them think I was considering it. It keeps Ivo bringing me information. It keeps the court watching me instead of watching the stairs." He met the giant's eyes. "I need you at the lower gate, Brannoc. The petitioners' entrance. When the Reckoning opens, the queue surges—centuries of them, all demanding their hearings at once. Hold that gate open long enough for the crowd to flood the halls. Chaos is our smoke. Serah takes Tam through the upper galleries. I draw the wardens."

Every word of it was true except the last one, and the last one was truest of all, just not in the direction he pointed.

Brannoc studied him for a long time. The big man had a way of looking at people as if reading the grain of them, checking for rot. Wren had watched him do it to strangers on the outlaw road, deciding whether they were kin or threat. Now the grain-reading settled on him and stayed.

"You sleep," Brannoc said finally. "Man who lies needs his sleep. Lies are heavy."

"I'm not—"

"Lies are heavy," Brannoc repeated, gently, and rose, and ducked out, and left Wren alone with the carved stars and the soft bed and the taste of ash.

* * *

Night, or the court's version of it. Wren sat on the floor with the contract spread before him and practiced.

Not signing. Preparing. There was a difference, and the difference was everything, and he had learned it long ago at a border gate under a sky that still had stars. An oath was not ink. An oath was the shape of a soul agreeing to a shape. The court's contract wanted his name—his fixed, legible, weighable name—and the river had already taken the oldest version of it, the boy he'd been before *keeper*, before Corvane, before twelve years of shut doors. What remained was thinner. A man made mostly of other people: Tam's laughter, Serah's stubbornness, Brannoc's terrible gentleness, even Ivo's dry sorrow.

*Let them weigh that,* he thought. *Let their scales choke on it.*

He took out the things he carried. A fisherman's luck-knot, frayed, kept as a reminder that small magics fed people. A splinter of the border gate's oak, warm even now. Tam's first attempt at a charm—a crooked tin star she'd hammered at age ten, that did nothing at all except exist, badly, beautifully.

He laid them in a row. Then he tore a strip from the contract's margin—Ivo would count the loss and understand—and wrote on it in the small tight hand of a man used to hiding:

*If I don't come back, it was mine to give. Don't follow. Don't grieve long. Open the shop anyway. Hang something in the window for me.*

He addressed it to no one, because it was to all of them, and because writing Tam's name would have broken the careful dam he'd built, and he could not afford to break it yet. Tomorrow night, at the altar, with the Pale Bench watching and the scales waiting, he would need every dry inch of himself.

He folded the note into the tin star and set it where Serah would find it, and lay back on the soft bed, and did not sleep.

Below him, the queue murmured on, patient as stone. Somewhere in the dark of the vaults, a vessel held a girl who didn't remember him, and Wren found, examining the thought honestly for the first time, that it didn't frighten him. She would wake into a world with stars again. She would meet a stranger named Wren Galloway, and maybe he'd sell her a luck-knot, and maybe she'd haggle, and maybe—maybe that was enough. Maybe love didn't need to be remembered. Only given.

*One more lie,* he told himself, staring at the stone stars that drank the light. *The kindest one. Then it's done.*

Far below, deep in the mountain's heart, a bell began to toll—slow, vast, iron-dark. Once. Twice. Three times.

The Long Reckoning had begun.
