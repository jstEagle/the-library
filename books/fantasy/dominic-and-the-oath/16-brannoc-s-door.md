## 16. Brannoc's Door

The door was heavier than a dead king's regret.

Brannoc had held doors before—gates at Vellin Ford, the breach at Marrow Hill, the infirmary door the night the plague came and he'd stood with his back against it while soldiers who were not yet exiles carried their dying comrades past him into clean air. He knew doors. A door was just a promise you made with your shoulders.

This one promised to eat him.

It was bronze and bone-white, taller than three men stacked, and it breathed. Not wind—the mountain itself inhaled through the seams, slow and patient, like something that had waited centuries for someone foolish enough to stand in its mouth. The vault corridor behind him rang with the alarm Wren's touch had triggered: a sound like a thousand small bells being strangled one by one.

*Go,* Brannoc had told them. *I'll hold it.*

And they'd gone. The little charm-seller with his burned-out oath and his terrible, stubborn love. The warden woman with her sister's name folded up inside her chest like a letter she couldn't bear to read. And Tam, hollow-eyed, walking because Wren asked her to walk, remembering nothing of the man who had crossed half a country to unmake her theft.

Brannoc pressed both palms flat against the bronze and set his feet.

The bells stopped. Silence pooled. Then the corridor on the far side of the door filled with footsteps—many, unhurried, the tread of things that had never once needed to run. The court's wardens coming to see what had disturbed their ledger.

The first blow hit the door like a falling tree. Brannoc grunted and leaned into it, boots skidding an inch across the black stone, and held.

"All right," he said to the door, to the wardens, to himself. "Let's talk about patience."

* * *

He had been born patient. That was the joke of him, the joke everyone in the border camps had told until he laughed along so they'd stop telling it: a giant, gentle as a milk-cow, who could have crushed any of them with one hand and instead spent his days stitching wounds and brewing willow-bark tea. Twelve years of exile because he healed soldiers on both sides of a war that wanted only one side counted. Twelve years of people flinching when he reached out to touch a fevered brow.

Patience, though. Patience was not gentleness. Patience was what a river did to stone.

Another blow. Something on the far side shrieked—a word in a language that made his teeth ache—and the door grew hot under his palms. He could smell the bronze beginning to weep, thin threads of molten metal crawling down toward his wrists.

"Ah, no you don't." He shifted his grip higher, to where the metal was still cool. His arms trembled now, honestly trembled; he was not a wall, whatever they'd called him, he was flesh and old scars, and flesh has opinions about fire.

That was when he heard them.

Not the wardens. Not the bells. Something else—far off, down the long throat of the approach corridor, where the petitioners queued. A murmur at first, like wind finding a crack in a shutter. Then voices. Then many voices, rising together, and underneath them a sound Brannoc knew from Marrow Hill, from every siege he'd ever stood inside of: the particular roar a crowd makes when it stops being afraid.

The queue. The petitioners. Centuries of them, he'd seen them as they passed—skeletons wrapped in funeral silk, children grown old waiting, a woman holding a claim-stamped deed to a farm that had crumbled to dust two hundred years before her number was called. They had stood in that line in perfect, broken order, because the court had taught them that order was all they had left.

Someone had told them otherwise.

Brannoc grinned, and it was not a gentle grin. "Well now," he rumbled. "Sounds like my kind of congregation."

* * *

Here is what Brannoc had done, in the hour before he put his hands on the door, and here is why he had done it:

While Wren and Serah and Tam slipped down the service stairs toward the vaults, Brannoc had walked—not crept, *walked*, letting the flagstones announce him—into the Hall of Waiting, where the queue wound like a pale river between iron railings. He had stood at its head, where a clerk's lectern sat empty, and he had looked down the length of that line until his eyes watered.

Thousands. Tens of thousands. Some barely holding shape, worn thin as communion wafers by the waiting. And at intervals along the railing, placards nailed up in the court's neat, merciless script: *Hearings suspended pending Reckoning. Return when your suffering is convenient.*

He had picked up the lectern—heavy as a plow-ox, light as kindling in his hands—and driven it through the nearest placard.

Then he had said, in a voice built for shouting across battlefields and softened for sickrooms, loud enough to reach the far end of the hall: "They've kept you waiting because waiting is how they win. But I'm telling you the Reckoning is *now*. Their reckoning, not yours. There are folk down in the vaults breaking open the ledger of every wrong this place ever swallowed. If you want your hearing—" and here his voice cracked, because he thought of his own hearing, the tribunal that had stripped his rank and named him exile for the crime of saving both armies' sons— "*if you want your hearing, walk down and demand it.*"

For a long moment, nothing. The silence of people who have been trained so thoroughly that training outlives hope.

Then an old man near the front—a petitioner so worn his face was more suggestion than feature—rose off his bench. He straightened funeral silk gone grey as cobweb. And he said, in a voice like dry paper: "My hearing is four hundred years overdue."

He stepped over the fallen railings.

The line moved with him.

* * *

Now Brannoc held the door and listened to the storm come.

The wardens on the far side felt it too. The blows against the bronze stopped. Footsteps retreated—not fleeing, he thought, but regrouping, which was worse. Through the seam of the door he saw torchlight scatter, heard orders hissed in that toothaching language, and understood: they would try to seal the petitioners out, lock the great gates of the Undercourt against the very justice-seekers it existed to serve.

"No," Brannoc said quietly. "You don't get to do that."

Because he knew something about doors that the court had forgotten. A door is a promise. And these people had been promised hearings for centuries. Promises, once remembered, are stronger than any bolt or bar.

He heard the mob reach the outer gates—heard the boom of fists and staves and one voice, impossibly clear above the din, the old man in the grey silk: *"We are owed! We are OWED!"*—and then the gates themselves, those ancient bronze leaves that had never yielded, began to groan.

Not break. Groan. Because somewhere beyond them, other hands were pushing too, and grief is a lever longer than any crowbar.

The door under Brannoc's palms shuddered. Not from the wardens—from the whole structure of the place, the mountain feeling its own foundation question itself. Cracks ran up the walls, fine as spider silk, and through them leaked light. Not torchlight. Starlight. Pale, cold, honest starlight, escaping from the vaults below where Wren Galloway was doing whatever reckless thing Wren Galloway had surely not told them he was going to do.

"Oh, you beautiful fool," Brannoc breathed, and laughed, and the laugh hurt his ribs and he didn't care. "You're giving it all back."

The far-side wardens broke. He watched them go through the seam—watched ranks of hooded figures turn from their formation and simply leave, dropping their instruments, because even servants of a system know the sound a system makes when it starts to fall, and no wage is worth standing under it.

The door swung inward an inch on its own weight. Brannoc let it.

He stepped through into the empty corridor, rolled his shoulders—something in his left arm screamed and he thanked it for its service—and turned toward the great gates, where the storm was breaking through.

They flooded past him. Thousands of the wronged, pouring into the halls of the court that had banked their grief like coin, filling corridors that had never once echoed with anything but clerk-silence. They didn't loot. They didn't burn, though gods knew some of them had earned the right. They went to the benches of judgment, the pale ones, the high ones, and they *sat down*—petitioners seating themselves where magistrates should be, the accused taking the accuser's chair, the whole rotten hierarchy inverted in the space of a heartbeat.

The old man in grey silk climbed onto the highest bench, the one shaped like a crescent moon, and looked out over the flood of faces.

"First hearing," he announced, and his paper voice carried impossibly far, the way things do in a hall that has finally decided to listen. "Case one. The matter of a farm, stolen 1,204 years ago, from a family whose name I can no longer remember—but whose *land* remembers them, and so do I."

A roar went up that shook dust from the ceiling.

Brannoc stood in the doorway with his ruined arm hanging loose, watching, and found his eyes burning. Twelve years he'd carried his own grievance like a stone in his boot—small next to these, small, but his. Nobody had ever held a hearing for him. Maybe nobody needed to anymore. Maybe the whole idea of a court you had to beg was dying in this room tonight, and what came after would be rougher and louder and more human, and maybe that was the point.

Somewhere below, the starlight through the cracks was growing brighter. Warmer.

He turned toward the vault stairs, because his family was down there, and Brannoc had learned one lesson better than any other in twelve hard years: you hold the door, yes. But when the door finally opens—

—you go through it.

He took the steps three at a time, following the light, and the last thing he heard behind him was case two being called.
