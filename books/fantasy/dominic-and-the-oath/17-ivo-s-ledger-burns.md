## 17. Ivo's Ledger Burns

Ivo had counted the entries of the Ledger of Small Cruelties for three hundred and eleven years, and in all that time he had never once read one aloud.

It was not that the rule forbade it. The rules of the Undercourt forbade a great many things—weeping at the bench, trading upward, looking directly into an open vessel—and none of them mentioned the ledger's voice, because in three centuries no clerk had ever thought to give it one. The ledger was read *to* Ivo, entry by entry, each dawn of the Long Reckoning, by the pale bench itself, in a voice like dry reeds breaking. He transcribed. He stamped. He shelved. That was the shape of his service: ink moving from left hand to right, grief passing through him without ever touching him.

That was what he told himself, anyway, on the morning the girl with the tin-starred coat stood trial.

He had watched Wren Galloway from behind his lectern during the Judgment of the Pale Bench, quill poised over the day's column, waiting to record the sentence. He had expected the usual arithmetic: oath weighed against mercy, mercy found lighter, mercy confiscated. It was how every case ended. It was how the court stayed balanced, and balance was all Ivo had ever been asked to love.

But the keeper had argued. Not begged—argued, in a cracked voice, about justice outside the law still being justice, and the bench had leaned forward on its pale haunches like something starving, and for one unguarded moment the whole court had smelled of hunger instead of law.

And Ivo's quill had stopped moving.

A stopped quill was heresy. A stopped quill meant the record failed, and the record was the court, and the court was everything beneath the mountain. He knew this the way he knew the weight of his own stamp. And yet his hand would not obey, because for the first time in three hundred years he had looked down at the open ledger—not as a clerk looks, but as a man looks at a mirror—and seen what he had been writing all along.

*Entry 44,201. A fisherman named Aldous Pyke, Corvane, sold the sound of his drowned brother's laughter for safe passage through a storm. Payment rendered. Laughter void.*

*Entry 44,202. A widow traded the last winter she remembered sharing with her husband for a hearing before the bench. Hearing denied; docket full. Winter void.*

*Entry 44,203—*

On and on, back through the centuries, columns and columns, whole shelf-lengths of small cruelties, each one legal, each one binding, each one signed *Ivo, Clerk-Magistrate of the Third Seal* in his own immaculate, unwavering hand.

He had not written the griefs. He had only written the *receipts*. But there was no difference the ledger could see, and now there was no difference he could see either.

The bench sentenced Wren Galloway to the exchange—that much went as the arithmetic always went—and the court guards came for the keeper with their hoods and their hooks, and Ivo stood very straight behind his lectern and did not write it down.

For the first time in three hundred eleven years, the record failed.

No one noticed. That was the terrible thing. The Long Reckoning ground onward, petitioners wailing in the queue beyond the doors, the vaults humming below, and the absence of one line of ink changed nothing at all. The court did not need its records to be true. It needed them to be *filed*.

Ivo looked at his hands. They were gray as ledger-paper, and they were shaking.

He should not have gone down to the burning room. There was no burning room, technically—the court called it the Annex of Expired Claims—but every clerk knew what it was. When a claim expired, when a soul was fully spent fueling the starless sky or sealing a border or keeping a magistrate's chair warm, the vessel went into the annex and the annex burned slow and cold, a fire that gave off light but never warmth, because warmth was not in the budget of the Undercourt.

Ivo went there between sessions, carrying nothing but his stamp.

The vessels sat in their niches like jars of dark honey, thousands upon thousands, each labeled in clerk-hand. He walked the aisles reading names. Not entries—names. *Maren Oduya, hearth-blessing, spent.* *Tobias Fenk, courage, spent.* *Anselm Bright, first love, spent.* Every label ended in *spent*, because everything in the Undercourt ended in *spent*, and Ivo realized, standing among them with his stamp heavy in his pocket, that he had personally certified perhaps nine thousand of these little deaths, and had felt about each of them precisely what he felt about rain: that it was weather, that it was lawful, that it was not his.

Somewhere in this room was Tam of Copper Lane. Somewhere was the sister of Serah Vail, if the warden had spoken true. Somewhere—his breath caught—was a vessel labeled *Ivo, diligence, partial*, filed two hundred years ago, the piece of himself he had traded for his post without ever admitting that was what the posting fee had been.

He put his thumb against the glass of his own name and felt nothing, because the feeling had been the payment.

"I want it back," he said aloud, to no one, knowing the annex did not hear, knowing the annex heard everything and cared about nothing, which was the same as the court entire, which was the same, he understood now, as he had been.

His stamp fell out of his pocket. It rang on the stone like a small bell, and lay there in the lightless light, and Ivo looked at it for a long time before he picked it up.

Then he did not pick it up.

He stepped on it instead, once, deliberately, feeling the old brass bite through his shoe sole, and the crack that ran through the seal of the Third Clerk-Magistrate sounded, to his ears, exactly like the first word of a sentence he had been trying to speak for three centuries.

The Long Reckoning reached its third hour past midnight, and the Pale Bench called for the ledger.

This was ritual: the great book borne up the aisle on its stand by the sitting clerk, opened to the day's column, read aloud in the bench's dry voice so that every claim could be ratified before the assembled dark. Ivo carried it now. His arms shook under its weight, though he had carried it ten thousand times without strain. What trembled in him was not the book.

At the far end of the hall, chained between guards, Wren Galloway watched him come with eyes that had already counted the cost of everything and paid it anyway. Beside him stood the giant Brannoc, bloodied from holding a door, and Serah Vail with her broken badge still smoking faintly where she'd burned it, and behind them, filling the gallery and spilling down the aisles, the mob that Brannoc had loosed from the queue—centuries of petitioners, the wronged and the unheard, packed shoulder to shoulder, silent in the way only people who have waited four hundred years for a hearing can be silent.

The bench spoke. *"Clerk. The record."*

Ivo set the ledger on the stand. He opened it to the day's column. He wet his quill out of pure habit, and the sight of the ink made something turn over in his chest like a ship righting.

"Clerk," said the bench again, in its reed-breaking voice. "Read."

And Ivo—who had served three hundred eleven years, who had stamped nine thousand vessels, who had traded his own feeling for his own position and called it duty—Ivo lifted his head, and did not read the day's column.

He turned backward.

Pages whispered over pages, back through the week, the month, the year, the decade, the century, until the book lay open three hundred years deep, to the very first entry bearing his signature, and the hall grew very quiet, because everyone understood at once that something was happening here that had never happened, and the Undercourt had no procedure for it.

"Entry forty-four thousand, two hundred and one," Ivo read, and his voice cracked on the number, and he let it crack, and kept going. "Aldous Pyke, fisherman of Corvane. Sold the sound of his drowned brother's laughter. The laughter was void. Mr. Pyke waited nineteen years in your queue and was denied a hearing because his claim was, in your words, *sentimental*. He is dead now. The laughter died with him. This entry is *unlawful*."

The bench went still in a new way. *"Clerk. You are reading the wrong direction."*

"No," said Ivo. "For the first time, I am reading the right one." He turned another page. "Entry forty-four thousand, one hundred ninety-six. Maren Oduya. Sold her grief for her daughter's fever-break, and the daughter died anyway, and the court kept both the grief *and* the fee, and refunded nothing, and ruled the contract sound. It was not sound. It was theft wearing a collar and a stamp. This entry is unlawful."

*"Clerk—"*

"Entry forty-three thousand, nine hundred and two." His voice was rising now, and the ink on his fingers seemed to darken as he spoke, as though the ledger were bleeding through his skin. "Tobias Fenk, aged nine, sold his courage so his mother could buy a verdict. The verdict was sold to someone else the same hour. The boy has been afraid of doorways for sixty years. I know because I filed him. I have filed all of them. Every cruelty in this book passed across my desk, and I called it weather, and I called it law, and I was wrong, and the wrongness is *mine*."

The Pale Bench rose.

It had not risen in living memory. It unfolded upward out of its dais like something that had been pretending to be furniture for a very long time, pale and jointed and vast, and its voice when it came again filled the hall to its vaulted dark.

*"The record is the court. The clerk is the record. You will be unmade."*

"Yes," Ivo agreed pleasantly, and tore out the first page.

The sound it made was not paper. It was a chain snapping somewhere far below, in the vaults, and a vessel glowing briefly bright and going free. He tore the next page, and the next, reading as he destroyed—each entry spoken aloud in full, name and grief and fraud, and with each torn leaf something shuddered through the mountain's bones. Lights kindled in the walls. Old wards unraveled with sounds like sighing. In the gallery, a petitioner four hundred years queued began, very quietly, to weep, and then another, and then the whole mass of them, a tide-groan of the unburied grieving finally permitted its own sound.

"The court claims souls may be traded," Ivo shouted over the tearing, over the roaring of the waking mountain, "but a trade requires *two willing parties*, and there is not one willing party in three centuries of this book! Every entry is coercion! Every entry is void! I move to strike them *all*!"

*"You are one clerk,"* said the bench, and for the first time its reed-voice frayed at the edges, and Ivo heard—wonder of wonders—fear in it.

"I was the record," he said. "And the record burns."

Wren Galloway was looking at him from the chains, and the keeper's eyes were shining, and he said—not loudly, but the hall was built to carry whispers—"Clerk. The exchange. They're taking me to the altar within the hour. If you're striking the contracts—"

Ivo was already turning pages forward, hunting, hunting, to the day's column, to the freshest ink, to the entry headed *Galloway, Wren — keeper's oath — complicity — sentence: exchange*, and he gripped the page in both gray hands and held it up before the risen bench like a lantern.

"This one," he said, "is void *first*."

He tore it out.

The hall went white. Every vessel in every vault below flared at once like a field of stars buried in stone, and the mountain groaned from crown to root, and the Pale Bench shrieked—a sound like a library drowning—as three centuries of fraudulent law came apart around it, page by page by page, in the hands of a clerk who had finally learned to read.

Through the blaze, Ivo saw the guards' chains fall away from Wren Galloway's wrists, unlatched by nothing, held by nothing, bound by nothing anymore at all.

"Go," Ivo told the keeper, still tearing, tears cutting clean lines through the ink dust on his face. "The altar. Whatever bargain they've offered you—refuse it. Contracts need a court to enforce them, and *I am striking the court*."

The keeper hesitated half a heartbeat—one look back, one nod that contained more gratitude than Ivo had earned and more than he could bear—and then he was running, the giant and the warden running with him, down the black throat of the hall toward the altar where a soul named Tam hung waiting in its jar of dark honey.

Ivo turned back to the ledger.

There were forty thousand pages left. His hands were bleeding. The bench was screaming. The petitioners had begun, unbelievably, to sing.

He wet his quill one last time, crossed out his own name in the colophon, wrote *witness* above it, and tore the next page free.
