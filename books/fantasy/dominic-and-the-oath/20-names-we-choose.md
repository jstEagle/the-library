## 20. Names We Choose

The first thing Tam learned about the world again was the sound of a kettle.

It came from somewhere below her, a thin whistle threading up through floorboards, and she lay in the narrow bed of the waystation loft and listened to it as though it were a language she had once spoken fluently and was only now translating. Steam. Water. Fire beneath iron. Someone had lit that fire. Someone had filled that kettle. Someone was waiting for it to sing.

She did not know their name.

That was the shape of everything now: a room with warm light in it, and a hole where the people should be. She knew what a kettle was. She knew what rain sounded like on slate, what tallow smelled like when it burned too long, what fear tasted like at the back of the throat. The world had left its furniture behind in her mind. It had only taken out the people and carried them away.

She sat up slowly. Her body ached in a deep, bruised way, as if she had been carried a long distance by someone who loved her but did not know how to carry things gently. Her hands—she looked at them a long time. Small hands, ink-stained at the cuticles, a burn scar across the left palm shaped like a crescent moon. *You made that yourself,* said some voice that wasn't memory exactly, more like the ghost of one. *You were learning to seal a charm and you flinched from the heat and the wax caught you.* She could almost see the shop. Copper counters. A bell over the door. A man with careful hands who moved through the room like someone trying not to disturb the air.

His face would not come.

She went downstairs because the alternative was lying in the dark trying to hold water in her fists.

* * *

The waystation was an old toll-house on the mountain road, all stone and low beams, with a hearth big enough to roast an ox and a table scarred by a hundred years of travelers' knives. The giant sat nearest the fire—he was called Brannoc, they had told her, and he had wept when she first opened her eyes, which frightened her until she understood that his tears were not for anything she had done but simply for the fact of her sitting up. He knitted. That was the second thing Tam learned about the world: giants knitted, badly, and pretended the dropped stitches were intentional.

The woman with the gray-streaked braid was Serah Vail, and she slept sitting upright with a sword across her knees even now, even here, even though the mountain behind them had crumbled into light. When Tam came down the stairs Serah's eyes opened instantly, tracked her, and then—deliberately, like a door being unlocked—softened.

"There's porridge," Serah said. "If you want it. There's honey too. Brannoc traded three socks for it."

"Four socks," Brannoc rumbled, not looking up from his needles. "And the fourth was nearly finished."

"And the man—" Tam began, and stopped, because the word *man* felt wrong, insufficient, like calling a cathedral a building. "The one who carried me out."

"Gone to check the road," Serah said. "He doesn't sleep much. He checks the road, and the well rope, and the shutters, and then he sits outside your door for a while, and then he checks the road again." She paused. "He'll want to know you're up."

Something in Tam's chest pulled tight, a thread drawn through cloth. "I don't remember him," she said. It came out flat, factual, the way you report weather. She had decided, in the loft, that this was how she would say it: without apology, without tears, because tears implied she had lost something she could grieve properly, and you cannot mourn a face you cannot find. "They told me I should know that. So you wouldn't—I don't know. Expect things."

Serah set down the cup she was holding with great care, as though it might otherwise shatter. "We know," she said. "Wren knows."

"Wren." Tam tried the name. It sat strangely on her tongue, a key that fit no lock she owned. "Is that really his name? They said he gave it up once. Gave up another name before that."

"He's got a whole drawer full of names," Brannoc said comfortably. "Like a magpie. But that one's his truest, near as I can tell."

Tam looked at the fire. "Then why does it feel like a stranger's coat?"

No one answered, and the kettle sang downstairs in her memory, and she ate her porridge with honey and thought: *I am a person made entirely of new things.* It should have been terrifying. Mostly it was terrifying. But underneath the terror, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, there was something else—the sense of a page not yet written on, which is its own kind of terrible freedom.

* * *

He came back at dusk, and she watched him from the window before he saw her, and this is what she saw:

A thin man in a patched coat, walking the mountain road with his shoulders hunched against the cold, stopping every fifty paces to look back the way he'd come—not afraid, she thought, but counting. Counting the distance between here and there. Making sure nothing followed them out of the dark. He had a bundle of firewood under one arm that he clearly could not afford to carry and carried anyway. His hair was going gray at the temples, though his face was young-ish underneath the tiredness, the way a house is young underneath the weather.

When he reached the gate he stopped dead, because she was standing in it.

"Oh," he said. "You're up."

"You're Wren," she said.

"Yes." He shifted the firewood. "Are you—how are you feeling?"

"I don't know you," Tam said. "I'm sorry. Everyone says I should. I can tell it hurts you when I say it, so I keep trying to say it gently, but there isn't a gentle version, so I've decided just to say it plainly and let us both get used to it."

For a long moment he stood there with his firewood and his gray temples and his careful hands, and she watched something move across his face—not hurt, or not only hurt. Something older and quieter, like a man watching a door close on a room he had already, long ago, finished grieving. And then he nodded.

"Plainly is good," he said. "Plainly is—yes. Thank you." He came past her into the yard, and then stopped again, and turned, and she realized he had been working up to something on the walk back, all those fifty-pace pauses, and this was it. "Can I try something? It might be strange."

"Everything is strange," Tam said. "Go ahead."

He set down the wood. He straightened, the way soldiers straighten, or men about to swear oaths. And he held out his hand.

"My name is Wren Galloway," he said. "I was born under a different name, on the far side of the border, and I gave it up to seal a gate, and I spent twelve years being small so no one would look too closely at me. I sold charms—luck-knots, mostly, harmless ones—and I told myself that was all I was, and I lied. I broke my oath to come after you. I walked into a court that eats souls and I offered mine up on an altar, and the only reason I'm standing here is that I'd already given away the self they needed to take. I have no idea who I am anymore, honestly. I'm rebuilding from spare parts." His voice cracked slightly on the last words, and he let it crack; he didn't smooth it over. "But I carried you out of that mountain. And I taught you to seal wax charms, and you burned your hand doing it, and you cried and then laughed at yourself for crying. And I made you tea every morning for six years, and you always stole the honey when you thought I wasn't looking, and I always let you, because you were—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "You're the best thing I ever built. Not a charm. You. So. My name is Wren Galloway. It's a pleasure to meet you."

Tam looked at his outstretched hand.

Somewhere inside her, in the vaulted dark where her memories had been ripped out, something stirred—not a memory, nothing so kind—but an echo of a shape. Like hearing a song you've never heard and somehow knowing where the chorus goes.

"That's the most anyone has ever introduced themselves," she said. Her own voice wobbled. She let it. He had let his. "Most people just say their name and wait for yours."

"I had twelve years of practice being incomplete," Wren said. "I'm done with it. If we're starting over, we start over properly."

She took his hand. His grip was careful, the grip of a man who handled fragile things for a living, and warm, and real, and hers to do whatever she liked with.

"Tam," she said. "Just Tam. Apparently that's all I've got right now. It'll have to do."

"It's a good name," Wren said gravely. "I chose it myself, you know. Well—you chose it, and I agreed to it very loudly."

And to her own astonishment, Tam laughed.

* * *

The days at the waystation took on a rhythm, and Tam learned that rhythm the way she had once, apparently, learned charm-work: by repetition, by failure, by small stubborn victories.

Mornings, Wren made tea, and she stole the honey, and he pretended not to see, and both of them performed this ritual with the solemnity of people reenacting a play whose script had burned—working from stage directions alone, trusting that the blocking was still true even if the lines were gone. Some mornings it worked. Some mornings she reached for the honey jar and felt nothing at all, and put it down untouched, and Wren's face did something complicated and then settled into kindness, and he said, "There's no rule that says you have to," and meant it, and poured his tea bitter, and drank it anyway.

Afternoons, she worked.

It had been Serah's idea. "Your hands remember," she'd said, setting a spool of copper wire and a tin disc on the table in front of Tam. "Souls aren't the same as skill. Whatever they took, they didn't take your fingers." And it was true, or half true—Tam's hands knew knots her mind had never seen, knew how to twist wire into a cage-star, knew the exact pressure that made solder flow like water. She sat by the window making small bright useless beautiful things, and the others drifted by to watch, and Brannoc commissioned a tin wolf that turned out looking more like a lopsided dog, and he declared it perfect and hung it around his neck.

One afternoon she made a luck-knot without thinking—a simple triple-loop in red cord, the kind sold in market stalls to fishermen—and when she held it up, Wren went very still across the room.

"That's the first thing I ever sold you," he said quietly. "First week of your apprenticeship. You bought it off me before I knew you were staying. You said—" He stopped, and laughed, short and surprised. "You said, *this is a scam, but it's a well-made scam,* and paid double."

"I have excellent taste," Tam said, and turned the knot over in her fingers. It meant nothing to her. It meant everything to him. And she found, examining that asymmetry, that she did not resent it—that there was a strange gift in being known by someone better than you knew yourself. "Tell me more," she said. "Tell me the ones I'd hate to hear. Tell me the time I was awful."

Wren's eyebrows rose. "You want the bad ones?"

"I want the true ones. The good ones are just decoration. If I'm going to be somebody, I'd rather be somebody who once set fire to your stockroom arguing about ward-theory."

"You did set fire to my stockroom," Wren said, with the reverence of a man reciting scripture. "Arguing about ward-theory. You were right, incidentally. The wards were wrong. The fire was also wrong, but the theory was right."

"Then I stand by it," Tam said, "on your testimony," and Wren laughed, really laughed, and something in the room changed temperature. Because that was the thing she was learning, day by day, knot by knot: memory was not only what you remembered. It was also what other people carried for you. She had lost her past, yes. But it existed in fragments all around her—in a giant's lopsided tin wolf, in a warden's gruff reports of the night Wren had blackmailed her, in the careful hands of a man who had crossed hell and offered up his soul and come back with nothing but her. She could rebuild from their testimony. She could choose which parts to keep.

That was the difference, she began to understand, between a grave and a foundation.

* * *

On the ninth night, she dreamed of the mountain.

Not the court—she had no dreams of the court, and suspected she never would, that part of her having been spent like currency and never returned. She dreamed instead of the moment after: cold air, starlight flooding back into the sky like water into a dry bed, and being carried, weightless, while a voice kept talking to her the whole way down. Talking and talking, desperately, endlessly, saying *you're all right, you're all right, I've got you, don't you dare leave, I only just got you back, I never even told you—*

"Told me what?" she asked the dream.

And the dream-Wren, made of starlight and exhaustion, looked at her with twelve years of grief in his face and said: *that you were never payment. Not to me. Never.*

She woke with wet eyes and lay listening to the house breathe—Brannoc's snore like distant surf, Serah turning on her bedroll, wind at the shutters—and thought about the hole inside her, and found that it had changed shape overnight. It was still there. It would always be there; she understood that now, the way you understand a scar. But holes were also openings. Things could grow through them.

In the morning she found Wren on the porch step, mending a boot with the intense concentration of a man avoiding a conversation, and sat down beside him, and said:

"Tell me about the gate."

He looked up sharply. "What?"

"The border gate. The one you sealed. Serah says we're camped two days' ride from it, and you keep checking the road south like it's going to follow you home." She hugged her knees. "I don't remember it. But everyone talks around it like it's a wound, and I've decided I'm done tiptoeing around wounds. Mine or yours. So. Tell me."

For a long time Wren said nothing. He tied off the bootlace, untied it, tied it again. Then he set the boot down and looked out at the road, at the pale line of it running down off the mountain toward the lowlands, toward Corvane, toward a border crossing where a wall of sealed magic had stood for twelve years and now—since the night the stars came back—stood with a hole in it the size of everything.

"When I swore that oath," he said finally, "I thought keeping was the same as closing. Lock the gate, hide the magic, keep everyone safe by keeping everyone apart. Twelve years I believed that. Then I watched a court buy a child's soul to keep the sky dark, and I understood that sealing things doesn't protect them. It just concentrates the dark somewhere you can't see it." He flexed his hands, those careful hands, as if they ached. "The wards are broken. The gate is open. And every instinct I have is screaming at me to go back and seal it again, because that's what a keeper does. And I think—" his voice dropped, honest and rough— "I think the answer is no. I think the answer is that a gate can be kept open. Warded fairly. Guarded, not imprisoned. But I don't know how to build that. Nobody does. It's never been done."

"So we'll figure it out," Tam said.

Wren turned and looked at her—really looked, the way he'd looked at her on the altar steps, according to Serah, when she'd been a hollow vessel and he'd refused to stop seeing her anyway.

"You don't even know me," he said. "Why would you sign on for that?"

"Because you introduced yourself properly," Tam said. "Because you told me the bad stories. Because you carried me out of a mountain and talked the whole way down." She shrugged. "I can't remember loving you. But I can decide to. Those aren't the same thing, and I've decided the second one might actually be worth more." She stuck out her hand, formal as a treaty. "So. Partners? You know gates. I apparently know wards. Brannoc can lift anything, Serah can threaten anyone, and none of us has anywhere else to be."

Wren looked at her hand. Then he laughed—wet-eyed, helpless, twelve years of smallness cracking open like river ice in spring—and took it.

"Partners," he said. "God help the border."

Above the waystation, the morning stars were fading into blue, each one burning steady and real, and Tam thought: *I don't know who I was. But I know who I'm choosing. And tomorrow we start walking south, toward the gate, toward whatever we build next—and for the first time since I woke up in this world, I am not afraid of the road.*

She went inside to pack.
