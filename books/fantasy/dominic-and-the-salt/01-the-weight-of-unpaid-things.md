## 1. The Weight of Unpaid Things

The Salt Guild kept its winter inventory in a room with no windows, which was sensible, because some of the things stored there did not do well when observed by daylight. Hazel had learned this in their first year, when they'd left the door open too long and a jar of borrowed courage had gone thin as vinegar. The guild master had made them copy the inventory ledger three times by way of punishment, and Hazel had copied it four, because they were thorough, and thoroughness had been their armor ever since.

Now, eleven winters later, they sat at the counting table with a stub of graphite, a pot of weak tea gone cold at their elbow, and the great ledger open before them like a mouth waiting to be fed.

Hazel counted the way other people prayed: steadily, without interruption, with the sense that something was listening.

*Item forty-one,* they wrote. *One grief, bottled, vintage unknown. Seal intact but weeping slightly at the seam. Recommend sale before spring thaw, when such things turn.*

*Item forty-two. Luck, borrowed, quantity: one fair wind's worth. Debtor: the fisherwomen of Lowmarsh. Interest accrued since Midwinter: considerable.*

*Item forty-three. Second chance, unused, provenance disputed.*

They paused over that one. Second chances were delicate stock. This particular item had come in off a smuggler's boat two autumns ago, wrapped in oilcloth and humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound is supposedly finished. It had been sold once already and returned, which never happened—second chances did not come back; that was rather the point of them—and the guild master had locked it in the iron cabinet at the back of the storeroom and told no one why.

Hazel did not ask why. That was another piece of their armor: they asked about weights and measures, about interest and depreciation, about everything except the questions that mattered.

Around them, the storeroom breathed. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling on all four walls, and on those shelves sat the impossible inventory of the Salt Guild: jars of last words, stoppered with wax and red string; a crate of shadows belonging to people who had paid to be rid of them and not yet decided what to be instead; a small lacquered box labeled *regret, powdered, one measure* that rattled softly whenever someone walked past it quickly. There was a barrel of sleep, harvested from a village that had dreamed the same dream for a generation and grown tired of it. There were seven bottles of names, each one a name its owner had outgrown.

And beneath all of it, under the floorboards that Hazel sat above, was the vault where the guild kept the things too dangerous to shelve. Souls, mostly. Fragments of souls. The guild traded in them rarely, quietly, at prices that would make a bishop faint. Hazel kept those accounts too. They had never once opened the vault door themselves. They entered the transactions blind, from slips of paper passed through the gap beneath the door by whoever held the key that month, and they recorded what the slips said, and they did not think about it afterward. This, too, was discipline. This, too, was armor.

"Hazel."

Brannick's voice came through the storeroom door, rough as a whetstone. He didn't wait for an answer; Brannick never waited for anything, least of all permission. He shouldered his way in, bringing the cold of the yard with him, snow melting off his shoulders in dark spots, and he stood there filling the doorway the way he filled every doorway, large and unapologetic, his beard crusted with ice.

"You're down here again," he said.

"Inventory doesn't do itself."

"Inventory could wait a day. Guild master wants runners briefed. Ashgate's posted something." He said the word *Ashgate* flatly, the way you'd say *weather coming*. "Crown business. Everyone's to hear it."

"I'll finish this first."

"You'll finish nothing," Brannick said, but there was no real weight behind it. He leaned against the shelf beside the door—a shelf holding bottled patience, which Hazel had always thought was a joke the universe played on anyone who worked with Brannick—and watched Hazel work with the expression of a man who had never understood the counting and never wanted to. "You know what I don't get about you, Villalobos?"

"My name's Zhou."

"That's what I don't get." He grinned, showing a chipped tooth. "You count things that can't be counted. Grief in jars. How do you weigh grief?"

"By how much it leaks," Hazel said, and wrote the figure for item forty-four without looking up.

Brannick laughed, a short bark, and pushed off from the shelf. "Briefing's at the bell. Don't make the old man send Wren to fetch you."

He left the door swinging. Cold air moved through the storeroom, and along the shelves the inventory stirred—the regret-box rattled, the jar of weeping grief shed one bright drop down its seam, and somewhere in the iron cabinet the disputed second chance gave a single soft hum, like a note held too long.

Hazel capped the ink, closed the ledger, and rested their hand flat on its cover for a moment longer than bookkeeping required.

It was going to be a strange winter. They could feel it the way sailors feel weather in old scars.

The briefing hall had been a chapel once, back before the guild bought the building and scraped the god's name off the lintel. You could still see where the letters had been, if you knew where to look—shallow ghosts in the stone above the door, worn smooth by a century of rain. Hazel looked at them every time they entered, the way you touch your tongue to a missing tooth.

Most of the guild was assembled: the runners in their grey wool, the collectors nursing morning wine, the mercenaries along the back wall where the light was worst. Brannick had claimed a bench and was sharing a flask with Osta and the twins. And up front, perched on the edge of the dais with her boots swinging because her feet didn't reach the floor, sat Wren.

She was sixteen that winter, or she said she was; with runners you took their word for it. She had a narrow fox face, ink-stained fingers, and a habit of watching Hazel across any crowded room with an expression she probably thought was unreadable. It wasn't. It said *I have news and I'm saving it*, which meant either mischief or trouble, and with Wren it was usually both.

Hazel took their place near the wall and Wren caught their eye and mouthed something that might have been *later*.

Then the guild master came in, and the room went quiet the way water goes still.

Master Corvane had run the Salt Guild for thirty years, and he wore those years the way he wore everything—neatly, deliberately, like a coat chosen to make people underestimate what was underneath. He was a small man, precise, with white hair cut close and hands that were never empty. Tonight they held a paper bearing the seal of the Crown of Ashgate.

"We are going dark," he said, without preamble. "Effective tonight."

The flask stopped passing. Somewhere a runner shifted, and the sound of it was very loud.

"A proclamation went up in Ashgate square yesterday. The crown has declared a hunt." Corvane read from the paper in a voice like a clerk reading a shipping manifest, which somehow made it worse. "'By order of His Majesty, all persons found carrying the gift of soul-craft—making, mending, moving, or trading in souls—are declared enemies of the realm. Any who harbor such persons share their guilt. Reward is offered for information leading to seizure.'" He folded the paper. "They burned a hedge-witch in the square this morning to explain the terms. Her name was Merrin Dole. Some of you bought luck from her. Remember the price she paid for selling it."

Silence. Hazel stood against the wall and felt the room tilt very slightly, the way a floor tilts when you've been drinking, except Hazel hadn't been drinking. They were thinking, with a strange floating clarity: *soul-craft. Making, mending, moving, trading.* Four verbs. Four categories of guilty.

Hazel Zhou did all four.

Had done, since before they could write. Their mother had taught them the first craft herself—how to hold a soul-fragment steady between cupped hands without scorching it, how to mend a torn thread of self the way you mend a hem, invisibly, so no one knows where the damage was. Hazel had hidden the gift for twenty years behind ledgers and ledgers and more ledgers, buried in competence, invisible in plain sight. No one at the guild knew. Not Brannick, not Corvane, not even Wren.

Especially not Wren.

"The guild has survived hunts before," Corvane was saying. "We survive them by becoming nothing. No trades until thaw. No deliveries beyond essential. Runners travel in pairs, never at night. Collectors stay home. Mercenaries—" his eyes flicked to the back wall, to Hazel, to Brannick, "—guard the house and say less than usual. Questions?"

Osta raised a hand. "How long?"

"Until the crown forgets us. Crowns forget slowly, but they do forget." Corvane tucked the proclamation into his coat. "That's all."

The room broke apart into murmurs, and through the murmurs Wren came sliding along the benches like weather, arriving at Hazel's elbow exactly as predicted.

"Walk with me," she said. "I delivered to the tanner's on Copper Lane this morning. Before the briefing. Before *going dark*." She put mocking quotation marks around it with her fingers. "You'll want to hear this."

"You were supposed to report deliveries to Corvane."

"I'm reporting to you first. You're the one who reads everything twice." She was already moving toward the yard, pulling her scarf up. Outside, the snow had that blue-white light of late afternoon, and the guild's courtyard lay quiet, the well roped off against ice, the salt-store sheds hunched under their loads. Wren stopped by the well and lowered her voice even though there was no one to hear.

"The client wasn't the tanner. Some woman I've never seen—fine clothes, city accent, gloves indoors. She takes the package, and then she looks at me. Really looks. And she says—" Wren's imitation of the fine voice was merciless, "—'Child, who taught you to carry things like that?'" A pause. "I said I didn't know what she meant. She smiled at me like she was doing me a kindness. And she let me go."

Hazel felt the cold move through them that had nothing to do with the season. "*Let you go?*"

"She said—" Wren hesitated, and here her fox face flickered, just once, showing something underneath that wasn't mischief at all, "—she said the hunt hasn't reached the children yet. Then she paid me double and shut the door." She shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing. "City people are strange. Probably nothing."

"Show me your hands."

"What? Why—"

"*Show me.*"

Wren scowled and shoved her mittens off. Her bare fingers were ink-stained and chapped and ordinary. But Hazel took them anyway, gently, the way their mother had taught them to handle fragile stock, and felt—with the gift they were not supposed to have, the gift that woke under their skin like a second pulse—that Wren's soul-thread was whole. Whole and bright and *young*, younger than it should have been, wound tight as a new spool. Untouched. Whatever the woman in the fine gloves had seen, she had seen it and walked away.

For now.

"You're shaking," Wren said. "Your hands are freezing, you know that? Like handling dead fish."

"Nothing." Hazel released her. "Go inside. Stay in pairs. And Wren—" they heard their own voice come out carefully flat, ledger-flat, "—if anyone else asks what you saw this morning, tell them exactly what you told me. Nothing. City people are strange."

Wren studied them for a moment with those too-sharp eyes. "That's what I said."

"Yes. Keep saying it." 

She went. Hazel stayed by the well a moment longer, watching their own breath climb the blue air, and thought about the word the gloved woman had used. *Carry.* Not *make*, not *trade*. Carry. As if the girl were a vessel, as if something had been placed in her that she didn't know she held.

Hazel pushed the thought down where they pushed everything that mattered, and went in to finish the inventory.

The storeroom at night was a different country. Hazel lit two lamps, banked the brazier, and unlocked the ledger cabinet, and the work resumed where it had left off: item forty-five, item fifty, item sixty. Weights and measures of the impossible. The tea was reheated and forgotten twice. The brazier ticked. Somewhere overhead the guild settled into its noises, doors and footsteps, Brannick's laugh rolling down through the floorboards once and fading.

It was nearly midnight, by Hazel's own reckoning, when they reached the oldest ledger.

It lived on the bottom shelf, spine cracked, covers scarred with water damage from some flood before Hazel's time. The guild called it the Black Ledger, though its cover was brown as old blood. It held the founding years, the wild years, the years before Corvane. Entries in six different hands, some of them barely legible, prices in currencies that no longer existed. Hazel audited it once a year, out of duty and dread, and every year found it exactly as they'd left it.

This year, it was not.

They knew before they touched it. The book sat wrong on the shelf—too light, canted at an angle, the way a book sits when pages have been removed. Hazel drew it out with both hands and set it on the table, and the lamps guttered though there was no draft, and the brazier ticked, and Hazel turned to the year they knew they would find.

The year of their mother's death.

The page was gone. Not torn roughly—cut, close to the spine, with a blade or a razor, by someone who knew books. The stub of it remained, and across the stub, in pencil so faint it might have been written and half-erased a hundred times, someone had noted the removal: *Entry withdrawn. Account transferred. See current file.*

No date. No initials. No entry in any later ledger matched it—Hazel checked, turning pages faster now, the graphite scratching, checking the transfer records, the vault slips, the annual reconciliations. Eleven years of meticulous bookkeeping, and the account had vanished like breath off glass.

Hazel sat very still.

Their mother had kept the guild's books before them. That was the inheritance: the gift, and the ledgers, in that order, though their mother had always said the order was reversed. *The numbers keep you safe, little wren. The numbers are the cage nobody sees.* She had died in this building, in the room above the counting table, in the winter Hazel turned nine. Fever, they'd been told. Quick fever, bad winter, nothing to be done. And eleven years later someone had cut her final page out of the oldest book in the house and written *account transferred* in pencil, and no one had told Hazel, and no one ever would have.

Unless the person who cut it had assumed Hazel would find it eventually. Unless the pencil note was a message.

*See current file.*

Hazel looked at the iron cabinet at the back of the storeroom, where the disputed second chance hummed in the dark. Then past it, to the floor. To the boards beneath the floor. To the vault whose key changed hands monthly, whose slips came through the gap beneath the door in handwriting Hazel had long ago learned to recognize without admitting it.

Corvane's handwriting.

They knelt. The floorboards were old oak, pegged, not nailed, and eleven years of auditing had taught Hazel which board was which. They had never pried one up. They had never needed to. They did now.

Beneath the board, in a cavity lined with oilcloth, lay a bundle of papers tied with red string—the same red string that stoppered the jars of last words upstairs. Hazel untied it with fingers that did not shake, because they were thorough, and thoroughness was their armor, and armor does not shake.

The topmost paper was a vault slip. Current file. Their mother's hand—not the faded hand of the Black Ledger entries but sharp, hurried, the hand of a woman writing fast in a bad winter:

*Received into vault, this 3rd of Frostmonth: one soul entire, young female, age approximately five years. Sold by consent of guardian. Payment deferred. Collateral named against deferred payment: H. ZHOU, minor child of seller. Terms: collateral forfeit upon default, default being failure of seller to complete payment within ten years.*

Ten years. Eleven winters ago.

Below the slip, in a different ink, in a different hand—smaller, colder, a hand like a signature on a bill of sale—one line had been added:

*Default confirmed. Collateral claimed. Collection pending.*

Hazel knelt on the cold floor with the papers in their hands and did the arithmetic they had spent eleven years refusing to do.

Five years old. Young female. Sold by consent of guardian, in the same season their mother died, in the same season the hunt list had first carried the name Zhou—though Hazel wouldn't learn that part until later, wouldn't understand until much later what their mother had bought with the sale, or what she had tried to buy, or how badly the arithmetic of love can fail.

But they knew the rest now. They knew it the way you know a weight has shifted in a dark room, by the way everything balances differently afterward.

Somewhere in the guild's vault, beneath the floorboards, filed among fragments and remnants and things too dangerous to shelve, was a soul entire. Young female. Age approximately five years.

And a runner with a fox face and ink-stained fingers had stood by the well that afternoon and said, *the hunt hasn't reached the children yet,* and laughed, and lied about what she saw, because lying was armor too, and everyone in this house wore it.

Hazel retied the red string. They replaced the papers. They pressed the board home and swept the dust flat with the side of their hand, and then they sat at the counting table until the candles burned low, with the Black Ledger open to the wounded place where their mother's final page had been, and wrote nothing at all.

Outside, the bell in the yard rang the hour—three slow strokes, the guild's own bell, the one Wren was learning to ring. Three o'clock. The coldest hour. Somewhere in the dark of the house below the counting room, in the vault under the floor, something that had once been a five-year-old girl waited to be collected.

Collection pending.

Hazel blew out the lamps and sat in the dark, and began, with the terrible calm of a bookkeeper who has finally found the error in the accounts, to plan.
