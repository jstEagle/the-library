## 2. What the Bell Ringer Knew

The package was small enough to fit in the palm of a hand, and heavy in the way that mattered things.

Wren had learned to read weight by feel before she'd learned her letters. The Salt Guild taught its runners early: a jar of bottled grief pulled toward the earth like a sleeping cat; borrowed luck rattled faintly when you shook it, though you were never supposed to shake it; second chances weighed nothing at all until you tried to put them down. This one—wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with grey wax stamped by no sigil anyone would admit to—had the particular density of something that remembered being alive.

"Deliver it whole," Hazel had said that morning, pressing it into her hands across the counting table. Their fingers had been ink-stained and cold, and they hadn't looked up from the ledger while they spoke, which was how Wren knew they were worried. Hazel only avoided eye contact when the numbers frightened them. "Don't open it. Don't shake it. Don't tell anyone what house you're going to, not even Brannick. Especially not Brannick."

"I know how to carry things," Wren said.

Hazel finally looked at her then, and their face did the thing it always did—the thing where all the sharpness fell away for half a heartbeat and left something older and more tired underneath, something that looked at her like she was an entry in a ledger that couldn't be balanced. "I know you do," they said. "That's why I'm giving it to you."

She was fourteen, and she had been running for the guild since she was nine, since the winter Master Corvane found her half-frozen in the salt sheds and asked her one question: *Can you keep your mouth shut?* She could. It was the first thing she'd ever been praised for, and she had built a whole self out of it, the way other children built forts from driftwood. Keep your mouth shut. Keep your eyes down. Be small, be quick, be nothing worth remembering.

The client lived past Ashgate, on the far side of the bell tower, in one of those narrow houses that leaned against each other like conspirators. The route took her through the morning market, where she moved the way she'd been taught—not hurrying, because hurrying drew eyes, but flowing, the way water finds the gaps between stones. A fishwife argued with a cooper. Two city watchmen stood at the corner eating chestnuts, bored as furniture. Nobody looked at the small girl with the satchel and the chapped hands. Nobody ever did. That was the guild's oldest magic, older than anything in the ledgers: the art of being forgettable, practiced so long it had worn smooth as sea glass.

The bell tower loomed over the last street, and Wren hated it without knowing why. It wasn't the bells themselves—they rang only for executions and coronations, and there hadn't been either in her lifetime. It was the way the tower stood over everything, patient, like something waiting for a debt to come due.

The house had a green door, and under the knocker someone had scratched a mark she didn't recognize: a circle with a line through it, like an eye being closed.

She knocked the pattern—three slow, one fast—and waited.

The door opened on a woman. That was the first surprise. Guild clients were usually men, or if they were women they were old and rich and smelled of money gone stale. This woman was neither young nor old, neither rich nor poor, wearing a coat that had once been fine and now was merely warm. Her hair was iron-grey and cut short as a soldier's. And her eyes—

Her eyes did the thing that eyes weren't supposed to do. They *saw* her.

Not glanced. Not noted-and-dismissed the way every eye in Ashgate dismissed her. They settled on Wren's face and stayed, and behind them something turned over, the way a ledger page turns.

"You're young," the woman said.

"I'm the runner," Wren said, which was the answer to every question.

"The guild sends children now." The woman stepped back to let her in, and Wren went, because refusing a door once opened was against the rules too. The house smelled of dried herbs and lamp oil and, beneath it, something faintly mineral, like the air inside a cave. "Or perhaps the guild has always sent children, and I've simply been away long enough to find it obscene."

Wren held out the package with both hands, the way she'd been taught. "This is for you. There's no message. Payment was made in advance."

The woman didn't take it. She was still looking at Wren—at her, through her, into whatever room of her the rest of the world never opened. "Say your name," she said quietly.

"I'm not allowed."

"No," the woman agreed. "You're not. But say it anyway. Just once. Just here." She closed the door behind them both, and the latch sounded very loud. "I want to hear what it sounds like when you lie about yourself."

Wren's mouth went dry. Every rule she owned stacked itself up inside her throat: don't speak, don't linger, don't be seen, deliver and go. But the woman's eyes had that turning quality, and Wren felt suddenly, horribly certain that this was a person who could read the inside of a thing the way Hazel read ledgers—that she was already reading Wren, cover to cover, and finding the entries interesting.

"Wren," she said. Just the name. Nothing else.

Something happened in the room. It was very small, and it might have been nothing—a draft, a settling of the floorboards—but the lamp flame leaned toward her as if listening, and the package in her hands grew heavier by a single degree, and the woman exhaled slowly through her nose like someone confirming a suspicion she'd carried a long way.

"There it is," the woman said softly. "Soul-craft. Untrained. Feral, almost. Who taught you to seal a name inside a word like that? No one? You did it yourself?" She laughed, but it wasn't a mocking laugh; it was worse than that. It was the laugh of someone recognizing kin. "Child. Do you have any idea what you are?"

"A runner," Wren said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Underneath the steadiness her heart was going like a rabbit's. "I deliver packages."

"Yes," the woman said. "And the packages are the least of what this guild moves, aren't they. Bottled grief. Borrowed luck. Second chances." She said the words the way Hazel said them—flatly, professionally—but where Hazel's flatness was armor, this woman's was contempt. Or grief. They sounded alike, sometimes. "And souls. Do they send you with souls, Wren? Do they let you carry those?"

"I don't ask what's in them."

"No. Of course you don't. That's the design." The woman finally took the package, weighing it in her palm exactly the way Hazel did, and something about the gesture made Wren's stomach turn over. "They build you small and quick and unasking, and they teach you that silence is a virtue, and by the time you're old enough to wonder what you've been carrying, you've carried so much of it that wondering would break you." She set the package on a table without looking at it. "I knew a runner like you once. In another city, under another name. She could seal a soul into a jar with a song. The guild called her a miracle right up until the day they spent her."

The lamp flame leaned again. Wren realized she had taken a step backward without deciding to.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because I'm going to let you live," the woman said, "and I find I want you to know why."

The words landed strangely, like coins dropped into a well whose bottom Wren had never questioned before. *Let you live.* As if living were a thing that had been granted rather than assumed. As if somewhere between the green door and this moment, her life had become a transaction someone else was choosing whether to complete.

"You should go," the woman said. "Out the back, through the yard, the way you came is already watched—I'm sorry, that's my fault, my arrival was noticed and I didn't move fast enough after. Tell whoever keeps your ledgers that the delivery is received and the account is closed. And tell them—" she paused, and for the first time her certainty flickered, showing something tired underneath, "—tell them the crown has started keeping lists of its own. Names of the gifted. And that some of those names are old. Older than the people who wrote them."

"I don't understand."

"No," the woman said gently. "But you will. That's the cruelty of it—you'll understand all of it eventually, and you'll wish you'd understood sooner." She opened the back door onto a frost-killed garden, grey stalks standing in grey light. "Go. Run well, little bell ringer. And when they ask you what you saw here today—"

Wren was already moving, but the woman's voice followed her through the doorway, quiet and precise:

"—remember that you can choose what to keep."

* * *

The guild hall sat low against the harbor hill, built of the same salt-bleached stone as everything else, deliberately less interesting than the buildings around it. Wren came in through the runners' door, gave her token to the board, and stood dripping meltwater in the entryway while her heartbeat remembered how to be slow.

Hazel was at the counting table, as always, bent over the winter inventory with a stub of pencil and the expression of a person doing arithmetic on a falling building. Around them the hall did its evening work: Brannick arguing with the quartermaster about crossbow strings; two of the older mercenaries playing dice with coins that were probably luck-borrowed; Master Corvane's door shut, lamplight leaking under it like something spilled.

"Received?" Hazel asked, not looking up.

"Received. Account closed." Wren hung her satchel on its peg. The peg was hers alone; that was a kind of wealth nobody outside the guild would understand. "No complications."

Hazel's pencil stopped.

It stopped for maybe two seconds, which was long enough. Hazel had a gift—not the gift, not the dangerous buried thing they thought nobody knew about, but a smaller human one—for hearing the shape of a sentence. Wren had watched them catch lies in merchants' contracts, in smugglers' boasts, in Brannick's expense reports, purely by noticing where the words sat wrong against each other. And now the pencil hovered over the inventory page, and Hazel's head tilted by a degree, like a bird hearing a worm under snow.

"No complications," Hazel repeated.

"None."

"The client was home?"

"Yes."

"And they took the package directly?"

"...Yes."

Hazel looked up then. Their eyes were brown and ordinary and missed nothing, and Wren stood under them feeling like a jar with a bad seal. *Remember that you can choose what to keep,* the woman had said. But the woman hadn't known about Hazel. The woman hadn't known that some silences were debts, and Hazel had paid for every one of Wren's since she was nine years old—had stood between her and Corvane's temper, had taught her letters by lantern-light, had once walked eleven miles through freezing rain to fetch a physicker when she broke her wrist on the north stairs. You didn't lie to Hazel. You lied *for* Hazel, the way you'd take a wound for family.

Except lying for someone and lying to them wore the same face, and Hazel was looking straight at it.

"What did the house smell like?" Hazel asked. Casual. Terrible.

"Dried herbs," Wren said. That part was true. "Lamp oil." Also true. She reached for the third thing and found the cave-smell waiting there, mineral and cold, and heard herself say: "Old stone."

A pause. Then Hazel nodded and went back to the inventory, and Wren breathed again, and hated herself a little for how good the breath felt.

She ate supper in the runners' alcove—barley and salt fish, the same as everyone, hierarchy ending at the table—and listened to the hall talk. Talk was weather in the guild; you learned to read it early. Tonight the weather was wrong. The dice players kept glancing at Corvane's door. Brannick had stopped arguing about crossbow strings and was drinking more than his share, staring at nothing. And near the hearth, two of the older members were speaking in the low, flattened voices people used when they wanted to be overheard by exactly one person.

"—third one this month, the hedge-woman off Tanner's Row. Gone in the night, no trial posted."

"Crown's sweeping. My cousin says the magistrates have new commissions. Sealed ones."

"Sealed commissions. For what, tax season?"

"For *names*," said the second voice, and then the fire popped, and the word was gone into the noise of the hall, but it left a hole behind it shaped exactly like itself.

*Names.*

Wren washed her bowl and told herself she was imagining the cold that walked up her spine. The woman in the green-door house had said *the crown has started keeping lists,* and Wren had filed it away as one more strange thing said by one more strange client, the kind of thing runners heard twice a year and were trained to drop at the threshold. But now the word *names* was sitting in the hall's warm air like a coin on ice, sinking, and everyone was pretending not to watch it go.

She found Hazel afterward, in the inventory room, re-shelving jars by candlelight. The room was the safest place in the guild—no one came in who didn't have to—and it smelled of wax and brine and the faint sweet rot of preserved feeling. Grief, mostly. The guild stored more grief than anything else. People sold it when carrying it got too heavy, and the jars hummed very quietly on their shelves, a sound you could only hear if you'd been born able to hear it.

"Hazel," Wren said. "At the delivery. Did you know who the client was?"

The shelving stopped.

"It was handled through three intermediaries," Hazel said. "Standard cutout protocol. Why?"

"She knew things. About the guild. About—" Wren hesitated on the edge of it, the whole story balanced there, the leaning lamp flame and the laughing-not-laughing voice and *I'm going to let you live.* "About what we carry. She talked like someone who used to work the other side of the counter."

Hazel turned around slowly. In the candlelight their face was unreadable, which meant it was working hard. "What did she say, exactly?"

And here it was. The moment the woman had promised her, arriving ahead of schedule. *When they ask you what you saw here today—remember that you can choose what to keep.*

Wren chose.

"She said the account was closed," Wren said. "She said the payment terms were satisfactory. She said—" and here she gave Hazel the truest false thing she owned, the lie shaped most like the truth, "—she said the guild should stop sending children, because children remember."

Silence, except for the shelves, humming their grief-hum.

"That's all?"

"That's all."

Hazel studied her for a long moment, and Wren stood in the study the way she stood under searchers' eyes at the gates: empty, pleasant, forgettable. She had done it ten thousand times. She was good at it. She was so good at it that sometimes she forgot herself, and that, she understood suddenly, was the trap the woman had described—the guild built you small and quick and unasking, and one day you looked for the self you'd folded away to fit the role, and couldn't find the creases.

"Children do remember," Hazel said at last, quietly. "That's the problem with them." They picked up the candle. "Get some sleep, Wren. Tomorrow I need you on the south run, and it's a long one."

Wren made it four steps toward the door before Hazel spoke again, and the voice was different—lighter, almost careless, which from Hazel was the most alarming register there was.

"One more thing. The client. When she looked at you—" the candle flame steadied in Hazel's motionless hand, "—did she look at you the way people look at a runner? Or the way they look at something on a list?"

Wren's blood went thin and cold.

"A runner," she said.

"Mm," said Hazel, and turned back to the shelves, and let her go.

She lay awake that night in the runners' loft, listening to the building settle and the harbor ice groan, turning the woman's words over like contraband. *Some of those names are old. Older than the people who wrote them.* And underneath that, quieter, the thing she hadn't let herself hear yet: the memory of the lamp flame leaning toward her when she said her own name, hungry and attentive, like something taking an inventory.

In the morning, before dawn, before the south run, she passed the great board in the entryway where the guild posted its routes and rules. Someone had added a notice overnight, in Master Corvane's cramped hand: **ALL RUNners REPORT. NEW CROWN PROCLAMATION AT ASHGATE. NO ONE GOES INTO THE CITY ALONE.**

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, someone else had written: *they burned a witch in the square yesterday.*

Wren read it twice. Then she went and woke Hazel, because whatever crack had opened in the world beyond the green door, it had just reached the guildhall—and she had the sudden, sick certainty that when the proclamation went up at Ashgate, some names on the crown's list would be older than anyone knew.

Maybe even names written in a dead woman's hand.
