## 3. Proclamation at Ashgate

The decree went up on the Ashgate market post an hour before dawn, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern rooftops, half the town had gathered beneath it to read what they already knew.

Hazel heard it first as a change in the quality of the crowd's silence—the particular hush of people reading something that frightens them. They had come to market with their baskets and their bargains and their children tugging their sleeves, and now they stood very still in the gray light, and the stillness spread outward from the post like a stain through water.

They shouldered through the press with a courier's satchel slung across their chest—guild colors hidden under a plain wool coat, because the Salt Guild had survived two hundred years by looking like nothing worth stopping for—and read the proclamation twice.

*By order of the Crown, let it be known that the practice of soul-craft is hereby declared treason against the realm.* The letters were tall and formal, inked by a court scribe who had never seen a hedge-witch pull a fever out of a child or bottle a widow's grief so she could set it down on good days and pick it up on bad ones. *All persons found carrying the gift shall be taken into custody. All persons harboring such persons shall be fined, stripped of property, and held to account. Informants will be rewarded.*

Below that, a list.

The crown's lists were famous for their thoroughness. Names gathered from parish records, from midwives' confessions extracted under gentler kinds of pressure, from the ledgers of arrested cunning-folk who had bought their own necks by selling their clients'. Hazel scanned it with the flat professional attention they gave any inventory, expecting to find neighbors, maybe. The miller's daughter who could calm spooked horses with a touch. Old Sefa at the tannery, whose charms were mostly superstition and salesmanship.

Their own name was fourth from the top.

*ZHOU, HAZEL — Ashgate district. Entered under prior record, year 11 of the old reign.*

Eleven years ago. Eleven years before this hunt existed, before anyone in the capital had cared what a guild runner did between deliveries. Someone had written Hazel's name down when Hazel was nine years old, and the crown had kept it, filed and waiting, like a debt marked *payable upon demand*.

And beneath the name, in smaller script, the notation that made Hazel's hands go cold inside their gloves: *collateral — see attached.*

There was no attachment. There never was, with the crown. Attachments lived in other books, in other hands.

Around them the crowd was beginning to murmur, to break apart, to perform the ordinary business of a market morning with too much care. A woman pulled her child close and walked quickly past the post without looking at it, which was its own kind of looking. An old man spat, thought better of it, and shuffled off toward the fish stalls.

Hazel copied the relevant lines onto a scrap of paper with a stylus that shook only slightly, folded it away, and went to find out what was burning.

* * *

It was the smell that told them before they reached the square. Not woodsmoke—woodsmoke was the smell of every hearth in Ashgate—but something sweeter underneath, something like burnt hair and burnt herbs and something else, older, that Hazel's body recognized long before their mind consent to. The smell of a gift leaving a body the fast way.

They came around the cooper's yard and saw the pyre in the middle of the square, and the woman on it.

She was small. That was the detail that would stay, later, when everything else had blurred: how small she was, how her feet didn't reach the edge of the platform they'd built, how the soldiers had bound her wrists in front of her rather than behind, as if even now they wanted her hands visible, counted, controlled. Her hair had come loose from its braid and hung across her face. She wasn't screaming. She was saying something, over and over, low and rhythmic, and the crowd was too large and too frightened to catch the words, but Hazel caught them, because Hazel knew the cadence. It was a counting rhyme. Hedge-folk used it to steady themselves before working—a way of putting the gift in order, one-two-three-four, everything in its place.

She was doing her craft the only way left to her. She was putting herself in order.

A captain stood at the base of the pyre with a torch not yet lit, reading aloud from a scroll in a voice pitched to carry. "—declared an enemy of the realm and of the natural order. Let all who practice the soul-arts look upon this and understand: the Crown does not negotiate with corruption. The Crown does not bargain."

*Bargain,* Hazel thought, and something in their chest turned over. Everything the guild sold was a bargain. Bottled grief, borrowed luck, second chances wrapped in wax seals and sold at fair rates to people who had nowhere else to take their impossible needs. If soul-craft was treason, then the entire inventory of the Salt Guild was a treason in forty-one crates, and every member of the guild was already guilty, and the only question was the order in which they burned.

The captain touched his torch to the kindling.

The crowd made a sound—not a gasp, exactly; more like the sound a building makes settling, a collective creak of something bearing weight it wasn't built for—and then went quiet again, because quiet was safer. Hazel stayed. Some part of them understood that staying was a debt they owed, that walking away would be a withdrawal from an account they'd need later, when the guilt came calling in the small hours. So they stood in the third row with their hood up and watched a hedge-witch named—she'd have a name, they all had names, the crown's list had names—watched her die in the Ashgate square on a market morning, and memorized the faces of the soldiers, and counted the exits, and did not move until the smoke stopped rising straight and began to lean eastward on the wind.

Then they walked back toward the guildhall at a courier's pace, unhurried, unremarkable, with the proclamation's words folded against their heart like a blade carried edge-inward.

*Collateral — see attached.*

Someone had put them up as surety eleven years ago. And there was only one person alive who had ever handled Hazel's accounts.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of lamp oil and old salt, which was to say it smelled like home, if home were a thing you could audit.

Master Corvane was in the counting room when Hazel came up the back stairs, standing over the winter inventory with his reading spectacles pushed up into his gray hair, and he looked up at Hazel's face and knew. He always knew. Twenty years running the Salt Guild had given him a talent for reading trouble the way Hazel read ledgers—line by line, finding the entry that didn't balance.

"Say it," he said.

"There's a decree posted. Hunt for soul-craft. Treason." Hazel laid the copied scrap on the table between them. "They burned a hedge-witch in the square this morning. Small woman. Didn't scream."

Corvane read the scrap once, slowly, the way he read everything, as though speed were a form of disrespect. Then he took off his spectacles, folded them, and set them down with a small precise click that Hazel had learned, over the years, to dread. The click meant the situation had moved past discussion into arithmetic.

"How many names?" he asked.

"Forty, maybe fifty. Parish folk, mostly. Village cunning-men. People who were never careful." Hazel paused. Their mouth was dry. "And me, master. Fourth from the top."

Something crossed Corvane's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning in dark water. Not surprise. Hazel catalogued it the way they catalogued everything, automatically, helplessly, and hated themselves for noticing, and noticed anyway.

"Not surprised," Hazel said quietly.

"The crown has been compiling lists for years," Corvane said. "Anyone who's paid attention knew it was coming. Your mother paid attention."

"My mother's been dead eleven years."

"Yes." He said it the way he said *yes* to a balanced column: final, factual, closed. Then he turned to the window, where the morning light was coming up thin and cold over the rooftops of Ashgate, and Hazel watched his shoulders settle into some decision he'd apparently begun making before Hazel ever climbed the stairs. "Scatter order. Tonight."

"Master—"

"You'll take the north road with Wren and the Doss twins. Brannick takes south with the strongbox keys. I stay; someone credible has to keep the hall looking ordinary, or we'll have soldiers kicking doors within the week." He turned back, and his eyes behind their tired lids were as steady as they'd ever been, and that steadiness was the thing Hazel had trusted since they were nine years old and orphaned and furious at the world. "Go dark. No guild signs, no guild phrases, no contact for sixty days unless I send word through the tollhouse at Marrow Ford. You remember the protocols."

"I remember the protocols." Hazel's voice came out flatter than they intended. "Master, my name is on that list with a notation. *Collateral, see attached.* Collateral for what? Attached to what? Who entered it?"

For a moment—one breath, no longer—Corvane looked at Hazel the way a man looks at a ledger page he knows contains a figure he cannot explain. Then the look was gone, shuttered, and he was only the guild master again, tired and competent and kind in the ways that cost him least.

"When your mother died, she left instructions," he said. "Sealed ones. I've honored them for eleven years, Hazel, because honoring them kept you safe, and keeping you safe was the last thing she asked of anyone in this hall. I'm not going to unpick that seal on a market morning with soldiers burning witches three streets away. When this blows over—"

"If it blows over."

"*When,*" Corvane said, with the full weight of a man who had survived two reigns and one currency collapse, "then we'll open whatever needs opening. Until then: scatter, go dark, keep the girl close. Wren especially. Do you understand me? Keep Wren close."

"Why Wren especially?"

He was already gathering papers, squaring the winter inventory into bundles tied with plain twine instead of guild ribbon. "Because she's young, she's careless, and she glows," he said shortly. "You've seen it. Anyone with eyes has seen it. Now go. You've a pack to make and a girl to collect, and I have a hall to make boring."

Hazel stood a moment longer in the doorway, holding the question they'd carried up the stairs—*who entered my name, and why does it say collateral*—and watching it fail to find purchase anywhere in the guild master's closed and careful face.

Eleven years ago, their mother had died in this hall, in the room above this one, with Hazel asleep down the corridor and the winter inventory half-audited on the table below. Eleven years ago, someone had written Hazel's name on a crown list that didn't yet exist. Those two facts sat side by side in Hazel's mind like entries in different columns that refused, stubbornly, to sum.

They went downstairs to pack.

* * *

Wren was in the stable loft, which was where Wren always was when the world got loud—curled in the hay above the cart bay with a stolen apple and a book she'd almost certainly not paid for, her boots hanging over the loft edge, swinging in a rhythm that meant she was nervous and hiding it. She was sixteen and quick-boned and quick-eyed, with the kind of face that opened like a door when she laughed, and she glowed. That was the only word for it. Not visibly—no halo, no shimmer—but Hazel had known her three years now and had never once managed to walk past her without feeling warmer, the way you feel warmer near a banked fire. Luck pooled around Wren like water around a spring. Bad things happened near her less often than probability allowed, and everyone in the guild had quietly arranged their lives to happen near her as much as possible.

"Rumor says there was a burning," Wren said, before Hazel's head cleared the loft floor. She didn't look up from her book. "Rumor also says you went to look. You always go to look. It's going to get you killed someday, going to look."

"It might get me killed today." Hazel swung up into the loft and sat beside her, and told her. All of it—the decree, the list, the hedge-witch and her counting rhyme, the scatter order, the north road. Wren listened the way she did everything, completely, her apple forgotten, her boots gone still.

"They burned her for bottling things," Wren said at last. Her voice was very level, which was worse than shaking. "That's all most of them do. Bottle things. Grief, mostly. Fear. My—" She stopped. Started again. "People just want somewhere to put the heavy stuff."

"I know."

"And we're on the list. All of us. Even you." Wren finally looked at her then, and her eyes were bright and fierce and sixteen years old, and Hazel felt the old oath move in their chest, the one they'd sworn silently over Wren's sleeping form three winters back, unwitnessed, unrecorded, the one oath in Hazel's life that appeared in no ledger anywhere: *not this one. Whatever it costs, whoever asks—not this one.* "Fourth from the top, you said. Why fourth from the top, Haz? You hide it better than anyone I know. Better than me. Nobody should even know you've got the gift."

"I don't know," Hazel said, and it was almost true. What they knew—what they suspected, what had been sitting in their chest since the market post like swallowed ice—was that nobody writes a name on a list eleven years early out of carelessness. Somebody had done it deliberately. Somebody had entered Hazel Zhou into the crown's records the same year their mother died, and marked them *collateral*, and collateral meant one thing in every accounting system Hazel had ever learned:

Somebody had used them to secure a debt.

"Wren," they said. "When we go north. If anything happens—if we're separated, if anyone asks you questions—you run. You don't negotiate, you don't explain, you don't be brave. You run, and you don't stop until Marrow Ford, and you ask for the tollkeeper."

"You'll be right behind me."

"That's not what I said."

"That's what you meant." Wren smiled, and the loft got warmer, and outside the stable window the smoke from the square was still leaning eastward over the roofs of Ashgate, thinning now, dispersing, becoming the kind of smell that by tomorrow the town would pretend it had never noticed. "You always mean it and you never say it. It's very annoying. Pack your bag, Haz. I'll get the horses."

She dropped lightly from the loft and was gone, her footsteps quick and certain down the aisle, and Hazel sat alone in the hay with the last of the morning light and let themselves, finally, think the thought whole:

*Mother. What did you sell, and what did you buy with it—and why does the crown's own list say I was the payment?*

Down in the hall, faintly, Master Corvane began moving through the counting room below, blowing out lamps one by one, putting the Salt Guild to sleep for the winter. And somewhere far beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the mountain itself, Hazel felt—for the first time in eleven years—something answer the question.

Not in words. In weight. Like a ledger opening somewhere in the dark, and a dry voice, patient as interest accruing, saying: *Ah. There you are. We've been carrying your account a long time, little one. Come and see what your mother signed.*
