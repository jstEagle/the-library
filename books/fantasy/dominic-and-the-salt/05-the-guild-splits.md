## 5. The Guild Splits

Brannick had counted votes before he could count coin.

That was what growing up in the Salt Guild did to a person. His father had been a tallyman for the old master, back when the guild's ledgers lived in wax-sealed chests and the worst thing anyone traded was a bad winter. He remembered sitting under the tally-table with his chin on his knees while the members argued above him, learning the music of it: who cleared their throat before lying, who went quiet when they meant yes, who said *the guild must survive* in the same tone other men said *I am afraid*. By nine he could call a vote from the sound of the room alone. By twelve he had learned that being right about the vote and being safe after it were two different skills, and that most men only ever learned one.

He stood now at the back of the guild hall with his arms crossed and his boots planted on cold flagstone, listening to thirty voices argue over whether to hand a child to the crown, and he called the vote before anyone had raised a hand.

It would fail. Hazel would lose. And the room already knew it, which was why they couldn't look at each other.

* * *

The hall had not been built for this many people this frightened. It was an old granary once, before the guild bought it and gutted it—thick stone walls, a roof of black timber beams, windows slitted high like a fortress deciding against trust. The Salt Guild's wealth hung around them on every side: shelves of inventory pulled down from the counting-house and stacked in hasty rows, because when Master Corvane ordered everyone dark, *dark* meant everything. Bottled grief in blue glass, stoppered with lead. Borrowed luck in clay jars stamped with the guild's salt-crook mark, each one faintly warm to the touch, like bread taken too soon from an oven. Second chances, which looked like nothing at all—small paper twists that weighed less than their own promise. The whole impossible inventory of the guild, crammed into a room where thirty mercenaries, runners, tallymen, and fence-brokers were trying to decide how much a girl was worth.

Wren herself was not in the room. That had been decided before the deciding began. She'd been sent upstairs to the dormer she shared with two other runners, told to bar her door and count the knots in the floorboards until someone came for her. Brannick had watched her go. Sixteen years old, all elbows and quick feet, and she'd walked up those stairs like a soldier walking to a wall she'd been assigned to hold. She hadn't cried. He almost wished she had. A crying child was easier to fail than a composed one.

"Read it again." That was Osla Vane, arms folded over her chest, gray braid like a rope over one shoulder. "The whole proclamation. Let everyone hear what we're choosing between."

Master Corvane stood at the head table with the broadsheet flat under his palms. He was not a large man; he had never needed to be. Forty years of running the Salt Guild had given him the particular authority of someone who knew where every debt was buried, including everyone's. His voice, when he read, was dry and even, a voice made for interest rates.

"'By order of the Crown, and by warrant of the Court Beneath the Mountain, let it be known that any soul found carrying the gift of soul-craft—the shaping, binding, bottling, or borrowing of soul-stuff—is declared forfeit. Any who harbor such a one partake of their crime. Any who surrender such a one shall be credited four hundred crowns and the Crown's further consideration.'" He let the last words sit. *Further consideration.* In the language of ledgers, that phrase had teeth.

"And there's the list," said Tam Wicker from near the door, a young mercenary with a burn-scarred jaw. "Attached names. They've got names."

"We know they have names," Osla said. "We've had three days of knowing."

"I want to hear mine read out," Tam said, stubborn. "I want to know if I'm worth four hundred crowns or just the burning."

Corvane looked down the page. Brannick watched him do it and saw something flicker across the old man's face—not fear exactly, more like a man finding an entry in his own books that he didn't remember writing. It was gone in half a breath.

"The attached list is long," Corvane said. "It includes hedge-witches, charm-mongers, luck-brokers, and—" a pause, small enough that only someone trained in pauses would catch it, "—several names associated with this guild. Which is why we are here."

"Say hers," Tam said. "Say the runner's name."

"Wren," Corvane said quietly. "Yes. Her name is on the list."

The hall exhaled. Thirty people breathing out at once sounded, Brannick thought, like wind through a keyhole.

He should say something now. He knew the shape of the argument; he'd built it himself over three sleepless nights, turning it over the way a man turns a coin he suspects is clipped. Surrender Wren and buy the guild its life. Four hundred crowns, plus *consideration*—plus, if you were honest, the crown's gratitude, which in a purge season was worth more than gold. It was arithmetic. It was even good arithmetic. Wren was one life against perhaps forty; Wren had no family in the guild, no patron powerful enough to bleed for her; Wren had, by every measure the guild used, the least weight on the scale.

And none of it mattered, because Brannick had delivered packages with her. Because last spring she'd carried a jar of borrowed luck through a flooded quarter and come back soaked to the ribs and grinning, saying the river owed her one now, she'd keep the receipt. Because she called him *old bear* when she thought he couldn't hear, and he had pretended not to hear because hearing it was the best thing that had happened to him in a year. Because the guild was supposed to be the place where nobody got spent.

Loyalty, he was beginning to understand, rots from the inside out, and the rot smells like reason.

"Hazel will speak against," Corvane said. "They've asked for the floor."

* * *

Brannick watched Hazel cross the hall, and hated how much he admired the walk.

They came unhurried, ledger under one arm—they always had a ledger; the guild joked that Hazel would be buried with one open on their chest—and stopped not at the head table but a few paces into the crowd, so that no one could look anywhere else without effort. Their face was the face they wore for audits: calm, exact, a little tired, the face of someone doing sums rather than begging.

"You've heard the numbers," Hazel said. "Four hundred crowns. Consideration. One name surrendered buys the rest of us a season's safety—or so the crown's paper promises. I've read the same paper you have. I've also read others." They opened the ledger. Brannick's stomach tightened; he knew that ledger. Everyone did. It was the oldest book the guild kept, the mother-book, the one with entries going back past living memory, and Hazel was the only soul allowed to touch it. "This is the guild's own record. Three hundred years of transactions. Would anyone like to guess how many times in those three hundred years the Salt Guild sold a person?"

Silence.

"Twice," Hazel said. "Both times the entry is marked *settled in full*, and both times, within ten years, the guild lost everything anyway. Once to fire. Once to the crown of that era, which took the money, took the person, and burned us regardless. Selling a soul has never once bought a guild its safety. It buys a season. Then the crown learns that we can be bargained with, and next year the price is a runner, and the year after it's a tallyman, and the year after that it's whoever's standing closest to the door when the soldiers knock. We are not buying safety. We are teaching them our price."

"They'll find her either way," said Marta Quill, the fence-broker, not unkindly. Marta had buried a husband and two sons to crown purges in another province; her hardness was grief wearing armor. "You know it. I know it. If we don't give her up clean, with terms, they take her dirty and burn the hall besides."

"If we give her up," Hazel said, "we confirm that the guild breaks its oaths when pressed. Every contract we hold is worth the paper it's written on only because the other party believes we don't sell our people. You want to talk about the ledger? Fine. Look at the ledger. Our entire trade runs on trust that we honor debts. Wren is not a debt. She is one of ours."

"She's also marked," said Tam Wicker. "Whatever the bell ringer saw in her—that client who let her walk—it's known. She carries the gift. Carrying it is the crime."

"So do I," Hazel said.

The silence afterward had a different texture. Brannick felt the whole hall flinch at once, a single animal.

Everyone had suspected, of course. You didn't work beside someone for years without noticing the small wrongnesses: the way bottled grief went quiet when Hazel handled it, the way their shadow sat a half-second behind them on bright days, the way luck-jars warmed when they passed. Suspecting was survivable. Hearing it was not.

"That's not the same," Marta said slowly. "Yours is—yours is bookkeeper's craft. Small things. Hers is—" She stopped.

"Mine is the gift," Hazel said. "There aren't sizes of it. There's only having it and not having it, and what the people who don't have it decide to do about those who do." They turned, slow, taking in the room. "So here is my proposal, since surrender is on the table. Write my name down instead. I'm older. I'm listed higher—I've seen the list, my name sits at the top of it, dated eleven years ago, which means the crown wants me more than it wants her, which means my surrender buys more. Give them me, and ask terms for Wren's passage out of the city. That is the honest version of the bargain you're considering. If you're willing to spend one of us, spend the one who costs more."

"No," Brannick said.

He hadn't planned to speak. The word came out of him like a splinter working free, and thirty heads turned toward the back of the hall, toward the big scarred man by the door whom everyone knew as Hazel's rival, the one who'd called their bookkeeping cowardice to their face more than once.

"No?" Hazel said, and there was something underneath the word, something Brannick couldn't name and didn't have time to.

"No," he said again, louder, pushing off the wall. "Because that's not a counter-offer, it's the same rot wearing different clothes. You're both doing sums with people. She's sixteen." His voice cracked on it and he let it. "And because I've heard this argument before. My father tallied for the old master, and I sat under the table during the vote when they sold the ferryman's daughter to settle the Ashgate debt, and everyone said the same things—arithmetic, seasons, survival—and I'll tell you what happened. The buyers came back in four years for more. They always come back for more. The guild that sells one soul is a guild that has already sold all of them; it just hasn't collected yet."

"So we die together instead?" Marta said. "Sentiment makes poor armor."

"Not sentiment." Brannick looked at her, then at Corvane, then at Hazel, whose eyes hadn't left him since he'd spoken. "Memory. I remember what this guild was. If we vote to hand her over, then whatever we are after tonight isn't the guild. It's just a gang with better stationery. Vote however you like. But don't call it survival. Call it spending."

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. He had heard the room at nine years old and he heard it now: the throat-clearing liars, the quiet yesses, the fear dressed as prudence. He called the vote before Corvane could.

Hands rose one by one, grim and deliberate. Marta. Tam. Osla, last and slowest, her eyes closed. When it was done, twenty-one hands stood against eight, and Corvane wrote the number in his book with a pen that scratched like a rat in a wall.

"The guild votes surrender," the master said. "Terms to be negotiated with the Crown's officers at first light. Wren is to remain confined until then. No one speaks to her of this." He closed the book. "Meeting ends."

Brannick caught Hazel's eye across the dying room. He expected anger. What he saw was worse: Hazel looked at him the way a tallyman looks at a column that finally adds up—as if Brannick's objection had confirmed something they'd already calculated, something with a date on it.

Then the crowd swallowed them both, and the candles started going out.

* * *

He drank alone in his rooms above the stable, which was where he did all his drinking, because the Salt House tavern had become a place where men avoided his eyes. The vote sat in him like a swallowed hook. Twenty-one to eight. He turned the numbers over and over and they wouldn't change, and somewhere past midnight he understood that he wasn't grieving the outcome—he'd known the outcome before the meeting started, had *counted* it before it started, the way he'd counted votes under his father's table. What he was grieving was the room itself. The smell of it. Fear has a smell, and tonight it had been everywhere, in the wool coats and the hot candle air, and he understood that he would never again stand in that hall and hear the old music. The song had changed key. From tonight forward, every vote would be sung in the new one.

Somewhere below, in the dormers, a sixteen-year-old girl was counting knots in the floorboards, trusting the people who had voted to sell her.

He set down the cup. He would go to her. Damn the master's orders—someone had to tell her, someone who wasn't a stranger with rope, someone who could put her out the coal-chute and onto the south road before first light. Terms, Corvane wanted. Brannick would show the crown some terms: terms involving distance.

He was pulling his coat from its peg when he saw the light under his own door was wrong.

His rooms opened onto the stable gallery, a plank walkway open to the night, and his door had no window—but the gap beneath it glowed faintly, a pale shimmer, like moonlight trapped indoors. Except the sky was overcast, and had been for days.

He stood very still. Old instinct, older than the guild: the stillness of a man who has heard the click of a trap.

He crossed the boards silently, put his eye to the crack, and saw nothing but his own dark room and, on the floor inside, a thin line of light where no light should be. He pushed the door open.

The sigil was drawn on his threshold, inside, in a substance that gleamed like wet salt and gave off a light with no source he could find: a circle broken by a crooked vertical stroke—the salt-crook of the guild's mark, but wrong, inverted, the hook turned downward like a claw. Around it ran a ring of characters in no alphabet he knew, though his eyes slid along them the way fingers slide on ice. He knew what it was anyway. Every old member knew. It hung in the corner of the guild's oldest tapestry, half-faded, beside a motto nobody translated anymore. The god's mark. The silent god's old sigil, the one from before the guild stopped swearing oaths aloud and started pretending it never had.

It was not addressed to the guild.

It was on *his* threshold. His door. His name, unspoken but unmistakable—a message, or a summons, or a claim.

Brannick backed out onto the gallery, one slow step, two, his heart hammering like a fist on wood. The night was black and still, snow beginning to fall in fat silent flakes, and the stable yard below lay empty—except that it didn't feel empty. It felt *watched*, the specific pressure of attention between his shoulder blades, vast and patient and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with appetite.

Down the gallery, a door opened. Hazel stepped out into the snow, wrapped in a cloak, a hooded lantern in hand—and stopped when they saw him standing there. Their gaze went from his face to his doorway, to the pale glow spilling over the threshold, and Brannick watched recognition move across their features like frost crossing glass.

"You see it too," he said. His voice came out rough. "Hazel. What is it? What does it mean?"

Hazel didn't answer at once. The lantern trembled, just slightly, in their grip. When they finally spoke, they spoke quietly, as if to the falling snow.

"It means it's not silent anymore," they said. "And it means we have until morning. Maybe less. Get your coat, Brannick. Whatever you were planning to do tonight—we're out of time to do it alone."
