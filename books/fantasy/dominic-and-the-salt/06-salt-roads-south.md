## 6. Salt Roads South

The smugglers' road began where the king's road ended, which was to say it began in mud.

Wren had run deliveries for the Salt Guild since she was nine years old—first on foot with parcels strapped under her coat, then on a borrowed mule named Clementine who had died fat and lazy in a warm stable two winters back—and she knew every courier's trick there was to know. Travel at dusk and dawn. Sleep in noon's blind spot. Never take the same door twice. But she had never traveled south of Ashgate, never past the tollstones into the hill country where the roads weren't roads at all but suggestions, and the people who walked them had faces like closed shutters.

Hazel walked ahead of her through the grey morning, hood up, pack riding high between their shoulder blades, and Wren watched them the way she'd been watching them for three days now: sideways, in glances, cataloguing.

Something was wrong with Hazel. Not wounded-wrong, not scared-wrong. Something quieter and worse. They moved like a person carrying a full cup of water across a shaking floor. Every step measured. Every breath rationed.

Three days ago Hazel had come for her in the guild hall's back corridor, past midnight, and said only, *We're leaving now, and you don't get to ask why until we're over the tollstones.* And Wren—who had spent her whole life learning that when an adult's voice went flat like that, obedience was the only coin worth holding—had gone upstairs, taken her bundle from under the floorboard, and kissed the doorframe of her little room goodbye without letting herself think about it too hard.

Now the tollstones were behind them. The hills rose brown and wet ahead. And still no questions asked, which was starting to feel less like patience and more like a debt accruing interest somewhere in her chest.

"You're limping," she said, because it was easier than what she wanted to say.

"I'm favoring my left knee. It's a strategy."

"It's a limp."

"Then I'm a strategist with a limp." Hazel didn't turn around, but Wren caught the ghost of a smile in the set of their shoulders, and something in her unclenched half a notch. "How are your feet?"

"Blisters on blisters. They're keeping each other company."

"Good. Pain you understand is better than pain you don't." Hazel said things like that sometimes, small grim proverbs, and Wren had always assumed they were jokes. Lately she'd started to suspect they were inventory—little truths Hazel carried the way the guild carried its goods, weighed and priced and stored against some future need.

They crested the ridge as the sun came up properly, and below them the valley opened: a river the color of old pewter, alder woods smoking with mist, and along the far bank a scatter of buildings with tarred roofs and no chimneys lit. A smuggler's haven did not announce itself with smoke. Smoke could be counted from a distance, and everything about these people was designed not to be counted.

"The Ferryman's Stair," Hazel said. "My mother used to trade here."

There it was again—the mother. Hazel had spoken of her twice since they'd fled, both times the same way, brief and careful, like touching a bruise to check if it still hurt. *My mother used to know this crossing. My mother kept accounts with the salt-smugglers.* Wren had never heard Hazel speak of family before. The guild raised everyone, that was its whole point; you were nobody's daughter or son, you were the guild's, and asking after origins was one of the many small rudenesses you learned early not to commit.

But Hazel kept saying *my mother* like a key turning in locks down the length of this road.

* * *

The man who met them at the river's edge was called Ossian, and he was missing three fingers on his left hand and most of his curiosity entirely. He looked at Hazel for a long moment across the water, and then he laughed—a dry, rusted sound—and said something in the low cant of the river folk that Wren only half followed.

"She said you'd come," Ossian translated, or perhaps repeated, switching back to the common tongue. "Years ago. Said, *if ever a tall child comes walking south wearing my stubbornness on their face, you put them in the good boat.*"

Hazel went very still.

"He means me," Wren realized, and then, absurdly, "I'm not tall."

"You're tall for the work," Ossian said, and waved them into the boat.

The good boat turned out to be a flat-bottomed ferry poled by a woman with white braids and forearms like anchor chain, and the crossing took the better part of the morning because the river was lazy and the woman refused to hurry it. Halfway across, in water so still the hills hung upside down beneath them, the woman spoke for the first time.

"Villalobos's kid," she said, looking at Hazel with an expression Wren couldn't read. Not pity. Something closer to accounting. "You have her hands."

"You knew her?" Hazel's voice came out carefully level, and Wren heard the effort in it, the way you hear the strain in a rope holding weight.

"Knew her? Girl, I ferried her forty times if I ferried her once. Always southbound. Always alone. Always paying double and never haggling, which told us more than anything she ever said." The woman planted her pole and leaned into it. "She bought passage for others, near the end. Quiet ones. Moved them north while she went south herself. Last time I saw her, she gave me a coin and said, *when my children come this way—and they will, one day, because I've written it so—carry them gently.*" She shrugged, vast shoulders rolling. "So we carry you gently. That's all any of us can do for the dead. Carry what they handed us."

Wren watched Hazel absorb this the way stone absorbs rain—nothing showing on the surface, everything soaking in. She looked away, out over the inverted hills, and felt the question she'd been swallowing for three days rise up her throat like floodwater.

*What did your mother do? What did she sell? Who were the quiet ones?*

She didn't ask. But she was beginning to understand that the answer had been paid for already, long ago, by a woman going south alone, and that Wren was sitting in a boat that woman had bought.

* * *

They traded favors the way other travelers traded coin.

That was how the smugglers' economy worked, Ossian explained over a supper of eel stew in a low room that smelled of tar and tallow: coin marked you, marked where you'd been and what you'd bought, but a favor left no ledger anyone could seize. You carried a sealed box to a widow in Harrow Fen and didn't ask what was inside. You sat up all night in a doorway watching for riders. You remembered a face and lied about having seen it. Every favor earned you a step further south and a name whispered to the next contact down the line, and the line ran, broken and winding, all the way to the mountain country.

"And beyond?" Hazel asked.

Ossian chewed his eel slowly. "Beyond is the court's shadow. Smugglers go under the mountain, sure—we do, sometimes, for goods worth the risk. But the under-court doesn't trade in favors, friend. It trades in debts. And debts don't stay paid. You want to reach it, you follow the old debtor's doors, and you pray whatever you owe when you knock is smaller than what they say you owe." He tapped the stumps of his missing fingers against the table, one, two, three, like counting. "I knocked once. This was my receipt."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The fire ticked. Somewhere above them a gull argued with the night.

Later, in the loft where they'd been given blankets, Wren lay listening to Hazel's breathing even out into sleep, and stared at the black rafters, and finally let herself think the thing she had been not-thinking since Ashgate.

She remembered the court.

Not the way you remember a story someone told you, all edges softened and details invented. She remembered it the way you remember your own kitchen—the specific cold of it, deep-earth cold that never varied, and the shelves, oh, the shelves, rising up into dark taller than any cathedral, and the sound the filed souls made when the air moved through them: a thin, continuous ringing, like ten thousand wet fingers circling the rims of ten thousand glasses. She had been small. She had been standing in an aisle of names. Someone had been reading hers aloud, slowly, tasting it.

She had told no one. Not the guild matron who'd found her wandering the mountain road at age six, barefoot, silent, with no history anyone could find. Not the other runners. Not Hazel, though Hazel of all of them had never pressed her about the before-time, had maybe understood without being told that some ledgers open onto pages you can't survive reading twice.

And she had never once said aloud the thing underneath the memory, the thing that made it unbearable:

*They sold me. Somebody chose to. And whoever it was got something they wanted badly enough.*

* * *

Two days further south, in a coal town built into the ribs of a dead mine, the hunters passed through.

Wren felt them before she saw them—felt it in the way the market square emptied, stalls folding up like startled wings, mothers calling children indoors with a particular brightness in their voices that meant fear. Then came the sound: hooves, many, unhurried. Hunters never hurried. Hurrying admitted urgency, and urgency admitted prey.

She and Hazel flattened themselves in the mouth of an alley between a chandler's shop and a shrine-house—abandoned, its little god's niche swept empty, because the crown had been emptying shrines for years—and watched the column ride past. Twelve riders in grey-and-gold. And walking among the horses, leashed not with rope but with something that made Wren's teeth ache to look at, a figure in a hood whose presence bent the air the way heat bends it over a forge.

"A finder," Hazel breathed. "They've fielded a finder."

"What does it find?"

"People like us. Like—" Hazel stopped. In the alley's shadow, their eyes found Wren's, and something passed across their face that she had never seen there before: guilt, naked and terrible. "Like you, Wren. It finds the gift. That's why we keep off the main roads. That's why—"

"That's why you really pulled me out of the guild hall," Wren said quietly. "Not because I'm a runner who knows the southern routes. Because I'm on the list."

Hazel said nothing. Which was an answer.

The finder's head turned as the column passed—a slow, blind rotation, like a compass needle hunting true north—and for one suspended moment Wren was certain it would stop, certain the hood would tilt toward the alley, toward her, toward the gift curled small and hot behind her sternum that she had spent ten years pretending wasn't there. Her hand found Hazel's sleeve without deciding to.

Then the column rode on, iron shoes striking sparks off the cobbles, and the finder's attention slid past like water over glass, and the alley exhaled.

"They'll circle back," Hazel murmured. "Finders sweep in spirals. We have until tomorrow, maybe less. There's a smuggler cache outside town—an old woman named Maret who owed my mother. We make her by dark, and we're in the high country before the spiral tightens."

They moved. Of course they moved. But that night, walking the last miles through pine-dark with the finder's needle-turning hood printed on the inside of Wren's eyelids, she found the fear had done something strange to her: it had burned away the last of her hesitation like fog off a field.

"Maret's house first," she said. "And then I'm telling you something, Hazel. Tonight. On the road, where you can't look at me while I do it."

* * *

Maret's house was a turf-roofed hovel dug into a hillside, smelling of peat smoke and dried fish, and Maret herself was tiny and ancient and took one look at Hazel and burst into tears—which flustered Hazel so completely that Wren had to hide a smile even through her terror. Maret fed them barley soup and hid their packs and told Hazel four separate stories about their mother, each more incriminating than the last (smuggled a cursed crown piece through three kingdoms; talked a river spirit out of drowning a village; won a knife-fight at seventy without drawing), and then, mercifully, slept.

Hazel walked out to check the perimeter. Wren followed.

The high country spread before them, moonlit and treeless, the mountain itself just visible on the southern horizon—a black shape darker than the sky, crowned with a smudge of permanent cloud. Somewhere under it, the shelves. Somewhere under it, her name, filed and ringing.

"Hazel." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. She started over. "I remember it. The court. Under the mountain."

Hazel went absolutely still beside her, and in that stillness Wren understood that this was not news—not wholly—and that understanding broke something open in her chest, and she kept talking anyway, fast, before the crack could seal.

"I was six, or I was six-ish, I don't know, time down there doesn't—there are shelves, miles of them, and the souls ring when the air moves, and mine was there, I *heard my own name*, somebody was reading it out loud like a price. And I got out, or I was let out, or I was *returned*, and I've never known which, and I never told anyone because—" Her breath hitched. "Because if I said it out loud, it would mean it was real, and if it was real, then someone sold me, and if someone sold me, then everyone who was kind to me afterward might have been—" 

She stopped. The moonlight lay on the ground like spilled salt.

"Wren." Hazel's voice was very quiet. "Whoever sold you—I've been reading their handwriting for eleven years."

The world tilted. Wren grabbed Hazel's arm without meaning to. "What are you saying."

"I'm saying your soul is in the under-court's books because my mother put it there. Payment deferred. Collateral named." A breath, ragged at the edges. "The collateral is me. She traded your soul to buy my name off the hunt list, and I found the entry three days before I woke you, and I should have told you in the boat, I should have told you at the river, and I didn't, because I'm a coward and because—" Their voice failed and restarted. "Because I needed you to trust me first. So you'd hear it from me and not from them."

The silence that followed lasted a hundred heartbeats. Wren counted them. It was what she did; she was a runner, and running meant counting distance, and right now distance was the only honest thing left.

"So all of it," she said at last, hollow. "The extra bread in my satchel. Teaching me the cipher. Coming for me first, that night. All of it was—"

"Debt," Hazel said. "Yes. At first. And then—" They turned to face her fully, and Wren saw that their eyes were wet and furious, mostly at themselves. "And then you were Wren. Fastest runner the guild ever had, funnier than me, braver than me, and I would burn every ledger in that hall before I let them file you on a shelf. The debt was the door, Wren. You're the reason I stayed in the room."

Far to the south, the cloud-crown of the mountain flickered. Not lightning. Nothing weather-made flickers green.

Wren looked at it, and at Hazel, and felt the strangest sensation settle over her—not forgiveness, not yet, that would take longer—but recognition. Two damaged entries in someone else's book, standing together in the dark, deciding what they owed each other and to whom.

"Then we're agreed," she said. "We steal me back."

"We steal you back."

The green light flickered again, closer than it should have been, off the mountain and out on the moorland now, drifting, patient, like something following a scent it had been promised. And Wren—whose gift had been whispering warnings her whole life and who had learned, at last, in the last year, to listen—heard it whisper now, clear as a bell struck in an empty room:

*It knows your name. Come alone, it says. Or bring no one at all.*
