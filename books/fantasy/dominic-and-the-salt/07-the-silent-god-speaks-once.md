## 7. The Silent God Speaks Once

The shrine found them before they found it.

Hazel had been walking the salt road south for six days, and for six days the god had been quiet in the way it was always quiet—not absent but *withheld*, the way a creditor withholds a letter. You could feel the envelope sitting unopened in some drawer of the world. Wren had stopped asking about it somewhere past the second night, when Hazel caught themselves answering a question no one had asked, aloud, to the dark: *"I know. I know you're there."*

Now the road bent along a dry riverbed where the salt had bloomed white up through the cracked clay, and there it stood—a ring of standing stones gone soft-edged with centuries of wind, a broken dome of masonry half-swallowed by the earth, and above the doorway, carved so deep the shadows still held their shape, the sigil.

The closed eye. The open hand beneath it.

Hazel had seen that sigil twice in their life before this. Once inked on their mother's wrist. Once scratched into the door of their room three nights ago, by someone in the guild who wanted them dead or gone or both.

"Is that—" Wren started.

"Yes."

"It's ugly," she said, which was her way of being afraid. At fourteen she had already learned that fear sounded better disguised as opinion. She shifted the bundle on her back—their whole winter now: two blankets, a pan, a brick of pressed tea, the mother's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against Hazel's spine. "Are we going in?"

Hazel looked at the doorway. Something in their chest leaned toward it the way a thirsty animal leans toward a sound of water, and that lean frightened them more than the soldiers behind or the mountain ahead. Oaths are supposed to sit in you like set bone. This one was *pulling*.

"It wants us to," they said. "That's reason enough not to."

They made camp anyway, because the light was failing and the riverbed gave cover from the road, and because—this was the truth Hazel did not say—they were tired of running from a thing that could apparently reach them anywhere. Better to let it come at a time of their choosing. Or near enough. They built a small fire in the lee of a fallen stone, and Wren laid out tea like ritual, and for an hour the world was almost ordinary: the wind worrying the salt flowers, the stars coming out hard and cold over the ridge, Wren telling a story about a tollkeeper's dog she'd once bribed with dried fish.

Then the fire went out.

Not down. *Out*—all at once, mid-flame, as if the fire itself had been called away. The cold arrived a heartbeat later, a cold with weight to it, pressing on Hazel's shoulders like hands.

"Wren." Their voice came out flat and strange. "Stay awake. Whatever happens, stay awake, and if I stop talking—if my mouth keeps moving but it isn't me saying things—you run south and don't look back."

"Hazel—"

"*Run.*"

The darkness by the broken doorway deepened. Not shadow exactly; more like a place where the night had been *kept*, hoarded for years and finally spent. And out of it came a voice that Hazel felt in their teeth before they heard it in their ears—a voice like a ledger opening, dry pages turning, a voice that had been silent so long it had forgotten how to be anything but enormous.

**LITTLE SCRIBE.**

Wren made a small sound and scrambled upright, and Hazel threw out an arm to keep her behind them, heart hammering against the oilcloth-wrapped ledger as though it could shield them both.

"You've been quiet," Hazel said. Eleven years of silence, and all they managed was an accountant's complaint. "Eleven years."

**I HAVE BEEN EATING.**

* * *

It did not speak the vision so much as pour it.

One moment Hazel was kneeling at a dead fire; the next they were elsewhere, and the elsewhere was dark—not the friendly dark of a cellar but the dark of *underneath*, of a place that had never been promised morning. They stood—or floated, or were held—in a vast low chamber floored with something smooth and warm as skin. And everywhere, filling the dark the way silt fills a river, were people kneeling.

Hundreds. Thousands. Kneeling in ranks that receded beyond sight, each one bowed forward, forehead to the floor, motionless. They wore the clothes of every decade Hazel had ever read about in the old ledgers—and clothes older than the ledgers, clothes from before the guild, before the crown, before writing maybe. Servants. Sworn servants. Hazel knew them without being told, the way you know your own handwriting: these were the ones who had taken the oath. The open hand. The closed eye. *I give my service and my self, kept and spent according to need.*

Some of them were translucent. That was the horror of it—that was the thing Hazel's mind kept slipping off of like a wet rope. The nearest figure, a woman in a mercenary's coat decades out of fashion, was worn thin as paper held to light. Hazel could see the floor through her shoulder. Through her *kneeling*, which should have been impossible, which meant the kneeling wasn't happening with knees anymore, wasn't happening with anything, was just the shape her devotion had hardened into after everything else had been drunk away.

And between the ranks moved the god.

Hazel never saw it whole. They saw what it passed through: a brightness that traveled the dark like lamplight under a door, pausing at one kneeling servant, then another. Where it paused, the servant grew thinner. Not quickly. Patiently. The way interest accrues. The woman in the old coat flickered as it fed, and her lips were moving, moving, moving—praying, Hazel realized. Still praying. Still grateful, maybe, in whatever remained of her. *Thank you. Thank you. Thank you,* said the lips, as the god drank the last color out of her hands.

**THEY SWORE,** the voice said, everywhere, gentle as a ledger line. **A GOD MUST HONOR A SWEAR. THEY GAVE THEIR SELVES TO BE KEPT AND SPENT ACCORDING TO NEED. THERE HAS BEEN NEED FOR A VERY LONG TIME, LITTLE SCRIBE.**

"Stop." Hazel's own voice, ragged, in the dead-fire dark of the riverbed—they could feel both places at once now, the cold stones under their real knees and the warm flesh-floor under their dreamed ones. "Show me what you want. Stop dressing it up."

The vision tightened. The god's brightness turned—there was no head to turn, but it *turned*—and came toward Hazel through the kneeling ranks, and as it came, the servants it passed lifted their faces.

Every face was a face Hazel knew.

Brannick, gray-eyed and sneering, kneeling with his throat offered up. The guild master, Vess Corrane, whose ledgers Hazel balanced every quarter, whose tea they brewed. The hedge-witch from Ashgate square, the one the soldiers had burned—her face intact here, unburned here, eyes wide and empty as coin slots. And at the end of the rank, thinnest of all, worn almost to a breath: a woman with Hazel's own jawline and a scar through one eyebrow, and on her wrist the sigil inked in faded brown.

"Mother." The word fell out of them like something dropped down a well.

**SHE WAS THE BEST OF THEM,** the god said, with something that in any other mouth would have been tenderness. **SHE KEPT MY BOOKS BEAUTIFULLY. SHE PAID ON TIME. SHE IS NEARLY FINISHED, AND WHEN SHE IS FINISHED HER DEBT TRANSFERS, AS CONTRACTED, TO HER COLLATERAL. THAT IS YOU.**

The vision began to close like a hand. In the last instant Hazel saw the ranks of the kneeling stretch away into the dark farther than any guild hall, any kingdom, any ledger could hold—and understood, with a lurch like a stair missing in the night, how many oaths had been sworn over how many centuries, and how many were still being paid.

Then they were on the riverbank, coughing, hands in cold ash, and the stars were back, and Wren was gripping their arm hard enough to bruise.

* * *

"What did it show you?" Wren asked, after Hazel had drunk half a skin of water and stopped shaking—or mostly stopped; their hands kept up a fine tremor they couldn't tax out of them.

"Servants." Hazel stared at the dead fire. "Kneeling. Being consumed. Slowly. For years. My mother among them." They laughed, and hated the sound. "She sold a soul to the court under the mountain to keep my name off the hunt list. I thought that was the worst entry in her book. It turns out she'd already given herself to something worse, years before, and the contract just—transferred. Like debt. Like everything in this world is debt."

Wren was quiet. Then, carefully: "My parents swore to something. Before they sold me. I don't remember what it looked like. I remember kneeling." Her voice stayed level the way a frozen pond stays level, and Hazel didn't dare step on it.

"I'm sorry," they said, because there was nothing else that was true.

"Don't be sorry. Tell me what it wants."

What it wanted, it told them while Wren dozed at last against the fallen stone—exhaustion dragging her under despite everything, the way it drags children under, thank all possible gods for that mercy. It waited until her breathing went slow and even, and then the darkness by the doorway gathered again, smaller this time, almost courteous.

**I WILL MAKE YOU AN OFFER, LITTLE SCRIBE.**

"Say it."

**THE COURT BELOW HOLDS A SOUL OF YOURS. WREN, FILED AND SEALED. YOU MEAN TO STEAL IT—I HAVE WATCHED YOU PLAN IT IN YOUR LEDGER MIND EVERY NIGHT SINCE ASHGATE. YOU WILL FAIL. THE COURT'S BOOKS ARE OLDER THAN MY BOOKS, AND ITS DOORS DO NOT OPEN FOR THIEVES.**

"And you can open them?"

**NO. BUT I CAN OPEN *YOU*.** The darkness pulsed, slow, like a heart deciding. **YOU ARE THE LAST SERVANT LEFT WHO CAN STILL SWEAR. THE OTHERS ARE SPENT OR SCATTERED OR BURNED BY YOUR CROWN'S SOLDIERS. WITHOUT A LIVING VOICE TO FEED ME, I STARVE, AND WHEN I STARVE COMPLETELY, EVERYTHING I KEEP GOES WITH ME—EVERY SOUL IN MY HOLDING, INCLUDING THE THIN REMNANT OF YOUR MOTHER, WHICH I COULD OTHERWISE RELEASE. COME TO ME ALONE, HAZEL ZHOU. SWEAR THE FULL OATH, THE OLD OATH, THE ONE YOUR MOTHER SWORE. GIVE ME YOUR SELF TO KEEP AND SPEND. AND I WILL CARRY YOU THROUGH THE COURT'S DOORS MYSELF, PAST ITS BOOKS, TO THE SHELF WHERE THE CHILD'S NAME IS FILED. YOU WILL TAKE HER OUT. YOU WILL WALK HER INTO THE SUN. AND NO ONE—NOT THE CROWN, NOT THE UNDER-CROWN, NOT ANY LEDGER ANYWHERE—WILL EVER COLLECT ON EITHER OF YOU AGAIN.**

The offer sat in the cold air between them, beautiful and terrible, and Hazel understood with terrible clarity why it was beautiful: it was shaped exactly like hope. Come alone. Save the girl. Free your mother. Pay only yourself.

Terror wearing the face of hope. Terror had been wearing that face since Ashgate square. Since the torn page. Since, if they were honest, since childhood—every kindness of their mother's re-priced now, every meal bought with a prayer, every bedtime story a down payment.

"And if I refuse?"

**THEN YOU GO TO THE MOUNTAIN ALONE, WITH YOUR KNIVES AND YOUR ACCOUNTING, AND YOU FAIL, AND THE CHILD'S SOUL SITS IN THE DARK FOREVER, AND I CONTINUE TO EAT WHAT REMAINS OF YOUR MOTHER UNTIL THERE IS NOTHING LEFT TO RELEASE.** A pause, dry as a page turning. **I DO NOT BLUFF, LITTLE SCRIBE. I AM THE ONLY HONEST PARTY IN THIS TRANSACTION.**

"That's true," Hazel admitted. "That's the foulest part."

**THINK. YOU HAVE UNTIL THE ICEFIELD. AFTER THAT, THE OFFER FREEZES SHUT LIKE EVERYTHING ELSE UP THERE.**

The darkness thinned. Before it went, Hazel spoke—one question, quiet, the kind of question you ask a debtor when you already suspect the answer:

"Why me? Why does the last servant have to be *me?*"

For the first time in eleven years, the god hesitated. When it answered, its voice had changed—smaller, and somehow worse for being small.

**BECAUSE YOUR MOTHER ASKED ME TO SPARE YOU. AND I ALWAYS PAY MY DEBTS.**

Then it was gone, and the wind came back, and the salt flowers whispered along the riverbed like thousands of tiny pages turning.

* * *

Hazel sat until the sky went gray at the edges. They took out the ledger, unwrapped the oilcloth, and by first light read their mother's entries again with new eyes, and saw what grief had kept them from seeing: in the margin beside the deferred payment, in her small precise hand, a single notation in the god's own shorthand, which Hazel had taken for a smudge all these years.

*Collateral may redeem by substitution. — H.V.*

Her initials. She had written herself into the escape clause. She had spent eleven years being eaten, slowly, in the dark, holding the door of that clause open with whatever was left of her—so that if it ever came to it, her child could walk free of everything, owing nothing, sworn to nothing.

Or so she'd believed. She hadn't known about Wren. She hadn't known the clause would one day look like salvation wearing her daughter's name.

Hazel closed the book. South, down the valley, the mountains had risen overnight into a white wall with a single pass cut through it, and on the pass, small with distance, something glinted—metal, moving. More than one of it.

Crown hunters, on the icefield, between them and the mountain. Between them and every choice.

Hazel woke Wren, and pointed, and watched the girl's face go pale and then set, fourteen years old and already braver than anyone Hazel had ever audited.

"How long do we have?" Wren asked.

Hazel judged the distance, the light, the lie of the snow.

"Until noon," they said. "Maybe less. We run."
