## 8. Hunters on the Icefield

The pass above Vell's Crossing had a name in the old tongue that meant *the place where the wind counts your bones*, and by the second hour of climbing it, Hazel understood why. The wind came off the icefield in long, patient strokes, not gusting but *sifting*, finding every gap in cloak and collar and resolve. It did not howl. It counted.

Hazel counted too. It was a habit the ledgers had ground into them: count everything, trust nothing, and never let the numbers show on your face.

Four days south of the guild hall. Three nights of borrowed barns and one of open snow, Wren shivering against their back like a small hot coal refusing to go out. Two horses, one of them lame since the ford. One road, narrowing as it climbed, until the road gave up entirely and became a white plain stitched with cairns that previous travelers had raised like prayers.

And behind them—Hazel had stopped turning to look, but they could feel it the way you feel a debt accru—behind them, hunters.

"You keep doing the thing," Wren said. She had come up beside them, breath steaming, her scarf wound up to her eyes so that only her forehead showed, chapped pink above the wool. "The thing where you go quiet and start doing sums with your face."

"I'm always doing sums with my face."

"Not like this. This is the bad kind." She kicked at a ridge of wind-carved snow and missed, her boots too big for her, cut down from a dead man's kit. "The kind where the answer is bad."

Hazel looked back along the way they'd come. The cairns marched south into haze. Somewhere under that haze was the toll road, and the ford, and the farmstead where a smuggler named Odile had fed them barley soup and looked at Hazel with her mother's name in her mouth and not said it, which had been its own kind of kindness. Somewhere behind all of that was the guild hall, and the vote, and the sigil someone had carved into Hazel's door in the dark—the coiled circle, the closed eye, the mark of the god who no longer spoke.

And behind *that*, gaining, were the crown's hunters.

"They'll have caught up to the pass by nightfall," Hazel said. "If they hired local trackers, sooner."

"So we go faster."

"We go *smarter*. Faster is how you die on ice." Hazel crouched at the edge of the field proper, where the snow gave way to old glacier, blue-green and glassed over, and pressed a palm flat. Beneath the surface, faint as a heartbeat under a floor, water moved. "There's a melt channel under this shelf. It's been eating the ice all winter. If we cross in a straight line, we cross over the weakest part."

"So we don't go straight."

"So we go where the ice is honest." Hazel rose, knees cracking. "Which means we go north first. Sideways. It'll cost us half a day."

Wren was quiet a moment. Half a day, with hunters behind. Hazel watched her do her own sums—she was guild-raised too, whatever else she was—and watched her arrive at the same figure.

"Half a day," Wren said, "or all of everything."

"That's the trade."

"Then let's pay it." She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her hand found Hazel's sleeve and held it for a breath before letting go, and Hazel understood that the lightness was a craft, like the ledger-hand, like the runner's easy stride. Everything the girl did was a craft. That was what the crown's list didn't understand about people like Wren. It thought the gift was the thing. The gift was only the raw material. The person was the craft.

They turned north, into the wind's teeth, and began the long sideways traverse of the icefield.

* * *

The icefield had its own weather, its own country. By midday the sun was a white coin behind gauze, and the light came at them from everywhere and nowhere, so that the world had no shadows and therefore no edges. Hazel navigated by the cairns when there were cairns, and by sound when there weren't—tapping the ice with an iron-shod staff and listening for the hollow note of the melt channels, the deep drum of solid ground. Wren learned the rhythm of it inside an hour. She always did. She had been the guild's fastest study at everything except, Hazel thought with an old and complicated grief, at lying.

She had lied to them, though. Back at Ashgate, before the decree, before everything: she'd come back from a delivery with a story full of smooth corners, and Hazel had let it pass, and then the crown's list had made the lie irrelevant and the truth enormous. *She remembers the court,* Hazel thought, watching the girl pick her way along a pressure ridge, small and sure-footed and sold before she could walk. *She remembers it, and she carried it alone for years, and she told me on the salt road like she was handing me something breakable.* Hazel still did not know what to do with the weight of it. They carried it the way they carried everything: in the ledger behind the eyes, in a column with no total.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a split boulder, a black tooth of granite the ice had failed to swallow. No fire. They chewed smoked fish and hardtack and drank snow melted over a sliver of sunstone, and Wren told a story about the first winter she'd spent with the guild, when she was seven and had tried to salt-preserve a snowman so it would last until spring. Hazel let the story wash over them and kept their eyes on the south.

The haze there had changed. It had *texture* now. Points of light where no light should be, moving with the patient discipline of people who did not need to hurry because they knew their prey had nowhere to be.

"Hazel," Wren said. She had seen them too.

"I see them."

"How many?"

Hazel counted the lights twice. "Six. Maybe seven. One of them is holding back. A watcher, or a mage."

"Can we outrun them?"

"On this ice, in the dark, with a lame horse?" Hazel shook their head. "We'd trade a fight we might survive for a fall we won't."

"Then we fight."

"Then we fight," Hazel agreed, and heard how calm their own voice was, and hated it a little. Calm was the guild's inheritance too. Calm was what you learned when you learned that panic was just interest compounding on fear.

They broke camp in the dark and moved north by the staff's hollow notes, away from the lights, buying miles they could not keep. The hunters did not hurry. Toward the coldest hour, when the sky went the color of a bruise and the stars came out hard and sharp as tacks, the lights stopped gaining—and stopped *hiding*. They fanned out across the icefield's southern rim, six torches in a line, and one of them rose up on a pole or a staff or a hand, and burned white, and threw their shadows long and blue across the ice.

There was no more sideways. There was only the line, and the ice, and the space between.

* * *

They came at dawn, because hunters are patient people and dawn is when the ice is hardest and the light is in their eyes' favor, and because cruelty, Hazel had learned from the ledgers, keeps excellent hours.

There were six of them on the line and the seventh behind, and they were not soldiers. Soldiers marched. These came the way water comes, spreading along the low ground, cutting off the cairn-line, herding without appearing to herd. They wore the crown's grey and the hunt's black sash, and they carried nets weighted with cold-iron beads, and crossbows, and long hooked poles for taking people alive—because the proclamation had been specific. *Carriers of the soul-craft to be taken living, for assessment.* Dead carriers were worth a bounty. Living ones were worth a career.

The one in the middle raised a hand, and the line stopped forty paces out, and he came forward alone across the ice with the unhurried gait of a man walking to a market stall. He was older than the others, weathered, with a grey beard cropped close and a scar through one eyebrow, and when he was twenty paces off he stopped and planted his pole and looked at Hazel with the frank professional interest of a man appraising livestock.

"Hazel Zhou," he said. "Keeper of the Salt Guild's books."

Hazel said nothing. Beside them, Wren had gone very still, the stillness of a small animal that has decided its best armor is silence.

"Name's Corvane. I've taken eleven of your kind this winter." He said it the way a man reports a harvest. "Eleven assessments. Two found fit for the court's service. The rest—" He shrugged. "The rest are paperwork now. I'd rather you were paperwork, honestly. The living ones cry."

"You're a long way from your warrant's jurisdiction," Hazel said.

"I'm exactly where my warrant says to be. It's a long warrant." Corvane shifted his grip on the pole. "Here's how the morning goes, bookkeeper. You put your hands out. The girl puts her hands out. We walk south, everyone's feet stay warm. Or my people get creative, and I'll tell you now, creative is not my preference on ice. Ice punishes improvisation."

"And if we run?"

"Then my crossbows shoot the horses first and your legs second, and we still walk south, only slower and with more crying." He smiled, and it was a professional smile, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "You're the one who keeps accounts. Tell me which ledger entry this is."

Hazel looked past him. Six hunters, spread in a shallow arc. Crossbows—two, maybe three, the rest holding the hooked poles low. The seventh figure still back at the rim, standing beside something that might have been a brazier, and around that figure the air had a bent quality, a smudge, like heat-shimmer in reverse. A luck-worker. The crown had hired a luck-worker to hunt soul-crafters, which was either irony or economy.

And under everything, under the wind and the ice and the bent air, Hazel felt the other thing. The thing that had been there since the shrine, since the vision of kneeling servants in the dark, since the god had stirred for the first time in eleven years and spoken one sentence into the marrow of their bones:

*Come alone. Bring me what I am owed.*

The god was awake now. Hazel could feel it the way you feel a tooth that has begun to die: not pain, exactly, but presence. A pressure behind the sternum. An attention. It had been riding along with them for four days, silent, hungry, watching through whatever part of Hazel it had never given back.

And it was watching now. Hazel could feel its attention swing toward the hunters like a compass needle finding north.

*Oh,* Hazel thought. *Oh, no. You're not waiting for the shrine. You're waiting for me to use it.*

"Last chance, bookkeeper," Corvane said.

Wren's hand found Hazel's. Her fingers were freezing and they gripped like iron. "Hazel," she whispered. "What do we do?"

What they did was what the guild had taught: never show the numbers. But the numbers had changed. Six hunters, two or three crossbows, one luck-worker, ice that would punish improvisation. Two of them, one lame horse, and a girl who had already been sold once and would not be sold twice.

Hazel had spent their whole life hiding the gift. Folding it small. Keeping it out of the ledgers, out of the guild hall, out of their own face. They had watched their mother do the same, and their mother had died anyway, and the dying had bought a lie that Hazel had only just learned the shape of. And all that hiding had purchased exactly one thing: it had kept the god asleep and unfed.

The god was awake now regardless.

"Get behind the horse," Hazel said.

Wren stared at them. "What—"

"*Behind the horse.* And whatever you hear, whatever you see—don't call my name."

Corvane's smile widened a fraction. He raised his pole. Behind him, the crossbows came up in their smooth, drilled unison, and at the rim of the icefield the luck-worker lifted both hands, and the bent air around them began to *shimmer*, and Hazel felt the man's borrowed luck come online like a drawn bow—luck not his own, siphoned from somewhere, from someone, wound around him in bright invisible cords, cords that would turn every bolt true and every stumble lucky and every one of Hazel's desperate improvisations fatal.

Hazel stepped forward onto the open ice, alone, and let the gift come up out of the cellar where they had kept it locked for thirty years.

It was not what the crown thought it was. The crown thought soul-craft was making—bottling, binding, selling. But every maker's craft has a mirror trade, and Hazel had always known, in the place where they kept the numbers they never wrote down, that they were not a maker. They were the other thing. They were the one who could read what had been done and *unwrite* it.

They looked at Corvane, at the bright cords of luck wound around him, and they did the thing they had done ten thousand times to entries in a ledger.

They found the line where the numbers didn't balance. And they pulled.

* * *

Later, Hazel would not be able to describe it in any way that didn't sound like a lie. The closest they ever came was this: it was like taking hold of the end of a thread in a woven cloth and pulling, and feeling the whole weave understand, all at once, that it had been woven.

Corvane's luck came apart.

Not violently. That was the horror of it. The bright cords simply *loosened*, the way a knot loosens when you find its secret, and the borrowed luck went home—to wherever it had been taken from, to whoever had sold it, Hazel didn't know and didn't ask—and Corvane stood on the ice for one full second as an ordinary man, unarmored by chance for the first time in perhaps his entire career.

Then the ice, which punishes improvisation, punished his.

It was nothing dramatic. A hairline seam under his boot, a shift of weight, the kind of slip that a lucky man would have caught a hundred times a day without noticing. Corvane went down hard, pole clattering, and the crack of his head on the glacier was small and precise, like a ledger line being ruled.

The crossbowmen hesitated. That was the luck's departure echoing through them—Hazel could see it, now that they had opened the door, could see the faint bright cords on all of them, thinner, cheaper, and they *frayed*, all together, like rigging in a storm. One bolt went wide and skipped off the ice into the murk. The other two loosed a half-beat late, and the half-beat was everything: Hazel was already moving, already behind the split boulder, and the bolts rang off granite.

And through it all, through the cold and the ringing ice and Wren's white face above the horse's back, Hazel felt the god *drink*.

It was not subtle. It was not gentle. It came up through the act like heat through a poker—the moment the luck unraveled, something vast and patient put its mouth to the moment and *drew*, and Hazel felt their own strength go with it, felt the unraveling cost them not effort but *substance*, some deep reserve they hadn't known they were spending until the spending was done. The ice tilted. The dawn went grey at the edges. Somewhere far away, in a dark that was not a place, Hazel heard—or did not hear, felt—the sound of a ledger being written in, a column filling, an account growing fat.

*Yes,* said the god, in no words at all. *Yes. Again.*

Hazel put a hand against the boulder and did not fall, and understood, with the cold clarity of the very cold, what the vision at the shrine had actually shown. Not servants kneeling in punishment. Servants kneeling at a *trough*. Every act of the gift was a meal. Every carrier was a cup. The god had not gone silent. The god had gone to *table*, and its sworn servants were the only food that satisfied, and it had eaten them one ledger at a time, and it had let Hazel live because Hazel was the last cup that could still be filled—and now Hazel had poured for it willingly, and it had tasted them, and it wanted more.

"HAZEL!" Wren's voice, cracking the rule, cracking everything.

The hunters were regrouping. Corvane was on his knees, blood sheeting into his eyebrow, and he was looking at Hazel with a new expression—not professional interest now, not appraisal. Recognition. The look of a man who has assessed eleven carriers and finally, at the twelfth, understood what the crown was actually afraid of.

"Take her!" he roared, pointing past Hazel, at Wren, at the horse. "The girl! Take the *girl*!"

Three hunters broke from the line, poles low, boots finding the ice with the desperate care of men whose luck had just been confiscated. The luck-worker at the rim was shouting something Hazel couldn't hear, hands weaving, trying to re-cord, to re-borrow, to rebuild the bright armor faster than Hazel could unmake it—and Hazel felt the god surge up inside them like a tide answering a hook, *yes, yes, again, unmake him too, unmake them all, I am so hungry, I have been so hungry*—

And Hazel, on their knees in the snow with blood in their mouth from biting their own tongue, did the hardest thing they had done in thirty years of hard things.

They closed the door.

They shut the gift away, mid-meal, with the god's mouth still open, and felt the fury of it like a slammed jaw, like a starved animal cheated at the fence. The world went ordinary. The hunters' luck was still frayed, still thin, but rebuilding, thread by thread, and Hazel had nothing left to stop it with except the oldest tools: granite, ice, a lame horse, and a girl.

"Wren!" Hazel was up, staggering, shouting now themselves. "The melt channel—the one from yesterday—*run the cairns north and cut east at the split stone!*"

Wren didn't ask. That was the craft in her. She was already moving, small and low along the pressure ridge, and the three hunters turned to run her down, and the ice did what the ice does.

The melt channel had been eating the shelf all winter, and Hazel had mapped its hollow notes yesterday with a staff and a patient ear, and the hunters—unlucky now, merely human, improvising—had not. The first man went through to his waist with a crack like a ship's timber. The second leapt, and his leap was a hand's breadth short, and the ice took him to the chest. The third stopped, and stood, and looked back at Hazel across forty paces of white, and made the calculation that soldiers everywhere have always made.

Corvane was shouting. The luck-worker was shouting. The icefield filled with shouting and cracking and the bell-note of breaking shelf, and through all of it Hazel got to Wren, got an arm under her, got them both moving north along the honest ice while behind them the crown's hunt came apart at the seams—not defeated, not destroyed, but *delayed*, which on the icefield was the same thing as forever.

* * *

They ran until the col was behind them and the light was high and white, and then they walked, and then they stumbled, and then they stopped in the wind-shadow of a cairn the size of a chapel, and Hazel sat down in the snow and discovered that their hands would not stop shaking.

Wren crouched in front of them. She had a cut on her cheekbone she hadn't mentioned, and her eyes were wide and wet and furious all at once.

"You *unmade* him," she said. "I saw it. There was nothing—there was no light, no words, he just—came apart, like a knot. Like a *sum*." Her voice dropped. "That's what you are. That's what you've been. All those years of hiding, and you can just—"

"I can unravel what's been done to a soul," Hazel said. Their voice came out scraped. "Not make. Not bind. Just... audit. Find the false entry and pull it." They looked at their shaking hands. "And it costs."

"You mean the magic."

"I mean what eats the magic." Hazel looked south, at the empty haze where the hunters had been, and felt the god there too, sullen and vast and *waiting*, its attention pressed against the inside of their ribs like a face against a window. "There's a thing under my oath, Wren. There's been a thing under it the whole time. Every time I use the gift, it feeds. And it's been feeding for eleven years on everyone who ever swore to it, and when it's finished feeding—" They stopped. The vision at the shrine rose again: the kneeling shapes, the dark, the slow patient consumption. "It doesn't let go of what it's eaten. It keeps it. Down in the dark. Still kneeling."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. The wind counted their bones.

"Then we don't use it again," she said finally. "Right? We go around. Over the pass, down to the tollhouse—old Maren's tollhouse, the smugglers said she'd pass us through—we don't fight, we don't unravel, we just *walk*."

"We walk," Hazel agreed.

But even as they said it, they felt the god's attention shift, sullen and knowing, and heard—not in words, never in words, in the grammar of hunger itself—its single patient sentence, the one it had spoken at the shrine, revised now, amended, annotated with the morning's meal:

*Come alone. Bring me what I am owed. You have seen what I pay for. You know I always pay.*

And far to the south, faint on the wind, a hunting horn sounded—once, twice—and then a third time, from the *north*.

From ahead of them.

Wren's face went white. "Hazel. That's the wrong direction."

Hazel got to their feet, legs burning, and looked north along the cairn-line, toward the tollhouse and the pass and whatever now waited between them and the only road down the mountain—and saw, just cresting the far ridge, small with distance, a single rider. Not grey. Not the crown's black sash.

A guild cloak.

"Someone's found us," Wren whispered. "Someone from home."

Hazel's hands had gone still at last—still, and cold, and curled as if around a pen. Whoever it was, they had come a long way through the crown's hunt to find two fugitives on the ice. And in Hazel's experience, which was long and written down and balanced to the copper, nobody traveled that far through a burning country except to collect a debt.

"Stay behind me," Hazel said, and walked north to meet them.
