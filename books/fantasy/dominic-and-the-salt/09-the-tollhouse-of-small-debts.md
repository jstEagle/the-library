## 9. The Tollhouse of Small Debts

The storm found them an hour before the tollhouse did.

Wren felt it first in her teeth, that high thin ache that meant snow was coming down off the pass like something with intent, and by the time the road bent around the shoulder of the mountain and she saw the light—a single yellow square burning in all that blue-grey dark—her fingers had stopped answering her properly. Hazel walked ahead of her the way they always did, one hand raised against the wind as if they could argue with it, their coat whipping sideways, salt-crusted at the hem from the smugglers' road they'd left behind two days ago.

"There," Wren shouted, and pointed, and Hazel turned, and for a moment the wind flattened everything between them and she saw her guardian's face clearly: exhausted, watchful, older than it had been a month ago. Older than it should have been.

"Tollhouse," Hazel called back. "Good. Bad news is we'll have to pay."

"Bad news is I've got four coppers and a button."

Hazel's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Then let's hope the tollkeeper doesn't want the button."

* * *

The tollhouse sat where the south road narrowed to squeeze between two walls of black stone, a squat stone building with a stable lean-to and a sign that swung on rusted hinges. The sign read: **PASSAGE TOLL — ALL PRICES NEGOTIABLE**. Beneath that, smaller, in a different hand: **NO REFUNDS. NO EXCEPTIONS. NO CRYING.**

Wren had heard of places like this. Every runner had. The Salt Guild's ledgers listed them under *fixed points*, which was guild-speak for things you couldn't cheat, couldn't bribe, and couldn't afford to insult. Tollhouses on the old roads didn't take coin—not usually, not only. They took other things. Time, mostly. Years off your life, measured out in hours of labor or decades of forgetting. Sometimes they took names. Once, when she was nine and eavesdropping where she shouldn't have been, Wren had heard Old Maret describe a tollhouse in the eastern passes that took *shadows*, and how the man who'd paid came out the other side flat and pale as paper, and how he'd thanked the tollkeeper anyway, because that was the worst part—the paying never felt like losing until long after.

The door opened before Hazel knocked.

The tollkeeper was a small woman, round as a teapot, wrapped in so many shawls she seemed less dressed than upholstered. Her face was pleasant and creased and utterly unremarkable, the kind of face you forgot while looking at it, and her eyes were the color of tea left too long in the pot. She looked at Hazel first, then at Wren, and something in the way her gaze settled made Wren's skin prickle.

"Two travelers," the woman said. Her voice was warm as bread. "One storm coming down the pass behind you like a debt collector. You'll want my fire and you'll want it badly. Come in, come in, before the wind decides to keep you."

Inside was small and hot and smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and, underneath it all, faintly, of salt. A fire crackled in a hearth big enough to roast an ox. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves stood jars—hundreds of them, thousands, glass and clay and one or two of cloudy crystal—and each jar held something that caught the firelight differently. Some held darkness that moved. Some held light that didn't. One, near the door at exactly eye height, held what looked like a summer afternoon: green-gold, drowsy, humming faintly with bees.

Wren stared at it until Hazel's hand closed gently on her shoulder.

"Tollkeeper," Hazel said, with the careful formality they used for people who mattered. "We need passage south. Two souls, one night's shelter, provisions if you sell them."

"I sell everything." The woman lowered herself into a chair by the hearth with the sigh of someone settling into a favorite story. "Coin I take, yes, for the shelter and the soup, because even miracles need firewood. But the passage toll—" she spread her plump hands, rings glinting on every finger, "—that's priced separately. And I'm afraid I don't set the price. The road does. It always has. I just do the collecting."

"And what does the road charge?" Hazel asked.

The tollkeeper smiled. Her teeth were very white and very even, like piano keys. "Memories."

* * *

They ate soup first. That was the rule, the tollkeeper said; nobody negotiated well hungry, and besides, her soup was famous in three valleys and infamous in a fourth. It was good—barley and root vegetables and some smoked meat Wren decided not to ask about—and it warmed her from the inside out until her fingers ached in the good way, the thawing way.

Hazel sat across from her, turning their spoon slowly, not eating much. Wren knew that look. It was the ledger look, the look they got when they were pricing something in their head, running columns of numbers no one else could see. Hazel had been doing a lot of that lately. Since Ashgate. Since the list. Since the night Wren had woken on the frozen road and found Hazel sitting up beside the dead fire, staring at nothing, saying quietly, to no one: *Mother, what did you do.*

"The toll," the tollkeeper said, when the bowls were empty, "is one memory per traveler. Not any memory—the road chooses. Or rather—" she tapped her own temple, "—I choose, on the road's behalf. I've been doing this forty years. I know what a crossing costs and what it weighs. Cheap memories make weak crossings. The road wants something real."

"What happens to them?" Wren asked. "The memories. After."

The tollkeeper gestured at the shelves. All those jars. All that caught light.

"They're kept. Some are sold—there are buyers for happy childhoods, especially. Grieving parents, mostly. Rich ones." She said it without apology, the way you'd say *it rains in spring*. "Some I keep. Some the road takes whole, and those I don't ask about." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved in her tea-colored eyes. "Now. Let's talk about you two, because I can already see this is going to be interesting, and interesting is my favorite kind of expensive."

She looked at Hazel. "You. You're carrying more than a pack. There's a weight on you like wet wool—I can smell the oath on you, old god-smell, temple incense gone sour. But that's not toll. That's your own business, gods help you." Something flickered across her pleasant face—recognition, maybe, or its cousin, wariness—but it passed quickly, smoothed away like a wrinkle pressed flat. "Your toll will be simple. A memory of craft, I think. Something you learned. Skills weigh well and travel well."

Hazel nodded slowly. Wren watched them do the arithmetic, watched them decide it was acceptable, watched them begin to sort through their own head for something to give up—and something in Wren's chest went tight and cold.

Because Hazel would pay it. Of course they would. Hazel paid everything, always, stood at the front of every line where the bill arrived, and Wren had spent seventeen years being protected by that habit and had never once, until this winter, understood what it cost the person paying.

"No," Wren said.

Everyone looked at her.

"My toll comes out of me," she said. "Not out of Hazel. Whatever the road wants, I pay it myself."

"Wren—" Hazel started.

"You've been paying for me since I was six years old." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "You paid the guild for my boots and my bread and my training, you paid the smugglers on the salt road, you paid that hedge-witch's sister in Vell with the scarred hands—you paid her with three days of your own sleep, I counted. I'm done being a line item in somebody else's ledger. This one's mine."

The tollkeeper's smile deepened by a single degree. "Oh, I *like* her."

* * *

The choosing happened at a small table near the fire, apart from the shelves, because—as the tollkeeper explained—memories got skittish when they saw where they'd end up. She produced a shallow clay bowl filled with something like water but heavier, silver-dark, and told Wren to hold her hands over it and think.

"Not of anything specific," she said. "Just open the doors and let me look through the windows. It doesn't hurt. That's the trick of it, and the pity of it too. If it hurt, people would think twice."

Wren put her hands over the bowl. The silver surface trembled, then stilled, then began—slowly, like frost forming on glass—to show pictures.

Her mother's kitchen, before. Flour on the counter and her mother's laugh, low and smoky, and the smell of cardamom bread, and the window light coming in gold because it was always late afternoon in that kitchen, always golden hour, forever. Wren flinched. The image wavered.

"Gently," the tollkeeper murmured. "Don't chase them away. Just let them pass."

More came. Learning to run messages in the guild's lower halls, the thrill of being trusted with something sealed. The first time she outran Brannick—he'd been furious about it for a week, and secretly proud, and everyone knew it. The night Hazel had found her crying in the salt cellar after her mother died, and hadn't said anything clever, had just sat down next to her in the dark among the barrels and stayed there until morning. The taste of stolen plums on a summer wall. The sound of Hazel's voice reading ledger entries aloud in the boring cadence that somehow made her feel safe, like the world was a thing that could be counted and therefore survived.

The bowl filled with all of it, golden and flickering, her whole small life laid out in little lit windows, and the tollkeeper studied it the way Hazel studied inventory: thoroughly, professionally, without mercy.

"Hm," she said. "Yes. I see why the road will want quality goods here." She reached out one ringed finger and touched the surface, and the images swirled, rearranged, and settled into three.

Three windows now, floating in the silver.

In the first: the kitchen. Cardamom bread. Her mother singing off-key. Golden light.

In the second: the salt cellar. Hazel's silhouette in the doorway. The particular silence of someone deciding to stay.

In the third: the wall, the plums, the sun, Brannick's shout of outrage below, Wren laughing with juice running down her chin, twelve years old and invincible.

"Choose," the tollkeeper said softly.

And here was the thing Wren understood then, the thing nobody warned you about, the intimate and terrible arithmetic of it: she could not choose the salt cellar. She needed that one. Whatever was coming—whatever waited beneath the mountain, whatever Hazel wasn't telling her about the god and the oath and the name on the crown's list—she needed to remember that someone had once chosen to sit with her in the dark. She needed it the way she needed her heartbeat. Choosing to lose it would be like choosing to stop breathing and call it bravery.

So it was the kitchen or the wall. Her mother or her freedom. The grief or the joy.

"That's cruel," Wren whispered.

"That's a toll," the tollkeeper said. "If it were easy, everyone would cross free, and the road would wash out, and the mountain would come down on us all. Cruelty isn't the point. Weight is. The road needs to know you gave up something that mattered, or the crossing won't hold, and things come up through crossings that don't hold." She paused. "I'll tell you a secret, girl, since you've got honest eyes. Most people, when they reach this table, try to give me the memory they think is worth least. And the road knows. Oh, the road always knows. It takes the one they were hiding instead. So don't hide anything. Choose with your eyes open. It's the only kindness I'm allowed to offer."

Wren looked at the kitchen. Her mother's voice hung in it, mid-song, mid-laugh, flour on her hands. Wren had built herself out of that room. Every good thing in her had been baked there in golden light. If she gave it up, would she still love her mother? Would the grief mean anything? Would she be a person who had been loved, or just a person with a hole shaped like love and no idea what fit there?

But if she gave up the wall—if she gave up the laughing, the running, the invincible afternoon—she'd be giving up the self that could survive what was coming. The runner. The one who climbed. The one who laughed at danger from the top of things.

*Which self do you lose?*

That was the real question. Not *what do you pay* but *who do you stop being*. The tollkeeper had known it all along. Probably the road had known it all along. Probably the road had been asking that question of every traveler since before there were roads, and the jars on the shelves were full of answers, thousands of small selves in glass, catching light.

Wren thought about Hazel, who paid everything, who carried everything, who was walking toward a mountain to steal back a soul because a promise made to a six-year-old in a salt cellar mattered more to them than their own name. Hazel would tell her to keep the kitchen. Hazel would say the past was load-bearing; you didn't pull out walls to lighten the load.

But Hazel wasn't the one at the table.

"The kitchen," Wren said.

Even as she said it, her hand moved toward the second window—the salt cellar, Hazel's silhouette—and stopped, hovering, trembling. No. She'd already chosen. She made herself say it again, louder, to fix it in the world. "I pay the kitchen. My mother's kitchen. The cardamom bread and the singing and the light. That's my toll."

The tollkeeper looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind the tea-colored eyes—pity, maybe, or respect, or just the professional satisfaction of a job done properly.

"A hard price, fairly named," she said formally, like words from an old contract. "The road accepts."

She lifted the first window out of the bowl.

It came away like a piece of film, like a soap bubble that had decided to be solid, shimmering, golden, and Wren heard—for one last time, from far away, from the bottom of a well—her mother's voice, singing, off-key, joyful. Wren lunged forward without meaning to. Hazel's hand caught her arm, gentle and immovable.

"It doesn't hurt," the tollkeeper repeated softly. "That's the pity of it."

She pressed the memory into an empty jar, and the jar filled with warm gold light, and the light hummed faintly, and somewhere in it a woman was singing a song whose tune Wren could no longer quite place, though the shape of the listening stayed, the feeling of being sung to, like a footprint where the foot used to be.

The tollkeeper corked it and set it on the shelf, third row, between a jar of blue silence and a jar of somebody's wedding day.

Wren felt—nothing. That was the horror. She felt fine. She felt *lighter*. Somewhere in her head there was a smooth blank space, painless as a healed wound, and she knew—because she could see the jar, because the jar was right there—that something enormous had lived in that space, and she would spend the rest of her life knowing the shape of it and never the substance.

"Mom," she said experimentally, to test the damage.

The word worked. The word was fine. But the word opened onto a hallway with a door at the end, and the door was locked, and through the keyhole there was only gold light and the distant sense of flour and song, and when she tried to press her eye to the keyhole the tollkeeper cleared her throat and the hallway folded up like a fan.

"It settles in a day or two," the woman said kindly. "You'll stop reaching for it. That's the mercy. Now—" she turned briskly to Hazel, rubbing her ringed hands together, "—your turn. Hands over the bowl. And none of your guild tricks, Salt-keeper; I've collected from your kind before, and the road remembers what your mother's handwriting looks like."

Wren went cold all over. Beside her, Hazel went stiller than still, the stillness of a held breath, of a blade halfway out of its sheath.

"You knew," Hazel said quietly.

"I collect memories, dear. Do you imagine I don't recognize the ones that walk in wearing coats?" The tollkeeper's smile was warm as bread and twice as final. "Sit down. Pay your toll. And then, if you're wise, you'll let an old woman tell you what the road showed me last week—because a crown hunter crossed this pass heading south, and he paid with a memory of orders, and I read the orders, because I always read the orders."

She tapped the rim of the silver bowl.

"And his orders had your faces in them."
