## 10. Brannick Finds Them

Brannick had been following their trail for nine days, and for nine days the trail had been lying to him.

That was Hazel's work, he knew. Nobody else in the guild could make a road argue with itself. He'd found bootprints on the salt flats that started mid-stride, as if the walker had stepped out of the air, and a cold camp outside Ferrow's Crossing where the ashes were still warm but the wind had swept them into a neat little pile, tidy as a grave. Twice he'd lost them entirely and twice he'd found them again by doing the thing no sensible hunter did: he'd stopped looking and let his feet choose. The god's old sigil was still inked on his door back at the guild hall, and whether it marked him as faithful or as prey, it had a way of pulling.

He told himself he'd come to warn them. He made himself believe it, mostly. Belief, Brannick had learned over twenty years of selling his sword to whoever paid, was like luck—you could borrow it, you could carry it in a pouch against your heart, and if you shook it hard enough it would rattle convincingly.

The mountains took him up through pine country gone gray with frost, past shrines with their offering-bowls full of snow instead of coins, past a hanging sign that read TOLLHOUSE AHEAD in letters someone had defaced with a single word: *RUN*. He didn't run. Running was for people with something left to lose, and Brannick had spent his carefully, the way a drunk spends his last coin—slowly, telling himself each piece didn't count.

He found them on the third morning above the tollhouse, in a hollow where the wind had carved a room out of the drifts. They'd built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and they were sitting close together the way people sit when they've stopped pretending they aren't afraid.

Hazel saw him first. Of course they did. They always saw everything first; it was the curse of bookkeepers, to watch the world the way a moneylender watches a debtor's hands. Brannick raised both palms, empty, and stood very still while the wind tried to push him down the slope like an unwelcome guest.

"You're a long way from your door," Hazel said. Their voice carried flat across the snow, stripped of anything he could use.

"Longer from my bed." He came down the slope with his hands still open, boots crunching, each step announced. "If I meant harm, would I announce myself?"

"If you meant harm, you'd have brought friends."

"I brought nothing." That much was true, and he let it show. No pack worth robbing, no blade drawn, no hunters at his back—he'd checked the ridgeline three times before descending, because if the crown's people had followed him here then everything he'd come to say would die in his mouth along with all of them.

Wren had not moved. She sat with her knees pulled up and her arms around them, watching him with the particular stillness of a small animal deciding whether the thing in the grass is a rock or a snake. She looked thinner than she had eleven days ago, when the guild had voted and the vote had gone the wrong way and Brannick had kept his mouth shut about it. Thinner, and older, in the way children go old overnight when the world shows them its ledger.

"Hazel," she said quietly. "It's the one who marked our door."

"I know who it is." Hazel hadn't taken their eyes off him. "Sit where you are, Brannick. Say what you came to say, and then keep walking south until you find whatever it is you're actually looking for."

*What am I actually looking for?* It was a fair question and he hated it. He crouched at the edge of the firelight, close enough to feel the warmth and be refused it, and for a moment nobody spoke, and the mountain made its small noises—the tick and groan of ice settling, a bird somewhere complaining about winter—and Brannick understood that this silence was a test, and that he was failing it by being comfortable in it.

"The guild master sold her," he said.

He watched the words land. Wren flinched the way you flinch at a sound you'd been expecting without admitting it. Hazel went very still—not the stillness of shock but the stillness of a person setting down something heavy so their hands are free.

"When?" Hazel asked.

"Two nights after you ran. A rider came up from Ashgate wearing merchant colors, which fooled nobody, least of all Maren. They talked behind closed doors for half the night, and in the morning there was new ink in the crown's purse and the guild's debts were—" he paused, choosing, "—*lighter*. By exactly the amount it costs to buy a pardon for a guild that's been trading in soul-craft since before either of us could hold a knife."

"You're certain it was Wren's location. Not mine."

"It was both. But yours they already had." He said it gently, or in the shape of gentleness, which was the best he could manage. "Your name's been on their list for eleven years, Hazel. Everyone in the hall knows it now. What Maren sold was the *where*. The girl. The route south. The smugglers you'd lean on." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something, though he'd never have admitted what. "I'm telling you because I'd rather you hear it from me than from a crossbow bolt."

"And the vote," Hazel said. "Where did you stand?"

Brannick looked at the fire. The fire didn't help. "I stood where standing was safe."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." He met their eyes then, and let them see the truth of it, because a lie needed a clean face and his wasn't. "I didn't raise my hand for surrendering her. I also didn't raise it against. There were twelve of us in that room, Hazel, and eleven of us did arithmetic, and arithmetic says one runner isn't worth forty lives. You'd have done the same sum once. Maybe you still would."

"No," Wren said.

They both looked at her. She hadn't raised her voice. She unfolded herself from around her knees with the deliberate slowness of someone handling something fragile, and she looked at Brannick the way you look at a debt collector who has come to the wrong house.

"You're wrong about the sum," she said. "You're not wrong that people do it. But you're wrong that it's arithmetic. It never was. It's just easier to write it down like numbers so you don't have to look at what it is." She tipped her chin toward him. "Did you do the sum out loud? In the room? Or did you just sit there being safe?"

The question went into him like a splinter working toward bone. He thought of the vote, the raised hands, the way the firelight had made everyone's faces the same color, guilt and mercy alike. He thought of the sigil on Hazel's door, and whose hand had painted it—his own, late, drunk, ordered to by men who wanted a message delivered and chose the messenger who wouldn't ask questions. *Loyalty beginning to rot*, he'd thought that night, and hadn't known yet whether the rot was theirs or his.

"I sat there," he said. "Being safe."

Wren nodded once, satisfied or finished, and looked away, and somehow that was worse than any curse she might have laid on him.

* * *

Hazel fed him eventually. That was the thing about Hazel that Brannick had never learned to guard against—they were scrupulous. Twenty years of ledgers had worn grooves in them so deep that even now, hunting fugitives on a frozen pass with a price on their head, they would not let a guest go unfed. He got barley broth and a heel of black bread and a strip of dried fish that tasted of the sea and of better days, and he ate it slowly because eating slowly was a kind of honesty too.

They talked while the light died. Or rather, Hazel talked, in that low auditing voice, asking questions the way other people asked directions: Where were the hunters massed? How many? Who led them—a name, a rank, a habit? Did the crown's list circulate beyond Ashgate, or was it sealed? Had anyone else run?

He answered what he could. Serell Vane led the hunt, a woman with a reputation for closing accounts. Fourteen riders, maybe twenty by now; crowns bred soldiers the way rot bred flies. The list was sealed, but seals leaked; every smuggler south of the pass already knew a bounty walked the salt roads. And yes—others had run. Three deserters in the first week, then seven, then a dozen, melting away into the hills with their blades and their grudges and their borrowed luck, calling themselves free.

"They call themselves the Unsworn now," Brannick said. "There's talk of heading over the mountains entirely, past the crown's reach, maybe to the coast. Ships. New names." He turned the wooden spoon in his fingers, watching the broth cool. "There's a place in it for you."

And there it was. He heard the offer leave his mouth and recognized it, distantly, the way you recognize your own handwriting on a note you don't remember writing. He'd rehearsed it on the trail, polished it, told himself it was rescue: *Come with me. Leave the girl to her fate, leave the god to its hunger, save yourself.* And underneath the rehearsal, deeper than the polish, another voice had been talking the whole time, quiet and reasonable, saying: *Bring her in yourself and the pardon covers you too. Bring them both and you'll never count coins again.*

"There's a place in it for both of us," he amended, and hated how smoothly the amendment came. "Coast, ships, new names. The god's silent, Hazel. Whatever oath you're carrying, it's a stone tied to a drowned man. Let it sink. Come be free."

Hazel was quiet for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, snow let go of a pine bough with a soft whump, and the whole dark seemed to listen.

"You marked my door," Hazel said at last. Not accusing. Just placing the entry in its column, the way they placed everything.

"I did." No point denying it; his hand, his brush, his guilt. "Because Torvald asked, and because refusing Torvald in front of the others had a price I didn't want to pay. That's the whole of it. That's the man you're talking to, Hazel. I'm not brave and I'm not loyal and I'm not good. I'm a survivor with a sword, and I'm offering you the same trade I took: live small, live long, look the other way." He leaned forward, letting the firelight find his face, letting them see every year of it. "It's not a bad life. It's just a smaller one than you were promised."

"Who promised me anything?"

"Your mother did." The words were out before he could weigh them, and once out, they could not be bought back. "Everyone knew she had plans for you. Big ones. Names picked out, a future sketched. Dominic, she used to say, when she'd had enough drink to be tender. *My Dominic.*" He shrugged, uncomfortable under Hazel's stare. "I figured you'd want to know somebody remembers."

Something moved behind Hazel's eyes—grief, or its accountant, entering it into some inner ledger Brannick would never see. When they spoke again their voice was level as a frozen lake.

"Thank you for the warning, Brannick. Genuinely. Now tell me the rest of it."

"The rest of what?"

"The part you practiced on the trail." Hazel's gaze didn't waver. "You're a bad liar, but you're a good salesman, and salesmen always bury the real price in the middle of the pitch. So. The Unsworn, the coast, the ships—fine. But somewhere in that speech there's a number, or a name, or a favor owed. Give it to me now, while we're warm, rather than later, when we're not."

Brannick sat with it. The fire burned down between them, patient as a creditor. He could lie. He'd lied his whole life; it was practically a trade skill. But lying to Hazel felt like forging a signature in front of the notary—possible, and then impossible, and then you were simply a man sitting in the snow holding a broken pen.

"Serell Vane knows I came after you," he said finally. "She let me ride out alone. Which means either she's a fool—which she isn't—or I'm carrying something for her." He reached into his coat, slow, showing his hand the whole way, and drew out a folded paper sealed with the crown's gray wax. "A writ of amnesty. My name on it. Conditional." He set it on the snow between them, where the firelight made the seal glow like a coal. "The condition is written inside. I haven't opened it. I swear to you on whatever's left of my honor that I haven't opened it. But we both know what it says, Hazel. We both know what I was supposed to do to earn it."

Wren had gone white. Hazel hadn't moved at all.

"So why are you telling us?" Wren whispered.

"Because I sat in that voting room and did the sum," Brannick said, "and I've been doing it every night since, and every night the answer comes out different, and I'm tired of sums." He laughed, short and ugly, at himself. "Twenty years I've survived by never choosing. Tonight the choice came and sat down at my fire and ate my bread, and I find I can't—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I can't be the hand that does it. That's all. That's the whole confession. I'm not offering you redemption. I'm offering you a coward who finally found a line he won't cross, and doesn't know what he is on the far side of it."

The wind moved through the hollow. The paper lay on the snow between them, sealed, patient, doing what sealed things do best: waiting.

Hazel picked it up. Turned it over once, reading the wax the way they read everything—thoroughly, without touching what mattered. Then they held it out over the fire.

"Don't," Brannick said sharply. "That's my—"

"Your what? Your pardon? Your proof you almost betrayed us?" Hazel's eyes caught the flame-light. "No. This is evidence. This is a name in a ledger, and I have spent my whole life learning what happens to names in ledgers." They did not burn it. Instead they tucked it inside their coat, deliberately, letting him watch it disappear. "You'll get it back when I decide what it's worth. Until then, consider it collateral."

"Collateral," Brannick repeated. The word tasted like ash and, strangely, like relief. "Against what?"

"Against the truth." Hazel rose, brushing snow from their knees, and looked down at him with something that was not forgiveness and not condemnation but the cold, clear attention of an auditor opening a fresh page. "Vane let you ride out alone, Brannick. Think about what that means. Not that she trusts you. That she *needs* you. Hunters who need a traitor always have a reason, and reasons have shapes, and shapes can be traced." They glanced south, toward the dark bulk of the mountain, where the stars ended in a jagged line. "You wanted to know what you are on the far side of your one uncrossed line? Fine. Walk with us tomorrow and find out. But sleep tonight knowing this: if Vane sent you, then Vane already knows where we are. And if she already knows where we are—"

Hazel stopped. Wren had gone rigid beside the fire, staring past Brannick's shoulder, up the slope, at the ridgeline where the pines stood black against the sky.

"—then someone's been listening to every word," she finished.

Brannick turned. High on the ridge, where no fire burned and no camp should be, a lantern kindled. Once. Twice. Three times—the signal he knew, the signal every guild mercenary knew, the signal that meant *found them, hold them, help is coming.*

Help was coming. Down through the dark and the snow, patient as a debt, it was coming.
