## 11. Descent Beneath the Mountain

The debtor's door was not where the smugglers' maps said it would be. It never was. Debts, Hazel had learned in eleven years of keeping the Salt Guild's ledgers, did not live where you expected them; they lived where they could be forgotten longest, in the seams of things, behind the plaster of ordinary walls.

They found it on the fourth day beneath the mountain's shoulder, in a village called Lowmere that appeared on no map and paid taxes to no crown—a village of slate roofs and smoke-stained chimneys where every third house stood empty and every empty house had its windows bricked over from the inside. The door itself was set into the side of a well-house, low and narrow, made of black oak gone silver with age, and it bore no handle. Instead, in the center of the wood, a slot had been worn smooth by centuries of hands: a slot exactly the width of a ledger page.

Hazel stood before it in the grey light before dawn, breath fogging, the forged collector's credentials heavy inside their coat, and felt the old familiar sensation—the one their mother must have felt at every threshold she ever crossed—of being weighed.

*You are carrying unpaid things,* the door seemed to say. *Everything that crosses here does.*

They tore a page from the false ledger Brannick had procured for them—a page recording a debt owed by one *A. Villalobos, deceased, balance outstanding*—and fed it into the slot. The wood drank it. Somewhere below, deep enough that Hazel felt it through the soles of their boots rather than heard it, something turned over like a sleeper deciding whether to wake.

The door opened inward onto darkness, and cold air rose out of it smelling of stone and candlewax and, faintly, unbearably, of salt.

Hazel went down.

* * *

The stair was older than the village above it, older perhaps than the mountain itself, or so it seemed—steps worn into concavities by feet that had been climbing and descending since before anyone thought to keep count. Hazel counted anyway. It was what they did. Numbers were the only prayer they trusted, and by the nine hundredth step they had stopped counting steps and started counting debts instead, cataloging them the way other travelers counted rosary beads:

*I owe Wren a rescue. I owe my mother an answer. I owe Brannick nothing, which is its own kind of debt now. I owe the silent god eleven years of service unperformed, oaths sworn and unfelt, and it is waiting somewhere below me with its mouth open.*

The light changed as they descended. Not brighter—that would have been simple—but *more*, as if the darkness itself were being itemized, each shadow assigned its own particular quality of dimness, and the eye, unable to resist an inventory, kept reading.

Then the court opened beneath them, and Hazel forgot how to breathe.

* * *

It was not a cavern. That was the first horror. Caverns were accidents of water and time, and this was nothing accidental. It was a hall vast beyond reason, pillared with columns of pale stone that rose and branched like trees, and between the columns hung chandeliers—not of crystal but of *glass bottles*, thousands upon thousands of them, each one stoppered and labeled, each one glowing faintly with a light that had no source except itself. Bottled grief, some of them. Hazel recognized the amber shimmer from the guild's own winter inventory. Borrowed luck, green-gold and restless. Second chances, which burned white and hurt to look at directly, the way snow hurts.

And souls. They knew that now. They had known it since the night they deciphered their mother's handwriting, but knowing a thing in ink and seeing it hanging by the hundred in a chandelier overhead were different knowings entirely, and the second one sat in Hazel's chest like swallowed ice.

The beauty of it was the worst part. That was the trap, they understood immediately—understood it the way you understand a con only after your purse is already lighter. Every surface gleamed. Every proportion pleased. The floor was veined marble polished to a mirror, and when Hazel looked down into it they saw not their own reflection but a version of themselves *arranged*: coat straightened, chin lifted, grief composed into something dignified and almost noble. The court did not show you what you were. It showed you what you might be worth.

*Everything owed,* Hazel thought, walking carefully across the marble, *nothing owned.* Because that was the truth humming under all this splendor, plain as a merchant's mark on a crate. The chandeliers did not belong to the court; they were collateral. The pillars themselves were mortgaged—Hazel could see the faint script running up the fluted stone like ivy, terms and conditions carved in a hand too small to read at any distance, and they suspected no distance was sufficient. You could spend a lifetime reading the fine print of this place and die still owing the reading.

A figure approached across the mirror-floor without seeming to walk. It was tall and grey-robed and its face was pleasant the way a receipt is pleasant: precise, official, bearing no expression that had not been stamped there by someone else.

"Collector," it said. Its voice was dry paper. "You are early."

"Debts don't wait," Hazel said, and was relieved to hear their own voice come out level, guild-trained, bored even. "Neither do audits."

"The last collector came forty years ago." The steward—it could be nothing else—tilted its head. "She also said debts don't wait."

Hazel's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of treachery. *Mother.* Forty years. Before Wren. Before the erasure. Before everything, then—or near enough to everything. "I'm not the last," Hazel said. "I'm the current one. The office continues."

"Offices continue." The steward produced, from somewhere within its robe, a ledger of its own—black binding, silver clasps, and when it opened it, Hazel's stomach dropped, because the pages were blank and yet somehow full, the way silence in a large room is full. "State your authority."

Hazel had rehearsed this. They drew the credentials Brannick had bought from a forger in Saltcross who charged triple for work intended to fool the dead, and held them out with the flat confidence of a man who has never once been audited himself.

The steward read them. Time behaved strangely while it read; Hazel had the sense that hours passed, or years, or that the interval simply wasn't tracked because nothing down here accrued interest except people.

"Auditor of Standing Accounts," the steward pronounced at last. "You will inspect the vaults. You will verify holdings against obligations. You will sign nothing." A pause, thin as a knife's edge. "Signatures have weight here."

"I know," Hazel said. "That's why I brought my own pen."

Something that might, in a living face, have been the ghost of amusement crossed the steward's features and died there politely. "Follow."

* * *

They walked the court's length, and Hazel made themselves look at everything, because looking was the job now, looking was survival, and because some traitor part of them wanted to memorize the beauty even knowing what it cost. The galleries along the walls were lined with doors—hundreds, thousands—each one smaller than the last as the corridor curved away, and each door had a nameplate. Not wood. Not metal. *Wax*, poured and pressed, names written into soft wax and left to harden, and Hazel understood with a lurch that these were not labels but seals, that the names had been poured into their shapes like molten lead into molds, and that to open any door you would first have to break the name.

"The vault of names lies below the treasury," the steward said, gesturing with a grey sleeve toward a stair descending from the main floor, wide enough for processions, guarded by statues whose faces were smooth as eggs. "Standing accounts are verified quarterly. Yours is overdue by—" it consulted nothing, "—one quarter, eleven days, six hours."

"War in the provinces," Hazel said. "Roads closed."

"There is always war in the provinces." The steward's tone did not change, but the air around it did, cooling by a degree. "There is always a reason a collector does not come. The court keeps reasons in the same vault as everything else."

They passed the treasury proper, and Hazel made the mistake of glancing in. Gold, yes, mountains of it, but gold stacked in the shape of *amounts*, arranged into sums, each pile a figure mid-calculation, and among the gold lay objects Hazel recognized with a sickness that climbed their throat: a child's shoe. A wedding ring sized for two fingers. A lock of hair braided with ribbon, red as rowan berries—Wren's color, Wren's exact shade, and Hazel had to close their eyes for three full strides and count backward from thirty before they could trust their face again.

*She was sold here. Her soul is filed here. And her mother took payment in whatever form, deferred, deferred, and my mother signed the collateral line with her own sure hand, and every kindness she ever showed me is re-priced in a book below my feet.*

"You are distressed," the steward observed.

"I'm appalled by your filing system," Hazel said. "Souls stored adjacent to jewelry? Any competent guild would segregate holdings by class. Sentiment contaminates valuation."

This, apparently, was the correct thing to say. The steward slowed its glide by half a step, regarding Hazel with renewed attention, the way a moneylender regards a debtor who suddenly shows unexpected assets. "The previous auditor said something similar."

"Good habits persist."

"Mm." The steward turned down a narrower gallery, where the light from the bottle-chandeliers fell differently—thinner, more careful, as if even the glow here were being rationed against future need. "Before the audit begins, you should understand the terms of your presence. All visitors to the court are entered. All entries accrue. Your inspection is authorized." The grey head inclined. "Your *departure* is a separate account."

"I'm aware," Hazel lied, smoothly, having not been aware until precisely that moment.

"Good. Collectors who are aware fare better than collectors who are surprised." The steward halted before a door unlike the others—taller, older, its wax seal unmarked, its nameplate blank. "The standing accounts. Verify as much as you require. I will return when the bells toll." A pause. "We do not have bells. You will hear them regardless."

It dissolved. There was no better word. One moment the steward occupied the corridor like a held breath; the next, the breath released, and Hazel stood alone before the blank-named door with their heart hammering out the only honest arithmetic they had left.

* * *

Inside, the standing accounts stretched farther than the hall above—shelves, cases, cabinets in ranks receding into a gloom that was respectful rather than dark, and every document in the place was *warm*. Hazel felt it as they walked the aisles: a faint animal heat rising off the paper, as if each contract slept, or dreamed, or remembered being alive.

They found the V drawer by following the alphabet the way a pilgrim follows stations. Vellum folders, each tabbed in that same small, terrible handwriting the steward used, and Hazel pulled the folder marked **VILLALOBOS** with hands that shook only after the folder was safely open, only once it was too late for shaking to matter.

The original contract lay inside. Their mother's signature sat at the bottom like a bird fallen out of a nest—familiar, broken, unmistakable. Above it, the terms. Hazel read them twice, three times, mouthing the words as though taste could verify what sight reported, and the aisle's warm dark pressed in around them while the sentence rebuilt itself in their chest, brick by freezing brick:

*A soul delivered to the court, minor female, in exchange for the perpetual removal of one name from all lists of hunt and seizure. Collateral pledged against default: the signer's issue, Hazel Villalobos, redeemable at the court's discretion.*

Redeemable. Like a coupon. Like a pawned watch. Eleven years of grief curdled, in the space of one paragraph, into something harder and more useful: *purpose with a blade's edge.* Her mother had traded Wren's soul to scrub Hazel's name from the crown's list—and the list had found them anyway, which meant somewhere in this court there existed a second copy, a failure of paperwork, a debt still open on both ends. Contracts failed. Even here. Especially here.

Hazel copied the reference number into memory—*Vault of Names, tier seven, case nine, shelf four*—and was sliding the folder home when they saw it: a fresh page tucked at the back of the file, newer vellum, newer ink, dated three days past.

An intake record. A soul received en route, escorted, expected.

The name on the page was *Wren.*

Not filed yet. *In transit. Below.* Already taken—taken on the road south, taken while Hazel negotiated upstairs with beautiful lies—and arriving now, tonight, into tier seven.

Far off, through stone and silence, Hazel heard the bells begin to toll.
