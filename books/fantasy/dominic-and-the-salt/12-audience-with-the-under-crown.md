## 12. Audience with the Under-Crown

The under-crown did not summon Hazel so much as *remember* them into its presence.

One moment they were standing in the antechamber of the debtor's door, the salt-iron taste of the mountain still coating their teeth, and the next the walls had simply agreed to be elsewhere. The corridor unfolded like a page being turned by a patient hand—stone giving way to galleries of pale root-glass, root-glass to halls hung with chandeliers that dripped upward, light pooling on the ceiling like rain deciding against falling. Everything here was beautiful in the way of an unpaid bill: immaculate, itemized, and quietly threatening.

Hazel walked because there was nothing else to do. Their boots made no sound on the floor, and neither did anything else. The court beneath the mountain was silent the way a ledger is silent—all its noise pressed flat between covers, waiting for someone with the authority to open it.

*You are expected,* the door had said. Not a voice. A fact.

They passed courtiers who were not courtiers. Figures of exquisite proportion stood in alcoves along the route, dressed in fashions from eleven different decades, and none of them breathed. Hazel's gift—the thing they had spent a lifetime folding small and hiding in the lining of themselves—twitched toward each one like a compass needle. Not alive. Not dead. *Filed.* Each figure was a soul kept in good condition, dusted regularly, displayed as proof of the court's solvency. One of them, near the end of the gallery, wore a runner's coat with the Salt Guild's stitch at the collar, and Hazel had to look at the floor for six steps before they could look up again. It wasn't Wren. It wasn't. But grief has poor eyesight and excellent aim.

Then the last door, which was not a door but a threshold where the air changed weight, and Hazel stepped through into the presence of the Under-Crown.

It sat—or rather, the room arranged itself around the place where sitting would be, if the thing had needed a body. What Hazel saw was a shape of gathered dark wearing a circlet of what looked like frozen debt: rings of ice within ice, each band engraved so finely that the engravings could only be read from inside. When it moved—and it did move, slowly, the way continents move—a sound came off it like pages turning in another room.

*Hazel Zhou,* it said, and the name arrived not in their ears but in their memory, spoken in their mother's handwriting. *Keeper of ledgers. Sworn of the silent god. Daughter of Mei Zhou, who paid well and paid once.*

"I'm not her daughter down here," Hazel said. "Down here I'm a collector. I've come to audit old accounts."

The dark shape inclined, amused or something shaped like amusement. *Yes. They always say that. The ones who come for their people. You all arrive speaking the language of business because business is the only tongue that doesn't tremble.*

Hazel kept their hands still. In the guild they'd learned that stillness was a currency too; spend it wisely. "Then let's keep the language clean. There's a soul filed in your vault. Wren. Youngest runner of the Salt Guild. I'm here to close her account."

*To close it,* the Under-Crown repeated, tasting the words. *Or to steal it? We know the difference. Thieves come in loud. Auditors come in with their mother's face.*

The words landed harder than any blow. Hazel felt their jaw set and hated how legible it must have been.

"Show me the contract," they said. "If you want to talk about my mother, show me the paper."

*Gladly.* The Under-Crown rose—not stood, *rose*, the way water rises in a locked room—and gestured with a limb that might have been an arm if arms were made of overdue time. Behind it, a section of wall opened like a throat.

What came out was a table. And on the table, a book.

Hazel knew books. Hazel had spent twenty years knowing books—their weights, their smells, the way a ledger lies in the hands when its entries are honest versus the way it sits when someone has cooked it. This book was old in a way that hurt: bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, corners rounded by decades of being held, held, held. On the spine, in faded gilt: *Zhou.*

Their mother's ledger. The private one. The one that had vanished the winter she died.

Hazel approached it the way you approach a grave you've only ever seen in dreams. The cover was warm. That was the worst part. Eleven years in the dark of the mountain and the cover was warm as a sleeping child.

"Open it," Hazel said, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they decided to let it crack. Let the court see. Some things were worth more spent than saved.

The book opened itself to a page near the end, and there it was—Mei Zhou's hand, the strong slanted script Hazel had learned their letters from, tracing the same alphabet across grocery lists and guild inventories and the margins of bedtime stories. Except this entry was different. This entry was written in two inks.

The first ink was brown-black, ordinary, human:

*Bought: erasure. Subject: H. Zhou, minor, age nine. Removal from crown registry of soul-craft bearers. Permanence guaranteed. Price negotiated below.*

The second ink was silver, and it moved. As Hazel watched, the silver characters rearranged themselves like fish schooling, and the price wrote itself out in full:

*Paid: one soul, deferred delivery. Collateral until delivery: the buyer's own line. Delivery date: upon the collateral's majority, or upon the crown's hunt reaching the subject, whichever comes first. Soul to be delivered: Wren, infant, currently residing [address]. Provenance: unclaimed. Unclaimed souls are lawful tender.*

Hazel read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. That was the horror of ledgers—they never changed, no matter how much you changed while reading them.

"No," Hazel said. It came out very small. "No, she wouldn't. She *hid* me. She taught me to hide. She never—"

*She never told you,* the Under-Crown agreed, gentle as interest accruing. *Buyers rarely discuss terms with the goods.*

*

The memories came anyway. That was the trap of it—Hazel had brought them along, every one, packed carefully for the journey south, and now the court unpacked them one by one and turned each over in its frozen light.

The winter their mother took them out of school and said, *you'll work the ledgers now, love, it's safer, people don't look at clerks.* Hazel had thought it was protection. Re-priced: it was concealment. A soul-crafting child off the registries, off the streets, folded into an inventory room where the crown would never think to check.

The night a crown assessor came to the house and their mother poured him wine and laughed at his jokes and sent him away smiling, and afterward she'd been sick for two days, shaking. Hazel had thought it was fear. Re-priced: it was the cost of the transaction closing. The erasure had been purchased that night. The assessor hadn't come to find a witch. He'd come to collect a signature.

And Wren. Sweet, sharp-eyed Wren, left on the guild's doorstep in a basket one spring morning, and Mei Zhou had carried her inside before anyone else could touch her, and everyone said how kind, how generous, how like her. Hazel remembered standing beside their mother looking down at the red-faced scrap of a baby and hearing her murmur, almost too soft to catch: *There you are. Right on time.*

Re-priced. *Right on time.* A delivery date arriving. An asset received, held in trust, fed and sheltered and taught to run messages—kept in good condition, the way the court kept its courtiers dusted—until the contract came due.

"She loved me," Hazel said to the empty beautiful room. Their voice sounded like someone else's. "Whatever else is true, she loved me."

*Both things can be entered in the same column,* said the Under-Crown. *That is what makes ledgers crueler than knives. A knife tells you only one story.*

Hazel wanted to burn the table. The book. The whole galleried, dripping, upward-raining splendor of the court. Instead they did what they had been trained to do since age nine, since the winter of the wine and the shaking, since the first torn page: they read the entry again, looking for the flaw.

Every contract had one. Every ledger balanced somewhere. Their mother had been the best bookkeeper the Salt Guild ever produced, and Mei Zhou did not sign papers without a back door.

"Permanence guaranteed," Hazel said slowly. "Erasure from the registry. But the crown's list has my name on it *now*. Fresh ink. Dated eleven years ago." They looked up into the frozen circlet. "Your guarantee failed. Or it was never meant to hold. Which is it?"

For the first time, the Under-Crown went still in a way that felt less like majesty and more like caution.

*The original entry was struck,* it said at last. *Struck cleanly, by the hand that wrote it. Your mother returned in the eleventh year and bought back the erasure—paid its reversal into the registry herself. We assumed she repented of the purchase.*

"You assumed wrong." Hazel's heart was going hard now, some cold arithmetic assembling itself behind their ribs. "She didn't repent. She *re-entered* me. Deliberately. Why would she put my name back on the hunt list unless—" and then they saw it, saw it whole, the way you see the whole shape of a debt only when the collector arrives.

Unless the erasure was never the point. Unless the erasure was bait. Unless their mother had known exactly what the court would do with a deferred soul, exactly how the vault worked, exactly what would happen when the hunt finally reached the Zhou name—and had built the whole thing backward from the rescue. Put Hazel on the list so Hazel would have a reason to come. Sold Wren's soul into the one vault in the world that could be robbed by someone carrying the right gift, with the right grudge, and the right mother's training.

*Or,* whispered the other voice, the tired one, the one that sounded like Hazel at three in the morning over a stubborn column of numbers, *or she was just desperate, and scared, and you're building a saint out of a woman who traded a child to save a child, because the alternative is that your whole childhood was a purchase price.*

The Under-Crown watched them think. It was very good at watching. It had, Hazel suspected, centuries of practice watching people discover what they were worth.

*You see the difficulty,* it said. *We hold a valid instrument. Signed, sealed, witnessed by the registry itself. The soul called Wren is ours by law older than your crown. And you—you are the collateral, come of age, walking freely through our halls as though debts were decorations.* The frozen bands of the circlet turned, slow as millstones. *We could take you both. The contract permits it. The contract *prefers* it.*

"But you haven't," Hazel said. "Because taking isn't collecting. You want the delivery made properly. Voluntarily. A soul handed over signs itself deeper than a soul seized—doesn't it? That's why the vault is full of *filed* things instead of *taken* things. Seizure leaves seams. You need me to agree."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere above them, impossibly far above, snow was falling on a mountain pass where a girl named Wren was either running free or already caught, and Hazel's chest ached around the not-knowing like a tooth.

*You are your mother's child,* the Under-Crown said, and it did not sound like a compliment. It sounded like an audit finding.

"I'm going to ask you one question," Hazel said. "And then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to hear it, because you showed me the book, and showing the book means you'd rather deal than devour. Even down here, even you, it's cheaper to trade than to fight a keeper on their own arithmetic."

*Ask.*

"The god I'm sworn to. The silent one. My mother dealt with you—but she *swore* to it. Two masters, one woman. So tell me: when she came back in the eleventh year, when she re-entered my name on the hunt list—did she come alone?" Hazel leaned forward over the warm grey book. "Or did she bring the god's sigil with her? Because I found its mark burned into the margin of her last page, and I found it carved on my own door three nights ago, and I am starting to think the silent god and this court have been running *joint accounts* for a very long time. And if that's true, then half of everything you own is mortgaged to something that eats its creditors."

The chandeliers dripped their upward light. The filed courtiers stood in their alcoves, dusted and patient. And the Under-Crown, ruler of everything owed beneath the world, drew back a fraction—an inch, no more—but Hazel had spent a lifetime reading the body language of balance sheets, and an inch, down here, was a scream.

*You have been talking to the servants we released,* it said. *On the icefield. In the shrine. They remember fragments. Fragments are not accounts.*

"They're receipts," Hazel said. "And I've got more coming. So here's my offer: leave Wren's page blank for one night. One night, your vault, my access—as an auditor, under your seal. If I can't show you a lien on your own throne by dawn, you can take me instead of her. Both of us. The contract prefers it, you said so yourself."

The dark shape considered. Around it, the room seemed to lean in, the way creditors lean.

*One night,* the Under-Crown said. *Auditor's seal. And keeper—if you are lying, if there is no lien, no joint account, no god's mark in our margins—then the vault will not need to be told to keep you. It will simply add your shelf.*

"Fair terms," said Hazel, whose hands, hidden in the folds of their coat, had closed around the memory-shard Wren had smuggled out word of—the shard that hummed against their palm even now, faint and furious, like a lock picking itself.

Somewhere deep in the mountain, a bell that had no rope began to ring.

The Under-Crown's frozen bands stopped turning.

"That," Hazel said quietly, "would be my colleague."

* * *

They were escorted—remembered, rather, from hall to hall—back toward the vault levels, past the alcove with the runner's coat, past doors that breathed shut behind them. The escort peeled away at the mouth of the descending stair, and Hazel stood alone in the dark that smelled of salt and old paper, listening to the bell ring on and on, irregular, urgent, human.

Wren was in there. Wren was awake in there. And whatever the girl had just done to make an entire under-mountain ring its alarms, she had done it alone, with stolen tools, the way Hazel's mother would have.

*Right on time,* Hazel thought, and started down the stairs, taking them three at a time, toward the vault of names where every shelf had just begun to wake.
