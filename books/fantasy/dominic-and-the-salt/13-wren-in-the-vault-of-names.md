## 13. Wren in the Vault of Names

They took her boots first.

That was the thing Wren noticed, the detail she would carry with her through everything that came after—not the chains, not the dark, not the cold that seeped up through the floor like the mountain breathing—but the boots. They were good boots, smuggler's boots, bought two towns south with Hazel's coin, and a gray-handed steward had lifted them off her feet without a word and set them on a shelf, side by side, toes pointing out, as if they might be wanted again someday. As if she might be wanted again someday.

Then they had walked her down.

Down was the only word for it. The court beneath the mountain did not have stairs so much as it had descents—long ramps of pale stone worn smooth by centuries of feet, lit by lamps that burned without flame, giving light the way a miser gives coin: grudgingly, and never quite enough to see by. The air grew warmer as they went deeper, which felt wrong. Wren had grown up in the guild's cold lofts above the salt roads, where winter got into your bones and stayed like a tenant. Here, beneath all that ice and stone, the warmth pressed against her skin like a hand that hadn't been invited.

The steward who led her was called Ferrow, or he answered to Ferrow, though Wren suspected names worked differently down here. He had a kind face. That was the worst part. He had the face of someone's uncle, someone who fixed fences and remembered birthdays, and he spoke to her gently the whole way down, the way you'd speak to a spooked horse.

"You'll be comfortable," he said. "It's important to us that you're comfortable."

"Where's Hazel?" she asked, for the ninth time.

"Being received," Ferrow said, for the ninth time, in a tone that suggested the answer satisfied him completely and ought to satisfy her too.

"And if they don't come?"

He glanced back at her then, and his kind face did something complicated—a flicker of pity, quickly filed away. "Everyone comes," he said. "One way or another. It's the coming *back* that varies."

* * *

The Vault of Names opened before her like a cathedral built by accountants.

It was vast—the largest space she had ever stood inside, larger than the guild hall, larger than the temple at Ashgate whose bell had cracked the year she was born—and every surface of it was shelving. Shelves rose from the floor into a darkness the lamplight never reached, and the shelves were not wood or stone but something between: bone-pale, faintly warm, and carved with grooves in patterns that hurt her eyes when she tried to follow them, because they weren't decoration. They were writing. The whole vault was one continuous ledger, walls and pillars and ceiling, every surface inscribed with names.

And between the names, in niches cut to exactly the size of a small glass vessel, sat the souls.

Wren had expected jars. Bottled grief was jarred; borrowed luck came in stoppered flasks; the guild shipped second chances in crates padded with straw. But souls were not kept in glass. Each niche held a shape of soft light, no bigger than a fist, glowing a color that seemed to shift depending on how she looked at it—the blue of a winter morning, the gold of lamplight through honey, once, unbearably, the exact green of Hazel's eyes when Hazel laughed, which was rare enough that she'd memorized it.

She could hear them. That was what undid her, standing on the threshold while Ferrow waited with terrible patience. She could hear them, very faintly, the way you can hear the sea inside a shell. Not words. Murmurs. Hundreds of murmurs layered over hundreds more, going up into the dark, and every murmur was a person remembering themselves.

"There are so many," she whispered.

"Eleven thousand four hundred and six," Ferrow said, with the mild pride of a man reciting his orchard's yield. "Well. Eleven thousand four hundred and seven, now." He nodded at her, apologetic. "You've just made it an odd number. We do prefer even."

* * *

Her niche was on the third tier, which required a rolling ladder that a second steward cranked into place with a squeal of old metal. They did not chain her to it. There was nothing to chain her to—the niche was simply a hollow in the shelf-wall, sized for a soul, and she was still wearing her body, which meant there was nowhere in the vault she could stand that wasn't also somewhere she could walk. This, she understood dimly, was the courtesy extended to souls awaiting processing. Processing happened upstairs. Down here, the living were merely stored among the stored, like a letter left on the wrong desk overnight.

They gave her a blanket. They gave her water in a cup that never emptied, which frightened her more than the chains would have, and bread that tasted of nothing at all, perfectly nourishing and perfectly forgettable, food designed by someone who considered taste a rounding error.

And then they left her alone with eleven thousand murmuring lights.

She cried, at first. She wanted to report, later, to whoever might eventually hear the report, that she had been brave from the first moment, but honesty mattered to her the way salt matters to a ledger—everything balanced, everything true—so the record should show that Wren Osk, fourteen years old, youngest runner of the Salt Guild, sat on the third tier of the Vault of Names and cried until her ribs ached, and hated herself for it, and kept crying anyway.

But crying is a finite resource, like everything else in the under-mountain. Somewhere past the second hour, it ran dry, and what replaced it was colder and much more useful.

She started talking to the lights.

"I'm Wren," she said to the nearest one, a soft amber glow nested in its groove like a coal banked for the night. "I don't know if you can hear me. I don't know if you're *in* there or if you're just... the part that's left." The amber light did not answer, but its murmur shifted pitch, the way a sleeper shifts when someone says their name near the bed. Encouraged, she went on. "I'm going to get out of here. And I think—I think maybe some of you are supposed to come with me."

The murmur around her swelled, just slightly, like wind finding a gap in a shuttered house.

* * *

The fourth soul along the shelf was awake. Properly awake.

She learned this because it said, in a voice like dry leaves settling, "*Don't apologize to them.*"

Wren nearly fell off the ladder. "You can talk."

"*I can complain,*" said the light. Its glow was a faded silver, tarnished at the edges. "*Complaining is the last thing they let us keep. Everything else gets itemized. Complaints aren't worth filing.*"

"What's your name?"

A pause, long enough that she thought the conversation had ended. Then: "*Maret. I was Maret Voss. I sold my grief to the guild forty years ago and the guild sold my grief here, and apparently my grief was attached to the rest of me by more than the appraisers thought. Sloppy work. I've had forty years to be bitter about it, and bitterness, unlike grief, survives bottling.*"

Wren moved closer, careful, the way you approach a bird. "You were a client? Of the Salt Guild?"

"*I was a widow. Grief is heavy and the guild pays well for heavy things.*" A dry rustle that might have been a laugh. "*Ask your questions, girl. I can hear them queuing up behind your teeth.*"

So Wren asked. How long had she been here—forty years, give or take the years that didn't count, time below being measured in audits rather than seasons. Could she see out—no, but she could hear, the vault carried sound the way a bowl carries water, every word spoken on any tier pooling down to the lowest shelves. What happened during processing—

"*Processing,*" Maret said, and her silver light dimmed, "*is when they read you aloud. Your name, your debts, the terms of your sale. Read aloud, you become legible. Legible, you become extractable. They take whatever the contract specifies and file the rest. Some souls are bought whole. Most are bought in pieces. The pieces go upstairs to the court. The rest stays here, waiting, in case the buyer wants seconds.*"

"And the pieces—the person who's left—do they feel it?"

Another pause. Longer.

"*I don't remember my daughter's face,*" Maret said at last. "*I know I had a daughter. I know I loved her. I know her name began with L. The love is still here—that's the cruel arithmetic of it. They took the face and left the love, and now the love has nothing to hold. That's what processing feels like from inside. Like waking up and your hands are gone and you keep trying to pick things up.*"

Wren put her hand flat against the shelf beside Maret's niche. The stone was warm. "I'm sorry."

"*Stop apologizing.*"

"It's not an apology. It's—it's a debt. I owe you sorrow. I'm paying it forward until I can pay it properly."

The silver light brightened a fraction. "*Hm. You'd have done well in the old days, girl. Before everything became negotiable, people used to understand that some things are owed regardless of ability to pay.*" The light flickered. "*Your friend. The one they took upstairs. Hazel Villalobos' child.*"

The cold went through Wren all at once. "How do you know that name?"

"*Because I heard the original transaction read aloud, forty-one years ago, on a night when the vault was quieter and my ears—such as they were—were sharper. A woman came down here to sell something no appraiser had ever priced before. She sold a debt she didn't yet own. She sold the erasure of a name from a list that hadn't been written yet.*" Maret's voice dropped, leaves settling deeper. "*The under-crown loves those. Pre-emptive purchases. Buy the misfortune before it ripens. Her name was Sefa Villalobos. The collateral she named was her own child. And the goods she purchased with that collateral were eleven years of a hunted childhood—no, wait. Let an old woman count. Eleven years and counting. The contract isn't finished, is it. That's why you're here.*"

Wren's mouth had gone dry, though the cup that never emptied sat full at her elbow. "Hazel doesn't know. About the terms. They think their mother *protected* them."

"*Child,*" said Maret gently, "*down here we learn that protection and purchase are spelled differently but pronounced the same.*"

* * *

Sleep did not come, exactly, but the vault imposed its own rhythm on her, a slow tidal drowsiness, as if the mountain breathed in hours instead of seconds. In the shallow half-sleep she dreamed without images—just the murmurs, folding around her, thousands of voices saying their own names to themselves in the dark, keeping themselves real the only way left to them.

She woke to footsteps.

Ferrow again, making his rounds with a lantern that cast no shadows, checking niches the way a shepherd counts sheep. He paused at her tier, looked at her sitting upright in her blanket, and gave her the uncle-smile.

"Comfortable?" he asked, sincerely.

"The bread has no taste," Wren said.

"We find taste encourages attachment. Attachment complicates processing." He tilted his head. "You slept well, for one so new. Most of them cry for days."

"Most of them planned on staying alive longer."

Something flickered behind Ferrow's kindness—annoyance, maybe, or respect; down here the two probably shared a drawer. He moved on, lantern bobbing, and Wren watched him go and thought, with a clarity that surprised her: *he checks the niches in order. Left to right, bottom to top. Every night. The same order.*

People who follow the same order every night have stopped expecting anything to disobey it.

* * *

It was Maret who told her about the shards.

"*Third niche from the end, top tier, directly above the ladder anchor. A soul named Ellery Quinn, gambler, sold his luck in installments until there wasn't enough left to lose. When they processed him, a splinter of his last lucky memory broke off during extraction. Faulty work. It fell behind the shelf and nobody filed it.*" A rustling pause. "*A shard of pure luck, girl. Small as a tooth. Luck is the most physical thing in this vault—it likes to be held. If you can reach behind that shelf, you can hold it.*"

"Why would I want a lucky memory?"

"*Because,*" Maret said, and her tarnished silver flared briefly bright, "*luck is the one substance the vault cannot bind. Bindings are contracts, and contracts require both parties to act according to their natures. Luck's nature is to misbehave. Hold a shard of luck against any lock in this mountain, and the lock will have a very unlucky evening.*"

Wren lay in the dark doing arithmetic. The ladder anchor was thirty feet away, up a tier, and the stewards made rounds twice a night, and her arms were short and the gap behind the top-tier shelving would be narrow as a coffin. Against that: she was the Salt Guild's fastest runner. She had spent six years learning every shortcut in a city that rearranged itself seasonally. She had once delivered bottled courage through a riot by walking calmly through the middle of it, because the fastest route is sometimes the one nobody thinks to guard.

And against all of it, the largest entry in the column: Hazel was somewhere above her, being *received*, and every hour that passed was an hour in which the under-crown's gentle, patient machinery turned Hazel's history into entries and entries into leverage.

She owed Hazel. That was the oldest line in her personal ledger, written before she could write, in the currency of a life pulled off a salt road and given a roof and a name and someone who swore—swore, out loud, with the god as witness—to keep her safe. Debts to family were payable in comfort. This debt was payable in motion.

On the second night, when Ferrow's lantern had passed and his footsteps faded up the long ramp, Wren climbed.

The ladder screamed a little under her weight and she froze each time, counting the silence, resuming on the eighth breath. The top tier was dust and darkness and murmurs so dense they felt like weather. She found Ellery Quinn's niche—third from the end—and knelt, and reached into the gap behind the shelf, into a space narrow and warm as the inside of a cupped hand, and felt, after a minute of blind searching that scraped her knuckles raw, something small and smooth and *alive*.

It hummed against her palm. It felt like the moment before dice land. It felt like Hazel's rare laugh.

"*Careful now,*" Maret whispered up from three tiers down, her voice carrying through the shelf-bone like a whisper through a wall. "*Luck bites the greedy. Tell it what you want. Plainly. Luck respects plain speech.*"

Wren closed her fingers around the shard and said, quietly, to the little warm scrap of a dead gambler's best day:

"I want the lock on the third-tier gate to fail tonight. Nobody hurt. Nobody blamed but me. Just—open."

The shard pulsed once, like a heartbeat agreeing to terms.

Below her, at the far end of the tier, the iron gate that sealed the stairwell—iron forged, Ferrow had boasted, with binding-script hammered into every link—gave a soft, tired click, and swung open an inch, and hung there in the dark like a held breath.

Wren stayed crouched on the top tier, heart hammering, and did not move toward it. Not yet. Because through the inch of open gate, drifting down from the levels above, she heard voices—one of them Ferrow's, deferential now, almost frightened—and the other one low and level and familiar, a voice that had read her bedtime stories from shipping manifests and taught her which lies in a ledger smell different from the truth.

Hazel's voice. Coming *down*.

And threaded beneath it, so deep in the mountain's murmur that she almost missed it, something else: a vast, slow attention turning over in its sleep, the sound of eleven thousand souls inhaling at once—as if every name in the vault had just felt the same draft under the same door, and was waiting, with her, to see who walked through.
