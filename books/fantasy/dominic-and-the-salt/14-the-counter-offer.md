## 14. The Counter-Offer

The under-crown did not sit on a throne. That was the first thing Hazel understood, and understanding it felt like a key turning in a lock they hadn't known was in their chest.

Thrones were for kings who needed to be taller than their subjects. The thing that ruled beneath the mountain had no such need. It occupied the center of its court like a debt occupies a household—everywhere at once, invisible until called upon, and then inescapable. It was a shape of pale stone and older shadow, folded through with veins of silver that pulsed in slow rhythm, and when Hazel looked directly at it, their eyes reported something different each time: a woman crowned with icicles; a child seated backwards on a chair too large; an open ledger so vast its pages were roads. It wore whatever its visitor owed it most.

Hazel owed it nothing yet. That was the only advantage they had, and they meant to spend it carefully.

"Auditor," the under-crown said, and its voice came from everywhere the way damp comes from stone. "You've come far to read our books."

"I've come to settle one account." Hazel kept their hands visible, empty, at their sides—the posture of an honest collector. Every muscle beneath the skin wanted to reach for the knife strapped against their forearm, but knives were currency down here too, and spending one now would be spending it cheaply. "One soul. Wren, entered under the name Wren Ashkar, sold by contract eleven years past, payment deferred, collateral named." They did not say *me*. Naming the collateral aloud would make it real in the way this place made things real. "I'm authorized to negotiate terms of release."

"Authorized." The silver veins brightened, dimmed. Somewhere in the vast dark behind the dais—or what passed for a dais, a shelf of black marble hung with chains of glass—a sound like pages turning, though nothing moved. "By whom? The guild? The guild sold you before you crossed my threshold, little auditor. By your god? Your god is very busy eating." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "By whom?"

"By myself." Hazel let the words fall flat and unadorned, the way one enters a figure that cannot be rounded or disguised. "That authorization is sufficient. I'm told you honor private contracts."

The court laughed. Not the thing itself—it had no lungs—but the court: the tall figures of the under-mountain nobility, beautiful the way frost on a drowned man's face is beautiful, their laughter ringing off the vaulted dark in harmonies that made Hazel's back teeth ache. Everything here was lovely. That was the trap laid plain, and Hazel walked its length anyway, because there was no other road.

*Count,* they told themselves, as they had been counting since the debtor's door. *Everything. Doors, distances, guards, turns.*

There were three ways out of the audience hall that Hazel had seen. The great stair they'd descended, now flanked by guards whose spears were made of frozen oaths—Hazel could feel them from here, cold and righteous, the way a scar feels in winter. A service corridor behind the dais, low-doored, through which servants of smoked glass carried trays of small bright objects Hazel chose not to examine too closely. And the floor itself: sections of it translucent, and beneath the translucent sections, shelves. Rows and rows of shelves, going down farther than the eye could follow, each shelf lined with names.

*Eleven rows visible from where I stand. Four aisles between them. Lanterns every third shelf, burning without flame.* Hazel catalogued the sight the way their mother had taught them to catalogue inventory—without mercy, without wonder, wonder being a tax the heart pays when the mind should be working. *Wren will be filed somewhere below the third tier. Souls of value are kept deep. She was bought young and healthy and whole; she'll have appreciated.*

The thought made bile rise, and Hazel swallowed it and turned the nausea into arithmetic.

"You are your mother's child," the under-crown said.

The words landed harder than any blade could have. Hazel had prepared for threats, for bargains, for the velvet menace of a creditor laying out terms. They had not prepared to be *recognized*, and they hated how much it shook them, and they used the shaking, because that was what their mother's training was actually for—not ledgers, not numbers, but the discipline of turning every wound into a working tool.

"So I've been told," Hazel said. "By hunters, by smugglers, by a tollkeeper who charged me double for the resemblance. I'm given to understand it's a marketable face."

"It is a face I helped choose."

The court went quiet around that sentence, quiet in the specific way of rooms where everyone knows something the visitor doesn't, and Hazel felt the ground tilt beneath the conversation without any ground moving at all.

*File that,* they told themselves, though their hands had gone cold. *File it and do not open it now. Open it never, if you're lucky. Open it later, if you must.*

"The contract," Hazel said. "Show it to me."

* * *

They brought it out on a tray of smoked glass, carried by a servant whose face was a smooth oval with two dents where eyes might once have been pressed, like thumbs in wet clay. The contract itself was modest. That was the horror of it—that all this, the hunt list, the burned witch in Ashgate square, the flight across the icefield, Wren's whole stolen life, rested on a single page of careful handwriting no larger than a merchant's receipt.

Hazel knew the hand immediately. Of course they did. They had learned letters copying it.

*Sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain: one soul, female child, age six, designation "runner." Payment deferred to maturity of subject. Collateral: my own child, Hazel Zhou, who shall be struck from all lists and hunts and reckonings, and shall remain struck, provided the collateral presents itself for collection upon demand or upon my death, whichever summons first.*

Signed in salt. Signed in blood. Signed in the particular exhausted pressure of a pen held by someone who had already been crying and had decided, mid-stroke, to stop.

*She didn't sell Wren,* Hazel thought, and the thought arrived like a lamp in a cellar, terrible and clarifying both. *She traded her. Traded her for me. Eleven years ago, when the first hunt lists went up, my mother put my name on the crown's paper herself—wrote it there, dated it, made sure it would be found—so that she could buy my erasure with something the court wanted more.*

And Wren had been the something. Six years old. Already running packages through the guild's back corridors, quick and laughing, the youngest runner anyone could remember. Their mother had looked at the two children in her keeping and chosen which one the mountain would take.

*Or she hadn't chosen at all,* whispered some stubborn corner of Hazel that refused arithmetic. *Maybe the court set the terms. Maybe collateral was the price of any erasure, and Wren was simply what she had to pledge against it. Maybe—*

But the page was in front of them, and the page did not say maybe. The page said *collateral: my own child*, and beside it, in smaller script, in the same exhausted hand: *Forgive me. Forgive me. Forgive me.* Three times, like a charm, like a prayer to a god who wasn't listening even then.

Hazel became aware, distantly, that their face had done something. That the court was watching it happen with the appetite of people at a play.

"Do you see now," the under-crown said gently, "why we accepted the trade? Grief makes excellent collateral. Yours especially. We have watched it compound for eleven years at considerable interest." The silver veins pulsed. "Your mother believed she was buying you a life. In a sense, she was. She just misunderstood whose ledger the payments would land in."

"What do you want," Hazel said. Not a question. They made it not a question by force of voice alone, and was distantly proud of that, even as everything else in them lay in pieces on the black marble floor.

*Three exits. Eleven rows. Guards at the stair, eight of them. Servants come through the low door every forty count—I timed it twice. The lanterns burn without flame, which means they burn on something else, which means they can be starved of it. Count. Count. Count.*

* * *

The negotiation took hours, and Hazel let it take hours, because every hour was another hour of walking the court's halls under escort, seeing its architecture, learning its grammar.

The under-crown's offer, when it finally stopped circling and landed, was exactly what Hazel had known it would be, because creditors have only two plays and this one had already spent the first.

"Take her place," it said. They stood now in a gallery above the vault proper, looking down through translucent floor at the shelves of names, close enough that Hazel could pick out individual entries if they leaned—which they did, casually, again and again, leaning here and there like a bored traveler at a ship's rail. Third aisle, second tier: a jar labeled *Merrin of the Fen Road, sold 40th year, cheerful disposition intact*. Fifth aisle, deepest visible row: names written in a script that moved, slowly rearranging itself, souls still fighting. And there—at the end of the fourth aisle, in a niche apart from the others, marked with a small silver seal shaped like a bell—

A single shelf. One name on it. *Wren Ashkar.*

Hazel's heart performed a full catastrophe, and Hazel stood very still and let it, and filed the location, and the seal, and the fact that hers was the only soul given a niche of her own, which meant either honor or isolation, and down here those were usually the same thing.

"Take her place," the under-crown repeated, "and the account closes clean. Your mother's debt, your mother's choice, honored by her own blood. There's a poetry in it that even the surface courts would appreciate. Or—" and here the voice dropped into the register of true temptation, the register that had doubtless talked a thousand desperate visitors into a thousand ruinous signatures—"bring me something worth more than a soul. Worth more than hers. Worth more than yours." A pause, savoring. "You serve a god, auditor. A god that eats its own sworn servants, though I suspect you've begun to guess that. Bring me your god. Bound, bled, or bartered. Its hunger is the only currency in either world I value above what sits in that niche below us. Deliver the silent one to my books, and I'll tear out her page myself, and yours besides, and we need never speak again."

*It wants the god dead or owned,* Hazel thought, with the cold clarity that had settled over them somewhere between the contract and the gallery, a clarity so pure it frightened them more than fear did. *It can't touch the god itself—the shrine-wards, the old oaths, something keeps it out. But a servant could carry it in. A servant with an oath could be a syringe.*

*I am not a person to this creature. I'm a delivery mechanism with legs.*

"Give me time," Hazel said. "A god isn't carried in a sack. I'll need to go back up. I'll need to prepare it—there are rites. Binding rites. You know how gods are about consent; it has to be *invited* to the table, or the meal poisons the eater."

Lies, all of it, smooth as ledger-lining, and Hazel watched the under-crown consider them and understood with perfect precision that the creature did not believe a word—and was going to permit the lies anyway, because permitting them cost it nothing. If Hazel ran, the court had other collectors, and Wren's niche had a lock. If Hazel returned with the god, the under-crown won everything. If Hazel returned alone and broken, that too was a kind of harvest.

Every branch of the tree fed it. That was what made it a crown.

"Three days," the under-crown said. "You'll be escorted to the debtor's door. You'll wear this." A servant offered a band of grey metal, thin as wire. "It remembers the way in. And it remembers you. Refuse to return, and the girl's page burns. Slowly. Souls burn slowly, auditor—you of all people know how long a soul takes to be destroyed properly. Ask your god sometime. It's had practice."

Hazel took the band. Their fingers did not shake. Later, alone, they might shake; down here, in front of the shelves, in front of the niche with the silver bell-seal, they would not.

"I'll return," Hazel said. "With what I promised, or with proof the promise was impossible. Either way, you'll have your answer inside three days."

"And you believe I'll simply release you to walk out?"

"No," Hazel said honestly, and the honesty was itself a gambit, the rarest coin a liar carries. "I believe you'll watch me go and count the hours, because you want the god more than you want me, and because you've already checked my pockets and found them empty. I'm worth more to you walking than kneeling. That's the only reason I'm alive right now, and we both know it, and pretending otherwise would insult us both."

For a long moment the under-crown was utterly still. Then the silver veins flushed bright, and the court around them murmured like a house settling, and Hazel understood they had guessed correctly—and that the guessing had purchased perhaps a day's grace, nothing more.

*Third aisle to the stair. Eight guards, spears of frozen oath. Low door, forty-count service rhythm. Niche at the end of the fourth aisle, bell-seal, one name. Wire band remembers the way in—so it remembers the way out, too, and everything between. Eleven rows visible, four aisles, lanterns without flame.*

*Wren. Fourth aisle. End of the line. Three days.*

* * *

They walked Hazel out themselves, through corridors that curved in ways architecture shouldn't, past doors labeled in scripts older than kingdoms. At the last turning, the servant with the smooth face paused, and in a voice like sand poured through a crack, said:

"She counts, you know. The girl in the niche. Counts everything. Hours, footsteps, the number of times the wardens pass." The dented eyes fixed on Hazel. "She counts the way her mother counted. It's how we knew whose child she'd grow into—the habit showed in the soul, early. We file souls by their habits, auditor. Yours would have gone on the same shelf."

Then the door opened onto cold air and grey daylight and the long climb back toward the world of weather, and Hazel stood in the threshold a moment longer than they needed to, letting the words finish their work.

*She counts. She's awake in there. She's counting the wardens' passes.*

Which meant Wren already knew the vault's rhythms better than Hazel ever could. Which meant that somewhere below, in a niche at the end of the fourth aisle, his best friend was doing exactly what Hazel had done in the court above—turning captivity into cartography, wound into tool, waiting into a weapon.

Hazel stepped through the debtor's door into the snow, and began, silently, to plan a burglary.

Behind them, far below the mountain's roots, something in the fourth aisle heard the outer door close—and started counting down.
