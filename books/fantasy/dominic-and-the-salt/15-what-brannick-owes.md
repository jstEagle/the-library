## 15. What Brannick Owes

Brannick had been a mercenary for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other: the ones who die are the ones who hesitate. He had watched men freeze on rooftops, on bridges, in doorways, and he had watched them fall. He had never frozen himself. Not once.

He was thinking about this now, standing at the mouth of the debtor's road beneath the mountain, because he had been hesitating for three days and it sat in him like a swallowed stone.

The road went down in switchbacks through rock the color of old bone. Somewhere below—far below, past the tollhouse and the icefield and all the small gray places between here and the sun—Hazel Zhou was walking into the under-court with a forged collector's seal and a plan Brannick could recite from memory, because Hazel had made the mistake of trusting him with pieces of it over brandy, back when they still thought of him as guild rather than rival.

That was the thing about trust. It didn't have to be earned to be spent.

Brannick adjusted the strap of his pack and started down.

* * *

He had told himself the story a hundred times on the way south, and like all his best stories, it improved with telling. The guild master hadn't sold Wren out of malice; she'd sold her location to save forty lives from the crown's list. That was arithmetic, not betrayal. And if the crown's hunters found Wren before Hazel did, then Wren would be taken alive—soul-crafters weren't killed, they were *collected*—and the guild would be spared, and everyone who wasn't a fourteen-year-old runner would get to keep breathing. Brannick had done worse math for less reason.

Except.

Except for the night he'd marked Hazel's door with the god's sigil, chalk-white on wet wood, and heard nothing inside, no stirring, no bolt drawn—and understood, standing there in the dark with his hand still raised, that he wasn't warning Hazel off so much as praying to something he'd stopped believing in years ago. Except for the way the sigil had seemed to drink the lamplight instead of reflecting it. Except for the fact that when he'd walked away, he'd walked faster than pride allowed.

And except for what he carried now, sewn into the lining of his coat: the letter from the guild master offering the under-court's stewards full cooperation in exchange for Hazel's name struck from the crown's rolls. Not Wren's name. *Hazel's.* Sell the thief, keep the ledger-keeper. The guild master had chosen, and she had not chosen the child.

Brannick was going down the mountain to deliver that letter. That was the whole of his errand. A man of nineteen years' discipline, delivering one folded piece of paper.

He kept telling himself he'd decide at the bottom. He'd been deciding at the bottom for three days.

* * *

The debtor's door opened for him the way doors open in dreams—before he knocked, without hinges he could see, exhaling air that smelled of candlewax and cold iron. Two stewards met him in the antechamber, tall and gray-robed, their faces pleasant in the way of well-fed hounds. One of them held out an open palm.

"Papers."

"I'm expected," Brannick said, and gave the name the guild master used in her correspondence: Mistress Ondrell, factor, Salt Guild, presenting credentials regarding an outstanding account.

The steward turned his palm upward. Patient. The other steward smiled.

Brannick surrendered the letter. He watched it pass from his fingers and felt something shift in the room—not the light, which came from nowhere and everywhere at once, but the *weight* of it, the way a room shifts when a debt is acknowledged. The first steward broke the seal with a thumbnail and read. His eyes moved once down the page.

"Mistress Ondrell offers cooperation," the steward said. "In exchange for the erasure of one name." He looked up, and his smile widened by a degree that Brannick felt in his spine. "She has already received her consideration. You may confirm this."

"Confirm how?"

"You are her collateral, Master Brannick. Her good-faith deposit against your delivery." The steward tilted his head, courteous as a banker. "You did know?"

The word went through Brannick like a blade finding the seam in armor. Collateral. He stood very still, the way he'd been taught to stand when a blow lands and showing pain means losing. Nineteen years. He had signed papers for the guild master a dozen times—standard sureties, standard bonds, the ordinary paperwork of a trade in impossible things. He had never read them closely. Who reads their own name twice?

"How long," he said. His voice came out flat and even, which surprised him.

"The original bond dates fourteen years back," the second steward said, producing—of course, of course they would produce it—a ledger. Black binding, silver clasps, pages soft as cloth. "Renewed annually. Your life is held against the guild's obligations. Currently—" the steward traced a line with one gray finger "—against this errand."

"And if I don't complete it."

"Then the account defaults." The steward said it the way a man says *the tide comes in*. "We collect what is owed. What is owed, Master Brannick, is you. All of you. Filed and shelved, like any other asset."

Brannick looked at the ledger page. There was his name in ink that seemed darker than ink should be, renewed year upon year in columns, fourteen entries deep, and beneath each renewal a small notation he now understood: *consideration deferred.* Fourteen years of being spent without ever seeing the coin leave his pocket.

He thought of Hazel saying, once, over cheap wine: *Everything in the guild is collateral, Brannick. Everything. Even us. Especially us.* He'd laughed and called it ledger-talk, the paranoia of a bookkeeper. Hazel had been doing the only honest accounting in the whole rotten house.

"What happens to me," Brannick asked slowly, "if I do complete it? If I deliver the letter and the name?"

"Then the bond transfers," said the first steward, "to whatever obligation Mistress Ondrell contracts next. You remain collateral. You will always remain collateral. That is the nature of the arrangement." He closed the ledger gently, almost kindly. "Unless someone pays you out."

* * *

They gave him a cell, because even guests in the under-court were assets, and assets were stored. The room was comfortable the way a strongbox is comfortable—clean, warm, windowless, lined with shelves that held things Brannick tried not to look at: jars of pale light, a birdcage with nothing in it that rustled anyway, a folded cloak that wept quietly into its own hem. On the wall opposite his pallet, someone had scratched tally marks going back further than the stone should have allowed.

He sat on the pallet and did the thing he had avoided for nineteen years, which was to look at his own accounts honestly.

He had joined the Salt Guild at seventeen, starving, after the war ate everything else. The guild master had fed him, trained him, paid him fairly by the standards of the trade—which were low standards—and never once lied to him outright. She had simply written him into her books as a thing of value, and value, in the guild, meant spendable. He had known this. Some part of him had always known it, the way you know the water you swim in is cold only when you imagine leaving it.

And what had he done with his knowing? He'd climbed. He'd taken the better contracts, the cleaner cuts, the jobs where the impossible goods changed hands and someone smaller than him paid the price. When the vote came to surrender Wren, he'd voted yes, and told himself it was arithmetic. When the guild master sent hunters after two children on a frozen road, he'd volunteered to carry the letter, and told himself he'd decide at the bottom.

There was no bottom. There was only the next decision, and the next, each one a little easier than the last, each one wearing the face of necessity. That was how a man became collateral without ever signing anything he meant.

Brannick lay back on the pallet and stared at the ceiling, and for the first time since he was seventeen he let himself feel the full weight of what he owed—not to the court, not to the guild master, but to a girl named Wren who had run messages for him when he was sick with winter fever and brought him soup she'd stolen from the master's own kitchen, grinning the whole time. To Hazel, who had trusted him with pieces of a plan that would kill them both if he sold them, and whose voice had never once wavered when they said *I'll come back for you* —because they'd meant it, and meant it toward *him*, of all people.

The tally marks on the wall seemed to count themselves in time with his heartbeat.

Somewhere far above, muffled through stone, a bell rang once—the court's bell, or maybe just ice settling. Brannick closed his eyes.

When he opened them, he knew what he was going to do, and it terrified him more cleanly than anything in nineteen years, because it was stupid. It was tactically indefensible. It was exactly the kind of hesitation-turned-madness that got men killed on rooftops, and he was going to do it anyway.

* * *

He asked to speak with whoever handled the Hazel Zhou account, and to his surprise—they granted it. Perhaps they wanted to watch him squirm. Perhaps the court enjoyed theater. Or perhaps, though he wouldn't learn this until later, the court already suspected what he intended and considered it amusing: another debtor volunteering to increase his own balance.

The auditor's chamber was round and lit by candles that burned without shrinking. The auditor was a figure of indeterminate age behind a desk of black stone, and when Brannick laid out his proposal, the auditor listened without expression, which somehow made it worse.

"Hazel Zhou intends to steal a filed soul," Brannick said. "Wren. Vault nine, third shelf, I can tell you where they'll enter and roughly when. My letter confirms the guild's cooperation. Deliver it, and you take them quietly, no alarms, no losses."

"That is the arrangement offered," the auditor agreed. "Why do you bring it to us early?"

"Because I want to renegotiate."

Something flickered behind the auditor's eyes. Interest, or hunger; in this place they were probably the same thing.

"Name your terms."

"Strike my name from the books. All fourteen renewals. Paid in full, discharged, gone. And strike Wren's entry too—leave her shelf empty when they come looking. In exchange I give you Hazel Zhou, delivered, and I hold the vault door myself while your people close the trap. No escape, no fight, no cost to you."

The auditor was quiet for a long moment. The candles burned without burning down.

"You understand," the auditor said at last, "that a soul cannot buy out a soul. You are offering yourself to purchase your own freedom. The mathematics do not resolve."

"I'm not offering myself," Brannick said. "I'm offering time. Every moment your collectors spend closing the trap around Hazel is a moment Hazel spends getting deeper into your vault. You want the theft stopped? Stop it at the door, with a man who knows how they move, holding it shut from the inside. That's worth more than my filing. Check your ledgers. I dare you."

It was a bluff wrapped around a truth, and he watched the auditor weigh it. The truth underneath: he didn't care anymore whether the mathematics resolved. He had spent fourteen years as a number in someone else's column. He would spend his last hour being a man instead.

The auditor drew a fresh page toward itself. "Your terms are noted," it said, "and declined. But a variant is acceptable. We will not discharge you—we do not discharge. However." The gray finger tapped the black ledger. "If you hold the vault door until the Zhou account is secured, we will mark your account *settled by service*. Not freedom. Settlement. The difference matters, in the end. Do you accept?"

Settled by service. Brannick translated it easily: *you will die on our terms instead of living on hers.* He found, examining himself, that he felt lighter than he had in years.

"Accepted," he said.

The auditor wrote, and the scratch of the pen sounded like ice cracking over deep water.

"One condition of mine," Brannick added, before the page could close. "When they bring Hazel in—if they bring Hazel in—you tell them I held the door. Don't dress it up. Don't call it loyalty or duty or any other lie. Tell them what it was."

"And what was it, Master Brannick?"

He thought about it honestly, which was new for him.

"Payment," he said.

* * *

The vault corridors smelled of dust and candlewax and, underneath, something sweeter—the smell of stored souls, which Brannick had no word for and never would, because he was not going to be alive long enough to need one.

He took up his position at the vault door with a stolen ring of keys and a steward's gray robe over his armor, and he waited. The shelves stretched away into dark on either side of the corridor, thousands of them, each holding a name, and he made himself look at them properly this time—all those lives filed away by people like him, people who'd done the math and kept walking.

His hands were steady. That was the strange part. Nineteen years of discipline, and it finally had somewhere honest to go.

Footsteps, far off. Light footsteps, quick, uneven—one set, moving fast, wrong for a patrol. Then a second set joining them, and a whisper he couldn't make out, and Brannick felt something in his chest crack open, because he knew that uneven gait. Wren. Out of her shelf or never shelved at all, loose in the corridors with Hazel somewhere behind her, and the alarm bells beginning to wake one by one above them like a storm coming down a mountain.

So much for the trap. So much for the clean exchange. The vault was waking, the shelves were rattling, names were peeling themselves off their pages in the dark—and Brannick stood in the doorway with his hand on the great iron lever that would seal the inner corridor shut, buying whoever ran through it a head start measured in heartbeats.

*Too late,* he thought. *Bought too late to enjoy.*

He grinned anyway. First honest grin in fourteen years.

The running footsteps rounded the corner—two figures, one tall, one small, silhouetted against the rising glow of a thousand waking names—and Brannick threw his whole weight against the lever.

"HOLD THE DOOR!" he bellowed, to no one, to everyone, to the god who had never answered and the ledgers that always did. "GO! I'VE GOT IT!"

The mechanism screamed. The door began to fall.
