## 16. The Theft of Wren

*Hazel*

The alarms did not sound like bells.

Hazel had expected bells—had braced for them the whole way down the debtor's stair, one hand on the wall, counting turns the way Brannick's sacrifice had bought them time to count. But the under-mountain court did not ring when it was robbed. It *hummed*. A low note rose through the pale stone like a finger drawn along the rim of a glass, and every candle in the corridor bent sideways at once, flames leaning toward the vault as if pulled by grief.

They ran toward it anyway.

The corridor walls were lined with niches, and in each niche sat something small and patient: a bird carved from smoke, a child's shoe filled with sand that never spilled, a jar of light with no lid. Collateral. The court kept everything it was owed on display, the way a moneylender hangs his debtors' portraits in the window. Hazel had walked past them an hour ago with a collector's blank face and a forged seal, cataloging nothing, memorizing everything. Now they ran past them again and the things in the niches *watched*, and Hazel understood with a lurch that the hum was not an alarm raised against thieves.

It was the sound of stored things beginning to stir.

*Wren got there first,* Hazel thought, and something in their chest that had been stone since Ashgate cracked just enough to let hope leak through like light under a door.

The vault door stood open.

Not broken—open, swung wide on hinges of green-gold metal, and beyond it the Vault of Names unfolded in tiers like an amphitheater dug into the mountain's marrow. Shelves upon shelves upon shelves, each shelf crowded with vessels: stoppered bottles, sealed boxes, locket-cages, candles with names burned into their wax. And light—not candlelight, not lamplight—coming from the vessels themselves, a soft gold breathing glow, as though ten thousand souls had all turned over in their sleep at once.

And in the middle of it, up to her elbows in a shelf of small clay urns, stood Wren.

She looked terrible. She looked magnificent. Her runner's coat was torn at the shoulder, her braid half-undone, soot on her cheekbone, and she was working with the fast, economical hands of someone who had spent years learning exactly how much force a thing could take before it broke. Urns after her touch glowed brighter. One by one, they were waking.

"Wren." Hazel's voice came out rougher than intended.

Her head snapped around, and for one heartbeat her face did something complicated—relief and fury and fear all fighting for the same ground—and then she threw an urn at them.

Hazel caught it on reflex. It was warm as a sleeping bird.

"That's for taking eleven days," she said. "That's for coming alone to the shrine. That's—" her voice snagged, "—that's for being alive. Put it back, it's not yours."

"It's not anyone's," Hazel said, setting it gently on the shelf. "That's rather the point."

"The point is we have maybe a hundred breaths before whatever guards this place arrives, and I've only freed the west tiers because the bindings are *layered*—" She was already turning back to the shelves, talking fast, runner-fast, the way she talked when she was frightened and refusing to show it. "Each vessel has its name written three times. Once on the vessel, once on the wax or the cord or whatever seals it, and once—" she pointed up, and Hazel followed her finger to the highest tier, where great ledgers lay chained to reading stands like captive birds, "—once in there. The master ledger. I can wake them but I can't free them. A woken soul in a stopped bottle is just a scream with nowhere to go."

"Then we do it properly." Hazel crossed the vault floor, boots silent on stone that seemed to drink sound, and stopped beneath the chained ledgers. Up close they could see the books were old in a way that hurt to look at—their covers not leather but something thin and veined, their spines stitched with hair. "Which one holds you?"

"Third stand. The recent acquisitions." Wren's voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone handling their own wound. "Page forty-one. My name's written in a hand I recognized, Hazel. That's the worst part. Whoever sold me wrote neat."

Hazel knew whose hand it would be. They had known since the tollhouse, since Wren had said *I remember the court* in the dark of a smuggler's cart. They climbed the iron stair two rungs at a time.

The third ledger fell open under their hands to page forty-one as if it had been waiting.

There it was. *One soul, female, age nine, delivered whole.* And beside it, in the margin, the deferred payment, the collateral clause, the neat, upright, unmistakable script of Marguerite Zhou—Hazel's mother—recording her daughter's life as surety against a debt of erasure.

Eleven years ago. Before the hunt. Before the guild. Before any of it, their mother had knelt somewhere in this cold and traded a child's soul for Hazel's name.

"Hazel." Wren had come up the stair behind them, quiet as she'd been taught to be quiet. She was looking at the page upside down. "That's your mother's writing."

"I know."

"You *knew*."

"I suspected." The lie tasted like ash. "I hoped I was wrong."

Wren was silent a moment. Below them, the hum deepened; freed-half-woken vessels pulsed in slow waves, gold light rolling across the tiers like tide. Somewhere far above, stone groaned.

"When we're out," Wren said finally, "you're going to tell me everything. In order. With dates."

"Yes."

"And I'm going to be furious for approximately the rest of my life."

"Also yes."

"Good." She exhaled. "Then get my name out of the book before I lose my nerve, because I've been looking at these shelves for two days and half of them are children, Hazel, and if we run without freeing them I will hear them for the rest of my life too."

* * *

*Wren*

Here was what nobody told you about seeing your own soul: it was smaller than you thought.

Wren had imagined, in the long dark hours of the vault, that hers would be something grand—a flame, a storm, a bird at minimum. Instead it sat on the shelf at eye level in a squat glass bottle the size of a fist, glowing a plain warm gold, and etched into the glass in the court's looping script: *WREN. ACQUIRED AGE NINE. CONDITION: WHOLE. INTEREST ACCRUING.*

Interest accruing. Eleven years of interest on her own self, compounded in the dark.

She had hated it. Then, somewhere in the second night, she had started talking to it—quietly, runner-quiet, in case the vessels could hear—and now, watching Hazel's knife-work on the master ledger's binding cords, she felt the bottle on the shelf below pulse faster, like a dog hearing its name.

"Careful," she said. "The chains on the stands aren't decoration. When I tried to lift the book earlier the chains drank the light out of my hands. Look." She held up her fingers; the tips were still pale, colorless, like wax. Sensation had come back that morning, mostly. Mostly.

"So we don't lift the book." Hazel didn't look up. Their knife moved along the page's gutter with the steadiness of someone who had spent their life cutting things precisely—entries, threads, other people's luck. "We cut the entry loose. A page can leave even if the book can't."

"Will that work?"

"No idea."

"Wonderful. Very reassuring. I'm glad I waited eleven years for this rescue."

"You weren't waiting. You were picking the lock with a stolen memory-shard, which frankly is more than I managed." Hazel cut the last cord holding page forty-one into the spine, and the page came free with a sound Wren felt in her teeth—a soft tearing, like a bandage leaving skin, like a word finally spoken aloud.

The vault *noticed*.

Every vessel flared at once. The hum became a chord, then a chorus, then something almost like voices, and Wren grabbed the page out of Hazel's hands because it was hers, it had always been hers, and she read her own name written in her mother-forgive-her-she-couldn't-help-the-word handwriting, and she did the thing she had rehearsed against the dark for two nights.

She struck it through.

No blade. Just her thumbnail, hard, dragging a line through *WREN* while she said, clear and loud in the singing dark: "Paid. Delivered. Returned. I was never yours."

The name unraveled off the page like thread pulled from a hem.

Below, on the shelf, the little bottle filled with light until it hurt to look at, and then the glass went dark—and then it *cracked*, a single clean line down one side, and Wren was moving before she decided to move, snatching it up, and it came apart in her hands like an eggshell and there was nothing inside at all, nothing except warmth spreading up her arms and into her chest, into the hollow place behind her sternum she had carried since she was nine and never had a name for.

It had a name now. It was *hers*.

"Oh," she said. Just that. She sat down right there on the iron stair because her legs had resigned without notice, and she laughed, and the laugh came out wet. "Oh, that's—that's what it's supposed to feel like. That's what everyone else has been feeling this whole time."

Hazel crouched beside her, and their face did the thing it did instead of smiling, the softening around the eyes, and Wren thought: *they don't have theirs either. They gave pieces of theirs away for years, oath by oath, and never once felt this.*

"We're not done," Hazel said gently.

"No." Wren wiped her face with the back of her wrist and stood, and the vault roared softly around them, thousands of woken souls pressing at their bindings, and above the highest tier the ceiling began, very slowly, to snow. Not snow. Ash. Or dust. Or the mountain shedding its skin. "No, we're really not."

* * *

*Hazel*

They worked the tiers together after that, and it went fast, and that was the catastrophe of it.

Fast was wrong. Hazel knew it in their bones—in the oath-scar on their palm, which had begun to ache with the old god's particular cold—but Wren was alight, Wren was *free*, and mercy is the fastest thing there is. Name by name, vessel by vessel: cut the entry, strike the name, speak the words Wren had coined and taught them in four breaths—*paid, delivered, returned*—and step back as the glass split and the warmth went home. The west tiers emptied first, then the south, gold light rising off the shelves in sheets, and the vessels that woke began to make noise, murmuring, sighing, some of them weeping, some of them laughing in small astonished voices like people surfacing from long drowning.

"They're waking faster than we free them," Hazel said, on the fifth tier, striking through a name—*TOMASIN, ACQUIRED AGE SIXTY-TWO*—and feeling the man's sixty-two years rush past them like wind. "The bindings are failing everywhere. We've pulled one thread too many."

"Then we pull the rest." Wren was already three vessels ahead, cracking stoppers with her thumb. "Faster."

"Wren—"

"*Faster,* Hazel." She rounded on them, lit gold from below, and for a moment she looked every year of what the court had taken and given back. "You want me to choose? I'm done choosing who gets left in a bottle. The crown hunts us for existing. This place *files* us. If it all comes down, let it come down on its own head."

The vault agreed with her, apparently. The humming climbed. Cracks spidered through shelves that no one had touched, through vessels sealed a century ago, and things older than the guild began to knock politely, then impolitely, from the inside.

And then the doors at the top of the amphitheater opened, and the court's collectors came in.

There were six of them, tall and gray-robed, faces smooth as unmarked pages, and they did not run. They simply descended, tier by tier, and where they passed, the woken souls went quiet—not silenced, *gathered*, drawn back toward their vessels like breath drawn in. The nearest collector reached the fourth tier and laid one long hand on a cracked bottle, and the crack healed.

"No," Wren breathed.

"Down," Hazel said. "Stairs. Now. The master ledger's still chained—if the entries are gone from the book, the collectors can't rewrite them. Everything we've struck stays free. Everything we haven't—"

"—gets put back." Wren was already moving, hurling herself down the iron stairs, and Hazel followed, cutting names as they went, striking entries blind at a dead run, *paid, delivered, returned, paid, delivered, returned*, a litany, a prayer to no one, to themselves. Behind them the collectors gained with terrible patience. Ahead, the vault's far door—the debtor's door, the way they'd come—stood open onto the corridor, and the corridor's niches had begun, all of them, to glow.

Everything stored was waking. Everything owed was calling in its debts at once.

Hazel struck the last reachable name—*MERRIT, ACQUIRED AGE THIRTY*—heard a woman gasp somewhere behind them like a swimmer breaking surface, and took the final stairs in two leaps.

The corridor. The niches blazing now on both sides, smoke-birds beating wings that had never moved, jars of lidless light overflowing. And at the end of the corridor, silhouetted against the far door, standing very still in the middle of all that waking—

A figure in traveling clothes, gray at the temples, with a ledger under one arm and a face Hazel had spent eleven years learning to forget.

Their mother looked at them across the burning corridor, and said, in the voice of every bedtime story Hazel had ever lost:

"You shouldn't have cut the page, little salt. Now the whole account comes due."
