## 17. The Court Wakes

The vault came apart the way a held breath comes apart—suddenly, and all at once, and with a sound like the world exhaling after eleven years.

Hazel felt it before they heard it: a loosening in the bones of the mountain, a shiver running up through the soles of their boots as though the stone itself had been clenching its jaw all this time and had finally, finally let go. The shelves around them were waking. Names that had been filed flat for decades sat up in their bindings. Souls that had been stacked like cordwood began to glow through their housings—glass jars, wax seals, folded papers, one entire shelf of small brass bells that rang without being touched.

"Run," Hazel said, and meant it as an order, and watched it fail, because Wren was already three shelves down with her stolen memory-shard prying at another ledger's spine, freeing names faster than Hazel could count them.

"Wren—*Wren.*"

"I'm not leaving them." She didn't look up. Her hands were steady in a way they hadn't been since Ashgate. "You said it yourself. Everything here is owed to somebody."

"I said everything here is *owned* by somebody, which is worse, which is why we're leaving before the mountain finishes deciding to fall on us."

Above them, the ceiling groaned. Somewhere far off, deep in the court's gilded corridors, an alarm was rising—a sound like a thousand small bells being rung backward, each toll unringing itself, the under-crown's version of a scream. Dust sifted down through the light of the soul-lamps, and the lamps themselves were guttering, because the lamps had been lit from stored souls, and the stored souls were leaving.

Wren pulled the shard free and a name slid off the page like water off oiled cloth. The jar beside it went dark, then bright, then simply *gone*, and somewhere very far above them—or perhaps below; distance worked strangely down here—Hazel heard a sound like someone waking from a long illness, gasping, alive.

"That one's free too," Wren said softly. Then she looked up, and her face was streaked with dust and something else, and she grinned at Hazel with all the feral joy of a sixteen-year-old who has just discovered she is very good at arson. "Come on. If we're running, we're running *generously*."

They ran.

The vault's main corridor sloped downward before it turned, and the turning was where the first of them stood waiting.

He was old. He was dressed in the style of forty years gone, guild-cut coat, salt-sigil at the collar, the kind of coat Hazel had seen hanging in the guild hall's back rooms, kept for ceremony no one remembered the meaning of. He glowed faintly, the way souls do when they've been out of a body long enough to forget what shadows are for. And when he saw Hazel, he did not flee. He stepped *toward* them, arms opening, and the memories hit Hazel like a wave hitting a seawall.

---

*A kitchen. Flour on every surface. A woman laughing—Hazel knew that laugh, had spent years trying not to remember it, and here it was whole and warm and unbearable. The woman is kneading bread and talking to someone off-frame, and the someone answers in a voice Hazel knows because it lives in their own throat: young, unbroken, still capable of singing.*

*The woman says: "If anything happens to me, the boy takes my books. All of them. Even the ones he can't read yet."*

*And the man in the guild coat says: "Mara, the god doesn't take bookkeepers."*

*And Mara Zhou laughs again and says: "The god doesn't take anyone until it's hungry."*

---

Hazel staggered against the wall. The corridor tilted. Wren caught their elbow—"Hazel? *Hazel*"—but Hazel couldn't answer, because the man was still standing there, glowing, patient, and more of him was pouring in: his name (Corvin Ashe, third ledger-keeper of the Salt Guild, sworn the year of the great frost), his death (a shrine, a kneeling, a hunger descending like weather), and beneath it all, threaded through everything, the simple human fact of him: he had liked card games and bad wine and had once walked six miles in the rain to return a widow's overpayment, two coppers, because a ledger that lied was worse than a ledger that burned.

"Hazel." Corvin's voice was the voice from the kitchen, older now, worn smooth by decades in the dark. "You have her eyes. She'd be insufferable about it."

"You're—" Hazel's voice cracked. "You're one of the missing. You're on the shrine wall. Brannick told me—the names scratched out—"

"Scratched out by our own hand, mostly. It seemed polite." He glanced up at the groaning ceiling, unhurried, a man checking weather. "The mountain is coming down. You should know what you're running from before you run into it. May I?" He gestured toward Hazel's temple, toward the flood already in progress. "It goes easier if you don't fight it. There are others behind me. We've been waiting a long time to be carried out."

"How many?"

"All of us." And Corvin smiled, sad and vast. "Every servant it ever swallowed. It never digested us, child. It *files* us. That's the thing you have to understand about your god."

* * *

They ran together then, the living and the dead, down corridors that were shedding their beauty like a snake sheds skin. The under-court had always been beautiful the way a ledger is beautiful—everything owed, nothing owned—and now the debt was being called in on the architecture itself. Gilding flaked away to show bare stone beneath. Fountains of silver sand ran dry mid-pour. Doors that had opened onto impossible rooms opened onto nothing, or onto other doors, or onto small dark spaces where single souls huddled, blinking, newly woken, holding their own names like strangers' coats.

And everywhere Hazel touched, the servants gave up their dead.

A woman in a hunter's leathers, sworn 1731, who showed Hazel the night she'd used the gift to pull a fever out of a dying child and felt the god drink the act like wine, felt herself grow *lighter*, thinner, less—until there wasn't enough of her left to hold a name.

A ferryman, sworn before the guild had ledgers, who remembered the god when it was small. *It lived under the ice on the lake,* he said, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. *We fed it because feeding things is what people do. First crumbs. Then coins. Then grief. It grew the way debts grow. Nobody ever decided to make it what it became. It just compounded.*

A hedge-witch whose death Hazel had witnessed in Ashgate square—no, not her death, her *grandmother's*, the witch burned two generations past, and the god had knelt at the edge of the fire and drunk the smoke, and the woman's soul had gone down into the mountain instead of up into whatever waits, filed under H for heretic, shelved between a moneylender and a midwife.

And through all of it, braided through every memory like thread through cloth, the same truth, repeated in a hundred voices across three hundred years:

*It was never silent. Silence is how it eats. It takes the oath, and the oath takes the servant, and the servant feeds it, and it grows quiet and vast and patient, and it calls the quiet peace, and the servants call the peace faith.*

Hazel understood then, walking and stumbling and weeping without slowing down, what their mother had known in that kitchen. The god had never left. The god had never gone silent. The silence *was* the god—the whole of it, the true body of it, a hunger so large it had learned to wear absence as a shape. Every prayer Hazel had ever sent up into that silence had been a coin dropped down a well. Every year of faithful service, an installment.

And the ledgers—the ledgers weren't records. They were *rations*. Every entry, every impossible trade, every bottled grief and borrowed luck, had been a meal set out on the guild's long table for a guest who never showed his face but always, always cleaned his plate.

"Mara figured it out," Corvin said, keeping pace beside Hazel though his feet made no sound. "Your mother. She found the oldest ledger—the one from before the guild, from when it was just a village feeding a lake-thing under the ice. She read the whole account. And she couldn't unswear—oaths don't work that way—but she could do arithmetic. So she made a deal. Her soul deferred, payment postponed, collateral named." His glow dimmed, gentled. "She bought you eleven years, Hazel. She knew it would come due eventually. She just wanted you grown when it did. Strong enough to do what she couldn't."

"What couldn't she do?"

"Burn the books while the god still had worshippers left to feed it. An oath can't be broken from inside alone. It needs everyone who holds a piece of it to let go at once." He looked ahead, to where Wren was hauling open a debtor's door with her shard, spilling three souls out into the corridor like birds from a cage. "Or it needs to starve."

The ceiling came down behind them.

Not gradually—all at once, a whole gallery of the court folding like a closing hand, and they ran through falling gold and screaming bells and the under-crown's alarm climbing to a pitch that was less sound than pressure. Ahead, the corridor ended at the great outer stair, the one Hazel had descended a lifetime ago in the debtor's door, and beyond it, impossibly far above, a coin of grey daylight.

Wren reached it first. She stopped. Turned. Looked back down the stair at Hazel, at Corvin, at the ragged procession of the freed dead stretching back into the dark farther than sight could follow.

"They can't come up," she said. Not a question. She'd seen it too—the way the souls slowed near the daylight, the way Corvin's edges went translucent at the threshold.

"No," Corvin agreed. "We're paid-out pages. We go where the wind takes ledgers." He smiled at Hazel, and it was her mother's smile, the kitchen smile, flour-dusted and fierce. "But we can walk with you to the door. And everything we are—you'll carry it up whether you mean to or not. That's the last kindness of the devoured, child. We get to be *useful*."

He pressed something into Hazel's hand. Not flesh—not quite—but weight, and warmth, and the shape of a memory chosen deliberately from all the ones Hazel had already taken: the kitchen again, the bread, the laugh, and this time the ending too, the part Hazel had never been given. Mara Zhou, alone at her desk, writing the final entry in her own hand. *Payment deferred. Collateral: my child. Let the debt find me first, and find me soft.*

"She wasn't afraid," Hazel whispered.

"She was furious," Corvin said. "It's better fuel. Now go. Your god is waiting at the top of the world, and it is *very* hungry, and you are carrying enough of us to feed it for a century—which means, for the first time in its long gray life, you are carrying something it will *negotiate* for."

Hazel climbed. Wren climbed beside them, her hand finding theirs in the dark, gripping hard. Behind them the court woke and woke and woke, souls pouring upward through the ruin like light through cracks, and the mountain shook with the sound of three hundred years of swallowed servants saying, all together, in one voice made of many:

*We remember. We remember. We remember.*

And Hazel climbed toward the winter sky with the knowledge sitting in their chest like a coal—unbearable, and burning, and *theirs*—

—and broke the surface into snow and wind and the smell of pine, and looked up, and saw the god already waiting at the ruined shrine, vast and gray and shaped like silence, its attention settling on them with the slow delight of a landlord finding his tenant home at last.

"There you are," it said, in a voice like an empty room. "You've brought my supper with you."
