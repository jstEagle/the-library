## 20. Reckoning at the Guild Hall

Wren had never seen the Salt Guild hall full.

In her three years as a runner she had known it as a place of half-people, always: someone sleeping off a delivery in the corner loft, someone counting coin behind the warded glass, someone gone south for a season and not yet back. The great room with its salt-stained beams had held perhaps a dozen bodies at its fullest, and most of those were arguing over a card game or pretending not to be asleep.

Now it held every soul the guild had ever sworn, and every one of them was silent.

They came because Hazel sent word they were coming—no threat in it, no plea, only a date and an hour and a line that read like a ledger entry itself: *I am bringing accounts to settle. All who hold shares may attend the reading.* In the guild's old language, that phrasing meant only one thing. It meant someone had died, or something had been stolen, or someone was about to be cast out. The reading of accounts was the oldest rite the Salt Guild kept, older than the crown, older than the mountain, older than the god whose name nobody said anymore. When accounts were read aloud before all members, they could not be unwritten. Whatever was said in that hour became true forever.

Wren stood at the back near the delivery door, where runners always stood, close enough to the cold air to feel it on her neck. She had argued against coming back at all. On the road down from the mountain, with the freed souls still whispering somewhere inside her like wind through shutters, she had told Hazel plainly: *Burn them from a distance. Let them read their own rot.* And Hazel had looked at her with eyes that still carried too much—memories of kneeling servants, of a mother's handwriting, of Brannick holding a door open with his body—and had answered in the flat voice Wren had learned meant the argument was already lost:

"Ledgers do harm in the dark. They have to be read in the light, or they just get rewritten."

So here they were. The light was gray winter noon through high windows, falling in pale bars across the floorboards, and the whole guild stood in it like salt in a wound.

Hazel entered alone.

That was deliberate; Wren knew it was deliberate. No escort, no weapon visible, no Wren at their shoulder. They wore their guild coat with the collar turned up and carried two things: a satchel heavy with salvaged ledger pages, water-stained and smoke-curled, and a small clay urn wrapped in wool. They walked the length of the room without hurry, boots ringing on boards that had heard forty years of oaths, and set both burdens on the master's table—the long scarred table where contracts were signed and shares divided and, eleven years ago, a page had been quietly torn away.

Guild Master Corvane sat behind it.

He was smaller than Wren remembered. As a child newly sold and newly delivered to the guild, she had thought him enormous—a man made of gray wool and gray beard and gray authority, whose word could move cargo across borders that officially did not exist. Now he was just an old man in a good chair, and his hands, folded on the table, were trembling slightly. He saw Hazel come and he did not stand. But he did not order the doors closed either, which Wren understood, watching, was its own kind of confession. A man planning to fight would have sealed the room first.

"The hunt is called off," Corvane said, before anyone else could speak. His voice carried the practiced weight of a man used to being the loudest thing in a negotiation. "The crown withdrew the decree eight days past. You've risked your lives coming here for nothing, child. Sit. There's tea. We can talk about your share—"

"We can talk about my share," Hazel agreed, and their voice was quiet, and the quiet did what shouting could not: it silenced him. "But first the reading. By right of the old forms, I claim the floor."

Murmurs moved through the assembled members like wind through wheat. Wren watched faces she half-knew—the smuggler Ondrey with his burned ear, the twin appraisers who could price a grief to the copper, old Sefa who had taught Wren to read seal-script when she was nine. Some of them looked angry. Most of them looked afraid. All of them knew what claiming the floor meant, and all of them knew that refusing the old forms in front of the full membership would cost Corvane more than hearing them out.

Corvane's jaw worked. "The forms are for deaths and debts."

"Yes," Hazel said. "Both."

* * *

They began, as the forms demanded, with the inventory.

Wren had expected rage. She realized, ten minutes in, standing by the door with her heart hammering, that Hazel had chosen something far crueler: arithmetic. They read the guild's holdings aloud, line by line, in the cadence every member had heard since apprenticeship—the cadence of stock-taking, of honest count. Bottled griefs, four hundred and six. Borrowed luck, unreturned, ninety-one units. Second chances, issued and unredeemed, thirty-three. And then the lines no audit had ever spoken, the lines Hazel had pulled from the deep ledgers and from the court beneath the mountain, now settling like ash into the silence:

"Grief harvested from clients who did not know they were selling. Luck stripped from marked households so their debts would deepen and their goods would come to us cheap. Second chances bought from the desperate at famine rates and resold at feast rates." Hazel turned a page. Their hands were steady. Wren wondered what it had cost them, on the road, to make their hands steady. "The Salt Guild has traded honestly in dishonest things for sixty years. That I could almost forgive. This—" they lifted the ledger so the nearest members could see the columns, "—is not trade. This is farming."

"Where did you get those figures?" Ondrey demanded from the middle of the crowd. "Those are sealed books. Deep-sealed."

"I got them," Hazel said, "from the court beneath the mountain, which keeps better records than we do. The court knows exactly what we've been feeding it. It has been keeping our accounts for years, gentlemen. It considers us partners."

The murmuring stopped. Wren saw the moment the word landed—*partners*—saw it travel face to face around the room, each member understanding it in their own private way. Some of them, she could tell, had suspected. The ones who handled the deep deliveries, the ones who never asked what was in the iron-bound crates bound under-mountain. Suspicion is a comfortable thing when it stays suspicion. Hazel had just made it arithmetic.

Then Corvane found his feet. "You come into my hall," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle of *hall*, "after weeks missing, after the crown put your name on a kill-list—and you lecture *us*? You, of all people? Ask the room who wrote your name on that list, Hazel Zhou. Ask who paid to keep you clean for eleven years while the rest of us bled for the guild!" He flung his hand toward the crowd. "Every kindness this guild ever showed you came out of that ledger you're waving around! Your schooling. Your coat. Your mother's funeral rites—we paid for those! Do you think love is free?"

The words hit the room hard. Wren felt several members nod, felt the ugly comfort of it spread—*yes, we fed her, we housed her, we are owed*—and she saw Hazel take it the way a seawall takes a wave: absorbing, unmoved, already knowing the shape of what was coming.

"You're right," Hazel said.

The room went still. Even Corvane faltered.

"Every kindness I remember was purchased," Hazel went on, and their voice did not rise at all, and somehow that was worse than any shout. "My mother sold something to buy my safety. I spent the autumn learning exactly what, and exactly to whom, and exactly how much interest has accrued. So let me be clear with you, Master, in front of everyone, in the old forms where nothing can be unwritten." They set down the ledger and placed one hand flat upon it. "I do not stand here innocent. I stand here *audited*. There is a difference, and the difference is that I have read my own account all the way to the bottom, and I am prepared to pay it. Can every soul in this room say the same?"

Silence.

"Now," Hazel said, and drew from the satchel a single sheet of paper, browned with age, its edge ragged where it had been torn free of a binding and later pressed flat again, "the specific entries."

* * *

Wren had heard the story on the road, in fragments, told in the tone of someone handling broken glass. Hearing it before the full guild was different. Each fact fell into the salt-stained silence like a stone into a well, and the well was very deep, and every member listened for their own splash.

The torn page from the year of Hazel's mother's death. The transaction recorded there—not erased, never erased, merely removed from view: *one soul, young, unclaimed, sold to the court beneath the mountain; payment deferred; collateral named.* The collateral named was Hazel, aged eleven. The payment deferred was erasure from the crown's hunt list—a list their mother had entered them on herself, years before, in her own careful hand, because the only protection she could buy her child was the appearance of a debt already settled elsewhere.

And the soul sold. Young. Unclaimed. Delivered to the guild within the year and raised as a runner.

Wren had known it since the icefield. Knowing it again, publicly, in front of the people who had raised her alongside Hazel—who had fed her, clothed her, taught her seal-script, and all along known her name was written in a dead woman's contract as currency—was like being flayed slowly with warm hands. She kept her back straight against the door. Runners don't run. She made herself listen to her own sale read aloud in the guild's own accounting cadence, and she watched the faces, and she catalogued, as she'd been trained, who flinched and who didn't.

Sefa wept openly. The twin appraisers stared at the floor. Ondrey's hand went to his belt knife and then, slowly, deliberately, away from it.

"And the final entry," Hazel said, and here at last their voice changed—flattened, went careful, went the way voices go at gravesides. "Master Corvane. Six weeks ago, when the hunt was declared and the guild voted on surrendering a child to save the rest, the vote failed. And that night, a runner's location was sold to the crown regardless. Not by vote. By private arrangement. The crown's hunters knew which pass we'd take. They knew our numbers. They knew—" Hazel paused, and Wren saw the pause cost them, saw it physically, a small collapse and recovery of the shoulders. "They knew because you told them. For a deferral on the guild's own exposure. You sold her twice, Master. Once eleven years ago, and once last month. The second time you took less."

"That's a lie," Corvane said, but he said it to the table.

"It's in the crown's own correspondence," Hazel said. "Recovered from the tollhouse at Gray Ford. Signed with your counterseal. I left the original with the magistrate at Ashgate this morning, together with copies of everything I've read today. The forms are satisfied, Master. The accounts are public. The crown will be reading them next." They looked up, and for the first time since entering, Hazel's voice carried something that was not arithmetic—something tired and old and almost gentle. "I'm not here to destroy the guild. I'm here to show it what it already is. What you all decide to do with that is yours."

* * *

It dissolved faster than Wren expected, and more painfully.

There was no vote—that was the strange mercy of it. Corvane tried to rally them, tried the old language, tried invoking the founder's oath and the god's protection, and somewhere in the third sentence he ran out of road. Members began simply leaving. Not storming—leaving, the way people leave a house where something has died: quietly, taking only what was theirs. Ondrey unbuckled his guild pin and laid it on the master's table with a click that echoed. Then one of the twins did the same. Then Sefa, weeping, pressing hers down beside the others like planting seeds in bad soil.

Some did not leave. Wren counted maybe twenty—the youngest, mostly, and the poorest, the ones whose shares were smallest and whose options were fewer. They gathered at the side of the hall in a loose, stunned cluster, and when Hazel finally stepped back from the table, it was to them Hazel walked, and Wren pushed off from the door and met them halfway, because some things you do not watch from doorways anymore.

"What happens to them?" Hazel asked quietly, meaning the twenty.

"They follow you, if you'll have them," Sefa said. Her voice was wrecked. "Or they scatter. Or—" she gestured at the pins glinting on the table like a little field of fallen stars, "—they learn to trade in possible things, if such a shop exists."

"It will," Hazel said.

Corvane remained at his table until nearly everyone had gone. Then he rose, straightened his gray coat, and walked the length of his own hall toward the doors with his head high, passing Hazel at a distance of six feet. He stopped.

"Your mother would have done it better," he said. "She'd have burned the ledgers and spared the speech."

"She did burn them," Hazel said. "Everything except the page that mattered. I finished her work. That's all this was."

He went out into the snow, and the door swung slow behind him, and the guild hall was empty except for the twenty, and the pins on the table, and the two burdens Hazel had carried in—one of which had not yet been opened.

* * *

They buried him at dusk, behind the hall, where the guild had always buried its own.

Brannick had come home ahead of them—Hazel had arranged it from Ashgate, paying, out of their own emptied purse, for the fastest courier in the city, because whatever else he had been, he had held the door. The grave diggers were guild men, two of the twenty who stayed, and they dug through frozen ground with salt-tempered spades while the light went blue and the first stars came out over the valley.

Wren had not liked Brannick. She wanted to be honest with herself about that, standing at the graveside with her mittened hands clenched, breath smoking. He had voted to give her up. He had marked Hazel's door with the god's sigil. He had come down the mountain intending betrayal and found his own name in the court's books instead, and had chosen—with minutes left, badly, bravely—to spend himself buying them time. She had spent the whole journey home composing the speech she would not give at his funeral, all the sharp things she was entitled to say.

Standing over the hole in the ground, she found she couldn't remember any of them.

Hazel spoke the old words, the short version, the ones for mercenaries who die outside guild walls: *The accounts are settled. The salt remembers. Walk easy.* The twenty repeated it in a low rumble. Snow began to fall, fine and dry, the kind of snow that means the deep cold has broken, though none of them quite trusted it yet. Wren watched the first flakes land on the fresh-turned earth and thought: *he never got to enjoy it.* Redemption bought too late to spend. She understood something about that. She had been currency once herself.

Afterward, when the others had drifted back toward the hall's fire, Hazel stayed at the grave, and Wren stayed with them, and for a long time neither spoke.

"He should have run," Wren said finally. "When he saw his name in the book. He could have run."

"He could have," Hazel agreed. "Most people do."

"Do you hate him?"

Hazel considered it honestly, the way they considered everything now, weighing it like an appraiser. "No. I hate how much it took. I hate that the court got him the same way it got everyone—by owning him first and calling it choice." They crouched, pressed one bare palm flat against the frozen earth of the mound, and Wren saw their lips move, not the old words, something private, something that might have been *thank you* or might have been *I'm sorry it was you.*

Then they stood, and looked east, where the mountain stood black against the last violet band of sky, and Wren followed their gaze and felt the whispering stir faintly inside her again—the loose souls, the unfiled names, still drifting through the world like snow that hadn't decided where to land. One of them, she thought, was very near tonight. One of them had followed them home from the shrine and had not yet moved on, and she could feel it now, patient as weather, at the edge of the firelight of her own mind.

"Hazel," she said. "Something came back with us."

"I know," Hazel said quietly, not turning from the mountain. "It's been waiting for us to finish burying the dead."

Above the valley, high on the mountain's shoulder where the ruined shrine stood, a light appeared—small, steady, wrong-colored, like a lamp burning underwater—and did not go out.
