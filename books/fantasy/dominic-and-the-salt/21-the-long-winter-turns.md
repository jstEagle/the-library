## 21. The Long Winter Turns

Wren woke to the sound of dripping.

Not the slow, patient drip of the guild hall's eaves in ordinary thaw—this was a chorus of it, hundreds of small voices falling from the roof, from the gutters, from the icicles that had hung like teeth along the dormer window all winter. She lay in her narrow bed beneath the sloped ceiling of the room above the shop and listened, and for a long moment she could not name what the sound meant. Then she remembered: water. Water moving on its own, unbothered, going wherever it pleased.

She got up barefoot on cold boards and pressed her face to the frosted glass.

The valley had turned overnight. Snow still lay thick on the rooftops and in the shadowed folds of the hills, but it had gone soft at the edges, sagging, dimpled with the prints of birds who had come out to test the air. The stream behind the shop—the one that froze so hard each winter the children used to skate it with knives strapped over their boots—was running loud and open, brown and quick with snowmelt, carrying slabs of ice downstream like boats leaving harbor. And above the ridgeline, where the mountain stood, the light was different. Warmer. As if something behind it had stopped holding its breath.

*Deep winter breaking early,* Hazel had told her once, back when Wren was small enough to sit on the counting table and swing her legs while the ledgers were balanced around her like city walls. *It's an old sign. The mountain's debts are settled.* She hadn't understood it then. She'd thought it was just one more piece of guild-lore, the kind of thing old members said to make the trade feel older and holier than it was—a business that sold bottled grief and borrowed luck needed its own superstitions the way other businesses needed accountants.

Now she understood. Now she had walked down into the place where the debts were kept, and she had pulled her own name off a shelf, and she knew exactly how heavy a settled debt could be.

Downstairs, she could hear Hazel moving. The particular sounds of Hazel: the creak of the third stair, the rattle of the kettle that never quite sat flat on the iron plate, the soft scrape of the broom. Ordinary sounds. Wren held onto them the way you hold onto a rope over deep water. For eleven days since they'd come up out of the mountain, she had woken every morning to those same sounds, and every morning there was a moment—a breath's length, no more—when she was not sure whether they would be there. Whether the whole winter had been a memory someone else had bought and sold, and she was still in the vault of names, lying on the shelf between strangers' souls, dreaming herself free.

But the drip went on. The water ran. And Hazel was downstairs making tea badly, because Hazel did everything with perfect care except tea.

Wren dressed quickly, pulling on the wool socks Brannick's mother had knitted for the runners years ago—heavier than any soldier's issue, gray with red stripes at the cuff, and still smelling faintly of lanolin no matter how many times they were washed. Brannick's body had come home nine days ago, carried up from the mountain road by deserters who refused payment. They had buried him on the south slope where the sun reached first, and his mother had put the red-striped socks in the ground with him because he'd always complained his feet were cold, even in summer, even standing in front of a fire. Wren wore the second pair now. It felt right. It felt like being carried by someone who had chosen, badly and bravely, at the end.

The shop was warm. Hazel had lit the stove before dawn, and the little front room smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender and, underneath everything, salt—the clean mineral smell that lived in the walls of every building the Salt Guild had ever owned, as if the trade itself had soaked into the timber. But the shelves were wrong now. Emptied of their impossible stock, scrubbed, and lined instead with jars of honey, bundles of needles, spools of good thread, lamp oil, boot wax, tallow candles. Possible things. Things a person could hold in one hand and know exactly what they were.

Except for one shelf. The low one, near the window, where the morning light fell first.

On it sat a single ledger. Blank.

Hazel stood at the counter with two cups of tea, and when Wren came down the stairs, Hazel pushed one across the worn wood without a word. The tea was terrible, as always—over-steeped, bitter, the leaves measured by eye instead of spoon. Wren drank it anyway. Some things you kept even when they cost you.

"It broke," Hazel said. Not looking up. Watching the steam curl off the cup. "Last night, sometime. You can hear it from the bedrooms."

"I heard." Wren wrapped both hands around her cup. "The whole valley will hear it by noon."

"Then we should go before noon." Hazel finally looked up, and Wren saw the thing in Hazel's face that had been there for eleven days and would, she suspected, be there for a long time yet: grief wearing competence like a coat two sizes too large. "Before everyone comes up the hill to stare at us."

"They're not coming to stare."

"Some of them are."

Wren couldn't argue with that. Word had spread through the valley faster than the thaw itself—faster than any runner the guild had ever employed could have carried it—that the hunt was called off, that the crown's decree had been quietly withdrawn, that the list of names burned in Ashgate square had been declared void by order of some clerk nobody had ever heard of. People said the mountain had done it. People said the god had died. People said a great many things, most of them half-wrong, which was the usual state of what people said. What they agreed on was this: whatever had been owed beneath the mountain was paid, and the proof was the weather.

So today they would bury the ashes.

* *

They carried them up the hill in two clay pots that had once held pickled onions, because neither of them owned anything grander, and because Hazel had said, in that dry voice that was either humor or armor or both, that the ledgers had spent their whole existence pretending to be more than they were, and it seemed fitting they should end in pickle pots.

The ash weighed almost nothing. That was the strangest part. Wren had expected weight—had expected to stagger under it the way she'd staggered under the vault's silence, under the eyes of the stored souls watching her pick their lock, under the knowledge of what her mother's mother had signed away and why. But Hazel had burned three ledgers' worth of paper in the shop stove over six nights, feeding the pages in one at a time, reading each entry aloud before it went into the fire. Every debt recorded. Every soul traded. Every deferred payment, every collateral named, every impossible thing the Salt Guild had ever moved through its hands—spoken aloud in Hazel's level voice until the words lost their power, and then given to the flame.

Eleven days of that. Wren had sat with her through four of them, listening, because she had decided that if Hazel was going to carry the names, Wren was going to hear them. Some of the entries she already knew. Her own was in there—dated the year she was born, in her grandmother's careful hand, *one soul, condition new, payment deferred, buyer below.* She had listened to Hazel read it and had felt, oddly, nothing. The entry was true. It had happened. But it was ash now, or nearly, and ash could not own anyone.

What stayed with her were the smaller entries. *Borrowed luck, seven years, returned damaged.* *Grief, bottled, vintage unspecified.* *Second chance, issued to J.M., terms forgotten by issuer.* Little transactions. Human ones. The guild had not only dealt in monsters' bargains; it had dealt in neighbors' desperation, in the small cruel arithmetic of people who needed something impossible and could only pay in things they didn't know they'd miss. Wren had sold a happy childhood memory in a tollhouse on the road south, and she still caught the edges of the absence some mornings—reaching for something warm and finding smooth wall. She wondered how many entries in those ledgers were just that. Someone reaching for warmth and finding wall.

The hill they climbed was the one above the old shrine—the ruined one, the one where the silent god had spoken once, showing Hazel kneeling servants in the dark, offering its bargain. Wren had not gone inside since. She didn't need to. She had seen enough of the dark places where gods kept their accounts. But the ground around the shrine was high and open, faced the morning, and the soil was good—loamy, dark, the kind of soil that forgave things. Hazel said flowers grew here once, before the guild claimed the valley. Salt-tolerant ones. Sea pinks and thrift and something with a name too old to remember, brought up from the coast by traders who paid their tolls in stories.

The snow was slushy underfoot, soaking their boots within the first hundred steps, and neither of them minded. There is a particular joy, known only to those who have survived a hard winter, in the feeling of wet snow soaking through a boot seam: the proof that the cold has lost its grip, that the world has loosened its jaw. Wren splashed deliberately through the deepest puddle she could find, and Hazel gave her a look that was ninety percent disapproval and ten percent something warmer, and Wren grinned, and for a moment they were just two people walking uphill in the mud, and none of it had ever happened.

Then they reached the top, and the moment passed, and it was time.

* *

Hazel dug the hole. Of course Hazel dug the hole; Hazel would have insisted on digging the hole if the ground had been stone. The shovel bit into the softening earth in steady strokes, and Wren stood beside the two clay pots and watched the steam rise off the turned soil, and tried to decide what she was feeling.

Mourning, certainly. That surprised her. She had hated the ledgers—or she had thought she had. But hating a thing and mourning it turned out to be neighbors, sharing a fence. Those books had raised her as much as any person had. They had set the hours of her childhood, the shape of her lessons, the boundaries of what was possible. Every runner learned to read from ledger entries. Wren's letters had come from a page recording the sale of a man's courage, dated nineteen years ago, and she had traced the looping script with a finger and thought it beautiful, and it had taken her until this winter to understand what the beautiful letters said. You could mourn a cage, she discovered. You could grieve the hands that built it, if the hands had also fed you.

And relief—yes. Relief so large it frightened her, the way open sky frightens people who've lived underground. No more lists. No more looking over her shoulder for crown hunters or guild masters or gods with empty stomachs. No more wondering which kindness had been purchased and at whose expense. The mountain's debts were settled. All of them. Even hers—even the oldest one, the one signed before she could walk, the one that had made her a commodity before she was a person. Settled. Paid. Done.

Both feelings at once, braided together like the red-and-gray wool on her cuffs. She was learning that this was how big feelings actually arrived—not pure, not sorted, but tangled. Grief and relief in the same handful of ash.

"Hazel," she said. "Do you remember my grandmother?"

Hazel paused, shovel resting. "I remember a woman who came to the hall twice a year and never smiled."

"That's her." Wren looked down into the pots. Fine gray ash, flecked with black where the ink had resisted longest. Somewhere in there was her grandmother's handwriting, the careful loops that had taught Wren her letters. "Do you think she knew? What she was really buying. When she sold me."

The pause went on long enough that Wren looked up. Hazel was staring at the far ridge, where the snow was sliding off the rocks in small white avalanches, the mountain shedding its winter like an animal shedding a coat.

"She knew what the contract said," Hazel said at last. "Whether she knew what it *meant*—" A breath. "I've read that contract more times than I can count, Wren. In the court, and after. And I keep finding new prices in it. I think some contracts are written so you can't know all of what they mean. I think that's how they get signed at all."

Wren nodded slowly. It matched what she remembered of the court beneath the mountain—its beauty that was a ledger, everything owed and nothing owned. "Then maybe this is for her too," she said. "Burying it. Even though she's not here to want it."

"Especially then," Hazel said. "Debts don't stop belonging to people when the people die. That's rather the whole problem, isn't it. So I suppose forgiveness works the same way." Hazel lifted the first pot. "Ready?"

They poured the ashes in together. It should have felt like a ceremony, and in a way it did—but mostly it felt like what it was: two tired people tipping pickle pots into a hole on a thawing hillside, ash catching on the wind and drifting, fine as flour, toward the shrine and the open sky. Wren watched the last of it go and felt something unclench in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. Eleven days of clenching. Eleven years of it, maybe, without knowing.

Hazel filled the hole. Patted it flat with the shovel blade, the way you'd settle a bed.

Then Wren knelt and began planting.

She'd bought the starts from the widow Ferrow two days ago—paid honest coin, possible price—and they waited now in a basket lined with damp cloth: sea pink with its grass-blades and tight buds, thrift with its tough little roots, and a handful of something the widow called *mountain rose*, which grew in scree and salt marsh alike and bloomed stubbornly pale. Wren worked them into the fresh-turned earth over the ashes, pressing the soil around each root ball with her thumbs, and Hazel knelt beside her and did the same, and for a while neither of them spoke. There was nothing that needed saying. The work said it. Roots into ash. Green over gray. Flowers that could grow where nothing sweet was supposed to survive.

When the last start was in, Wren sat back on her heels and surveyed the small raw patch of earth. It looked like nothing much. A scar, almost. But scars healed into skin, and skin forgot, mostly, that it had ever been broken.

"They'll bloom by midsummer," she said.

"If the valley lets us stay that long," Hazel said, but gently, testing.

"The valley wants us to stay." Wren picked up the empty pots. "Half the town's already decided the thaw is your doing. Old Marette told me yesterday that you broke the mountain and the mountain said thank you. By next month you'll be a saint. They'll want a festival."

"I'd rather have quiet customers."

"You'll get neither."

Hazel almost smiled. It was a real one this time, small and crooked, and Wren counted it among the day's harvest.

* *

They started down as the sun cleared the ridge, and the whole valley opened below them, steaming and dripping and running loud with meltwater. Smoke rose straight from chimneys. Somewhere a dog barked at the sound of ice cracking on the stream. Children were already out on the lane, stamping the slush, shouting, alive in the way children are alive when the long confinement ends.

And Wren stopped on the path, halfway down, because she saw something at the bottom that made her heart do a strange double step.

There was a figure coming up the hill toward them. Small. Quick. Familiar in the shoulders, in the way of walking like someone perpetually late. One of the guild's youngest runners—one of the three who had chosen to follow Hazel when the guild dissolved, who had helped carry boxes of possible goods up from the hall and stack them on the shop's new shelves. Behind her, at the foot of the hill, two more figures waited by the gate. And beyond them, at the edge of the lane, a small crowd was gathering the way crowds do when something is about to happen—shopkeepers with their aprons still on, the smith, the widow Ferrow, old Marette leaning on her stick.

The runner reached them, breathing hard, cheeks bright, and thrust out a folded paper.

"This came up from Ashgate this morning," she panted. "Official seal and everything. They wouldn't say what it was, just that it had to reach you today."

Hazel took it. Broke the seal. Read it once, quickly, and then again, slowly—and Wren watched Hazel's face do something complicated, the way a frozen stream does when the ice begins to shift: still on top, everything moving underneath.

"Well?" Wren asked.

Hazel lowered the paper. Below them, the crowd had grown. The whole valley seemed to be climbing the hill now, drawn up the thawing slope as if the season itself were calling them, and at their head, walking slowly, leaning on the smith's arm, came a face Wren knew from the guild hall's oldest portraits—a face that had no business being upright, let alone walking.

"Hazel," Wren said quietly. "That's—"

"I know who that is," Hazel said. And folded the letter, and put it away, and started down the hill to meet them.
