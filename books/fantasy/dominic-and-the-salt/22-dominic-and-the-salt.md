## 22. Dominic and the Salt

The shop had been a bakery once, before the winter that lasted too long, and it still smelled faintly of old bread if you stood in the right corner on the right morning—warmth soaked into the walls so deep that even three owners of grief had not managed to scrub it out. Dominic liked that about the place. It was a building that remembered what it was for.

They had signed for it in their new name.

*Dominic Villalobos,* written in careful ink on the deed, the letters still strange under their hand, like a borrowed coat that fit better than anything they'd owned before. The clerk at the registry had squinted at them over her spectacles and said, "Villalobos? There was a salt-trader by that name, years back. Woman. Sharp as broken glass."

"My mother," Dominic had said.

"Dead, I heard."

"Died owing nothing," Dominic said, which was true now, in every way that mattered, and the clerk had stamped the page without further questions. In Ashgate, in the first spring after the mountain's debts came due, that was the highest praise anyone could offer: *died owing nothing.* People wore it like a medal. They said it at gravesides and over doorsteps and to children who asked too many questions about the bad years. The valley had learned, all of it, what a debt could grow into when you left it in the dark long enough.

The shop traded in possible things.

That was the rule, painted on the sign Wren had made—she had good hands for lettering, steadier than anyone expected from a girl who'd spent her childhood sprinting across rooftops with other people's secrets—and the rule was absolute. Salt. Flour. Lamp oil. Thread. Dried apples. Beeswax. A small, honest stock of boots and mittens come autumn, because Wren insisted no runner should ever again have to choose between cold feet and cold silence. Nothing bottled. Nothing borrowed. No luck, no grief, no second chances sealed up in glass with wax stamped by someone else's ring. If a thing could be weighed on the scale behind the counter, they sold it. If it had to be measured some other way—in want, in fear, in the currency of *please*—they pointed such customers gently toward the road and wished them well.

Some people came anyway, hoping.

They always hoped. That was the nature of people, and Dominic had learned it the hard way, ledger by ledger, year by year: hope was the one commodity that never went out of season. A woman came in on the fourth day after opening, wringing a scrap of cloth in her hands, asking—carefully, casually, the way people did—whether they might have anything for a sick child beyond what the physicker could do. Anything at all. She didn't say *magic.* Nobody said magic anymore; the word had gone the way of the hedge-witches, burned out of common speech. She just looked at Dominic with a face like an open wound and waited.

"We have willow bark," Dominic said. "We have honey, and we have a blanket, wool, good weave, and it's yours if she needs it more than you need coin."

"That's not—" The woman stopped herself. Her jaw worked. "That isn't what I meant."

"I know what you meant." Dominic set the blanket on the counter anyway, folded soft. "I'm sorry. What you're looking for doesn't live in shops anymore. Maybe it never lived anywhere it wasn't eating something alive."

The woman took the blanket. She paid for the willow bark with coins counted out twice, because her hands shook, and at the door she paused and said, without turning around, "They say there was someone. In the mountain. Someone who opened the doors and let them all out."

"People say a lot of things," Dominic said.

"They say he had his mother's eyes."

Dominic said nothing to that. The bell above the door chimed the woman out into the bright cold morning, and Dominic stood a while among the shelves of flour and lamp oil and honest salt, feeling the name sit on them like sunlight through glass—warm, and thin, and theirs only because they chose to keep wearing it.

* * *

Wren ran deliveries.

She had argued for it, of course. She had argued the way she did everything now, with a directness that sometimes startled people who hadn't known her before—the vault, the winter, the walking free. Something in her had been filed away down there alongside her soul, and when it came back it came back sharpened. She was fifteen going on forty, and she delivered thread to the seamstress and salt to the smokehouse and a jar of rendered tallow to old Maren at the edge of town, and she did it at a dead run because, as she put it, "I spent two years standing still on a shelf. I'm making up the distance."

"You could walk," Dominic told her, most mornings.

"Walking is for people who haven't been inventory." And she'd be gone, boots hammering the frost-soft lane, scarf streaming, laughing at something private.

But she came back. That was the difference between Wren now and the runners of the old guild, the ones who went out with packages and prayers and came back hollowed or didn't come back at all. Wren went out and came back, every time, and each time she crossed the threshold she announced herself the same way—shoving the door open with her shoulder, calling out, "It's me!"—and Dominic understood, without either of them ever saying it aloud, that this was a ritual. That somewhere beneath the mountain, in the vault where names were filed like preserves, no one had ever knocked. No one had ever needed to announce themselves to a shelf.

So the door stayed unlocked during the day. So the bell above it rang bright and unbroken, cheap tin, no enchantment in it at all, and every time it sounded Dominic felt the same small private relief, the same breath let out.

Today she came back flushed and snow-dusted, shaking sleet from her hair, and dropped onto the stool behind the counter like she owned it, which in every way that counted she did.

"Maren wants to know if you'll take eggs against next month's salt," she said. "And the seamstress says your thread order is short two spools of blue and she won't be bullied about it, so don't try. And—" she hesitated, and the hesitation was unusual enough that Dominic looked up from the tally-book. "There's a man been asking after you. At the crossroads inn. Not crown. Not guild—not anymore. He had a scar through his eyebrow and he paid for his room in coin that had the old god's sigil scraped off it. Somebody tried, anyway. You can still feel the shape under the thumb."

Dominic kept their hands still on the ledger. Outside, the sleet ticked against the window like fingernails.

"What did he ask?"

"If the shopkeeper here used to be somebody else." Wren pulled off her wet gloves finger by finger. "I told him the shopkeeper here has always been Dominic, born behind the bakery, very boring childhood, lots of bread. He laughed. Then he gave me this and said to tell you the shrine on the north road is empty now, but the ground around it isn't, if you understand him, and that some of the ones who came out of the dark are looking for the person who opened the doors. To thank. Or to ask. He wouldn't say which."

She set a folded paper on the counter. It was cheap paper, traveler's paper, creased many times, and Dominic opened it with the particular care one reserves for things that might be debts.

Inside, drawn in charcoal, rough but unmistakable, were kneeling figures. Dozens of them, ranks of them, receding into the margin like a congregation sketched from memory by someone who had knelt among them. And below the figures, in a hand that shook slightly:

*We remember our names now. We owe you ours. Come to the north road when the thaw is done, if it pleases you. If it does not please you, we will trouble you never, and love you anyway.*

Dominic read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting the words settle into the place inside them where the servants' memories still lived—that flood of borrowed lives poured into them in the collapsing dark, the taste of a hundred different mothers' kitchens, a hundred oaths sworn in good faith to a thing that had been eating its faithful all along. Those memories had faded since, mercifully, thinned to an undertone, a choir heard through a wall. But some nights they still rose, and Dominic lay awake cataloging strangers' joys and strangers' sorrows, keeping them, because no one else could.

"They're building something up there," Wren said quietly. "Aren't they. Where the shrine was."

"Maybe a garden." Dominic folded the letter along its old creases. "Maybe just a fire to sit around. They spent a long time kneeling in the dark, Wren. If all they want is to stand up in the daylight together, that's not a debt. That's just living."

"And if they want more than that?"

"Then they can ask, and I can say no, and the world will keep spinning." Dominic smiled, and found—to their mild surprise—that the smile was easy. "That's the whole trick of it. That's what nobody knew. You're allowed to say no. Even to gods. *Especially* to gods."

Wren studied them for a moment with those sharp runner's eyes, the ones that had picked apart a vault's lock with a stolen memory-shard and would have picked apart a fortress if it stood between her and the sky. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and reached for the egg basket Maren had sent.

"Thaw's nearly done," she said. "I could run the north road in two days. Three, with mud."

"Not yet." Dominic took the basket from her hands and set it down, and made their voice gentle. "Let them finish building whatever it is. Let the ground settle. We've earned a spring where nobody owes anybody anything—including us. We don't rush toward the past, Wren. We've done enough rushing."

She grumbled. She always grumbled. But she stayed, and they inventoried the afternoon together, flour and oil and thread, and outside the sleet turned to rain, and the rain turned, near dusk, to the first soft snowmelt hush of a winter ending early—again, still, a gift the whole valley kept half-expecting to be repossessed.

* * *

The blank ledger sat on the top shelf, spine out, waiting.

It had been there since opening day. Wren had bought it at the stationer's—a plain thing, good paper, unruled—and placed it there herself without explanation, and Dominic had understood immediately, the way you understand a stone in a shoe. Some objects carry their meaning whether or not anyone speaks it.

For eleven years, or twenty, depending on how you counted from the beginning of things, there had been ledgers. Ledgers of the Salt Guild, black-bound and iron-clasped, full of impossible arithmetic: grief by the dram, luck by the hour, souls entered under *goods received* with payment deferred and collateral named in a dead woman's steady hand. Ledgers that recorded everything except what it cost. Ledgers that had taught a child raised among them that every kindness had a price column, that love was a line item, that the question *what am I worth* always had an answer written by someone else.

Those ledgers were ash. Buried in the garden behind this shop, under salt-tolerant flowers—sea-pink and thrift, stubborn blooms that fed on bitterness and turned it color. Wren had planted them. She visited them sometimes, in the way of the young, who grieve loudly at first and then go quiet and keep watch. Dominic went too, some evenings, and stood among the flowers and thought about a mother who had done a monstrous thing out of a monstrous love, and about how both of those truths could live in one person, and how the flowers didn't care. They just grew. That was their entire philosophy, and Dominic had come to think it was better than most.

But the blank ledger was different. The blank ledger was a choice.

Every night at closing, Dominic took it down, opened it to the first page, and held the pen over it. Every night, so far, they had closed it again and put it back.

Tonight was no different, until it was.

The shop was shut. Rain on the roof. Wren asleep upstairs in the room with the good window, breathing slow and even, the sleep of someone whose name belonged to no one but herself. Dominic stood at the counter with the lamp turned low, and took the ledger down, and opened it, and this time—without deciding to, without ceremony, the way the truest things happen—they wrote.

*First entry,* they wrote. *Sold: nothing. Bought: nothing. Owed by this house to any soul, living or otherwise: nothing.*

Then, underneath, because the pen seemed to want to keep moving:

*Held in trust, no charge: one blanket, given freely to a frightened woman. One memory of a happy morning, returned to its owner unasked-for. One promise to visit the north road when the thaw is done—not because it is owed, but because it is wanted.*

They looked at the words a long while. Ledger-writing, after all these years, was in the blood; it came out level and clean. But these entries balanced differently than the old ones. These entries gained weight the longer you read them instead of losing it.

A knock at the door.

Small. Two raps, hesitant. At this hour, in this valley, a knock could mean anything—it had been barely a year since a knock might have meant hunters, or guild enforcers, or worse—but the valley had changed, and besides, the knock was small. Small knocks were usually children, or the very old, or people carrying something fragile.

Dominic opened the door on a young man, maybe seventeen, soaked through, holding a bundle wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a baby. His eyes were red-rimmed and desperate and hopeful, and Dominic knew that look. Had worn that look, once, across a counter much like this one.

"Please," the boy said. "They said—you have to hear me out—I've got my sister's voice in a bottle. Just her voice, from before the fever took the rest of her. I've carried it two hundred miles. Everyone said the shops that trade in that are all gone, all burned, but then someone said—down in Ashgate, there's a shop, and the shopkeeper there—"

He faltered. He looked up at Dominic's face, and whatever he saw there made him grip the bottle tighter.

"The shopkeeper there is the one who opened the doors," he whispered. "Is it true? Can you—could you let her out? Please. I don't want to sell her. I just want to give her back."

Rain fell off the eaves. Somewhere upstairs, Wren shifted in her sleep. On the shelf behind Dominic, the ledger lay open to its first page, to *nothing owed, nothing sold*, to a future unwritten and a pen still warm.

And Dominic—who had burned the ledgers, broken the oath, paid the god's price, taken back a stolen soul and a stolen name, and built from all that ruin a small shop that traded only in possible things—looked at the boy, and at the bottle, and at the long dark road behind him, and stepped aside to let him in out of the rain.

"Come in," said Dominic, and reached for the lamp. "Tell me everything. We'll see what can be done."
