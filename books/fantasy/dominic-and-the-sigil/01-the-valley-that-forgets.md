## 1. The Valley That Forgets

There are three rules to working the border road, and I invented all of them, which means they're the only rules in the world I can be sure of.

Rule one: never take everything. A traveler who walks out of the valley with a head full of fog will stop at the first inn and tell everyone the valley is haunted. A traveler who walks out with most of his memories intact but missing, say, his mother's face, or the smell of his wedding dinner, or the name of his second-favorite horse — that traveler goes home and tells everyone the road through Thackeray's Vale is long but perfectly ordinary, and he'd thank you not to ask about it, because thinking about it gives him a headache.

Rule two: never take from someone who's crying. Crying people are already losing something. Taking from them makes you a thief in the way that matters, the way you can't argue yourself out of.

Rule three: work alone. This one isn't really a rule so much as an observation about my life.

My name is Rosalind Thackeray, though I only know the surname because it's carved over the door of the house I woke up in eleven years ago, and I only know the given name because I found it stitched into a coat that fits me suspiciously well. Whether the coat was mine before, or whether I simply grew into the shape of whoever left it there — that's the sort of question you stop asking around year three. The valley doesn't answer questions. The valley answers by taking the question away, along with whatever else was rattling loose near it.

Here's how the trade works, for those of you keeping ledgers.

The Vale sits in a fold of hills where the maps go vague — deliberately vague, I've come to believe, though that belief cost me two good years of sleep. Travelers come through on the border road because it's the shortest route between the river towns and the mountain passes, and because no one has ever told them what waits here. What waits here is the wards. They're invisible, tasteless, odorless, and hungry. They sit over the valley like weather, and anything that enters them starts to forget — softly at first, then thoroughly, then completely. By the time a traveler reaches the far side, he's an empty coat walking. The valley keeps what it takes. Nobody knows why. Nobody remembers asking.

But the wards are greedy, and greedy things are sloppy. In the hour or so between crossing the boundary stones and forgetting how to count, a traveler's memories sit close beneath his skin like coins in a loose pocket. And I have hands.

I call it lifting. It isn't quite theft, because I don't keep most of what I take — I sell it. And it isn't quite robbery, because by the time I'm finished, the wards would have eaten the whole purse anyway. I'm not stealing the coin. I'm skimming the cream off milk that's about to curdle. That's the story I tell myself, anyway, and after eleven years I've got it polished smooth as a river stone. Stories about yourself are the one kind of memory the wards never seem to touch. Draw your own conclusions.

* * *

That particular morning began the way they all begin: gray, damp, smelling faintly of iron, with the mist sitting on the hills like a cat that owns the place. I was on the road before dawn with my hood up and my hands warm in my sleeves, because the lifting works better when the subject is cold and half-asleep and their memories haven't yet put up the day's shutters.

My mark came along at mid-morning: a tinker, middle-aged, leading a mule loaded with kettles that sang at every step like a choir with complaints. He had the look of a man who'd made this crossing before — which meant he'd forgotten making it, which meant the wards had taken something from him once already, and the scar tissue of that loss would make him easier to work. Second crossings always are. The mind, having lost a room once, stops guarding the other rooms.

I fell into step beside him the way you do. Road etiquette: you don't walk alone in the Vale if you can help it. Everyone knows this without knowing why, which is exactly the sort of arrangement the valley prefers.

"Fine morning," said the tinker.

"Lovely," I agreed. "Going to rain by noon."

He squinted at the sky, which held no opinion whatsoever, and decided to trust me. People do that out here. They hand you little pieces of themselves — opinions, fears, the names of their children — because the fog makes them lonely and loneliness makes them generous. I collect the small stuff first. It's cover. While a man is telling you about his daughter's cough, his attention is down in his chest somewhere, and the doors upstairs stand open.

We walked. He talked. His daughter's cough turned into his wife's temper, which turned into the price of copper, which turned into a story about his father teaching him to solder, back when — and here his voice did the thing voices always do in the Vale, the little stumble, the reach for something that wasn't where he'd left it.

I reached in and lifted.

You want to know what it feels like? Imagine drawing a thread out of a sleeve. There's a faint resistance, a whisper of unraveling, and then the thread comes free and the fabric holds its shape, mostly, except now it's missing one line of stitching and nobody will ever find the hole unless they go looking. What came off the tinker was his father at the soldering bench: heat-shimmer, the smell of rosin, big cracked hands guiding small ones. A whole afternoon, folded up small as a moth.

I didn't take all of it. Rule one. I took the afternoon itself but left him the fact of it — left him knowing his father had taught him the trade, even if the scene itself was gone. A memory with the picture torn out still leaves the frame on the wall. Frames are enough to live around. Most of us do.

"Your father taught you well," I said.

"He did," the tinker said, and frowned slightly, the way you frown when a word lands in a room you thought was empty. Then the frown smoothed over, because that's what the wards do — they iron things flat while you watch, and you watch them do it, and you forget you watched.

We parted at the fork above Miller's Hollow. He went east toward the river towns with his singing kettles and his neatly amputated past, and I stood in the road feeling the stolen afternoon warm against my ribs like a stone pulled from a fire, and I told myself the thing I always tell myself:

Somebody had to. If not me, the wards would've taken the whole shelf instead of one book.

It's a good speech. Eleven years old and it still gets applause every time.

* * *

The broker's shop sits in the drowned quarter of Ashfall-on-Vale — a town that isn't on any map, populated entirely by people who couldn't say how long they've lived there. Vess keeps his premises behind a chandler's, up a stair that creaks in a pattern I've memorized, which is the closest thing I have to knowing a place by heart, since heart-memory is the first thing the wards confiscate.

Vess received me as he always does, seated behind a desk too large for the room, wearing gray gloves, with his face doing its usual impression of a locked drawer.

"Tinker?" he asked.

"Tinker."

"And?"

I set the memory on the felt pad between us. You'll want to know how a memory becomes an object you can set down. Short answer: practice. Long answer: you fold it into a drop of the subject's sweat, or a thread from their cuff, and the ward-haze in the valley air does the rest, curing it into something like a bead of smoked glass. Inside each bead, the afternoon plays forever, tiny and complete. Vess says they keep. He'd know. He buys them by the score.

He held the bead up to the lamp, and for a moment his face forgot to be a locked drawer. Something moved behind it. Hunger, maybe. Or recognition.

"Soldering lesson," I said. "Father and son. Warm work. Sells to any guild-house with an apprentice program, or anyone who ever had a father they're not sure they remember."

"Mm." He set it down. "Fourteen."

"Twenty. It's clean, whole, and it weeps. Sentiment pieces always fetch more in autumn."

"Sixteen, and I'll throw in the news."

Vess deals in news the way other men deal in leftovers — reluctantly, and only when it's going bad. "What news?"

"There's a party coming through tomorrow. Quality. Six riders, armed, from the capital side. They're asking after the road conditions." He paused, gloved fingers tapping once. "Asking after them specifically. As in, named. As in, they used a word I've never heard a capital man use about the Vale."

My turn to keep my face shut. "Which word?"

"*Perimeter.*"

I laughed, because the alternative was to let him see the cold go through me. "The Vale isn't anybody's perimeter. The Vale isn't anywhere. Ask any map."

"I did," Vess said mildly. "Ask any map. Funny thing about these beads of yours, Thackeray. Cartographers' memories fetch the worst prices. Half of them are blank where this valley should be. Not fogged. Blank. Like the page was cut out and a different page glued in behind it."

"Sixteen," I said, "and we're square."

He counted the coins out without haggling further, which should have been my first warning. Vess never pays without a fight. A man who stops haggling has stopped seeing you as a supplier and started seeing you as something else — cargo, perhaps, or a piece already on the board.

At the door, his voice followed me, casual as spilled salt: "Riders pass through tomorrow. You might consider taking a day off the road."

"You worried about me, Vess?"

"I'm worried about stock levels," he said. But he didn't look at his ledger when he said it. He looked at me.

* * *

I took the day off the road. I told myself it was caution; it was actually cowardice with better tailoring, and both of those are just names for the same instinct that's kept me alive eleven years: *don't be seen by anything that knows what you are.*

So I spent the day in the house with the name carved over the door, doing the thing I do on days off, which is searching it. Again. For the thousandth time. Because here's the flaw in my comfortable arrangement, the splinter under the polish: I have no memories of my own to protect. The wards took mine before I ever learned to lift. Whatever Rosalind Thackeray was before he woke up in this house, the valley ate it, and I've spent eleven years picking through the bones of my own home looking for the crumbs it missed.

Floorboards: checked, all forty-two, twice. Chimney flue: checked. The space under the stairs where a child could hide: checked, and once I found something there — a wooden bird, whittled clumsy, painted blue — and I carried it around for a week like a relic before deciding it proved nothing except that somebody, sometime, had owned a knife and a lazy afternoon.

Today I went through the coat again. The one with my name in it. I've gone over every seam, and I know what it holds: nothing. But I held it up to the window light anyway, and I caught myself doing the thing I catch myself doing, which is talking to it.

"What was I?" I asked the coat. "Before? Was I happy? Was I somebody's —" and I stopped, because the sentence was heading toward *somebody's son*, and that sentence has teeth.

The house said nothing. Houses in the Vale are good at that. They've had practice.

Outside, the light was starting to fail, and I realized I'd spent the whole grey day standing in a room holding an empty coat, waiting for riders I was pretending not to fear, and I thought: *this is what I am. A man who steals other people's yesterdays because his own were confiscated.* The thought should have been bitter. Mostly it was just quiet. Quiet like the valley. Quiet like the wards, sitting overhead, patient as weather, eating the world one afternoon at a time.

And underneath the quiet — because there's always an underneath, and rule four, the unwritten one, is *never listen to the underneath* — underneath, something in me leaned against the walls of my own skull like a prisoner testing bars, and asked, in a voice I almost didn't recognize as mine:

*If you got your memories back, thief — whose would they be?*

I put the coat away. I banked the fire. And that night I dreamed, for the first time in eleven years, of a coronation: trumpets, gold light, a crowd roaring a name —

— and I woke at dawn with the dream already fading, already being ironed flat by the patient weather, and only one detail left clinging like a burr: a voice, deep and young and terrified underneath the joy, saying *remember this, whatever happens, remember this* —

— and the knock came at my door, hard, urgent, and through the wood, Vess's voice, stripped of all its locked-drawer calm:

"Thackeray. Get dressed. The stars are going out."
