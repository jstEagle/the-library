## 2. Thirteen Coins

There are three rules to selling a stolen memory, and I invented all of them myself, which is the closest thing I have to an inheritance.

Rule one: never sell what you can't describe. A buyer wants to know what he's getting, and if you can't put words to it, neither can he, and then you're both standing in Vess's back room staring at each other like two men who've forgotten why they came. Which, in this valley, is not a metaphor. It's a Tuesday.

Rule two: never sell the same memory twice. The wards scrub a traveler clean within a league of the border stones, but what I lift before that is mine, and mine once means mine only. Double-selling gets found out. Getting found out, in the memory trade, means someone remembers you wrong on purpose.

Rule three — and this is the one that keeps a thief breathing — never keep anything that glows.

I broke rule three the night the coronation came through.

* * *

He arrived at dusk, the way they all do, when the light goes amber and forgiving and makes even the road look like it's going somewhere. He was a courier type: lean, dust-caked, leading a mule that had clearly out-negotiated him on every stop of the journey. His name was Aldery, though I didn't know that yet. What I knew was the walk — the slightly too-careful gait of a man carrying something valuable and trying not to think about it, because thinking about it makes you check your pockets, and checking your pockets tells every cutpurse from here to the coast exactly where to look.

The valley has three roads and one inn, and I'd already bought his dinner, so when he settled onto the bench beside me with his ale, I was just a friendly local face. Rosalind Thackeray, purveyor of small kindnesses. The valley forgets everything else about its people; it might as well let us choose what little remains.

"You're not from here," he said, which was true, and also true of everyone, including the people who'd been born here and didn't know it.

"Nobody's from here," I said. "That's rather the point of the place."

He laughed. Travelers always laugh at that, the way you laugh at a joke you don't fully hear over loud music. Then I bought him a second ale, and my fingers found the side of his neck where the pulse runs shallow under the skin, and I took.

Taking a memory is nothing like theft as the ballads imagine it. There's no yanking, no violence. It's more like lifting a stone off a well and watching the water rise to meet you. You feel the shape of it first — memories have weight, texture, temperature. Most are dull things: ledgers, quarrels, a woman's laugh heard once and hoarded for years. Aldery's surface thoughts were coin counts and a sore tooth and an obsessive rehearsal of the phrase *"thirteen, no fewer"* — which told me either he was a gambler or a messenger, and gamblers don't ride mules into valleys that erase them.

Then I went a layer deeper than I usually bother with, because his cargo-sense was humming like a struck bell, and there it was.

A coronation.

Not a description of one. Not a courtier's recollection, secondhand and scented with incense. This was *inside* it — heat from ten thousand bodies packed into a cathedral, gold so heavy on the brow that the wearer's neck ached with it, the smell of oiled steel and lilies, and underneath it all a terror so vast and quiet it made the ceremony's joy feel like paint over rot. A young man's hands, gloved, trembling. A voice saying words I couldn't hold on to, sliding away like fish. And a thought, clear as a knife laid flat on silk:

*I am not ready, and no one alive knows how much that matters.*

I'd lifted thousands of memories off the border road. Farmers' harvests, merchants' affairs, soldiers' nightmares, once — memorably — an entire childhood spent falling out of boats. Never anything like this. Stolen memories usually feel borrowed, secondhand, like wearing another man's coat. This felt like being *looked at*. Like whoever had lived it had left something in it on purpose, the way you leave a lamp burning in a window for someone whose face you've forgotten.

I sat back. Aldery was still talking about the price of feed in the lowlands. My hands were shaking, and I hid them around my mug like a man warming himself, which was even half-true.

Here's what I should have done, per my own rules: I should have skimmed the top layer, kept the useful trivia, sold the rest in slivers. Instead I did something I hadn't done since I first taught myself this trade — I kept it whole. All of it. The heat, the dread, the trembling hands, the terrible private sentence. I wrapped it up and tucked it into the deepest fold of myself, where I keep the few things I refuse to trade, and I told myself it was because a whole coronation would fetch a whole price.

That was a lie, and I knew it was a lie, and knowing it changed nothing. Some hungers aren't about profit. Mine is simpler and worse: I want to remember *something*. Anything. The valley took whatever childhood I might have had before I was old enough to guard it, and every memory I steal is a window I open in a house with no walls. I have stood inside kings' funerals and fishermen's weddings and one truly alarming bear attack, and I have come away from all of it hungry. So when a memory arrives that feels less like merchandise and more like a hand extended across a crowded room — well. Rule three exists because I know myself.

I walked Aldery to the border stones at dawn, all friendliness, and watched the wards take the rest of him. It's not violent. He simply went quiet mid-sentence — he'd been telling me about his sister in Dellamere — and then he turned his mule around and rode back the way he came, whistling, carrying a head full of weather and nothing else. The valley does not hate travelers. It doesn't do anything as personal as hating. It just smooths them flat, like a thumb over wet ink.

I waved goodbye to a man who no longer remembered waving hello, and I went to find Vess.

* * *

Vess keeps shop behind the tanner's row, in a building that smells of oak gall and old paper, which is the smell of money in a town where nobody can prove they own anything. He's older than the buildings, possibly than the valley, a narrow man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like two coins dropped down a well — you hear them land, eventually, but you never see the bottom.

"Thackeray." He didn't look up from his ledger. "You have the look of a man who's brought me something."

"I have the look of a man who hasn't decided yet."

"That is also the look of a man about to bring me something." He set down his pen. "Sit. You loom."

I sat. Around us, his shelves held the trade's paraphernalia — lead-lined boxes, vials of grey salt, a brass instrument like a sextant crossed with a tuning fork that I'd been told never to touch and therefore wanted to touch desperately. Vess deals in more than memories, though memories are the coin of our particular realm. He deals in certainty, which is scarcer.

"A coronation," I said. "Whole. Firsthand. Inside the skull of whoever wore the crown."

Vess went very still. Vess going still is like a lake going still; you realize only afterward how much had been moving before.

"Theirs," he said. Not a question, which was strange. Every buyer asks *whose*. Whose grief, whose triumph, whose wedding — provenance sets the price. He asked instead: "Yours to give?"

"Mine to sell. Same thing, most days."

"And the traveler who carried it?"

"Gone over the border. Clean."

"Naturally." He opened a drawer and took out the payment scale — actual scales, brass, older than any coin minted in living memory. "Describe it."

So I did, carefully, per rule one. The heat, the gold, the lilies and steel. The trembling hands. And I hesitated — I'll admit it, I hesitated — over the last part, the sentence about readiness, because some animal instinct told me that naming it aloud in this room would be like reading coordinates to an artillery officer.

But Vess deals in precision, and vague goods fetch vague prices, and I am a professional.

"There's a thought in it," I said. "*I am not ready, and no one alive knows how much that matters.*"

Something happened to Vess's face. Nothing dramatic — a tightening around the mouth, a blink held a fraction too long. But I've spent my life reading faces in the two seconds before people arrange them, and I'd swear on my own nonexistent grave that what crossed his was recognition. Not surprise. Recognition, followed immediately by the effort of unrecognizing.

"Unusual," he said.

"It's a strong piece."

"It's more than strong." He was already moving along the shelf, pulling down boxes. "It's *situated*. Do you understand what I mean? Memories have geography. Most are rooms. This is a crossroads." He set three boxes on the counter, then a fourth, then, after a pause during which I could hear my own heartbeat getting ideas above its station, five more. Eight boxes. Lead-lined. Each one worth more than the inn, probably more than the inn's landlord's secrets.

"Vess." I kept my voice level, because a trader who sounds surprised dies rich in someone else's ledger. "What's the count?"

"Thirteen coins," he said, "per box."

Thirteen. I want you to appreciate what that number did in the air between us. Memory-brokers pay in standard weights — tens, fifties, hundreds. Nobody pays thirteen of anything. Thirteen is a ritual number, a ward-number, the number of turns in certain locks I'd only heard about from drunks. Either Vess was insulting me in some code I didn't speak, or he was telling me something in one I did.

"Generous," I said.

"Suspiciously," he agreed, with the ghost of a smile. "Take it or leave it."

Now, rule one of surviving Vess is to haggle, and I opened my mouth to do it, and then I looked at the boxes, and then at his eyes — those wells — and I understood, with the cold clarity that occasionally visits thieves and is always unwelcome, that he wasn't paying for a memory. He was paying for delivery. I was not a merchant in this transaction. I was a courier who had finally carried the thing he'd been steered toward, and the coins were a receipt.

"Questions," I said instead of haggling. "I get questions answered. That's my price adjustment. One question per box."

"Eight questions." He almost looked amused. "Ask."

I had eight questions prepared, in the way you prepare exits from a room you expect to need them for. Where did the memory originate. Why the number thirteen. Who else trades in coronations. What happens at the border stones, really, under the wards. Whether the stars used to be closer.

I asked none of them.

Instead I heard myself say: "Why do you already know whose memory this is?"

The silence stretched long enough to have a biography. Vess closed his ledger with a soft, deliberate sound, like a door being shut on a sleeping child's room.

"Because," he said, "some things pass through this valley more than once. And I have been keeping accounts for a very long time." He pushed the boxes across the counter. "Go home, Thackeray. Count your coins. Don't open the memory again — not tonight, not alone. And if a stranger comes to your door asking after a coronation..."

He paused, and for the first time since I'd known him, the well-eyes looked genuinely bottomless.

"...be somewhere else."

* * *

I counted the coins by candlelight, because counting money is a prayer thieves actually believe in. Thirteen to a box, eight boxes, a hundred and four coins, stacked in towers that threw long shadows up my wall like bars.

I should have felt rich. I have felt rich before; it lasts about as long as the walk home from Vess's.

What I felt instead was the memory, pressing against the folds I'd tucked it into, warm as a live coal. And beneath it, fainter, a thing I hadn't noticed at the border stones, hadn't noticed in Vess's shop, and could not now stop noticing: the coronation memory had edges. Clean ones. Deliberate ones. Real memories fray at the borders, all loose threads and smell and irrelevant detail. This one was *finished*. Shaped. Someone had cut it to fit something — a box, a sigil, a slot in a larger design — and it had been waiting, patient as seed stock, for a mind loose enough to carry it.

For a thief loose enough to carry it.

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the valley do its nightly work — that vast soft silence where forgetting settles over the rooftops like frost. Somewhere out there, the wards hummed their tuneless hymn, smoothing every traveler flat, keeping the valley empty and the world outside unknowable.

And for the first time in my careful, competent, thoroughly lonely life, I wondered whether the valley wasn't keeping something *in*.

Or — worse, colder, truer — whether it wasn't keeping something *in* for someone.

The coins caught the last of the moonlight. Thirteen apiece, gleaming like tiny closed doors.

Three nights later, the first star went out.
