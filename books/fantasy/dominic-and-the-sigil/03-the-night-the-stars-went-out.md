## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

I want it on the record that I noticed the first star die before anyone else did.

This matters to me because in a valley that forgets everything, being the one who notices is the closest thing to a legacy I'll ever have. Someone has to remember that Rosalind Thackeray saw it coming, even if that someone is only me, and even if by morning I won't be sure whether I dreamed it.

It was the third hour past dusk, and I was lying on my back on the roof of the tollhouse with a stolen memory of honeyed wine dissolving on my tongue — don't ask how I got it, or rather, do ask, but know that the answer involves a wool merchant, a flask, and a level of gullibility I find frankly insulting in a grown man — when the star over the northern ridge went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Went out*, the way a candle goes out when someone pinches it: deliberate, instant, offended into darkness.

I sat up so fast I nearly rolled off the roof, which would have been an undignified end for the valley's finest memory-thief. Drowned falling off his own roof. They'd have forgotten me inside a week anyway, but still.

The northern star was gone. In its place, nothing — a hole in the sky like a missing tooth.

Now, you have to understand something about stars in this valley. We have them. Of course we have them; the wards may eat your mother's face from your memory, but they leave the sky alone, because the sky belongs to no one and remembering it does no one any good. Every night the same scatter of lights, every night the same slow wheel. I had counted them once, out of boredom, out of that particular madness that comes from living somewhere that refuses to hold onto anything — four hundred and eleven visible from the tollhouse roof, give or take the ones near the horizon that liked to play games.

Four hundred and ten now.

"Go out," I told it. "Come back."

It did not come back. Stars are bad listeners. So are valleys, so are wards, and so, historically, am I.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the wards, which is to say here is what I'd pieced together from thirteen years of working the border road, because nobody tells you anything in this valley and the wards certainly don't:

They sit along the ridgeline like a fence made of silence. Travelers come through — merchants, pilgrims, the occasional lost soldier who never seems lost enough — and somewhere around the third milestone their memories start to fray. Names go first. Then faces. Then the reasons they set out at all, until they arrive at the valley's heart blinking and mild and unable to say why they came, only that they're terribly thirsty and isn't the weather fine.

My trade lives in that gap between the third milestone and forgetting. I get to them while the memories are still whole enough to lift — a touch to the temple, a whispered word Tam-the-herbalist taught me before she forgot she ever taught me anything, and the memory comes away like fruit off a stem. I keep what sells. Coronations and weddings and battles fetch the best prices from Vess. First kisses I sometimes keep for myself, though I've never been able to explain why, since I can't remember having one of my own.

And here is the other thing about the wards, the thing I learned the way you learn not to touch a hot stove: they work both ways. Whatever's outside the valley doesn't remember us either. The road beyond the pass might as well end at the ridgeline for all the world recalls there's anything past it. I once asked a traveler — this was years ago, a surveyor with ink-stained fingers and a memory like a well-stocked pantry — what lay north of the mountains. He looked at me with genuine confusion and said, "North of the mountains? Nothing's north of the mountains." And then he frowned, because some part of him knew that was wrong, the way you know you've forgotten a word that's sitting right on the tip of your tongue.

That frown haunted me for a season. Then the valley took it from me too, and I only know about it now because I wrote it down, because writing survives the wards — mostly, mostly, the ink fades slower than the mind — and my journals are the only autobiography in existence written entirely in secondhand pieces.

So: four hundred and ten stars. One dead. And me on a roof with wine-flavored memory on my tongue and a feeling in my chest I didn't have a name for yet, though by morning I would: it was the feeling of a lock turning somewhere very far away, or very close, and not knowing whether you're the thing being locked in or locked out.

* * *

The second star went out while I was still telling myself the first one was atmospheric interference.

I'm not proud of how long I clung to that explanation. Fog. Clouds. A particularly ambitious bird. But the sky was clear as glass, cold and hard and honest, and the second star — east-southeast, low over the chapel ruins — simply ceased, and this time I saw the manner of it. It didn't fade. It *pulled inward*, the light gathering itself up like a spilled drink running backward into the cup, and then it was gone, and where it had been there was a darkness so complete it looked less like absence and more like presence. Like something had arrived where the star used to be and was staying.

"Right," I said aloud, to the roof, to the valley, to whatever ran the wards. "No."

Because here's the thing about terror: it arrives late for me. It always has. Some defect of my construction — fear shows up after the fact, knocking politely while I've already done the stupid brave thing, asking if perhaps I'd like to panic now. So instead of panicking, I climbed down, fetched my coat and my knife and the little tin box where I keep the memories too strange or too precious to sell, and I started walking toward the border road.

Toward the dying stars. Toward the thing every instinct bred into me by thirteen years of survival said to walk *away* from.

I told myself it was professional curiosity. A thief surveys new territory; that's just sound practice. I told myself that if the wards were failing, the travelers' memories might last longer tonight, and a man has to think of his margins. I told myself several other things, each less convincing than the last, and none of them were the truth, which was simpler:

If the sky was going dark, I wanted to see it happen. Because a valley that forgets everything had somehow left me a person who couldn't stop looking at the horizon, and I had spent thirteen years pretending that hunger was ambition when it was actually grief — grief for a world I'd never seen, on the far side of a fence I'd never chosen.

The road was empty. That should have been my first warning. On any given night there were carts, campfires, the mutter of travelers bedding down against the cold. Tonight: nothing but wind and my own footsteps and the smell of the pines going sharp and strange, like the air before lightning.

At the second milestone, the third star died directly overhead, and I felt it.

I don't mean I saw it. I mean I felt it go, the way you feel a door slam in another room — a pressure change, a shiver through the soles of my boots, up through my teeth. And beneath my feet, faint but unmistakable, the ground hummed. The wards. Thirteen years I'd lived inside them without ever once hearing them, the way you don't hear your own heartbeat, and now they were humming like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, you're scared."

I'd never thought of the wards as capable of fear. They were weather to me, geography, the shape of my prison described in a language I didn't speak. But that hum had a timbre, and the timbre was a held breath, and things only hold their breath when they're afraid of what comes next.

The fourth star went out over the western ridge, and the hum climbed half a tone.

* * *

I reached the old watchpost at the fifth milestone — a stone hut the size of a coffin, built by hands nobody remembers, where I do my best work lifting memories from travelers too tired to notice a shadow leaning close — and I stopped, because the sixth star didn't go out quietly.

It screamed.

There's no better word. Light doesn't scream, but this one did — a thin, high, silver sound that started above the treeline and drilled straight down through the night and into the bones of the valley, and every hair on my arms stood up, and the wards' hum broke into discord, dozens of notes fighting each other, a choir losing its mind.

And then, all across the sky, the rest of them began to go.

Not one at a time anymore. In clusters, in strings, like lamps snuffed down a corridor by someone walking fast. North ridge: gone. The river of faint lights I'd never bothered naming: gone, gone, gone. Each death came with its own small cry, pitched differently, and layered together they made a sound I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe — a sound like breaking glass, yes, but also like tearing silk, also like the last note of an enormous instrument finally allowed to stop playing after centuries.

The wards shrieked back.

I dropped to my knees in the middle of the road. I'm not ashamed of it. When the fence you've lived inside your whole life starts screaming, kneeling is a reasonable response. The air itself seemed to thicken, then thin, then thicken again, breathing raggedly, and above the ridgeline I saw them — the ward-lines themselves, visible for the first time in my life, great pale threads of light strung along the peaks like the strings of some vast instrument, and they were *fraying*. Unraveling thread by thread, each broken strand burning out with a pop of white sparks that fell upward, which is not a thing sparks should do.

A strand snapped directly overhead. The crack of it went through my skull like a whip.

And then — this is the part I could not invent if I tried, the part I checked three times against my own journal the next morning because I was certain grief or terror had bent my memory — and then the sky opened.

Not literally. No hole, no hand reaching down. But the darkness where the stars had been wasn't empty anymore. It was *deep*. It went back. All my life the night sky had been a ceiling, close and decorated, four hundred and eleven candles hung just overhead. Now the ceiling was gone and I was staring up into a depth that had no bottom, a blackness so vast and so full — full of distance, full of *elsewhere* — that my eyes watered trying to focus on it.

And in that depth, faint as breath on a mirror, new lights. Dim ones. Thousands of them. Stars I had never seen, in patterns I had never seen, arranged around a soft band of pale glow that arched from one horizon to the other like a river of milk.

The world outside.

Thirteen years behind a fence, and I had never once suspected there was more sky than this. Nobody had. The wards hadn't just kept the world from remembering us — they'd painted us a ceiling, a modest, manageable universe, four hundred and eleven lights and not one more, because a prisoner who can see infinity starts asking questions about doors.

I sat down in the road. I think I laughed. I honestly can't tell you; the journal says I laughed until my ribs hurt, and then it says I cried, and then it stops making entries for a while, which the journal never does, because the journal is the one part of my life that never blinks.

Terror and exhilaration, the two of them holding hands in my chest like children. Because the sky was dying — the stars I knew were being murdered one by one by something with intent, and the wards were coming apart around me like wet paper, and when they finished coming apart, whatever had been kept out would stop being kept out, and I had a sudden vivid understanding that the valley had never been protecting the world from us.

It had been protecting us from the world.

But God, the *sky*. The real one. I lay back in the dirt of the border road, in the shadow of my own prison's collapsing walls, and I looked up into the true size of things, and some animal part of me that predated the valley, that predated my stolen name, that maybe predated me entirely, looked at that river of pale light and *recognized* it. Not remembered — recognized. The way your hand recognizes a railing in the dark. Somewhere under everything the wards had left me, something in me knew that band of light, knew it the way you know your own reflection, and it was singing, silently, furiously, *home, home, home*—

The last ward-thread snapped.

The sound was exactly like breaking glass, if the glass were the size of the sky. Every remaining star overhead went out at once — all of them, together, a single chord cut mid-note — and the valley dropped into darkness deeper than any night, lit only by the alien constellations of the true sky and the last white sparks of the wards drifting upward like snow falling the wrong direction.

Then the sparks went out too.

And in the ringing silence that followed, from the direction of the pass, from the world outside, from the dark where four hundred and eleven stars used to be, I heard footsteps.

Unhurried. Deliberate. Coming down the border road toward me, crunching on frost, accompanied by the soft whir and click of wings — a bird, flying low, keeping pace with the walker.

I did what any sensible thief would do, with his prison's walls down and the infinite sky gaping overhead and something approaching out of the dark that had killed the stars.

I hid behind the smallest rock I could find, knife drawn, heart hammering, and watched the darkness come.

* * *

The footsteps stopped twenty paces from my rock.

"Rosalind Thackeray," said a voice — human, male, worn smooth as river stone, and it knew my name, which no voice outside my own head had spoken in thirteen years, because names don't survive the road and mine especially shouldn't have, I'd made sure of it, I'd buried it so deep even I had to dig — "I know you're there. Your valley kept excellent records, and so did I."

The bird landed on the milestone beside him. In the light of the strange stars I could see it was a crow, sleek and disreputable, head cocked, regarding me with one eye that caught the starlight like a drop of oil.

"I've walked a long way," the voice said, and now I could hear what the smoothness was covering: it was fraying, like the ward-threads had frayed, a voice running out of itself. "Further than you'd believe. And I have something that belongs to you. Or you belong to it. The distinction stopped mattering around the fourth mountain."

I should have run. Every rule I'd built my life on said run — unknown means danger, danger means gone, gone means alive. Thirteen years of rules.

But the sky was open, and the crow was watching me with an expression I can only call *expectant*, and the man's voice cracked on his next word like ice on a pond in spring, and underneath the terror, underneath everything, that wordless thing in my chest that had sung *home* at the band of stars was now singing something else, quieter, more terrible:

*him.*

"Come out," the stranger said gently. "Please. I don't think I have the strength to wait long, and what I'm carrying — it needs to reach someone before I—"

He didn't finish. There was a sound like a sack of grain dropping, and the crow cried out — actually cried out, a raw human-sounding note of alarm — and I was moving before my fear could file its objection, scrambling over the frost-hard ground toward the fallen shape at the roadside, because it turns out all my rules had one exception, one flaw, one door the valley never found to lock:

I have never once been able to walk past someone falling.

He was still breathing when I reached him. His hand was locked around a small iron-bound box, knuckles white as the frost, and the crow hopped onto his chest and looked at me with both eyes now, and said, in a voice like a dry leaf learning to talk:

"Well? Don't just stand there, boy. He hasn't got all night. And neither," she added, with what I would later learn was characteristic understatement, "do you."
