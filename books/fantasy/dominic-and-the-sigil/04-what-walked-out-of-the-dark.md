## 4. What Walked Out of the Dark

I want it on the record that I did the sensible thing first.

When the knocking started—three slow strikes against my door at an hour when even the valley's ghosts had given up pretending to be people—I blew out my candle and stood very still in the dark, knife already in my hand, and waited for whoever it was to go away. That's rule one of surviving a place that eats memories: don't answer doors. Don't open windows. Don't be interesting. The valley forgets you if you let it, and I had spent eleven years making myself as forgettable as possible, which is its own kind of tragedy when you think about it too long, so I try not to.

The knocking came again. Weaker this time. Then something heavy slid down the other side of my door with a sound like a sack of wet grain hitting stone, and a voice said, in a tone of profound irritation that seemed to come from somewhere near the ground:

"He's dying. Are you going to stand there being prudent, or are you going to be useful? I know you're in there. I can smell the candle tallow and the cowardice."

Now, here's the thing about living alone in a valley where nobody remembers anything: you get used to voices. You hear them in the wind off the wards, in the mutter of travelers whose minds are coming apart like cheap rope. You learn to sort them into categories—harmless, dangerous, and harmless-but-annoying—and file them away without opening anything.

But this voice was *clear*. Sharp as a ledger line. And it was coming from below knee height.

I opened the door.

I'd like to say it was compassion. It wasn't, entirely. It was curiosity, which has killed more thieves than any guard dog ever born, and I include myself among the dead-to-rights. But there was also this: in eleven years, nothing in the valley had ever spoken to me like I was worth arguing with. The wards didn't argue. The travelers didn't remember me long enough to. And whatever was out there on my step had opinions and a vocabulary and what sounded suspiciously like contempt for my survival instincts, and I found, to my considerable embarrassment, that I wanted to hear more.

So much for rule one.

* * *

He was sprawled across my threshold like something the road had chewed up and spat back out as a warning to others. Tall, or he would have been if he'd been vertical—a long frame gone gaunt, the kind of thin that comes from running rather than fasting, though from the state of him I'd guess he'd done both. His coat had been fine once. Good wool, silver thread at the cuffs, now stiff with mud and darker stains I didn't need a physician's training to identify. There were wounds on him I could count and wounds I couldn't, and the ones I couldn't worried me more: no blade marks, just places where his skin had gone grey and faintly translucent, like wax held too near a flame.

And clutched against his chest, both arms wrapped around it the way a drowning man holds driftwood, was a box.

Not a large one. About the size of a hymnal, bound in black metal that drank the moonlight instead of reflecting it—which, given that the moonlight itself had been behaving strangely since the stars went out, was saying something. No lock that I could see. No hinges either, now that I looked. Just a seamless dark thing, humming very quietly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have ended.

"Water," the man rasped, without opening his eyes. "Please."

"You're not supposed to be able to talk," I said, because apparently my bedside manner needed work. "Nobody remembers how to talk by the time they make it this far up the road. The wards take the words before the border takes them."

"The wards are dead." He coughed, and the sound had gravel in it. "You haven't noticed? Looked up lately?"

"I noticed."

"Then stop gawking and fetch the water."

There was a crow on my fence post. I should mention her now, because she'd been there the whole time and I hadn't seen her, which bothered me then and bothers me more in retrospect. Black feathers gone oil-slick blue in the moonless dark, head cocked, watching me with the flat golden appraisal of something deciding whether I'm predator, prey, or furniture.

"He's right about the water," she said.

I dropped the knife.

To be clear about the sequence of events: I heard a bird speak in a dry, precise, faintly bored voice; my body interpreted this as an attack; and my hands, which have saved my life a hundred times, chose that moment to betray me completely. The knife hit the step. The crow looked at it, then at me, and made a sound I can only describe as a laugh, if a laugh could be filed under *disappointed*.

"Oh, wonderful," she said. "A thief with nerves. He'll do."

"He'll do for what?" I asked, retrieving the knife mostly so my hands had a job. "Who are you? What is that thing?" I nodded at the box. "And why does a bird talk?"

"One question at a time, and none of them well," the man said. "Water first. Then we'll see how much of the rest I live to tell you."

Here is where I should have shut the door. Here is where every rule I'd built my life on—the rules that kept me fed, hidden, and hollow—said *close it, bar it, wait until morning, and if he's still there, roll him into the ravine and check his pockets.* That's not cruelty. That's arithmetic. A stranger bleeding out on your step in a valley that erases memory is not a person; he's a liability wearing boots.

I went and got the water.

I've thought about why. Not that night—there wasn't time—but later, in the weeks after, when I had leisure to take my own character apart and examine the seams. Part of it was the talking crow, who had said *he'll do*, implying some prior expectation of me that I badly wanted explained. Part of it was the box, humming its buried-bell hum, because a thief looks at an unopenable lock the way a starving man looks through a bakery window. And part of it—this is the part I dislike admitting—was that he had said *please*. Eleven years in the valley, and I couldn't remember the last time anyone had said please to me. Or thank you. Or my name, which, granted, neither did I.

Loneliness makes you stupid. I've since learned it can also make you brave, but that night it just made me stupid, and stupid carried the water.

* * *

I got him inside and onto the floor by the cold hearth, because my bed was a pallet and my pride wouldn't survive dragging a dying stranger onto it anyway. He drank like a man who'd been rationing hope along with everything else—small sips, deliberate, throat working hard. Up close he smelled of ozone and old blood and something else underneath, something like the air before lightning, and I recognized it with a lurch: it was the smell the border road had carried ever since the stars went out. The smell of the wards failing. He was soaked in it.

"Your coat's ruined," I said, because small talk is a reflex even at the end of the world.

"It was ruined in better company." He managed something that was almost a smile. It cost him. "You're the one who works the road. The memory-thief."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards. "I don't know what you mean."

"You lift recollections off travelers before the wards dissolve them. Sell them to a broker in the low town. Vess." He said the name carefully, tasting it. "Yes. Your face does something interesting when I say that name. Guilt, mostly."

"How—" I stopped. Started again. "The valley doesn't remember. Nobody remembers me. That's the entire point of the place."

"The valley doesn't remember," he agreed. "But some things were never in the valley to begin with." He gestured, weakly, at the crow. "She watches roads. She watches everyone on them. She says you have quick hands and a crooked heart and that you always leave the travelers their names."

"That last part's practical," I muttered. "People without names panic. Panicking people run into ravines, and bodies in ravines bring ward-inspectors."

"Mm," said the crow, from the windowsill she'd claimed. "That's the reason you tell yourself, certainly."

"Do you have a name, or do I call you Bird?"

"Quill." She preened once, sharply, as if the name required maintenance. "And before you ask the obvious: yes, I talk, no, I won't explain it to your satisfaction, and yes, I am smarter than you. Most things are. Few of us admit it."

"She's also modest," the man said, and coughed again, longer this time. When it passed, there was blood on his lips, and the grey translucency had crept past his collar. Whatever was eating him wasn't in any hurry, but it wasn't dawdling either.

"Let me look at those wounds," I said.

"No." It came out flat, final. "Save your skill and your salves. I know what's killing me, and it isn't the cuts." He shifted, wincing, and pulled the black box around in front of him, resting both hands on its lid like a man taking an oath. "What's killing me is that I ran out of road. This needs to go further than I can carry it. Which means it needs someone with quick hands, a crooked heart, and enough sense to leave people their names."

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the offer."

"There's an offer? Because so far it's been a dying man, a rude bird, and a box that hums. My favorite combination. I collect all three."

"Rosalind." He said my name, and the room went strange around me. Not because of magic—though I've since learned there was plenty of that in play—but because hearing your own name in a place built to erase names is like hearing music in a house where all the instruments were burned years ago. You'd forgotten it was music. Then someone plays a note, and something in your chest breaks open along an old seam. "I know what you are. I know what you've lost. And I'm telling you that the box knows too."

"That's ominous and vague. Very persuasive."

"Open it," said Quill, from the sill.

"Don't," said the man, at the same moment.

They looked at each other. Something passed between them—an argument conducted in silence, old and worn smooth with repetition.

"Not yet," the man amended. "Not until I'm done. If you open it early, it opens *you*, and we haven't the time to scrape you back together." He settled back against the hearthstone, and I watched the grey creep another inch, patient as tide. "Listen, then. I'll be brief, because brief is all I have left."

* * *

He told me a story. I'll give it to you the way he gave it to me—broken, breathless, with gaps I filled in later and gaps I never filled at all.

He said the stars hadn't died. They'd been *put out*, one by one, over years, by something that needed the sky dark to work unwatched. He said the kingdom beyond the valley had a Regent now, beloved and terrible, who wore grief like jewelry and whose face appeared on coins and banners and in the dreams of anyone who slept too near the capital. He said the valley I lived in—the valley that forgot—wasn't a natural wound in the world. It was built. Warded. Made forgetful on purpose, a blindfold tied around a secret so large that even the secret's jailers weren't permitted to know it.

"And the secret?" I asked.

He smiled again, and this time it wasn't almost anything. It was full and bitter and sad, and it frightened me more than the blood had.

"In the box," he said. "Or the beginning of it. The rest is scattered. Hidden. Carried by people who don't know they carry it, in places that have forgotten themselves." His hands tightened on the lid. "It has to reach the threshold before the thirteenth turn. After that, the binding sets permanent, and everything inside stays inside forever—including, I suspect, the sky."

"The thirteenth turn of what?"

"The moon counts differently now," Quill said. "Thirteen turns to the cycle, where there used to be twelve. The extra turn is a keyhole. Or a deadline. Depends which side of the door you're standing on."

I looked at her. I looked at him. I looked at the box, which hummed its buried note, and I swear on whatever I have left to swear on, the hum changed when I looked—lifted slightly, the way a sleeping animal's breathing changes when it hears your footsteps and decides, provisionally, not to be afraid.

"I'm a thief," I said. "Not a courier. Not a hero. I steal memories off half-dissolved travelers and sell them to a man who pays in coin and questions in equal measure, and I like my life exactly the size it is, which is small enough to hide behind a door."

"Yes," the man said. "That's why it has to be you."

"That's the opposite of why it should be me."

"Small lives fit through small gaps." He closed his eyes. "Big heroes cast big shadows, and the Regent's hounds hunt shadows. But a thief with a crooked heart and quick hands, who leaves people their names..." He exhaled slowly. "You might slip under. You might actually carry it through."

"I might also die."

"Everyone does that part regardless," said Quill. "The only variable is what you're carrying when it happens."

I sat back on my heels. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a face. Somewhere down the valley, the dead wards ticked and settled, a great machine cooling into ruin, and I felt—for the first time in eleven years—that the walls of my small life had a crack in them, and wind was coming through, and the wind smelled like everything I'd never let myself want.

"What's in the box?" I asked. "Truly. No riddles."

The man opened his eyes. They were brighter than they should have been, fever-bright, and fixed on mine with an intensity that made the hair on my arms stand up.

"A king," he said.

And then, because the world apparently decided I hadn't been destabilized enough for one night, the crow spoke again.

"Ask him his name," she said softly. "Go on. Ask him what he was called, before the road took it."

"I—" The man's hand rose, trembling, toward the box. "My name was—"

He never finished it. The grey reached his heart, or wherever such things keep their clocks, and he went out between one breath and the next—not dramatically, not with a last speech, just a man-shaped absence settling into the shape he'd left behind. The fire of him snuffed. The room got colder by degrees.

For a long moment, nobody moved. Then Quill dropped from the windowsill, crossed the floor with a strange formal gravity, and stood over the body with her head bowed. Whether crows mourn I couldn't say. She stood like something that knew the rites, whatever they were, and was performing the parts that could still be performed.

"He knew," she said finally, quietly. "He knew it would end here. On a stranger's floor. He came anyway." She looked up at me, one gold eye catching the candlelight. "Well, thief. He's your problem now. So is the box. So, I regret to inform you, am I."

"I didn't agree to anything."

"You fetched the water." She hopped onto the dead man's chest, delicately, and then onto the box, and looked at me with an expression I can only call *expectant*. "In this business, that counts as a signature."

I should have thrown them both out. Box, bird, body, all of it, over the ravine and back to the dark that made them. Every rule I had said so. Survival said so. Eleven careful years of being nobody said so, loudly, in the voice of a man who had learned that wanting things is how the valley gets its hooks into you.

Instead I knelt beside the stranger I'd known for a single hour, and I did the one thing I do better than anything in the world.

I picked his pocket.

Gently. Respectfully, if theft can be respectful—it can, I insist, when the alternative is letting the valley's damp and rats have everything. I found a folded map that seemed to be inked in nothing at all until I tilted it toward the candle, and then lines bloomed across it like frost forming, silver-white, a road drawn in starlight leading out of the valley and east, east, east, to a mark shaped like an eye. I found a purse, light, which told me he'd spent everything getting here. And I found, sewn into his lining, a scrap of parchment with three words in a cramped, urgent hand:

*THE THIRTEENTH TURN.*

Underlined twice.

Quill watched me work the whole time with professional appreciation. "Quick hands," she said. "Told you."

Outside, far off down the valley, something howled. Not a wolf. Wolves have warmth in their voices. This was the sound the wards used to make, back when they worked, except wrong—like a song remembered incorrectly, played by something that had only had the melody described to it. And it was coming closer.

The box hummed. The map glimmered. The crow waited.

"All right," I said, to the box, to the bird, to the dead man, to the crack in my small life. "All right. But I'm keeping the purse."

Which is how, at the tail end of the worst night in the history of nights, I came to be packing a bag I swore I'd never pack, while a talking crow supervised and a locked box hummed its low buried note—and how I failed, entirely, to notice that the map's silver lines had begun, very faintly, to pulse. Like a heartbeat.

Like something on the other side of the paper, waking up.
