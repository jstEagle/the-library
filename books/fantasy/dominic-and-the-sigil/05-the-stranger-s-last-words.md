## 5. The Stranger's Last Words

I have a rule about the dying. It's not one of my official rules—those are the tidy ones, the ones I recite to myself like scripture: never take from the desperate, never take twice from the same traveler, never take more than you can sell. The rule about the dying is quieter and older and I've never said it out loud, because saying it would make it real: *stay until the end.*

Nobody stays until the end in this valley. The valley doesn't stay for anyone. But when a man is leaking his last hours onto your floorboards, the least you can do is not steal from him on the way out. So when the stranger's breathing went ragged somewhere past midnight, I put down the cloth I'd been using to clean the wound in his side, sat back on my heels, and waited.

He was propped against my good chair—the one with only three legs that I keep because it leans at an honest angle—and the locked box sat beside him where he'd insisted it go before he let me touch him. *The box first,* he'd said. *Then me.* A man with priorities. I respected that even as I resented it.

Quill had gone quiet on the windowsill. Not sleeping quiet. Watching quiet. Every few minutes she'd tilt her head at the stranger the way she tilted her head at locked things, and I got the distinct impression she was listening to something underneath his voice.

"Name," I said, because you can't sit vigil with a man all night and not know what to call him.

"Aldous." The word cost him. Everything cost him now; I could see the price of each breath ticking up like a merchant's spite. "Aldous Merrin. Once."

"Once what?"

"Cartographer." He almost smiled. It didn't survive the journey to his mouth. "You'll want to know why I came. Everyone always wants the why first."

"I don't," I lied. "I want you to stop talking and save your strength."

"And I want a river of wine and the return of my thirtieth year." His eyes found mine in the lamplight. They were the color of tea left too long, and there was something in them that frightened me more than the wound did—a kind of certainty. Dying men usually look surprised. He looked *scheduled*. "Rosalind Thackeray. Yes, I know your name. You sold a memory of a coronation to Vess four days ago. Gold crowns, a boy king, bells."

My spine went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the draft under my door. "How does a dying cartographer know what I sell?"

"Because I bought its twin twenty years ago." He laughed, or made the shape of a laugh, and pressed a hand to his side. When it came away dark, he looked at it with mild annoyance, like a man checking a leaky roof. "Vess paid you too much. He always pays too much for the ones that matter. It's how you know."

*It's how you know.* I thought about thirteen coins sitting in my floor cache, and about how I'd told myself they were fair payment for fair goods, and about how the memory had felt wrong in my hands all the way to Vess's door—too warm, too heavy, like bread meant for someone else's table.

"You knew him," I said. "The boy in the memory. Dominic."

"I drew the maps for the army that lost him." Aldous closed his eyes. "That's a sentence I've been carrying so long I forget other people haven't heard it. Draw me some water, thief, and I'll tell you a story, and then I'll die, and you'll wish I hadn't, because stories are heavier than boxes."

I gave him water. Of course I did. That's the trouble with rules—they sound very firm right up until a bleeding man asks you for a cup.

* * *

He talked for most of an hour, and I won't pretend I understood all of it, because I didn't. There were words in it that shouldn't exist—*sigil*, *binding*, *the crown-thing*, *the thirteenth turn*—and there were names, and there were places he described with the loving precision of a man who had measured them, and through all of it ran one thread pulled tight enough to sing:

The valley was not a valley. It was a lock. And everything inside it—including me, including my whole remembered life, such as it was—was furniture arranged around that lock.

I should have argued. I should have told him he was feverish, that the wards held fine, that I'd stolen memories off this road for eleven years and the sky had never once gone dark. Instead I heard myself say, in a voice I didn't recognize:

"The stars. Last night. They went out."

"They didn't go out," Aldous said gently. "They were taken. Or rather—" he winced, shifted, "—they were *called home*. The valley kept a copy of the sky, thief. A beautiful forgery. And now the original owner has come to collect, which means the lock is failing, which means we are all standing in a room whose walls are learning they're paper."

Quill made a sound I'd never heard from her—not a caw, not a croak. Something low and grieving, like a hinge in an empty house.

"She knows," Aldous said, without opening his eyes. "She was there. She'll tell you when she's ready. Crows keep time differently than we do. Now. The box."

"I'm not taking your box."

"You're taking my box, my map, and my debt." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a key no bigger than my thumbnail, black iron, shaped like a bird mid-flight. "And you're going to listen, because I have perhaps another hour in me and I refuse to spend it arguing with a man who steals memories for pocket money while a kingdom rots."

"That's slander. I steal them for rent."

For a moment—one bright, stupid moment—he laughed properly, and for that moment he wasn't dying at all, just a tired old mapmaker amused by a young fool. Then it folded away and he was gray again and reaching for the box.

"Open it."

* * *

Inside the box there was no gold. No letters, no weapons, none of the cargo a sensible courier dies carrying. There was a map.

I mean that literally, and I want you to understand what I mean, because I have spent my life around forged memories and false skies and I know a counterfeit when I hold one, and this was not one. The map was drawn on something like vellum but softer, and across it ran roads and rivers and ranges rendered in lines of faint silver light—not ink, not paint. Starlight. Inked in starlight, laid down by someone who had stood in the sky itself and copied what they saw.

And it was wrong, in the way that matters. Whole provinces were smudged out. Cities sat where cities had no business sitting. Rivers ran uphill toward a capital marked with a small, careful symbol: a circle, bound by thirteen lines.

"Thirteen marks," Aldous murmured, watching my face. "Count them."

I counted. Thirteen lines binding the circle, and at each line's end a tiny star drawn in that silver light—but two of the stars were crossed out, struck through with the same luminous ink, and one more was half-faded, dimming as I watched, the light guttering like a candle in a doorway.

"It counts down," Aldous said. "Every turn of the moon, a star goes. When the thirteenth goes out, the binding closes forever, and whatever's caught inside it is caught past any unmaking. Past grief, past regret, past gods." His breath hitched. "We are at the tenth star, Rosalind. Three turns left. Maybe less, now that the sky's been called home."

"What's caught inside it?"

He looked at me for a long time. Long enough that I saw him decide something—not whether to tell me, but how much. "A king," he said finally. "A true one. And a great many other things besides, and some of them are in this box, and some of them are walking around wearing a stolen face, and at least one of them, I suspect—" he stopped himself, shook his head. "No. One riddle per night. Find Odile. She'll draw you the rest."

"Who is Odile?"

"The best cartographer I ever knew, and the reason I'm dying in a thief's hovel instead of my own bed." Another almost-smile. "She'll hate that I sent you. She hates being right."

The box also held three objects beneath the map, wrapped in oilcloth. I didn't unwrap them. He watched me decide not to, and nodded slowly, like I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was sitting.

"Not yet," he agreed. "One is yours already, though you don't know it. One belongs to the road. And one—" his hand hovered over the third bundle and withdrew, trembling, "—one belongs to the dead, and I am nearly one of them, so mind how you carry it."

"Aldous. What am I carrying?"

"The thirteenth turn," he whispered.

I leaned closer. His eyes had fixed on something over my shoulder—past me, past the wall, past the forged edge of the world—and his lips kept moving after the sound stopped, shaping words with no air behind them. *The thirteenth turn. The thirteenth turn.* Over and over, softening, slowing, a coin spinning down toward stillness.

And then, at the very end, with the last of his breath, he said it plain. Clear as a bell over water. Clear enough that Quill flared her wings and I felt it in my teeth:

"*Tell her I finished the map.*"

* * *

He died a little before dawn, in my chair, facing east, which is where the stars used to be.

I did the things you do. I closed his eyes. I covered him with my blanket because I didn't own a shroud. I sat with him while the window went gray and then pale and then the color of old milk, and I thought about how I'd known him for one night, and how in one night he had known more of me than anyone alive—my name, my trade, my sale, my address—and how strange it is that grief doesn't check references. It doesn't care that you met yesterday. It arrives anyway, takes a seat, and refuses to leave.

I buried him at noon behind the house, under the crooked rowan, because the ground there was soft and because I liked the idea of him having a crooked tree. I dug with my own hands, which took hours, and Quill supervised from a fence post with the gravity of a foreman, and neither of us spoke, because what is there to say over a man who carried a box across a starless kingdom to give it to a stranger?

At the bottom of the grave, before I filled it, I put one thing: a coin. Not one of Vess's thirteen—I kept those; sentiment doesn't pay rent. An ordinary copper, worn smooth, the kind travelers pay me in without knowing they've paid. "For the ferryman," I said, because every land has its own superstitions and I'd rather be polite to all of them. "Whoever he is. Tell him a mapmaker is coming who draws very well."

Then I filled the grave, and the valley was silent around me, and for the first time in eleven years the silence sounded less like peace and more like holding its breath.

* * *

I spent the evening packing, though I hadn't decided to go. That's the trick of decisions sometimes—they make themselves while your hands are busy. Spare shirt, knife, the coins, the flint. The box. The map, which I'd rolled and unrolled six times by lamplight trying to find the route Aldous had traveled, and couldn't, because the roads on it moved. Slowly, subtly, the way clouds move—you'd swear they hadn't shifted until you looked away and back. A living map. A map that was *looking back.*

Around midnight Quill dropped from the sill onto the table beside the box and stood over it with both feet planted, feathers sleeked flat, and said, in her rusted-gate voice:

"He was telling the truth, you know. About all of it."

"I assumed so. Dying men rarely bother lying."

"Dying men lie constantly. It's their last chance to be interesting." She picked at the table with her beak, once, twice—a habit she had when she was choosing words carefully, which was rare enough that it scared me. "But he was telling the truth. And there's a part he didn't tell you, because he promised someone he wouldn't, and I keep worse promises than he did."

"Quill."

"Your name isn't an accident, Rosalind." Her eye, black and wet-bright in the lamplight, didn't blink. Birds don't blink the way we do, and when one holds your gaze like that, it feels like being weighed. "Thackeray. Say it slowly. Listen to what lives inside it. There's a reason a fragment of a king's coronation felt like a message in your hands. There's a reason Vess overpaid. There's a reason a dying cartographer walked out of a starless waste and collapsed at *your* door, of all the doors in this valley."

"Because I'm the nearest thief?"

"Because you're the nearest *keeper*." She hopped once, closer to me. "He finished the map, he said. Fine. Then finish his errand. But understand what you're agreeing to, boy. Once you cross that border, nothing calls you back. The valley will try. Oh, it will *try.*"

"Let it try," I said, and heard my own voice, steady, and marveled at it, because inside I felt anything but.

Quill studied me a moment longer, then dipped her head—the closest she ever came to a bow. "Then we leave before dawn. Take the west road while the fog's thick. And Rosalind?" She turned back at the window, silhouetted against a sky with nothing in it. "When it calls you—and it will call you—remember that voices from behind sound exactly like voices from ahead. The valley has no new tricks. Only old loves."

* * *

I slept two hours and dreamed of bells.

Gold crowns, a boy king, bells—the memory I'd sold, playing itself back in the dark of my skull like a song stuck in the throat. Except in the dream I wasn't watching the coronation. I was *in* it. Standing on the steps. Wearing the weight. And everyone in the crowd had their faces turned up toward me with an expression I recognized from the inside out, because it was the same expression I wear in the mirror every morning: hungry for something they couldn't name.

I woke before the fog lifted, shouldered the box, and stood a long moment in my doorway looking back at the room. Eleven years in it. My kettle. My crooked chair. My marks on the doorframe where I'd measured my height the first week, absurdly, as if I might grow into a past that wasn't coming.

Behind me, far off down the border road, something sighed—a long, low exhalation, like the valley turning over in its sleep. Like a lung remembering its purpose.

"Old loves," I repeated, and stepped out, and shut the door, and did not lock it. Let whoever came next make of it what they could.

Quill flew ahead into the fog, a small dark punctuation mark against a blank white page, and I followed the west road with a dead man's box on my shoulder and his last words in my ears, and I did not look back.

Mostly.

There's a bend in the road, a quarter mile out, where the fog thins and you can see the whole valley spread below you one final time—my roof, the border stones, the road where I'd done my quiet work for eleven years. I told myself I wouldn't look. I looked. And in the gray half-light, at the edge of my vision where the fog was thickest, something stood at my gate. A figure. Small, still, familiar in the way a coat is familiar on the wrong shoulders.

It raised one hand. Not waving. Beckoning.

I walked on. I counted my steps to keep from running—four hundred of them—and at step four hundred and one the fog closed behind me like water over a stone, and the valley was gone, and I was alone on a starless road with a crow, a box, and a promise to a man I'd known for a single night.

Three turns of the moon. Two stars already crossed out.

Somewhere ahead, a woman named Odile hated being right, and a kingdom I'd never seen was waiting to find out what I'd stolen next.
