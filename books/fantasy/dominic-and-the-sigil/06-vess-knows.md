## 6. Vess Knows

The broker's shop smelled of tallow and old paper, the way it always did, and Vess was behind his counter doing what he always did: nothing, beautifully. He had a talent for stillness that most men spent lifetimes failing to achieve. His hands were folded on a ledger closed with a brass clasp, and his eyes—pale as watered milk—were already on me when I pushed through the door with its bell that never rang right.

"Rosalind." He said my name the way he priced things: carefully, and lower than he meant to. "You look like a man who hasn't slept."

"I slept fine."

"You're lying. You lie the way other men breathe—it's your best quality—but I've had thirty years of practice reading thieves." He unfolded his hands. "Sit down."

I didn't sit. Sitting in Vess's shop was how you ended up owing him something you couldn't name. "I'm not here to trade."

"No," he agreed. "You're here because the sky went out and the wards died and you spent last night holding a dying stranger's hand, and now you have questions you're too proud to ask anyone but me." He smiled without warmth. "Ask them."

My hand went to my coat pocket where the map lay—the one inked in starlight, the one the stranger had pressed into my palm along with a locked box and two words: *the thirteenth turn*. I hadn't shown it to anyone. I hadn't told anyone about the box, or the crow currently roosting on my windowsill back home, or the way the stranger's blood had soaked through my shirt while he whispered about turns like a man counting steps down into a cellar.

"How do you know about the stranger?" I asked.

Vess opened the ledger.

That was all. He turned it around on the counter so the pages faced me, and there was my own handwriting looking back at me—or near enough, because every memory I'd ever sold him was catalogued there in his neat clerk's script: dates, descriptions, prices paid. *Coronation, golden hall, thirteen coins. Widow's first kiss. Soldier's last morning. Fisherman's daughter, age six, the drowning.* Page after page after page. Fifteen years of my life as a thief of other people's pasts, itemized like jars of pickled eel.

"You keep records," I said. My voice came out flat. Good. Flat was safe.

"I keep *accurate* records. There's a difference, and the difference is going to matter to you in about four minutes." He tapped one entry with a blunt finger. "This one. The coronation. You sold it to me eleven days ago for thirteen coins—which, incidentally, I overpaid for deliberately, because I wanted you thinking about it. And then last night, an hour before the wards failed, someone tried to buy it back from me at any price I named."

"Who?"

"A courier wearing colors I haven't seen since before you were born. Royal livery, if the crown still had a royalty to wear it." He leaned back. "I refused. An hour later, the stars started going out."

* * *

I want to say I handled the next part well. I want to tell you I asked sharp questions and kept my footing. What actually happened is I gripped the edge of his counter hard enough that my knuckles went white, and I said, "Start from the beginning, Vess, or I walk out and you never see me again."

He considered that. With Vess, consideration always looked like boredom. "The beginning," he repeated. "Fine. But understand—I only know what I was told, and I was told by people who are dead now, mostly. The valley wasn't always called the valley. It doesn't have a real name anymore because names were the first thing the wards ate. Before that, it was a border province. Fertile. Boring. The kind of place kings forget until they need soldiers from it."

"And?"

"And sixty years ago—give or take; the records were burned, which should tell you something—it became something else. It became a lock." He spread his hands. "You've lived inside a prison your whole life, Rosalind. So have I. We just never saw the bars because the bars were made of forgetting."

"The wards erase memories. That's their function. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows what they were *told*. The wards don't erase memories. They *contain* one." His pale eyes fixed on me. "One memory, specifically. One very large, very old, very angry memory, folded up small and nailed to the center of the valley like a moth to a board. Everything else—the travelers' recollections dissolving on the road, the fog on the borders, the reason nobody who leaves remembers why they came—all of that is runoff. Spillage. The containment ward has to eat *something*, and it eats whatever remembering comes near it."

I thought about the road. About the travelers who walked through with their lives trailing behind them like scarves in wind, and how I'd learned to snatch those scarves before the valley swallowed them. Fifteen years I'd thought myself clever. A poacher in someone else's preserve.

"What memory," I said, "is worth building a prison the size of a province?"

Vess looked at me for a long moment. Then he reached under the counter and brought out a cloth-wrapped bundle, and unwrapped it, and set it between us.

It was a sigil. Metal, silver-dark, no bigger than my palm—a disc worked with lines so fine they seemed to move when I wasn't looking directly at them, concentric rings around a central seal shaped like a crown seen edge-on. It hummed against the counter. Not sound, exactly. More like the feeling you get when someone says your name in a room you thought was empty.

"This came through my shop twenty-two years ago," Vess said quietly. "A ward-smith, half-dead, carrying it out of the deep country. He told me what it was before he died on my floor. It's called a binding sigil, and inside it is bound a king. Dominic. The last true king of this country, deposed and unmade, his self cut into pieces and sewn into this metal so that the thing wearing his crown could never be challenged by him again."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the dead wards made no sound at all, and somehow that silence was louder than anything.

"That's a story," I said. "That's a story told to frighten children."

"It is. And like most such stories, it's a ledger entry with the interesting parts removed." He turned the sigil over. On the back, scratched small and hurried, were characters I almost recognized—ward-script, the kind Tam's master would have known, though I didn't know that yet. "The valley isn't a prison built *around* a man, boy. The valley IS the prison. Every ward, every fog bank, every stolen memory on that road—they exist to keep one bound king from being remembered back into existence. Because here's the thing about binding magic, the thing they don't put in the stories: a bound thing can be *unbound*. And an unbound king is a problem the current management would very much like to avoid."

I stared at the sigil. At the crown-shaped seal. Something cold was walking up my spine, vertebra by vertebra, and it was carrying a question I didn't want to let it finish asking.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the wards are dead, Rosalind. Whatever held the valley shut for sixty years broke last night, star by star, glass by glass. And because a dying man left you a box and a map drawn in starlight, and because you sold me a coronation memory eleven days ago that I'd bet my life wasn't a traveler's memory at all." He closed the ledger. "It was HIS. A piece of Dominic, loose in the world, moving through travelers' heads like a coin changing hands—and you fished it out of the current and sold it to me for thirteen coins."

* * *

I walked out of the shop. I want that on the record. I walked out, into the street, under a sky with no stars in it, and I stood there breathing like a man who'd been underwater.

Because here's what Vess hadn't said outright but had made perfectly clear anyway, the way he makes everything clear—by pricing it:

If the valley was a prison, then I was inventory.

Fifteen years of working the border road. Fifteen years of rules—never sell the same memory twice, never keep more than three, never remember your own past because you don't have one, never wonder why. I'd built a whole architecture of competence on top of a foundation I'd never once examined: that the valley simply *was*, the way weather is, the way hunger is. And the whole time, I'd been a component. A mechanism. The prison's own little enzyme, digesting the spillage, keeping the system tidy. Every traveler I'd robbed had been robbed twice—once by me, once by the design that made my robbery necessary.

And worse. Worse than worse. If the wards ate memories, and I had no memories of my own—

No. I wasn't going to think that thought standing in the street. I went back inside.

Vess hadn't moved. Of course he hadn't. He was pouring two cups of something dark from a pot on his little stove, and he handed me one without asking, which was its own kind of trap.

"Say the rest of it," I said.

"There isn't much rest. The ward-smith who died here—he talked, at the end. Delirious, maybe. He said the binding was supposed to be temporary. Thirteen turns of some calendar nobody uses anymore, and then the sigil either breaks or becomes permanent, depending on choices made by people who aren't alive yet." He shrugged. "He also said the king's memories wouldn't stay bound. That they'd leak. Scatter. Hide themselves in the world like seeds. And that someday someone would come through this valley collecting them without knowing what they were collecting."

The cup was warm in my hands. I looked at it instead of at him. "You think that's me."

"I think you're the only memory-thief in a prison full of amnesiacs, and a dying stranger gave you a treasure map, and the sky went out the same week you sold me a king's coronation." He drank. "I think the thirteenth turn is coming, and I think you're already standing in the middle of it, and I think—" he paused, and for the first time in fifteen years of knowing him, Vess looked genuinely uncomfortable, "—I think you should ask yourself why a man with no past survived the wards for fifteen years when everyone else's history dissolved inside a season."

The cold thing on my spine finished climbing and settled at the base of my skull.

"My past," I said slowly, "dissolved. Like everyone else's."

"Did it? Or was there nothing there to dissolve?" Vess set down his cup. "I've bought hundreds of memories from you, boy. Travelers' memories, yes. But sometimes—rarely, maybe once a year—you'd bring me something strange. A memory that felt old. Heavy. That resonated wrong, like a bell cast from bad metal. I never asked where those came from. You never knew. They were the ones I paid the most for." He met my eyes. "I've kept every one of them. Never resold a single one. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That you're a hoarder."

"That somewhere in my back room there's a shelf of memories that might be pieces of a king, and that you've been selling them to me for years without knowing it, and that the crown's courier offered me a fortune last night to buy them back—which means the crown knows the shards are moving, which means the clock Vess-the-ward-smith talked about is running." He stood. From beneath the counter he took a second bundle, smaller, wrapped in oilcloth, and set it beside the sigil. "The ledger. Every memory you ever sold me, catalogued. If you're going to go chasing a dead king across a starless kingdom, you'll want to know where the pieces have been."

"Why would you help me? You've never helped anyone in your life. You've *transactioned* at people."

Something moved across his face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Because thirty years ago I was on the wrong side of a war, and I've been paying interest on it ever since, and a man gets tired of being a ledger." He pushed the bundles toward me. "Take them. No charge."

From Vess, *no charge* was more frightening than any price. I took them anyway. My hands weren't quite steady, and he noticed, and pretended not to, which was the kindest thing he'd ever done for me.

At the door I stopped. "The coronation memory. The one I sold you. Can I—"

"It's yours," he said. "It always was. I suspect it was never anyone else's."

I walked home through streets where people were pretending it was a normal morning, because pretending was the valley's native language and even with the wards dead, we spoke it fluently. Behind me, the shop bell didn't ring right. Ahead of me, my rooms waited, and in my rooms there was a locked box, a map inked in starlight, and a black crow who had watched me all night with eyes like wet ink and who had, I now realized with a lurch, never once eaten anything I'd offered her.

On my windowsill, Quill tilted her head as I climbed through the window, and said, in a voice like a hinge that had learned to talk:

"Well? Did the fat man finally tell you whose memories you've been carrying around in your pockets?"

I froze with one leg still out the window.

"Whose," I said, "have I been carrying?"

Quill ruffled her feathers, and if a bird can look pitying, she managed it. "Sit down, thief. This is going to take longer than you have."
