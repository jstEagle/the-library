## 7. Quill Speaks Plainly

The stranger was three days dead, and the crow was still talking to him.

I want it noted that I did not scream. I made a sound—something dignified and brief, like a man discovering a spider in his boot—and then I stood very still in my kitchen doorway with a kettle of water I no longer intended to pour, because the crow on the corpse's chest had turned her head toward me with one bright eye and said, quite clearly:

"He told me you'd feed me."

* * *

Let me back up. Three days earlier, or maybe four—the valley makes liars of calendars—a bleeding man collapsed against my door with a locked box under his arm and a black crow riding his shoulder like a small feathered executioner. I'd dragged him inside because my hands moved before my survival instinct could lodge its usual objection. He'd lasted one night. In that night he'd managed to press the box into my arms, whisper "the thirteenth turn" with his last breath, and die looking at me with an expression I've been trying not to think about since. Recognition, I'd have called it. Which was absurd, because I didn't know him.

I thought I didn't know him.

The box sat on my table where I could watch it. The map inside was inked in something that glowed faintly, like starlight that had given up on the sky above us and decided to make its own arrangements. And for three days the box had done nothing, the map had done nothing, and the only sounds in my house were the ones I made myself—which is exactly how I like it.

Then this morning, while I was deciding whether the polite thing was burial or burning, the crow had opened her beak and spoken in a voice like a well-read woman with a cold.

So now we come back to the kettle, and the doorway, and my dignity hanging by a thread.

"You can talk," I said. Brilliant opening. Years of negotiating memory prices with Vess, and when actual magic spoke to me in my own kitchen, that's what I produced.

"I can talk," she agreed. She hopped off the dead man's chest—she'd been standing on it like a gargoyle with opinions—and picked her way across the table, avoiding the box with a delicacy that told me she knew exactly what it was. "I could talk before he found you. I simply judged the information distribution poorly timed."

"Information distribution," I repeated.

"I served a king, thief. We speak precisely." She fixed me with the eye again. Gold-ringed, sharp as a coin. "And you are going to want to sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"Sit down, Rosalind."

My name in a crow's mouth went through me like cold water through cloth. I hadn't told her my name. The dead man hadn't known it—I never gave travelers anything but a false smile and lighter pockets. I sat down anyway, mostly because my knees made the decision unilaterally.

"How do you know my name?"

She fluffed her feathers, settling onto the table's corner like a magistrate taking her bench. "Better question: how do you not know yours? But we'll get there. First—" she nodded at the corpse by the wall, and something in her voice went quieter, rougher around the edges, "—we should see to Aldric. He carried that box two hundred miles on a broken leg. He deserves better than my standing on him."

I buried Aldric behind my house in an hour of honest digging, which is more ceremony than the valley gives anyone. The crow—Quill, she said, when I asked—watched from the fence post and said nothing the whole time, which somehow felt like a eulogy.

When I came back inside with dirt under my nails and the particular emptiness that follows putting someone in the ground, Quill was waiting on the table beside the locked box.

"Now," she said, "the part where your life stops making sense."

"It stopped making sense the moment the stars went out."

"No. That was when it started being *honest*. There's a difference, though I grant you it doesn't feel like one at two in the morning." She hopped closer to me, head tilting that mechanical, unnerving tilt crows do, except hers carried intent. "Ask me what you're wondering. You've been chewing on it since I spoke."

A dozen questions fought each other in my throat. I picked the smallest one first, the way you test ice near the edge. "You served a king. What king?"

"Dominic."

The name landed in my chest like a stone dropped into a well I'd thought was full. I waited for the splash. It never came. Just depth, and dark, and the strange sense of the word falling a very long way before stopping somewhere below my ribs.

"That means nothing to me," I said.

"Mm." Quill studied me with terrible patience. "Say it again."

"Don't be absurd."

"Say it *again*, Rosalind."

"Dominic." My voice came out strange. Half mine, half borrowed. And underneath the word, so faint I might have invented it, something *shifted*—like a floorboard in a house you've lived in all your life suddenly giving slightly, telling you there's a room beneath.

Quill's feathers rose along her neck. "There it is," she breathed. "Gods' teeth, there it is. Do you know how long I've flown, listening for that resonance? Through nine towns and a war zone and one extremely undignified incident with a fishmonger's cart?"

"What resonance? What are you talking about?"

"I'm talking," she said, hopping up onto the back of a chair so she could look down at me, which I suspected was deliberate, "about smell. Crows aren't dogs, but we have our own nose for things. I can scent a piece of Dominic the way you'd scent woodsmoke. His memories were torn out of him when they bound him into the sigil—scattered across the kingdom like seed grain, hidden in chapels and tombs and lighthouses by people who loved him and hoped. Thirteen pieces. Thirteen turns of the moon until the binding goes permanent and everything he was belongs to the thing wearing his crown forever." She tapped the locked box once with her beak. *Tok.* "That box holds the key to finding them. Aldric died carrying it. And I—" her voice caught, actually caught, a hitch in all that precision, "—I have spent eleven years following the smoke."

"And I'm what?" I asked. "A cookfire? Some stray whiff you caught on the wind?"

Quill was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the valley did its valley noises—wind in the pines, the creek, the endless soft nothing of a place that eats remembering for breakfast. Inside, a dead man's crow prepared to ruin my life, and took her time about it.

"You're not a whiff," she said finally. "You're a bonfire. You're the strongest resonance I've felt since the binding itself. When you said his name just now, something under your skin answered." She leaned forward. "Rosalind. Your forgotten past isn't just tangled in Dominic's memories. I don't think you *have* a past separate from them. I think some piece of what they tore out of him didn't stay scattered. I think it grew legs and learned to steal."

* * *

Here's the problem with living in a valley that eats memories: you get very good at constructing yourself out of whatever's lying around.

My rules. I built them myself—or I believed I had—out of habits that felt older than my ability to verify. Never take a whole life; take fragments, sellable ones. Never follow a traveler home. Never let them see your face twice. Keep a ledger of what you've sold so you know what you're still allowed to miss.

But here's what I'd never said aloud, not even to myself in the dark hours: I didn't know where those rules came from. They were just *there*, load-bearing walls in a house with no foundation. I had skills with no apprenticeship. A broker's name memorized without ever being told it. An ache shaped exactly like a person I couldn't describe, grief with no funeral attached to it.

And now a bird was telling me the house wasn't mine at all.

I laughed. It came out wrong, too high, the laugh of a man on a ledge pretending it's a balcony. "You're saying I'm a piece of a king. That I'm—what, a walking fragment? A loose memory that learned to pick pockets?"

"I'm saying I don't know," Quill said, with a bluntness that was almost kind. "I know what you smell like. I know that box is going to tell us more, and I know the map inked in starlight doesn't glow for everyone—it glowed for Aldric because he carried king's blood in him, distant, diluted, but real. Ask it why it glows brighter when *you* hold it. Go on."

I didn't want to. I picked up the box anyway, because apparently my hands had decided to keep making decisions without consulting me, and turned it over. The starlight lines inside showed through the seams, faint as breath on glass. When my fingers wrapped the wood, they brightened. Not much. Enough.

I put it down like it had bitten me.

"This is nonsense," I said. "This is a con. I run cons; I know their architecture. You show up with a dead man, a magic box, and a story that flatters the mark—you're not a fragment of some forgotten king, you're the *chosen one*, special, destined—and before he knows his pockets are empty, he's crossing the kingdom doing your errands." I jabbed a finger at her. "Except I don't have pockets left, crow. So either you're the worst-designed con in history, or this is real, and I genuinely don't know which frightens me more."

"If I were conning you," Quill said mildly, "I'd have led with the chosen-one angle instead of burying it under fishmonger carts and precise vocabulary. Also, I'd have picked a mark with money." She preened one wing, unhurried. "But you're right to check the architecture. So let's inspect the foundation together. Tell me your earliest memory."

"I don't have one. Nobody here does. The wards—"

"Not the wards. The wards erase. But erasure leaves edges, thief. Even a page torn out leaves the stub of it glued to the spine." Her gold eye held steady on me. "What's the oldest thing you remember? The very first. Reach for it honestly."

I reached.

And I found—nothing. Not fog, not blankness, the way the others in the valley described their pasts, a smooth white absence like snowfall. What I found was a *shape*. An edge. Something had been there, and it had been removed cleanly, surgically, with care—and the removal was old. Older than the wards failing. Older, possibly, than me.

"The others are smudged," I heard myself say slowly. "Charcoal rubbed with a thumb. Mine's cut. Scissors."

"Yes," Quill said softly. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. The valley didn't take your memories, Rosalind. Someone took them long before you ever arrived here—and then the valley kept you safe from noticing. Like a prison keeping a prisoner from reading his own sentence." She ruffled, resettled. "You came to the border road already hollowed out. Everything since—your rules, your trade, your loneliness—that's furniture you built inside an empty room. Good furniture! Sturdy. I don't mean to insult the craftsmanship. But a room built to be empty is still a room built by someone, and I would very much like to know who."

The vertigo hit me then, properly, the way the plan promised it would. Not dizziness of the body—dizziness of the *floor*. Every fact I'd stood on developed a trapdoor. My competence: whose instincts were those, really? My rules: whose mercy, whose caution? The hunger—for a past, for a self, for something I'd been calling homesickness with no home attached—was that even mine? Or was it a king's homesickness wearing my ribs like a coat?

If my loneliness wasn't mine, who had I been grieving all these years?

"Stop," I said aloud, and realized Quill had been watching me spiral with the professional patience of someone who'd seen men unravel before. "Just—stop. Give me a minute where I'm a person and not a question."

"Take ten," she said. "I'll have a drink of your water and judge your housekeeping."

I stood at my window and looked out at the valley. It looked the same as always—green, gentle, forgetful, the border road winding off into hills that hid the world. I'd hated it quietly for years without knowing why. Now I knew why. It wasn't a home that had failed me. It was a cell that had succeeded.

And yet—and this was the part that scared me most—if everything I remembered of myself was constructed, if Rosalind was furniture in an emptied room, then what was left? Take away the rules, the trade, the wry commentary, the careful loneliness. What remained?

I looked at my hands. Thief's hands. Steady, scarred at the knuckles, good at lifting things from people without their noticing.

They didn't feel like a stranger's hands. That was the thing. Whatever Quill's theory was, whoever had hollowed me—these hands were *mine*, worn in, broken and healed on my watch. Maybe identity wasn't the room. Maybe it was what you did with your hands inside it.

Or maybe that was exactly the comforting lie a stolen fragment would tell itself.

"You've gone quiet in a productive way," Quill observed from the table. "Either you're accepting the truth or building a very elaborate denial. Both look similar from outside."

"Both feel similar from inside, too." I turned back to face her. "Suppose I believe you. Suppose I'm—tangled in this. A splinter of a bound king walking around in a stolen life. What does that make the shards you're hunting? If a piece of Dominic ended up as *me*—alive, walking, thinking—then what are the other thirteen? More people? Are there twelve other thieves and farmers out there who used to be a coronation and a war council?"

Quill went very still. Stillness, on a crow, is louder than speech.

"...I hadn't considered that," she admitted. "Eleven years, and I hadn't considered it. I've been treating them as objects to collect." Her voice dropped. "If any of the others woke the way you seem to be waking—"

"Then your mission has a body count you haven't counted."

"*My* mission." Her feathers flattened. "Careful, thief. The mission was Aldric's and mine and a dozen dead loyalists'. We've bled for it while you sold memories by the roadside. You don't get to inherit the guilt along with the glory."

"Guilt?" I laughed again, and this time it came out right, bitter and low. "Crow, I don't even know whose guilt fits me. That's rather the point."

She had the grace not to answer that immediately. Instead she hopped down to the locked box and stood beside it, small and black and certain, an exclamation point with wings.

"Here's what I know," she said. "Dominic was good. Not saint-good—stubborn-good, mule-good, the kind that gets kings killed, which is why someone wanted him gone badly enough to build a prison the size of a valley around his absence. And if a piece of him got loose and became a man, then that man spent years stealing memories from travelers—not to use them, not to sell more than he needed, but to *keep* them. To keep them from dissolving. Do you understand what you've actually been doing on that border road, Rosalind? Everyone else in this valley hoards for profit. You've been running a rescue operation for strangers' pasts and calling it theft because the truth was too big to carry."

My throat closed. I thought of the ledger. Hundreds of entries. Fragments I'd saved from the wards' teeth and passed to Vess, telling myself commerce, telling myself survival, telling myself anything except—

No. No, I wasn't going to let a bird rewrite my motives in one speech. People flatter marks; it's step one.

"Even if that's true," I said roughly, "even if every sentimental word lands—what do you want from me? The map. The box. Fine. I'll look at the map. But then what? Walk out of the only world I know, chasing thirteen pieces of a dead man who may be half of me, against a crown-thing that extinguished the stars? With what army? You, me, and a locked box?"

"With a crow, a thief, a map in starlight, and the truth," Quill said. "Which, historically, is how most impossible things get started. Armies come later, if you're lucky. Usually they come late."

I looked at the box. Starlight seeped through its seams like something patient, something waiting to be asked.

"One condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When this is done—whatever 'done' means—someone tells me who cut my memories out. Not who bound the king. Who gutted *me*, and why, and whether it was mercy or surgery or punishment." I held her gaze. "Because I'm not doing this for Dominic, crow. I barely know the man. I'm doing it because somebody made me a question mark, and I intend to find the hand that held the pen."

For a long moment Quill said nothing. Then she dipped her head, once, formal as a treaty.

"Agreed. And Rosalind—" something flickered behind the gold of her eye, old and tired and strangely tender, "—for what it's worth, I hope you hate the answer. The alternatives are worse."

"Cheerful."

"We served a king. Cheerfulness was never in the job description." She turned to the box and rapped it twice with her beak, sharp, deliberate. *Tok-tok.* "Now. Aldric sealed this to open for king's blood or king's echo. He worried, near the end, that it wouldn't accept me—too much bird, not enough blood." She glanced sideways up at me, and her voice went almost gentle. "It'll accept you. Open it."

I set my hands on the lid. The starlight under the seams pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm—and the lock clicked, soft as a knuckle cracking, before I'd even decided to try.

Inside, on velvet gone grey with years, lay a second map. Not the one Aldric had shown me. This one was older, folded and refolded a thousand times, and drawn across it in faded royal violet was a route marked with thirteen circles—and beside the first circle, in a cramped, urgent hand I had never seen before and recognized instantly, bone-deep, the way you recognize your own handwriting:

*Start where the water remembers the bell.*

Somewhere under my sternum, that floorboard gave again. Deeper this time. And far, far below it, something that had been sleeping in the dark of me rolled over and began, very quietly, to wake.

Quill watched my face and read it like a ledger.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better go find a drowned chapel."

"A what?"

"The bell, thief." She spread her wings, black against the grey light of a starless morning. "Chapels drown. Bells remember. And if my nose is right—and it has been right for eleven years—the first piece of the king you're carrying around in that hollowed-out chest of yours is waiting for us under the water."
