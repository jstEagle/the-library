## 8. Leaving the Valley

The dead border looked exactly like the living one had, which struck me as a design flaw. A thing that has held a man captive his whole existence ought to announce itself—ought to have spikes on it, or at least an unpleasant smell.

Instead there was just the road, and the milestone with its worn inscription, and the mist that sat along the valley's rim like a cat pretending it wasn't watching you pack.

I'd made my decision at dawn, which is when all bad decisions look their most reasonable. Quill had spent the night on my windowsill making small disapproving sounds, like a kettle deciding whether to boil.

"You could stay," she'd said, in her voice like a page turning in a dry room. "The wards are dead. The travelers will come through remembering everything now. You'll be rich."

"Rich," I said, "in memories of other people's lives. Which is what I already am. It's just that now I won't even have the excuse of scarcity."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's a diagnosis."

She'd hopped once, closer to the box. The stranger—the poor nameless man who'd bled out on my floor whispering about thirteenth turns—had left me two things besides grief: the locked box, warm as a sleeping bird no matter how long it sat in cold air, and a map inked in starlight that only showed itself after dark, drawn in lines of silver that seemed less like ink than like holes punched in the dark where stars used to be.

The map pointed out of the valley. Everything I needed, apparently, was out of the valley. My whole life had been spent learning the shape of things coming *in*—the traders, the pilgrims, the lost. Nobody ever told you how to leave.

I packed badly. I want that on the record. A man who has stolen from three hundred travelers should be able to pack a bag in under an hour, but I stood in my one room holding objects up to the light and asking each of them, *did I steal you? do you belong to someone else?* And worse: *do I belong to me?*

In the end I took the knife, the flint, the coil of good cord, the map (folded into darkness so it wouldn't show in daylight), the box (because it was warm, and because leaving it felt like leaving a door open behind me), and one item I'm ashamed of: a river-smooth stone I've had as long as I can remember, which is not very long, and which might have been mine or might have been lifted off some merchant's shelf during my first week of trade. I couldn't prove either way. That's the valley for you. Even your keepsakes come with a question mark.

Quill rode on my shoulder out the door. She weighed almost nothing. I kept being surprised by her absence of weight, the way you're surprised by kindness from strangers.

"The crow stays outside the walls," said the gate-warden, who was mostly ceremonial now, since there were no walls to speak of and nothing left to ward. Old Maren, gray as her own doorstep, squinting at Quill with the expression of a woman who has seen every animal in the valley and trusted none of them.

"She talks," Maren added, as if this settled something.

"So does the miller," I said, "and he's never once been right."

Maren looked at me a long moment. Her eyes were the pale blue of ice over deep water, and I realized she was memorizing my face, the way I used to memorize travelers' faces before lifting their memories. There is a particular hunger in that kind of looking. I recognized it and it frightened me more than anything else that morning.

"You'll forget us anyway," she said. "Whatever's out there. Whatever took the stars. People who leave don't come back as people."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

She stepped aside. She didn't say goodbye. Neither did I. Some doors you close gently so they can pretend they were never open.

* * *

The border road climbs for two hours through birch scrub that gets thinner and more apologetic the higher you go, until the trees give up entirely and there's just grass and wind and the milestone. I'd walked this far before, many times, always turning back at the same spot—not because anything stopped me, but because beyond the milestone the road simply *stopped mattering*. Not visibly. It didn't fade or dissolve. It just became uninteresting, the way a sentence becomes uninteresting when you can't remember its first word. I would stand there, feel a gentle fog roll through my intentions, and find myself walking home without having decided to.

Today the fog was gone. Today the road went on being a road, stubbornly, all the way to the crest, and past the crest I could see—

I sat down on the milestone.

Beyond the valley, the world fell away in folds of hills going blue with distance, and rivers stitching silver through them, and far off—so far it might have been imagination—a smudge of city smoke rising into a sky that had no stars in it at all. Not clouds. Just absence, a black velvet stretched edge to edge, and where stars should have been there was nothing, and the nothing was somehow louder than any sky I'd known. Our valley sky had been modest and gray and full of soft cloud. This sky was a wound.

"It's worse at night," Quill said quietly, from my shoulder. "Or better. Depends on what you need it to be."

"What happened to them? The stars?"

"The crown ate them." She preened once, a small violent motion. "Not all at once. One per turn. Twelve turns, twelve stars, and then—" she stopped.

"And then?"

"Then the thirteenth turn. Which doesn't get a star." She looked at me sideways, one eye round and black and old. "You should ask the box."

"The box doesn't talk."

"Not yet."

I decided not to pursue that. A man has to ration his terrors or they eat him by noon.

* * *

It started an hour past the milestone, when the sun was leaning west and my legs had found their traveling rhythm. First it was my mother's voice.

I should explain: I don't have a mother's voice. I have no memory of a mother. The valley saw to that, or something before the valley did; my earliest recollection is of standing in Vess's shop at perhaps ten years old, holding a stranger's memory of a wedding like a hot coal, knowing with total certainty that I knew how to do this and having no idea who taught me. So when a voice behind me on the road said, clear as a bell across water, *"Rosalind. Rosalind, love, come back now,"* in a voice pitched exactly the way a mother's voice would be pitched—warm, tired, patient with worry—I knew two things at once.

One: it wasn't real.

Two: whoever built the valley knew precisely what voice would work on me, which meant they knew things about me that I did not.

"Don't answer it," Quill murmured.

"I wasn't going to answer it."

"Your feet were."

My feet had, in fact, slowed. I looked down at them with betrayal. They resumed their business with exaggerated innocence.

The voices came in waves after that, spaced like a fisherman working his line. My mother again, further off, wounded now: *"He's forgotten us. Rosalind, you've forgotten us."* Then Vess, smooth as oiled leather: *"Thackeray. You're walking away from inventory. Think about that ledger. Think what you owe."* Then—this was the clever one, the one that nearly worked—a chorus of travelers whose memories I'd taken over the years, all speaking at once in a soft overlapping murmur: *thank you, thank you, thief, keeper, thank you,* as though the valley itself had distilled every transaction we'd ever made and found that I was the common term.

And underneath it, fainter than all the rest, something that wasn't a voice at all. More like a hum in the teeth. Like the sound a house makes when it settles—or when it's thinking about collapsing.

"They're pulling from your own head," Quill said. "They can't put anything in you that isn't already there. That's why it's your mother. That's why it's him." She nodded back down the road, toward the valley, toward the shop, though we'd walked too far to see either. "A prison doesn't need new tricks. It just needs your own ones, played back loud enough."

"Is that how it got you?" I asked. "Back when you helped make it. Did it use your voice on you too?"

Her feathers went flat and still, the way a person goes quiet when a question finds bone.

"Yes," she said.

We walked on.

* * *

By dusk the voices changed tactics, and this is the part I'll tell honestly, because a man who lies in his own chronicle is only lying to himself with extra steps.

They stopped calling me back and started letting go. That was worse.

*"Goodbye, Rosalind,"* said the mother-voice, and it sounded relieved, and that relief broke something in me I hadn't known was load-bearing. Because somewhere under all my rules and my competence and my wry interior narration, I think I had believed the valley loved me in its way—that the forgetting was a kind of mercy, that the fog on the road was a hand on my shoulder saying *not yet, not yet, stay where it's safe.* And here at the end, the hand was opening. The valley wasn't grieving. It was releasing a hold it had never pretended was anything but a grip.

"You understand what that means," Quill said. We'd made camp in the lee of a fallen wall, some old boundary marker from a kingdom that had presumably mattered once. The box sat between us, radiating its faint warmth. Overhead, the starless dark showed the map's silver lines bleeding through my pack, patient as arithmetic. "The voices stopping. It means the valley's done with you. Either it thinks you're harmless now—out of range, no threat—or—"

"Or it's saving its strength," I said. "For whatever comes next."

"Or that."

I fed the fire a little. Out here wood burned differently—quicker, brighter, as if the air itself were thinner and more eager. Everything out here was eager. The wind, the dark, the distance. The valley had been a held breath. The world was an exhale, and I was a small man standing in the middle of it trying to learn how to breathe.

"Do you hear it too?" I asked her. "The humming. In the teeth."

"All the time." She tucked her head under one wing, then thought better of it and looked at me instead, one black eye catching the fire. "It's the sigil. The binding. Every mile we walk toward the threshold, it'll hum louder. By the tenth turn it won't be a hum anymore. By the thirteenth—" She stopped herself, which was becoming her habit.

"You keep doing that."

"Some sentences shouldn't be finished," Quill said, "until the listener is stronger."

* * *

Near midnight I woke to the sound of breaking glass, very far away, and sat up with my heart going like a trapped bird.

Quill was already awake. "Not glass," she said. "Echoes of it. The wards are dying properly now, all the way down to the roots. What you heard was the last of them letting go of the people inside."

"The people inside. They'll start remembering."

"Everything. All at once. Every traveler who ever passed through and lost something, everyone born in the valley who never had anything to lose—" She paused. "It will be very loud in there tomorrow. Grief usually is."

I thought of Maren on her gray doorstep, memorizing my face. I thought of her tomorrow, waking with forty years arriving in her skull like a flood through a floorboard. Whose face would she be remembering? Her daughter's, maybe, grown and gone and forgotten decades ago. Or nothing at all, if she'd been born there—if her whole remembered life was about to begin.

"We should go back," I said, and heard myself say it, and hated how easy it was.

Quill said nothing. She let the silence sit there between us, fat and patient, until it grew heavy enough that I had to pick it up and examine it.

"No," I said. "No. Because going back doesn't fix it. Going back means standing in the middle of a drowning town with a bucket. And the box stays warm whether I'm there or not, and the map stays inked whether I read it or not, and the thirteenth turn comes whether anyone's counting or not. The kindest thing I can do for that valley is finish what that dying man started." I looked at my hands in the firelight. Thief's hands. Keeper's hands. Whosever hands. "But write it down, Quill. Write down that I wanted to go back. When you tell this story—and I know you will, crows gossip worse than brokers—write down that the wanting never stopped. I just outran it."

"Every day?"

"Every day."

She dipped her head once, formal as a treaty. "Then burn the bridge," she said. "Properly. Not the road. The bridge is the part of you that still listens."

So I did the small mad thing. I took the river-stone out of my pocket—the one I couldn't prove was mine—and I set it on the flat top of the milestone we'd camped beside, and I spoke to the valley for the last time, out loud, like a fool, like a son, like a debtor settling accounts.

"If any of you was ever real," I said, "keep this. It's the only thing I own that might have been yours."

Then I walked away from it and did not look back, which cost exactly as much as it sounds like it cost, and I am not going to describe the price. Some ledgers stay closed.

* * *

Dawn came gray and starless over hills that had no reason to exist and did anyway. The humming in my teeth had changed key overnight—lower, slower, almost musical now, almost a melody trying to remember itself.

And the box had moved.

Three feet, maybe four, from where I'd set it. Toward the east. Toward the road. There were no tracks around it except Quill's little three-toed prints, and she swore—swearing being a thing she did with tremendous dignity for a bird—that she hadn't touched it.

"It's warm," she said, "and it's awake, and it knows which way we're going. Which suggests it knows where we're going. Which suggests—"

"Don't finish the sentence."

"—that somebody in there is counting the days too."

I picked up the box. Through the lock, faint as a pulse under a wrist, I felt it answer the hum in my teeth—two rhythms, slowly finding each other, like strangers discovering they know the same song.

Somewhere ahead, nineteen days away or thirteen or however the counting truly ran, a ruined threshold waited under a moon that had been spending itself coin by coin. Somewhere behind, a valley full of newly remembering people woke to grief and gifts in equal measure. And somewhere in my own skull, in a room I couldn't find, a fragment of a king I'd never met was waiting to find out whether I was his thief, his keeper, or his ghost.

"East," I said.

Quill lifted off my shoulder, circled once against the empty sky—an arrow pointing nowhere and everywhere—and flew.

I followed the bird and the warmth and the hum, and I did not look back again. Mostly.
