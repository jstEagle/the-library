## 9. The Road of Unlit Lanterns

Leaving the valley, it turned out, was the easy part. The hard part was everything that came after, which is the way of most things.

The border road unspooled eastward through country I had never seen and could not have named if you'd paid me—which, historically speaking, was my whole profession. Without stars overhead, the land had a strange flat quality to it, like a stage set someone had forgotten to light properly. Hills were just darker smudges against the darkness. Trees made shapes that could have been anything: hands, gallows, old women bent over washing. Quill rode on my shoulder and offered commentary on all of them.

"That one's a tree," she said, for the fourth time in an hour.

"I know it's a tree."

"You said the last one was a tree. It was a scarecrow."

"It was also a tree."

"It was wearing trousers, Rosalind."

I didn't answer, because she was right, and because my feet hurt, and because three days out from the valley I had still not entirely convinced myself that the world beyond its wards wasn't going to simply fold up and vanish like a bad dream at dawn. Every mile we walked, I kept waiting for the sky to remember how to be a sky. It never did. Just black, seamless black, from horizon to horizon, as though someone had taken the night and shaken out all its pockets.

The box rode in my satchel against my spine, where I could feel it with every step—a faint warmth, a faint hum, like a sleeping animal. Thirteen turns, the stranger had said, dying on my floor with his blood drying into the floorboards. Whatever that meant, the box knew. Sometimes at night I'd catch it ticking softly to itself, patient as a clock in an empty house.

* * *

We found the first village on the fifth day, or rather we found what was left of one.

It sat in a river valley, maybe forty houses clustered around a well and a shrine, and every single lantern in it was dark. Not broken—dark. They hung from hooks by doorways and along the well's rim, dozens of them, glass intact, wicks trimmed, and not one lit. In their place, at the village center, someone had raised a pole, and on the pole hung a banner of white cloth with nothing on it at all. Blank. And the villagers had knelt before this nothingness so often that the grass beneath it had died in the shape of their knees.

"Cozy," Quill muttered.

I pulled my hood up and did what I do best: I walked in like I belonged there. This is harder than it sounds when you've spent your life in a valley that erases faces. Out here, people remembered you whether you liked it or not, and being remembered is a kind of exposure.

A woman was drawing water at the well. I asked her—casual, road-worn, harmless—for bread, offering a coin I'd lifted off a corpse back near the border, which felt appropriately circular.

She looked at me the way you'd look at a man who'd asked to borrow your knife to cut something invisible. "You're from outside," she said.

"I'm from lots of places."

"The Regent says there's no outside anymore." She hefted her bucket. "There's only what He allows."

"He," I said. "And who's that?"

The woman went still in a way that frightened me more than any weapon could have. She set down the bucket, turned to face the blank banner, and bowed her head. Then, without looking at me again, she said, quietly, quickly, like a prayer spat sideways: "Don't say *that* about Him. Don't ever say His name wrong. There are men who count the ways people say it wrong." She picked the bucket back up. "Bread's at the temple house. You'll kneel first. Everyone kneels."

"What happens if I don't?"

"Then the lanterns come for you." She glanced at the dark lanterns hanging everywhere, glass eyes catching no light at all. "They only light when they're hungry."

I got my bread. I even knelt—I'm not proud, but I'm alive—and while my knee pressed into the cold flagstone before that empty banner, I felt something I hadn't expected: a faint answering pulse from the box in my satchel. A slow, sick recognition, like a dog hearing its owner's whistle from across a crowded market.

*He* was real. The thing the stranger had died carrying word about. The thing that wore a crown somewhere east of us. And whatever was locked in my satchel knew Him the way a scar knows the blade.

Quill waited for me at the village edge, hopping from foot to foot with agitation. "They took the lights down," she said. "Did you see? A whole village, afraid of fire. Afraid of *seeing*. That's not worship, Rosalind. That's what fear looks like after it's been living somewhere long enough to start decorating."

"Come on," I said. "Before the lanterns get hungry."

* * *

Three more villages, same story. Blank banners, dead lanterns, kneeling people who spoke of the Regent in the hushed, flattened voices of the abused. In one town, a crier stood in the square reciting the Regent's edicts to an audience of nobody, and the edicts were things like *Gratitude is safety* and *The night is a gift; do not ask for stars*, and everyone who passed nodded along with the automatic rhythm of people who have learned that nodding is cheaper than thinking.

By the ninth day I'd learned the shape of the kingdom, and the shape was this: a fist, closed around everything.

That was the night I met Tam.

* * *

It was raining—not water, but something finer, colder, falling out of that starless black like sifted flour—and I'd taken shelter under the leaning eave of a wayhouse that had been burned and rebuilt and burned again, judging by the timber. I had a small fire going behind the ruin, out of sight of the road, and I was eating the last of my stolen bread and rehearsing arguments with Vess in my head, which is a habit of mine and a waste of good rehearsal, since I always win them in my head and lose them in person.

Quill stiffened on my knee. "*Someone*," she breathed.

I heard it a heartbeat later: running footsteps, uneven, exhausted, coming up the road through the rain. I put out the fire with my boot and slid into shadow, hand on the knife. Old rules. New country, same rules.

The runner burst through the weeds into the ruin, stumbled over a fallen beam, and landed practically in my lap—a girl, fourteen or fifteen maybe, thin as a fence rail, hair cropped short and crooked with a knife, which told me she'd cut it herself. She had a leather satchel strapped across her chest the way soldiers carry ammunition, and when she saw me she scrambled back and produced from her sleeve—

I want to be accurate here. From her sleeve she produced what appeared to be a copper wire twisted around a chicken bone.

"Don't come closer," she said. "I've warded this whole ruin. One step and it takes your fingers off."

I looked at the ruin. I looked at the bone. "That's a chicken bone."

"It's *keyed*," she snapped, and then her legs gave out, which rather undermined the threat, and she sat down hard in the wet ash and started shaking. Not crying—shaking, the deep mechanical shudder of someone who has been running for a long time past the point where running made sense.

Quill hopped over and regarded her with one bright eye. "Her satchel's humming," the crow reported. "Same note as your box. Different song, same key."

That interested me. I crouched down, keeping a careful arm's length, and pushed back my hood. "Warded ruins, keyed chicken bones, and a bag that sings. You're a long way from home, whoever you are."

"I don't have a home." She wiped her face with a grimy sleeve, leaving a streak that somehow made her look more defiant, not less. "That's the point of running away."

"And yet most runaways manage to run *toward* something."

"Fine." Her chin came up. "Toward nowhere in particular, then. Are you going to rob me? Everyone robs me. Two men tried yesterday. The ward took the first one's fingers. The second one ran."

"The second one sounds cleverer than the first."

She blinked at me. Something in her face recalibrated—the look of a person encountering humor in a place where they'd stopped expecting to find any. "Are you making a joke? While deciding whether to rob me?"

"I find jokes improve my decision-making."

"Well—" She faltered, then rallied. "Well, make them faster, because I can't sit here. If the lantern-men catch my trail—"

"Lantern-men," I said carefully.

Her whole body changed. Fear does that to some people—makes them smaller. With her it made her sharper, like a blade drawn half out of its sheath. "You haven't seen them?" She scanned the dark road behind us, the rain-sifted black. "White cloaks. They carry lanterns that burn with no flame—you can't warm your hands on it, it doesn't behave right, it goes toward lies. They walk the roads checking that villages are kneeling properly. And they collect—" She stopped herself. Looked at my satchel. Looked at Quill. Looked back at me, and I watched her arrive at a decision with visible effort, the way you watch someone jump a gap too wide for them.

"They collect ward-breakers," she finished quietly. "Runaways like me. Because I know things."

"What kinds of things?"

"The kind that got my master killed." She said it flatly, the way you say things you've said to yourself so many times the words have worn smooth. Then she held out her hand—the chicken bone still clutched in it, absurdly, like a knight extending a sword hilt-first. "Tam," she said. "Apprentice ward-smith. Formerly. Currently unemployed and extremely dangerous, as established."

I shook her hand. It was freezing. "Rosalind," I said. "Thief. Currently employed."

"Employed by who?"

I hesitated. Three weeks ago I would have lied—it was reflex, breath itself. But the box was humming against my spine, and this shaking, stubborn child had a satchel singing the same note, and I was tired, bone-tired, of being the only person in any room who didn't know the story he was inside.

"By a dead man," I said. "Mostly. Come by the fire. It's out now, but we'll fix that, and I'll tell you about him."

* * *

She ate like a wolf and talked like a dam breaking.

Her master had been a ward-smith of the old school, before the Regent's men licensed the trade into obedience. He'd taught her the grammar of wards—how binding-work was built on turns and thresholds, how certain rites only functioned at specific alignments, how a sigil, once closed, needed thirteen turns to become permanent. She said that last part casually, over a crust of bread, and I nearly dropped mine.

"Thirteen turns," I repeated.

"Mm. Any working left incomplete at the thirteenth turn unravels. That's why the old smiths counted everything in thirteens." She frowned at me. "Why?"

"Why did your master die?"

The frown deepened, traveled inward. "He forged something," she said slowly. "Years ago. Before I was born, maybe. He never said what. But when the stars started going out, he got strange—locked himself in the forge, burned papers, drank. And one night he packed me off to my aunt's with a message and half a ledger page, and by the time I understood the message was wrong, deliberately wrong, sent to keep me away—his forge was ash and there were white cloaks asking questions in the village." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had curled into fists around the bread. "So I went back for his notes. What was left of them. And I've been running since."

"The ledger page. Do you have it?"

She stared at me. "I just told you my master was murdered and you want the *paper*?"

"Yes. Because paper can be read twice, and grief can't be undone either way, and I've spent nine days walking through a kingdom where nobody asks questions, and you're the first person who might know answers. So yes. The paper. Please."

Something shifted in her expression—not trust exactly, but the beginning of its scaffolding. She dug into her satchel, past instruments I couldn't identify, and brought out a scrap of heavy vellum, scorched at one corner, covered in her master's cramped hand. Numbers. Dates. Turns.

And in the margin, circled twice, a phrase that stopped my breath:

*If the vessel fails, the sigil holds. If the vessel wills it open—*

The rest was burned away.

"Vessel," I whispered. My hand drifted, without my permission, to the satchel, to the warm ticking weight against my spine. Beside me, Quill had gone very still, feathers tight to her body, and I remembered what she'd told me on the road out of the valley: *Your own forgotten past is tangled in them.* In *them*. In the memories. In the king.

In me.

Tam was watching my face. "You know what this is about," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know a piece of it," I said. "Maybe a thirteenth of it." And I opened the satchel, and let her see the box, and told her about the stranger, and the map inked in starlight, and a king bound into a sigil by the thing that wore his crown.

She listened the way I imagine her master used to listen to wards being tested—totally, hungrily, every failure noted. When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Rain sifted down through the ruined roof. Somewhere far off, impossibly, a lantern flickered on the dark road, burning its flameless lie of a light.

"My master's notes are in a cache two days east," she said finally. "Half a ledger, ghost-notes, the flaw-diagrams for a binding sigil he helped design." Her chin lifted, and there it was—the spark, the first coal of something that would take years to become a family but that I recognized even then, the way you recognize weather coming. "If we're doing this, thief, we do it properly. Wards, flaws, turns. All of it."

"*We*," I said.

"You fed me. You didn't rob me. You laughed at the chicken bone instead of asking why it wasn't a knife." She shrugged, elaborately casual, fooling neither of us. "I've decided you're the least terrible option available."

"High praise."

"I'm saving the rest." She held out her hand again, and this time the chicken bone was gone, and her grip was warm. "Two days east. Try to keep up."

Quill launched off my shoulder, wheeled once through the rain, and settled on Tam's other side like a bird taking possession of new territory. "Oh good," she said. "Now there are two of them."
