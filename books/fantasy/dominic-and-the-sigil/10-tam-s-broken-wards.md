## 10. Tam's Broken Wards

I learned to lie before I learned to ward. My master said the two crafts were cousins—that a good ward was just a truth told so firmly that nothing else could get in, and a good lie was a ward built around your own heart. He said it while mending a hairline crack in a threshold-stone, his spectacles pushed up into his grey-streaked hair, his hands steady in the way of a man who had never once doubted what they were for.

His name was Aldous Wren. Remember that name, because I have spent every night since he vanished trying to forget it, and failing, and being glad of my failure.

* * *

We were camped in the lee of a collapsed granary three days out of the valley, and Rosalind was doing that thing he did—sitting with his back to the fire so the light wouldn't reach his face, turning the locked box over and over like a man worrying a tooth. Quill perched on his knee, preening with the aggressive serenity of a bird who knows she's the smartest creature present and is being gracious about it.

"You're going to wear a hole in it," I said.

"It's already got a hole in it," Rosalind said. "That's rather the point of boxes."

"The point of boxes," Quill said, "is that they keep things *in*. Which this one is failing at, incidentally. Has been since the border. You'd know if you had a nose."

"I don't want to know what's leaking out of it," Rosalind said, which was a lie, because everyone wanted to know, including me, especially me.

Here is what I hadn't told them yet: I knew what leaked out of boxes like that one. I had grown up around boxes like that one. My master's workshop had shelves of them—ward-sealed, sigil-locked, humming faintly with the particular note of containment magic, a sound like a struck bell that never quite finishes ringing. As an apprentice I used to fall asleep to that hum. It was the sound of safety. It was the sound of things staying where you put them.

It was also, I understood now, the sound of a king screaming into a box for longer than I had been alive.

* * *

Let me tell you about the night my master left, because it matters, and because I have only told this story once before, to a hearth full of strangers who didn't believe me, and I would like to tell it better this time.

Aldous Wren was not a village hedge-witch or a traveling tinker. He was the finest ward-smith in the kingdom, possibly in the history of the kingdom, and he kept his forge in the deep cellar of a house in Marrowgate that looked, from the street, like the home of a moderately successful coffin-maker. This was deliberate. Coffin-makers are left alone. Ward-smiths who build prisons for kings are not.

Yes. That. I'm going to say it plainly now, because I've spent three days saying everything except that, and Rosalind deserves plain speech even if he never gives it back.

My master forged the binding sigil.

Not a copy. Not a repair. The original—the one that holds Dominic, the true king, inside a shape no bigger than a coin, worn on the breast of the thing that stole his crown. I grew up eating bread bought with the wages of that work. I learned my letters from the drafts of it. There was a page pinned above my cot for two years—a practice exercise, a fragment of the binding geometry—and I fell asleep under it every night like other children fall asleep under pictures of ships.

He told me about it exactly once, the night before he vanished. And here is the part I need you to understand, the part that has been sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone ever since: he wasn't proud of it. He was *ashamed*. But shame, in a man like Aldous Wren, did not look like weeping. It looked like precision. He had spent twenty years making the binding perfect, and then twenty more making it imperfect, quietly, in ways no one would notice, because a perfect prison is unforgivable and a flawed one might someday be opened.

"He asked me to help him," I said to the fire, finally, because Rosalind had stopped pretending not to watch me and Quill had stopped pretending to sleep. "The night he left. He said the sigil had thirteen turns—thirteen points where the binding could be unmade—and that each turn needed something different. A vessel. A threshold. A willing hand." I pulled my knees up. "And then he said, 'Tam, girl, I built the lock, but I built it crooked on purpose, and now I'm too old to walk to the door.'"

"What door?" Rosalind asked.

"That's the thing. He never said. He drew me a map instead—" I stopped. My hands had gone cold, and not from the night air. "No. He started to draw me a map. And then the lamps went out. All of them, all at once, the way lamps do when a ward the size of a valley takes a breath. And when they came back he was gone, and the map was gone, and there was a scorch mark on the table in the exact shape of a hand."

Quill's head came up. "A hand," she repeated. "How large a hand?"

"Large," I said. "Larger than mine."

"Ah," said the crow, in the voice of someone confirming a suspicion she has carried for years and finding it heavier than expected.

* * *

Rosalind didn't say anything for a long time. He was good at silence, Rosalind—I noticed that early, in the way you notice weather. When he finally spoke, it wasn't the question I expected.

"Why didn't you tell us sooner?"

Because I'm a coward. Because telling makes it real. Because if I say *my master built the cage* out loud, in front of the crow who served the man in the cage, then I stop being a runaway apprentice having an adventure and become something much worse: the daughter of the crime itself.

What I actually said was: "Because I thought it didn't matter anymore. The sigil's done, sealed, worn by the Regent. What good is knowing how a lock works when the lock is a hundred leagues away and guarded?"

"Every good," Rosalind said. "Locks are how thieves think. You've been traveling with a thief for three days and you haven't noticed?"

"I've noticed," I said. "I've mostly noticed that you steal memories and then look sad about it."

"That's called depth," Quill said.

But Rosalind leaned forward, firelight catching the edge of his face, and there was something in his expression I hadn't seen before—not the broker's calculation, not the survivor's wariness. Hunger. The specific hunger of a man who has just learned his whole life was someone else's plan and wants, more than anything, a plan of his own.

"Thirteen turns," he said. "Say it again. All of it. Everything he taught you."

So I did. I talked until my voice went rough. I told him about the flaw Aldous built into the third turn—a misalignment no inspector could catch, a deliberate half-degree of error that meant the binding *wanted* to come apart there, the way a stitch wants to unravel if you find its end. I told him about the seventh turn, which required moonlight of a specific quality—"not full, not new, the moon counting, he said"—and how I'd assumed that was poetry until an astronomer's apprentice in Marrowgate told me the moon has a thirteenth phase the almanacs leave out, a turn of its own, and my master had laughed for a full minute when I reported it to him, laughed like a man whose worst joke had finally landed. I told him about the willing vessel, and watched Rosalind go carefully, deliberately blank, the way people do when a prophecy has just put its hand on their shoulder.

"And the flaws," he said. "You said he taught you its flaws. Plural."

"He taught me four," I said. "There are thirteen turns and he taught me four. He was going to teach me the rest and then—" the hand-shaped scorch mark, the dark, the empty chair. "Then he didn't."

"Four is more than anyone else alive has," Rosalind said. "Four is a fortune."

"Four is a confession," I said. "He wasn't teaching me to be clever, Rosalind. He was teaching me to *atone*. His, not mine. Except—" and here my voice did the thing I hated, cracked down the middle like bad glaze, "except it is mine too, isn't it? I mixed the alloys for the outer casing. I was nine. He said it was a lesson in tempering silver. I held the crucible tongs for the prison of a living man and I thought I was learning a trade."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the starless dark, something moved—wind, probably, or one of the crown's hounds, or just the world being the world—but nobody reached for a weapon, because we were all busy not looking at each other.

Quill hopped down off Rosalind's knee and walked across the dirt to me, which crows do not do lightly; a crow approaching you is either an insult or an honor and you generally can't tell which until afterward. She looked up at me with one flat black eye.

"My king trusted a smith," she said. "Trusted him enough to let him close with the sigil-work. Trusted him, and the binding still happened, and I have spent years deciding whether that trust was foolishness or whether the smith betrayed it." She tilted her head. "Tonight I heard his apprentice confess her sins to a campfire, and I find I have my answer. A betrayer does not spend twenty years building doors into his own lock. A betrayer does not leave behind a girl who mixes alloys at nine and hates herself for it at nineteen." She fluffed her feathers, briskly, as if settling the matter. "You are not the crime, Tam. You are the apology. They are different things, though they wear the same face."

"I don't feel like an apology," I said. "I feel like a weapon someone forgot to aim."

"Then we'll aim you," Rosalind said, simply, and something in my chest turned over—not warmth exactly, but the memory of warmth, the shape warmth leaves behind.

* * *

We didn't sleep much after that. Rosalind spread out Vess's ledger on a flat stone and I knelt over it with a stub of charcoal, doing what I should have done the first night, what guilt had been too heavy to let me do: reading the ledger not as a merchant's record but as a smith's.

Because that's the thing about wards and ledgers—they rhyme. Both are systems. Both have weak points. And Vess, whatever else he was, had recorded his purchases in a hand so precise it was practically a diagram, and the locations attached to certain entries—entries paid for at rates far above market, entries marked with a small symbol I recognized with a lurch because I had seen it a thousand times on my master's drafts—

"That symbol," I said, tapping the page. "That's a turn-marker. Thirteenth-turn notation. My master used it to flag points of structural weakness in the binding." I looked up at Rosalind. "Vess hasn't been buying memories. He's been buying *coordinates*. Someone sold him—or told him—where the turns sit. And these entries—" my finger moved down the column, "—these are clustered. Four clusters. Fourteen entries. One for each turn he knows about, plus spares."

"Or decoys," Rosalind said.

"Or decoys," I agreed. "Which means whoever wrote this ledger either has my master's notes or has my master's *hand*." I said it before I could stop myself, and then sat back hard, because the implication arrived all at once, the way weight arrives on a bridge that's been creaking for miles.

Aldous Wren hadn't just vanished. Aldous Wren had gone *somewhere*, and was possibly still writing, still noting, still flagging weaknesses in the thing he'd built—unless the crown had found him first and put his knowledge to use, in which case every cluster in that ledger was a trap laid with a father's handwriting.

"We follow the clusters anyway," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question.

"We follow the clusters anyway," I agreed, because what else was there? Go home? Home was a coffin-maker's front door over a forge that smelled of shame. The road ahead at least pointed somewhere.

Quill flew up to the broken granary wall and stood silhouetted against the lightless sky, and for a moment she looked less like a bird than a sentinel, a small black gargoyle against a darkness with no stars to relieve it.

"First cluster's nearest the drowned chapel country," she said. "Two days east, if the roads are honest. Roads rarely are."

* * *

Near dawn I took out the one thing I had saved from the workshop: a scrap of vellum with my master's last lesson on it, the fourth flaw, written in his cramped leftward-slanting hand. *The ninth turn requires a voice that remembers being free,* it read. *All else may be forced or forged. This cannot.*

I had read it a hundred times and understood it never. Sitting there in the grey false dawn with a thief, a crow, and a box that hummed its unfinished bell-note, I thought I finally began to.

Everything else about the unbinding could be stolen, tricked, fought for, bargained over. But somewhere in this starless kingdom there had to be a voice that remembered freedom—and it would have to give itself freely, or none of the rest of it mattered.

I folded the vellum away and looked east, toward the drowned chapels and the first cluster, and felt the stone in my chest shift at last—not gone, but moving, becoming something with edges I could grip.

Guilt, my master used to say, is just purpose that hasn't found its work yet.

Mine had just found plenty.
