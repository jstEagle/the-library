## 11. The Memory Broker's Ledger

Vess's ledger was bound in eel-skin, which I know because I once tried to sell a memory of an eel-fisher to him and he corrected me on the taxonomy before naming his price. The book smelled of tallow and iron filings. Its pages were written in a cipher that should have taken weeks to crack and instead took Tam one long night, a candle stub, and the offhand observation that Vess was "the kind of vain man who'd key a cipher to his own initials."

She was right. The man had encrypted his entire black-market enterprise with a rotating substitution built on *V-E-S-S-V*. I have stolen memories from war-heroes and widows, from a bishop and a bounty-hunter, and I have never encountered vanity so useful.

"Here," Tam said at dawn, tapping a column of entries near the back. "Same notation, over and over. A sigil mark, a number, and a word that isn't a word."

I looked. The non-word repeated eleven times down the page: *Havish*. Beneath it, in smaller script, coordinates rendered in the old surveyor's style — degrees, minutes, and a bearing taken from a star that no longer existed.

"Place name?" Tam asked.

"Or a person," I said. "With Vess, it's usually money. But this" — I ran my finger along the margin, where someone had drawn a small, precise compass rose in ink so fine it looked like it had been stitched — "this is cartographer's hand. Brokers don't draw. They count."

Quill, who had been sleeping with her head under her wing in a manner she maintained was dignified and everyone else called pathetic, opened one eye. "The compass rose has sixteen points," she croaked. "Modern roses have eight or thirty-two. Sixteen went out of fashion before I hatched, and I am old enough to remember when crows were respected."

"You're not old enough to remember anything respectable," Tam said.

"I remember your master's handwriting," Quill said mildly, and Tam shut up, because that wound was still open between us — the apprentice of the man who forged the binding, traveling with the thief trying to break it, each of us carrying our guilt like contraband we couldn't fence.

We left the ruined exchange-house before full light. Vess himself was three days gone into the countryside with a satchel of my sold memories and, presumably, instructions from whatever fed at the other end of him; I did not let myself think about that more than necessary, because thinking about it made my hands shake, and shaking hands are bad for lockwork. The ledger came with us. Stealing from a broker felt less like theft and more like picking up something I'd dropped.

* * *

*Havish*, it turned out, was not on any map.

This surprised me less than it should have. I had spent my whole life in a valley designed to be forgotten, guarded by wards that ate the names of anyone who lingered. Of course there were other places unlisted. Of course the kingdom under its starless sky was riddled with holes — little pockets of erasure where people like me had been shelved. The valley wasn't unique. It had just been the biggest, and the best-fed.

We found Havish by walking away from everything. Three days north of the border-road, past villages whose lanterns burned but whose windows stayed shuttered — the crown-thing's face was stamped on coins now, and on the cheap devotional prints hawked at every crossroads, and the villagers bought them the way you buy protection from a bully, quickly and without meeting anyone's eye — past a forest where the trees had grown in perfect, unnatural rows, as if planted by someone with a grudge against wilderness.

It was Quill who spotted it. She'd been flying scout above us, which she did in short, aggrieved loops, complaining constantly about the wind. Near dusk on the third day she dropped out of the grey air and landed on Tam's pack with a thump.

"There's a house," she said, "that is arguing with the hill."

"A house that's what?"

"Arguing. The hill wants it buried. The house declines. There's wardwork on it, old work, the kind that hums if you put your ear close. And there's smoke from the chimney that goes *up* properly, which around here counts as a miracle."

We crested the hill as the last light failed — though with no stars, *last light* had become a technicality; the sky simply stopped pretending — and saw it below us in a fold of the land: a long, low building that might have been a coaching inn once, its stone walls patched with newer stone of mismatched shades, its windows glowing amber. Around it, faintly visible in the dark, stood a ring of standing posts carved with characters I didn't recognize, and the air inside the ring tasted different. Cleaner. Like the difference between held breath and released.

"That's a ward-ring," Tam said quietly. Her voice had changed. It always did around wardwork — some mixture of reverence and grief. "A real one. Not containment work. Sanctuary work. Whoever raised it wanted to keep things *out*, not keep people in." She paused. "It's beautiful."

"It's also going to notice us before we knock," I said.

"Then we knock loudly and look harmless."

Tam could manage harmless. She was nineteen, freckled, and carried her tools in a way that read as *tinker*. Quill could manage harmless by pretending to be an ordinary crow, which lasted four seconds before she said anything. I had spent years cultivating harmless at the valley border, but the valley had fallen, and my face, apparently, had changed with it. Strangers looked at me differently now. Like I was a door left open somewhere.

The ward-ring hummed as we crossed it. Not an alarm — a question. I felt it pass through me like cold water, reading, weighing. Tam whispered something with her fingers spread, an appeasement-gesture I hadn't taught her, and the hum settled into something almost companionable.

The door opened before we reached it.

* * *

The woman in the doorway was old in the way mountains are old — not frail, just finished arguing with time. Her hair was white and cut short as a soldier's. Her eyes were pale grey, the color of a map's empty margins, and they moved over Tam, over me, over Quill perched on my shoulder, cataloguing us with the reflexive precision of someone who had spent her life deciding what went where.

"You're following Vess's ledger," she said. Not a question.

"We're following answers," I said. "The ledger was the only trail."

"Vess sells to both sides of any war," she said. "Remember that, boy, however far you've come on his paper." She stepped back from the doorway. "You'd better come in. The crow too. Especially the crow."

Quill stiffened on my shoulder. "Do I know you?" she demanded.

"You knew my maps," the woman said. "Every royal courier bird in the eastern reach learned my coastline symbols. I'm Odile. I drew the kingdom you were born in, thief, back when it had stars. Come in before the dark does."

Inside, Havish was warm and crowded and impossible. The coaching inn's great room had been gutted and rebuilt into something between a workshop and a refugee kitchen: long tables buried under instruments, rolls of vellum, jars of ink in a dozen colors; a hearth big enough to roast a lamb whole, with a pot of something lentil-based doing its patient work; bedrolls stacked along one wall like cordwood. And people — perhaps thirty of them, eating, mending, arguing softly over a game of stones near the fire. A one-eyed woman teaching two children their letters. An old man with ward-scarred hands — I recognized the burns; the valley's border-smiths had worn identical ones — mending a fishing net he would never need. A young person with cropped hair and a broker's tally-scar on their forearm, who looked up at me with instant, correct suspicion.

Exiles. Every one of them. I knew it the way you know your own handshake.

"How?" was all I managed.

"The wards fell," Odile said, hanging my coat — she had taken my coat, and I had let her, and I noted that about myself — on a peg by the door. "All of them. Not just your valley's. There were pockets like yours scattered across the reach, places the crown's magicians buried inconvenient things or inconvenient people. When the stars went out, the bindings failed. Whatever anchored them died." She turned and looked at me with those margin-grey eyes. "Something is happening, boy. Something large. And these people walked out of their prisons into a kingdom gone dark, with nowhere to go, until they followed rumors to an old traitor's door."

"You're not a traitor," Tam said.

Odile smiled without warmth. "I refused to falsify the royal survey charts after the Regent's ascension. I redrew the borders to match what the crown claimed rather than what the land actually contained. When I objected — publicly, loudly, and in front of the Guild — I was stripped of rank, struck from the rolls, and exiled." She shrugged. "So yes. Traitor, disgraced cartographer, and now innkeeper for the forgotten. Titles accumulate."

"Why does everyone who helps me have a terrible history?" I asked, mostly to myself.

"Because only broken people know where the doors are," Odile said. "Sit. Eat. You look like three days of bad road, which by your dust you are." Then her gaze sharpened. "But before you eat — show me the rest of what you took from Vess. The ledger alone wouldn't bring you here. You saw the entries marked *Havish*."

I hesitated. Old habit. In the valley, information was currency and spending it carelessly got you robbed. But Quill pressed her claw meaningfully into my shoulder, and Tam was already looking at me with that expression — disappointed, patient, twenty years older than she was — and I thought: *this is why you have no family, Isandro. You count coins at the wrong moments.*

I set the box on her table.

Not the stranger's locked box — that stayed against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, its weight as familiar now as my own pulse. This was my working kit: picks, quills of anesthetic ash, and the small lead-lined case where I kept the memories too strange to sell. Including the coronation fragment I'd lifted off a traveler in the valley's last week, the one I'd sold Vess a copy of, the one that had felt less like merchandise and more like a message.

Odile opened the case with the confidence of someone who had decided it was hers. She held the memory-shard up to lamplight. Inside the crystal, tiny as an insect in amber, a figure in a crown turned its head — and even at that scale, even blurred, I saw Tam go white and Quill spread her wings half-mast, the crow's version of removing a hat.

"That's him," Tam whispered. "That's Dominic."

"Yes," Odile said softly. "I charted his progress through the northern provinces when he was crown-prince. Drew his route myself. He waved at my survey-balloon once, the ridiculous boy." Her thumb moved across the shard's surface, and her voice, for one sentence, lost its fortress-wall quality. "I wondered for years what happened to him. Now I'm drinking soup in his tomb. The gods have a sense of humor and it is entirely structural."

Around the room, the noise had died. People had drifted toward the table — the one-eyed woman, the ward-scarred smith, the suspicious broker's-clerk — drawn by the shard the way moths attend flame. Thirty exiles, looking at the face of the king whose fall had, in ways none of us yet understood, made all of us.

"He's scattered," I said. "Bound into a sigil the Regent wears, broken into pieces, hidden across the kingdom. We've recovered one shard from a drowned chapel site — we think. We have a dead stranger's map inked in starlight, a talking crow with a criminal record, a runaway ward-smith, and a broker's ledger pointing here. That's the entire inventory."

"It's more than most people start rebellions with," Odile said. "And less than we'll need." She set the shard down gently, as if it might bruise. "You came to Havish because Vess's ledger shows eleven payments from this house. Let me save you the burglary: I buy memories, thief. Fragments. From exiles who pass through and carry scraps of the old kingdom — the true kingdom, pre-usurpation. Songs, faces, borders, names. I'm not fencing them." She pulled a drawer, and out of it came a map.

I have seen beautiful maps. I grew up beside a broker who kept a framed survey of the valley like pornography. But I had never seen a map like this.

It covered the whole table. It was the kingdom — but charted in negative. Where stars should be, Odile had marked empty circles in silver ink, hundreds of them, each annotated in her fine stitching-hand with dates and bearings. Overlaid on the darkness was a web of gold lines connecting certain stars to certain places: a chapel, a tomb, a tower, a lighthouse. And in the corners of the map, in red, small as secrets, were drawings. A crown. A sigil. A face.

"What is it?" Tam breathed.

"A map of missing things," Odile said. "I have spent the year since the sky went dark recording every star that vanished, where it hung, when it fell, and what was buried nearby when it did. Because I am old, and stubborn, and I noticed something the Guild's astronomers were too proud to see." Her finger traced a gold line. "The stars aren't dying. They're being *collected*. Pulled down and stored. Each one anchored to a place, and each place guarding something." She looked up at me. "Your drowned chapel sits beneath the star called the Lantern. Your dead stranger's map was drawn in starlight — which means whoever made it could still touch them. And the pattern, boy — the pattern is a constellation being disassembled piece by piece, and the pieces are being used as locks."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind me, the one-eyed woman made a sound like a swallowed prayer.

"So the shards of the king," Tam said slowly, working it aloud, "are locked under the same stars the Regent stole. He didn't just bind Dominic. He *imprisoned the sky* to do it."

"And when the binding needed renewing," Odile said, "when the thirteenth—" She stopped herself. Looked at me. "How much do you know about turns?"

"Enough to hate the number thirteen," I said.

"Then you know more than my cook." She rolled the map closed with the tenderness of a surgeon sheathing a blade. "Stay tonight. Eat. Sleep — genuinely sleep, you have the eyes of a man who dreams in inventory. Tomorrow I'll show you the full chart, and the girl can examine my ward-posts, and the crow—" 

"I have a name," Quill said.

"—the *crow* can tell me whether her memory of the old coastline is accurate, because if it is, I have been drawing the Salt Reach wrong for forty years and I will need to sit down." Odile paused at the edge of the firelight, and for a moment the innkeeper fell away and I saw the court cartographer, the woman who had told the truth about borders to a usurper's face. "One thing more, thief. Vess's ledger lists what he paid me. It does not list what I asked him to find."

The room went very quiet.

"I asked him to watch for a specific kind of traveler," Odile said. "Someone carrying fragments that resonated wrong — memories that rang like the king's own. He found nothing for eleven months." Her grey eyes fixed on mine, and I felt read, charted, placed on her grid like a feature of the landscape. "And then, three weeks ago, he wrote to say he'd found a memory-broker's boy from the valley selling a coronation fragment that sang."

Tam looked at me. Quill's claws tightened on my shoulder.

"He's been selling your memories, Rosalind," Odile said, "to *me* — copies, at least — for months. Did you truly think a man as careful as Vess let a prize like you walk out of his shop?"

From inside my coat, against my ribs, the dead stranger's locked box — silent through three days of road, through ward-crossings and river fords and one undignified scramble down a scree slope — gave a single, soft click. Like a clock. Or a countdown.

Odile looked at the box, and her face did something I had never seen a face do: hope and horror in the same expression, perfectly balanced.

"Well," she said. "Open it."
