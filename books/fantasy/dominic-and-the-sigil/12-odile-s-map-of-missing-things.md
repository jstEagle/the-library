## 12. Odile's Map of Missing Things

I have been drawing maps for fifty-three years, and I will tell you the truth that no cartographer admits at dinner parties: a map is a confession. Every line you draw says, *I was here, I saw this, I believed it mattered.* And every line you leave out says something worse.

The map on my table said everything I had spent thirty years trying to unsay.

It hung from the rafters of the safehouse like a funeral banner — a full chart of the kingdom, inked in my own hand back when my hand still belonged to the Crown, and then amended, year by year, in whatever I had: charcoal, berry-stain, once, in a fit of sentiment, my own blood. The original lines showed rivers and roads and the borders as they were. The amendments showed something else entirely. Small black circles. Hundreds of them. Each one a place where a star used to be.

I call it the Map of Missing Things, though I never say so aloud. A woman my age learns which of her obsessions are safe to name.

* * *

They arrived at dusk, the three of them, looking exactly as desperate as I'd hoped and more dangerous than I'd budgeted for. The ledger had promised me a thief with no past; it had not promised me the crow.

"Odile Vasseur," I said, before any of them could speak. "Formerly Royal Cartographer to the House of Aurel. Currently nobody. You'll want to hear both titles, because the second one is the honest one."

The thief — Rosalind, thin as a winter branch, with the particular watchfulness of a man who has counted every exit in a room before noticing the people in it — looked at the map above my head instead of at me. Good instinct. Most people look at the old woman first and the work second, and then they miss things.

"That's the valley," he said. His voice did something complicated around the word. "On your map. It's marked."

"It is."

"You've marked it with a circle."

"I mark everything that's missing," I said. "Stars. Rivers that were diverted. Names struck from registers. The valley went into my book eleven years ago, when the last survey team I sent there came back unable to tell me what they'd seen. One of them wept for six hours without knowing why." I let that sit. "You came out of it, I take it."

"Recently," he said.

"And you remember being in it?"

"Some of it. Enough."

"Enough," I repeated, and laughed, because if I didn't laugh I would have said something unforgivable about how much of myself is enough these days. "Sit down, all of you. There's soup. It's bad. That's not false modesty; I'm a cartographer, not a cook, and I've been feeding exiles the same bad soup for nine years, and none of them have died of it yet, which I consider a professional achievement."

* * *

Here is what I did, before I was nobody. I mapped the sky for the Crown. Not the stars themselves — the astronomers had those — but the *relationship* between stars and ground. Where starlight fell strongest, where ley-surveyors found their instruments singing, where the old shrines stood that predated the kingdom by a thousand years. My charts told the Crown where the heavens touched the earth most closely. They called it the Celestial Survey. They gave me a title, a stipend, and a seal, and I was proud of all three, which should have been my first warning. Pride is how institutions get their hooks into people who ought to know better.

Then the Regent took the throne, and the first thing his new court asked for was not more of my survey but less of it. They wanted the sections concerning the northern observatories destroyed. Then the sections concerning the moon's path. Then, quietly, through channels I wasn't meant to trace, they wanted *me* destroyed, or at least discredited, which for a woman whose only assets were her eyes and her hands amounted to the same thing.

I burned what they asked me to burn. Let no one tell you I was brave. I burned the charts because the alternative was the block, and I am not made of the stuff that chooses blocks. But I burned copies and kept originals, and I kept surveying after I fled, and over thirty years of exile I documented every place in this kingdom where a star used to be visible and now is not.

There are four hundred and twelve of them. I know because I have counted twice, and the second count was worse than the first, because the number had grown.

"They're going out in an order," Rosalind said, standing under the map with his neck craned, while the crow — Quill, she called herself, and I will get to her, because she deserves her own paragraph and possibly her own chapter — perched on his shoulder like a small judgmental god.

"Yes."

"The pattern isn't random."

"No," I said. "It's a spiral. Starting at the capital and winding outward. Which means whoever put them out started at the center and is working toward the edges, and the last places to go dark will be the far borders." I tapped the valley on the map with my walking stick. "Which means your valley was one of the first things they built and one of the last things they'll finish. Or rather—" I corrected myself, because precision matters even in hell— "*unfinish.*"

Tam, the ward-smith girl, had been quiet since they arrived in the way young people are quiet when they're carrying something heavy and don't yet trust you with it. Now she spoke. "Why put out the stars at all? What does darkness buy them?"

"Everything," I said. "Darkness buys everything. Think about what a star is, from the perspective of a government. A star is a fixed point that everyone in the kingdom can see and agree upon. It doesn't collect taxes. It can't be bribed. It hangs there night after night saying *here is north, here is the same sky your grandmother saw,* and it does this for free, and it cannot be edited." I looked around the table at the faces of the exiles — my exiles, the ones who'd drifted to my door over nine years because I fed them bad soup and drew them maps home they could never use. "A king who puts out the stars is a king who wants to be the only fixed point left. Take away the sky and every road becomes a rumor. Every distance becomes unknowable. Every person becomes lost, and lost people follow anyone who seems to know the way."

Quill made a sound from the thief's shoulder. If you've never heard a crow express contempt, it is a low, gravelly *krrr*, like a stone being disappointed. "She's done this speech before," Quill said. "Twice tonight already, I'd wager. Old women and their speeches."

"I beg your pardon," I said.

"She's right, though," Quill added, which I found more annoying, somehow, than the insult. "About all of it. I watched the stars go out from the inside of the spellwork. It wasn't vandalism. It was architecture. You don't darken four hundred and twelve points of light by accident. You do it to hide the seams."

"Seams of what?" Tam asked.

Quill looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the box he carried, the locked one, the dead stranger's legacy, and I saw him decide something — saw the little internal transaction complete, the risk weighed against the need, the coin flipped and caught. That's how thieves think, I remembered later. Everything is a wager, and the trick is knowing which losses you can survive.

"Seams of a prison," he said. "Or maybe not a prison. We're not sure yet. But there's a king under all this darkness, and pieces of him scattered across the country, and I've apparently volunteered to go pick them up."

* * *

Now. Before I go further, I owe you honesty, because this chapter of our story belongs to me, and a map is a confession, and so am I.

When Rosalind said *pieces of him scattered across the country,* something happened in my chest that I had not felt in thirty years. It was not hope. Hope is a young person's emotion; hope assumes the world owes you something. This was older and meaner. It was the feeling of a debt coming due. Of a ledger opening to a page you wrote long ago and prayed everyone had forgotten.

Because I knew about the shards. Not by that name, and not in detail — detail was never given to me — but I knew. In my last year at court, before the fall, I was summoned to map something I was told to forget mapping. A circuit of sites. Twelve locations, chosen along the moon's path, each one surveyed in secret, each one prepared for the burial of something. I drew the charts. I was very good at drawing charts. And when the work was done, a man with kind eyes and a clerk's voice thanked me and told me the survey would be classified at the highest level, and I went home and got drunk for the first time in my life, and the next morning I did the cowardly clever thing: I drew the true map, hid it inside a false one, and told no one.

Thirty years I sat on it. Thirty years I told myself I was keeping it safe, when the truth is I was keeping myself safe, because a secret that could get you killed is also a secret that keeps you important, and importance is its own kind of prison, and I am an expert on prisons, as you will shortly see.

And now a starving thief with no memory and a talking crow had walked into my kitchen carrying a box of the king's stolen self, and the page in the ledger had turned whether I liked it or not.

* * *

"So," I said, when the soup was gone and the candles were low and the others had drifted toward sleep except the ones too wound up to manage it. "You want to know where to find the first piece."

"I want to know if you know," Rosalind said carefully. "I'm not going to assume."

"That's refreshingly polite for a thief."

"I'm a retired thief. The profession's been in decline since the stars went out. Nothing left worth stealing."

"Everything left is worth stealing," I said. "That's precisely the problem. Come here." I rose, joints announcing themselves as usual — I have learned to treat my body's complaints the way I treat bad weather, acknowledged but unargued-with — and took down the map, and beneath it, from the false bottom of the chest where I keep the things that matter more than my life, I brought out the other map.

Rosalind went very still when he saw it. People do, the first time. It's drawn in silverpoint on vellum treated with moondust solution — a trick from the old observatory guilds, before the guilds were dissolved — and in ordinary light it looks like nothing, a blank sheet with faint ghost-lines. But hold it near flame, or near anything that remembers the sky, and the true chart surfaces: twelve points arranged along the arc of the moon's path, each marked with a sigil I copied from documents I was never supposed to read.

"This is the survey," I said. "The real one. The one they classified. Twelve sites, prepared in the last year of the old reign. I was told they were granaries." I smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile; I save those for special occasions. "I have since concluded they were not granaries."

"How do you have this?" Tam breathed. She'd crept close, ward-smith's eyes drinking in the silverpoint lines. "This is Crown-sealed work. The paper alone—"

"The paper alone was my job, child. I was the Royal Cartographer. I mapped what the Crown needed hidden, and I was good enough at it that they never imagined I might keep a copy of the hiding." I set the two maps side by side on the table, the black-circled sky-chart and the silverpoint burial-chart, and did the thing I had rehearsed in my head for thirty years and never once done aloud. I overlaid them.

The room went quiet.

Four hundred and twelve dark places on one chart. Twelve buried sites on the other. And where the spiral of dying stars crossed the arc of the moon's path — there. And there. And there. Three intersections, clean as stitches.

"The stars aren't just being put out," I heard myself say, and my voice sounded younger than it had in decades, the voice of the woman with the seal and the stipend. "They're being put out *in order*. Along the binding-line. Whatever ritual darkened the sky, it's following the same path the old survey did. The darkness isn't decoration. It's maintenance. Someone is re-tightening the prison, site by site, and using the starless places to do it, because—" and here the old cartographer in me finished the thought before the frightened woman could stop her— "because starlight is the one thing that erodes a binding laid along the moon's path. The old shrines were built where they were built for a reason. Light falls differently there. The Crown-thing isn't hiding from us. It's hiding the prison from the *sky*."

"Which means," Rosalind said slowly, "the places where stars used to be are the places the binding is thinnest."

"And the places the binding is thinnest," I said, "are the places a buried shard would be easiest to reach. Assuming a shard is buried at one of my twelve sites at all. Which is a magnificent assumption built on thirty-year-old secrets and a dead man's box, and I would not stake a horse on it, let alone a life."

"But you'd stake yours," Quill said, watching me with one flat eye.

I looked at the old crow, and the old crow looked at me, and between two creatures who had both served crowns and been discarded by them, some understanding passed that I will not describe here because it would embarrass us both.

"I already staked mine," I said. "Thirty years ago, when I drew this instead of burning it. Everything since has just been interest accruing." I tapped the first intersection point, up in the hill country where the River Sorrel floods its banks every spring. "Drowned chapel. Saint Verany's, it was called, before the flood took it. The survey crew sealed something in the crypt below the chapel floor, and then the river did what rivers do, and now the whole business sits under forty feet of cold water, which I consider the finest security arrangement in the kingdom, because it means even the Crown-thing's hounds would have to get wet."

Rosalind studied the point on the map. "How deep?"

"Forty feet, I said."

"I heard you. Forty feet is fine. I grew up—" He stopped. The whole table watched him stop. A man reaching for a memory and finding the shelf empty; I have seen that flinch on a hundred faces in this house, including, most mornings, my own.

"You grew up somewhere," I said gently, "and you don't remember where, and whether you could swim is a question your body will answer before your mind does. I understand. Here's what I can offer: my grandson's diving gear, such as it is, a rope, and the accumulated bitterness of an old woman who has waited three decades for someone to give her secrets a purpose."

"You have a grandson?"

"I have a diving apparatus I stole from a drowned village. The provenance is sadder but the sentence is shorter." I began rolling the maps. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be. "We leave tomorrow, before dawn, because the hounds will come eventually — they always come eventually, ask anyone in this room — and because I am seventy-one years old and I refuse to die in this kitchen waiting for them."

* * *

That night I could not sleep, which at my age is neither unusual nor interesting, but this time it wasn't my bladder or my ghosts. It was the map. I lay in my cot listening to the safehouse breathe — Tam's restless turning, the soft tick of the stove, Quill's occasional dream-grumble from the rafter where she'd appointed herself sentry — and I thought about redemption.

I don't believe in it, mostly. I believe in repair. Redemption implies the world forgives you, and the world has never once forgiven anybody; I've read the ledgers. But repair — repair is work you can actually do. You find the broken thing and you mend it or you carry it to someone who can, and you don't get to be forgiven, you just get to be *useful*, and at seventy-one I have learned that useful is the only absolution on offer.

For thirty years my map sat in its false bottom doing nothing, and I told myself that was prudence. Tonight a thief with no past had held it up to candlelight and traced the spiral of dying stars with one finger, and I had watched his face and understood that he understood it faster than my survey teams ever had, and I thought: *this is the repair. Not the map. Him. An old woman's sins, finally given legs.*

He was a stranger. He was possibly, according to the crow's mutterings, something stranger than a stranger. He would probably get himself killed in a flooded chapel, and possibly get me killed too, and I found that I did not care, and that frightened me more than the hounds did, because caring is how you lose people, and I have lost enough people to furnish a cemetery.

But I lay there in the dark and I thought about the drowned chapel, and about whatever slept beneath its floor in a lead-lined vault that I had helped design and had never once been allowed to see filled, and I thought: *thirty years ago I helped bury something and told no one. Tomorrow I help dig it up.*

If that is not redemption, it is at least symmetry, and a cartographer learns to settle for symmetry.

* * *

We left before dawn, as promised. Four of us: the thief, the girl, the crow, and the old woman with two maps and one good pair of boots. The rest of the exiles stayed behind, because someone must keep the soup warm and the door barred, and because Odile Vasseur has learned the hardest lesson of exile, which is that you cannot bring everyone, and choosing is the price of leading.

The river country opened gray and dripping under a sky with nothing in it. No stars, no moon — the moon itself had gone dark two winters past, swallowed by the spiral, and I confess that of all the missing things on my map, the moon is the one I grieve most. A sky without a moon is a page without margins. Anything can be written on it.

Rosalind walked beside me with the box slung across his back, and Quill rode his shoulder, and after a while the crow said, conversationally, "You're coming because of guilt."

"What a rude bird you are," I said.

"Guilt and maps," Quill continued, undeterred, in the tone of a creature cataloguing inventory. "Old women travel light except for their guilt. It's the heaviest luggage there is."

"And what do you travel with, Mistress Quill? Since we're itemizing."

The crow was quiet for three wingbeats. "Same cargo," she said finally. "Different warehouse."

Ahead of us, the River Sorrel glittered black under a starless sky, running high with spring melt, and somewhere beneath that moving water lay a chapel, a crypt, a vault, and the first piece of a king — and I adjusted my pack straps, and checked my bearings against landmarks no star would ever confirm again, and led my strange little expedition down toward the flood.

Forty feet of cold water. One drowned God's house. And riding on the shoulders of a thief with no past, the entire redemption of Odile Vasseur, former Royal Cartographer to the House of Aurel, currently nobody, soon to be either an instrument of restoration or a cautionary tale.

I have been both before. The water, at least, will be honest with me.
