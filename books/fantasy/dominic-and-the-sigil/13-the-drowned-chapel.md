## 13. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had been drowning for a hundred years and looked like it intended to keep at it out of sheer stubbornness.

We came over the ridge an hour past dawn, Odile leading with her map held open despite the wind doing its level best to turn it into a kite. Below us the valley floor had become a shallow grey sea, stitched through with the tops of dead trees. And there, maybe two hundred paces out from the drowned village's last standing wall, rose the chapel's bell tower — tilted like a drunk leaning on a friend, its spire snapped off, its windows dark. Water lapped at the belfry arch about ten feet below where the roof used to be.

"There," Odile said, and her voice did something I hadn't heard from it before. It went soft. Old women shouldn't sound like that unless they're looking at graves. "Saint Vellin's. I charted it twice. Once when the stars still hung above it, once after they fell. Both times I marked it *missing*."

"Missing how?" Tam asked.

"The stars that used to shine over this valley don't appear on my charts anymore. The place was erased from the sky before it was erased from the ground." She folded the map with great care, as if it might bruise. "That's where you'll find your shard, boy. Underneath everything that was made to be forgotten."

Quill rode on my shoulder the whole descent, silent in that particular way crows have of being loud by saying nothing. When we reached the waterline — black, flat, smelling of rot and old stone — she finally spoke.

"It's cold down there," she said.

"You've mentioned. Twice."

"It bears repeating. Cold water stops hearts, Rosalind. I've seen it happen to kings." A pause, feathers ruffling. "Well. One king."

"That's comforting, thank you."

"I'm not here to comfort you. I'm here to smell memories, and I'm telling you the truth: whatever's down there has been marinating for a century. It'll go down easy but it won't come up gentle."

Tam knelt at the water's edge and started rigging the lamp — one of Odile's strange pieces, a glass globe filled with what looked like liquid starlight, salvaged from the safehouse. It gave off a pale silver glow without any heat at all, which Tam assured me meant it couldn't explode, and which Odile assured me meant nothing, because ward-craft had never once in history been prevented from exploding merely because it was cold.

"The tower shaft should give you a clear line down," Tam said, hands moving with that quick sure economy she had whenever her master's training took over and stopped her stammering. "Nave's flooded to the vaulting, so you'll come out under the roof into open water. The crypt is below the altar. If Odile's right about the erasure wards, the shard will be sitting in the reliquary like it was put there yesterday."

"And if she's wrong?"

"Then you get to drown somewhere historically interesting." She glanced up at me, and for once didn't look away. "You don't have to be the one who goes down. I've dived flooded culverts before."

"In ward-smith culverts," I said. "This is a chapel that the sky itself deleted. If something down there eats divers, it should eat the professional memory thief first. That's just good economics."

Nobody laughed, which I'd expected. I'd learned by then that my found family found my jokes less funny the closer we got to dying, which seemed backwards to me, but grief does strange things to comic timing.

* * *

I stripped to my shirt, and Odile tied the lamp cord around my waist, and Quill hopped onto my shoulder one more time and pressed her beak briefly against my ear — not speaking, just touching, the way you'd squeeze someone's hand.

"For what it's worth," she murmured, "Dominic hated water too."

"That's *very* worth mentioning now, yes."

"He'd have gone anyway." Then she flew back to Tam's arm, and there was nothing left to do but breathe three times and go under.

The cold hit me like a debt collector. Down and down the tower shaft, the lamp swinging at my hip throwing silver light across stones furred green-black with a century of silt, and I will confess here, in the honest pages of what will eventually be somebody's book, that every foot of depth I screamed at myself. Not out loud. Out loud would have been fatal. But some part of me that remembered being a thief on the border road — small thefts, warm inns, rules that kept me alive — kept insisting this violated every rule I'd ever made. Rule one: never work wet. Rule two: never work for free. Rule three: never dive into erased chapels for kings who may or may not deserve saving.

Rules, I was discovering, are what cowards call their fears when they want them to sound like wisdom.

The shaft opened. I kicked out into the nave.

The lamp turned the flooded church into something out of a dream someone had been ashamed of. Pews drifted in slow orbit, swollen with water, trailing weed like the tails of drowned animals. Fish — small, blind-looking, pale — scattered from my light in silver flickers. Above me the vaulting arched away into darkness; below me the floor waited, and on the floor, exactly where Odile's charts said it would be, the altar stood beneath a coat of silt like a sleeping thing under a blanket.

And around the altar, the water was *wrong*.

I can't describe it better than that. Wrong. Where everywhere else the flood moved in lazy currents, here the water lay perfectly still, a column of absolute calm rising from the altar to the surface, as if the chapel were holding its breath. My little lamp's light bent oddly entering that column, curving inward, eager.

Memories do that, I know now. They pull light toward themselves. They always wanted to be found.

I swam down the last few yards, hooked my fingers under the altar's lip, and found the reliquary set into the stone — a small iron box, unlocked, its lid already open. Empty except for one object the size of a walnut, glowing faintly, patiently, the way embers glow hours after everyone's gone to bed.

A shard. A piece of a king.

I reached in and picked it up.

* * *

People assume stealing a memory feels like taking a purse. Quick fingers, a small weight, done. But I'd spent years lifting recollections off travelers on the border road, and I knew the truth: taking a memory is *entering* someone. You slide into the shape of their remembering, and for a moment you wear their life like a borrowed coat, and sometimes the coat fits so well you forget whose shoulders you're standing on.

I had never taken a shard this old, this dense, this *deliberately kept*. And I was underwater, and my air was finite, and I had no choice at all.

The moment my skin touched the shard, Dominic's coronation opened around me like a door.

Not watching it. *Inside* it.

I was tall. That was the first absurdity — I was suddenly, overwhelmingly tall, and broad, and my body ached in the pleasant way of a body that had ridden hard for days and been glad of the reason. I could feel a heavy cloak settle on shoulders built to carry it. I could feel sweat under a crown of hammered silver, and the enormous, ridiculous itch of it against my temples, and the desperate awareness that a king must not scratch his own head during his own coronation.

I was looking out through his eyes at ten thousand faces.

Sunlight. Real sunlight, gold and warm — I hadn't felt true sunlight since the stars went out, and even in memory it nearly broke me. It fell across a crowd that stretched to the horizon of the great square: farmers with sunburnt necks, merchants in their best, children sitting on fathers' shoulders, soldiers holding back nothing at all because nobody needed holding back. The noise came through Dominic's ears like weather — cheering, vast and rolling, and underneath it bells, dozens of them, all the bells of the capital ringing at once.

He was happy. God help me, he was *happy*, and happiness that size is a physical force. It moved through his chest in waves. He looked out at all those faces and saw, individually, a woman holding up a baby so it could see; a baker with flour still somehow on his sleeve; an old soldier crying without embarrassment. And Dominic — I stood inside his joy and it was mine for three heartbeats — thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*I will never let anything happen to them.*

And directly underneath that thought, in the same instant, quiet as a coin slipped into a pocket:

*Something will. Something always comes. What do I do when it comes?*

There it was. Joy and dread in the same breath, braided so tightly you couldn't tell which thread was which. He loved them all already, these strangers, and because he loved them he was already afraid, and because he was afraid on their behalf rather than his own, I understood in that flooded dark chapel a hundred years later that he had been a king for exactly as long as it took to make that double thought, crown or no crown.

The archbishop raised the crown — no, wait, the crown was already on him, I felt its weight — raised the *scepter*, and spoke words I half-heard, because Dominic wasn't listening. He was looking at one spot in the crowd where a young woman stood apart from everyone, dark-haired, not cheering, watching him with an expression I recognized only because I'd worn its twin every day of my life in the valley.

She knew something. And he knew she knew. And he loved her — the knowledge of that hit me like the cold water had, total and undeniable — and she had come to his coronation anyway, carrying whatever terrible thing she carried, because she wouldn't miss it for the world.

He lifted his hand to her. Half the crowd took it as a blessing aimed their way and roared louder.

And then — the memory shivered. The light guttered. Somewhere behind the sun a shadow moved that wasn't cloud, and Dominic's dread, the second thread of the braid, pulled suddenly tight, and he thought — quickly, guiltily, a king's private arithmetic —

*After the feast. I'll deal with it after the feast. One day. Let me keep today.*

The bells rang. The crowd roared. The shadow behind the sun grew a fraction wider, patient as winter.

*Let me keep today.*

* * *

I came back to myself underwater with perhaps a minute of air left and no clear memory of deciding to swim.

The ascent was chaos — lamp banging, silt boiling up around me, my hands shaking badly enough that I cracked my knuckles twice on the tower shaft's stone. I remember thinking, with perfect lucid idiocy, *he scratched his head, actually, right after the scepter part, when the archbishop turned away, and it was the most human thing I ever felt anyone do.* I remember breaking the surface gasping, and Tam hauling me over the belfry ledge, and Odile wrapping a blanket around my shoulders with hands that pretended not to tremble.

Quill landed on my knee and looked at me with one bright eye.

"Well?" she said.

I tried to answer and found my voice had gone somewhere. I held up the shard instead — dull now, spent of its vision, just a lump of faintly luminous stone in my palm. Tam leaned in. Odile leaned in. Even the wind, for a moment, seemed to lean in.

"It's him," I managed finally. "It's really him. Not the stories, not the martyr, not the weapon Vess wants him to be. He was—" My throat closed. I swallowed it down. "He was happy, and he was scared, both at once, the whole time, and he loved them. Ten thousand strangers. He looked at a woman holding up a baby and decided the whole world was his to protect."

Odile sat back slowly on her heels. In the flat grey light, with the drowned chapel at our backs, the old cartographer looked like a woman being handed a bill she'd been dreading for forty years.

"That's not all," she said. It wasn't a question.

"No." I turned the shard over. "There was someone. A woman in the crowd who wasn't celebrating. She looked at him like she'd come to say goodbye." I met Odile's eyes. "And he saw something behind the sun. Something coming. And he decided to deal with it *tomorrow*."

Silence, except for water lapping against the tower stones below us, patient as everything else in this kingdom.

"So he was warned," Tam said quietly.

"He was warned at his own coronation. By someone he loved. And he chose one more day of joy first." I closed my fist around the shard. It was warm, the only warm thing for miles. "I don't know if that makes him a fool or the most honest man who ever wore a crown. But I know this much—"

"What?" Quill asked.

"Whoever warned him — she knew what was coming and she came anyway. Which means somebody out there, a hundred years back, tried to save him *before* the sigil, *before* the valley, *before* all of this. And if she failed..." I looked east, toward the capital I hadn't seen yet and already hated. "...then whoever helped the crown-thing win was someone standing close enough to touch him."

Quill went very still on my knee. So still that later I would understand she had gone still the way people do when a sentence walks past wearing their own name.

Odile was already unfolding her chart, finger moving to the next mark. "The ledger lists another cache," she said. "North. In a general's tomb, if Vess's notes are honest." Her eyes flicked up to mine, sharp. "Can you carry more of him, boy? Because what you just described — joy and dread braided together — that's not the last of it. That's only the coronation."

Below us, the drowned chapel held its silence, and its still column of unbroken water, and whatever else it had kept safe for a hundred years against the day a thief would come swimming down into the dark to fetch a king home.

I closed my hand tighter around the shard and stood up.

"One tomb at a time," I said. "But Odile — if the next one shows me what came out from behind that sun..."

I didn't finish. None of us needed me to. We packed the camp in silence, and by midday we were walking north, four exiles and a crow, carrying a piece of a happy, frightened man toward the next place the sky had deleted.
