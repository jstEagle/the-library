## 14. The Crown's Hounds

I want it on the record that I said something was wrong.

Nobody remembers that part later—everyone remembers Odile shouting and Rosalind running and Quill screaming like a feathered kettle—but I was the one who stopped mid-sentence over supper and said, "The wards just tasted wrong." Because that's a thing ward-smiths do. We taste them. Not literally, mostly. A well-made ward has a hum to it, like a wet finger on a glass rim, steady and clean. The safehouse wards had been humming along for three days while we hid among Odile's exiles, learning her maps, eating her terrible dried fish, pretending we weren't waiting for the sky to fall.

That evening the hum went sour. Like someone had licked the glass first.

"Wrong how?" Rosalind asked. He had that thief's stillness about him, the kind that looks lazy until you realize he's already counted the exits.

"Sour," I said. "Like copper. Like—" I didn't have the word yet. I'd have it later, in the worst possible way. "Like something reading them."

Odile set down her cup with the deliberate care of a woman who had once drawn maps for kings and now drew them for people who couldn't afford to be found. "How long would it take to break wards you helped design?"

"I didn't help design *these*," I said. "But my master's hand is all over this valley's work, and if his hand is here, then whoever's hunting us knows his grammar." I swallowed. "Minutes. Maybe less."

"*Then we're leaving,*" Odile said, and the room moved.

Here's what nobody tells you about found family: it assembles itself at exactly the speed of a disaster. One moment you're a table of strangers passing bread; the next, everyone knows their job without being told. Rosalind went for the box and the shards. Quill went for the window, because Quill always goes for the window, it's her whole personality. Two of Odile's people—a scarred ferryman named Brannic and a girl called Wren who never spoke above a whisper—took up positions at the door like they'd rehearsed it, which, knowing Odile, they had.

And me? I did the thing I'd been dreading since the drowned chapel. I took the shard out of its oilcloth.

It was warm. It was always warm, the way a stone is warm after sun even on a cloudy day, holding light that isn't currently there. Dominic's memory of the war room sat in my palm like a coal that dreamed. And underneath the warmth, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, something pulsed back when the wards soured. *Recognition.* The shard knew what was coming for us.

"They can feel it," I said. "Whatever's coming—they're following the shard, not us."

"Then the shard leaves," Rosalind said, holding out his hand. "Give it here. I'll run it out the back, draw them off—"

"No." I don't know where the *no* came from. It came from somewhere below training, below sense. "You carry the box. You carry the map and the ledger and every other thing Vess wants. If they take you, this is over. If they take me—" I turned the shard over. "If they take me, it's only me."

"That's not nothing," Rosalind said quietly.

"It's less than everything," I said, "which is the currency we're spending tonight."

*

They came through the eastern wall, which should have been impossible, because the eastern wall was three feet of old stone wrapped in six layers of Odile's best quieting-work. They came through it the way water comes through a cracked dam—not fast, at first. A bulge. A groan. Then the stone didn't break so much as *agree* to be elsewhere, folding outward like paper held near a flame, and the night poured in.

I've seen sigil-bound constructs before. My master built one, once, to demonstrate why we should never build sigil-bound constructs. It had been the size of a dog and dumb as a boot heel, all grinding purpose and no mind. These were worse. These were *shaped*. Three of them, man-high and man-shaped, wearing armor the way a corpse wears clothes—draped, not dressed—and where faces should have been there were smooth plates of dark metal etched with a single mark I knew down to my bones.

The binding sigil. My master's binding sigil, rendered small enough to wear.

"Oh," I said, very calmly, to no one. "Oh, he's been *busy*."

"TAM!" Odile's voice cracked across the room like a whip. "Back door, NOW—"

The first hound moved.

I want to describe it properly, because later—in the bad hours—I kept losing the details, and the details are the proof it happened. It didn't walk so much as arrive in installments: a smear of motion, then suddenly it was across the room, and Brannic met it with an iron-banded boat hook, because Brannic had been a ferryman before he'd been a fugitive and you fight with what your hands remember. The hook caught the hound across the shoulder-plate and *sang*, a high wrong note, and the hound turned its blank face toward him with the unhurried interest of something that has never once been hurt by anything that mattered.

"Brannic, the *neck-seam*—" I shouted, because I could see it, the join where the plates met, the flaw, the grammar error. My master taught me that every binding has a seam. Every cage has a door somebody forgot to lock from the inside.

Brannic twisted the hook and drove it into the seam and the hound came apart in a spray of sparks and black glass, and for one glorious half-second I thought: *we can do this.*

Then the second hound hit the table.

*

Afterward, piecing it together from what others saw and what I saw and what I'll admit to seeing: the room became a storm of specific disasters. Wren pulled Odile through the kitchen. Rosalind got the box up onto his shoulder and Quill landed on him shrieking "*left, LEFT, you beautiful idiot*" and he went left through the smoke. The third hound ignored all of them.

It came for me.

Of course it came for me. I was holding the shard, and the shard was singing, and whatever wore the crown on the far side of those metal faces wanted its song back.

I ran. I'm not proud and I'm not ashamed; I ran the way ward-smiths are trained to run, which is *toward the wards*, because a smith who dies outside her own defenses has wasted her education. I got two steps into the corridor before the hound's hand closed on my collar and lifted, and I learned that being held by a construct feels like being held by a decision rather than a creature. No malice in it. No anger. Just grip.

Its face-plate swung close. Where eyes would be, the etched sigil began to glow, and I felt it *reading* me—the same sour-copper feeling from the wards, but pointed inward now, paging through me like a clerk looking for a ledger entry. Looking for the shard.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing I'd want carved on my marker if markers existed for people like me: it wasn't going to find it. Because while its fingers were closing on my collar, my other hand had already done the arithmetic.

A shard is a piece of a king's soul cut loose and made solid. Solid things can be carried. Solid things can be hidden. And there is exactly one hiding place in this world that no hunter can search, no broker can buy, and no sigil can read—because the one thing the crown-thing cannot do, the thing it bound a king to avoid, is go *inside* a living person willingly.

My master used to say: *the best ward is the one that costs you something to make.* He meant materials. He meant years.

He never said what it might cost to *use* one.

I put the shard in my mouth and swallowed it whole.

*

It went down like swallowing a star.

I don't mean that prettily. I mean it burned the whole way, a line of white heat from tongue to throat to chest, and when it landed—wherever it landed, behind my sternum, in the soft ordinary country of my body—it *rang*. One clear note, felt rather than heard, and every nerve I owned stood up and paid attention.

The hound dropped me.

Not gently. Its fingers opened like the sigil on its face had flinched, and I hit the floorboards hard enough to bite my tongue, and blood and shard-light mixed in my mouth, and the hound staggered back a full step with its blank face tilted, listening, the way a dog tilts its head at a sound it can't source.

Because it couldn't find the shard anymore. I could feel that, too—feel the hound's confusion as a pressure against the new thing in my chest. The shard wasn't gone. The shard was *everywhere and nowhere*, distributed through a living nervous system that had never consented to be architecture and was making its objections known.

*Tam,* said something, in a voice like a bell heard underwater.

Not the hound. Not out loud. Inside, behind my ribs, in the warm coal-dark where the shard had settled.

*I know what you did,* the voice said, and it sounded—gods help me—it sounded *grateful*, and it sounded *sorry*, both at once, the way only a king's ghost can manage. *You should not have done it. But oh, you brave, foolish—*

"Shut up," I whispered, spitting blood. "Shut up, shut up, I'm busy."

The busy part was this: the hound had recovered. The glow on its face steadied. Whatever confusion the swallow had bought me was spent, and now it simply reached down and took my wrist, and then my other wrist, and bound them with something that wasn't rope—black glass links that grew together, sealing, the same material its dead brother had shattered into. Sigil-glass. Living restraints. Somewhere under my terror, the smith in me noted: *self-repairing matrix, master's later work, he improved the seams, damn him, damn him for improving anything.*

From the kitchen, Odile's voice, raw: "TAM!"

"GO!" I screamed it with everything I had. "ODILE, GO, IT'S ME THEY WANT—"

A pause. A horrible pause, the length of a heartbeat and a half, in which I heard a seventy-year-old cartographer decide whether to spend her life on mine.

Then Wren's whisper, urgent, and the back door banging, and Odile's footsteps—running away, hating it, and I loved her so much in that moment that it hurt worse than the glass on my wrists.

The hound lifted me like luggage. Through the broken eastern wall, into the night, where the third hound waited beside a figure I hadn't seen before: a man in a grey coat, unarmored, unarmed, holding a lantern with a shuttered flame. He looked tired. He looked like a tax collector at the end of a long quarter.

He looked at me the way Vess used to look at merchandise.

"The apprentice," he said. His voice was mild. That was the worst part, the mildness. "The master's last student. She'll do. She more than does." He raised the lantern, and the shuttered light fell through its slit directly onto my chest, and inside my ribs the shard *answered*, glowing through my skin like a brand seen through a shirt.

"She swallowed it," the hound-thing holding me reported, in a voice like gravel settling.

"I know," the mild man said. "That's why she'll do." He smiled, and it was a small, tidy smile, the smile of a man checking an item off a list. "You can't cut out what she's taken, little smith. You can't starve it out or burn it out or wait it out. All you can do is come with us, and listen, and learn what it says to you. And eventually—" he tapped the lantern, once, gently, "—you'll tell us. Everyone tells us. It's only a question of how much of *you* is left when you do."

Inside my chest, the shard pulsed. Warm. Steady. Patient as a buried king.

*Hold on,* Dominic's voice said, or the memory of him, or the piece of him that lived in the war-room shard now lodged behind my heart. *Hold on, Tam Thackeray's-own-nobody. Help is coming. I know the thief. He always comes back for what's his.*

"He's not mine," I said out loud, to a mild man who couldn't hear it, to a hound that didn't care, to a king made of light in a girl made of fear. "He's not anybody's. That's the whole point of him."

The mild man tilted his head. "What was that?"

"Nothing," I said, and lifted my chin, and let them carry me into the dark—toward the capital, toward the gilded prison I didn't yet know the name of, toward the voice that was already beginning, very softly, to speak through my sleeping hands.

But that's later. First, the road. First, the long dark ride, with a king's ember burning in my chest and one thought repeating like a ward-chant, like a prayer, like a promise I intended to collect on:

*Rosalind. You beautiful idiot. Come left.*
