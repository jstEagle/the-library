## 15. Bargaining with the Broker

The road to Vess's counting-house had changed, the way everything changed once the stars went out and stayed out. Before, it had been a smuggler's track through gorse and shadow, the kind of road you could walk a hundred times and never learn, because the valley ate the learning. Now it was just a road, stubborn and ordinary, and I could have found it blindfolded, which was a strange new kind of wealth — to know a road, to own it in my head like a coin I'd never have to spend.

I was about to spend something far more expensive than road-knowledge.

Quill flew above me in slow circles, a black comma against a sky the color of a dead man's lips. She'd been quiet since we'd buried the stranger under the rowan tree, quiet since Tam's name had stopped being a word and started being a wound. Crows are supposed to be quiet anyway, but Quill's silences had textures, and this one was the texture of guilt.

"You could say something," I offered.

"I could," she said, and said nothing else.

The counting-house sat in a hollow where three dead lantern-roads met, a squat stone building that had once been a tollhouse and had never stopped behaving like one. Vess collected tolls on everything — memories, favors, secrets, apologies. Especially apologies. I had seen him make a man pay for the privilege of being forgiven.

The door opened before I knocked. It always did. Vess had a gift for knowing who stood on his step, which should have frightened me more than it did, and would have, if I'd understood then just how well he knew me.

"Rosalind Thackeray," he said, warm as a hearth and about as honest. "You've been away. Your absence has been noted. Noted things appreciate in value, you know."

"Everything appreciates in value around you, Vess. That's the whole trick."

"Come in, come in. You look terrible. Grief suits some people. On you it looks like a coat borrowed from a larger man."

* * *

The counting-house inside was exactly as I remembered, which was itself a small miracle — shelves of labeled jars, each one holding a memory in suspension, glimmering faintly like fireflies that had given up on flying. Vess's ledger lay open on his desk, a book so fat it had its own gravity. I had sold him a hundred memories over the years. I had never once been allowed to read the ledger.

I tried anyway. Old habits.

"Ah-ah." He closed it with one finger, gentle as a man shushing a child. "You know the rules."

"I know you make up the rules as they benefit you."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "That is the rule."

I sat. My hands wanted to shake, so I put them under the table like a card player hiding a bad hand, except my hand wasn't bad. It was the best hand I'd ever held, and I was about to fold it.

"Tam," I said. "The ward-smith's apprentice. Your people took her from the safehouse on the hill. I want to know where she is."

Vess's eyebrows performed a small, elegant dance. "Your people? I wasn't aware you had people, Rosalind. When you worked for me, you were famously un-peopled. A man of jars and no jars."

"I've expanded."

"So I hear. A king's memories, a talking crow, a cartographer who draws places that no longer exist. You've assembled quite the collection." He steepled his fingers. "And now you want to add a captured girl to it."

"I want to know where she's being held."

"And what will you give me for it?"

I had rehearsed this on the road. I had rehearsed it in my sleep, which was a mistake, because in my sleep I'd bargained with a version of Vess who had a conscience, and I'd woken up angry at a man who didn't exist.

"Name your price," I said, which was a lie, because there was a price, and we both knew it, and the whole conversation was a ritual we performed so that afterward I couldn't claim I hadn't known.

Vess leaned back. "You've grown, Rosalind. Before, you would have haggled. You would have offered me three lesser memories and a favor and pretended the favor was worth two memories. Now you just sit there with your grief on like a lamp and say *name your price*." He clicked his tongue. "It's almost touching. It makes me feel like a man robbing a church."

"Then feel it and name the price."

He opened a drawer. From the drawer he took a jar — empty, clean, its stopper wrapped in velvet — and set it on the desk between us with the tenderness of a priest setting out communion.

"You know what this is for."

My mouth had gone dry. "I have memories."

"You have *fragments*. Scraps of other men's lives. A coronation you sold me already, and good riddance, it was giving the other jars ideas. No." He tapped the empty jar. "I want the one you've been carrying like a coal in your pocket since the wards fell. The one that came back to you. *Yours.*"

* * *

Here is what I had, and what I was about to lose, and I will tell it plainly because I will not get to tell it again.

When the valley's wards collapsed, everything the valley had eaten came flooding back — to the valley, to the land, to the people who'd lived their whole lives inside its forgetting. Most of what returned to me was nothing. A dog's name. The smell of the kitchen where I'd slept as a child. The particular way the border road looked at dusk in the years when dusk still had stars in it.

But one memory came back whole, and warm, and *mine* in a way nothing else in my life had ever been mine.

A summer evening. I was small — six, maybe seven. There was a woman kneeling in a garden, teaching me to fold a paper boat, her hands over mine, and she was laughing at how badly I folded, and there was a man in the doorway behind her with his sleeves rolled up, and neither of their faces would ever come into focus, the way faces in precious memories refuse to, but I knew — I knew the way you know your own heartbeat — that these were my mother and my father, and that the garden was behind the house where I was born, and that for one entire evening I had been a child who was loved by name.

I had lived twenty-some years in a valley that scraped me hollow every dawn. I had built a self out of stolen parts, a thief's mosaic of other people's weddings and funerals and first kisses. And then the wards fell, and for a few weeks — a few *weeks* — I had owned one evening of being somebody's son.

Vess wanted it.

"Why?" I asked. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "It's worthless to you. It's a garden and a paper boat. There's no king in it, no sigil, no secret. It's just — mine."

Vess looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes that I mistook, then, for pity.

"That," he said softly, "is precisely why it's priceless. Memories like that are the currency everything else is measured against. Do you know what I could buy with a jar containing a man's entire childhood love? I could buy loyalty. I could buy a life's obedience. One whiff of that, held under the nose of any broken creature in this kingdom, and they'd follow me into a fire." He smiled. "You're not selling me a memory, Rosalind. You're selling me a key that fits every lock."

"And Tam's location is worth that?"

"Tam's location," said Vess, "is worth whatever you'll pay for it. That's not cruelty. That's just arithmetic."

Quill shifted on the windowsill. "Rosalind," she said, low, a warning with feathers on it.

"I know," I said.

I looked at the jar. Empty glass, patient as a grave. Somewhere in the capital, in a pleasure-quarter prison with gilded bars, a nineteen-year-old girl who had swallowed a king's memory to keep it from the crown's hounds was sitting in a cell because she'd had the courage I was now discovering I didn't. She had given up her body's safety for the mission. I was being asked to give up a garden.

It didn't seem like a fair trade. It didn't seem like a trade at all. It seemed like Vess had found the one coin I couldn't counterfeit and named it as the toll.

And I was going to pay it, because the alternative was a world with one more person in a cage and one fewer person who knew how to unmake cages.

* * *

"Before you do this," Vess said, and his voice had changed — gentler, almost kind, which from Vess was more frightening than any threat — "let me tell you what you're actually trading. You're trading the only proof you have that you were ever loved. Everything else in your head is borrowed. That evening in the garden is the one thing in you that's real. Give it to me, and you'll remember that it existed — the way you remember a story someone told you — but you will never again feel it. It will be a fact, not a warmth. Are you following me, boy? I'm not buying a memory. I'm buying the floor you stand on."

"You've made your point."

"I haven't made it well enough, or you'd already be out the door."

Quill hopped down onto the desk, between me and the jar. Up close, her eyes were the color of the sky used to be. "He's right," she said. "Walk out. We'll find Tam another way. Odile knows the capital's underways. There's always another way."

"There isn't," I said. "There's always another way except when there's a girl in a cell and a clock with thirteen turns on it. You of all people know about clocks, Quill."

The crow said nothing. Crows can't flinch, but she managed it.

I put my hand on the jar.

"Wait." Vess raised a finger. "One professional courtesy, since you're about to become the most interesting transaction of my career. You should know that I'll take good care of it. I don't damage merchandise. Your evening in the garden will be kept in velvet and glass, dusted weekly, and — who knows — perhaps one day you'll be in a position to buy it back."

"Perhaps one day you'll be in a position to sell it back at a fair price."

"Oh, Rosalind." He laughed, genuinely delighted. "You say the most optimistic things."

I uncorked the memory the way you pull a tooth — fast, before the nerve can argue. I held the evening in my mind one last time: the paper boat, the laughing hands over mine, the man in the doorway with his sleeves rolled up, the light coming down through the garden like a blessing that didn't know it was temporary.

*Goodbye,* I told it. *Thank you for being real.*

Then I let it pour out of me and into the glass.

I won't describe what it feels like, because I can't anymore, and that's the whole point. I remember that it hurt. I remember that afterward I sat in Vess's chair with my eyes open and my hands flat on the desk, feeling like a house after the furniture's been carried out — the rooms all still there, the echoes all wrong. And I remember that I could still see the garden, could still see the boat, could still see the woman's hands — but it was like watching it through the jar's own glass, from the outside, a stranger's summer in a stranger's garden.

A fact, not a warmth.

Vess stoppered the jar and held it up to the light, and for a moment his merchant's mask slipped and something hungry and old looked out of his face at the glow. Then it was gone, and he was Vess again, wrapping the jar in velvet with the care of a man handling a relic.

"Tam," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "You owe me a location."

"Rowan Street," Vess said promptly. "The House of Delights, third door past the fountain with the broken mermaid. They keep her in the amber room on the top floor — they think the amber dampens ward-craft, and they're almost right, but your girl is better than they think she is, so move quickly. Two days, perhaps three, before they transfer her to the Regent's own keep, and after that, Rosalind, not all the paper boats in the world will get her out." He slid a slip of paper across the desk. A key, drawn in ink. "The underways enter through the cistern. Odile will know it. Tell her Vess sends his regards, and that I remember when she could still hold a pen."

I stood. The room tilted and settled. Quill landed on my shoulder, and her claws were gentle, and I was grateful she didn't say anything kind, because I would have come apart.

At the door, Vess spoke once more, and there was something in his voice I couldn't read — something that almost sounded like regret, if regret could wear a merchant's coat.

"Rosalind. A last piece of advice, free of charge, which should tell you how seriously to take it. When you get her out — and you will, you're stubborn enough — ask her what the sigil has been saying to her. Ask her exactly, word for word. And then ask yourself why a thing that wears a king's crown would bother whispering to a smith's apprentice about *you*."

I turned. "What does it say about me?"

Vess smiled his hearth-warm smile and touched the velvet-wrapped jar at his breast like a man touching a saint's bone.

"Buy it back someday," he said, "and I'll tell you."

The door closed behind me. The dead lantern-roads stretched out in three directions under a sky with no stars, and somewhere on the third of them, a girl was waiting in a golden room for people she'd never met to come and get her.

Quill pressed her beak against my ear. "You did the right thing," she said.

"I know," I said. "That's the worst part. I'd feel so much better if it had been the wrong thing. Wrong things don't leave a hole."

We started walking. Behind us, in the counting-house, a jar full of a child's summer evening sat in velvet on a merchant's shelf, and I did not look back, because I had finally learned the one rule the valley never taught me:

Some doors you burn, and some you just stop turning around for.

Two days to the capital. Two days to the House of Delights, the broken mermaid, the amber room.

And in the amber room, something was already whispering my name.
