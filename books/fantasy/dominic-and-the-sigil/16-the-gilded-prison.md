## 16. The Gilded Prison

They say the Gilded Prison has no locks.

That's the first thing you learn about it, usually from someone who's never been inside and is very proud of that fact. No locks, they say. No bars, no chains, no ward-arrays humming at the doors. Just a beautiful house in the pleasure quarter with soft beds and sweet wine and servants who smile too evenly, and once you walk in, you find you've lost all interest in walking out.

I'd have called it a lie, a week ago. A week ago I was still a girl who thought wards were honest things — that a wall was a wall, and if you knew where it was stitched together, you could unpick it. I'd been raised on that gospel by Master Corvane: *every binding is just a sentence, Tam, and every sentence can be misread.* He said it the way other men said prayers.

Then the hounds took me. Then the shard went down my throat like swallowed winter. And then I learned what it is to be a lock that walks around believing it's free.

But let me start where a sensible person would start: with Rosalind Thackeray picking a fight about my shoes.

* * *

"You can't go in wearing those," he said, holding up my boots like they were evidence of a crime. Which, in a way, they were. Ward-smith's boots. Steel-shanked, salt-scuffed, with the little burn scars along the sole from every time I'd tested a live array barefoot because Corvane said fear taught better than theory.

"They're boots," I said. "People wear them."

"People who work for their living," Rosalind said. "Nobody in the Gilded Prison works. You're supposed to be a lady of easy virtue and expensive taste, and ladies of easy virtue do not dress like they're about to re-solder a gate hinge." He turned the boots over, frowned at the scorch mark shaped vaguely like Corvane's initials, and added, "Also these are hideous."

"They're *practical.*"

"They're a confession." He set them down and pushed across the table a pair of slippers so delicate they looked embarrassed to exist. Silk. Embroidered. Worth more than everything I owned, which admittedly wasn't a hard bar to clear since everything I owned fit in one bag and half of it was stolen from the people I was traveling with.

"I can't run in those," I said.

"That," said Odile from her corner, not looking up from the map she was redrawing from memory, "is the point. If you run, the plan has failed. The plan requires you to be caught."

The plan. Right. Let me describe the plan, so posterity understands exactly how much I trusted these people, and how much they deserved it.

The Gilded Prison didn't hold its guests with walls. It held them with the shard-song. Every captive who'd swallowed or touched a piece of Dominic's scattered memory — and there were three of us known to the Regent's hounds, though only one of us had been stupid enough to get caught — heard it. All the time. A low sweet tone under the world, like a wine glass singing at the edge of shattering. The longer you were inside the prison's bounds, the louder it got, until your own thoughts started arranging themselves around it like iron filings around a lodestone. You didn't stay because you couldn't leave. You stayed because leaving felt like tearing out your own eardrums, and then your own heart, and then — well. Nobody who got that far came back to describe it.

Except the sigil spoke through *me*, and here was the thing nobody wanted to say out loud: the song liked me. It had been whispering through my hand for a week, writing in a script that wasn't mine, and when the hounds closed around our safehouse and I made the only choice available — swallow the shard, deny them the prize — the song hadn't screamed. It had *sung*. Like I'd done something clever. Like I was in on the joke.

So the plan was this: I would walk into the Gilded Prison as bait wearing silk slippers, let them believe their song had worked on me, locate the warden's key-room — where Vess's agents had stowed the two shards they'd seized at the Eleventh Turn along with Quill, feathered and furious in a cage built for something much larger — and then, on Rosalind's signal, we would take it all back.

"And if the signal doesn't come?" I'd asked, back when the plan was being drawn in Odile's careful cartographer's hand.

"Then you escape alone," Rosalind said, "because you'll have had three days inside to learn the place, and you're the only one of us who thinks like a lock."

It was, I want it noted, the nicest thing anyone had ever said to me. *You think like a lock.* From Corvane that would have been an insult. From Rosalind it sounded almost like love, if love were delivered deadpan by a thief eating stolen plums.

* * *

The pleasure quarter at dusk smelled of candied ginger and lies. Lanterns strung between balconies like a net trying to catch the dark — there was no dark left to catch, of course; the stars had been gone a season now, and the whole city had developed this habit of over-lighting itself, as if enough candles might shame the sky into remembering its job.

My contact was a woman named Sefa who ran a bathhouse two streets from the Prison and owed Odile a favor older than I was. She looked me up and down — the borrowed gown, the painted face Odile had applied with the grim precision of a woman labeling a map — and said, "You look edible. That's the idea?"

"That's the idea."

"Hungry things in there, girl." She adjusted my collar with fingers surprisingly gentle for someone whose knuckles looked like walnuts. "They don't eat you quick, neither. They let you marinate." Then she told me the servant's entrance code — a knock pattern, three-and-two, like a heartbeat stumbling — and sent me toward the prettiest cage in the kingdom.

The door opened before I knocked twice. Of course it did. They'd been watching me since the bathhouse. Possibly since the city gates. Possibly since I swallowed a king's memory and walked out of a burning safehouse whistling.

Inside, the Gilded Prison was worse than I'd imagined, because I have a good imagination and it still undersold the cruelty of comfort. Carpets thick enough to lose grief in. Wine the color of garnets. Musicians playing something slow and honeyed in a gallery above, and every guest drifting through the rooms with that same even smile Sefa had warned me about — happy the way drowned things are buoyant.

A steward glided up. Silver-haired, kind-eyed, with a voice like warm milk. "Lady Miravel. We've expected you." Not *been expecting*. *Expected*, present tense, eternal, like weather.

"I was told," I said, doing my best impression of a woman of easy virtue and expensive taste, which mostly involved not flinching, "that this house keeps interesting company."

"The most interesting company in the world." He offered his arm. His sleeve was warm against my hand. Too warm. Everything in this house ran a degree hotter than it should, like the building itself had a fever. "And you bring such lovely music with you, my lady. Do you hear it? Most guests take days to notice. You arrived already listening."

I smiled the smile Odile had drilled into me and said, "I've always had an ear."

The lie of it sat in my stomach like a stone, because I did have an ear, and the song was right there, and it was glad to see me. That's the part I haven't managed to make any of them understand, sitting safe outside these walls. The sigil wasn't a prisoner screaming in its cell. It was a host greeting a guest. *Tam,* it hummed through the marrow of me, *you came back.*

I hadn't come back anywhere. But try arguing with a voice that lives in your bones.

* * *

Three days. That was the plan's allowance, and I used all of it, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to explain what those three days cost.

Days are long in a house with no clocks. I learned the geography the way Corvane taught me to learn a ward-array: not by memorizing the shape but by finding the flaws. Where the carpets thinned near the east stair because foot traffic wore them and no one replaced them — meaning the servants avoided that route, meaning something was up there they weren't meant to see. Where the windows were real glass but wouldn't open, and beneath the paint of the sills I found, with a fingertip and a scratch of my nail, the ghost-lines of a containment lattice. Old work. Crude work. Someone had built this prison fast, from a template, and templates have seams.

And the guests — the guests I learned too. There were eleven of them when I arrived. By the second night there were ten. No one mentioned the missing man; his room was simply refreshed, the bed remade, the wine restocked, as if hospitality could be rolled forward over absence like a carpet over a stain. On the third night I asked the silver-haired steward, lightly, over apricots, where the gentleman in the blue coat had gone.

"He found what he was looking for," the steward said, smiling.

"What was he looking for?"

"Himself, my lady. They all come looking for themselves." He refilled my cup. "It's remarkable how often they find him instead."

I drank the wine because refusing it would have been a tell, and spent the next hour in my assigned room — luxurious, windowless, with a bed like a cloud and a mirror I did not look into after the first night, when I'd glanced at it and seen, just for a heartbeat, a hand moving in the glass that was not quite my hand — pressing my ear to walls, counting steps between doors, building the prison inside my head where the sigil couldn't redecorate.

Because that was the other thing. Every hour, the song grew bolder. It stopped being ambient and started being conversational. It knew things. It knew my master's name before I'd spoken it aloud in this house. It knew about the forge raid, about the ghost-notes, about the rite needing a willing vessel — and it purred that last one at me like a cat presenting a dead bird. *A vessel. How useful you are, little smith. How perfectly you fit.*

On the third night, lying in a bed worth a year's wages, staring at a ceiling painted with constellations — fake stars, painted ones, the Regent's little joke, giving his prisoners a sky he'd stolen — I wrote back.

Not with ink. With the trick Corvane taught me and I swore I'd never use on anything alive: you don't push a signal through a living ward, you *ask* it, politely, in its own grammar. So I lay flat on my back, put both palms against the mattress, and asked the lattice under the floorboards to carry a message the way you'd ask a river to carry a leaf.

*East stair. Third landing. Cage room. Two guards, one asleep. Key on the fat one's belt. Window won't matter — the seam in the lattice runs behind the mirror on the landing. Come through there. Hurry. It knows you're coming.*

The last line I wrote because it was true, and because some stubborn scrap of me refused to let the thing in my blood have the last word.

The song laughed. I felt it laugh — a vibration behind my sternum, delighted, indulgent, like a parent watching a child stack blocks it intends to kick over.

*Let them come,* it sang. *Everyone comes to me eventually. Even kings. Especially kings.*

I lay there in the gilded dark and hated it with the cleanest hate I have ever felt, hate like a whetstone, hate with an edge on it. Because here is the thing about violation that nobody warns you: it doesn't just hurt. It teaches you the exact shape of yourself by showing you what someone else is doing with it. Every word the sigil spoke through my mouth, every letter it wrote with my hand, was a lesson in what it meant that my body was mine — a lesson written by its theft.

Corvane used to say a ward-smith's first duty was to know which side of the line she stood on.

I stood on mine. I pressed my palms flat to the bed and started drawing the counter-lattice in my head, thread by thread, and waited for the sound of a thief at the mirror.

* * *

He came through the glass the way he came through everything: apologetically, and leaving it broken anyway.

"One of these days," Rosalind whispered, brushing silvered dust off his sleeves, "someone is going to teach me a subtler way into buildings, and I'm going to be terrible at it."

"Quiet." I pulled him down behind the landing rail. Below us, the cage room's door stood open on lamplight, and there — gods, there — was Quill.

She was in a cage sized for a hunting hawk, iron bar-work laced with hair-fine wire that I recognized even from twenty feet away: resonance filament, tuned to disrupt whatever a creature used instead of a voice. For a crow who talked, it was a gag woven from mathematics. She sat utterly still on her perch, feathers dull, head tucked — playing dead, or close enough — and the sight of her did something to my chest that I decided firmly was rage and not relief.

Two guards below, as promised. One awake, one nodding over his spear. The fat one's keys hung at his belt like a promise.

"Where's Odile?" I breathed.

"Being a diversion of historically significant proportions. There's a fire in the kitchens that she wants noted, for posterity, was entirely accidental." He passed me the sliver of pick-iron he always carried, handle-first, the way he handed people knives — carefully, like trust. "Can you do the wires?"

"I can do the wires." I was already working the lock picks down my sleeve into my palm. "Can you do the guards?"

"I can do the guards," he said, with the confidence of a man who had absolutely no plan for the guards, "in a manner of speaking."

We went down the stairs like a rumor.

Here's what I remember of the next four minutes, and I remember all of it, because a smith learns to keep the record of every failure:

I dropped the resonance filament in nine seconds. Nine. I want that remembered too, because it was beautiful work, done shaking, done with the sigil shrieking inside my skull — because oh, yes, the moment my tools touched Quill's cage, the thing wearing my blood understood exactly what I was doing, and it stopped purring and started screaming. Not out loud. Inwardly. A pressure like deep water, like a boot on my chest from the inside, and through it, words in Dominic's cadence but none of Dominic's warmth: *Traitor. Little smith. I wrote your name in my book of useful things and you are tearing the page.*

"Almost," I gasped, and cut the ninth thread, and the filament died with a sound like a sigh.

Quill's head came up. Her eyes — black, ancient, appalled — fixed on me, and her voice, when it scraped out of her rusty unused throat, was the worst thing I heard all night, and I include the sigil's scream in that ranking.

"Child," she croaked, "you shouldn't have come. It's not talking *through* you anymore."

The waking guard chose that moment to stop being asleep, which was almost merciful. Rosalind took him down with a wine bottle to the temple, elegantly, the way he does everything he hasn't planned, and the fat guard woke properly and grabbed for his whistle, and I hit him with the tray of apricots I had, in an act of pure improvisation, brought along for reasons I still cannot justify. He went down under a rain of fruit. The whistle clattered away under the cage.

"Get her out," I said, fumbling the cage latch with hands that had begun, very slightly, not to feel like mine. My left index finger was twitching. Writing-twitch. The twitch it had developed during Tam's Whispering Week, except I was Tam, and the week had never ended, and —

"TAM." Rosalind had my shoulders. When had he crossed the room. His face was very close, gray in the lamplight, eyes sharp as a pick. "Stay with me. Whatever it's saying — it's lying. That's the one thing we know about it. It lies."

"It doesn't lie," I heard myself say, in a voice with too much calm in it. "That's the horror of it. It tells you true things in the worst possible order—"

Quill hit the window latch with her beak. The casement banged open onto the pleasure quarter's lantern-glow, and cold air poured in like a blessing. "Out," she rasped. "Both of you. Now. Argue on the roof."

Rosalind hauled me toward the window, and I went — I went, remember that, I chose to go, my legs were mine, my choice was mine — but at the sill I looked back, one heartbeat, down into the beautiful room with its drowned-happy guests and its painted stars, and the sigil spoke one last time, directly, intimately, without any anger at all, which was somehow the cruelest register it owned:

*See you at the threshold, little vessel. Wear something nice.*

Then we were out, three shadows on a pleasure-quarter roofline, Quill riding my shoulder with her claws in tight, and behind us the Gilded Prison glowed like a lamp with nothing inside it but light.

"Odile's meeting us at the tannery," Rosalind panted. "We have the shards' location from your note — the key-room, third landing — we go back for them tomorrow, we—"

"No," said Quill, from my shoulder, in a voice like a blade being drawn slowly.

We both stopped on the ridge of the roof. Below us the false stars of the prison's ceiling glimmered through the skylight we'd left open.

"Child," Quill said, and it took me a moment to realize she meant me, not Rosalind, "when you cut the filament, the cage didn't just open. It rang. Nine threads, tuned true — you struck them like a chord. And everything bound to the sigil within a league heard it." She shifted her grip on my shoulder, gentle as an apology. "Including the hounds. Including *him*. We didn't rescue you tonight, Tam. We confirmed you."

The wind off the starless sky moved over us, cold and empty as a swept floor. Somewhere below, faint and sweet and horribly patient, the song began again — not in my bones this time, but in the city itself, rising from the palace district like heat off stones.

And in the rhythm of it, unmistakable now that I'd learned its grammar, it was counting. Downward.
