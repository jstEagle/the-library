## 17. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The safehouse Odile had chosen sat above a shuttered dyeworks in the Tanners' Quarter, which meant we smelled like old blood and vinegar for three days before anyone said anything about it. Tam said something first. She had a gift for saying things first and regretting them second.

"It's strategic," Odile told her, not looking up from the map she was ink-dragging across a scarred table. "Nobody with power comes to the Tanners' Quarter unless they're buying silence or selling it."

"And we're doing which?"

"Both, ideally."

I liked Odile's honesty. It was the kind of honesty you could lean against without worrying it would tip over into comfort.

We'd been in the capital four days, and the capital was wrong in a way I didn't have vocabulary for yet. I'd grown up in a valley that ate memories, so I knew what forgetting looked like from the inside — the soft edges, the polite blankness, the way people smiled at you because they couldn't remember why they shouldn't. This city wasn't soft. This city remembered everything and had decided to be afraid of all of it. Every lantern burned behind shuttered glass. Every door had a ward-mark chalked over the lintel, most of them botched by people who'd learned the shapes from their grandmothers' grandmothers and understood none of them. And everywhere, on every wall, every fountain, every coin, was the same face: young, golden, serene, wearing a crown that looked less like jewelry and more like a lid.

The Regent's face.

"He's beautiful," Tam said the first time she saw a fresco of him, in the way you might say *the well water tastes sweet* right before the fever starts.

"He's propaganda," Odile said.

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

They were both right, as it turned out. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

On the fourth night, Quill woke me by standing on my chest and pecking gently at my collarbone, which is crow for *get up, something is about to happen, and I have elected you to witness it.*

"I was having a good dream," I lied. I hadn't dreamed since the valley died. I hadn't decided whether that was grief or relief.

"You were lying awake listening to Tam talk in her sleep," Quill said. "Again. In the other handwriting."

That shut me up. Tam had been writing in Dominic's hand for two nights now — small, careful script that flowed out of her while she slept, filling the margins of Odile's maps with fragments. *The thirteenth turn.* *The lighthouse keeps what the lover kept.* *Ask the thief; he carries more than he knows.* We'd transcribed everything and understood almost none of it, but the last line had a way of following me around the safehouse like a smell.

I dressed in the dark, took the box — always the box, the locked thing the dying stranger had pressed into my hands on the night the stars went out — and followed Quill up through the rafters to a trapdoor that opened onto the dyeworks roof.

The roof was a country unto itself. Drying racks stood in ranks like gallows, hung with cloth gone stiff and colorless. Beyond them, the city fell away in terraces toward the palace district, where the lanterns burned brightest and the fear wore better clothes.

"Why are we here?" I asked.

Quill hopped along a drying rack, feathers slicked flat against the wind. "Because tonight the Regent rides to the Lantern Vigil. Once a year. He shows himself to the city so they remember what they're afraid of looks kind." She paused. "And because I need to see him. I haven't seen him since—" Her head tilted, that bird-gesture that meant she was deciding how much truth to spend. "Since before."

"Before what?"

"Before I helped put him in a box," she said, and then, quickly, before I could arrange my face: "There. The Processional Road. Look."

* * *

You hear the procession before you see it. Not drums — the Regent's parade has no music, which is somehow worse — but the sound of ten thousand people being quiet at once. A held breath stretched across a whole city. Then the lanterns along the Processional Road went out one by one, west to east, like a fuse burning backward, and into that engineered darkness came the palanquin.

It was carried by twelve bearers in silver-gray, moving with a smoothness that no twelve human legs should manage in unison. It was open-sided — no curtains, no guards pressing close, because what did the Regent of a starless kingdom have to fear? — and lit from within by candles that gave off light the color of an old bruise.

And in it, reclining as if borne on a fainting couch rather than paraded through a terrified city, was the most beautiful man I had ever seen.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had left. I had spent two days inside Dominic's coronation memory, retrieved from a drowned chapel with my lungs full of floodwater and someone else's joy. I knew that face. I knew the exact way the light caught his cheekbone when he turned to receive the crown. I knew the nervous swallow before his oath, the laugh he tried to smother when the archbishop's mitre slipped, the moment his eyes found someone in the crowd — a small dark bird on a bannerpole, though I hadn't known it then — and softened. I had felt that face smile from the inside. I had worn it like a coat myself, for eleven minutes underwater.

So I can tell you with authority: the man in the palanquin was identical to King Dominic Aurelian Valcrest, down to the mole beneath the left ear, down to the slight asymmetry of the brows, down to the laugh lines — the *laugh lines*, preserved in amber, on a face that had never laughed.

And it was wrong. Completely, catastrophically wrong, in the way a wax figure is wrong, in the way a portrait's eyes are wrong if you stare long enough. The proportions were right. The stillness wasn't. Dominic — even in memory, even filtered through candlelight and floodwater — had moved like a man who occupied his body loosely, generously, as though it were a house he kept meaning to tidy. The thing in the palanquin wore its body like a garrison wears a fortress. Nothing shifted. When it turned its head to acknowledge the crowd, the neck rotated and the shoulders stayed perfectly square, and the crowd flinched as one animal, and the thing smiled at their flinching with Dominic's mouth, and I understood for the first time in my life why our ancestors had invented the word *uncanny* instead of just saying *wrong.*

"That's him," I said. My voice came out flattened, pressed thin. "That's the face from the coronation. That's—"

"Yes."

"But it isn't."

"No," Quill said.

I gripped the edge of the drying rack until my knuckles ached against cold iron. Below, the palanquin drifted past a knot of children holding paper lanterns — some festival tradition, some small defiance — and the Regent extended one hand, graceful, benignant, and one of the children stepped forward to present her little flame to him, and I watched Dominic's beautiful stolen face bend down toward her with an expression of such perfect warmth that my stomach turned over, because I had *felt* that expression happen, had been inside it, had known what it cost and what it meant, and this thing was performing it note-perfect with nothing behind it at all. An echo of an echo. A memory of kindness, replayed by something that had eaten the original.

"The sigil," I said slowly. "Vess said the king was bound into a sigil. A crown-sigil." I looked at Quill. "If the usurper wears the king like a coat—"

"Then everything the coat remembers," Quill said, "it can wear too."

The child had reached the palanquin. The Regent took her paper lantern in both hands — Dominic's hands, elegant, ringed, *right* — and cupped its little flame, and for one heartbeat the bruise-colored candlelight inside the palanquin flickered gold, and the crowd made a sound like a sigh, and I wanted to scream at them, *that's not mercy, that's mimicry, it learned that gesture from a dead man's muscle memory—*

And then the Regent looked up.

Directly up. Directly at the roof of the dyeworks. Across forty yards of darkness, past the ranks of empty racks, into my eyes.

I know how that sounds. I know what you're thinking: coincidence, the bird moved, the moonlight caught something. There was no moon. The sky over the capital was a black ceiling, starless, seamless, and the only light was the palanquin's own bruised glow, and the Regent's gaze came up like a hook on a line and caught me exactly, precisely, personally, and held.

Its eyes were Dominic's eyes and there was nothing in them. No, worse than nothing. Nothing would have been restful. There was *attention* in them — vast, patient, hungry attention, the attention of something that had been looking for a particular shape in the world and had just found it sitting on a roof in the Tanners' Quarter holding a locked box.

It knew the box. I would swear that on whatever's left sacred in a kingdom without stars. It looked at me, and it looked at what I was carrying, and its borrowed mouth curved — not into the public smile, but something smaller, private, proprietary, the smile of a collector spotting a piece that had wandered off his shelf.

Then it raised one hand — not to the crowd, not in blessing — and beckoned.

A single curl of one finger. Come here.

The child's lantern went out in its hand. All down the Processional Road, every relit lantern guttered at once, and the crowd gasped and knelt, thinking it majesty, thinking it mystery, and the palanquin resumed its glide, and the face turned away from me as smoothly as it had turned toward me, and the breath of ten thousand people resumed.

I discovered I was on my knees on the roof. I don't remember getting there. Quill was pressed against my shoulder, feathers puffed to twice her size, and she was making a sound I'd never heard from her — low, continuous, a rattle deep in the crop, the sound of a very old bird remembering a very bad thing.

"Rosalind," she said. "Rosalind, breathe."

"It saw me."

"It saw the shards. The resonance. You carry pieces of him, and the crown-thing—" She stopped. Started again, plainer, the way she spoke when she was frightened enough to drop the poetry. "It's been searching for those pieces since the binding. Vess was collecting your sold memories to track them. And now it's seen the largest piece of all walking around in its own skin, carrying the box, and it *beckoned*, Rosalind. It doesn't beckon at strangers. It beckons at belongings."

"Charming." My voice shook. I let it. Nobody up there but a crow. "So what do we do?"

"We get off this roof."

"We get off this roof," I agreed.

But I didn't move yet, because below us, trailing the palanquin at a respectful distance, walking alone in the gutted lantern-light, came a figure I knew: narrow shoulders, ink-stained fingers, the walk of someone who'd learned to make herself invisible beside important people and never quite unlearned it.

Vess.

He wasn't supposed to be here. He was supposed to be three cities south, per his own ledger, buying grief off a widow in Marrowford. Instead he walked in the Regent's wake like a dog that knows exactly how long its leash is, and halfway down the Processional Road he stopped, turned, and looked up at our roof — not searching, not guessing. Looking directly at us, the way you look at an appointment you've kept.

And he touched two fingers to his lips, and blew me a kiss.

Then he turned and followed his master into the dark, and Quill said, very quietly, "Now we get off the roof," and this time I moved.

* * *

Tam met us at the trapdoor, wide-eyed, holding a page of sleep-writing like it was on fire. Which, given what it said, maybe it was.

"He wrote something new," she whispered. "An hour ago. I couldn't wake up, I just watched my hand do it." She thrust the page at me. In that careful, flowing hand that wasn't hers:

*He has seen you. Do not run along the roads he paved. The thirteenth turn is coming. Trust the crow. Trust the girl. And thief — trust yourself, even now. Especially now.*

Below us, muffled by floorboards, Odile's voice rose from the map room, sharp with alarm, calling our names.

The hunt had changed shape. Until tonight it had been a search. Now it was an introduction.

"He knows I'm coming," I said.

Tam looked at the page, then at me, and did the bravest thing I'd seen yet: she nodded like it was good news. "Then he also knows," she said, "you're bringing him home."

Somewhere across the city, in a palace built around a hollow man, a bell began to toll — thirteen times, slow as a funeral, though no one in the capital had ever explained why thirteen.
