## 18. Quill's Confession

The crow had been sulking for three days.

I want to be precise about this, because "sulking" is not a word most people would apply to a bird the size of a loaf of bread with opinions the size of a cathedral. But I had spent enough nights sharing a fire with Quill to know her moods the way a sailor knows weather. There was her *hunting* silence, all attention and appetite. There was her *thinking* silence, which came with a particular tilt of the head, like a scholar reviewing a difficult text. And then there was this: the flat, deliberate, pointed silence of someone who has something to say and is waiting for you to be worthy of hearing it.

She sat on the broken lintel of the safehouse doorway, feathers puffed against the cold, watching me sort Odile's maps by candlelight. She did not sleep. Crows do, I've since learned — they tuck their beaks under one wing and become small, absurd bundles of trust. Quill hadn't done that since we'd left the drowned chapel. Since the second shard. Since the war room.

Since she'd seen what Dominic had ordered built in that valley, and understood it before any of us did.

"You could just say it," I told her, without looking up. "Whatever it is. You've been rehearsing it so long you've worn a groove in the air."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below us, Tam was asleep in the loft, still writing in her sleep sometimes, letters crawling across scraps of parchment in a hand that wasn't hers. Odile was at her table in the map room, redrawing coastlines from memory of a sky that no longer existed. The safehouse breathed around us, an old smuggler's warren of stone and secrets, and outside it the starless dark pressed against every window like a face at the glass.

Quill hopped down from the lintel. Landed on the table. Walked through my map of the eastern passes with the total disregard for cartography that only crows and generals possess, and stopped directly in front of me.

"I helped make it," she said.

I looked up. "Make what?"

"The binding." Her voice — and I will never get used to a crow having a voice, low and dry as paper turning — did not waver. That was the worst part. She'd practiced until it couldn't. "The sigil. The thing around his neck. Around *his* neck, wearing his face. I helped forge it."

The candle flame stood very still, the way flames do when they're pretending not to listen.

* * *

Let me back up, because I have learned that confessions only land properly if you know what ground they're falling on.

Three days earlier, in the general's tomb, I had held the second shard against my palm and fallen into Dominic's memory the way you fall into deep water — all at once, and not gently. I had been the king. Not watched him; *been* him, from inside, the way I'd been him once before at his coronation. And in that memory, Dominic — young, crowned barely two years, already tired in a way kings are never allowed to show — had stood over a table covered in ward-diagrams and given the order.

*"Build the valley,"* he said. *"Seal it. Let nothing remember inside its borders."*

We had all assumed, after that, that the story had turned. That Dominic had built the prison himself. That the thing wearing his crown had perhaps merely inherited a cage designed for some other purpose, or worse, that our whole quest was a fool's errand — carrying home a man who had chosen his own disappearance. Odile had argued for turning back. I had refused. We were fractured over it still, politely, the way families fracture — everyone setting the table together and nobody meeting anyone's eyes.

And through all of it, Quill had gone quiet.

Not crow-quiet. *Guilty*-quiet. I knew guilty. I had spent my whole life in a valley that manufactured guilt out of stolen memories; I could smell it on a person like rain coming. And for three days the crow who talked in her sleep — actual dreams, actual muttering, I'd heard her argue with someone named Corvess about the proper angle for a ward-anchor — had sat apart from the fire, preening the same wing over and over, and said almost nothing.

So when she finally spoke, standing on my map with her head lowered like a penitent, part of me was not surprised.

Part of me. The other part felt the floor tilt.

"Start from the beginning," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

Quill ruffled her feathers. Settled. Began.

* * *

"I was hatched in the palace mews," she said, "which is a sentence that means more than it sounds like. The royal mews wasn't for falcons, whatever the tour-guides told visiting merchants. It was for *listeners*. Birds bred and bound to carry sound. A whisper spoken in the western tower could be heard, word for word, by whoever wore the listening-charm three miles away. Every court in the world does something like it, they just lie about it less prettily. We were called the Crown's Ears, which is flattering if you don't think about it, and I didn't, because I was young, and being useful is its own kind of flattery."

She paused. I fed the candle another inch of wick. Outside, wind moved through the dead orchard behind the safehouse, and the bare branches clicked like counting beads.

"Dominic was different from his father. His father collected whispers the way misers collect coins — hoarded them, spent them on grudges. Dominic mostly ignored them. He'd come into the mews sometimes, late, when he couldn't sleep, and talk to us. Not *through* us. To us. He thought we couldn't understand him. He'd sit on the floor among the perches with his crown set aside — he hated the weight of it, he said it gave him headaches, though he'd never admit that publicly — and he'd talk about whatever was eating him. The border lords. The treasury. A woman he was writing letters to and never sending." She paused again, and something moved behind her eye, black and bright and old. "He talked about fear. Someone was poisoning people close to him. His chamberlain first. Then his Master of Coin. Both within a season. Both deaths ruled natural. He didn't believe it, and he was right not to, because it wasn't poison, and it wasn't natural, and it wasn't finished."

"What was it?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd seen its face from a rooftop in the capital, identical to the king's and wrong in every particular.

"We didn't know. That was the horror of it. Something was hollowing out his court from the inside, wearing people, and the only person it hadn't touched was Dominic himself — which, we understood later, was because it *needed* him untouched. Whole. You can't wear a coat you've torn to rags." Her voice flattened further. "So he came to us. Or rather, he came to the mews, and I was the one who answered, because I was the best listener they had, and because — " here her composure cracked, just once, a hairline fracture — "because I loved him. In the way birds love. Which is absolutely, and completely, and without any sense whatsoever of proportion."

I said nothing. The candle burned.

"There was a smith," she went on. "A ward-smith, the finest alive. Tam's master — yes, *that* master, the one who vanished, and don't think I haven't noticed how that thread keeps knotting itself back into this story. He came to the palace in secret, brought in through the mews because it was the one entrance nobody watched. He and Dominic and a handful of us — the listeners, the ones Dominic trusted because we couldn't be worn, couldn't be replaced, couldn't lie even if we wanted to — we planned it over eleven nights. The assassination attempts weren't going to stop. Whoever — whatever — was doing it would keep hollowing its way inward until there was nothing left of the court but itself wearing Dominic's friends like gloves. So Dominic decided to become impossible to kill."

"The binding," I said.

"The binding." She nodded, slow, each dip of her head like a small bow. "The sigil wasn't a cage. That's what none of you understand yet, and what I should have made you understand sooner, and what I couldn't say because saying it meant admitting everything else. The sigil was a *vault*. It took the essential Dominic — his self, his name, his true memory, the irreducible core of what made him *him* — and locked it somewhere no blade could reach, no usurper could corrupt, no poison could touch. What remained walking around in his body afterward was — " she searched for the word, found it, hated it — "*bait*. A shell with enough of the surface-Dominic left in it to seem worth killing, and nothing inside worth destroying. The plan was that the crown-thing would take the bait. Would seize the throne, put on the body like a coat, exactly as it had done to the chamberlain and the Master of Coin. And then it would discover that the coat was empty. That the thing it needed — the living core of the rightful king, the one anchor its existence required — was sealed beyond its reach, in a sigil carried by a bird who would fly it somewhere no one would ever find."

My mouth had gone dry. "You were supposed to carry it away."

"I was supposed to carry it away," Quill agreed. "To the valley. Which wasn't a prison, Rosalind. It was a *vault*. Dominic built it — ordered it built, yes, that shard showed you true — to hide something. Himself. The valley's wards didn't erase memories because he wanted prisoners who forgot. They erased memories because a vault full of remembering things is a vault that can be interrogated. Forgetting was the lock. I was to fly in, hide the sigil in the deep wards where even the valley's own magic couldn't read it, and wait. Days. Months. However long it took for loyalists outside to destroy the crown-thing and unbind him."

"And instead?"

"And instead," Quill said, "everything went wrong in the order things go wrong when clever people build plans with too many moving parts. The unbinding rite required the original forge — the ward-smithy. The smith vanished. Whether he ran, or was taken, or *chose* to vanish, I don't know, and I've had years to wonder. Without him, no rite. Without the rite, no unbinding. The loyalists who were supposed to storm the palace scattered or died or forgot whose side they were on, because that's what a kingdom does when its stars start going out — it forgets it ever had sides. And the sigil stayed hidden in the valley, in the deepest ward, and the crown-thing sat on the throne wearing its empty coat, and it could not die, and it could not live properly, and neither could he." She lifted her head. "And I stayed. Because I was the only one who knew where it was hidden, and leaving felt like abandoning him twice."

"How long?"

"Forty-one years," said the crow.

The number sat between us. Forty-one years. Longer than I had been alive, though "alive" was becoming a complicated word where I was concerned. Forty-one years of a bird keeping vigil over a locked box in a valley that forgot everything, including — eventually, I realized, with a lurch — her.

"That's why you can smell his memories," I said slowly. "That's why you found me. You didn't stumble onto a thief who happened to be tangled up in the king's past. You've been *searching*."

"I've been searching since the wards fell," she said. "When the valley broke open, the sigil's hiding place broke with it, and the shards — the pieces of him the vault shed over the decades, like a wound weeping — they scattered along the roads. I followed them. And every trail led back to you. The coronation memory you sold Vess. The way the shards resonate when you're near. The way you *knew things*, thief, things you shouldn't know, directions you shouldn't feel." Her head tilted, and for the first time that night her voice lost its rehearsed steadiness and became simply tired. "I told myself it was coincidence. Birds are good at telling themselves things. We have to be; we live too long to afford honesty."

"But you don't believe it's coincidence anymore."

"No," Quill said. "I don't."

* * *

I got up then. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply informed me we were done sitting. I walked to the window, where the starless dark pressed its face against the glass, and I stood there with my arms crossed like a man guarding a door that had already been opened.

Because here is what was roaring in my skull, and I'll render it honestly even though it shames me: I wasn't thinking about Dominic. I was thinking about *me*.

A fragment cut loose and made flesh. That was what Quill suspected — she hadn't said it outright, not yet, but it hung off every sentence like a loose thread begging to be pulled. If the vault had wept shards along the roads for forty years, and if every shard resonated near me, and if I remembered nothing of my own life before the valley — well. I had spent chapters' worth of nights telling myself I was a thief with a bad memory. It turns out I might have been the *memory*.

But that revelation belonged to a later hour. This hour belonged to the bird.

"Why tell me now?" I asked the window. "Why not on the road? Why not the night the stranger died, or at the chapel, or any of the hundred moments when lying would have been easier?"

Behind me, wings beat once — the short hop from table to chair-back to the shelf beside the window, her traveling path, the route she'd mapped across every room we'd sheltered in. She landed at my shoulder height, close enough that I could see the frost of her breath, because apparently crows breathe visibly when the truth costs them enough.

"Because of the tomb," she said. "Because you saw him order the valley built, and you thought he built a prison, and Odile wanted to turn back, and you kept going anyway, and I let you all walk forward on a false map because correcting it meant showing you mine." Her feathers tightened against her body. "A confession made to protect yourself isn't a confession. It's a transaction. I've watched you make those your whole life, thief, and I promised myself I wouldn't do it too. So I waited until it cost me something. Until you might look at me the way you look at Vess."

I turned around. She held my gaze, which crows can do longer than people, and which she did now without blinking, as if blinking might let the moment escape.

"Does he know?" I asked. "Dominic. In the shards. Does he know what you did?"

"He knows I carried him to the valley. That much survives in the fragments — he remembers falling, and flying, and dark. Whether he knows I helped *forge* the thing he's locked inside..." She shook her head, a strange gesture on a bird, too human. "I've had forty-one years to imagine his face when he finds out. I've imagined it angry. I've imagined it worse than angry. I've imagined it *understanding*, which is somehow the worst of the three, because understanding means he'd know I did it out of love, and then he'd have to decide what to do with a love that imprisoned him."

"And what do you want me to say?" I asked. "Truly. Not the diplomatic answer. You've heard me bargain; you know I can smell a diplomatic answer at fifty paces."

What I expected was deflection. What I expected was the crow equivalent of *whatever you can give me*. What I got was:

"I want you to say you don't forgive me. Yet. And that you might someday, and that you'll tell me honestly when you do or don't." Her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Half-forgiveness is the only honest coin I have left, and I'd rather hold half of yours than a whole forged one."

I laughed. I couldn't help it — a short, cracked sound, entirely inappropriate, and Quill's head snapped around with genuine offense until I raised a hand.

"You realize," I said, "that you just described my entire profession. Half-truths sold as honest coin. I built a life on it. And here you are, a bird older than my whole existence, offering me the same currency and calling it virtue."

"It's not the same."

"No," I admitted. "It's not. Because you're asking me to hold the debt, not pay it. That's different. That's—" I stopped, because the word arriving in my head was *trust*, and trust was a thing I had stolen descriptions of but never owned.

I looked at her for a long time. The candle burned down another ring. Below us, faintly, Tam murmured in her sleep — and the murmur had cadence, structure, the rhythm of dictation, as though someone far away were reading aloud into her ear. Neither of us commented on it. Some horrors you agree silently not to look at directly.

"Here's what I can give you tonight," I said finally. "I believe you loved him. I believe you thought you were saving him. I believe the plan was better than most plans, which is damning praise, because most plans are garbage. And I believe that forty-one years of vigil is a payment no court in any kingdom could levy." I held up a finger before hope could finish forming in her one visible expression. "And I am *furious* with you. Not for the binding. For the silence. For letting us march toward the thirteenth turn carrying a map you'd already read and hadn't shown us. Forgiveness for the first thing might come. Forgiveness for the second is going to have to be earned on the road, in front of me, one honest word at a time."

Quill was quiet for a moment. Then she dipped her head — not the penitent's bow from before, but something smaller and realer, the nod of one professional acknowledging another's terms.

"One honest word at a time," she repeated. "I can afford those. Barely."

"Good. Then start now." I crossed back to the table and pulled the ledger toward me — Vess's ledger, the one we'd recovered, the one whose entries had begun to look less like bookkeeping and more like a hunt. "If the sigil was a vault, then the crown-thing has spent forty-one years looking for it. And if Vess has been buying memories connected to the shards — my memories, specifically — then either Vess is the luckiest broker alive, or someone has been using him as a net." I tapped the page. "Look at this entry. Dated six years ago. 'Coronation fragment, provenance uncertain, purchased from a traveler.' Six years ago, Quill. Before I ever met Vess. Someone was collecting pieces of Dominic while I was still in the valley stealing pocket-watches and first kisses."

Quill hopped to the table. Read. I watched her go very still, in the specific way of a creature remembering something it had chosen to forget.

"That purchase date," she said quietly. "Six years ago. That's not when the collector started hunting, thief. That's when the collector started *finding*." She looked up at me, and her eyes reflected the candleflame like two small dark moons. "There's a mark next to that entry. Do you see it? A circle with thirteen points."

I saw it. Of course I saw it. I'd been staring at it for days, telling myself it was a merchant's stamp, a printer's ornament, anything other than what it obviously was.

"The thirteenth turn," I said.

"The thirteenth turn," Quill agreed. "Which means Vess isn't just a broker who bought the wrong memory once. Which means the net has a weaver. And which means, Rosalind—" she spread her wings, not to fly, but the way people spread their hands when the news is too large for a still posture— "someone has known about the shards, and the turns, and possibly about *you*, since before any of us knew there was a quest. We haven't been following the ledger."

She tapped the thirteen-pointed mark with her beak, once, precisely.

"The ledger has been following us."
