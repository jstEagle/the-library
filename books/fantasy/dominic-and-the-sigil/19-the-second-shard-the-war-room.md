## 19. The Second Shard: The War Room

The general's tomb had been sealed for eleven years, which meant the lock was older than Tam and twice as stubborn.

"It's not locked," Tam said, for the fourth time, crouched beside the bronze door with her sleeves shoved to the elbow. "It's *listening*. There's a ward on it that waits for someone to try the handle, and then it decides whether you're worth opening for."

"And what did it decide about me?"

"That you're a thief with king's blood in your pocket." She tapped the satchel where the first shard slept in its wool wrapping. "The door likes that. Doors always respect a good burglary."

I decided not to examine too closely why a dead general's tomb would respect burglary, mostly because the answer was going to be *because he stole everything he was buried with*, and we'd already walked three days through starless hill country to get here. Retreating felt worse than ignorance. Ignorance at least came with scenery.

Quill perched on my shoulder and made the low croaking sound she used when she wanted to say something and had decided against it. She'd been making it since dawn.

"You have an opinion," I said.

"I have several opinions. You've heard most of them."

"The one you're swallowing now."

She preened my hair with her beak — affection or grooming, hard to say with Quill, possibly both, possibly neither. "General Marrec Vane," she said at last. "He built the border wards. The outer ones. The ones your valley wore like a coat."

"You knew him?"

"I knew his handwriting. He signed the requisitions for the binding forge. Ink the color of dried blood; the man had no imagination on paper and all of it in stone." A pause, wings shifting. "If anyone's tomb holds a piece of Dominic, it's the man who helped bury the sky."

That was the other reason none of us had spoken much in three days. Burying the sky. The stars hadn't come back. We'd left the valley expecting the dark to be temporary — a wound, not a condition — and every night since, the black above us had been total, patient, and close, like the underside of a lid.

Odile said the stars weren't destroyed. They were *withheld*, which is a cartographer's word for *stolen by someone who intends to keep them*, and she'd started saying it in the tone of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like.

* *

The door opened for me the way Tam promised it might: grudgingly, with a sound like an old man clearing his throat before delivering bad news. Inside, the tomb smelled of cold stone and old tallow and something faintly resinous — preservative salts, maybe, or just the particular loneliness of places nobody has visited on purpose in a decade.

Marrec Vane lay in state on a granite bier, carved in marble at half again life size, because generals are like fish in that regard. His effigy's hands were folded over a sword, and his face wore the expression of a man listening to a report he intends to disbelieve.

"Charming fellow," I said.

"He held the northern passes for thirty years," Odile said quietly. She'd gone still in the doorway, the way she did around anything old and official. "My father served under him. He used to say Vane never lost a battle and never won a friend."

"Efficient."

"Lonely," she corrected, and moved past me into the dark with her lantern held high.

The war room was behind the burial chamber — of course it was; Vane had apparently arranged to spend eternity within shouting distance of his maps. Long table. Sand trays. Little carved markers for armies that had been dust longer than the wood they stood on. And along the far wall, a relief map of the kingdom so detailed I could see the river bend below my own valley, rendered in blue glass.

I found it without meaning to. My hand went to it the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth.

There it was. The valley. Carved shallow, ringed in painted mountains, and set into its center — a tiny disc of white stone, polished smooth, no marker, no label. Every other feature of the map had a name etched beside it. Cities. Forts. Shrines. The white disc alone sat silent, like a word deliberately left out of a sentence.

"Tam," I said. "Come look at what isn't written down."

She came. She looked. Her jaw did the thing it does when she's working out a ward's grammar in her head. "That's a socket," she said finally. "See how the stone's polished? Something sat there once. Something small enough to hold in two hands." She glanced at me, then at the satchel, and I watched the thought land on her face like a bird choosing a branch. "Something sigil-sized."

The room got very quiet. Lantern-light has a way of making silence physical — you can feel it pressing on the back of your neck.

"Open the satchel," Odile said.

* *

Here is what I know about memory-shards, having carried one across half a starless kingdom: they are warm. Not body-warm. Hearth-warm. They hold heat the way a held hand does, and when you unwrap them near another piece of themselves, they hum — a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in whatever part of a person keeps score of things unsaid.

The first shard had shown me Dominic's coronation from inside his skin. I had worn his joy like a coat cut for someone slightly larger than me, and his dread fit worse — a dread he'd carried smiling, all through the cheering, all through the oaths, while the crown settled onto his head with a weight no one else could see.

We set the first shard into the white disc's socket and it fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

The war room woke up.

Not literally. I want to be clear about that, because Tam grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise and Odile swore in three dead languages, and none of us needed to believe we'd raised a ghost. What happened was smaller and stranger: light rose out of the sand trays. Thin, gold, sourceless, pooling along the carved borders of Vane's maps like sunrise remembering its route. And in the air above the great table, an image assembled itself out of nothing but light and insistence — figures, translucent as breath on glass, standing exactly where living men had stood eleven years ago.

Four of them. A crow on the shoulder of the tallest.

"Oh," Quill said. It was the smallest sound I'd ever heard her make.

The figures were already mid-conversation, the way memories are. No preamble. Memory never bothers with doors; it just sets you down in the middle of the room.

Dominic stood at the head of the table, and up close — closer than the coronation, closer than ceremony allowed — I could see he was tired in the specific way of a man who has been carrying a decision for weeks and has finally set it down. Younger than his statues. A scar through one eyebrow that the royal portraitists had all conspired to forget. He wore no crown. That detail snagged in me and wouldn't let go. In his own war room, on the night this happened, the king had come bareheaded.

Vane stood opposite him, arms crossed, marble-jawed even in memory. Two others I didn't know — a woman in smith's leathers with burn scars to the elbow, and a thin scholar whose name I'd later learn was Ellery Voss, though that's a thread for later, and later took its time arriving.

"—can't be killed," Vane was saying, and his voice had gravel and grief in equal measure. "You've tried. We've *tried*. It gets back up wearing whoever's nearest—"

"I know what it does." Dominic's voice was quiet, and quiet, in that room, was louder than shouting. "I was there when it wore my brother."

The woman in leathers looked down. The scholar wrote something and stopped writing.

"So we don't kill it," Dominic went on. "We starve it. It feeds on what people remember — that's the whole architecture of it, Marrec, it lives in the shape other minds give it. Take the memory away, take the worship away, take even the hatred—" he laughed, and there was no humor in it, only exhaustion with a blade's edge, "—take the *name* away, and it has nothing to stand on."

"Then kill everyone who knows it," Vane said flatly. "Is that the order? Say it plainly, if it's the order."

"No." And here Dominic leaned forward, both hands flat on the table, and I felt — actually felt, standing eleven years downstream in a dead man's cellar — the moment the whole shape of my life had been decided. "We build them a valley. One place, warded, where memory comes loose. Anyone who enters forgets what they carried in. Not just the thing's name — everything near it. Its history. Its *host*. We put it at the center under a binding sigil, and we let the forgetting do what steel couldn't."

"To hide it," Vane said slowly.

"To hide it," Dominic agreed. "Not to punish it. Not to imprison anyone." He straightened, and looked at each of them in turn, and his eyes — I knew those eyes, I'd seen them from the inside, and seeing them from outside was like meeting a stranger wearing your own reflection — his eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "No one goes in who doesn't choose to. Wardens volunteer. They serve ten years and leave with their memories intact — the valley takes only what crosses the inner line. And when it's done — when the thing starves down to nothing — we unbind, we take the stars back off its shelf, and we give the valley back to the map. Every soul who served walks out free."

The woman in leathers spoke for the first time. "And if it doesn't starve?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has a price tag.

"Then the valley stays," Dominic said, "and the sigil stays, and I stay with it. That's the cost of the design and I'm the one who designed it. Write that into the founding charter. Underline it."

The image held a moment longer — Vane's face doing arithmetic with his principles, the scholar's pen frozen, the crow on Dominic's shoulder with her head cocked at an angle I recognized, because I'd spent months learning to read it — and then the light guttered, and folded, and was gone. The sand trays went dark. The tomb resumed being a tomb.

Quill didn't move. Quill, who narrates funerals, heckles priests, and once delivered a forty-minute critique of my disguise involving a bishop's hat, said nothing at all.

"Rosalind," Odile began carefully, "the shards — the ones we're collecting —"

"Are his memories," I finished. "Scattered when the binding went in. Pieces of the king, cut loose so the sigil could hold what was left." I looked at my hands. They were steady, which felt like a betrayal of something. "So the story we've been telling ourselves — the stolen king, the usurper, the rescue —"

"Is still true," Tam said fiercely. "It's *also* true that he built the cage. Both things are true. People are allowed to be both things."

"Sure," I said. "People. But we aren't rescuing a person, are we? We're unbinding a prison, and the prisoner may have poured the foundations himself. Which makes this less a rescue and more—" I hunted for the word and hated the one I found. "—a *compliance*."

Nobody laughed. It hadn't been a joke, but jokes are how we'd survived nine chapters of this road, and its absence told me more than any argument could.

* *

We camped that night in the tomb's antechamber, because outside the wind had teeth and inside the dead at least kept their opinions to themselves. Tam slept with the shards wrapped against her chest like a hot coal she was smuggling. Odile sat up with her charts, correcting them by lantern-light, striking out roads that led nowhere anymore.

Quill and I took first watch, which meant Quill slept on my knee and I stared at General Vane's marble frown and conducted the argument I'd been losing since sundown.

Because here was the thing I hadn't said out loud, the thing sitting in me like a swallowed hook:

*I remembered the valley differently.*

Not the facts — the facts matched. Wards, forgetting, a center no one spoke of. But the feeling. All my life in that valley, underneath the theft and the loneliness and the rules I'd made to survive, there had been a texture to the place, a hum beneath the hum, and I had always read it as malice. A trap. A lid screwed down over something screaming.

The memory had given me a new reading, and the new reading was worse in a quieter way: it had felt like *mercy*. Like a hospital built over a wound. Like a king kneeling in the dirt with his hands cupped around something terrible, saying *stay small, stay asleep, I'll wait with you.*

And if the valley was mercy — then the wards falling, the stars going out, the thing walking free in the world wearing a borrowed crown — wasn't a prison breaking.

It was a patient dying of neglect, and we were the ones who'd pulled the nurse away.

"You're doing the face," Quill said, without opening her eyes.

"What face?"

"The one where you argue with yourself and lose on purpose so you can feel principled about losing."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You have four faces like that. I've catalogued them." She opened one eye, black and bright as a dropped coin. "Say the real question."

I looked at her a long time. She'd flown beside Dominic for years, if the shards and her silences were honest, and she'd helped build the binding — she'd admitted that much on the road, in the dark, in a voice I'd pretended not to hear crack. If anyone alive knew whether the king in that memory was a jailer or a martyr or both—

"If he chose it," I said. "If the binding was his plan, his design, his *underlined charter* — then every step we walk toward the threshold, we're walking toward undoing a man's own last act. Odile's right to worry. The moral ground just moved under us and I can't feel which way it slopes."

Quill roused herself, feathers settling, and for a moment she looked less like a crow and more like something old wearing a crow, which happens sometimes and I've learned not to mention it.

"Do you know what he said to me," she said, "the night before they closed the valley? Not in the war room. After. On the wall, in the wind, when everyone else had gone to bed believing in him." She hopped once along my knee. "He said: *Quill, if I ever send you to fetch help — if I ever ask to be pulled out of this — remind me what I built it for.*"

"And did you?"

"I'm flying beside you, aren't I?" She turned her head away, toward the tomb door, toward the starless dark beyond it. "But Rosalind — listen to me, because I've had eleven years to turn that sentence over and I still don't know which end of it is the lie. Either the thing in the crown has grown strong enough to make him ask for rescue—" her voice dropped to nearly nothing, "—or the thing was never starving at all. It was *waiting*. And it built the waiting into his mercy, and he never saw it, because he was kind, and kindness is the one door that thing knows how to knock on."

The lantern guttered. Somewhere in the dark behind the war room door, the first shard hummed in its socket, soft and constant, like a sleeper turning over.

"One more day's walk," I said, "and we reach the drowned chapel where Odile's chart says the second fragment lies." I rubbed my eyes. "One more piece of a man who may have been his own jailer. And then a third after that, if the lighthouse story holds. At some point, Quill, we have to decide whether we're gathering a king or assembling an argument."

"Mm," said Quill, who was already asleep.

I stayed on watch until dawn, which out here was just the dark changing its mind slightly, and I thought about the little white disc on the map with no name beside it, and I wondered — with the specific, cold clarity of three in the morning —

what else the valley had been hiding besides the thing in the crown.

Because the map showed the disc empty. The disc was supposed to hold a sigil.

And the sigil, last I'd seen it, was hanging around the Regent's neck in the capital, worn openly, gleaming, *displayed* —

which meant either the map was wrong,

or the thing wearing Dominic's face had taken the binding off a long time ago,

and everything since had been theater staged for an audience of thieves.
