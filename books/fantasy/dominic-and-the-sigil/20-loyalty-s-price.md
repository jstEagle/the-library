## 20. Loyalty's Price

I have drawn maps of places that no longer exist. Coastlines drowned before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. Cities razed so thoroughly that the stones were ground into mortar for other cities, which were themselves ground in turn. I once charted a river that the crown insisted had never flowed at all — I was exiled for that one, though the river, to its credit, kept flowing.

So believe me when I tell you: I know what it looks like when the truth changes shape under your hands.

It looks like the second shard did, sitting on Odile's table — my table, my safehouse, my kitchen where I had fed these children soup not three weeks past — glowing faintly like a coal refusing to die.

*Dominic ordered the valley built.*

I turned the thought over the way you turn a stone looking for what's underneath. And underneath was this: if the king chose his own prison, then everything we were doing — every mile Rosalind walked, every shard he pulled from floodwater and tomb and lighthouse, every risk Tam carried in her swallowed throat — all of it might be a delivery service for a dead man's suicide.

"You're quiet," Rosalind said. He was watching me from across the table with those thief's eyes that missed nothing, which is the worst quality in a person when you are trying to decide whether to betray them gently.

"I'm thinking," I said.

"That's what worries me. You think like a cartographer. You draw lines and then you expect everyone to stay inside them."

"Lines keep people alive," I said. "Ask anyone who's ever wandered off one."

Quill shifted on the windowsill. The crow had been silent since we'd played the shard — silent in the particular way she went silent when she knew something, which, I had learned, was most of the time. Her feathers caught the shard-light and threw it back wrong, greenish, like old copper.

Tam sat closest to the thing. She always sat closest now. She said it was because her ward-craft let her read its edges without it reading her back, and I believed her, mostly, but there was something else in the way she leaned toward it — the lean of a person toward a voice only they can hear. She had been writing in that hand that wasn't hers again that morning. *The war room smelled of tallow and rain,* she'd written, in a script two hundred years dead. *Father will be furious. Good.*

"The shard doesn't lie," Tam said quietly. "Ward-work can't hold a lie. That's the whole point of binding magic — truth is the mortar. If the memory shows Dominic giving the order—"

"I know how binding works," I snapped, and then, because she flinched and because she is twenty years old and carrying a king's ghost in her belly like a swallowed stone, I softened it: "I taught half the cartographers in this kingdom, girl. I know how records work too. Records lie all the time. They just lie politely."

"This isn't a record," Rosalind said. "It's a memory."

"A memory of what? A room? A decision? We saw him give an order. We didn't see why." I stood, because sitting felt like agreeing. My knees objected; I ignored them the way I ignore most things that complain without offering solutions. "A man orders a valley built to hide something. Fine. What? The shard showed us walls going up and wards being laid and a border drawn like a scar across the land. It didn't show us what was inside them when they closed."

"It showed us enough," Quill said.

Everyone looked at her. The crow rarely spoke plainly unless she'd decided plainness was kinder than silence, and her silences had been getting longer.

"He chose it," she said. "You want me to say it in smaller words, Odile, but there aren't any. Dominic chose the binding. I was there. I helped forge it. I've told you that much already."

"You told us you believed it would save him from assassination. That's a different sentence than *he asked for it.*"

"Is it?" Quill ruffled her feathers, which is a crow's shrug. "From where I was standing, both sentences ended with the same man in the same cage."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about leading exiles: you do not lead them anywhere. You simply stand still long enough in one place that they begin to orbit you, and then one day you realize you are responsible for the weather inside someone else's sky.

I had been a cartographer of the royal court for thirty-one years. I had mapped the kingdom's bones for three kings, and I had loved the work the way you love anything that lets you pretend the world is knowable. Then King Aldric's court decided my maps were more dangerous than useful — because accurate maps show where things are hidden, and hidden things are the currency of courts — and I was given a choice between a cell and a road, and I have never liked cells.

The safehouse was mine. Found it myself, bought it with money I'd salted away in false bottoms of map tubes for a decade, warded it myself with techniques I'd learned from a ward-smith named Merrin Stave — Tam's master, as it happened, though the world is small in the ways that hurt. Every board, every beam, every secret door was mine by right of having imagined it.

And now a thief and a crow and a runaway and a box full of a dead king's memories had moved into it, and I was expected to march them all toward the moonlit threshold because a boy with stolen eyes had decided that truth was worth more than survival.

I understood him. That was the problem. I understood him completely, and understanding has never once stopped me from arguing.

"Say it plainly," Rosalind said. "You want us to stop."

"I want us to *think*," I said. "There's a difference. Stopping is what cowards call thinking when they're tired. Thinking is what careful people call stopping when they're frightened. I am both, tonight, and I'd like the record to reflect that I am choosing the word *think*."

He almost smiled. I saw him fight it down, because he knew I'd take it as victory.

"Odile." He spread his hands over the table, over the shard, over the starlight-inked map the dying stranger had left him — the map I had spent four nights cross-referencing against my charts of missing stars. "If Dominic chose the binding, then the crown-thing didn't steal his throne. It accepted his surrender. And if it accepted his surrender, then unbinding him might be—" He stopped. Even now, even angry, he wouldn't say it cruelly. "Might be the cruelest thing we could do to him."

"Might be," I agreed. "Or might not. That's my entire point. *Might* is not a foundation, boy. You don't build a war on *might*."

"We don't have time for foundations. Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days is plenty of time to be wrong in a new direction."

That got a laugh out of Tam — a short, startled bark of one, quickly smothered, the laugh of a girl who'd learned that laughing in her master's forge could get you burned. But it broke something loose in the room, some of the pressure, and I hated her a little for it and loved her more, which is the general arithmetic of found families.

"Look at it from the outside," I said, and I pulled my chair around so I could draw while I talked, because I have never in my life been able to think without a stylus in my hand. I sketched fast on the back of an old chart: a valley, a border, a sigil. "Two hundred years ago, give or take, a king builds a prison-valley in the middle of nowhere. Inside it, eventually, a memory-thief grows up with no past. Outside it, a crown-thing wears the king's face and rules in his name. Now. Which of those facts serves whom?"

Rosalind frowned at the sketch. "Go on."

"If Dominic wanted to trap the crown-thing — truly trap it, the way the third shard suggested, the way the lover's letter implied — then the valley makes sense as a cage built *around himself*, because the thing can't survive without its host. Bind the host, bind the beast. Yes. Clean. Elegant. I'd sign my name to that design." I tapped the stylus against the paper. "But here's what doesn't fit. If the crown-thing is trapped by the binding, why does it rule? Why does it collect memories through brokers? Why does it send hounds after shards? A prisoner doesn't run the prison."

"Unless the prison is leaking," Tam said.

"Everything leaks," I said. "That's not an answer, that's a maintenance schedule."

Quill made a sound I had learned to read as a crow's version of clearing her throat, which meant she was about to say something she'd been holding onto. "The binding wasn't meant to last two hundred years," she said. "It was meant to last thirteen turns. One cycle. Long enough for the crown-thing to starve, or fade, or find another host willingly — Dominic hoped, near the end, that it might simply... leave. Find a body that wanted it."

"And it didn't leave," Rosalind said.

"It didn't leave. And the thirteenth turn came and went, and the binding held anyway, because Merrin Stave built better than he was paid to. And after that—" Quill's voice did something strange, a crack in a very old wall. "After that, nobody was steering. Not the king. Not the beast. Just the machine, running on its original instructions, forgetting, decade by decade, what it was for."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind worried the eaves of my safehouse, and I thought about all the maps I'd drawn of coastlines that eroded, rivers that shifted, mountains that were older than the idea of kingdoms and would outlast every line anyone drew around them.

"So the valley is a machine with no operator," I said slowly.

"The valley is a wound that healed wrong," Quill said.

* * *

We argued until the candle burned down to a thumb's width and Tam fell asleep sitting up, her head tipping against Rosalind's shoulder with the boneless trust of the young, and he went very still so as not to wake her, and I watched him do it and felt the fracture lines in my resolve deepen.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole rotten heart of the thing.

I had voted for this family. Not formally — we hadn't taken votes yet, though the night was coming when we would, and I dreaded it — but I had chosen them, one by one, the way you choose which broken things to carry home. The thief with no past who stole other people's because his own had been cut out of him. The apprentice whose guilt had calcified into purpose. The crow who had betrayed a king out of love and had been paying interest on it for two centuries. Even the box, even the dead stranger's map, even the shards — I had let them all in past my wards, and my wards, I will have you know, are excellent.

And now the evidence said the mission itself might be a mistake. Might be worse than a mistake — might be a mercy performed as violence. Unbind the king, and perhaps we freed him. Or perhaps we dragged a man out of a sleep he had chosen, tore open the seal he'd set with his own hands, and handed his body back to the thing that had been waiting two hundred years for exactly this.

Turn the shards over to the Regent, Vess had offered, in effect, through every generous price and every careless question. Stop walking, and live.

I had been old for a long time. Old people know that survival is not the same as living, but we also know something the young resist: that sometimes the difference doesn't matter, because the dead don't get to enjoy being right.

"Odile." Rosalind had shifted Tam to the bench behind him without waking her, and now he stood, and in the low light he looked, for a moment, less like a thief and more like something older — a silhouette with a king's posture, though he didn't know it yet, poor lamb. "Tell me what you're actually afraid of. Not the argument. The fear."

I should have lied. I'm good at lying; I once convinced three kings that the world looked the way I drew it. But he had a way of asking questions like he was picking a lock — patiently, respectfully, certain the mechanism would yield — and my mechanisms have always yielded to patience.

"I'm afraid we're carrying him somewhere he didn't ask to go," I said. "I'm afraid that every shard we gather is a brick we're pulling out of a wall he built, and when the wall comes down, whatever the wall was holding back comes down on top of a girl who swallowed a piece of a king to protect it. I'm afraid that loyalty means knowing when to stop following someone." I looked at my own hands, ink-stained, spotted, honest. "I followed three kings, Rosalind. I know how it ends."

"How does it end?"

"With you drawing their borders for them until one day you draw a border they don't like, and then you learn what the inside of a cell looks like, or the inside of a road, which is the same view with worse weather."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "I'm not asking you to follow me."

"No?"

"I'm asking you to look at the map. You're the only one who can read it properly. The stars went out in a pattern, Odile — you said so yourself, the first week, you said it wasn't random, it was *legible*. Someone extinguished the sky in a specific order. And the pattern points at the observatory. At the threshold. At the thirteenth turn." He leaned forward. "If Dominic chose his binding, then someone else chose the darkness. Those are different hands. I want to know whose."

I stared at him.

Because — damn him. Damn him twice. He was right, and I had seen it and filed it and not said it aloud, because saying it aloud meant the mission continued, and the mission continuing meant Tam kept carrying her burden toward a rite that might kill her, and I —

I had drawn maps of places that no longer exist. But I had never once drawn a map of a place I was afraid to go.

"The extinguishing pattern," I said at last. "Yes. I ran it against the old astronomical tables. Against the coronation dates. Against the ward-laying sequence from the second shard." I pulled the chart from beneath the others — I had hidden it, which tells you everything about what I was doing, hiding evidence from myself — and spread it flat. Thirteen constellations, blotted out in sequence, each darkness landing on an anniversary.

Rosalind traced the sequence with one finger. His finger stopped at the seventh mark. "This one. This is the valley."

"The valley's founding," I confirmed. "And the eighth—" I touched the next mark, and my mouth went dry, because I had known this for six days and told no one, and the telling of it now felt like opening a door I'd bricked shut. "The eighth is the date the third shard was hidden. The lighthouse. The lover's letter."

"So someone is counting our steps," Quill said softly. "Ahead of us."

"Someone is *replaying* them," I corrected. "These dates are all two hundred years old. Whatever put out the stars is re-enacting Dominic's life in reverse. Or—" and here my voice did the thing voices do when they deliver news they don't believe themselves, "—or the sky isn't being extinguished at all. Maybe it's being *remembered*. Maybe the stars aren't gone. Maybe they're being held somewhere, the way the valley holds memories, the way the sigil holds a king."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that settles on the shoulders.

"Then turning back doesn't save anyone," Rosalind said quietly. "If the sky is part of the binding, the binding is bigger than the valley. Bigger than the throne. There's nowhere to turn back *to*, Odile. The whole kingdom is inside the prison. We've been inside it since the day we were born."

I looked at my chart. All my beautiful, accurate, useless charts — coastlines and roads and rivers, the whole knowable world — and understood, with the particular nausea of a cartographer confronting a bad projection, that I had been mapping the inside of a cell and calling it a country.

"Well," I said finally, and reached for the wine, because some discoveries require ceremony. "In that case, my objection stands, but my alternative has collapsed, and I have never in my life supported a plan solely because every other plan was worse."

"That's the most encouraging thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it." I poured three cups — Tam got hers saved, she'd need it when she woke — and pushed one across to him. "But hear my terms, thief. We go on. We go to the observatory. And if, at any point, the evidence says Tam dies for this — not risks, *dies* — then we stop, and I don't care what the stars are replaying or whose face the Regent wears. Some prices aren't paid. They're refused. That's the line I draw, and you will stay inside it, or you will draw your own map without me."

Rosalind held my gaze. And I saw it in him then, clear as contour lines: the fracture running through him too, the same one running through all of us — the knowledge that love and truth were pulling in different directions, and that whichever one we chose, we'd lose something we couldn't map.

"Agreed," he said. "On one condition of my own."

"Name it."

"When we vote — and we will vote, all of us, including Tam — you argue your case honestly. No softening it because you think I'll break. If you believe we're delivering a king to his execution, say it in front of everyone."

"Oh, I'll say it," I said. "I've been rehearsing it for days. I have *visual aids*."

For the first time all night, he laughed — really laughed, tired and cracked and human — and Quill made her dry rustling sound that was her version of the same, and outside the wind kept worrying the eaves, and above the wind, above the roof, above everything, the sky stayed black and legible, counting down dates no one had lived yet.

I blew out the candle. In the dark, the shard glowed on like a small stubborn heart.

Nineteen days. And tomorrow, I would have to help the family decide whether we were rescuing a king — or robbing him of the only peace he'd ever chosen.

I only hoped, when the vote came, that I was brave enough to lose it gracefully.
