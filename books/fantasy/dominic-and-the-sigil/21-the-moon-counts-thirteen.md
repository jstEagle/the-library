## 21. The Moon Counts Thirteen

The astronomer's name was Pellam Wren, and he had been living in Odile's attic for three years without anyone downstairs knowing he existed.

I learned this the way I learned most things in the safehouse: by accident, by theft, and by being told afterward that I hadn't been supposed to know. I'd gone up for a coil of copper wire—Tam needed it for a ward-lattice she was rebuilding from memory, muttering the whole time about her master's sloppy joins—and found instead a narrow room under the slanted roof where every surface that wasn't window was covered in chalk.

Not maps. Chalk circles. Dozens of them, overlapping, annotated, argued with. Some had been erased so many times the plaster had gone gray and velvety, like old slate. And in the middle of it all, on a stool too small for him, sat a long gray man with a long gray beard and eyes like two chips of night sky, squinting up through a hole cut in the roof as if the hole owed him money.

"Stars are gone," he said, without turning around. "In case you hadn't noticed."

"It's been hard to miss."

"Most people manage." He turned then, slow as a door in winter. "You're the thief. The one carrying the king in a box."

"The box is empty," I said. "The king is scattered. And I'm not carrying him so much as being followed by him." Quill, on my shoulder, made a sound that was either agreement or indigestion.

Pellam Wren studied me for a long moment, the way astronomers study things they can't touch. Then he said, "You'll do," which I would learn was the warmest sentence in his inventory.

* * *

His name for himself was an exile twice over. First the crown-thing had burned the observatories—it didn't like instruments that could measure what it had stolen—and then, when the order came down to recant, Pellam had stood up in front of the Royal Academy and read aloud, in full, his paper proving mathematically that the night sky could not simply be switched off. They took his chair, his pension, and very nearly his hands. He escaped with the paper and one brass instrument wrapped in his coat, and he had spent three years in the attic doing the only thing left to do: counting.

"You can't unmake stars," he told me, tapping the chalk circles with a finger like a dried reed. "You can hide them. You can fold the light away, tuck it behind something. But it's still there, and it still *moves*, because the thing hiding it has to hold the fold, and nothing holds anything perfectly. There's always leakage. Always error." He smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "The Regent makes errors. Every twenty-eight days, give or take, there is a night when the sky almost remembers itself. A shimmer. A ghost of light where a star used to be. I call them the turns."

Quill shifted on my shoulder. "How many turns?" she asked, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it did when she already suspected the answer and hated it.

"Thirteen," Pellam said. "I've counted twelve since the binding completed. On the thirteenth, the fold closes permanently. The sigil sets, like a bone knitting wrong. Whatever is bound inside it becomes part of the mechanism. No seams. No way in." He looked at me over the tops of his spectacles. "No way out."

"And when is the thirteenth?"

He didn't answer right away. He got up—that took a while; the man moved like a folding rule extending—and went to a shelf, and took down a ledger bound in gray leather, and opened it to a page dense with figures in a hand so small I thought at first it was print.

"Nineteen days," he said. "Nineteen nights, if we're precise, and I am always precise. The turn falls on the new moon after next. After that—" he closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin "—Dominic isn't a prisoner anymore. He's mortar."

* * *

I want to tell you I received this news with dignity. That I nodded gravely and went down the stairs and reported it to the others in measured tones, and that we formed a plan with sensible margins.

What actually happened is that I sat down on the astronomer's stool—the one too small for both of us—and put my head between my knees and breathed until the room stopped trying to spin off its chalk foundations.

Because here is the arithmetic nobody had done out loud before. We had one shard recovered from a drowned chapel, and a second buried somewhere in a general's tomb across half a starless kingdom. Tam had swallowed the first one, which meant our safest container for the king's memories was also a nineteen-year-old girl with nightmares and a talent for swearing. Odile was still walking on a leg the hounds had nearly taken apart. Quill was old—older than any crow had a right to be, though she bristled whenever anyone mentioned it—and Vess knew we were moving, because Vess had always known everything, because every coin I'd ever sold him had been a little bell tied to my ankle.

And now the sky itself was a clock, and someone had just shown me the hour.

"Nineteen days," I said into my knees.

"Eighteen, by tomorrow morning," Pellam said helpfully. "I find precision clarifies grief."

"It doesn't. It's terrible. You should stop."

"I've been told." He lowered himself back onto his own stool with the groan of a man who had opinions about furniture. "Can I tell you something else, thief? Something worse than the number?"

"There's worse than nineteen days?"

"There's always worse. That's astronomy's whole gift to the world." He pointed his reed of a finger at the largest chalk circle on the wall, the one drawn over and over until it had worn a groove in the plaster. "The turns aren't random. They follow the moon's cycle because the binding was anchored to it—the smiths who forged your sigil used lunar sympathy to hold the folds shut. Which means the last three turns are the strongest. The sky will almost open on those nights. Almost shine. And the Regent will feel every one of them, like a splinter working deeper." His eyes found mine. "The crown-thing knows the count too. It has always known the count. Nineteen days is not just your deadline. It's *its* deadline. Everything it does from tonight until the thirteenth turn, it does knowing time has finally run out for one of you."

* * *

Downstairs, I told them.

I told them in the kitchen, because Odile held her councils in kitchens—in her experience, she said, people lie less when they're holding bread. So we stood around the big scarred table among the exiles' supper dishes, and I gave them Pellam's numbers straight, no varnish, because these people had earned the truth from me the hard way: by giving me theirs.

Odile took it standing, arms folded, weight off the bad leg, her face doing the thing it did where it became a map with the legend torn away. Tam took it sitting, turning a heel of bread over and over in her fingers until it looked like something excavated. Quill took it from the mantel, feathers tight to her body, saying nothing, which from Quill was a shout.

"Nineteen days," Odile repeated at last. She said it the way you test ice. "To cross the salt country, raise a general from his tomb, recover the shards, reach the observatory ruins at the threshold, and perform an unbinding rite that requires—" she paused, delicately, over the word "—a willing vessel."

"That's the sum of it."

"And you're asking whether we go."

"I'm telling you the count," I said. "The going is yours to decide. It always was. That's what this place is. Nobody's conscripted here."

There was a silence with a lot of history in it. Around us the exiles had gone quiet—not leaving, just stilling, the way a household stills when it senses the shape of a decision in the air. A woman named Brannock who mended boots by the stove set down her awl. Two brothers who ran messages along the river road stopped pretending to eat. Everyone had come to this house having lost something to the dark sky; everyone understood, without it being said, that the count belonged to all of them now.

Tam broke it. Of course Tam broke it.

"Well, we're obviously going," she said. "Are you mad? The tomb's four days' ride. The rite needs the shards, the vessel, and whatever my master's notes say about the third component—I've been translating the cipher, I'm most of the way through—so the real question is whether we leave tomorrow or tonight, and I vote tonight, because every hour we sit here arguing is an hour the Regent spends getting ready, and I for one refuse to be mortar." She said *mortar* with particular venom, as if Pellam had invented the word specifically to offend her.

"TAM," Odile said.

"What? Someone had to say it."

"No one had to say it *first*." But Odile's mouth was doing something complicated at the corner, something that on a younger face would have been a smile. She looked around the kitchen—at the boot-mender, at the message-brothers, at me—and I watched her make the calculation she'd been making since the day the hounds wrecked her safehouse: what is this worth, what am I worth, what are any of us worth if we stop now.

"We go tomorrow," she said. "Tonight we plan, we pack, and everyone sleeps, because I have led enough doomed expeditions to know that tired people die first. Tomorrow at first light." She unfolded her arms. "Rosalind. A word. Not here."

* * *

She took me to the chart room, which was really a closet with pretensions, its walls hung with her life's work: the Map of Missing Things, star by extinguished star, each one inked with the date it died and the places below it that had changed after. In lamplight the whole wall seemed to breathe.

"Do you know why I keep this?" she asked, without turning around.

"To remember what was taken."

"Mm. Half marks." She touched one small mark near the top—a star she'd charted as a girl, the first one she ever drew. "I keep it because forgetting is the enemy's entire method. The valley forgot. The kingdom forgot the stars. The Regent wants a world with no prior version, no original against which he can be judged a forgery." Now she did turn, and her eyes were very level. "So when you stood in my kitchen and offered these people a choice, honest as a bill of sale, I want you to understand what I heard. I heard a thief who has spent his whole life taking memories offer to carry one home for free. And I heard the count ticking under it. Nineteen days. Nineteen."

"You think we can't make it."

"I think we can't afford to find out we can't." She crossed to the map and tapped a point far east, past the inked borders of anything she'd surveyed firsthand. "The threshold observatory. Ruined since before my grandmother. There's a road that reaches it—but the last hundred miles run through the salt flats, and the flats are where the Regent's patience ends. If he means to stop us, that's where. Which means we must arrive with everything solved except the last problem, or not arrive at all." She let her hand fall. "Get some sleep, keeper. You look like a man counting."

"I am a man counting."

"Then count this too." Her voice dropped, gentled, became for a moment the voice of an old woman rather than a commander. "Whatever you are—thief, fragment, weapon, boy—you are the only person alive who chose this. Dominic didn't choose his cage. The rest of us didn't choose exile. You stood in a dead valley and picked up a box." She patted my arm once, briskly, as if closing a seam. "That counts for more than nineteen days. Remember it when the counting gets loud."

* * *

It got loud that same night.

I couldn't sleep—which surprised no one, least of all me—and so I climbed back up to the attic, because there was something I wanted from Pellam Wren and it wasn't numbers. I found him awake, naturally, bent over his eyehole with the brass instrument trained on a patch of black that, to me, contained absolutely nothing.

"Show me a turn," I said.

He grunted. "Next one's in six days. Too soon."

"You said they leak. Show me the leak."

Another grunt, but he shifted aside, and I put my eye to the instrument, and for a long moment there was only darkness and the ache of holding still.

Then—there. Faint as breath on cold glass. A shimmer where nothing should shimmer, a trembling suggestion of light, like a candle burning in a house you can't quite see. It pulsed, very slowly. Once. Twice.

Like a heartbeat.

"He's still in there," I said. My voice came out strange, scraped. "All this time. Under everything. He's still—"

"Counting too," Pellam said quietly. "Yes. Every soul in this kingdom is counting now, thief. The difference is that most of them don't know it yet." He settled onto his stool beside me, and we watched the ghost-light pulse together in silence, two exiles keeping vigil over a heartbeat the size of a sky.

After a while, Quill landed on the windowsill. She'd been out flying—she flew every night now, restless, ranging farther than she admitted—and she came in through the roof-hole with wind in her feathers and something in her bearing that made both of us go still.

"What is it?" I asked.

She looked at me for a long moment, head tilted, one bright eye catching the lamp.

"Tam's writing again," she said.

And I was moving down the stairs before she finished the sentence, three at a time, the count hammering in my ears like a fist on a door—nineteen days, eighteen, seventeen—and when I burst into the kitchen, the lamp still burning low, there she was: asleep at the table, cheek on her folded arms, the heel of bread forgotten beside her.

And in front of her, in ink still wet, in a fine antique hand none of us had taught her, a page covered edge to edge with writing that ended, in letters pressed deep enough to tear the paper:

*Nineteen days is longer than I have. Hurry.*
