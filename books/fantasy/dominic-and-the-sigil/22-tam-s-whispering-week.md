## 22. Tam's Whispering Week

The shard started talking on a Tuesday.

I know it was a Tuesday because Rosalind had just come back from the well complaining that Tuesdays were cursed — he'd dropped the bucket, torn his sleeve on the pump handle, and been glared at by Odile's goat, which is a serious offense in this household — and I'd laughed so hard at his impression of the goat that I didn't notice, at first, that someone else was laughing too.

Someone inside my head.

It wasn't a voice, exactly. Voices have direction. This was more like remembering something I'd never known — a laugh that arrived pre-loaded with context, warm and dry and slightly self-mocking, the laugh of a man who has learned to find himself ridiculous before anyone else can beat him to it. It sat behind my sternum like a swallowed coal.

"Tam?" Rosalind said. "You've gone strange."

"I'm always strange."

"You've gone *stranger*."

He wasn't wrong. My hands had stopped moving. I'd been mending a shirt — one of Rosalind's, since he treats clothing the way a moth treats clothing — and the needle was hovering in midair, and I realized I couldn't remember picking it up, only that it was there, and that some part of me knew exactly how a king's tailor used to set a seam.

*Oh no,* I thought.

*Oh yes,* said the coal behind my sternum, in the tone of a man settling into an uncomfortable chair for a long stay.

* * *

Let me back up, because I've been keeping this chronology like a ward-diagram: strict lines, labeled nodes, everything accounted for. That's how my master trained me. Wards are just stories told very precisely to very stupid stone, she used to say, and if your story has gaps, the stone will fill them with teeth.

Day one: the shard speaks. I tell no one. Not out of secrecy — out of the bone-deep certainty that if I say it aloud, it becomes real, and real things have consequences, and I have had enough consequences for one lifetime, thank you.

Day two: I catch myself writing.

This is the part I still can't think about without my skin crawling. I woke at dawn with ink on my fingers and three pages of script on the table in front of me, and it wasn't my hand. Mine slants left and presses hard enough to emboss the next sheet; my master said I wrote like I was trying to stab the paper. This hand flowed rightward, light as breath, letters tall and narrow with flourishes on the descenders — the handwriting of someone who'd been taught penmanship by people paid to notice such things.

And it wasn't gibberish. That's what undid me. If it had been gibberish I could have burned it and blamed the wine. But it was a letter, or the start of one, addressed to *my dearest heart* in a tone of such naked tenderness that I felt like a burglar reading it. Which, in fairness, is exactly what I was. The shard had been stolen from Dominic — from whatever was left of him after the binding — and now it was living in my chest cavity like a tenant who pays rent in memories, and it was using my hands to write love letters to a woman four hundred years dead.

I read it twice. Then I folded it into quarters and hid it under the loose floorboard where I keep the things I can't look at directly, which is getting to be a crowded neighborhood.

Day three: I told Quill.

Not because I wanted to. Because she landed on my windowsill at breakfast, fixed me with one glittering eye, and said, "You smell different."

"I bathed."

"Don't be disgusting. You smell *layered*. Like someone wearing another person's coat." She hopped closer, head tilting with that mechanical precision crows affect when they're being ominous. "The king's coat. It's coming through you now, isn't it? The shard."

I should mention that Quill is a crow who served Dominic before the binding, helped forge it, regretted it for four centuries, and processes guilt by being insufferably blunt. She is also, despite everything, the closest thing I have to a grandmother, if grandmothers could fly and held grudges against entire cosmological structures.

"It talks," I admitted.

"They always talk." She said it lightly, but her feathers did a thing — a ripple from shoulders to tail, the crow equivalent of a shudder. "Mine talked. For years after the forging. I'd wake up and know things about grain tariffs. Do you know how upsetting it is to dream in grain tariffs?"

"What did you do?"

"Loved him anyway." She picked at the windowsill with her beak, not looking at me. "That was the problem, in the end. We all loved him, and we all thought we knew best, and between us we built a prison around a man who'd only asked us to trust him." Her beak clicked shut. "So. What does yours say?"

"That's the thing," I said. "It doesn't say anything. It *writes*."

Quill went very still. Crows shouldn't be able to go still like that; they're mostly motion held together with opinions. "Show me."

* * *

She read all three pages standing on my table, walking slowly along each line, and I watched her the way you watch someone defuse a bomb you're sitting on.

When she finished, she was quiet for long enough that I counted her heartbeats — crows' hearts go fast, a sewing-machine rhythm, and hers had slowed to something almost human.

"This is his hand," she said finally. "I'd know it anywhere. He wrote to Elara every night of the war. Even the nights nothing happened. *Especially* those nights. 'Nothing happened today, and I wanted you to know it.'" She tapped the page with her beak. "This is that letter. Or its twin. He must have written it a dozen times, practicing the words until they were true."

"Elara," I repeated. "The lighthouse woman. From the third shard."

"The same." Quill's eye caught the morning light and threw it back sharp as a blade-point. "You understand what this means, girl?"

"That I'm haunted by stationery?"

"That the shard isn't speaking to you. It's speaking *through* you. There's a difference, and the difference matters." She hopped onto my knee, which she never does, which meant she was frightened, which meant I was allowed to be terrified. "A shard that speaks is a memory trying to get out. A shard that writes is a mind trying to get *in*. Dominic isn't haunting you, Tam. He's learning you. Learning your hands, your habits, your wards. Because somewhere under all that grief and love-letter nonsense, there's a strategist, and strategists don't waste a vessel."

"And that's bad?"

"It depends entirely," said Quill, "on what he's planning to do with you."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having a dead king in your head: it gets *domestic*.

By day five I'd stopped flinching when knowledge arrived uninvited. I'd be working the morning bread and suddenly know the exact ratio of salt to flour favored by the palace kitchens during a famine year, when they stretched the loaves with ash and prayed nobody noticed. I'd be setting a minor ward on the chicken coop — Odile's goat had developed opinions about chickens, and the chickens needed representation — and my fingers would move through patterns I'd never learned, old forms, court forms, the kind of ward-smithing they taught before the craft got practical and mean.

He didn't push. That's what saved us, I think. Whatever Dominic was doing in there, he did it gently, the way you'd settle into a shared room with a stranger — quietly, apologetically, taking the smaller bed. When I slept, I dreamed his dreams, but at their edges, like watching through a window. Coronations where everyone smiled except the man being crowned. A war room smelling of tallow and fear. A lighthouse, always a lighthouse, its beam sweeping across water that held no reflection.

And once — only once — I dreamed something that was neither his nor mine. We were both in it, standing in a corridor made of the space between wards, and he looked at me — really looked, the way my master used to look at a sigil she suspected was lying to her — and he said:

"You're afraid of me."

"Not of you," I said, because apparently in dreams I'm brave. "Of what you'll cost me."

"Ah." And he smiled, and it was the smile from the coronation shard, the one Rosalind described — joy with dread underneath it like a riverbed under clear water. "Then we understand each other. I've been afraid of myself for four hundred years. You're catching up quickly."

Then the corridor collapsed into chickens, because dreams are merciful that way, and I woke up laughing and crying at the same time, which Rosalind tells me is called "hysterics" and requires tea.

* * *

Rosalind found out on day six, because of course he did. The man misses nothing; it's his whole tragedy. You can't hide anything from a thief whose original sin was stealing memories — he reads people the way I read wards, every line and flaw and place where the story has gaps.

He cornered me in the kitchen, where I was copying out the night's pages — because I'd started transcribing them properly, on Quill's advice, keeping a ledger of what the king wrote through me, because if you can't stop a haunting you can at least make it *organized* — and he leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed and said, "You've been sleeping four hours a night and hiding paper under the floorboard. The floorboard, Tam. I steal things professionally. Did you think the loose board was safe?"

"Nothing's safe from you. That's the problem with this house."

"Whose handwriting is it?"

I could have lied. He'd have known. So I slid the pages across the table instead and watched his face while he read.

I want to record what his face did, because it mattered. First the thief-face, quick and appraising, cataloguing the hand, the flourishes, the quality of the ink. Then something older moved underneath it — recognition, but secondhand, the way you recognize a face from a description. He'd lived inside Dominic's coronation, down in the drowned chapel. He knew the man's interior weather better than any of us.

"He writes to her," Rosalind said softly. "Every night. Four hundred years, and he's still—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Does it hurt? When he uses your hands?"

"A bit. Like wearing gloves that are slightly too small."

"And you didn't tell me." Not angry. Worse — hurt, quiet, the specific hurt of someone who grew up with no family and therefore takes family breaches seriously.

"You were busy with the maps. And I kept thinking it would resolve itself, and then I kept thinking if I told you, you'd do something reckless—"

"I would never."

"—and then I kept thinking, actually, that's exactly what you'd do, so I waited until I understood it myself." I pushed the latest page toward him. "Look at the last one. Really look."

He looked. His eyebrows did the thing.

The last letter was different. Shorter. Addressed not to Elara but to *the keeper of my hands*, which is either the most romantic or most alarming salutation I've ever received, and signed with a single word: *Thirteen.*

"Thirteen turns," Rosalind murmured. "The astronomer's count. Nine days left." He looked up at me, and his eyes had gone bright and hard, the way they do when the thief and the believer in him reach the same conclusion from opposite directions. "He's counting with us. He knows the deadline. Tam — he's not just riding along. He's *preparing*."

"For what?"

"For the rite. For the unbinding. For whatever happens when the sigil opens and everything he is comes pouring out." Rosalind gathered the pages with unexpected gentleness, squaring their edges like something precious. "You're not haunted. You're a *drafting table*. He's sketching himself onto you, piece by piece, so that when the moment comes, there's somewhere for him to land."

The kitchen went very quiet. Somewhere outside, the goat screamed at a cloud, because the goat has no respect for dramatic pauses.

"And if there's nowhere for him to land?" I asked. "If the vessel fails? If *I* fail?"

Rosalind looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide not to lie to me, which is the highest compliment that man knows how to pay.

"Then we find out why my master's notes say the rite needs a willing vessel," he said. "And we make sure the willing vessel is the best ward-smith either of us has ever met. Because I've seen her work, and frankly, the competition is a dead king with borrowed hands."

* * *

Day seven. Tonight.

I'm writing this in my own hand, pressing hard, slanting left, stabbing the paper — proof of ownership, if nothing else. Outside, the starless dark presses against the windows like a face. In nine days we ride for the observatory, and Odile is arguing with the maps, and Rosalind is sharpening things that don't need sharpening, and Quill sits on the lintel every evening facing east, toward the capital, toward the thing wearing her king like a stolen coat.

And I am keeping a ledger of a ghost.

But here's what I haven't told them yet. Here's what I found tonight, at the bottom of tonight's pages, written in that flowing rightward hand while I slept, in ink I don't remember opening:

*To the keeper of my hands —*

*You asked once what I will cost you. I have been considering an honest answer, which is rare for kings.*

*I will cost you sleep. I will cost you the comfortable fiction that your mind belongs to you alone. And on the thirteenth turn, I may cost you everything, because the thing in the crown knows you carry me now, and it does not forgive its own fragments.*

*But hear this, ward-smith, and weigh it: I did not choose to speak through you. Yet of all the vessels this age might have offered — and I have worn worse than a brave girl with steady hands — I am glad it was you. Your wards sing. Did you know that? Your master taught you the old grammar, the one they buried. When you build, I remember why we built.*

*Nine days. Rest while you can. And if you dream of the lighthouse — and you will — know that the woman in it was real, and the love was real, and I have carried both so long they have become load-bearing.*

*— D.*

I read it four times. Then I did the bravest thing I've done all week, braver than swallowing a shard, braver than facing hunters: I wrote back.

Just one line, in my ugly leftward scrawl, beneath his beautiful script:

*Your wards sang too. I've seen the shards. Build with me on the thirteenth turn, and we'll see what two craftsmen can make.*

Under the floorboard, the paper waits. Under my sternum, the coal burns steady.

And this morning, when I woke, there was a new line added at the bottom of the page, in that tall, flowing hand, pressed so lightly it barely dented the paper — the handwriting of a man who has just been answered for the first time in four hundred years, and is afraid to believe it:

*It's a bargain, keeper. Now teach me your grammar — because the thing in the crown has learned mine.*
