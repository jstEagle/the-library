## 23. The Third Shard: The Lover's Letter

The lighthouse stood at the end of the world like a man who'd been left there on purpose.

That was Odile's description, anyway, delivered in her dry cartographer's rasp as we picked our way down the cliff path. She had a gift for making geography sound like gossip. "Built to guide ships into a harbor that silted up two hundred years ago," she said, tapping her map of missing things—the chart where every extinguished star had a small inked circle, like a wound dressed in black. "The light kept burning for a generation after. Ships' crews swore they saw it sweeping the dark for a harbor that wasn't there anymore." She glanced at me sideways. "You should feel at home, thief. A thing doing its duty long after anyone remembers why."

I did feel at home. That was the problem with this whole expedition: everything we found felt like it had been waiting for me specifically, and I resented being expected.

The path down was shale and old rope, and Quill rode my shoulder complaining about it in her usual register—somewhere between a drill sergeant and a jilted duchess. "If I wanted to die on rocks above grey water," she muttered into my ear, "I'd have done it properly, in battle, with an audience. Not trudging. Trudging is how cowards die."

"You can fly," I pointed out.

"Which is why my suffering is *chosen*, and therefore noble."

Tam scrambled ahead of us both, twenty years old and made entirely of elbows and certainty, finding handholds in rock that had no business offering them. Ward-smith's apprentice turned ward-breaker turned the closest thing I had to a little sister, which was a development I hadn't authorized. She still carried the second shard inside her—not swallowed this time, but *kept*, tucked behind a lattice ward she'd woven under her own ribs, because apparently that was a thing you could do if your education had been sufficiently criminal. The shard hummed through her sometimes. Lately, when she slept, her lips moved.

We didn't talk about that. We talked about the lighthouse instead, because talking about Tam's whispering week felt like poking a bruise to see if it had gotten worse. It had gotten worse. So: lighthouse.

"Vess's ledger lists three payments here," Odile said. "Before he was Vess-the-traitor, before we knew—" She stopped herself. We all stopped ourselves. Saying *before we knew he'd been collecting our trail for the crown-thing all along* had become a reflexive silence in our little family, one more thing we carried without discussing. "Three payments," she finished instead. "Lamps, oil, and a sum marked only as 'the keeper's silence.' Someone paid good money to keep this place quiet."

"Or paid good money to keep someone quiet *in* this place," Tam said from below. Her voice echoed off the wet rock. "There's a door."

There was indeed a door, iron-banded, set into the lighthouse's base as though the tower had grown around it rather than the reverse. It was locked with a ward, and Tam knelt in front of it the way other people kneel in front of altars, fingers spread, eyes half-closed, reading.

"It's old," she said. "Older than the valley wards. Same hand, though—I know the penmanship." Something crossed her face that might have been grief for a master who'd vanished and left her his flaws as inheritance. "It'll let us through. It was built to let someone through." A pause. "Someone specific."

"Define specific," I said.

She looked back at me, and I didn't love what I saw in her expression. "It's keyed to resonance. To... shards. To him." She meant Dominic. Everyone meant Dominic these days; he was the weather we all lived under. "It wants the king."

"I'm the nearest thing to the king currently available," I said, "which is a sentence I'd like never to repeat aloud again," and I put my palm against the iron, and the ward tasted me, and the door opened with a sigh like something exhaling after a very long held breath.

* * *

Inside, the lighthouse smelled of salt and lamp-oil and time. Spiral stairs. Gull droppings fossilized on the landings. And everywhere, everywhere, paper.

Not scattered—*archived*. Bundled letters tied with cord, stacked in oiled crates, sealed against damp with wax that had gone amber. Someone had spent years down here preserving correspondence, and not their own. I recognized the hand on the topmost bundle before I recognized anything else, because I'd seen it twice now: once in the war-room shard, signing orders for a valley to be built; once in Tam's sleep, moving her fingers across the air as she wrote words that weren't hers.

Dominic's hand. Sharp-shouldered letters, impatient descenders, the script of a man who thought faster than his pen could follow.

"He kept them," Tam whispered. She'd gone still by the crates, and her voice had that careful flatness she used when the shard behind her ribs stirred. "All of them. Every letter she ever sent."

"She?" Quill asked from my shoulder, though I noticed the crow's feathers had gone tight and sleek, the way they did when she knew an answer and dreaded it.

"The lover," Tam said. "There are hundreds here. He answered every one. Look—" She lifted a bundle, careful as a relic. "His drafts. The ones he didn't send. Crossed out and rewritten. He wrote her *every day*, even near the end, even after—" She stopped.

"After he decided to trap himself inside a sigil to catch a creature wearing his face," I said. "Yes. I've read ahead in this particular tragedy."

Nobody laughed. We were past laughing at that particular joke; it had been funny for exactly one evening around Odile's fire, and then it hadn't.

Because here was the shape of what we knew, and I'll lay it out plainly since laying things out plainly was becoming my unwanted specialty: the crown-thing—the Regent, the thing that wore Dominic's face like a well-tailored coat—could not survive without its host. The third shard's rumor, dragged out of a drowned chapel ledger and a general's tomb and half a bottle of exile courage, was this: the binding went both ways. Cut the sigil loose, and you didn't just free a king. You unmade the parasite that had ruled a starless kingdom for years, because the thing had no self of its own. It was a shadow, and Dominic was the wall it stood against.

And shadows, when the wall comes down, don't drift away politely. They tear.

So we climbed the stairs to find the third shard, and none of us said what we were all calculating, which was the arithmetic of one life weighed against a kingdom's worth of them. I caught Tam doing sums with her mouth. I caught Odile refusing to look at her own hands, which was how she prayed.

At the top of the tower, the lamp room opened around us like a glass eye. Below, the sea worked at the rocks, patient as a creditor. Above, where stars should have been, nothing. The same nothing we'd walked under since the night the sky went out. But here, in this room built for watching heavens, the nothing felt personal. Deliberate. A stage swept clean.

The shard sat in the lamp housing itself.

Of course it did. Where else would you keep a piece of a king's soul but in the mechanism built to burn? The great Fresnel lens stood around it in rings, prisms within prisms, and at its heart, where the flame should be, something small and pale turned slowly with no wind to turn it—a splinter of light, or a splinter of memory pretending to be light. It cast no glow. It drank the grey daylight and gave nothing back, and standing near it I felt that familiar pull behind my sternum, the hook-and-line sensation I'd learned to dread and crave in equal measure. The shards recognized something in me. I tried very hard not to wonder what.

"Quill," I said. "You served him. You were there. What am I about to see?"

The crow was silent long enough that I turned to look at her. She was staring at the lens with one bright black eye, and when she spoke, her voice came out smaller than usual, stripped of its duchy-and-dagger polish.

"The truth," she said. "Finally. Whether any of us want it or not."

I reached into the lamp housing and took hold of the shard.

* * *

The world folded.

I have been inside Dominic's memories three times now, and each time the crossing gets less like drowning and more like remembering—which frightens me more than drowning ever did. Drowning is done *to* you. Remembering is something you participate in.

I was in a study I knew from the war-room shard, but warmer now, candlelit, later. Rain on high windows. Dominic stood at a writing desk with his coat off and his collar open, and he looked—this is the part I still can't say cleanly—he looked *happy*. Tired past the bone, hunted in ways I now understood intimately, and happy, because there was a letter open on the desk in front of him and he was touching the edge of the paper the way you touch something you're afraid will be taken away.

The letter was signed *E*. Just that. Everything else in the letter I felt rather than read, because the memory came to me from inside him and he wasn't reading the words anymore; he was reading the person behind them. A woman who wrote about gulls and tides and the correct way to insult a harbor master, and then, in the last lines of every letter, in a smaller hand, said the true thing. This one said: *Come away from the capital. Come to the light. Whatever wears your face can follow the roads; let it wear itself out walking.*

And Dominic, king of a kingdom already going grey at the edges, sat down to answer her—and here the memory thickened, here the candlelight bent strange—and I understood, with the terrible clarity the shards specialize in delivering, that this letter was written after the decision. After the valley plans. After the war room where he'd ordered a prison built and told his generals it was to hide something.

He wrote to her honestly. That was the horror of it. No lies, no protecting her with silence. *They think I mean to hide myself,* his pen moved, sharp-shouldered, impatient. *Let them. A prison for a king is the perfect disguise for a trap. It cannot live without me, Elowen. It knows it, and it is afraid, and afraid things make mistakes. I will bind my true self where it cannot reach and leave it wearing my shape alone, and alone it will starve. It may take years. It may take everything. But I did not take this crown to keep it. I took it to keep them, and I find I can keep them better as a ghost than as a man.*

Then, smaller, the true thing, in the last lines, the way she'd taught him:

*Do not wait for me at the light. Do not keep the lamp burning. I am asking you the hardest thing I have ever asked anyone, which is to forget me while I am still alive to be forgotten. If you love me, let the harbor fill with silt and let the ships go elsewhere and let yourself go elsewhere too. There is no version of this where I come back whole. There is only the version where the thing that took my face dies of it.*

*I am not doing this to be a story. I am doing it so that no one ever has to tell one about me.*

He sealed it. He did not send it.

I watched—was him—as he set it in a crate with the others, hundreds of unsent answers, a correspondence conducted entirely with himself, a man loving a woman by refusing to burden her. Then I watched him walk down the tower stairs one last time, past the lamp he would not let her tend for him, and stand at the base of the lighthouse in the rain, and look up at the sky where the stars were still lit, all of them, a whole heaven he was about to spend as currency—

—and the memory ended on the sound of the door ward tasting his blood and opening for him, *specifically for him, forever*, and the last thing I carried back was not grief or resolve but something smaller and worse: the way his hand lingered on the iron. The way a man says goodbye to a door.

* * *

I came back to the lamp room on my knees, which was undignified, and Tam's arms were already around my shoulders, which was worse, because I couldn't pretend I hadn't needed it.

"Well?" Odile stood at the glass, back to me, watching the empty sky. She always asked that way, facing away, giving you privacy to lie if you needed it.

I told them the truth. All of it. The trap, the starving shadow, the unsent letters, the harbor master insults, the last line—*no one ever has to tell one about me*—and I heard my own voice go rough on that part and didn't fight it. Wry has its limits. I've found most of them personally.

When I finished, the sea filled the silence, working the rocks.

"So it's simple," Odile said finally, still facing the glass. "Simple the way a knife is simple. Unbind the sigil, free the man, kill the Regent. One motion." Now she turned, and her old cartographer's face was doing something I'd never seen it do: failing to be composed. "Except the binding isn't a cage around him, is it? It's a knot he tied *through himself*. Pull it apart wrong and you don't free a prisoner. You unravel the rope. Both ends."

"And if the Regent unravels first," Tam said quietly, "then Dominic—" She stopped. Behind her ribs, I swear I could feel the second shard listening. "The thing can't survive without its host. But maybe neither can the host. Maybe the binding is the only thing holding him together too. Maybe we cut it and we lose them both, and we've traded a stolen king for a dead one and a kingdom that still has no stars."

"That's not certain," Odile said.

"No," Tam agreed. "Neither is the other thing."

Quill hopped down from the lamp housing, feathers dull, and stood on the floor between us like a small black verdict. "I helped forge it," she said, for perhaps the hundredth time since her confession, and for the hundredth time it landed like the first. "I believed it would save him. And now I'm telling you what I believed then, which is that he knew. He knew the cost when he tied it. He chose it with his eyes open and his heart in a crate of unsent letters." Her head cocked, that bird gesture that manages to be both alien and heartbreakingly familiar. "The question was never whether the price is terrible. The question is whether we're allowed to pay a price he already agreed to."

Nobody answered her. Outside, the sea kept counting the rocks, patient as arithmetic.

It was Tam who broke it. She reached into her collar and drew out the second shard on its cord, and it pulsed once against her skin, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"There's a draft," she said. "In the crate. I felt it while you were under—it's still humming, it's the reason the shard woke. A letter he started the night before the binding and never finished. One line." Her voice did something complicated. "It says: *Elowen. If someone comes to undo what I've done, tell them—*" 

She looked at me.

"It stops there," Tam said. "Tell them what?"

Above us, in the glass eye of the ruined lamp room, under a sky with no stars in it, the third shard in my fist pulsed once—like an answer trying to arrive, or like a man, somewhere very far inside a knot of light, hearing his own unfinished sentence quoted back to him and straining toward the end of it.

I closed my hand around the shard and felt it beat.

"Nineteen days," I said. "We'd better find out."
