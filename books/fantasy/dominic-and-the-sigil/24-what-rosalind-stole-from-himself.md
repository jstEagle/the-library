## 24. What Rosalind Stole From Himself

The lighthouse gave us three things: a shard wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax that smelled of myrrh and old arguments, a dead gull on the stairs, and the beginning of the end of everything I thought I was.

I'd have traded two of those back without hesitation.

We made camp in the keeper's room at the top of the tower, because Odile's knees had opinions about descending ninety spiral steps in the dark, and because the glass lantern room still caught whatever pale suggestion of light the sky offered and threw it around like it was being paid to. Tam was asleep against the wall with her coat rolled under her head, her fingers twitching now and then — she'd been writing again in her sleep, little loops of a hand that wasn't hers, and none of us had mentioned it out loud yet. Denial is a load-bearing wall in any found family.

I sat by the cold hearth turning the shard over in my hands. It was small — they all were — a sliver of something like smoked glass that shouldn't have been warm but was, faintly, the way a stone stays warm hours after the fire's gone. The third one. Three pieces of a king I'd never met, and every time I touched them too long I got the same feeling: like reading someone's letters over their shoulder. Intimate. Indefensible.

"You're doing the thing," Quill said.

She was perched on the windowsill, preening, which is crow for "relaxed," except nothing about her was relaxed. Her feathers had been wrong since the drowned chapel. Wrong the way a person's posture goes wrong when they're carrying a sentence they haven't said yet.

"What thing?"

"The brooding thing. You hold the shard like it's a coin you're deciding whether to spend or swallow."

"I'm deciding whether it's warm because of ambient magic or because I'm having a stroke."

"Crows don't say 'ambient.'"

"Crows also don't lecture."

Quill hopped down onto the hearthstone and looked at me. I want to be clear about what that look did to me, because crows' eyes are black buttons, comic, harmless — and hers stopped being either of those things. She looked at me the way Vess used to look at merchandise he was about to lowball. Appraising. Recalculating.

"Give me your hand," she said.

"Absolutely not. Last time you asked for my hand you took a splinter of ward-glass and drew a symbol on my palm 'to see if it would glow.' It glowed. I didn't sleep for two days."

"It won't hurt."

"That's what you said about the symbol."

"And I was right! It didn't hurt." A pause. A feather ruffled. "It just meant something."

The hearth was cold and the tower was quiet and Tam breathed in her slow sleep-rhythm, and I thought: *one more strange thing in a year of strange things, what's the cost.* That's the thief's arithmetic, the one that got me through the valley — always price the risk before you feel the fear, because fear inflates the number.

I held out my hand.

* * *

What Quill did was simple and terrible. She took the shard from me — gently, in her beak, like a mother bird handling an egg — and pressed it to the back of my wrist, just below the old scar I've carried since before I could remember, which is a sentence that describes most of my scars.

And the world rang.

Not sound. Something older than sound, the way the valley's wards breaking had been less a noise than a *fact* arriving all at once. The shard sang one clear note. And something under my skin — under my skin, inside the meat of me, in a place I had no name for because no one had ever told me I owned such a place — answered it.

Harmony. That's the only word. The shard sang and something in me harmonized, matched pitch, fell into tune like it had been waiting years for someone to play the root note.

The warmth left the shard. I felt it go — felt it drain out of the glass and into me, or out of me and into the glass, direction meaningless when two things are the same thing — and for one long second I wasn't sitting in a ruined lighthouse at all. I was standing on a balcony with salt wind in my face, wearing a crown that weighed exactly as much as a decision, looking down at a crowd that loved me and knowing — knowing with the flat certainty of memory, not guesswork — that I would betray every single one of them within the year, and that I would do it to save them, and that they would never know which was true—

Then it was gone, and I was on the floor, and Quill was saying my name in a voice pitched to carry, and Tam was awake and holding my shoulders, and the shard lay dead and cool as river stone between us.

"Don't," I said, before anyone could ask. My mouth tasted like coronation wine. "Don't ask if I'm all right. Ask me something else."

Tam, bless her, asked the right thing: "Whose balcony?"

"A king's," I said. "Mine."

* * *

Here's what I knew already, and had been not-knowing very carefully for weeks, the way you don't look directly at a loose tooth:

The shards resonated near me. Not metaphorically — Odile had charted it. Each recovered fragment hummed louder in my pocket than anywhere else in the room. Tam said it was because I was the carrier, the designated thief, and I'd accepted that explanation the way you accept a bridge that wobbles: quickly, and while walking faster.

I remembered Dominic's memories from inside his body. Not like visions, not like stories told — like recollection. When I dove into the floodwater beneath the chapel, I hadn't watched a young king crowned; I'd felt wet wool against my own neck, had known where his childhood scar itched, had flinched from a cheer because I recognized a face in the crowd that wanted me dead.

And there was the valley itself. The valley that forgets, that erases travelers' pasts, that sat for decades around one prisoner whose self was locked in a sigil — and somehow produced, out of all its nameless drifters, exactly one man who could steal memories before the wards ate them. One man with a talent so specific, so surgically suited to the task of gathering scattered recollections, that if you'd designed him on purpose you couldn't have done better.

I had told myself for twenty years that I was lucky. Lucky to survive the wards, lucky to have a trade, lucky, lucky, lucky. Nobody survives luck that consistent. Luck that consistent has an author.

Quill waited until Tam had gone back to pretending to sleep and Odile was snoring in the honest way of women who have earned it, and then she said, quietly, in the dark:

"You're a fragment of him. Cut loose. Made flesh."

There it was. No ceremony. Crows don't do ceremony.

"How long have you known?" I asked. My voice came out level, which surprised me. Somewhere inside, the rest of me was screaming in a register only dogs and dead kings could hear.

"Since the drowned chapel. Properly since your hand glowed. Suspected since I first smelled you." She ruffled. "You smell like him, Rosalind. Under everything else — the valley dust, the road grime, the appalling cheese you eat — you smell like the sigil does. Like the binding. Like the night it was forged."

"The night it was forged," I repeated. "You mean the night you helped forge it."

"Yes."

"So I smell like your worst sin."

"You smell like the reason I'm still flying," she said, and there was something underneath the dryness that I chose, for both our sakes, not to examine. "He cut a piece of himself free before the binding closed. A piece that could remember. A piece that could move through the valley without being erased, because the valley doesn't erase what it can't find — and a fragment hidden inside living flesh isn't in the ledger. He hid a key inside a lockpick and threw the lockpick into the world."

"Why would he—" I started, and then stopped, because the answer arrived before the question finished, the way answers do when they're yours. "Because the sigil could be unbound. Because someday someone would need to gather the shards, and the shards sing to each other, and—"

"—and only one note in the whole world could call them home," Quill finished.

I looked at my hands. Thief's hands, scarred and clever, hands that had picked a hundred pockets and lifted a thousand memories from strangers who'd never miss what they couldn't keep. They looked the same. That was the obscene part. Everything had changed and my hands looked exactly the same.

"Say the rest," I said. "You're circling it like a corpse. Say it."

"He didn't just make a key," Quill said. "He made an heir. A spare. If the unbinding failed — if the vessel died, if the threshold was lost, if thirteen turns came and went — the fragments wouldn't vanish. They'd need somewhere to settle. Someone to *hold* them." Her head tilted. "You weren't just built to carry him home, Rosalind. You were built to be him if he never comes back."

* * *

I went up to the lantern room because I needed walls made of glass and a floor that swayed in the wind, something unstable outside to match the instability in. Also because I needed to not be near people who might look at me with pity, or worse, with awe.

Thief, keeper, or king.

That was the menu, apparently. Twenty-some years of survival reduced to a choice between three costumes, none of them mine.

The thief, at least, I understood. The thief had rules. Never lift from someone who can't afford the loss. Never sell a grief. Never take a name — names are the spine of a person, and a man without his name will pay anything to get it back. I'd written those rules myself, hadn't I? In the valley, alone, with no one to teach me?

Hadn't I?

Or had some dead king's hand been on mine the whole time, writing his conscience into me along with his cunning? Was my decency even *mine*, or was it inheritance, a codicil in a will I'd never read?

The keeper was simpler. Carry the shards, deliver the king, stand aside. A packhorse with a heartbeat. There was a comfort in that — purpose without ownership. You can't lose yourself to a question if your job description is a satchel.

But the third option. The king. The spare. The backup Dominic, waiting in the wings of my own skin in case the main performance failed. Every time I tried to look at that possibility straight-on, something in me flinched — and the flinch itself was damning, because it wasn't horror. It was recognition. Some part of me stood in that lantern room and looked south toward the capital and the throne and felt what Dominic had felt on that balcony: the crown's weight, the crowd's love, the terrible arithmetic of betraying everyone to save them.

I put my hand flat against the cold glass. Below, the starless country stretched away, dark and patient. Nineteen days, the astronomer exile had said. Sixteen now. The moon was counting turns we could barely keep up with.

"Talk to me," I said to the dark, because the dark had been talking to everyone else all year. "Tell me who I am."

The dark, naturally, said nothing. It's the one broker in this kingdom who doesn't deal.

So I did what I do. I inventoried.

Fact: I could not remember my childhood. Not blurred, not faded — absent, with the specific smoothness of ground intentionally scoured. The valley's wards erased travelers. But the wards broke months ago, and everyone else's memories had come flooding back in ugly, disorienting tides — Tam crying over a mother she'd forgotten she missed, Odile suddenly able to read the dead languages of her old maps. Everyone remembered. Except me. My past stayed gone, neat as a swept floor, because there was nothing under the sweep. You can't restore what was never stored.

Fact: I had appeared in the valley as a child, alone, and no one — not Vess, not the oldest drifters — ever recalled my arrival. The valley forgot, yes. But Vess kept ledgers. Vess remembered *everything*. And Vess's ledgers had no first page for me. I simply began, like a story whose opening chapters were torn out by someone who didn't want them read.

Someone had torn them out. Someone had walked a child into a forgetting-valley and left him there with a talent for theft and no origin, and let the years sand him smooth, and waited.

Dominic. Dominic had done it. Or the part of Dominic that planned three moves ahead of his own imprisonment — the part that ordered a valley built to hide something, not imprison someone; the part that bound himself around a monster to trap it; the part that would cut off his own hand and teach it to pick locks if that's what the plan required.

I laughed, up there in the lantern room. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, half sob. Because here was the joke, and it was a good one, the kind Vess would have paid triple for:

I spent my whole life stealing memories from other people because I was starving for a past of my own. And the punchline was that I *had* one — a magnificent one, a royal one, soaked in war and love and treason — and it was locked in the very shards I was carrying, and reclaiming it would mean ceasing to exist. The keeper's reward for delivering the king was erasure. The key's reward for opening the door was becoming the hinge.

Every thief knows the rule: never steal from yourself. It's the one crime with no profit in it.

And it was the only job I had left.

* * *

They found me there at gray dawn, all three of them, which meant Quill had fetched them, which meant she'd decided I shouldn't be alone with the arithmetic. Small mercies from small birds.

Odile brought tea. She handed me the cup without comment, leaned on the rail beside me, and looked out at the lightless horizon with the expression of a woman charting coastline she already knew was doomed.

"You heard," I said.

"Quill talks in her sleep. Loudly. For someone with no lungs."

"Of course she does." I drank the tea. It was terrible. It was perfect. "So. You've had all night to decide what I am."

"I've had all night to decide what *matters*," Odile said. "Which is different." She tapped the rail. "The boy I served under — before the disgrace, before all of it — used to say a map isn't the territory, it's an argument about the territory. Everyone in this company is arguing about what you are. King. Key. Weapon. Decoy. I say the argument's beside the point."

"Enlighten me."

"You pulled Tam out of a cage you had no obligation to enter. You gave Vess the one memory you loved to buy her freedom. You dive into flooded chapels and tomb-cold crypts for a man you've met only as a stranger's joy." She shrugged, old shoulders rising and falling like weather. "Whatever you're made of, that's what it does. The rest is cartography."

From the doorway, Tam — hollow-eyed, shard-voiced, seventeen and carrying more than any seventeen-year-old should — said: "For what it's worth, the thing inside me likes you. Says you feel like coming home." Then she winced at her own words. "Sorry. He says a lot of things at three in the morning."

"And Quill?" I asked. "Does she get a vote?"

"She's voting with her feet," Odile said. "She hasn't left your shoulder since midnight."

I looked, and there she was — I genuinely hadn't noticed, which tells you how far gone I was — riding my shoulder like she'd been carved there. She didn't meet my eye. Crows, I've learned, find confession easier at a distance.

"For the record," she muttered, "I don't care whose flesh you are. I've flown behind kings and I've flown behind thieves, and you're the first one who ever shared his rations with a bird who lied to him. That's rarer than royalty."

High praise. Damning praise. Praise that made my eyes sting, which I blamed on the wind, and nobody was rude enough to contradict me.

* * *

But here's the thing about found families, the thing nobody warns you: they choose you, and their choosing is heavier than any chain. A cage you can hate. A debt you can resent. But four people standing around you at dawn, handing you terrible tea and saying *whatever you are, you're ours* — that you carry. That you carry the way Dominic carried a crown, knowing it weighs exactly as much as a decision.

And the decision was coming. I could feel it assembling itself out of the facts, the way a storm assembles out of heat.

Because the unbinding needed a willing vessel. Tam had already volunteered — had said it with that inherited courage of hers, ward-smith's blood speaking through ward-smith's bones — and I had said no, and she had said *you don't get to protect me from my own choice*, and neither of us had won. But now there was a second vessel available. A better one. A vessel that was, by design, a piece of the king himself — the one vessel the rite might accept without tearing a stranger apart. Me. Standing right here. Pre-owned, broken in, practically begging for the job.

One vessel slot. Two candidates. And if I took it, I solved everything: Tam lived, Dominic woke, the kingdom got its king back, and the world lost a thief nobody would mourn longer than a week. Clean arithmetic. The kind of trade I'd been making my whole life — give up the thing you love so someone else keeps theirs.

Except.

Except Tam would fight me. Except Odile would chart six reasons I was wrong. Except Quill would sit on my shoulder and refuse, absolutely refuse, to let me spend myself like a coin without at least listening to her argue the exchange rate. Except that somewhere between a drowned chapel and a lighthouse, these exiles and liars and runaways had become the first thing I'd ever had that felt like *mine* — not stolen, not inherited, not assigned by a dead king's contingency plan — and spending myself meant spending them too. Their grief. Their mornings. Their terrible tea.

The thief said: run. Take the shards, take the girl, skip the rite, live.

The keeper said: deliver. Stand aside. Be the satchel.

And the king — the quiet, crowned stranger in my marrow — said nothing at all. Just that balcony wind. Just that weight. Just the knowledge that sometimes you betray everyone you love to save them, and they never learn which was true.

Sixteen days.

Below us, the road bent south toward the observatory, and somewhere on that road, I knew, the answer would stop being a question and start being a choice — and choices, in my experience, are the one thing no one can steal from you, and the one thing everyone tries to take.

"We vote tonight," Odile said quietly, watching my face. "All of it. On the table. What you are, what Tam's offering, what happens at the threshold."

"I know."

"Rosalind." She put her hand over mine, and her grip was a cartographer's grip, precise and unyielding. "When they vote — whatever they say — remember that I've drawn maps my whole life, and I have never once seen a border that told the truth about the land on both sides of it."

I didn't know what she meant. I think maybe she didn't either. But I held onto it anyway, the way you pocket a coin you can't yet price, and we went down together to tell the others that the thief, the keeper, and the king had finally agreed on one thing:

It was time to decide.
