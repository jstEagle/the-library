## 25. The Family Decides

I have drawn maps of places that no longer exist. I have charted coastlines that drowned before my grandmother was born, and I have done it with a steady hand and a clear conscience, because a cartographer's first duty is to record what is true, not what is convenient. It took me sixty years and one royal disgrace to learn that the second duty is harder: to decide which truths deserve to survive the telling.

That night in the safehouse, with rain hammering the slates like a debt collector, I learned it again.

* * *

We had finished the second shard three days prior — the war room, the general's tomb, the smell of wet stone still clinging to Rosalind's coat — and since then our little household had developed the particular silence of people avoiding a conversation they all know must happen. Tam mended nets she had no use for. The boy, Pellin, polished lanterns we dared not light. Quill sat on the mantel preening with the aggressive casualness of a bird who has recently confessed to regicide-adjacent crimes and does not wish to discuss it.

And Rosalind — Rosalind cleaned his knives. He did this often. I suspected he found the repetition soothing; there is something honest about a whetstone. It tells you exactly what it thinks of you, in small bright sparks.

I called the vote at sundown.

"Sit down," I said, "all of you, because I'm an old woman and I refuse to say this twice."

They sat. Even Quill hopped down to the table's edge, which from her was the equivalent of removing one's hat.

"You all know why we're here," I began. "But knowing a thing and saying it aloud are different trades, and I've been paid in both." I set my hands flat on the table, the way I used to when presenting charts to men whose crowns depended on my lines being right. "The shards tell us Dominic ordered the valley built. Not as a prison for himself — or not only. To hide something. And they tell us the binding may have been his choice, made freely, for reasons we do not yet understand. Which means the thief sitting at this table — who may be a piece of the king cut loose into flesh, who may be a weapon aimed at the crown, who may be nothing but bait in a trap twenty years deep — is standing on ground we cannot map."

Rosalind didn't look up from his knife. But he stopped moving the stone. In him, that was a shout.

"So we vote," I said. "Not on whether he stays under guard — we're hardly in a position to guard anything, half of us sleep with our boots on out of habit. We vote on whether we continue. Whether we keep walking toward the threshold with him at the front of the line, knowing that every step might be delivering exactly what the crown-thing wants delivered."

Silence, except the rain. Then Tam said, "Who counts?"

"Everyone who eats at this table," I said. "Quill included. She's earned stranger votes than this."

"Flatterer," Quill croaked.

"I'm dying, dear. Flattery is all I have left that isn't rationed."

* * *

Let me tell you how the vote went, and let me tell it honestly, because Rosalind will read this someday — he reads everything in my papers, thinking I don't notice — and because honesty is the only inheritance I intend to leave.

Pellin voted yes first, quickly, the way children vote: with their whole chest and no arithmetic. "He pulled me out of the ash-farm," the boy said. "Whatever he is, he's the man who pulled me out." Then he went red and studied the table.

Tam voted yes too, but slowly, and I watched her weigh it. She has the ward-smith's mind — she cannot look at anything, including a person, without seeing the flaws in its construction. "If he's a decoy," she said carefully, "then Vess already knows where we are, and throwing him out changes nothing except making us smaller. If he's a weapon—" she looked at Rosalind then, directly, and something passed between them that I pretended not to see, "—then I'd rather know which way it's pointed while it's pointed near me. Yes."

Quill voted last of the yeses, and her speech was short enough to fit in a beak. "I helped forge the binding. I have spent every year since being sorry about it in increasingly creative ways. If there's any chance he's a fragment of my king, then abandoning him would just be one more thing to be sorry about, and I am at capacity." A pause. "Also, he feeds me better than the palace ever did. Yes."

Then there were two voices left, and the room got very quiet around them, because everyone knew whose they were.

Mine, and his.

"I'll abstain," Rosalind said.

"No," I said.

"It's my own fate being decided. It's improper—"

"Improper." I laughed, and it came out sharper than I intended. "Boy, I drew the borders of a kingdom for a living. Do you know what I learned about propriety? It is the word powerful men use for the fences they build around other people's choices. You don't get to abstain from your own life. Not here. Not in my house." I softened it as best I could. "Vote. That's an order from a disgraced royal cartographer, which is nearly as good as a royal one."

He looked at me for a long moment. He has eyes that are difficult to hold — not because they're cold, but because they're *full*, like wells you suspect go down further than anyone has measured. Lately, since the shards, they've been fuller still. Sometimes I catch him mid-glance wearing an expression that doesn't belong to him at all: older, sadder, regal in a way that sits wrong on his young face, like a crown borrowed from a larger head.

"Yes," he said finally. "I vote yes. Continue." A pause, and then, with that thief's shrug of his: "Besides, if I'm a trap, I'd rather spring it somewhere with decent company than alone in a ditch."

"That," said Quill, "is the most sensible strategic reasoning I have heard from anyone in this house."

Which left me.

* * *

Here is what I did not say at that table, and what I will say now, into paper, where it can't interrupt me.

I voted against him. Once. Quietly, months ago, before the valley, before the shards, back when he was only a rumor Vess's ledger kept circling — a memory-thief in a forgetting-valley who might matter someday. When Odile came to me with the plan to find him, I said: *and if he's the crown's creature? What then?* And Odile — myself, that is, younger by mere months but feeling centuries younger now — said: *then we'll know soon enough.*

I have spent my whole life believing that knowing soon enough is worth any price. It is the cartographer's creed. Chart the reef, even if the chart sinks ships. Truth first, consequences to the survivors.

But sitting at that table, looking at a young man who had given away his most precious memory to save a girl he'd known a fortnight, who carried a dead stranger's box across a starless country because a crow asked him to, who cleaned knives to calm his nerves the way I used to ink compass roses — looking at him, I found that my creed had developed a crack.

Because here is the ugly arithmetic none of us wanted to speak aloud: if Rosalind is a fragment of Dominic, then the person we'd be voting on isn't fully a person at all. He's a *piece*. An instrument, possibly still strung, possibly still played. And instruments don't get votes. Instruments get *used*. Every argument for continuing was also, secretly, an argument for using him — walking our own possible weapon to the threshold because the alternative was leaving it unattended.

I could not in good conscience vote yes on those terms.

So I changed the terms.

"Before I vote," I said, "I want something added to the ledger. Not a condition — a promise. If it turns out he's a weapon, or a decoy, or anything else that isn't simply *himself* — then we treat that as information about what was done *to* him, never as a verdict on what he *is*. Nobody in this house uses him. Nobody sacrifices him for the mission, not even himself. If the choice comes down to the king or the keeper, we choose the keeper, and the king can be angry with us in whatever afterlife kings frequent." I looked around the table, letting my old woman's glare do the work of ten arguments. "That's my price. Yes, contingent."

"And if someone breaks it?" Tam asked.

"Then they answer to me, and I am *very* tedious when I'm angry."

Rosalind had gone still again. Worse — he had gone *unsteady*, which I'd never seen. His hands, always so sure on locks and knives, had developed a small tremor, and he pressed them flat against the table to hide it, and failed.

"That's a stupid promise," he said quietly.

"Probably."

"You should use me. If it comes to it. I'm — whatever I am, I'm the cheapest thing on this table. You're a royal cartographer. She's the last person alive who served him honestly. The girl is the only ward-smith who knows the sigil's flaws. I'm a thief with a hole where his childhood used to be. The math—"

"The math," I interrupted, "was invented by people who wanted an excuse to spend other people's lives. I did the crown's math for forty years, boy. I drew the lines that told generals where to spend villages. I am *finished* with that arithmetic." I leaned forward. "Vote stands?"

He swallowed. Whatever he was going to say, it died somewhere behind his teeth, and what came out instead, barely audible over the rain, was: "...Yes. Stands."

"Then the motion carries. Five yes, none no, one abstention—"

"I didn't abstain," Quill said.

"—one *abstention*, fine, and one contingent yes, mine, now discharged. Motion carries. We continue." I stood, because old knees announce themselves and also because I wanted the moment to end standing up rather than sitting in it. "Someone put water on for tea. We leave for the observatory road at first light."

Nobody moved. They were all looking at Rosalind.

He was crying. Badly, quietly, the way people cry who learned to do it where no one would notice — head tilted so the tears ran along his jaw instead of off his cheeks, breath held until his shoulders shook. A man drowning politely.

"Oh, you absolute fool," Quill said softly, and hopped onto his shoulder, and pressed her head against his ear, which is the closest a crow comes to an embrace.

"I don't—" he started, stopped, tried again. "No one's ever—" Another failure. Finally he managed, in a voice scraped raw: "You voted on whether to trust me. And you all just — you voted *yes* anyway. Knowing. You knew and you chose—"

"That's generally what trust means," I said. "The alternative word is 'certainty,' and certainty is for gravestones."

He laughed — a broken, wet sound, but a laugh — and put a hand up to Quill's feathers, and for a long moment nobody spoke, and the rain filled the silence the way rain does, generously, asking nothing.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the others had gone to their beds and the fire had burned down to its patient red arithmetic — I heard the floorboard outside my door creak, and then stop, and then creak again, closer.

I opened the door. He was standing there holding a candle like a boy sent on an errand, clearly having rehearsed several speeches and discarded all of them.

"Couldn't sleep?" I asked.

"I slept for twenty years inside a valley that ate memories," he said. "Sleep and I have a complicated relationship."

"Come in, then. There's brandy. It's terrible. It's medicinal."

He came in and sat in the chair by my chart table, among my rolled maps and my brass instruments, and for a while he said nothing, turning his cup slowly, watching the terrible brandy move.

"Why did you add the promise?" he asked eventually. "Before your vote. Why make it a condition?"

"Because conditions are the only promises people believe," I said. "Unconditional things make them nervous. They start wondering what's wrong with the goods."

"No. Really."

I considered lying to him. I am good at it; I lied to a king for years, mostly by omission, which is the courtier's preferred method. But he had cried at my table tonight, and a person who cries at your table has paid some kind of toll, and tolls ought to buy truth.

"Because I needed to know what I believed," I said. "A vote tells you what you feel. A condition tells you what you're willing to *lose*. And when I wrote it — king or keeper, we choose the keeper — I discovered I meant it. Which surprised me. I met that king once, you know. Dominic. Before everything. He reviewed my maps personally, the year I was appointed. He was..." I searched for it, and found, to my irritation, that my eyes had gone hot. "He was very kind to a nervous young woman with ink-stained fingers. Told her the kingdom would be safe as long as someone kept drawing it honestly. Twenty years later I helped draw the lines of his prison without knowing it. So perhaps the promise wasn't for you at all. Perhaps it was penance." I shrugged. "Old women are allowed mixed motives. It's one of the few privileges of the office."

Rosalind looked at me for a long time. And then he did something I did not expect, though perhaps I should have: he reached into his shirt and brought out the box — the dead stranger's box, the one with the lock that answered to no key we'd found — and set it on my chart table between the dividers and the ink pot.

"When we reach the threshold," he said, "if the rite needs a vessel, and Tam can't do it, and it comes down to me — I need you to remember you promised. Even if I argue. *Especially* if I argue. I'm told I argue well when I'm terrified."

"Where'd you hear that?"

"Vess. Once. One of the few true things he ever said to me." His mouth twisted around the name. "I'm keeping a list of them."

I looked at the box. Starlight-inked map inside, or so the stranger had claimed with his last breath; a lock that had defeated Tam, Quill, and one very determined retired forger of royal seals. It sat there on my table like a held breath.

"Why give it to me?" I asked.

"Because you're the only one in this house who draws lines and means them," he said. "And because if something happens to me on the road — if Vess collects his debts, if the crown comes calling — someone should hold it who won't open it out of curiosity." A ghost of the thief's smile. "Curiosity and I have a history."

I took the box. It was lighter than it looked, the way important things usually are.

"Get some sleep," I told him. "First light."

At the door he paused. "Odile. Thank you." And then, before I could deflect it, the wry voice back in place like a mask settling: "For the record, your brandy is a war crime."

"Noted for the archive," I said. "Now go."

* * *

I did not sleep either. Instead I sat with the box and my charts and the dying fire, and I did something I hadn't done since my disgrace: I drew a new line. Just one, small, in the corner of my working map of the road ahead — a mark at the place where the observatory ruins sit below the moonlit threshold, with a notation beside it in my old surveyor's shorthand.

The notation read: *here the map ends and the territory begins.*

Because that is the truth I arrived at, in the end, the one the vote taught me: I have spent my whole life drawing the world as it was, and it has all been preparation for the one task that remains — helping to draw the world as it will be. And whether the young man sleeping down the hall is a thief, a king, a weapon, or a wound given legs and a knife collection, I find that I no longer care overmuch what he *is*.

What matters — what the whole starless kingdom turns on — is what we *choose* to make of each other. And tonight, five voices and one crow chose him. Tonight the family decided.

I blew out the candle.

And in the morning, when I rose before dawn as old women do, I found a note pinned to the box I'd guarded all night, written in a hand I did not recognize — elegant, archaic, unmistakably *regal* — though Rosalind swore on everything he had left that his own hand had touched neither ink nor pin.

It read, in full:

*Thank her for me. Tell her the kingdom was always safest in the keeping of honest drawers of lines.*

*— D.*

Below it, in Rosalind's familiar scrawl, shakier than usual:

*I don't remember writing the top part.*
