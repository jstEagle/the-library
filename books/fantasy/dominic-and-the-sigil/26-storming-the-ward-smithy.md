## 26. Storming the Ward-Smithy

I want it on record that I did not want to go back to the smithy.

Not the way Odile didn't want to cross the salt flats, which meant she'd already packed three maps and sharpened every blade in the safehouse. Not even the way Rosalind didn't want things, which was its own private religion. I mean I genuinely, sincerely, with my whole traitor heart, did not want to walk through the doors of the forge where Master Aldous had taught me to bend light and bind breath, because the last time I'd stood in those doors he'd been screaming at me to run, and I had run, and running is the kind of memory that follows you around like a dog you can't shake off.

But the ghost-notes were there.

That's what Quill called them, anyway, when she'd finished her confession and picked at her feathers for a while and finally said, in the small voice a crow shouldn't have: *Aldous wrote everything down. Even the parts he shouldn't have. If the unbinding rite exists on paper anywhere, it's under his floorboards.*

Under his floorboards. In his forge. The forge with the Regent's hounds quartered two streets over, because of course it was. The crown-thing wasn't stupid. It kept its enemies' houses like keepsakes.

* * *

We went in at the turn of the night watch, which sounds brave and was mostly arithmetic. Odile counted the patrol rotations for two days from the loft above the tannery — she counted everything, that woman, she'd have counted the hairs on your head if you let her get close enough — and announced that we had forty minutes between rounds. Forty minutes to find a dead man's notes in a building that wanted us dead too.

"Forty minutes," Rosalind repeated, in the voice he used when he was doing sums about survival. "The valley gave me thirty seconds at a border crossing and I made a living out of it. Forty's generous."

"You're not stealing memories," I said. "You're stealing from a forge. Forges bite."

"Everything bites," Rosalind said, "if you're bad at it." He smiled the smile he thought nobody noticed didn't reach his eyes anymore. Since the third shard — since the lover's letter, since we learned that unbinding might kill the king and kill him right along with it — his smiles had gone thin as ward-glass. He carried the knowledge the way I carried the shard in my belly: somewhere central, somewhere warm, somewhere that ached when the weather turned.

Because yes. I still had the first shard inside me. Swallowed outside the safehouse while the hounds came through the walls, and it had lived there ever since like a coal that wouldn't cool. Some nights I could feel it turning over in its sleep. Some nights it dreamed, and I dreamed with it, and the dreams had coronation music in them.

Tam, Aldous used to say, you have a talent for standing exactly where the lightning wants to strike. I'd thought it was praise. I'd been fifteen and stupid about praise.

* * *

The smithy hadn't changed, which was the worst part.

Other buildings in the capital wore their fear openly — boarded windows, sigil-chalk scrawled over doorways, the little shrines to the Regent that people built out of terror and called devotion. But Master Aldous's forge sat behind its iron fence like it had never heard an empire fall, brick still soot-black, chimneys still breathing heat, the great doors banded in bronze with the old smith-mark: a hand holding fire, and beneath it the motto he made me copy a hundred times until my letters stopped wobbling.

*What is forged may be unmade.*

I'd carved those words into my own palm once, with a burin, age twelve, to prove I could take pain. He'd found me bleeding at the bench and hadn't scolded me. He'd just said, very quietly, *Good. Now learn to take worse.* And then he'd taught me wards for six years, and then he'd vanished into the dark one morning leaving nothing but chalk dust and a half-eaten apple, and then the hounds came asking questions, and I ran.

Rosalind crouched beside me in the fence shadow, and Quill sat on his shoulder wearing the particular stillness she wore when she was remembering something older than all of us.

"Door wards?" he asked.

"Mine," I said. My mouth had gone dry as kiln-brick. "He let me set the outer layer myself. Last month before he disappeared. Said a ward's only as strong as the worst student who ever touched it, so make sure the worst student is good."

"And?"

"And I was good," I whispered. "That's the problem. I know every flaw I left in it, because he made me write them down in my own hand so I could never pretend they weren't mine." I looked at the door and felt six years old and sixty at once. "He knew I'd come back. That's why he trained me that way. He built the lock and then he handed the key to the person most likely to need it."

Rosalind glanced at me sidelong. "You realize," he said, wry and gentle at once, "that's what everyone keeps doing to you. Handing you keys. Vess's ledger, Odile's map, now this. At some point you're allowed to be angry about being load-bearing."

"I'll schedule it," I said. "Between the raid and the part where we probably die."

"That's very full. I'll pencil you in for grief instead."

And that's the thing about Rosalind that nobody who met him later would believe: underneath all that stolen polish, he joked like a man defusing something. You just had to notice what the something was.

I put my palm flat against the bronze band, found the flaw — third joint of the hand, where I'd miscounted a syllable and covered it with a knot instead of admitting it — and unpicked my own work like pulling a splinter from flesh. The door exhaled warm air that smelled of iron and coal-oil and, faintly, unbearably, of Master Aldous's pipe tobacco.

"Forty minutes," Odile murmured through the earpiece charm she'd insisted on making, though it crackled more than it carried. "Thirty-eight now. Move."

* * *

Inside, the forge was lit by banked coals that should have died months ago.

I stopped dead on the threshold. Rosalind bumped into my back, which he pretended he hadn't. "Coals don't keep," I said. "Not without feeding. Someone's been tending this place."

"Someone," Quill said, from the rafter she'd ghosted up to, "or something." Her eye caught the coal-light and held it. "Ward-smiths leave their fires in trust. There are birds — no. Never mind the birds. Look for the floorboards."

The workshop smelled the way it always had: flux and charcoal and the sweet metallic tang of silver being drawn. His bench sat against the far wall, tools hung in their exact places, each handle worn to a shine by hands that weren't going to wear them anymore. His stool had a dent in the seat shaped like him. I had to look away from the stool.

"Third floorboard from the hearth," Quill said. "Left of the quench trough. He told me once, when he thought I was only a bird and couldn't carry secrets. Birds carry everything. That's rather our whole profession."

I knelt. The board came up with a sound like a held breath released, and underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a bundle: a ledger bound in grey leather, a ring of chalk-stubs worn to nubs, and a folded paper sealed with wax that bore the smith-mark, pressed by a thumb that shook.

My hands shook too, unwrapping it. Rosalind watched the door. Somewhere out in the street, boots passed in rhythm — the patrol, early or late or exactly on time, I couldn't tell, and my whole body turned into a listening thing.

The paper was a letter. Short. Addressed not to me, which hurt, and then I read it and understood it was addressed to whoever was brave enough or foolish enough to be kneeling where I knelt, which hurt differently.

*If you are reading this, the binding has begun to fail, or someone is trying to fail it faster.*

*I built the sigil. Write that on my stone if I get a stone: Aldous Vane built the cage, believing it a sanctuary. Dominic asked for it. Understand that. He asked. He knelt in this forge and asked me to bind him, because the thing that wears his face cannot exist unhosted, and a cage around both of them was the only mercy anyone could think of.*

*It was never meant to be forever. Forever was the price of getting it wrong, and I got one line wrong. One line. The vessel clause. I wrote 'a willing soul' where I should have written 'a willing sovereign.' I meant any soul that chooses. The sigil heard a king's word and read it narrowly, the way kings' words are always read.*

*So the rite needs a vessel — a living will, offered freely, to stand in the threshold and hold the door open while the binding comes apart. It will hurt. I won't lie on paper; paper deserves better. It will hurt the way being born hurts, and for roughly the same reason.*

*I have written out the full rite in the ledger. Check my margins. I was drunk on fear when I drafted it and sober when I corrected it, and the corrections matter.*

*Tam — if it is Tam reading this, and some stubborn corner of me believes it will be — I did not vanish. I was taken. They came for the man who built the cage, and I went quietly so they would stop looking for the apprentice. Whatever they've told you I did, whatever shame you've been carrying like a tool you never chose: put it down. Your hands are your own. They always were. I only ever sharpened them.*

*What is forged may be unmade. I am sorry I left you the unmaking.*

*A.V.*

I don't remember sitting down. I was suddenly on the floor of the forge with the letter in my lap and my eyes burning and Rosalind's hand hovering near my shoulder, not touching, because he understood about touch the way I understood about locks — that some things you offer and some things you wait for.

"He didn't run," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "All these years I thought — everyone thought —"

"He took the blame with him," Rosalind said quietly. "On purpose. So you'd be small enough to overlook." He paused. "That was either love or arrogance. With masters it's usually both."

"It was love," I said, with a certainty that surprised me. "Read the corrections. Arrogance doesn't correct itself."

* * *

The ledger took twenty minutes to read and cost us eleven of our forty.

Master Aldous's handwriting marched across the pages like soldiers: diagrams of the sigil's thirteen turns, each turn annotated, the ninth crossed out twice and rewritten, the twelfth marked with a single furious underline. The unbinding rite occupied the last six pages, and it was beautiful, the way avalanches are beautiful — clean geometry stacked on clean geometry, all of it aimed downhill at something fragile.

At the heart of it, the vessel clause. *A willing soul, freely given, standing in the threshold.* And beside it, in fresher ink, the correction, boxed and underlined:

*NOTE: the sigil will test the offering. It does not accept sacrifice. Sacrifice is taking yourself; willingness is giving yourself. It knows the difference. Anyone who walks in hoping to die will be spat out, and the rite will collapse, and there will not be a second chance. Whoever stands there must want to live afterward. Must want it badly. That is the door hinge: a person who intends to survive.*

I read that paragraph four times. On the fourth reading I noticed my hand had drifted to my own stomach, to the coal-warm weight sleeping behind my ribs, and Quill noticed too. Crows notice everything; it's their whole profession.

"No," Rosalind said, before I'd said anything, before I'd decided anything, which meant he'd been watching me decide it for four readings.

"I'm the only one who can hold a channel," I said. "It's not heroics, it's craft. A vessel has to modulate the flow, ground the backlash, keep the threshold stable while thirteen turns unwind — that's ward-work, that's literally my apprenticeship, that's the exam I never got to sit—"

"Tam."

"—and the shard already lives in me, so the resonance is established, so there's less risk of rejection—"

"Tam." He crouched to my level. His eyes had that starless-valley black in them, the color of everywhere the light refused. "The rite requires a willing vessel. The rite also, according to your dead master's margins, nearly kills the willing vessel. I've spent this entire journey collecting people the world threw away, and I am telling you, as the family's designated thief: I refuse to give anything back that I've decided to keep."

Something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was there.

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever stolen from me," I managed.

"I don't steal from friends," he said. "I borrow. Extensively. Without asking." He held out his hand for the ledger, and I gave it, and our fingers were both steady, which felt like a shared lie we agreed to keep telling.

* * *

We were at the hearthboard, replacing it, when the coals spoke.

I don't know how else to say it. Banked fire has a voice if you're a ward-smith — heat carries charge, charge carries pattern, pattern can carry words if the words were burned in deep enough and recently enough. Master Aldous had known I'd know that. Of course he had. He'd taught me that, in the spring of my fourth year, as a party trick: *listen to the fire, Tam, it gossips.*

The voice that came up through the coals was hoarse and thin and unmistakable, recorded into the embers the way you'd press a seal into wax, and it said:

*"Turn nine is false. I lied in the ledger, deliberately — if you're hearing this, they've taken the ledger once already and returned it, which means they've read it, which means the true rite must live somewhere they'll never think to look. Turn nine is not what the page says. The page says the vessel must stand within the circle. That is wrong. That is the trap. The vessel must stand OUTSIDE it — at the threshold mark, the old observatory's moon-door — or the binding simply transfers, whole, into the vessel, and you will have built the crown-thing a new king with extra steps.*

*I'm sorry. I couldn't write it where they'd find it. Fire remembers longer than paper burns.*

*Tam. If it's you. Check the ninth turn. Trust the ninth turn. Trust yourself past the point where trust feels reasonable, and then trust a little further.*

*What is forged may be unmade. Go and unmake me."*

The embers settled. The forge went quiet as a held tongue.

For a long moment none of us moved. Then Quill dropped from the rafter and landed on the hearthstone, and she looked — there's no other word — stricken.

"The observatory," she said. "The moonlit threshold. The ruined observatory where the rite must occur. Everyone has been saying it. The shards have been pointing at it. And your master just told us—" her feathers flattened along her neck, "—he told us the circle is a mouth."

Outside, boots again. Closer. Off-rhythm — not a patrol, patrols march, these were hunting-stride, and Odile's voice crackled in my ear, stripped of all its counting calm: *"Hounds. Two streets and closing. Whatever you have, have it NOW."*

I looked at the ledger in Rosalind's hands. Ninth turn, page marked, the false instruction waiting in neat soldier-marching script for someone trusting enough to follow it into a new cage.

"Nine days of turns left," Rosalind said. "One trap discovered. One master avenged-in-advance." He tucked the ledger inside his coat, against his heart, where he kept everything he intended to keep. "Let's go be unreasonable somewhere else."

I took one last look at the bench, the stool with its Aldous-shaped dent, the motto banded in bronze on the doors I'd unlocked with my own flaws.

*What is forged may be unmade.*

"Coming, Master," I whispered, to a man who was maybe alive and maybe ash and definitely still teaching. Then I ran, out into the dark, with the true rite burning behind my ribs in a language only fire remembered — and ahead of us, somewhere past the salt flats, the thirteenth turn waited, patient as a lock, hungry as a mouth.
