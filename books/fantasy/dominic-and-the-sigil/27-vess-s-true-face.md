## 27. Vess's True Face

There are three rules I learned in the valley, and I broke all of them on the same morning, which should tell you something about how the rest of my life was going to go.

Rule one: never sell a memory you can't afford to lose. Rule two: never trust a man who pays too much. Rule three: never go back to the broker's shop twice in one week.

I had broken rule one at the drowned chapel, when I gave up my childhood — the reclaimed one, the one Tam and Odile and even Quill had helped me dig out of myself like splinters of glass that turned out to be stained-glass windows once you held them to the light. I'd traded it to Vess for Tam's location, and I'd told myself it was a fair price, because Tam was breathing and a childhood is only breath remembered.

But rules two and three were still intact, and I intended to keep them that way right up until the moment I decided to break them both before breakfast.

"We shouldn't be here," Tam said. She stood in the mouth of the alley across from Vess's establishment — *Establishment* being the polite word for a building that looked like a tax collector's fever dream, all shuttered windows and one door that never quite closed all the way, as if the building itself couldn't decide whether it wanted visitors. "Rosalind. We *shouldn't* be here."

"Correct," I said. "And yet."

"And yet," Odile repeated, in the tone of an old woman who has followed younger fools into alleys for sixty years and has stopped expecting any of them to listen. She had her map-roll under her arm and her bad hip angled toward the wall, which was how she stood when she expected trouble. "Say your reasoning aloud, thief. I want it on record that it sounded stupid before we died."

"Fine." I kept my eyes on the door that wouldn't close. "The rite needs a willing vessel. Tam's master's notes say so. They also say the unbinding has to happen at the threshold during a turn — and there are only four turns left, and the Regent's hounds have found us twice now, both times within days of us finding a shard." I let that sit. "The hounds didn't follow our trail. Hounds don't read maps. Someone's been telling them where we'd be."

Odile went very still, the way she did when she'd already seen the shape of a thing and hated it. "Vess."

"Vess sold me the ledger that led us to his own safehouse. Vess paid me thirteen coins for a coronation memory worth three. Vess asked no questions about where I got any of it, and a broker who doesn't ask questions isn't discreet — he's *informed*." I turned to look at them both. "Every bargain I ever struck with that man, he wasn't buying memories. He was reading them. I've been mailing our route to the enemy one sale at a time."

Tam's face did something complicated. "Then why walk into his shop?"

"Because if I'm right, he already knows everything — where we've been, what we carry, who we are. The only thing he doesn't know," I said, "is that we know. And a trap you can see is just a room with bad manners."

"That," Odile said, pushing off the wall, "was almost clever. I'm noting the date."

* * *

The inside of Vess's shop smelled the way it always had: lamp oil, old paper, and beneath it something faintly sweet and wrong, like fruit rotting behind a locked pantry door. The shelves of bottled memories caught the gray light and threw it back in colors that didn't exist outdoors. I had spent years of my life in this room selling pieces of strangers, and it had never once occurred to me to wonder why a man would build a business at the edge of a valley whose entire purpose was forgetting.

It had not occurred to me because I was a thief, and thieves are experts at not looking at things.

The bell over the door rang its little brass note, and Vess emerged from the back the way he always did — unhurried, immaculate, wiping his long white hands on a cloth that was cleaner than his customers. He was a thin man built out of angles and patience, with hair gone silver in a way that looked less like age and more like frost. His smile arrived on schedule.

"Rosalind Thackeray." He said my name the way he always had, savoring it slightly, as though it were a vintage. "Twice in one month. People will say we're courting."

"I need information," I said. "The unbinding rite. The original sigil-work. Your ledger mentioned a ward-smithy in the capital —"

"Mm." He set down his cloth, folded it, set it aside. Every motion precise. Every motion rehearsed. "You know, I wondered when you'd come to me for that. Not *whether*. When." He glanced past my shoulder. "You've brought friends. How unlike you. You used to work alone."

"People change."

"People are changed," Vess said gently. "That's rather different."

And then he did the thing that made the bottom drop out of my stomach, though it took me a moment to understand why. He walked to the shelf behind his counter — the high shelf, the one I'd never been allowed to touch, the one holding bottles that weren't for sale — and he took down a small vial of silver-gray light and set it on the counter between us with a click.

I knew that light. You spend enough years handling stolen memories, you learn their colors the way a midwife learns cries. Silver-gray, shot through with a thread of gold: that was a coronation. That was *my* coronation, the first thing I ever sold him, the memory I'd lifted off a dying traveler on the border road back when I still believed the valley was the whole world.

"You kept it," I said. My voice came out flat. "You said brokers resell. You said nothing stays on your shelves longer than a season."

"I lied," Vess said pleasantly. "About a great many things. It's the foundation of my profession." He touched the vial without picking it up, the way a man touches a rosary. "Do you know what I actually do, Rosalind? Not buy and sell. Anyone can buy and sell. I *catalogue*. A memory is a map of a mind. And a mind, properly read, tells you where it's been, what it wants, and what it will do next." He smiled again, and this time the smile stayed too long, the way a held note stays too long. "You sold me forty-one memories over six years. Forty-one little letters home."

"Home," I said. "Whose home?"

He tilted his head, and for just a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — the lamplight moved wrong in the shop. The shadows leaned toward him instead of away. The sweet rotting smell thickened. And when he spoke again, his voice hadn't changed at all, and that was the worst part, that it hadn't needed to.

"The crown's, naturally."

Tam made a small sound beside me. Odile's hand went into her coat, where she kept the knife she pretended she didn't.

"The Regent knows about the shards," Vess continued, conversational, terrible. "He has known since the first one surfaced. Do you imagine a thing that has worn a kingdom for thirty years wouldn't feel its prison rattling? Every fragment of Dominic that comes loose rings like a cracked bell. All I had to do was stand near the crack and wait for the echo." He spread his white hands. "And you, Rosalind. You wonderful, predictable creature. You carried the echo straight to me and wrapped it in ribbon. The chapel shard. The tomb shard. The lighthouse. Shall I continue? I have the dates memorized. I have *everything* memorized. Memory is rather my field."

My mouth was dry. "The traveler. On the border road. The one whose coronation I stole —"

"— was a courier of mine, yes. Seeded. The wards erased him on schedule; you harvested him on schedule. Even better than planned." Vess picked up the vial now, held it up to the light, watched the little gold thread turn. "You were meant to be a sensor, dear boy. A tripwire with a face. We built the valley around the king, and we staffed its one road with a boy who'd sell anything, and we waited to see what the dark would cough up. And then—" he looked at me over the vial, and his eyes were kind, and kindness from a thing like him was obscene, "—then you went and did something unplanned. You started *keeping* things. You gathered a family. You grew a spine. You became, and I say this with genuine professional admiration, a problem."

Behind me, very quietly, Odile said: "Thief. The back door."

"There is no back door," Vess said, without looking at her. "There hasn't been since Tuesday. But please — finish your thought. I want you to understand it was yours."

"Why tell me?" I asked. My heart was hammering and I made myself stand still, because running was what he wanted, running was the whole design — flush the fox, run it to ground. "Why reveal yourself? You could have taken the shards at the safehouse. You could have taken them a dozen times."

"Could I have?" Vess set the vial down. "The shards answer to willingness, Rosalind. Surely you've learned that by now. They can't simply be seized — believe me, my master's hounds have tried, and what they brought back was screaming and ash. The fragments must be *carried*, willingly, to the threshold, by someone the king's remnant trusts." He clasped his hands. "Or delivered by someone they trust who has been turned. Which brings us to my offer. Yes, an offer — you didn't think I summoned you here to gloat? Gloating is for people with time."

"I'm not giving you the shards."

"You misunderstand. I don't want the shards." He leaned forward across the counter, and up close his eyes were wrong — not evil-wrong, just *assembled*-wrong, like a face painted by someone who had only had faces described to them. "I want you to finish the job. Bring them to the threshold. All of them, willingly, trusting, at the thirteenth turn — and then step aside, and let my master's rite close the binding forever. In exchange: the valley reborn. Your valley, your road, your trade — except this time you'll remember choosing it. Your family alive and pardoned. The girl's master found. Everything you've been crawling across a starless kingdom to build, handed to you intact." He smiled. "All you lose is a dead man's freedom. And you've met him, haven't you? Inside the shards. You know what he chose. You know he built your prison himself. Ask yourself whose side the truth is actually on."

And that was the knife of it, because I *had* seen what Dominic chose. The war-room shard sat heavy in the box under my coat, and it whispered the same question Vess was whispering: maybe the king wanted his cage. Maybe I was carrying a man to a door he'd nailed shut from the inside.

For one long moment I actually wavered. I'll admit it. There in the smell of rot and lamp oil, with a family at my back and a dead man's doubt in my chest, some tired corner of me thought: *take the deal, take the valley, take the easy ending.*

Then Quill landed on the counter.

She came through the gap above the door that never quite shut — the gap I'd noticed for six years and never once wondered about, because the building couldn't decide if it wanted visitors, but a crow always could. She hit the counter in a burst of black feathers, snatched the silver-gray vial in her beak, and said, in her crisp, disapproving voice:

"He's stalling. The hounds are two streets out. Also, this is mine now."

"*Bird*—" Vess began, and for the first time his composure cracked, just a hairline, just enough to show the pressure underneath.

"Sixty years I've regretted what I helped make," Quill said, wings mantled, feathers bristling, the vial gleaming in her beak. "Don't think I won't add a broker to the list." And she dropped the vial.

It shattered on the floorboards, and the coronation spilled out — trumpets, gold light, a young king's joy and dread, six years of my oldest sale — and the whole shop flinched. Every bottle on every shelf shivered in sympathy, memory calling to memory, and in that half-second of chaos Vess's eyes left mine for the first time.

"Run," I said.

We ran.

* * *

We went out the front, because the back was sealed and because Vess — I understood this even while sprinting — *wanted* us to run somewhere specific, and the only way to beat a designed trap is to be unpredictable about which part of it you die in. So instead of fleeing out of the city the sensible way, toward the salt flats and the open dark, I turned us inward, into the tangle of the old quarter, where Odile's missing-things maps knew shortcuts that hadn't existed on any official chart for a century.

Behind us, unhurried as ever, the shop door closed all the way for the first time in its existence. And then it opened again, and what came out made no sound at all.

"Hounds," Tam gasped, keeping pace beside me, her ward-sense prickling visibly — she ran with her fingers spread like she was feeling spiderwebs in the air. "Three. No — four. They're not following scent, they're following *me*, the shard in me, they — Rosalind, they're following all of us, we're all lit up like lanterns—"

"Then we put out the lanterns," I said, which was bravado, because I had no idea how, and neither did anyone else.

We cut through a fish market just waking up, scattering gulls and curses. Odile ran badly and ran anyway, jaw set, refusing every offered arm with a glare that could curdle milk. Quill flew overhead in ragged circles, and when I glanced up I saw her dip toward the rooftops, toward the shapes crouched against the graying sky — tall, wrong-jointed shapes with the sigil's mark burning where their faces should have been, patient as furniture.

They weren't chasing. That was the detail that turned my blood to well water. They were *herding*. Every alley we chose, every turn, the pressure eased in exactly the direction we were already leaning, gentle as a shepherd's dog, and the direction they were herding us was south. Toward the walls. Toward the gate. Toward the salt flats, wide and flat and moonless, where four hunters could run down anything on two legs.

"They want us on the flats," I shouted. "It's a kill box!"

"Everything is a kill box!" Odile shouted back. "The world is a kill box! Pick your box and run!"

So we ran south, because the threshold was south, because nineteen days had become eighteen, because the only thing worse than walking into their trap was standing still long enough for them to spring it politely. Behind us the city woke and yawned, ignorant, starless, crowned. Ahead the gates opened onto the pale nothing of the salt, and the hounds flowed along the rooftops above us like water finding its level.

And in my ear, low and grim, Quill flew close and said the thing I was already thinking, the thing I'd carry out onto those flats like a stone in my boot:

"He let us go, Rosalind. Remember that. Whatever happens next — every road we take from here, ask yourself who paved it."
