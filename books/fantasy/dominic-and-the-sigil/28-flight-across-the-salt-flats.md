## 28. Flight Across the Salt Flats

I have drawn maps of every kingdom that ever pretended to be civilized, and I will tell you a truth no cartographer puts in the margins: every map is a confession. You can read a nation's fears in what it leaves off its charts. Uncharted reefs. Unnamed villages. Borders drawn in confident ink over swampland nobody wanted.

The Salt Flats were the kingdom's great omission. Three days' walk of blinding white crust where an inland sea had died and nobody bothered to mourn it properly—no roads, no wells, no landmarks, and therefore, on every royal map I'd ever inked, a polite blank space with the words *barren wastes* written across it in my predecessor's hand.

Barren wastes. I had laughed at that once, in another life, with a title and a seal and a king who trusted me to know where things were.

Now I was crossing it at night under a sky with nothing left to navigate by, carrying a satchel of memory-shards, a stolen astrolabe, and a wound in my side that I was pretending was a cramp, because if I named it correctly I would have to stop walking, and I did not intend to stop walking until we reached the observatory or fell down trying.

"You're limping," Rosalind said, without turning around. He had a thief's eyes even now, even exhausted—always reading the ground ahead of his feet, always counting exits.

"I'm *walking with character*," I said. "There's a difference."

"Your character is bleeding through your shirt."

"My character has been bleeding through various garments since before you were born, boy. It's how you know it's mine."

He didn't laugh. That worried me more than the blood. Rosalind Thackeray could find the funny bone in a funeral; when he went quiet, the world had earned it.

Behind us, Tam stumbled along with Quill perched on her shoulder like a small black judgment. The girl hadn't slept properly since the prison. She kept her hands balled in her pockets, and twice tonight I'd caught her murmuring to herself—not praying, not talking to Quill, but answering something only she could hear. The shard inside her was waking up as the turns ran out. She said it felt like sharing a bed with a stranger who talked in his sleep.

"Two miles to the pass," I announced, because announcing things is what old women do instead of screaming. "The narrows between the salt hills. If Vess wants to take those shards from us, he has to send his hounds single file."

"And then what?" Tam asked.

"Then they come single file," I said, "into a very narrow place, where a very stubborn old woman will be waiting with a very sharp piece of iron."

Rosalind stopped. Turned. Looked at me the way he looked at locks he intended to open.

"No," he said.

"It wasn't a question."

"It was a terrible plan wearing a question's clothes." He stepped in front of me, which was either gallantry or stupidity, and with that boy it was usually both wearing matching boots. "You can barely stand."

"I can stand *exactly* as long as I need to," I said. "That's all standing has ever required. Ask any statue."

Quill ruffled her feathers on Tam's shoulder. "She's dying," the crow said flatly, because crows have never learned the kindness of euphemism. "I've seen it before. In the war. The ones who say they'll hold the pass are the ones who mean: bury me facing the enemy."

"Thank you, Quill," I said. "You are a comfort and a delight."

"I'm honest. It's different."

* * *

Let me tell you about the night we crossed the Salt Flats, because someone should record it accurately, and accurate recording has always been my particular sin.

There was no moon. There had been no moon worth the name for years—the crown-thing had seen to that, dimming the sky the way a miser dims lamps—but tonight even the thin grey smear of it was absent, and the stars were still gone, all of them, every one Odile the cartographer had charted in her youth and spent her disgrace trying to map their absence. We walked by feel and by Rosalind's uncanny thief-sense for open ground and by the faint phosphorescence of the salt itself, which glimmered pale green under our boots like the corpse of the sea remembering it used to shine.

And behind us, out of the dark, came the sound of the hounds.

Not dogs. I want that understood. Sigil-bound hunters don't bark; they *ring*, a low bell-tone that travels through the ground and up through your boot soles and settles behind your sternum like a swallowed churchbell. You feel them coming in your teeth. Tam heard them first—of course she did—and went rigid, and whispered, "Three. No. Four. One of them is big."

"The big one's for me, then," I said. "They always send the big one for the loud one."

Nobody argued that I was the loud one. That stung a little, honestly. I've built a career on being the loudest person in any room I'm thrown out of.

We reached the narrows an hour past midnight, if midnight still meant anything, which I maintain it does out of pure professional principle. The salt hills rose around us in wind-carved ridges, white cliffs no taller than a man, worn into flutes and hollows that whistled when the wind moved through them—a whole dead ocean playing its own requiem. Between two of the largest ridges ran a defile maybe eight feet wide, walled in crusted salt that glittered faintly, the only road through for anyone who didn't fancy a long detour across crust that would crack and swallow a horse to the girth.

It was a good position. I should know. I once mapped a fortress into existence out of less than this, back when the king asked me where we could make a stand against three times our number and I gave him a goat track in the Greywethers and a lie about reinforcements, and we held, and he laughed, and the world still had a shape I recognized.

"Odile," Rosalind started.

"Don't waste breath arguing with me," I said. "I've argued with kings. You're a significant step down, and I say that with love."

"With love," he repeated, flat.

"With enormous love. Now listen. The shards go with you. All of them. Tam goes with you, because the shard inside her needs the threshold and the threshold needs her. Quill goes with you, because that bird has more lives left to spend and I won't have her spending them on my account. And I stay here, in the narrows, with the iron and the salt and my considerable experience of making trouble last longer than it should."

"You'll die."

"Everyone dies, boy. The trick—which I learned from watching you steal memories from travelers who thought they were safe—is to decide what gets taken from you and when." I took his face in my hands. He let me, which told me he already knew how this argument ended. His jaw was stubbled and his eyes were red-rimmed and he looked, in the green salt-light, exactly like what he was: a fragment of a broken king who had chosen, against every design of fate and sigil, to be a person first. "Go on. I'll catch up."

"That's a lie."

"It's a *stubbornness*," I corrected. "Lies are for people with imagination. I'm a cartographer. I only deal in terrain I've personally verified, and I have personally verified that I am too mean to die in a ditch in a blank spot on the map."

He pressed his forehead to mine. For a moment neither of us said anything, and the wind played the salt hills like pipes, and somewhere behind us the bell-hounds rang closer.

"If you're not at the observatory by the eleventh turn—" he began.

"Then leave my share of the wine to Tam, who'll drink it badly, and my instruments to the archive, and put on my headstone: *She drew the line here.*" I smiled. "Literally. That's a cartographer joke. You wouldn't get it."

"I get it," he said. "It's terrible."

"It's mine." I shoved him, gently, toward the dark. "Go. Before I remember that I raised you into a sentimentalist and disown you."

Tam hugged me—quick, fierce, smelling of prison straw and ward-smoke and, underneath it, of the strange cold perfume of the thing that spoke through her. "Hold the pass," she whispered.

"Obviously," I said. "What else would I do, surrender? I have a reputation."

Quill landed on my shoulder, just for a moment. Crows weigh almost nothing. I don't know why that surprised me; you'd think something that carries that much guilt would be heavier.

"When this is done," the crow said quietly, "there is a debt between us, Odile of the Greywethers. You held a door for a king I helped break. I do not forget doors."

"Fly, bird," I said. "Debts keep."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about holding a pass alone: it is mostly waiting, and the waiting is worse than the fighting, because during the fighting you're too busy to count your own heartbeats.

I wedged myself into a fold of the eastern ridge with my back against the salt and my knife—my good knife, the one with the antler grip that a king once handed me with the words *for drawing lines, Odile*—held low against my thigh. I'd piled loose salt-crust at the mouth of the defile, not as a barricade—you can't barricade anything with snow that bites—but to make noise, to make them think the passage was watched by more than one old woman and her bad hip.

The hounds came forty minutes later. I know because I counted, the way I count everything. Counting is prayer for practical women.

Four shapes flowed into the narrows like spilled mercury given ambition. Up close, the sigil-bound hunters were worse than the rumors: man-shaped, mostly, but wrong in the way a portrait is wrong after water damage, features run and re-set, their skin bearing the faint gold tracery of the binding script that kept them moving long past the point where meat would have quit. Their eyes were the worst part. They had no eyes at all—just smooth patches where eyes should be—and yet they saw. They saw the way wards see. The way prisons see.

The big one stopped ten paces away and rang. Not a challenge. A *message*: *give us the shards and the vessel and the fragment-of-the-king called Rosalind, and you may keep your life, which the Regent notes has little market value.*

"They sent you to negotiate?" I called, keeping my voice steady, because a shaking voice carries farther than a steady one and I didn't want the words reaching the others. "The Regent must be short-staffed. Or cheap. Both, knowing him."

*The Regent offers,* the hound rang, *mercy.*

"He offered me mercy once before," I said. "Thirty-one years ago, when I refused to burn the maps of the valley. Do you know what he did instead? He erased me. Title, seal, name off the rolls—as if a person could be uncharted." I stood up out of my fold, slow, letting the knife hang loose. My side screamed. I informed my side, privately, that it would be compensated later. "So here's my counteroffer, and I'll draw it plainly so even a blind thing can follow the lines: the shards are going to a ruined observatory, the vessel is going to kick your master's teeth out through the top of his skull, and the king is going home. That's the route. It's final. I checked the surveying myself."

The hounds came all at once.

I will not give you a blow-by-blow; battle reports are for generals and liars, and I have been the first and mistaken myself for the second often enough. What I remember is this: the narrowness saved me, because four cannot come at one old woman eight feet wide without queueing, and queuing is death to hunters bred for the pack. What I remember is the first one's throat opening under the antler-grip knife with a sound like tearing parchment, and the gold script in its skin guttering out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. What I remember is the second one's claws opening my forearm to the bone and me laughing, actually laughing, because pain is just the body's way of insisting it's still on the map.

What I remember most is thinking of them—Rosalind with his thief's grin and his terrible jokes, Tam with her chalk-dusted fingers and her borrowed courage, even that feathered little traitor Quill—and thinking: *this is what family is.* Not blood. Not oath. It's the people whose faces arrive, uninvited, in the exact moment you need a reason not to fall down.

The third hound got inside my guard. Of course it did. I was slow, and old, and leaking.

Its hand went into my side below the ribs, and the world went white and then green, green as the salt-light, and I heard myself make a sound I hope the wind took away before it reached anyone's ears. But my knife was already moving—I'd taught it the route years ago, back and up, the Greywethers maneuver, thank you, Your Majesty, for that lesson in goat tracks—and the hound rang once, high, a bell cracking, and folded.

Which left the big one.

It stood at the mouth of the defile and regarded me with its smooth blind face, and it did not charge. Smart. The smart ones are always the last, because stupid things spend themselves early—that's true of hounds and men and kingdoms alike. Instead it rang, low and long, and I felt the note crawl through the salt and up my spine, and I understood, with a cartographer's clarity, that it wasn't attacking.

It was reporting. Marking my position on *its* master's map. Telling the Regent: *the old woman is here, and she is dying, and the others are two miles ahead in the dark, and here are their bearings.*

"Oh no you don't," I rasped, and threw myself at it.

I have fought perhaps nine serious fights in my long, ill-advised life, and I have won six, and I want it recorded that the seventh was a draw achieved through cheating, terrain, and sheer refusal. We went down together into the salt crust, which broke beneath us like the skin of a frozen lake, and I got the knife in twice, three times, feeling the binding-script heat against my knuckles like a stove-top, and it got its claws into my shoulder and my scalp and everywhere, and at some point the ringing stopped and the thing under me stopped too, and I lay there in the broken white with the green light fading and my blood soaking down into the corpse of a sea that had waited centuries for rain.

"Drawn," I told the dark. "Seventh fight. Draw. Write it down."

* * *

I don't know how long I lay there. Time went soft, the way distance does in fog. I catalogued my injuries the way I'd catalogue a damaged manuscript—left arm unusable; right side, deep puncture, below the ribs, bleeding slower now, which is either healing or the beginning of not having enough left to bleed, hard to say; scalp, superficial; pride, total loss.

Get up, Odile.

I got up. Mostly. I got up the way a ship gets up out of drydock—badly, with groaning, in stages. I found my knife. I found the satchel I'd carried for thirty-one years, with its rolled maps of a valley that was never supposed to exist, and I held onto it, because some cargo you don't set down even when your hands stop working properly.

Then I started walking. North. Toward the observatory. Toward the children I'd collected along the road like strays—who, I note for accuracy, had in fact collected *me*, though I'll deny saying so if asked.

One step. Another. The salt crunched. Somewhere far off, the wind played its dead-sea pipes, and I thought I heard, threaded through them, a sound I hadn't heard in thirty-one years: bells. Small ones. The kind they ring in village chapels at dawn. Which was impossible, because there were no chapels out here, and no dawn, and no bells left in a kingdom that had sold its sky.

Unless.

Unless the twelfth turn was close. Unless the threshold was waking. Unless everything we'd bled for was about to matter, and I was going to miss it by a mile and half a pint of blood.

"Absolutely not," I informed the universe. "I did not survive a usurper, a disgrace, a prison, a crow, and four sigil-bound horrors to expire in a *blank space on the map*. I refuse. Find another blank space. This one's taken."

I walked. I counted steps, because counting is prayer. At step four hundred I sat down without meaning to. At step four hundred and eleven, I saw lights ahead—green salt-light, yes, but above it, impossibly, a thread of silver, like moonlight pooling in a place where no moon hung.

And rising out of that silver, silhouetted against it, two figures. One limping. One small, with a crow-shaped shadow circling her head like a dark halo.

Rosalind had turned back. Of course he had. Stubbornness runs in families—it has to; it doesn't come from anywhere else.

"Odile!" Tam's voice, cracked and young. "Hold on! Hold on, we've got you!"

"Caught up," I managed, as the silver light washed over me and my knees finally, finally filed their formal complaint. "Told you. Cartographers... don't deal... in terrain we haven't verified..."

The ground tilted up to meet me, and the last thing I saw before the dark took its turn holding *me* was Rosalind's face, furious and wet-eyed and grinning all at once, and beyond his shoulder, growing brighter with every heartbeat—

a star.

One star. Returning. Right above the ruined observatory, burning like a signature on a document the sky had finally gotten around to signing.

"Well," I whispered. "About time somebody drew that line."
