## 29. The Tenth Turn

There is a particular kind of tired that arrives after you have run for three days across a salt flat with a bag of someone else's memories digging into your shoulder and a crow shouting directions at you from above. I had read about exhaustion once, in a stolen memory of a soldier's — a man who described it as a wall you eventually pass through, on the other side of which is a strange clean calm. He was lying, or I am built wrong, because I never found the wall. I just kept getting more tired, the way a candle keeps getting shorter.

But there was this: on the third night, cresting the last ridge of the grey hills with Tam wheezing beside me and Quill riding my shoulder like a small feathered judgment, I looked down and saw the observatory, and for one moment — one whole, unbroken moment — I forgot to be afraid.

It sat in a bowl of the hills like a broken eggshell, white stone gone bone-grey under the moonless sky, its great dome split open to the heavens like a skull that had been thinking too hard. Even ruined, even half-swallowed by centuries of lichen and ivy, it had the posture of a place that had once been important. The kind of building people had crossed kingdoms to reach, back when there were stars to cross them for.

"There," Quill said, and her voice did something I had almost never heard it do. It went quiet. "That's where he used to bring me. Before."

"Before what?" Tam asked.

"Before everything," said Quill, and would not say more.

* * *

We came down to it slowly, because after the salt flats none of us trusted open ground anymore. Vess's agents had nearly taken us twice on the crossing, and Odile —

Odile was not with us. That is the sentence I keep having to start over, because every time I write it in my head it comes out wrong. Odile held the pass at the far edge of the flats so that we could get away, and she told us — shouted it, actually, over the sound of hound-claws on salt crust, with an arrow already standing in her shoulder — *go on, I'll catch up.* And Tam had cried, and Quill had screamed something vicious and ancient, and I had done the arithmetic that leaders do, the ugly little sums where a person becomes a number, and I had turned north and run.

She has not caught up. Three days. I tell myself the flats are wide and she is stubborn and stubbornness has carried her through worse. I tell myself that if she were dead, some part of me would know, the way you know when a tooth has gone bad before it starts to hurt. I tell myself a great many things, and then I look at the empty road behind us, and I stop telling myself anything at all.

The observatory's front doors had long since fallen in, or been taken — good white oak, probably, feeding somebody's fire a hundred years ago. We walked in over the threshold like trespassers, though Quill insisted we were the opposite of trespassers, that this place had been waiting for us, which is exactly the sort of thing a building says about itself right before the floor gives way.

Inside, the great hall opened upward into the broken dome. Moonlight — real moonlight, thin and silver and the only light left in the world that hadn't been stolen — poured through the split in the roof and lay across the floor in a long pale stripe, and in the middle of that stripe stood the thing I had been carrying three days' worth of nightmares to find.

The threshold.

I don't know what I expected. An altar, maybe. A door. Something with obvious hinges. What stood there instead was a ring of white stone set into the floor, perhaps ten paces across, carved so densely with script that from a distance it looked like solid metal. The moonlight touched it and the carvings drank the light and gave it back changed — silver going to gold going to a color I don't have a name for, the color of the sky before dawn if dawn were a feeling instead of a time.

Tam made a sound low in her throat. "Ward-work," she whispered. "Old ward-work. Older than my master. Older than his master's master." She knelt at the ring's edge without touching it, the way you'd kneel at the edge of a well, and her fingers hovered over the script. "This isn't built *on* the ground, Rosalind. This is holding the ground together. This whole valley of hills — I'd bet my remaining fingers it doesn't exist anywhere except inside this circle."

"The eye of the needle," I said.

"The eye of the needle," she agreed.

Quill fluttered down from my shoulder onto the ring's rim, and her talons clicked against the stone, and the stone — I swear this, and I know how it sounds — seemed to lean toward her. Like a dog recognizing a footstep.

"He stood here," she said softly. "The night they bound him. Right there, in the center. He asked me to leave. I wouldn't." A pause. Her feathers settled, one by one, the way a person's shoulders settle when they've decided to carry something. "I've regretted it for a very long time. Though I suppose if I'd gone, none of you would be here, and the world would still be dark, and I'd be a regret with no purpose. Regret with a purpose is called a plan. I read that somewhere. Possibly in a memory you stole."

"I don't steal anymore," I said. "I keep."

"Mm," said Quill, who had known me too long to let a rebranding go unchallenged.

* * *

We made camp in the observatory's library, because Tam found a chimney that still drew and because the library had one intact wall, and after the salt flats a wall felt like a luxury. There were books, most of them ruined, their pages bloomed with damp and their ink faded to ghosts of ghosts. But the shelves themselves told a story. Astronomical charts. Tables of lunar phases. And everywhere, worked into the wood, into the iron fittings, into the very mortar: thirteen. Thirteen spokes on the wheel-carvings. Thirteen notches on the reading desks. Thirteen stones in the arches, always the thirteenth slightly different, slightly darker, as if the builders couldn't help leaving a door in everything they made.

"The thirteenth turn," I said, turning a dead book over in my hands. "The stranger's last words. Vess's ledger. Now this." I looked up at the broken dome, at the black sky showing through. "Nineteen days, the astronomer said. Then whatever window this rite needs closes forever."

"Eighteen now," Tam corrected. She was building a fire with the economy of someone who had grown up around forges, arranging the wood like she was setting a bone. "And it's not just a window closing. It's a door locking. My master's notes were clear — the binding sigil has thirteen degrees of turn, like a key. Each turn of the moon since the binding has seated it deeper. On the thirteenth, it seats true, and nothing unseats it. Not a willing vessel. Not a god. Nothing." She struck the flint, and firelight jumped up and made her face flicker between girl and ghost. "He designed it that way on purpose. Whoever 'he' was. The original smith. He wanted the lock to be permanent."

"Why would anyone build a prison that can't be opened?"

"Because they were afraid," Tam said simply. "Fear builds better than hope does. Hope forgets to add the second lock."

Quill, from her perch on a shelf, said nothing, but she had gone very still in the way she went still whenever the binding came up, and I had learned by now that her silences were louder than her opinions.

* * *

I slept badly, which is to say I slept the way a knife sleeps, all edge. Somewhere past midnight I gave up on it entirely and walked back out into the great hall, and that was where I found Tam, sitting cross-legged at the edge of the threshold-ring with her journal open on her knees, writing.

Writing in the other hand.

Her left hand moved smooth and sure while her right lay slack, and the script flowing off the nib was nothing like her usual cramped, forge-trained lettering. It was tall and old-fashioned, with flourishes, the hand of someone educated by tutors who cared about beauty. Dominic's hand. I had seen it in the shards, in the margins of war maps and the foot of a coronation oath.

"Tamsin," I said quietly.

She didn't stop writing. "He's close," she said, and her voice was hers but layered, like two singers an octave apart. "Now that we're here, he's — loud. Happy. Scared. All of it at once." The pen paused. "He says the ring remembers him. He says—" and here her face did something complicated, grief and wonder fighting for the same ground, "—he says he can feel the moonlight. Through me. It's the first light he's touched in thirty years, Rosalind. He's crying. I'm crying. I don't know whose tears these are."

I crouched beside her, careful of the ring's edge, careful of everything. "What else does he say?"

Her eyes came up, and for a moment they were not entirely her color. "He says thank you. He says he knows what it's cost. He says—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened, and when she spoke again it was in her own voice alone, roughened: "He says the vessel must be willing. Truly willing. Not brave-willing. Not desperate-willing. The sigil can taste the difference." She looked down at her own hands, both of them now, as if checking they were still hers. "Rosalind, I need to say something and you're going to hate it."

"Then say it fast."

"It should be me." She said it level, no tremor, which somehow made it worse. "I'm the ward-smith. I know the flaws in the binding because my master taught me its flaws before he vanished, and I have spent every mile since learning the rest. If something goes wrong inside that ring, I'll know what broke and how. You won't. You're a thief with a bag full of a king."

"I'm a fragment of a king," I corrected. "Which is worse, or better, depending on the day."

"You're a person," Tam said fiercely, and the firelight caught the wet on her cheeks. "Whatever you're made of. You're a person who chose us. That's not raw material. That's not a component." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears. "So think about it. That's all I ask. Think about it before you do the noble stupid thing."

I thought about telling her I'd already decided. I thought about lying to her, which would have been easy, because I am very good at lying and she is very tired. Instead I said, "I'll think about it," and watched her decide to believe me, and hated myself a little for how much relief that cost us both.

* * *

Near dawn — real dawn, grey and starless, the sun rising behind cloud like a coin behind frosted glass — Quill woke me by walking up my chest and standing on my sternum, which is her way of knocking.

"Rooftop," she said. "You'll want to see this. Or you won't. Either way, come."

The dome could be climbed by way of a maintenance stair that had mostly survived, and we came out onto the curved white back of the observatory with the whole bowl of hills spread below us and the sky pressing down like a lid. The air was cold and tasted of coming weather. And east, along the road we had come by — the road Odile had not come by —

Lanterns.

A line of them, strung out along the valley floor, moving. Not hurrying. Processing. Dozens of lights in a slow, deliberate procession, the way you'd travel if you wanted the people ahead of you to have plenty of time to watch you come.

"That's not a patrol," Tam said behind me. "Patrols move fast and sloppy. That's a parade."

"It's an invitation," Quill said grimly.

I counted the lanterns without meaning to. Old habit — counting exits, counting coins, counting turns. I got to twelve and stopped, because the thirteenth light was not a lantern at all. It was taller, brighter, burning a shade of gold I recognized down to my bones, the same impossible gold as the threshold-stone drinking moonlight.

The color of the sigil.

"They know we're here," Tam whispered. "They've known since the flats."

"Since before the flats," Quill said. "Vess collected every memory our boy ever sold. Every bargain was a map. They didn't follow us here." She fluffed her feathers against the wind, and beneath the grimness in her voice there was something older and sadder. "They *escorted* us. We were never running toward the threshold, Rosalind. We were being herded to it. The only question left is whether they want the rite performed — or prevented."

Below us, unhurried, patient as a key entering a lock, the procession of lights began to climb the hill.

Behind me, faint as breath, the threshold-ring in the hall below began — very softly, very sweetly, like a struck glass — to sing.
