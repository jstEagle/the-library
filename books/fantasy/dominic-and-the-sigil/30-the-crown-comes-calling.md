## 30. The Crown Comes Calling

The trouble with exhaustion, I've decided, is that it makes everything look reasonable.

I'd noticed this before, in smaller ways. On my third night without sleep in the valley, the border road had seemed like a perfectly sensible place to lie down. Once, starving after a bad week of trade, Vess's moldy bread had looked like a feast. Tiredness is a liar with a gentle voice. It tells you that whatever is nearest is also best, that the path of least resistance is a road and not a trap.

So when the Regent of the starless kingdom walked into our ruined observatory on the morning of the tenth turn—alone, unarmed, wearing a traveling cloak the color of wet slate instead of his crown—I understood, even as my heart tried to climb out through my ribs, that he had chosen this moment deliberately. We were nine days from the sigil turning permanent. We were four people and one crow, half of us wounded, all of us running on fumes and spite. And here came the most dangerous creature in the world, carrying not an army but an offer.

"Master Thackeray," he said, and smiled.

It was Dominic's smile.

I want you to understand what that did to me. I have seen that smile from the inside—from the inside of Dominic's skull, in the coronation shard, when a nineteen-year-old king turned to his assembled court and grinned like the whole ceremony was a joke between him and his oldest friend. I have carried that smile in my chest for weeks now, along with his dread of the marriage negotiations, his grief for a general buried too soon, his terrible handwriting on a letter to a lover whose name the flood took. I know that smile better than I know my own face, which, given recent revelations about whose face mine actually is, is saying something.

And there it stood, on the wrong man, in the doorway where the moonlight used to come in.

"You're not welcome here," Odile said. She had a crossbow. She'd had it up since before he finished materializing out of the stairwell shadow, which told you something about her reflexes at seventy-one. "There are wards."

"There were," the Regent agreed pleasantly. "Your apprentice's work. Very good work, for someone who learned from my smithy. I've left them intact, incidentally. I'm not here to break anything." He spread empty hands. "May I sit? Standing conversations always feel like negotiations, and I promise you, Rosalind, this isn't one."

"It feels like one," I said.

"Because you're a trader by instinct. Everything feels like negotiation to you. It's one of the things I admire about you." He lowered himself onto a fallen lintel with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once worried about ambush, which was either arrogance or truth, and with him it was impossible to say which was worse. "But no. This is a gift."

Behind me, Tam made a small sound. Quill, perched on her shoulder, had gone absolutely still—not the stillness of a sleeping bird but of one doing arithmetic. Later she would tell me she'd been counting exits and finding them all imaginary.

"I'll stand," I said.

"As you like." He folded his hands. Up close, in honest light—or the closest thing we had to honest light, which was a lamp Tam had warded three times over—the resemblance to Dominic was worse than uncanny. It wasn't resemblance. It was the same face, the same hands, the same way of tilting the head when listening. Like looking at a portrait that kept breathing. The wrongness lived underneath, in the pauses, in the half-second delay before each expression arrived, as though the face were being remembered rather than worn. Which, I supposed, it was.

"You've seen the shards," he said. "All three, yes? The coronation. The war room. The lighthouse." He said *lighthouse* gently, the way you'd touch a bruise on someone else to show you knew exactly how much it hurt. "Then you know more about Dominic Vale than anyone alive, including, I suspect, Dominic Vale. So let's not waste the tenth turn pretending. You know what he built the valley for. You know what he bound himself to do. You know—" and here the smile flickered, and beneath it for one unguarded instant was something old and tired and hungry, "—what the binding costs."

"Costs," I repeated. "That's an interesting word for it. Most people would say *kills*."

"Most people haven't read the rite." He glanced at Tam. "No offense intended, child. Your master taught you the flaws, I'm told, but not the architecture. A vessel doesn't die in the unbinding. A vessel is *spent*. There's a difference, if a grim one. Ask your crow sometime about the first draft of the sigil, and what happened to the woman who tested it."

Quill's feathers rose. Tam's hand came up and covered the bird protectively, absently, the way you'd shield a child's ears.

"But I'm getting ahead of myself," the Regent said. "Gifts first. Recriminations later, if you insist on them."

* * *

Here is what he offered, laid out in a voice like warm honey poured over cold iron.

He offered me my valley back. Not the memoryless prison version—the valley *repaired*. Wards rebuilt, but honest ones this time, wards that guarded rather than erased. He said the word *keeper*, and I felt it land somewhere under my sternum like a coin dropped down a well. Keeper of the border. Steward of the crossing. A house with a real roof. Travelers passing through safely, tipping their hats to the strange man who lived at the edge of the maps.

He offered me a past. "You're a fragment of him," he said, matter-of-fact, watching me flinch. "You must know it by now—the crow knows, you suspect, and I *confirmed* it the day your broker sold me your first memory. Did you wonder why I paid thirteen coins for a coronation any fool could have described? I wasn't buying merchandise, Rosalind. I was taking inventory. Every memory you stole and sold, I bought back, because every one of them hummed. They were his. Which means some part of you is his. Which means the rest of you—the parts you think of as *yourself*, the boy with no history, the thief with rules, the lonely man working the border road—is recoverable. I can give you back twenty years of a life you don't remember living. A mother. A name before Thackeray. Whatever the cutting left behind."

He offered me family. This was the cruel one, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, because cruelty done politely is still cruelty, it just files off the sharp edges so you can swallow it easier. "The exiles will scatter when this ends," he said. "Odile wants redemption; give her the archive she dreams of and she'll drown in maps until she dies content. The ward-smith will go back to forging; it's all she knows. But you—you'll have nothing. A name that isn't yours, a face that belonged to a king, and a hole where your life should be. Unless," and he leaned forward, and for a moment the mask slipped so completely that what looked out at me wasn't a usurper or a monster but something genuinely, horribly *lonely*, "unless you take what I'm offering. A household. A purpose. People who will know you and keep knowing you, because I'll see to it that they remember. You've been collecting other people's memories your whole life, Rosalind. Doesn't it tempt you, just once, to be given one?"

And then, softly, the price: "All I ask is the shards. Willingly delivered. No rite. No vessel spent. No thirteenth turn. Dominic stays bound, the crown stays worn, and the kingdom keeps its peace—which, whatever your exiles tell you, is a real thing that real people are grateful for. You've traveled my roads. You've seen the villages. No wars. No succession crisis. One ruler who cannot die, cannot be corrupted, because he has no self to corrupt. Order, Rosalind. You of all people should appreciate order. You lived by rules in a lawless place. Imagine rules everywhere."

* * *

I want to be honest with you, whoever ends up reading this—and someone will, because I intend to write it all down eventually, in a book with an honest title. I want to be honest, so I'll admit it plainly:

For about ninety seconds, I wanted to say yes.

Not the valley, particularly. Not even the memories, though God, the memories—a mother, a childhood, a *before*—that temptation was a live coal in my chest the entire time he spoke. What got me, what nearly undid me right there on the observatory floor with my found family watching, was the word *family*. Said in Dominic's voice. Because I'd spent weeks inside that voice by then, inside his joy at a coronation, his dread in a war room, his love in a lighthouse letter, and some treacherous corner of me had already decided that being spoken to in that voice felt like being spoken to by home.

The valley had stolen my past. The Regent was offering to sell it back, plus interest, plus a future, plus people. All it cost was a king's freedom, a friend's courage, a bird's sacrifice-in-waiting, and a girl's willingness to burn herself as fuel.

A bargain. Just another bargain. I'd made hundreds. Steal from travelers before the valley ate their minds; sell what you can carry; never take more than you need; never let them see your face twice. My rules had kept me alive for years. And the Regent's offer fit inside those rules so neatly it was obscene. Nobody had to get hurt. Nobody had to be *spent*. The shards were just merchandise, and merchandise gets sold, and the seller walks away rich.

Ninety seconds. Maybe less. Long enough for Odile's crossbow arm to tremble. Long enough for Tam to whisper my name like a question. Long enough for Quill to hop once along Tam's shoulder, closer to me, and fix me with one black eye that said, in crow, everything her confession hadn't managed: *I helped build this cage once. Don't make me watch you walk into it freely.*

And long enough for me to notice the thing the Regent had carefully not said.

"You keep using his name," I said.

The pause. The half-second delay. The face remembering itself.

"Dominic Vale," I went on, stepping forward, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "the coronation, the war room, the lover's letter. You know his name. You know his face, obviously—you're wearing it. You know his habits, his jokes, his terrible handwriting. But in three shards and thirty-odd days of carrying his memories across your kingdom, do you know what I never found? Not once?"

"Tell me," said the thing wearing the crown, in the voice of the man it had caged for twenty years.

"Hatred." I let that sit. "Dominic feared you—he ordered the valley built to hide something, he bound himself to trap you, he was terrified, he was desperate, he was brave in the way frightened people are brave. But nowhere, in anything of him I've touched, is there *hate* for you. Not even contempt. Do you know why?" I was close enough now to see the seams, the faint wrongness around the eyes where the borrowed light didn't quite settle. "Because you're not his enemy. You're his *prisoner*. He didn't bind himself to punish you. He bound himself to hold you, the way you hold a fever, the way you hold a wound closed. You hate him. That much is obvious—it leaks out of you like heat off a forge. But he never hated you back, and it's eating you alive, isn't it? Twenty years in a coat that doesn't know it's a coat, worn by a man who pities you."

The temperature in the observatory dropped. Not metaphorically. The lamp flame shrank to a blue bead, and frost feathered across the broken astrolabe beside me, and Odile's crossbow creaked as her grip spasmed with cold. For one full second, the thing in front of me stopped pretending to be a man, and what filled the space was vast and furious and *hungry*, and every survival instinct I owned screamed at me that I had just made the worst trade of my life.

Then it folded itself back into Dominic's shape, smoothed the smile back on, and laughed—a warm, easy, terrible sound.

"Oh, you're *good*," it said admiringly. "Thirteen coins, and worth every one. Do you know, in twenty years, no one has spoken to me like that? Everyone kneels or runs. You *appraise* me." It rose from the lintel, unhurried again. "Keep the shards, then, if you must play courier. Keep your exiles, your crow, your brave little smith. I withdraw the offer—for now. Offers improve with age, Rosalind, like wine or grudges. Come find me when the cost of the rite becomes real to you. When you're holding the girl who trusts you, deciding whether she burns. I'll be generous. I'm always generous to thieves who finally understand what things are worth."

He walked to the stairwell door. Paused.

"One courtesy, since you gave me honesty." He didn't turn around. "The broker Vess collected eleven of your sold memories before we met. Eleven pieces of a dead king, filtered through a stranger's theft. Do you know what they add up to, when you lay them end to end?" Now he did turn, and Dominic's eyes were kind, which was the worst thing yet. "They add up to instructions. He hid the shards where only a thief could follow. He built the trail for you specifically, Rosalind. Before you ever left the valley, some part of him had already chosen his carrier." A beat. "Ask yourself what else he chose for you. Ask yourself whether the man who planned his own imprisonment would leave the ending unplanned."

And he was gone, down the stairs and into the starless morning, leaving frost and silence and the smell of cold iron behind him.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The lamp recovered its yellow. Frost sublimated off the astrolabe in wisps, like the room exhaling.

"Well," Odile said at last, lowering the crossbow with a shaking hand. "Either that was the most elaborate bluff in recorded history, or we are all going to die in nine days."

"He offered you an archive," Tam said to her quietly. "Did you hear it? He knew what you dream about."

"He knew what I *fear* wanting." Odile set the crossbow down and rubbed her eyes. "Different thing. Though not by much, at my age." She looked at me. "You did well, boy."

"Did I?" My voice cracked on the second word, betraying me entirely. "Because I spent most of that conversation shopping. You all saw it. I was standing there doing sums."

"We saw you refuse," Quill said. She launched off Tam's shoulder, crossed the room, and landed on mine, settling against my neck with the fierce possessive warmth of a small creature who has decided a large one is hers. Her voice, when it came, was rough. "In the valley, everyone refused him because they were afraid. You refused him while tempted. That's the only kind that counts. Believe me. I've watched both."

Tam hadn't moved. She was staring at the door, arms wrapped around herself, and when she spoke her voice had that doubled quality it had carried since the gilded prison—one voice laid thin over another, like a watermark. "Rosalind. When he talked about the vessel. About being *spent*." She swallowed. "It wasn't just information. He was measuring me. Looking at me the way you look at merchandise." She turned, and her eyes were wet and level. "He thinks I'm the answer to the rite. He wanted me to hear it."

"He wanted all of us to hear it," I said. "Divide and discount. Oldest trick in the broker's book."

"Yes," Tam said. "And it worked, a little. On all of us." She took a breath. "Which is why, before we lose another hour, we need to talk about what happens on the thirteenth turn. Because I've been doing my own sums. And I think I've known the answer longer than any of you."

Quill went very still on my shoulder.

Outside, beyond the broken dome of the observatory, the starless sky pressed down like a held breath, and somewhere far away, nine days began counting themselves down.
