## 31. Tam Chooses

The night before the twelfth turn, I couldn't sleep in the observatory, so I went down to the great dome where the rite would happen and sat on the floor with my back against a pillar that some astronomer had carved with star-charts four hundred years dead.

The shards were in their lead-lined case at the center of the room, humming. Not loudly. More like the way a sleeping cat hums when it dreams of hunting — you feel it in your sternum more than your ears. I'd been hearing them for weeks now, ever since the drowned chapel shard had been joined by the war-room shard and the lighthouse shard and the others Rosalind had carried across half a starving kingdom. But tonight they were singing something new. Tonight they were singing *soon*.

"Stop eavesdropping," I told them. "It's rude."

They sang on. Rude things. Kings are like that, even broken ones.

Here's what nobody tells you about being an apprentice ward-smith: you spend years learning how to keep things out, and then one day you realize the whole discipline is really about knowing exactly how things get in. A ward is just a door with opinions. And I knew this door. My master — Sera Vane, who forged the original sigil and then vanished into legend or a bog or possibly both — had taught me its flaws over three winters of grumbling and chalk dust and one memorable incident involving a goat. She taught me where the binding flexed. Where it breathed. Where a person could stand inside it and not be crushed, because she had built that person-shaped hollow into the design herself, all those years ago, and never told anyone why.

I think about that a lot. The hollow. You don't build a hollow into a prison unless you're planning for someone to be there.

* * *

Rosalind found me at dawn, or what passed for dawn now, which was the sky going from black to a slightly apologetic grey, like the whole firmament had been asked to smile for a portrait it didn't sit for. He had two cups of something hot that he claimed was tea and I claimed was punishment water, and he sat down next to me without asking what I was doing, which is one of the hundred small ways he is better than most people.

"You're sitting in the ritual circle," he said.

"I'm acquainting myself with it."

"It's going to try to unmake whoever stands in it."

"Yes, well." I drank the punishment water. It was terrible. "We're acquainted now."

He looked at me the way he looks at locks he's decided not to pick yet — patient, cataloguing, faintly amused. Nineteen days on the road together will teach you a person's silences, and his silence right then said *you've decided something and I'm going to hate it.*

"Tam."

"Don't say my name in that voice."

"Somebody has to stand as the vessel," he said. "And we established — Odile's charts, the astronomer's maths, Quill's—"

"Quill's confession, yes, I was there, I flew there in a crow's pocket, metaphorically." I set down the cup. Might as well say it plainly. Plainly was always easier than true. "The rite needs a willing vessel. Someone who can hold contact with the sigil long enough for the unbinding to take, without their mind being scraped out and used as jam. Your master's ghost-notes say the vessel has to *want* to survive, Rosalind. Wanting is load-bearing. That's the flaw Sera built in, and it's the flaw that saves us."

"So volunteer Odile."

"Odile is seventy-one and holding her ribs together with spite and cartographer's pins."

"Volunteer me, then."

"And let you stand in the circle?" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You? You're a *fragment* of him. You're a piece of Dominic cut loose and made flesh — the sigil wouldn't scrape you, it would *recognize* you, it would pull you in like water finding a crack, and then we lose the thief and the king in the same afternoon and I have to write a very short book about it."

"That's dramatic."

"I'm a smith's apprentice. We're taught to dramatize structural failure. It's the only poetry we get."

He was quiet a while. Below us, in the bowl of the dome, the shards hummed their soon-song, and the moonlight came through the broken roof in a long silver slash across the floor — the only light left in the kingdom that hadn't been stolen, and even it was rationed now, thinner every night as the thirteenth turn approached.

"The Regent offered me everything," Rosalind said finally. "Yesterday. From the ridge. Did Odile tell you?"

"She told us. She also threw a boot at the messenger, so the details were mostly delivered at high volume."

"He offered me the valley back. My memories back. A family I can't remember wanting." Rosalind turned the cup in his hands. "And the horrible thing is I understood it. For about ten heartbeats I wanted it so badly I could taste it. And then I thought — I already have a family. They argue about boots and burn the tea and one of them is a crow with a criminal past. Why would I trade that for a counterfeit?"

My throat did something embarrassing. I covered it by being brisk. "Get to the point, thief."

"The point is I know what choosing costs. I chose to leave the valley. It's the one memory I kept, the one the magic couldn't reclaim, and I know exactly what it weighed." He looked at me. "So if you choose this, don't you dare pretend it weighs nothing. I'll know. I'm a professional."

"I would never," I said. "I charge properly for everything."

* * *

I told the family at midday, in the observatory's old reading room, with Odile's charts spread on the table and Quill perched on the arm of Rosalind's chair wearing the particular stillness she wears when she already knows what's coming, which is most of her stillnesses, if I'm honest.

Odile took it worst, which is to say she took it best, which is to say she went very quiet in the way of people who have buried too many younger companions to do anything theatrical about one more threat. She is a disgraced royal cartographer, and her maps of missing things are the reason any of this is possible, and she has hands that shake now unless she flattens them against a table, and she flattened them against the table and said, "No."

"Odile—"

"No. Absolutely not. You are nineteen years old and your entire qualification for this is that a madwoman taught you wards before abandoning you in a border town."

"My entire qualification," I said, "is that Sera Vane was not a madwoman, she was a planner, and she built a person-shaped hollow into the sigil thirty years ago, and she spent three years teaching me exactly how that hollow works, and then she disappeared, which I always thought was desertion but have recently reclassified as *delegation*." I pulled the ghost-notes from my coat — the pages we'd taken from the ward-smithy, Sera's hand marching across them in furious little glyphs. "Look at the margin here. Page nine. *The vessel must know the door from the inside.* Not endure it. Know it. That's craft language, Odile. That's not a prophecy, that's a job posting."

"She could have posted it while present."

"She trusted me to follow."

"She abandoned you."

"Yes," I said, and found, to my surprise, that it didn't hurt the way it used to. "She did. And I've been angry about it since I was fourteen, and I did the sums last night, and here's what I found. Every ward-craft trick I know, I know because of her. The way I read stress-lines in stone. The way I can feel where a binding flexes. The way I survived carrying the chapel shard inside me for a week without going mad — that wasn't luck, that was technique, she trained me for exactly this whether she meant to or not. Courage isn't something you generate from nothing, Odile. It's an inheritance. You get it handed down, tool by tool, from people who built things to last." I tapped the ghost-notes. "This is my inheritance. I'm using it."

Odile stared at me for a long moment. Then she did the thing she does instead of crying, which is start re-organizing charts that were already organized.

"You sound like her," she said at last, to the charts. "Vane. She talked like that. Terribly certain. It was infuriating."

"People tell me that."

"They mean it as a warning."

"I take it as a reference."

Quill made a sound from the chair arm — crows can't laugh, quite, but they can produce a noise that files the same paperwork. Then she hopped down to the table, walked between the candlesticks, and fixed me with one eye like a jeweler appraising a suspicious gem.

"The girl is right," Quill said.

"Oh, wonderful," Odile muttered. "Both of them."

"I helped forge the binding," Quill went on, in that flat, precise way she has when she is confessing rather than conversing. "I know its architecture better than anyone living, including the Regent, who only wears it. And I tell you this: the vessel does not need strength. Dominic had strength enough and it availed him nothing. The vessel needs *craft*. Someone who can feel the shape of the binding and move within it the way a smith moves within a forge — knowing which heat burns and which merely shapes. The child has carried a shard in her belly and come out still speaking in her own grammar. That is not fortune. That is training expressing itself under fire." The crow's head dipped, briefly, in something that might have been a bow. "Sera Vane built her key first and her lock second. I watched her do it. I simply never understood until now which part was the key."

The room went quiet. Rosalind's face did something complicated. Mine, presumably, did something simpler and stupider, which is the face of a person discovering they have been loved on purpose, in advance, by someone who couldn't stay to see it.

"She named the goat after me," I said, because someone had to ruin the moment and I have seniority in that role. "That's neither here nor there. I just want it in the record."

* * *

Rosalind argued with me for another hour, which I respected, and lost, which I intend to remind him of at every available opportunity for the rest of a life I am now extremely committed to having.

His last argument was the good one, the real one, so I'll record it honestly: he said that the rite required a willing vessel, and willingness couldn't be faked, and how could I be sure — truly sure, in the marrow — that I was willing and not just performing courage for an audience of exiles?

And I told him about the night the stars went out.

I wasn't in the valley for that. I was in my master's workshop in Hallowmere, alone, eleven years old, mending a heating rune for a widow two streets over because Sera was off drinking with engineers. When the sky went dark, star by star, I stood in the doorway and watched, and I felt the wards of the world fail like glass, and every adult in my street went inside and barred their doors.

And I went out. Not bravely. I was terrified. My legs shook so badly I had to hold the fence. But I went out into the starless dark and I walked to the widow's house and finished mending her rune by touch, because she was cold, and cold was a problem, and problems were mine.

That was the night I became a ward-smith, though I didn't have the word yet. Not when Sera signed my papers. In the doorway, in the dark, holding a fence post, deciding that fear was weather and weather doesn't get to make your decisions.

"So no," I told Rosalind. "I'm not performing. This is just the largest cold window on the continent, and I am the only one on this road who knows how to mend runes by touch."

He gave up the way he gives up everything — gracefully, and while stealing something. I didn't discover until evening that he'd slipped his knife into my belt-sheath sometime during the argument, the good one, the one he traded a coronation memory for in Vess's shop a lifetime ago. There was a note tucked in the sheath: *For the vessel. If you die I'm taking it back and also I will be unbearable about it forever.*

Wry, that one. Emotional depth, allegedly. Mostly he's a pickpocket with a bibliography.

* * *

We spent the last hours before the twelfth turn preparing, and I won't dress it up: it felt like a funeral that everyone was determined to attend cheerfully. Odile drew the threshold diagrams in silvered chalk while narrating her own corrections under her breath like a liturgy. Quill inspected every corner of the dome and pronounced it "adequate," which from Quill is a knighthood. Rosalind sat with the shard-case open, talking low to the humming pieces of a king, and I pretended not to hear him say, "Whatever happens tomorrow, she comes out of this with her own name intact. Swear it, you stubborn ghost. I'll accept nothing less."

And I sat in the center of the circle one last time, alone, learning the door from the inside.

The sigil noticed me. I can't describe it better than that — the vast binding far away in the capital, threaded through the thing that wears Dominic's stolen face, turned its attention toward me like a lighthouse swinging its beam. And through the shards, faintly, something reached back. Not words. Not yet. Just a pressure, a question, the way a locked door asks the hand on its handle: *who's there?*

"A friend of the smith," I answered. "And a better one than she deserved. Probably."

The pressure withdrew, almost politely.

Almost politely, and that was the strange part. Sera had described the binding as cruel, and the ghost-notes called it merciless, and everything we'd suffered on this road argued the same. But doors are made by their makers, and I knew my maker, and underneath the cruelty of the sigil I felt — for one heartbeat, no more — a structure built by someone who had intended, someday, for it to be opened.

A person-shaped hollow. Waiting all these years.

I was still sitting with my hand flat on the chalk-line when the sentry-horn sounded from the western ridge — three short notes, then one long, which in Odile's signal-code meant riders, many, and coming fast.

I stood up. The chalk clung to my palms like snow.

Above the observatory, against the starless sky, I saw lantern-light spilling down the ridge road, and ahead of the lanterns, unmistakable even at this distance, a figure on a white horse moving with the easy confidence of a man who has never once in his life been chased.

Vess.

Every bargain, every coin, every memory we'd ever sold him — I felt them all rearranging themselves behind his smile, and I grabbed the shard-case, and I ran to raise the alarm.
