## 32. The Eleventh Turn: Betrayal

The eleventh turn rose two nights before we reached the observatory, and the moon came up the color of a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

I remember thinking, in the last hour before everything went wrong, that we looked almost like pilgrims. Tam walking ahead with her hood up, humming a warding cadence under her breath the way other people hummed songs. Odile's maps rolled tight against my back where I'd taken over carrying them, because Odile was still gray-faced from the salt flats and refused with her whole spine to admit it. Quill riding the wind above us in wide, lazy circles, doing her impression of an ordinary crow, which had never once fooled anyone but which she maintained out of professional pride.

And me. Rosalind Thackeray, keeper of stolen things, carrying three memory-shards wrapped in wool and oilcloth against my chest like the world's most dangerous locket. Two days to the threshold. Two days, and then Tam would do something terrifying with wards, and I would do something more terrifying by standing still for it, and Dominic—king, stranger, fragment-of-myself-I-couldn't-yet-look-at-squarely—would finally get his name back.

I should have known better than to feel hopeful. Hope, in my experience, was the thing brokers sold you right before they weighed the scale.

It started with a smell. Woodsmoke and wet iron—the smell of sigil-work, the smell of the hounds. Quill dropped out of the sky so fast she nearly took my ear off on the way down.

"Down," she said, and there was no laziness in her voice at all. "Down, down, down, all of you, into the gully, *now*."

We went. The gully was shallow and smelled of old rain, but it was dark, and dark had been our friend since the stars went out. I pressed my cheek to the cold mud and watched the ridgeline above us.

For a long moment there was nothing. That was the worst part, afterward—I'd had time to believe she'd been wrong. Quill was wrong approximately never, but hope is a persistent weed.

Then the ridge lit up.

Not firelight. Sigil-light—that flat, wrong white that cast no shadows, the light of the binding itself, and it crawled along the ridgeline in a line of torches-that-weren't-torches, twelve of them, spaced with the precision of people who had drilled this. Who had *practiced*. Below each light, a figure stood, and every one of them wore Vess's livery: the gray-and-gold of a broker's house, tailored for violence.

"They knew," Odile breathed beside me. "Rosalind. They knew the route."

"They've always known the route," I said, and heard my own voice go flat and cold, the working voice, the thief's voice, because the alternative was to let it shake. "Every memory I ever sold him. Every shard he ever paid me for. He wasn't buying merchandise." I watched the white lights and made myself finish the thought, because finishing thoughts was what I did instead of screaming. "He was taking inventory."

Twelve agents. Three shards. One box of starlight-inked map, one apprentice who could read the rite, one cartographer who knew every unlit road in the kingdom, one crow, one thief.

They didn't come down into the gully. They didn't need to.

The lead agent raised a hand, and the ground beneath us woke up. Old wards—planted, patient, laid across this whole valley of the gully days or weeks ago—bloomed through the mud in threads of white light, and I felt them take hold of the air the way fingers take hold of a wrist.

"Net," Tam said, already moving, hands sketching counter-sigils so fast they blurred. "It's a containment lattice, it won't kill us, it'll just—" She swore, an impressively technical oath for someone raised by a ward-smith. "It'll just make everything inside it very quiet and very easy to collect."

"How long can you hold it open?" Odile asked.

"How long can you hold a door shut against a flood?"

"That's a no, then."

"That's a *measured assessment*," Tam snapped, and shoved light back at the lattice with both palms.

Quill did not wait for the plan to fail. Quill never waited for anything; waiting was for creatures with longer lifespans. She went up out of the gully like a thrown knife, black against black, straight at the lead agent—and I understood, in the half-second before I understood nothing else, what she intended. Not to wound. To *take*. The lead agent carried a case, long and slim, locked with a sigil-seal, and whatever was in it, Quill had decided it belonged to us.

"QUILL!" I don't know if I shouted it aloud or only inside. It doesn't matter. Some names you shout in a register the world keeps for you.

She got there. I will carry that to whatever end waits for me: she *got there*, talons first, and the agent went down, and the case flew free, spinning end over end through sigil-light—

—and the second agent, who had been waiting exactly as long as the first had been practicing, simply raised a hand, and the air around Quill closed.

No net. No cage. Just the binding-sigil, drawn in light too fast for any eye but a crow's or a god's, and my friend folded up inside it like a letter being sealed. She hit the ground small and silent and did not move, and the white lines held her, and somewhere behind my ribs something that had been holding since the salt flats finally, quietly broke.

* * *

Afterward, I tried to reconstruct the fight the way I reconstruct any theft—step by step, hand by hand—and found I couldn't. There are holes. There is a gap where Odile put herself between the agents and Tam and did something with her rolled maps and her stick and her considerable fury that bought us thirty seconds. There is a gap where Tam screamed a word and part of the lattice turned red and then black and died. There is a gap where I bit someone. In the version I choose to keep, I bit them hard.

What I know for certain is how it ended, because endings are the parts thieves always remember.

It ended with the three of us backed against the gully wall, the lattice reformed and tightening, and the lead agent—a woman with a broker's neat braids and a soldier's flat patience—standing over the case, which had landed near her feet. Which had, I realized with a nausea that arrived late and complete, been empty. Bait. The whole ambush had been built around making Quill want the case, because everyone who knew us knew that Quill wanted things faster than she thought about them, and someone had studied us the way I studied marks.

Vess had studied us. Of course he had. He'd had years of my confessions, my sales, my little tells, all of them filed and priced.

The woman opened her hand. On her palm sat a single coin. Thirteenth-in-the-mint, star-marked, exactly like the ones Vess used to pay me with.

"The broker sends his regards," she said, "and his apology. He genuinely liked you. He wants you to know that."

"Where is he?" My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "The pleasure-quarter prison wasn't good enough for him anymore? He had to come watch in person?"

"He isn't here." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "He's where the shards need to be assembled, which is where you were going. You've saved him two days' travel. He thanks you for that as well."

Behind her, agents were lifting Quill's sealed form onto a litter. Carefully. Gently. They'd been instructed to keep her alive, and that instruction, more than anything, told me how much Vess knew about what mattered to us. You do not take hostages unless you understand exactly whom your enemy cannot bear to lose.

"The shards," the woman said, palm still extended, patient as interest accruing. "You can carry them to me, or my colleagues can carry you to them. Either way, the broker gets what he bought. But one way, the crow flies again someday, and the girl keeps her hands, and the old woman keeps her map-collection and her opinions. The other way—" She shrugged, elegantly. "Well. You've seen the hounds work."

Tam grabbed my sleeve. Odile said my name, low, the warning tone, the *don't-do-the-stupid-brave-thing* tone.

But here is what nobody understood about me, not even, I think, me: I had spent my whole life trading memories because they were the only currency I had. And I had learned, in the valley that forgets, exactly what everything costs. I knew the price of those shards to the copper. I knew what Dominic's scattered self was worth, and what Tam's hands were worth, and what Quill was worth, which was more than the sky, which was more than the sky having stars in it again.

I also knew a con when I saw one. And this was a con—not mine, but its structure was familiar. Give the mark a choice. Make both doors lead to the same room. Let him pick the lock on the one that hurts least, and feel clever while you empty his pockets.

So I gave them the shards. All three, unwrapped, gleaming their faint and terrible gleam in the white light, and I watched the agents pack them into the real case with the reverence of men handling relics, and I smiled at the lead agent with everything I had left, which was almost nothing, and said:

"Tell Vess he taught me everything I know about overpaying."

She almost laughed. I'm going to hold on to that. Somewhere in that machine of a night, a professional nearly laughed at my joke, and then the lattice took my knees, and the world went white, and then it went away.

* * *

They did not take us to the capital. That was the mercy, or the trap—by the eleventh turn the difference had gotten academic. They marched us along the high road toward the observatory, bound in light that drank at the edges of my thoughts whenever I pushed against it, and they carried Quill's litter behind us where I could see it, because Vess understood cruelty as logistics.

Tam walked beside me with her chin up and her eyes wet and furious. "They're going to use me," she said. "You know that. A willing vessel—they need a vessel, and now they have a ward-smith who's already been touched by the sigil once. I'm not a hostage, Rosalind, I'm a *component*."

"I know."

"And Quill—"

"I know."

"Say something, then."

I looked at the litter. At the small dark shape inside the seal, feathers rising and falling too fast, alive, alive, alive. And I said the truest thing I had.

"They made one mistake," I said. "They counted the shards. Three in the case, three accounted for. They searched me twice. They checked my boots, my coat seams, the lining of Odile's maps." I turned my head and found Tam's eyes. "But nobody searches a man for something he doesn't know he has."

Tam frowned. "That's either wisdom or nonsense."

"It's usually both."

Because here is what I hadn't said aloud, what I had barely admitted in the privacy of my own skull since the drowned chapel: when I went inside Dominic's coronation, when I stood in his body and felt his joy and his dread pour through me like water through a broken roof—something stayed. A residue. An echo. The fourth shard, the one no ledger recorded, kept not in oilcloth but in the marrow of a man who was maybe a fragment of a king and definitely a thief. Vess had bought my memories for years and never found it, because even I hadn't known it was there until it answered.

They had the shards. They had the vessel. They had the threshold two days ahead of us and my friends in chains.

And they had me, marching willingly into the heart of it, carrying the one component they didn't know existed, with no plan, no crow, and no idea whether I was the weapon or the decoy after all.

The moon climbed higher, thin as a paring, counting down. Eleven turns gone. Two remaining.

Somewhere ahead, in a ruined observatory on the edge of everything, Vess was waiting to receive his delivery—and for the first time since the stars went out, I realized I was glad to be going to meet him.

After all. Nobody knows how to rob a fence better than the thief he trained by accident, one overpaid memory at a time.
