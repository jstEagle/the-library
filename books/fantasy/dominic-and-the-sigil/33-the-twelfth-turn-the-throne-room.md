## 33. The Twelfth Turn: The Throne Room

They did not bind my hands.

That was the first insult. Chains are a courtesy, in their way — they say *we fear you*. What the Regent's guards gave me instead was an open corridor and four spears at my back, which says something worse: *run, little thief, we'd enjoy the exercise.* My wrists felt naked without rope. I hadn't realized how much of my dignity I'd been keeping in my knuckles until someone took away even the option of clenching them.

Quill rode my shoulder the whole way through the palace, talons light as a held breath. They hadn't caged her either. I understood that too by then — Vess had told me, smiling his broker's smile with too many teeth in it now: *the crow stays where you stay, or she flies home to us. She remembers her debts.* Quill said nothing. She only pressed closer, feathers against my jaw, warm as a coal wrapped in grey silk.

The throne room was exactly what a stolen kingdom would build when it wanted to look like itself: vaulted, gold-lit, hung with banners of a house that had ended two generations ago. Every surface was right. Every surface was wrong. It was like looking at a face in a mirror that lags half a heartbeat behind your own — everything in place, nothing in time.

And on the throne, the thing wearing Dominic's face.

I had seen that face from the inside once, in the drowned chapel — had felt it laugh, had felt it grieve, had worn its joy like borrowed clothing. So I knew what I was looking at, and what I was looking at was not it. The features were identical. The arrangement was identical. But a face is not a mask; it is a language, and this one spoke fluent imitation with no native accent underneath. When the Regent looked at me, it was the way a portrait looks at you: patient, painted, waiting for the museum to close.

"Rosalind Thackeray," the Regent said. Its voice was beautiful. That was the worst part. It sounded like a man who had been loved. "You've cost me a great deal."

"You should see what you've cost everyone else," I said. "Though I suppose you don't keep receipts. Vess does that for you."

Something flickered across the painted face. Not anger. Interest. A collector finding a coin he didn't know he wanted.

"They tell me you speak directly," it said. "That you bargained your childhood for a girl's life. That you carried a dead stranger's box across three hundred miles of unlit country because a bird asked you to." It leaned forward, and the throne — actual gold, actual age — creaked under a weight that wasn't physical. "Tell me why. You have nothing left to steal and nowhere left to run. Why does a thief keep walking toward the one door built to stop him?"

*Because I'm a fragment of him,* I didn't say. *Because somewhere in your sigil there's a piece of me missing, and I want it back.*

What I said was simpler, and truer, and more dangerous: "Because I met two prisoners in one lifetime, and I've decided I don't like the odds."

* * *

They made me stand in the center of the room while the court assembled around me — courtiers in silk the color of drowned things, guards whose armor bore the sigil worked small and silver at every collarbone. I counted them out of habit. Forty-one souls, give or take. Tam was not among them; neither were Odile's exiles; neither was anything of mine except a bird with a guilty conscience and a head full of a king's memories that weren't mine but fit me anyway.

Vess stood near the dais, hands folded, watching me with the expression of a merchant who has finally closed a very long account. I hated how relieved part of me was to see him. Twelve turns ago he'd been the only fixed point in my valley — the one man who always paid, who never asked twice. Now I knew what his constancy had been: surveillance with excellent manners.

"The thirteenth turn comes tomorrow night," the Regent said, conversationally, the way a man discusses weather over wine. "At moonrise, the binding becomes permanent. Whatever is left of the king inside my sigil will be mine in fact as well as name. And you—" the painted eyes moved over me like fingers taking inventory, "—you will be given a choice. You may serve. You will be given back every memory you ever sold me. Your childhood. Your mother's voice, if you had one. The valley reborn, its wards mended, its people safe inside their forgetting. All of it, freely returned, if you kneel."

"And if I don't kneel?"

"Then tomorrow night I will take the shards out of your friend Tam myself, and I will not be gentle about the vessel." It smiled with Dominic's mouth, and it was like watching a cathedral burn politely. "But I would rather you knelt. Coercion spoils the flavor of victory. Ask any usurper."

The court laughed. Thin, quick laughter, the kind people make near large animals.

I looked at Vess. "Did you hear that? He said coercion spoils the flavor. You've been coercing me since I was nineteen years old and starving behind a ward-line. How do I taste?"

"Sour," Vess said, without apology. "But you ripen."

* * *

Here is what nobody in that room understood, because nobody in that room had spent a decade learning to read people the way I read locks: I wasn't standing there to bargain. I was standing there to listen.

Because the thing about a prison built around a king — the thing Tam's master's ghost-notes had promised us, the thing the war-room shard had hinted and the lover's letter had confirmed — is that prisons are made of walls, and walls are made of wards, and wards are made of *words*, and words can be spoken *through*.

Tam had heard it first, in her whispering week: the sigil speaking through her hand, writing in a script that wasn't hers. She'd taught me the trick of it before the eleventh turn took everything — before Vess's agents seized the shards and Quill and our hopes in one clean motion. *He's still in there,* she'd said, gripping my wrist hard enough to bruise. *Rosalind, he's awake, he's been awake the whole time, and the binding leaks. It has to leak. Nothing that tight holds forever. If you get close enough — if you speak to him through the thing that wears him —*

So I stood in the middle of a hostile court, forty-one enemies deep, beneath a throne and the thing sitting on it, and I stopped performing, and I reached down into the place where Quill said my blood remembered being royal, and I spoke.

Not aloud. Not entirely.

*Dominic.*

It went out of me like heat off coals — a word with weight, aimed past the painted face, past the gold, down into whatever dark cellar of the world holds a bound king. I put everything I could into it. The coronation I'd carried inside his body at the drowned chapel. The dread he'd felt under his own joy. The letter he'd written to a lover whose name the shard had mercifully kept private. *I know you're in there. I've been in there. I know what your hands feel like. Answer me.*

The Regent stopped mid-sentence.

I mean that literally. It had been listing my crimes — theft, sedition, the corruption of a ward-smith's apprentice, the desecration of a general's tomb — and between one word and the next it simply stopped, the way a clock stops, and the whole room noticed at once, the way a room notices when a clock stops. Silence has a texture. This silence was splintery.

Then the Regent's mouth moved again, and the voice that came out was not the Regent's.

It wasn't Dominic's either, not fully — it was both, braided, a man's voice speaking through a stolen throat with the thief's tongue pinned under it. Like hearing two people talk in one breath.

"*Who speaks?*" it said. "*Who carries my voice in their mouth?*"

The courtiers nearest the dais drew back. One of them fainted with the soft, practiced grace of someone who had rehearsed fainting. The guards' spears wavered — pointed at me, then uncertainly at their own sovereign, then nowhere useful.

"I do," I said aloud now, letting the whole room hear. Letting *him* hear. "Rosalind Thackeray. Memory-thief of the valley you built to hide something — I read your orders, Your Majesty, in a dead man's tomb. I know you chose this. I know you built the prison around yourself on purpose." I took a step forward. Nobody stopped me. Four hundred years of protocol apparently did not cover *the prisoner addresses the throne in two voices.* "I also know what the crown-thing can't survive without. I know why you did it. And I know it's going to kill you both unless someone unbinds you tomorrow night."

The Regent's face — *their* face, the shared face, flickering between occupant and owner like a lantern behind stained glass — turned toward me, and for one moment the paint fell away entirely.

What looked out of it was tired.

Gods, it was so tired. Two lifetimes of tired, folded into one pair of eyes, and behind the tiredness a mind still moving like water under ice. "*Thackeray,*" the braided voice said, and this time the emphasis was different, weighted toward the deeper current, toward the man. "*The valley. You lived in the valley. Tell me — the stars. Did they shine, in the valley? Before they went out?*"

"No," I said. "Never. I thought the sky was just... empty. I thought everyone's sky was empty."

Something moved through the borrowed face that I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Or the particular agony of a man learning that his sacrifice had been misfiled — that the hiding-place he'd built had become a blinding, and the people inside it had grown up thinking darkness was the natural state of heaven.

"*I built that valley to hide a key,*" the voice said. "*One key. And the crown-thing hid the lock inside my own name, and the wards ate the stars to feed themselves, and I have watched through these borrowed eyes for two hundred years while my kingdom forgot my face and thanked the thing that stole it.*" The voice dropped, roughened, became — briefly, terribly — just one man's voice, alone. "*You said you know what it cannot live without. Say it plainly. Here. Where it must hear.*"

So I said it plainly, in front of the whole court, in front of the herald and the guards and the fainting courtiers and the thing itself:

"It can't survive without you. It's not a parasite wearing a host — it's a shadow wearing a lamp. Cut the sigil, end the binding, and it unravels. That's why it needs you permanent by the thirteenth turn. That's why it's terrified. That's why it's offering me deals instead of killing me where I stand." I spread my empty hands. "Your Majesty — Dominic — you're not its prisoner anymore. Tomorrow night, if we do this right, you're its executioner."

The throne room held its breath. Even Vess had stopped smiling.

And then the Regent laughed.

It started in the painted face and spread outward, through the courtiers, through the guards — no, wait. It didn't spread. It was *echoed*. The courtiers laughed a heartbeat late, the guards a heartbeat after that, the whole room laughing in ripples like a pond remembering a stone, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach that none of them had chosen to laugh at all. The Regent's mirth moved through them like weather. Like a ward. Like a leash.

"Oh, *thief*," the Regent said, and the beauty came back into the voice, and the tiredness fled behind the paint. "How touching. Two prisoners, recognizing each other. Shall I tell you what I recognize?" It rose from the throne — slowly, savoring it, letting the gold catch fire on its shoulders. "I recognize a fragment. A splinter of my host, cut loose and made flesh, carrying my shards across my kingdom in a locked box. Do you truly believe you found those memories by accident? That a crow led you? Darling, I *scattered* them. Every shard you recovered, you recovered along roads I chose. You have been my courier from the first mile. And tomorrow at moonrise, when the binding closes, everything you gathered for me arrives gift-wrapped at the altar of my permanence."

The floor did not tilt. I made it not tilt through sheer professional stubbornness.

"That's not true," I said, and hated how thin it came out.

"It's the truest thing you'll hear all century." The Regent descended the dais steps one by one, and the court bowed in waves before it like wheat before a scythe. "But here is my genuine offer, since you've earned one honest thing today: kneel, deliver the vessel and the shards, and I will let the crow fly free. She served my host loyally once. Sentiment is a luxury I occasionally permit myself."

Quill's talons bit my shoulder. Not much. Just enough to say *don't you dare.*

And I stood there in the gold and the banners and the borrowed face of a dead dynasty, twelve turns deep, one turn left, holding a truth in each hand and no way to weigh them — his trap or my hope, his story or mine — when the Regent leaned in close enough that I could smell it. Not perfume. Not rot. Something older: cold iron and starless night, the exact smell of the valley's border road at dusk.

"One more thing, Thackeray," it whispered, in a register only I could hear, and the voice in that whisper was suddenly, perfectly, *his* — Dominic's, unbraided, urgent, running fast beneath the Regent's words like a river under ice. "Listen carefully, because I can only hold the mouth this long. The shards are false bait — *some* of them. The third, the lover's letter — that one it never scattered. It never knew it existed. Everything else you carry, it can track. But the letter is real, and the rite is real, and the willing vessel is not what you think — ask the smith's ghost-notes about the *second reading* — and thief — *thief* —"

The Regent straightened. The paint slid back over the face like a visor coming down.

"Guards," it said pleasantly, to the room, "our guest is tired. Take him below. And bring me the crow separately — I find I have questions about old loyalties."

Four spears closed in. Quill screamed — a raw, human-sounding scrape of fury — and beat upward into the rafters as gauntleted hands grabbed for her, wheeling once above the throne, once above me, black against the banner-gold—

—and then she folded her wings and dove, straight down, straight at the Regent's face, talons out, a small grey bird against a large golden darkness, and the last thing I saw before they shoved me through the door was the crown-thing's painted eyes going wide with something I had never once expected to see there.

Fear.
