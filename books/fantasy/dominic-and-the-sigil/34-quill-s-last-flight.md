## 34. Quill's Last Flight

They kept Quill in a cage of glass and light.

I know how that sounds. I know every word of it sounds like something out of a ballad sung badly in a tavern, but I have stopped apologizing for the shape of my life. The cage was real. I saw it. Vess had it carried through the throne room on poles like a reliquary, and inside it the crow who had once perched on a king's shoulder sat very still and very small, her feathers dull as old lead, watching us with one eye and then the other, because crows cannot blink both eyes at once and she refused to give either of them up.

It was the twelfth turn. One turn left before the thirteenth, when the sigil would close forever and Dominic—whatever was left of him, folded into that black ring Vess wore on a chain around his neck like a trophy or a leash—would be gone past any calling.

We were prisoners. That part is simple. The rest of it took me a long time to understand, and I understood most of it too late, which is the way understanding usually arrives: like rain after the crops are already dead.

* * *

Let me tell you what the lowest point of my life looked like, since you've come this far and deserve honesty.

It looked like a cell in the pleasure-quarter prison, all silk cushions and gilded bars, because the Regent had a sense of humor the way a knife has a sense of humor. It smelled of incense meant to soothe, which only made it worse, the way a lullaby sung by a jailer is worse than silence. Tam was beside me, still shaking off whatever the sigil had been whispering through her for weeks. Odile was two cells down, her hands bandaged from where she'd fought the agents on the observatory steps. And the shards—the shards we had bled for, drowned for, buried friends for—were gone, sealed into a case that Vess carried at his hip like a merchant's strongbox, which was, in the end, exactly what it was.

And Quill was across the room in her cage of glass and light, and she was not speaking.

That was the worst of it. A talking crow who will not talk is a silence with feathers, and I had spent enough nights in the valley to know what silences can do to a person.

"Quill," I said, for maybe the hundredth time. "Say something cutting about my hair. Say something smug about being right. Say anything."

She turned her head. One dark eye found me, and in it there was something I had never seen there before, and it took me a moment to name it because I had no practice with it.

It was apology.

"Don't," I said. "Whatever you're about to say, don't you dare apologize. You got captured. It happens. It happened to all of us, and some of us didn't even have wings to make an escape interesting."

Her beak opened. Closed. When she finally spoke, her voice was smaller than I'd ever heard it, stripped of its usual theatrical relish, and that frightened me more than the Regent's whole court had.

"They showed me the casing," she said. "While they carried me here. They wanted me to see it."

"The casing?"

"The outer shell of the sigil." She hopped once along her perch, restless, her claws clicking against the glass. "The binding has layers, Rosalind. Tam's master built it that way. An outer casing to protect the true knot, the way a seed coat protects the germ. While the casing holds, nothing can touch the heart of it. Not the rite. Not the thirteenth turn. Nothing."

"So we break the casing."

"With what?" She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a snapped twig. "Ward-craft? They have Tam. Steel? They have everything else. The casing was forged in starlight and sealed with a sacrifice, boy. That's what my master told me, three hundred years ago, when I still thought we were saving him instead of burying him alive."

I went cold. "Sealed with a sacrifice."

"You know how bindings work. Everything costs. The outer casing cost the most, so they made it out of the thing that loved him best." Her voice did not waver, but her feathers did, a fine tremor running through them like wind over water. "A willing soul, freely given, bound into the shell. That's why it's held so long. Love makes poor mortar for walls but excellent mortar for cages. Nobody thinks to look for the lock inside their own devotion."

The cell was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the silk curtains, the pleasure quarter went on with its music, oblivious, obscene.

"Quill," I said carefully. "What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you that I helped forge the binding." She said it flatly, the confession she'd already made once on the road, the one I had half-forgiven. "And I'm telling you that what I helped forge can be unmade by the same hand that made it. The casing knows me, Rosalind. Three hundred years, and it still knows me. If I go to it—if I give myself to it the way I gave myself to the forging—it will open. For one turn. Maybe less. Maybe only long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

"For Tam to work the flaw her master taught her. For you to reach the heart of it. For Dominic to hear his own name spoken by someone who means it." She fixed me with both eyes then, one and then the other, and her voice steadied into something older and harder. "One turn of vulnerability. That's what I can buy. That's the price of admission to the last door."

"No," I said.

"It hasn't asked you yet."

"I'm saying it first. No."

She fluffed her feathers, settling, the way she always did when she intended to ignore me thoroughly. "You know," she said, almost conversationally, "when I met you, you were a thief in a valley that eats memories, and you had rules. Rule one: never steal from someone poorer than you. Rule two: never sell a memory you couldn't bear to lose. Rule three—" she tilted her head— "rule three I forget. You never told me rule three. I always suspected there wasn't one."

"There wasn't," I admitted. "Rule three was a bluff. It made people nervous."

"Mm. And now look at you. Ready to trade your whole self for a king you met inside a memory." Her eye glinted. "Hypocrisy sits poorly on you, thief. You'd sell your soul for him, but you won't let an old bird spend hers?"

"That's different."

"Why?"

"Because—" and here my voice failed me, which was humiliating, and I pressed on anyway, because humiliation and I were old dance partners by then. "Because you're the only one left who knew him before. You're the only one who remembers the kingdom with stars in it. If you die, that dies with you. The world needs—"

"The world needs nothing from me but this." She cut me off gently, which is a skill crows have and men rarely manage. "Rosalind. Listen to me now, because I won't say it twice and I refuse to say it tearfully. I am three hundred years old. I watched the man I loved most in the world get folded into a ring to save him from a knife, and I told myself it was mercy, and I have lived inside that lie longer than your entire civilization has stood in its current form. Every day since has been interest paid on a debt. Tomorrow, one way or another, the account closes." She hopped closer to the glass between us. "The only question is whether it closes clean or whether it closes with me still hiding behind the word 'mercy.'"

* * *

Vess came for me at dusk.

He came alone, without guards, which should have told me everything; a man who travels without guards is a man who does not believe he can be harmed, and Vess had earned that belief the hard way, by being the crown-thing's herald for longer than most kingdoms last. He settled onto the cushion opposite my bars with the ease of a man visiting an old debtor, and he smiled, and his smile was the same smile he'd worn in the valley when he paid too much for a coronation memory and asked too little.

"You know the strange thing, Rosalind?" he said. "Of everything you sold me—and you sold me such lovely things, such precise little thefts—the coronation was never my favorite. My favorite was the market day. The one with the woman selling honey pears. You kept that one eleven years. Eleven years! And then one morning you woke up and decided you could live without it." He sighed. "I often wondered what changed."

"You know what changed. You arranged half of it."

"I arranged all of it," he said pleasantly. "But arrangement isn't the same as authorship. Even I only read the score. Someone else wrote the music." He leaned forward. "The Regent would like to make you an offer. A generous one. But you already know the offer—you heard it on the rooftop, you heard it in the throne room. Identity. Family. The valley reborn, warm and forgetting, just the way you liked it." His head tilted. "So instead let me ask you the question underneath the offer. Why do you keep saying no? You don't even remember why you want the shards. Half of you is a hole where a king used to be. What is it you think you're protecting?"

And I'll tell you the truth, which is that for one long moment I didn't have an answer. That was the trap of the thing wearing the crown: it never offered you lies. Lies you can spit at. It offered you versions of the truth, sanded smooth, and waited for you to pick one up.

But then, across the room, Quill moved on her perch. Just slightly. Just enough to catch my eye, and to dip her head once toward the window, where the light of the pleasure quarter's lanterns came through—and beyond the lanterns, far up, the place where stars should have been and weren't.

And I remembered rule three. There wasn't one. There never had been. Which meant the rules had never been the point. The point was that I made them myself, out of nothing, in a valley designed to hollow me, and I had held to them anyway. Whatever else I was—thief, fragment, weapon, decoy—I was the sum of my choices, and my choices had led me here, to a gilded cage, refusing a comfortable lie.

"Because it's wrong," I said to Vess. "That's the whole answer. It's wrong, and somebody has to be the one who says so out loud, and apparently that somebody is a memory thief with bad hair."

Vess studied me for a while. Then he stood, brushed off his coat, and said, almost kindly: "The Regent will be disappointed. He does hate disappointment. It makes him... hungry." At the door he paused. "Oh—one more thing. The bird. The Regent has decided she's served her purpose as bait. Tomorrow at dawn she goes into the forge-fire. A pity. She was quite the singer, once."

He left. I sat in the silk-and-gold dark and listened to my heart do something ugly and determined.

* * *

Tam came to me that night. Not in person—in person was impossible—but through the walls, through the ward-craft she'd been practicing in secret since she was nine years old, sending her voice thin as thread through the prison's own wards, turning the enemy's cage into a speaking tube. Her master would have been proud. Her master, I suspected, would also have been terrified.

"Rosalind." Her whisper was ragged. "The sigil talks to me. All the time now. It knows I can hear it. And tonight it told me something it shouldn't have."

"Tell me."

"The casing. It's afraid of the casing opening. It keeps checking it, the way you check a wound. And Rosalind—it checks it near the bird. Every time they move her cage, it flinches." A breath. "Quill was right. The casing knows her. It's *made* of her, somehow, or of what she gave. And if she gives again—"

"It opens."

"If she gives again, it opens. For one turn." Tam's voice cracked. "Rosalind, she asked me to help her die. She asked me to ward the forge-fire so it takes her cleanly, and to hold the flaw open after, and I said yes, because she asked, and because I owe her, and because—" she stopped, and started again, angrier, braver— "because everyone in my life who taught me anything taught me that a ward exists to protect something. And tomorrow night, the thing worth protecting is a door."

I put my hand flat against the cold gilded bar and thought about a crow stealing crumbs off my windowsill in the valley, back when I thought she was just a clever bird following a fool with a box. I thought about her voice in the dark saying *the thirteenth turn*, no—that was the stranger, dying at my door. Quill's voice had said other things. *Left at the split oak.* *You walk like a man being followed by his own shadow.* *Don't sell the funny ones, thief, they're worth more than the sad ones.*

Three hundred years old. And she had spent them walking toward this one dawn.

"Tell her yes," I said. "Tell her I said yes. And tell her—" my throat closed, and I made myself finish, because she deserved finished sentences— "tell her rule three was a bluff, but this isn't. Tell her I'll be at the door when it opens. Both of us thieves. One last job."

Through the wall, faintly, I heard Tam begin to cry. And beneath the crying, so faint I might have imagined it, a crow's voice saying something old in a language with stars in it.

* * *

Dawn came gray and gold over the pleasure quarter, beautiful the way rotting fruit is beautiful. They marched us into the great hall to watch—that was the Regent's cruelty, to make witnesses of us—and there was the forge, dragged up from the ward-smithy, breathing fire, and there was the cage of glass and light set before it, and there was Vess in his fine coat holding the case of shards, and above it all, on the throne that had been a king's, the thing wearing Dominic's face sat smiling with a mouth that had never learned how.

They opened the cage above the fire.

And Quill did not wait for them.

I will tell it plainly, because she would have wanted it plain, no ballad nonsense, no swooping heroics invented after the fact. She simply stepped off the perch. Wings open—not flying, falling with style, the way she'd once described flight to Tam—and she dropped toward the forge-fire, and in the instant before the flames took her she spoke, loud and clear and carrying, in the voice of a bird who had outlived empires:

"*I take it back. All of it. The forging, the binding, the mercy. Unmake what I made.*"

The fire went white.

Not orange, not gold—white, the white of starlight, the color the sky had been before the crown-thing ate it, and the whole hall screamed backward from the glare, and through the blaze I saw it: the sigil's outer casing, the invisible shell that had wrapped the black ring for three hundred years, cracking like river ice in spring. Cracks of white light spidering across the air itself, and through every crack came a sound like a thousand small voices exhaling at once, and the ring on Vess's chain turned from black to bare naked iron, exposed, vulnerable, *open*—

"One turn!" Tam was screaming, already moving, her hands already weaving the flaw her master taught her, the flaw she'd swallowed shards to carry. "The door is open—one turn, Rosalind, GO—"

I went.

I ran through a hall full of reeling courtiers and a blinded herald and a Regent rising from its stolen throne with its borrowed face contorted into something ancient and starving, and I ran because a small bird had bought the door with everything she had, and grief is a fuel that burns hot and clean and does not ask permission.

Behind me, the white light guttered. Behind me, the cracks began, slowly, terribly, to knit closed. One turn. Less than one turn.

Ahead of me, at the center of the unraveling shell, the heart of the sigil hung in the air like a held breath—and inside it, faint as a pulse, something that had waited three hundred years felt the first touch of open sky.

And reached back.
