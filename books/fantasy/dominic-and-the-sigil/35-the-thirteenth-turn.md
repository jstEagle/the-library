## 35. The Thirteenth Turn

*Rosalind*

The observatory had no roof worth the name and no stars left to look at, which struck me as a poor design for an observatory, but the moonlit threshold didn't care about my opinions on architecture. Moonlight pooled in the ruined dome like water in a cupped hand, silver and patient, and it fell on Tam where she knelt in its center with thirteen memory-shards arranged around her in a circle that Odile's charts swore was exact to a hair's width.

Nineteen days of road, three turns spent, one crow gone ahead into the dark, and it came down to this: a girl with soot under her fingernails and a thief who couldn't remember why he'd ever wanted anything else.

"You're doing the face again," Tam said without looking up.

"What face?"

"The one where you're composing your own eulogy and you want someone to notice."

"I don't compose. I improvise." I crouched at the circle's edge, careful not to smudge the chalk lines she'd redrawn twice since midnight. "It's a better art form."

She looked up then, and her eyes were doing something I didn't like—something old and steady that belonged to ward-smiths and martyrs, not to apprentices who stole apples and complained about her boots. The shard inside her had been speaking all week. It spoke through her handwriting now, through the way she held still.

"Rosalind." She said it plainly, the way you say a name when you've decided you might not get another chance. "The rite needs a vessel. A willing one. Someone whose shape can hold what comes out when we crack the sigil open. My master's notes were very clear about that part."

"And I told you I'd do it."

"And Quill told us *she'd* do it, and now Quill is—" Tam stopped herself. Good. Some sentences shouldn't be finished out loud. "I'm not letting you volunteer out of guilt."

"Guilt's the only currency I've got left. I traded everything else to Vess."

"Then let me pay too." She pressed her palm flat over her sternum, where the swallowed shard sat like a coal that refused to cool. "I'm the only smith alive who knows the flaws in the binding. If something goes wrong inside the rite, you'll need hands that understand wards from the *inside*. That's me. That has to be me."

Above us, the broken dome framed a sky that was not empty anymore. That was the thing nobody warned you about—the thirteenth turn didn't just make the sigil permanent. It made everything else *loud*. The dark between the dead stars had begun to move, slowly, like something turning over in sleep. The crown-thing knew where we were. It had known since Vess smiled at us across his ledger and took everything I'd sold him and turned it into a map with our names written in the margins.

"They're coming," Odile said from the doorway, her voice flat as one of her charts. She leaned on the crutch they'd splinted for her on the salt flats, and she did not lean lightly. "Herald's outriders. An hour, maybe less if they push."

"Then we start now," Tam said.

"No," I said. And then, because lying to her had stopped working somewhere around the salt flats: "Yes. Fine. Yes."

Odile crossed the dome as fast as a wounded woman could, which was faster than I liked, and took up position by the threshold stone—a slab of moon-white rock at the observatory's heart, older than the kingdom, carved with the same sigil-patterns Tam drew from memory. "I'll hold the door," she said. "You two do whatever it is you're going to do. And Rosalind."

"Yes?"

"If this works—if he wakes up—you tell him I'm sorry about the maps. He'll know what it means." Her jaw set the way it had on the pass, holding back the hounds while we ran. Stubbornness was how that woman said *love*, and I'd learned to read it. "Go on. I'll catch up. I always catch up."

It was a lie and we both knew it, and we both let her tell it, because some lies are load-bearing.

Tam began the rite.

* * *

*Tam*

Master Corvane taught me that every ward is a sentence, and every binding is a lie told so carefully it becomes true. *You unbind nothing by force,* he wrote in the ghost-notes I'd carried for six years. *You unbind it by telling a better truth.*

So I told the better truth.

The shards woke one at a time as I laid them into the chalk. Each one hummed a different note of Dominic's life—the drowned chapel gave up its coronation joy, the war room its terrible arithmetic, the lighthouse its love letter written to a woman forty years dead—and each note settled into the circle like a guest finding their seat. Thirteen memories. Thirteen turns of the moon since the sky went black. Thirteen coins Vess once paid for one stolen scrap of a king, because Vess had always known exactly what the merchandise was worth.

The sigil above us answered.

I felt it before I heard it—that pressure behind the eyes, the whisper that had lived in my skull for a week, writing letters in a dead man's hand. But tonight the whisper wasn't whispering. Tonight it was *listening*. Somewhere past the salt flats and the gilded prison and the throne room where Rosalind had spoken into the dark and something had spoken back, the crown-thing was gathering itself, wearing its stolen king like a coat it refused to take off, and it was coming here to stop us or to keep us, whichever required less effort.

"Rosalind." My voice cracked on the second syllable. "The vessel-position. Now."

He stepped into the circle like a man stepping off a cliff he'd already decided was a staircase, and lay down on the threshold stone with his head pillowed on his arm, absurdly casual, as if this were a hayfield in summer and he intended to nap.

"For the record," he said, staring up through the broken dome at the moving dark, "if I wake up as a king, I expect better boots. And if I wake up as nothing, tell Odile she owes me eleven copper for the lantern I bought in Harrow's Cross."

"The lantern burned up on the flats."

"Then tell her anyway. It's the principle."

I knelt beside him and put my hands on the stone, on either side of his temples, the way the ghost-notes showed. Ward-craft is mostly knowing where to put your hands and being brave enough to leave them there.

"Last chance to run," I said.

"I never learned how. Valley-raised, remember?" And he smiled—not the wry armor-smile, but the real one, the rare one, the one that made our strange little family feel like a family instead of an accident. "Truth over safety, Tam. You taught me that. Well. You and a talking crow."

I closed my eyes and spoke the first true word of the unbinding.

* * *

*Rosalind*

Being a vessel, it turns out, feels like being a window in a storm.

The rite opened me the way a key opens a lock—not violently, but with an awful mechanical intimacy, tumblers turning in places I hadn't known I had. And through me poured everything the shards carried, everything of Dominic that Tam channeled down through her scarred palms and into my chest: the coronation, yes, but also the boy before it, the fear after it, the war room where he'd ordered a valley built to hide a thing rather than cage a man, the lighthouse letter, the long deliberate choice to bind himself—to trap the crown-thing inside his own living flesh because it could not survive without a host, and he would rather be a prison than be eaten.

And underneath all of it, resonating like a bell struck against another bell, the fragment. Me. Whatever piece of a king had been cut loose and made flesh and dumped in a forgetting valley with no name and no past and a talent for theft.

I understood it all at once, in the way you understand things when understanding is the last luxury you can afford: the reason the valley's wards never quite erased me. The reason Vess paid too much. The reason Quill had looked at me that night in the safehouse and said, *oh, you fool, you beautiful fool, it's you.*

The sigil felt me feeling it.

Something vast turned its attention toward the little window of me, and through Tam's channeling and the moonlit threshold and thirteen shards singing their thirteen notes, I heard—for the first time without a usurper's mouth in the way—the actual voice of Dominic of the line of Aurelian, king, prisoner, architect of his own cage:

*Who is carrying me?*

"A thief," I said, out loud, into the storm-light. "Formerly of the valley. Currently unemployed. I've been keeping your memories warm for you. They're a bit creased."

A pause. Even now, even then, I swear I felt something that was almost laughter, worn thin by decades of captivity but unmistakably, stubbornly alive.

*You kept them? All this time?*

"Somebody had to. Turns out it was me. Turns out it was always going to be me."

*Then keep them a moment longer,* the voice said, and there was grief in it, and resolve, and something that sounded terribly like an apology pre-emptively issued. *Because to come out, I must pass through you. And passing through, I will take what is mine—and some of what is yours.*

"I know," I said. "Tam told me. The notes said it. I volunteered anyway."

*Why?*

And there it was. The question at the bottom of everything, the one the whole starless road had been asking in a hundred disguises—why does a man with nothing give up the nothing he has?

"Because I stole a lot of things in my life," I said. "Memories mostly. Little bright pieces of other people. Sold them, kept some. And I never once stole anything for anyone else until I met a dying stranger with a locked box and a rude crow, and I thought—here, finally, here is a theft worth committing. Stealing a king back from the thing that ate him." I laughed, and it hurt, and I kept laughing because the alternative was worse. "Let's be honest with each other, Your Majesty. Truth over safety. Truth over loyalty. Truth over self. I've got a whole litany. Shall I recite it while we work?"

*Recite it,* said the king inside the sigil. *And hold on.*

"TAM!" I shouted. "NOW!"

* * *

*Tam*

I broke the binding the way my master would have wanted: precisely, irreverently, and with a great deal of shouting.

The thirteenth turn arrived as I spoke the final word—not as moonrise, though the moon was high and white and burning like a coin someone had nailed to the sky, but as a *sound*. A sound like breaking glass, the same sound the valley's wards had made when they died, except this time it came from everywhere, from the sky itself, from the sigil that had hung over the whole starless kingdom like a lid on a pot.

Rosalind screamed.

I have heard people scream in terror and in pain, and this was neither. This was the scream of a man being *unmade and remade simultaneously*, of a lock and a key and the door all crying out at once. The light that came off him was not light exactly—it was memory given a body, thirteen colors that had no names, pouring upward through the broken dome into the moving dark.

And the dark *tore*.

I saw it happen. Gods help me, I saw it. The crown-thing came apart the way a coat comes apart when the stitching dies—all at once, everywhere, thread by thread unraveling into nothing, because a parasite cannot survive its host leaving, and its host was leaving. Through Rosalind. Through the willing vessel. Through a thief who had chosen to be a doorway.

The Regent—I will not call him anything else, whatever he wore—did not scream. That was the worst of it. He simply became less, and less, and less, a shape losing its argument with existence, and the last thing to go was the face, the identical wrong face, and just before it unraveled entirely the mouth moved and I heard, faint as a memory of a memory:

*"…cold… it was so cold in the dark before him…"*

Then nothing. Then silence. Then—

Stars.

Not all of them. Not yet. But one—two—seven—stars rekindling in the torn dark like sparks from a struck flint, and moonlight flooding the observatory so bright I had to throw my arm across my eyes, and beneath my hands the threshold stone going quiet for the first time in a hundred years.

Rosalind stopped screaming.

I crawled to him. I don't remember deciding to; my body just went. He lay on the stone, breathing, alive, and when I turned him over his eyes were open and fixed on mine and they were—empty. Not dead-empty. *New*-empty. The look of a man standing in a house where all the furniture has been carried out, seeing the rooms clearly for the first time.

"Tam," he said. Slowly, like a word in a language he was learning. "That's your name. Tam."

"Yes," I said, and my voice broke clean in half. "Yes, that's me."

"I know you." He frowned, reaching. "I know—I know there was a lantern, and a crow, and—there was something I was supposed to remember. Something I *kept*." His hand flew to his chest, patting, searching. "Where did it go? Where did I put it?"

Behind us, in the doorway, Odile made a sound I had never heard her make—small and cracked, a cartographer discovering the map was wrong. And beyond her, limping into the moonlight with singed feathers and one wing held wrong and absolutely no right whatsoever to be alive, came Quill.

"Oh good," the crow croaked, collapsing onto the threshold stone next to Rosalind's head. "You're alive. Both of you. Barely. Terribly. We'll discuss my heroics later, there will be a ceremony, I've already drafted remarks." Her voice wobbled on the last word. She covered it with a cough. "Well, thief? Do you remember me?"

Rosalind stared at her for a long, long moment. His empty new eyes searched her ragged feathers, and something moved behind them—not memory, not yet, maybe never—but something older than memory. Recognition's stubborn cousin.

"No," he said honestly. And then, reaching up one trembling finger to touch her breastbone, gentle as a man handling something holy: "But I think I'd like to start."

Quill put her head under her wing, which is crow for weeping, and outside the broken dome the stars kept kindling, one by one by one, and far away across the rooftops of a kingdom learning to see again, a man who had been a king for a century and a prison for longer opened his eyes in the ruins of the throne room, mortal, diminished, and free—and began, slowly, to walk toward the moonlight.

Toward us.

Toward the thief who had carried him home, and could no longer remember why.
