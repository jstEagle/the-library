## 36. Two Names, One Dawn

I woke to the taste of ash and the smell of rain, and the first thought I had—my first thought in thirteen turns, in thirteen years, in a lifetime I had spent as a pattern pressed into metal—was: *I am cold.*

You have no idea what a gift that is. Cold is a complaint, and complaints require a body, and a body requires a self to complain on behalf of. For a very long time I had been neither warm nor cold, neither here nor there, only *witnessing*—a king-shaped echo rattling around inside a sigil, inside a crown, inside a thing that wore my face the way a thief wears a stolen coat. I had watched through its eyes while it ruled my kingdom. I had screamed into its ears while it smiled at my people. I had memorized every cruelty and called it penance.

And now I was cold, and lying on my back on the stones of a ruined observatory, and someone was crying.

Not the pretty kind of crying, either. Not the delicate, single-tear grief that poets get away with. This was ugly, honking, full-bodied weeping, the kind that comes from a person who has been holding themselves together with ward-craft and stubbornness for nineteen days and has finally, blessedly, run out of thread.

I opened my eyes. The sky was doing something impossible.

The stars were coming back.

Not all at once. They returned the way memory returns—flickering, doubtful, a few bright points at first, then constellations assembling themselves like a sentence being remembered word by word. The Hunter. The Weeping Sisters. The old Lantern that my father had taught me to find when I was a boy who still had a father. Each one that kindled drew a sound out of the crowd of exiles scattered across the observatory floor—gasps, laughter, someone singing a fragment of a star-song that hadn't been sung aloud in years because there was nothing to sing it *to*.

I lay there and watched the sky remember itself, and I thought, with the peculiar detachment of a man who has just been unwoven and rewoven: *I did that. Or she did. Or he did. Or all of us did, and none of us will ever agree on the accounting.*

The crying, I discovered when I turned my head, was coming from Tam.

She was sitting on the cracked flagstones with her knees drawn up, ward-chalk still dusting her fingers, staring at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. Which, I suppose, in a sense they had—for a week she had been writing in a hand that wasn't hers, my hand, the hand I'd used to sign treaties and love letters and one deeply regrettable edict about river taxes. She had carried my shards inside her like swallowed coals. She had volunteered for that. She had stood in front of a room of frightened exiles and said, in a voice that shook and did not stop, *my ward-craft makes me the only one who can survive contact,* and she had been—mostly—right.

"Tam," I said.

My voice came out as a ruin. Thirteen years of disuse will do that to a throat. It came out as gravel and rust and the ghost of a command, and Tam's head snapped around, and her eyes went wide, and then she was scrambling across the stones toward me and also away from me at the same time, a crabwise, conflicted motion that ended with her kneeling an arm's length off, staring.

"You're—" she said. "You're *talking*. Out loud. With a *mouth*."

"It's overrated," I rasped. "The mouth. Mostly it just gets you into trouble."

That was when the second thing happened, the thing I had not let myself hope for, because hope is a currency and I had been bankrupt for a long time.

A boy came around the broken rim of the observatory dome. He moved like a thief even though, as far as I knew, he had nothing left to steal—light-footed, watchful, one hand half-raised as if to catch a door before it slammed. He was thin. He was filthy. There was a burn scar along his jaw that I did not recognize, and a way of holding his shoulders that I did, and his eyes—

His eyes were mine. Not similar. *Mine.* The grey of them, the slight downward tilt, the way the left one squinted a fraction when he was suspicious, which was, judging by his expression, constantly.

Rosalind stopped when he saw me looking at him. And I watched the strangest thing happen on his face: recognition, and then the loss of recognition, like a wave running up sand and failing to reach the castle.

"I know you," he said slowly. "I know—I *knew* you. I carried you. I—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "There was a coronation. There was a war room. There was a letter, a lighthouse, a woman's handwriting on the back of—" His hands dropped. "It's gone. It's all *gone*. I can feel the holes where it was. Why are the holes shaped like *you*?"

"Ah," I said, with the vast wisdom of a man who had been awake for approximately forty seconds. "Yes. That."

* * *

Here is what I knew, lying on the observatory floor while the stars stitched themselves back overhead and a thief looked at me with my own eyes full of someone else's grief:

The unbinding had required a willing vessel. That was the flaw Tam's master had built into the sigil and hidden in ghost-notes for her to find—the binding could hold a king against his will forever, but it could only be *undone* by someone who chose it, freely, knowing the cost. The crown-thing had spent thirteen years certain that no one would ever pay that price. It had not counted on a thief with a talent for taking things, or an apprentice with a spine of forged steel, or a crow with a debt the size of a kingdom.

Rosalind had offered himself. That much I remembered from the inside, from the last bright terrible moment before the sigil tore open—his hands on the casing, Tam channeling the shards through her ward-lattices, Quill's feather-burst of light as she shattered the outer ring, and Rosalind saying, not to me, not to anyone, just saying it because it was true: *You don't get to be the only one who sacrificed something.*

The magic had taken him at his word. It had taken everything he'd stolen—every traveler's memory he'd lifted on the border road, every fragment he'd hoarded and sold and treasured, every scrap of a life he'd assembled out of other people's leavings. And it had taken the shards too, the pieces of *me* that he'd carried across a starless kingdom, because a vessel must be emptied before it can be filled, and the rite is not sentimental. The rite is arithmetic.

The memories had gone home. Some to me—I could feel them settling, the coronation, the war room, the letter, whole decades of myself returning like prodigal children. And some, I understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the morning air, had gone back to the travelers of the valley, to farmers and peddlers and pilgrims who would wake in the coming days with sudden inexplicable knowledge of a king's joy and a king's dread, and would spend the rest of their lives wondering where it came from.

And Rosalind—the boy who had been a fragment of me all along, cut loose before the binding, made flesh and set loose to forget—Rosalind had been emptied too. The rite had taken his stolen hoard and his inherited shards and the strange tangle of the two, and left him standing at the bottom of himself with nothing but the one thing the magic could not classify: the night he chose to walk out of the valley.

You cannot reclaim a choice. That is the one clause even the oldest ward-smiths never managed to write. A choice belongs to the chooser, past all unbinding, past all arithmetic.

So he stood there, hollowed and whole, and he did not know who I was, and I knew everything he had done for me, and the dawn was coming up over a kingdom that had its stars back and did not yet know it had its king.

"Someone should say something encouraging," Tam offered, from her knees, wiping her face with a chalky wrist. "I would do it, but I've had a king in my head for a week and I'm not sure any of my thoughts are original anymore."

"Tell me about it," I said.

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

* * *

They got me upright, eventually. It took three of them and a length of rope that Odile would have hated to see used so undignified a purpose, but Odile was not there, and the space where she should have been was a silence everyone stepped around carefully, the way you step around a grave.

I will tell you what I was, in those first hours, because it matters and because no one else will say it plainly. I was *small.* Thirteen years as a sigil had left me a king in name and a ghost in substance. My legs shook on the observatory steps. My hands—my hands, which had signed the founding charter of the valley, which had held a sword at the border, which had held a woman's letter in a lighthouse until the ink nearly transferred to my skin—my hands trembled around a cup of tea that someone pressed into them, and I gripped it with both fists like a child because I was afraid of dropping it, and I was right to be afraid, because I did drop it, twenty minutes later, and Tam cleaned it up without a word and pretended not to see.

Mortality, it turns out, is mostly logistics. Hunger. Cold. The humiliating architecture of a body that expects things of you. I had forgotten—or had never known, the memories of my boyhood were still settling like sediment—how *loud* a body is. The sigil had been silent. The sigil had been nothing but watching. Now there was a constant animal chatter: *eat, sleep, the scar on your ribs aches when it rains, you are fifty-one years old and you have the knees of a man who knelt on stone for thirteen years, which, to be fair—*

And through all of it, Rosalind watched me.

He didn't mean to. I could see him trying not to. But every time I moved, his eyes flicked over, cataloguing, the way a thief catalogs—and I understood, with a tenderness that startled me, that he wasn't studying a stranger. He was studying the holes. He was trying to map the shape of what he'd lost by looking at what remained, the way you can measure a missing tooth by running your tongue along the gap.

Near midday, he sat down next to me on the observatory's broken balustrade. Below us, the exiles were building a cookfire out of the ruins of a telescope mount, which Odile would have called blasphemy and which everyone else called breakfast.

"You should know," he said, without looking at me, "that I resented you. For most of it. The carrying. You're very heavy, for a memory."

"I've been told I'm dense."

"And then somewhere on the salt flats I stopped resenting you, and I can't tell you why, because that part's gone too." He picked at the mortar between the stones. "There's a hole where the reason should be. It's the worst one. It's shaped like—like deciding something."

I thought about the throne room. I thought about the crown-thing's mouth shaping my words, my voice, while I screamed from the inside—and about the moment, near the end, when something in the screaming had changed, because a thief had been dragged in chains before the throne and had looked up at the thing wearing my face and *spoken to me*, not to it. *I know you're in there,* he'd said. *I've carried you. I'd know you anywhere. Come home.*

"I can tell you," I said. "If you want. I was there for all of it. I'm the only witness who was inside your own head."

He finally looked at me then, and there it was again—the squint, my squint, suspicion as a family inheritance neither of us had asked for. "That's a terrible offer."

"It's the only one I have. I can't give you back the memories. The rite took them where they belong. But I can—" I searched for the word, and found it, and it embarrassed me. "I can keep them for you. The ones that were yours. The coronation as you experienced it, the crossing, the salt flats, all of it. I could be your—" *archive,* I almost said, and stopped, because that word had a future attached to it I wasn't ready to speak aloud. "I could hold onto them. Until you want them back. If you ever do."

"That's not how memory works."

"I was a sigil for thirteen years. I have extremely strong opinions about how memory works, and none of them are encouraging."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, a crack in the exhaustion, and I found I would have crossed the kingdom again for it, which was an alarming thing to feel about a person I had known, in this body, for half a day.

"Quill's gone," he said abruptly.

I knew. I had felt her go—the outer ring of the sigil shattering, a small bird against a large darkness, her light going out the way a candle goes out when a hand closes around it. I had felt it and been unable to do anything, which was the whole of my existence for thirteen years compressed into a single instant.

"She helped forge the binding," I said. "She told me once—inside, in the dark, when the crown-thing slept. She said she'd do it again. She said she'd have rather been my murderer than my mourner."

"That sounds like her." His voice caught. "Sounded. That sounds—"

"It sounds like her," I said, and let the tense stay broken, because some things should not be smoothed over. "And she'd be furious to hear us talking about her in the past tense while her body's barely cold. She'd call it premature. She'd call it *sloppy eulogy.*"

That got the smile back, wetter this time. We sat with it. Below, the cookfire caught, and the smell of something frying drifted up, and the stars faded one by one into a blue morning sky that had no business being as beautiful as it was.

"Rosalind," I said.

"It's the only name I've got. The rite checked."

"It's a good name. I chose it." I said it before I could stop myself, and then, since the damage was done: "Before the binding. Before I went into the valley to hide the—there was a boy in the border villages, a ferryman's son, who carried people across a river that forgot them. He kept a ledger of every crossing so no one would be lost. His name was Rosalind. I thought about him a great deal, in the dark. I thought, *if I ever get out, I want to be the kind of person who keeps a ledger.*"

The thief stared at me. The wind came up off the valley below and moved his hair, and for a moment the resemblance was so complete it was like looking into water.

"You're lying," he said, but without force. "Nobody's that—"

"I was a king. We're exactly that sentimental. It's why they let us keep the throne."

* * *

Evening came, and with it the others, drifting back to the observatory in ones and twos from the villages below, where word was moving faster than any of us could: *the stars are back, the Regent is gone, the Regent unraveled on the observatory floor like smoke in a draft.* They came with bread and questions and a wariness that I did not blame them for. I was a king returned from a sigil, diminished and strange, and kingdoms have been broken by less.

I stood at the dome's broken edge and let them look at me. It seemed the least I could do. I had spent thirteen years being looked at without being seen; I could stand still for an hour and be seen without being looked at.

Tam stood at my shoulder. She had appointed herself my herald, or my bodyguard, or my translator—her role was not entirely clear, even to her, but she had planted herself there with the immovability of someone who had carried a king in her belly for a week and was not about to let the world be rude to him now.

"He's mortal," she announced, to the crowd, in the tone of a woman reading a proclamation. "He's tired. He dropped his tea this morning. If anyone has a problem with that, they can take it up with me, and I've got ward-chalk and no patience."

A murmur ran through the crowd—not hostile, not warm. Uncertain. I understood it. I was the proof that the world could be unwoven and rewoven, and proof is frightening.

It was Rosalind who broke the standoff, though I don't think he meant to. He came up through the crowd with a bowl of the fried something from the cookfire, shoved it into my hands, and said, loudly, in the voice of a man settling an account in public:

"Thirteen years I carried you across a starless kingdom. The least you can do is eat."

And the crowd laughed—not much, just a breath of it, a crack in the fear—and something in the evening loosened, and people began, carefully, to talk to me as though I were a person, which I was, newly, and badly.

Later, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the exiles slept in nests of cloaks among the fallen instruments, Rosalind sat beside me again. He had been sitting beside me all day, I realized. A shadow with my eyes.

"I had a dream," he said quietly. "Just now, dozing. There was a valley. There was a road, and travelers, and I was taking things from them—little lights, little—memories. And I was happy. That's the part I can't make sense of. It was lonely and it was cold and I was *happy*, because I was good at it." He looked at his hands. "Was that mine?"

"It was yours," I said. "It's in the ledger."

"The what?"

"Nothing." I would tell him about the archive later. About the thing I was already building in my head, in the space where a throne used to be: a place where every memory the rite returned could be kept, and copied, and *chosen*—where the people who got their stolen years back could come and look at them before deciding whether to wear them. A keeper of forgotten things. I had a name for the position. I had, embarrassingly, only one candidate. "Go back to sleep, Rosalind."

He did. He slept like a stone, like a boy, like someone who had set down a weight so large he'd forgotten his own spine was curved around it.

And I sat awake under the returning stars, mortal and diminished and cold in a way that was a gift, and I held his memories for him the way you hold a coat for someone at a door—carefully, hopefully, not knowing if they'll ever turn around and take it back.

Above the observatory, where the first star had rekindled, a second one was kindling now, and a third, and I counted them the way you count coins you don't yet trust, and far to the south, in the valley that forgets, I would swear the wards had begun—very faintly, very stubbornly—to hum again.

Which was impossible. Which was, I was beginning to learn, the kingdom's native language.
