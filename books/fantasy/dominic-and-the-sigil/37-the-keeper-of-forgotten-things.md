## 37. The Keeper of Forgotten Things

I had been a king for eleven years and a sigil for thirteen more, and in all that time I had never once swept a floor.

This is not a metaphor. I mean it literally. There were four hundred servants in the palace at Carrowmere when I was crowned, and by the time I was old enough to notice them they had learned to move like weather—present everywhere, seen never. My boots were polished while I slept. My fires were laid before I thought to be cold. If a goblet fell from my hand it was replaced before the sound finished echoing, as though grief itself were a thing kings were not permitted to experience unassisted.

So on the morning I stood in the gutted shell of the old observatory with a broom in my hands—a broom, an actual broom made of actual twigs bound to an actual stick—and looked down at a floor thick with thirteen years of ash, owl droppings, and the collapsed remains of somebody's ambitious pigeon coop, I confess I did not know what to do with it.

"Push," said Tam, passing me with an armload of shelving. "Pull is the one that comes toward you."

"I knew that."

"You absolutely did not."

She was right. I did not. I pushed anyway, and the broom did its humble work, and I discovered something that no chronicler of my reign had ever thought to record: sweeping is *marvelous*. It has an end. You begin with chaos and you end with a small neat pile, and the pile does not require diplomacy, or sacrifice, or the wearing of a crown heavy enough to reshape your spine over decades. The pile simply sits there, being swept, until you dispose of it.

I disposed of it into a corner, because disposal systems were also outside my education, and Tam sighed at me the way she sighed at everything now—not with contempt but with the particular fondness of someone who has decided you are her problem forever.

* * *

Let me tell you how we came to be sweeping an observatory, because it was not a decision so much as a slow gravitational settling.

The throne room of Carrowmere still stood. That was the trouble. A kingdom can survive a dark sky; what it cannot survive is an empty chair with everyone looking at it. In the weeks after the thirteenth turn—after the unbinding, after Vess's agents scattered like startled rooks, after the Regent unraveled in front of three hundred witnesses into a smell of cold iron and a drifting grey dust that smelled, faintly and horribly, of my own childhood—the question arrived from every direction at once: who rules now?

The answer, according to the council of lords who assembled within nine days (they were efficient; grief is excellent for efficiency), was: *you, Your Majesty, obviously.*

The answer, according to me, was: *absolutely not, and if you put that thing near my head again I will feed it to a crow.* Quill was dead by then, which made the threat hollow, which made it worse to say, which meant I mostly didn't say it. But I meant it.

Here is what none of them understood, and what I could barely explain even to myself: I had spent twenty-four years inside that sigil watching my own life through glass. I remembered being crowned—I remembered it twice, once from inside my body and once from inside Rosalind's stolen shard, which is an experience I recommend to no one. I remembered the war rooms where I ordered the valley built, and the letter I wrote to Sera that I sealed myself inside the binding to protect. I remembered everything.

And because I remembered everything, I knew something the lords of Carrowmere did not want to hear: the kingdom that had built my prison was not ruled badly. It was ruled terribly, by a thing wearing my face, yes—but the machine beneath it, the wards, the ledgers, the quiet erasures, the valley full of people whose memories were harvested like a crop—that machine had been designed by ministers I appointed. By generals I trusted. By *me*, in the war-room shard, ordering a valley built to hide something rather than imprison someone, and letting the hiding curdle into imprisonment because no one, least of all a king, ever goes back to check on his clever ideas.

A man who remembers everything cannot pretend he deserves a throne. He can only decide what to do with the remembering.

So I refused. Publicly, at the assembly, with the empty crown sitting on a velvet cushion between us like a dead animal at a funeral. I told them I would help rebuild—laws, borders, the return of the stars, all of it—but I would not wear the crown, and I would not sit the throne, and if they insisted they could take up the argument with the part of me that was still occasionally a very large bird-shaped grief, and see how far they got.

Then I walked out of my own capital with a thief's coat over my arm and went looking for a building nobody wanted.

* * *

The observatory had been mine once. Not mine-mine—it belonged to the crown, which belonged to the Regent, which meant it had belonged to the thing that ate me, which is a chain of ownership I am still untangling in my head. But before all of that, before the binding, there had been an astronomer named Odile.

You know her as the disgraced cartographer with the ledger of missing things and the map of every place a star used to be. What you may not know is that she had been charting the sky's slow theft for years before the night the stars actually went out, and that the crown-thing had stripped her title for saying so out loud, and that she had spent two decades waiting, with a patience I can only describe as geological, for someone to come along who might do something about it.

When I told her I wanted the observatory—not as a seat of power, not as a symbol, but as a place to put things back—she looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that had watched too much sky go dark.

"You want to build an archive," she said.

"I want to build a *returning*. An archive implies things just sit there. These memories need to go home."

"To people who don't remember wanting them."

"To people who don't remember anything. Yes." I turned the idea over in my hands, testing its weight. "The valley erased thousands of lives. The sigil scattered mine. Vess collected fragments for years and sold them like spices. Every one of those fragments belongs somewhere—to a mother, a wedding, a first betrayal, a last conversation. Somebody has to carry them back."

"And that somebody is you."

"That somebody," I said, "is us. Plural. I've done the arithmetic, Odile. One king alone builds another tower. One king with a family builds a door."

She snorted—which from Odile is applause—and said, "You'll need a roof that doesn't leak, a cataloguing system better than 'piles,' and at least one person who understands ward-craft well enough to keep the memories from dissolving. Where exactly are you planning to find—"

The door opened. Tam came through it carrying an armload of salvaged ward-lattices under one arm and an opinion under the other, which was her usual load.

"—never mind," Odile finished.

* * *

They came, is the thing. That is the part I keep turning over at night, lying in a cot in the observatory's east tower where the throne's ghosts can't find me. They came.

Rosalind came first, of course—he came before there was anything to come *to*. He arrived at the ruined threshold with nothing, because the unbinding had taken everything: every stolen memory, every fragment he'd carried across a starless kingdom, every scrap of the life he'd pieced together in the forgetting valley. He woke on the flagstones with Tam crying over him and Quill's feather still clenched in his fist, and when he finally spoke, his first words were, "Did it work?" and his second were, "Who is Rosalind?"

We told him. We have told him forty times since. Each telling lands differently, because each time he is a slightly different person assembling himself out of our accounts. He keeps a book now—the first book of the archive, though he doesn't know that yet, because he writes in it privately, in a hand that is entirely his own and yet, when he forgets himself, curls at the ascenders exactly the way mine does. I have decided not to mention this. Some mirrors are kinder left covered.

Tam stayed because Tam had nowhere else and, more honestly, because the sigil had spoken through her for a week and she wanted, more than anything in the world, to understand what had lived in her mouth. She catalogues the shards' residue now. She built the preservation wards herself—flawed ones, honest ones, warded against their own failure in a way her master never taught her. Her guilt has become architecture. I think that's the best thing guilt ever does.

Odile came with her maps and her ledger and her terrible handwriting, and appointed herself Keeper of the Sky, which means she spends her days charting which stars have returned and which haven't, and her evenings drinking tea and informing us precisely how much of the heavens we have failed to fix so far. She is currently at sixty-one percent. She mentions this daily.

And then the others. The exiles from the safehouse. The salt-flats runners who carried Odile down from the pass after she held it—she walks with a stick now and uses it as a pointing device during arguments, which she claims improves her aim. Ward-smiths who fled Tam's old guild. Two of Vess's former couriers who came to surrender themselves and stayed to work off their ledgers, filing memory-vials alphabetically with the fervor of the genuinely ashamed. Even a handful of valley-born, people who crossed the dead border to find out who they'd been, standing in the archive's reading room holding vials of their own childhoods like strangers holding tickets to their own funerals and christenings at once.

We formalized it on a Tuesday, because Rosalind insisted ceremonies should happen on days nobody romanticizes. No coronation. Instead: seven founders, a charter read aloud in the observatory's great dome, and a single rule written above the door in letters Odile painted large enough to read from the road:

**EVERYTHING HERE BELONGS TO SOMEONE ELSE.**

That's the whole charter, really. Everything else is plumbing.

* * *

My work, they decided without consulting me, is the listening.

There is a room in the observatory's west wing—we call it the Quiet—where the returned memories are matched to their owners. Most people arrive frightened. A memory of yourself that you don't remember is a strange inheritance; some refuse theirs, some clutch them too hard, some weep, some laugh, one elderly woman threw hers at my head (a memory of a first kiss; she said the boy had been a fool; she took it back an hour later).

But some of them, when the memory settles home, want to talk. And here is my confession, the thing I have told no one except the page: I am good at this. Twenty-four years inside a sigil taught me to listen the way roots drink—slowly, invisibly, without interrupting. I sat in the dark inside that binding and heard the whole kingdom thinking, and now the kingdom sits across a table from me in daylight and thinks out loud, and I find that I can bear it. More than bear it. There is a moment—almost every time—when someone says *and then I remembered*, and their face does the thing faces do, the unlocking, and I think: *this is what the crown was supposed to buy. This exact moment. And it cost a valley, and a sky, and a crow, and it was never purchasable at all. It only ever had to be given away.*

Rosalind sits in on the difficult sessions. He says it's to learn the collection, but I think it's because in other people's returned memories he is searching, patiently and without hope, for any trace of his own. There won't be any. The magic took what it took. But he comes anyway, and afterwards he asks me questions about the fragments he once sold to Vess—"Was the coronation really like that? Was I there?"—and I tell him true things, and he writes them down, and between us we are rebuilding a man out of secondhand timber.

He asked me once, in the early weeks, whether it bothered me. Giving pieces of my life to strangers.

"No," I said, and found it was true. "A king hoards. I'm told I'm retired."

"And what are you, then?"

I thought about it properly, because he deserved a proper answer. "A keeper," I said. "Keepers don't own the things they keep. That's rather the point."

* * *

Yesterday we opened the doors officially. There was a ribbon, because Tam insisted, and Rosalind cut it with a stolen knife, because he insisted, and Odile declared the observatory the Archive of Returned Things, Founders Seven, in a voice that shook only slightly on the word *founders*.

Afterward we climbed to the dome, all of us, and looked out through the great lens housing at the evening sky. Sixty-one percent. The eastern constellations were back—the Shepherd, the Broken Oar, the long dim river of stars Odile calls the Old Road. To the west there was still a hole where three bright ones used to hang, a black socket in the sky, and I felt it the way you feel a missing tooth.

"One year," Odile said, "to get the western sky back. Give or take a decade."

"We'll manage," Tam said.

Rosalind stood a little apart, as he always does, at the edge of the family rather than the center of it—not excluded, just calibrated that way, like a star that orbits rather than burns. He had the feather out. Quill's feather, kept flat in a book like a pressed flower, black shot with green, the way she'd been in the light before the casing took her. He touched it the way other men touch saints' bones.

"She'd have hated the ribbon," he said.

"She'd have stolen it," I said.

He smiled—one of his real ones, rare, crooked—and for a moment the dome was quiet enough that I could hear the archive below us: pages turning, vials clinking, the murmur of people in the Quiet saying *and then I remembered*, the small machinery of a world putting itself back together one recollection at a time.

It was Rosalind who broke it, the way he always breaks silence—sideways, lightly, so you don't notice the cut until later.

"There's a box in the cellar," he said. "From the stranger. The one who died at my door. We never opened it. The lock finally gave this morning."

Tam frowned. "The thirteenth-turn box?"

"The same." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, and not hope either, but the specific expression of a man who has found a door in a wall he'd believed was solid. "Dominic. The map inside was inked in starlight. Starlight that shouldn't exist anymore."

Below us, in the Quiet, someone laughed at a recovered joke from thirty years ago.

"Show me," I said.
