## 38. What Rosalind Kept

The archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the sea — though the nearest sea was four days' ride and Odile insisted the smell was my imagination, or possibly the ghost of the drowned chapel still clinging to my coat. I had stopped arguing with Odile about anything by then. It was a losing sport, like arguing with a map.

The archive itself had been a granary in its first life, an observatory in its second, and a ruin in its third, which I supposed made it a building with more pasts than most people. Dominic had bought it with the last of the treasury he'd bothered to reclaim, and he'd had it fitted with shelves — endless shelves, oak and iron, climbing toward a ceiling that still bore the scorch marks of the night the stars had gone out. He called it the Archive of Returned Things, which was a mouthful, and Tam called it "the library," which was a lie, and I called it home, which was neither, and all three of us were right.

The work was simple, which is not the same as easy.

When the sigil shattered on the thirteenth turn, everything it had swallowed came loose. Memories, mostly. Centuries of them, ripped from travelers on the border road, from villagers who'd forgotten their mothers' faces, from soldiers who'd forgotten why they'd marched. The magic that had held the valley's forgetting in place unraveled like a dropped knitting project, and all those stolen years went drifting across the kingdom like dandelion seed, looking for the heads they'd been plucked from.

Most of them found their way home on their own. That was the strange mercy of it. A memory knows its own shape the way a river knows its bed. But some got tangled. Some arrived in the wrong dreams, or pooled in low places like fog, or simply sat in the air over a crossroads, humming, waiting for someone to come collect them.

That was my job now. Keeper of Forgotten Things. I went where the humming was, with a ledger, a lantern, and a set of glass jars Tam had warded so that memories couldn't leak out the seams. I gathered the strays, catalogued them, and matched them to the living whenever the living could be found. When they couldn't — when a memory's owner had died in the long dark years, or been unmade by the crown-thing's hunters, or simply never existed in the first place because the valley's wards had worn holes in people's histories — those memories came back to the archive, to the shelves, to the long slow work of deciding what to do with a past nobody was alive to claim.

I was good at it. That was the bitter joke of my life: I'd spent twenty years stealing memories, and now I spent my days returning them, and it turned out the skills transferred almost perfectly. I could read a memory's weight by holding its jar. I could tell grief from guilt from joy by the color of the light it gave off — grief burned blue-white and steady, guilt flickered like a bad lamp, joy was warm gold and always, always tried to escape through the glass. I knew how to handle the fragile ones and how to brace for the violent ones. I knew that people's memories of their children were heavier than memories of anything else, and that memories of ordinary afternoons — bread rising, rain on a roof, a dog asleep in a doorway — were the ones people wept hardest to get back.

"You've got a talent for sorrow," Odile told me once, not unkindly, sorting maps beside me in the reading room. "Always did, I suspect. Even before you knew what you were."

"I've got a talent for theft," I said. "Sorrow just happens to be what everyone was carrying."

She'd snorted and gone back to her maps, and I'd gone back to my jars, and that was the shape of our days.

* * *

It was a grey morning in the second month after the unbinding — the sky still starless, though Dominic swore the dark was thinning, that he could feel the old constellations pressing against the far side of it like faces against a window — when I found the jar that shouldn't have existed.

I'd been re-shelving a crate of strays from the eastern villages. Ordinary stock: a wedding, a funeral, a harvest, a quarrel between brothers that had lasted thirty years because neither of them could remember what it was about. I was halfway through the crate, my fingers grey with shelf-dust, when I lifted a jar that was heavier than it had any right to be.

Small jar. Clay-colored glass, the kind Tam fired cheap for memories that weren't worth her better work. But it pulled at my hand like a bucket at the bottom of a well.

I set it on the table and looked at it, and the looking took a long time, because I recognized the light inside it.

Every keeper learns to read the glow. This one was gold — joy, or something adjacent to joy — but it was a strange gold, shot through with threads of blue-white grief, and around the edges it flickered the way guilt flickers, and at the center it burned steady and stubborn as a lamp in a doorway.

It was the color of me. Of the inside of me, at least, of the particular mixture I'd been carrying since the night the stars went out: the thief's guilt and the exile's grief and the stubborn, stupid, warm-hearted decision to keep walking anyway.

"Quill," I said. My voice came out strange. "Quill, come look at this."

She came. She was older now, her feathers gone grey at the wingtips, and she'd taken to sleeping on the shelf above my desk in the archive, on the theory — she informed me — that I was constitutionally incapable of looking after myself and someone had to keep watch. She hopped down onto the table, cocked her head, and looked at the jar for a long moment.

"Well," she said. "That's yours."

"I know it's mine. That's the problem. The unbinding was supposed to take everything." I turned the jar slowly in my hands. "I gave it all up. Every stolen memory, every fragment of Dominic I was carrying, every year of my life I couldn't account for. The rite took it. I stood in the threshold and felt it go, like water out of a broken jug. I remember — " I stopped, and laughed, because it was still funny, in the way that only terrible things are funny. "I remember nothing. That was the bargain. That was the *point*. I chose truth over my own past, and the past went where it was supposed to go, and I woke up on the observatory floor with Tam crying on my shoulder and no idea who I was except what other people told me."

"And yet," Quill said.

"And yet," I agreed. "There's a jar on my table with my whole self in it, and I don't remember putting it there."

"Tam warded these jars," Quill said. "After the rite. She said — what was it — 'some things shouldn't be allowed to wander off just because they're asked nicely.' She warded every container in the building. Maybe one of your memories got caught in a jar on its way out. Maybe it refused to go."

"Memories don't refuse."

"Yours did," Quill said, "or you wouldn't be holding it."

I looked at her. She looked at me, with the flat black patience that crows have perfected over ten thousand years of watching people be idiots.

"Are you going to open it," she said, "or are you going to stand there holding it until your arms fall off? Because I've seen you do the second thing, and it takes hours."

* * *

I didn't open it that day. Or the next. I put it in my desk drawer, under a false bottom I built for it myself, which should tell you something about how much a thief can change and how much he can't.

I told myself I was being careful. I told myself the responsible thing was to study it first, catalogue it, determine its provenance, consult Tam about the warding, maybe ask Dominic whether a memory that had survived the unbinding might be dangerous — a splinter of the old magic, a trap left by the crown-thing, any of the reasonable explanations. I was very good at reasonable explanations. I'd had twenty years of practice constructing them on the border road, little stories to tell myself about why the thing I was doing was fine.

But the truth, which I was trying, in my new life, to be honest about, was simpler and worse: I was afraid of it.

Here is what I had, after the unbinding. I had a name — Rosalind, given back to me by Tam, who'd heard it enough times to keep it safe for me. I had a face, which the mirror confirmed was mine even if the confirmation felt like a formality. I had a family — Tam and Odile and Dominic and the scattered exiles of the safehouse, who had voted to keep me when I might have been a weapon, and who now fed me and argued with me and saved me a seat at the table as if belonging were a thing you could just *do*. I had work that meant something. I had a crow who insulted me daily and would have died for me, and had very nearly done so, and I had the memory of that sacrifice the way you have a scar: not as an experience, but as a fact about your body.

What I didn't have was a past. Not mine, anyway. I knew Rosalind Thackeray the way I'd have known a character in someone else's novel. *He was a fragment of Dominic, cut loose and made flesh. He worked the border road. He sold memories to a broker named Vess, who was a monster in a merchant's coat. He gathered a family. He carried a king home.* All true. All secondhand. I could recite my own history like a ledger entry, and it sat in me with all the warmth of a ledger entry.

Dominic had it worse, in a way — no, not worse. Different. He'd woken diminished, mortal, stripped of the vast slow centuries the sigil had drunk, but he still had his self. His childhood, his coronation, his wars and his mistakes and the terrible clarity of the night he'd bound himself to trap the crown-thing — all of that had come back to him, or most of it, and he carried it with the careful expression of a man who has read his own biography and isn't sure he likes the protagonist.

But I was a man made entirely of other people's accounts of me. And in my desk drawer, under a false bottom, was a jar that might hold the one account that was mine alone.

* * *

On the fourth night I gave in. Of course I gave in. I was never going to *not* give in; the four days had been theater, a little ritual of resistance so I could tell myself I'd tried.

The archive was empty at that hour. Dominic kept scholar's hours and Tam kept forger's hours and Odile kept the hours of an old woman who'd earned the right to sleep whenever she pleased, so past midnight the reading room was mine alone, lamplit and quiet, the shelves rising around me like a congregation.

I took out the jar. I broke the ward — Tam had keyed them all to me, which she'd done without telling me, which was its own kind of message — and the seal came away with a sound like a held breath released.

The memory took me the way memories do, all at once, no courtesy, no transition.

* * *

I am in the valley. I know it's the valley the way you know your own hand in the dark. The border road runs grey under a sky that has never once shown a star, and the wards hum their glass-note at the edge of hearing, and I am walking it at dusk with a stolen memory in my pocket and a hunger in my chest that has no name because I've never been given the words for it.

I am young. Younger than I was on the night the stars went out. The face I wear in the memory is my face but softer, unlined, a face that hasn't yet learned what it's for.

And I am standing at the crossroads where the border road meets the pilgrim track, and there is a traveler there — a woman, grey-cloaked, tired, a warded lantern at her belt — and she is looking at me with an expression I will spend years trying to describe and will finally find the words for only now, in this archive, at this desk: she is looking at me the way you look at a door you thought was painted shut and have just realized is not.

"You're the thief," she says. Not accusing. Almost gentle.

"I'm a trader," I say, and my voice is my voice, younger, defensive. "I trade fairly."

"You steal memories from people before the valley eats them, and you sell them to a man in a coat full of other people's lives." She smiles, tired. "I've met your broker. I know what he is. And I know what you are, Rosalind Thackeray, even if you don't."

"Nobody knows what I am," I say. "I don't know what I am. There's nothing before the valley. I've checked. There's nothing."

"That's what I came to tell you." She steps closer, and the lantern light catches her face, and I know it — I know it from somewhere deeper than memory, from the place the shard-resonance lived, the place where Dominic's blood in my veins recognized its own. "There *is* something before the valley. You were cut from something larger and set down here like a seed, and the valley was built to keep you from growing. And in nineteen days — in a hundred and ninety days, in a year, in however long it takes — the thirteenth turn will come, and the sigil will close, and everything will be permanent. Everything. Including you."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," she agrees. "But you will. And when you do, there will be a night — there's always a night — when the valley calls you back. When it offers you your rules and your road and your little competences, and asks you to stay small and safe and useful. And on that night, you'll have to choose." She presses something into my hand — small, cold, a coin? a key? the memory blurs there, deliberately, the way important things blur — "I can't give you the answer. Nobody can. That's what makes it a choice. But I can tell you this: whatever you decide, decide it *yourself*. Not the valley's version of you. Not the king's. Not the thief's. You."

And then the memory jumps — the way memories do, the way the important ones always skip the connective tissue and land on the bone —

— and it is night, months later or years, the night the stars are going out one by one above the valley, and I am standing in my own doorway with a packed bag and the locked box under my arm and Quill on my shoulder, and behind me the valley is calling. Not with voices, not with phantoms, though those came too. With *offers*. With the warm pull of the only home I'd ever known, the border road and my rules and my jars, the small safe shape of a life built inside a prison and mistaken for freedom. And I can feel how easy it would be. I can feel the door of the valley standing open behind me like a held breath.

And I — the young me, the softer me, the me who doesn't yet know he's a fragment of a king — I stand in that doorway for a long time. Long enough that Quill shifts her feet. Long enough that the cold comes through the floor.

And then I say — out loud, to no one, to the valley, to myself — "No. I think I'd rather be lost than kept."

And I step over the threshold, and the valley screams behind me, and I burn the bridge — literally, the old toll-bridge, oil and flame, because severance should cost something — and I walk into the dark with a stolen box and a talking crow and no idea who I am, and it is the single most frightening thing I have ever done.

And it is the first thing I have ever *chosen*.

* * *

I came back to myself at the desk with the empty jar in my hands and tears on my face that I had no memory of starting.

The lamp had burned low. Somewhere in the building, a floor creaked — Odile, or Dominic, or the building itself settling into its fourth life. Quill was still on the table, watching me with her head cocked, and she had the decency not to say anything for a full minute, which for Quill was practically a eulogy.

"Well," I said finally. My voice was wrecked. "That's where it went."

"The night you left," she said.

"The night I left. The one memory the unbinding couldn't take." I turned the empty jar in my hands. "Do you know why?"

Quill was quiet a moment longer. When she spoke, she spoke plainly, the way she did only when it mattered. "Because it wasn't a stolen memory. It wasn't a fragment of Dominic, or a scrap off the border road, or something the valley pressed into you. The rite reclaimed everything that was given to you, everything you were *made* of. But that night wasn't made. That night was *done*. By you. Out of nothing." She hopped closer, grey wings folded, black eyes bright in the lamplight. "The magic could take back what you were. It couldn't touch what you chose."

I sat with that. Outside the archive windows, the sky was black and starless still, but I found — and this surprised me, this undid me a little — that I wasn't looking at the dark the way I used to. I wasn't looking at it as a wound. I was looking at it as a page. Something had been written there once; something would be written there again; and in between, there was work.

"I don't remember anything," I said. "I still don't. The valley, the road, the shards, the throne room — all of it, gone, like the rite promised. I know my life the way I know a story I was told." I looked at the empty jar. "Except this. Except the one night where I was nobody's fragment and nobody's thief and nobody's keeper. Just a man at a door, choosing."

"Then that's the truest thing you own," Quill said.

And here is what I did with it, because a keeper's habits die hard: I didn't shelve it. I didn't catalogue it or jar it or assign it a number in the ledger. I let it stay where it was — loose, uncontained, warm in my chest like a coal that refused to go out. Some things shouldn't be archived. Some things you carry.

I blew out the lamp. I sat in the dark of the reading room, in the archive we'd built out of a granary and a ruin and each other, and I felt the shape of my life settle around me — not the shape I'd been given, and not the shape I'd stolen, but the one I'd walked into on my own feet, burning a bridge behind me, choosing lost over kept.

And that was when I heard it: footsteps on the archive stairs, unhurried, familiar, and then Dominic's voice in the dark, quiet and a little unsteady, saying my name.

"Rosalind. You need to come see this. There's a light in the sky."
