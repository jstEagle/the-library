## 39. Stars Returning

A year is a strange unit of measurement for a man who spent most of his life forgetting things. I used to measure time in travelers—how many crossed the border road, how many memories I could lift before the wards dissolved them, how many coins Vess would pay. Now I measure it in catalog cards. In shelves. In the particular creak of the third stair of the observatory, which announces Tam before I ever see her, because she refuses to fix it and calls it "the house's voice."

Tonight the whole house was climbing those stairs.

* * *

The archive lives in what used to be the Regent's ruined observatory, which sounds grander than it is. The dome still has a hole in it shaped like a question, and we've decided to keep it that way. Odile says a building should admit what it doesn't know. Odile says a lot of things like that now, from her chair by the south window, where she rules over our maps with a cane she uses mostly as a pointer and occasionally as a weapon against anyone who touches her ink pots without asking.

She nearly didn't live to be a tyrant about ink pots. The salt flats took a year of her life and gave back a limp and a stubbornness so dense it has its own gravity. She caught me rearranging her star-charts last month—the returned ones, the ones charting where stars *used to be*—and she looked at me over her spectacles and said, "Thief."

"Keeper," I corrected.

"Same trade, better hours." And then she let me keep doing it, which is how Odile says *I love you* in all the languages she never learned to speak.

* * *

We gathered on the roof an hour before dark, because Tam insisted. She'd done the arithmetic herself—her master's arithmetic, the ghost-notes' arithmetic—and she said if a star was going to come back, it would come back tonight, above this dome, at this hour, and she would not have us miss it because someone (me) overslept.

So there we were: me, Tam, Dominic, Quill's empty perch, and a thermos of something Tam brews that she swears is tea and I swear is a threat.

Quill's perch stays on the roof rail. We oiled the wood when it weathered, because none of us ever said out loud that we were keeping it, but all of us kept oiling it. Some griefs you furnish rather than discuss.

Dominic sat with his hands around the cup he wasn't drinking, watching the east go bruise-colored. A year ago he woke up mortal and diminished—a king with no crown, no kingdom, and, for a few terrible weeks, no memory either, until the archive began returning what the sigil had scattered. He remembers more every month. He says it's like being handed his own life back in parcels, some of them damaged in shipping.

He also says the strangest part isn't remembering being king. It's remembering being *bound*—a century pressed flat inside a sigil, wearing another creature's body like a coat worn from the inside. He doesn't talk about that much. When he does, he talks quietly, the way people talk near sleeping animals.

"You're staring," he said, without turning.

"I'm observing. It's my profession."

"Your profession was stealing."

"I've been promoted."

That earned me half a smile, which from Dominic is a parade. A year ago he smiled like a man remembering how; now he smiles like a man who means it, and I have decided not to examine too closely which parts of that are my influence and which parts are simply a man growing into a face that was never allowed to be his.

Tam came clattering up the stairs with the telescope's lens cap under one arm and her notebook under the other. Her notebook goes everywhere with her now. Half of it is ward-craft. Half of it is a hand that isn't hers—or wasn't, during that terrible week when the shard spoke through her. The hand comes back sometimes, faintly, like a scar that aches before rain. She's made peace with it the way you make peace with weather: you don't fight it, you just dress appropriately.

"It'll be the northeast quadrant," she announced, planting herself at the telescope. "Low. Just above where the ridge cuts the sky. My master's notes put the first rekindling exactly—" she checked the page, though she'd memorized it weeks ago "—now-ish."

"Now-ish," Odile repeated from her chair, which two of us had carried up three flights because Odile does not accept limitations, only accommodations. "Your master built a prison around a god-king and your faith rests on his 'now-ish.'"

"My master built the flaw into the prison that let us unmake it," Tam said, chin up. "His 'now-ish' has a better track record than most certainties."

I said nothing, because I was busy feeling the thing I always feel when this family of mine argues about old crimes: that complicated gratitude. Every one of us was mangled by the binding. Every one of us was also, in ways we're still untangling, saved into each other's company by it. I've stopped pretending that's simple.

* * *

Full dark came down soft.

And here is the thing about a starless sky that I never understood until I lived under it: you stop looking up. A whole kingdom learned to look down, at its feet, at its work, at the ground that couldn't disappoint it. For a hundred years nobody in this land saw their own reflection in anything above them. You forget you're the kind of creature that belongs under stars. You start believing you're the kind that belongs in ditches.

I know, because I lived in a ditch of forgetting for years and called it survival.

So when Tam suddenly went rigid at the eyepiece and said, in a voice I'd only heard once before—the night the wards broke—"Oh. Oh, it's *doing it*"—

None of us looked through the telescope. All four of us looked straight up.

At first: nothing. The northeast quadrant just black, the ridge-line blacker. Tam's knuckles white on the brass. Odile's hand found mine without either of us deciding it should. Dominic set down his untouched cup.

Then the darkness above the ridge *thinned*. Not brightened—thinned, the way fog thins when something warm moves behind it. A pinprick of gray. Then gray with an edge to it. Then a point of light so small and so absolute that it seemed less like something appearing and more like something that had always been there, finally being permitted.

It hung there. Trembling slightly, or maybe that was my eyes.

Nobody spoke. I think we were all afraid that speaking might count as claiming it, and none of us felt entitled. A hundred years of dark, and here was one light, returned, and what do you say? *Welcome back? Sorry about the wait?*

It was Tam who broke, of course. Tam, who carried the enemy's voice in her skull for a week and came out the other side writing letters in a dead king's hand. She laughed—one short, cracked sound—and wiped her eyes with the back of the wrist holding the notebook, smudging her own arithmetic, and said:

"We did that. We—we *unmade* it, and the sky noticed."

"The sky didn't notice us," Odile said gruffly, but her voice had gravel in it, and her grip on my hand said otherwise. "The sky noticed the binding breaking. We were just the crowbar."

"The crowbar gets some credit," I said.

Dominic stood. He'd gone very still, the way he does when memory arrives in one of its parcels—except this time nothing was being handed to him. This time he was looking at something that had been taken from him before he ever wore a crown, something older than his reign, older than his theft, older even than the thing that stole him. A star. Just a star. And he looked at it like a man reading a letter from home.

"There will be others," he said quietly. "One a season, perhaps faster. The ward-lattice is unraveling cleanly. Tam's flaw held."

"Our flaw," Tam corrected. "You helped carry the shards."

"I carried boxes. You carried the impossible."

"And Rosalind carried us," Odile said, and then looked appalled at herself, and covered it by jabbing her cane at the telescope. "Someone check whether the light is steady or flickering. Flickering means the lattice is still unstable in that sector and I'll need to revise the return-charts."

"Odile," I said.

"What?"

"Let it just be a star for five minutes."

She glared at me. Then she lowered the cane. Then—this is the part I'll write in the archive ledger, because someone should record the miracles, even small ones—she stayed silent for almost the full five minutes, watching the light with the rest of us, an old woman who had charted missing things her whole life finally watching something un-miss itself.

* * *

Later—I don't know how much later; time went soft up there—Tam fell asleep against the telescope tripod, notebook open on her knees. Odile dozed in her chair, cane across her lap like a scepter she'd earned the hard way. Dominic and I stayed awake, because kings and thieves both come from professions that distrust sleep.

"Do you remember," Dominic said, "the night you chose to leave the valley?"

"That's the one memory I kept," I said. "The magic couldn't reclaim it. Apparently choice is non-refundable."

"It's the memory I envy most in the whole archive." He turned the untouched cup in his hands. "Everyone remembers things that happened to them. Yours happened *because* of you."

I wanted to say something wry. That's my armor; I've told you that. But up there under one returned star, with my family asleep around me, the wry thing wouldn't come. What came instead was the truth, plain as the light on the ridge:

"A year ago I was a thief in a valley that forgot me daily. If you'd told me I'd end up keeper of everything everyone else lost, living in a house with a hole in the roof on purpose, drinking Tam's punishment-tea—I'd have picked your pocket and walked away."

"And now?"

I looked up. The star burned on, small and patient, and somewhere behind it, invisible, other lights waited their turn in the dark like a held breath.

"Now I'm waiting for the rest of them," I said. "One a season, you said?"

"At least."

"Good." I pulled my coat tighter. "Because I've started a new shelf in the archive. Big one. Empty."

"For what?"

"For everything the stars remember," I said, "that nobody down here thought to write down."

Dominic laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and above us the first star of the returned sky trembled on its pinprick thread, and I could have sworn it winked.

Somewhere below, in the dark of the valley I no longer feared, something else stirred: a second point of light, faint, far south, where no chart of ours predicted one.

Tam slept on. But I saw it.

And I reached for my pen.
