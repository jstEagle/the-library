## 40. Dominic and the Sigil

The trouble with writing the truth, I discovered in my first week as Keeper of Forgotten Things, is that truth is heavier than it looks.

Lies are light. A lie is a coin you can flip across your knuckles, spend anywhere, forget in a pocket. But the truth — the whole of it, the valley and the wards and the thing wearing my king's face, the crow who broke her own heart to fix it, the girl who swallowed a shard of a dead man's joy and carried it like a coal in her chest — the truth is a stone you have to carry with both hands, and every time you set it down you have to decide whether to pick it up again.

I picked it up. Ink-stained, sleep-deprived, on the forty-third draft of a sentence about the drowned chapel that still wasn't right, I picked it up.

"Rosalind." Dominic's voice came through the archive door the way his voice did everything now: quietly, with an apology built into it. "You've been at that desk since before dawn."

"I'm working."

"You're glaring at the page like it owes you money."

"It does. It owes me *everything*." I looked up. He stood in the doorway with two cups of tea and the particular carefulness he'd worn for a year now, ever since waking mortal and diminished, like a man relearning the weight of his own hands. The crown had never suited him. The tea tray did. That was the joke none of us said aloud: the kingdom had fought a war over him, bound him into a sigil, starved the sky of stars to keep him hidden — and what he'd wanted, all along, was to be useful in a small room with people who knew his name and didn't kneel.

He set a cup beside my elbow. Steam curled up and fogged the bottom edge of the page, which was, frankly, an improvement.

"How many words today?" he asked.

"Four hundred. Two hundred of them are 'the.'"

"A pace even Vess couldn't fault."

I flinched. He noticed; he always noticed; that was the other thing about being known. "Too soon?" he asked.

"It's been fourteen months."

"And yet some names stay sharp." He sat on the corner of my desk, which no king should do and which he did anyway, because the throne stood empty in the capital and he'd told the assembled lords, in front of witnesses, that he would rather keep a library than a crown. Half of them thought it was humility. Tam thought it was tactics. I knew it was neither. It was a man who had spent thirteen turns inside a sigil learning exactly how much of himself he'd been willing to lose, and who had come out the other side wanting only the parts that fit in a room this size.

"The chapter's fighting me," I admitted. "The thirteenth turn. I've written it six ways. Every version is either too big or too small."

"What happens in it?"

"You were there."

"I was *dissolved* there." He smiled, and it was the new smile, the one with cracks in it, the one I trusted more than the old coronation-day brightness I'd once stolen off a stranger's face and sold for thirteen coins. "Tell me what it felt like from your side of it. You're the one who remembers."

That was the arrangement, in the end. That was the whole strange economy of the archive. The magic had taken back what it had given me — every stolen memory, every fragment I'd lifted from travelers on the border road, every shard of Dominic I'd carried across a starless kingdom. They'd gone out of me like water through open fingers, and I had woken on the observatory floor knowing my own name and almost nothing else. But choice, it turned out, is not a memory. Choice is a bone. It stays in the body after everything else is stripped away. And I had chosen, at the threshold, with the sigil tearing open and Tam screaming and Quill already a small dark smear against the breaking dark — I had chosen truth over safety, over family, over self.

So I remembered nothing and knew everything, and Dominic remembered everything and could not bear to look at most of it, and between us we made one fairly functional person, spread across two.

"Start with the cold," I said, mostly to myself, and wrote: *The last turn began with cold, because all the important things in my life have begun with cold — the valley mornings, the river under the drowned chapel, the moment the wards died and the sky came down like a dropped chandelier.*

Dominic read over my shoulder, silent. Then he said, "You didn't have a chandelier in the valley."

"No one will know that."

"I'll know that."

"Then it's a good thing," I said, "that this book isn't for you."

* * *

The archive had been a granary once, then a barracks, then, during the Regent's reign, a place where inconvenient documents went to be burned. We kept the burn-room. It seemed honest to leave the scorch marks on the ceiling — a reminder of what places like this are for when people get frightened. Odile had insisted on it. "An archive without a memory of burning," she said, "is just a pile of paper waiting to become smoke."

Odile ran the cataloguing floor now, on a cane she'd carved herself from the door of the pass where she'd held off Vess's agents so we could run. She walked with a hitch and refused, absolutely refused, any suggestion that she slow down. "Go on," she'd say, whenever someone offered an arm. "I'll catch up." It had been her joke while bleeding out on the salt flats, and it was her joke now, and we all let her have it because some jokes are load-bearing.

She'd recovered. Mostly. The wound had taken the mapmaking hand — her left, the fine one — but she'd taught herself to draw with her right, and the charts she produced now hung along the archive's long wall: the kingdom as it used to be under stars, the kingdom as it was without them, and, pinned in the middle, the newest one, updated monthly, with small gold dots appearing at the edges like a rash of returning light.

Three stars, as of last month. All near the observatory. All faint.

"They're coming back faster than the astronomer predicted," Tam told me that afternoon, finding me in the stacks with ink to my elbows. She'd grown half a head taller in the year and carried herself differently — not softer, exactly. Settled. Like a ward that had found its true anchor-point. "Odile says if the rate holds, the sky over the capital clears within five years."

"And over the valley?"

Tam was quiet a moment. The valley. None of us had gone back. There was nothing to go back to — the wards were dead, the memoryless fog had lifted, and the people who'd lived there were slowly discovering that they had pasts, most of them ordinary, a few of them terrible, all of them arriving like weather. Some had thanked us. One had thrown a rock. I understood the rock better than the thanks.

"The valley gets its stars last," Tam said finally. "It's farthest from the threshold. That's just geometry." Then, gentler: "You could visit. Just to see."

"I don't need to see it." I slid a returned memory-case onto its shelf — glass and silver-leaf, holding somebody's grandmother's kitchen, somebody's first snow. "I spent twenty years there not seeing anything. I'm done giving it the satisfaction."

"That's not why you won't go."

No. It wasn't. The real reason sat in my chest where the stolen memories used to sit: a hollow, clean and aching, shaped exactly like a life I couldn't recall. Whatever I had been before the valley — before the wards scraped me blank and left me a thief with quick hands and no history — whatever fragment of Dominic or whoever else I'd been, that was gone into the unbinding, spent like a coin, and no ledger in Odile's catalogue listed it. I was a man made entirely of choices, standing in a building full of everyone else's yesterdays.

Some nights that hollow sang. Most days it was just a room in me with good light, waiting.

"Besides," I said, "if I go back, who's going to finish this book? You?"

"I write excellent marginalia."

"You write *threats*. In the margins of my drafts. With arrows."

"Motivational arrows." She grinned, took the tea cup Dominic had left, drained it cold, and left the way she did everything: like someone who had decided where she was going and saw no reason to make a ceremony of it.

I looked at the margin of my current page. Sure enough, in her sharp little hand: *FINISH IT OR I'M TELLING QUILL'S STORY MYSELF AND I'LL GET THE DIALECT WRONG ON PURPOSE.*

* * *

Quill's perch stood by the archive's south window, empty, dusted daily, because Odile dusted it daily and nobody had ever discussed why.

We had buried what we could find of her at the observatory threshold — a handful of black feathers and the outer casing of the sigil, cracked down the middle like an egg. Tam had warded the grave against crows, then undone the ward the same evening, weeping, saying it wasn't right to keep other crows from visiting, even though we both knew no crow would come, because there had never been another crow like her.

She talked. Gods, how she'd talked. Sarcasm and confession in equal measure, shame and loyalty braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was holding. She had helped forge the binding — had perched on the ward-smith's shoulder while the sigil was cut, believing, truly believing, that a cage was kinder than a coffin, that a king dissolved was a king preserved. And when she learned what the binding had actually fed — when she understood that the crown-thing wore its prisoner like a coat and called the wearing mercy — she had flown into the heart of it, a small bird against a large darkness, and broken the casing with her own body, and bought us one turn. One turn was all we needed. It was also, precisely, all she had.

In the book, I gave her the longest chapter. It was the least I could do and also the most, because every time I tried to write her dialogue I heard her voice correcting me — *I'd never say it that sweetly, thief, sharpen it* — and I sharpened it, and the pages got better, and the grief got a shape it could be carried in.

That was the secret of the whole archive, really. That was what I'd learned in a year of Keeper work, sorting returned memories into cases and shelves: grief doesn't shrink. It just learns to be shelved where you can reach it.

* * *

Vess we did not shelve. Vess we tried, convicted, and — at Dominic's insistence — released.

"He was a herald," Dominic had said at the hearing, "not an architect. The crown-thing made him out of whatever it found. Fear, mostly, and ambition, and a very good head for accounts." The old Dominic might have said *mercy* and meant *strategy*. The new one said it and meant it, which was somehow more unsettling. "He tracked the shards because he was made to track them. Put him somewhere with ledgers and leave him alone."

So Vess kept ledgers now, in a comfortable cell with a window, doing the archive's accounting unpaid, and every quarter he sent up a note in his immaculate hand: *Your expenditures exceed your budget. Also, chapter nine is structurally unsound.* I hated that he was right about both. I hated more that he'd been right all along, back on the border road, paying too much for a coronation memory, asking too little — every bargain surveillance, every coin a hook. When I'd brought him the box and the map inked in starlight, I'd thought I was selling merchandise. I'd been delivering a report.

But here was the thing I'd learned about being robbed: it teaches you exactly what a thing is worth. And I had been robbed of a self, and so I knew, now, precisely what a self costs. Which meant I could price everyone else's honestly. Even his.

Even mine.

* * *

On the last day of autumn, with the first snow ticking against the south windows, I wrote the ending.

Not the battle — the battle had taken me three months and forty-one drafts and one screaming argument with Odile about whether the salt flats scene needed her dying speech included verbatim ("Include it," she'd said, arms crossed, cane planted. "If you soften it I will haunt you, and I have experience haunting"). Not the throne room, where I'd spoken to the man inside the monster and something had answered through the usurper's mouth — two prisoners recognizing each other across a borrowed face. Those chapters were done, sealed, argued over, done.

The ending. The part after. The part nobody writes, because the story is supposed to stop when the crown breaks and the stars start coming home.

But the story doesn't stop. That's the lie every ballad tells. The story keeps going, into the morning after, into the year after, into the granary-turned-archive where a diminished king makes tea and a limping cartographer redraws the sky and a ward-smith's apprentice leaves motivational threats in the margins and a hollow sits in a thief's chest, clean and aching and, lately, almost companionable — a room with good light, waiting.

I dipped the pen. Outside, the snow came down soft as ash, and somewhere above the cloud, three stars and counting were burning their way back into a sky we'd emptied to hide a king.

*They ask me sometimes,* I wrote, *what I kept. After the unbinding took everything — every stolen hour, every borrowed face, every shard of the king I carried across a starless country — they ask what was left in me. As if the magic must have missed something. As if a thief always has one last coin sewn into his coat.*

*Here is what I kept. Not a memory. A night. The night I chose to walk out of the valley with a dead stranger's box under my arm and a talking crow on my shoulder, past the phantom voices calling me home to a home that had never once known my name. The magic couldn't reclaim that night, because it was never anyone's to take back. It was never Dominic's, never the valley's, never even mine in the way memories are mine. It was simply the moment I became the person who did the next thing, and the next, and the next, until the next thing was a desk in an archive and a pen running dry.*

*I am not the king. I am not the fragment of the king, not anymore; that went into the unbinding with everything else, and what woke on the observatory floor was new — a keeper, not a thief, a man with no yesterday and a great many tomorrows. Dominic says that sounds lonely. Dominic says a lot of things, mostly while making tea. What I tell him is this: everyone else in this building knows where they came from. Their memories came home to them in glass cases, labeled and shelved. I am the only one who gets to be made entirely of what comes next.*

*Someone has to be. Otherwise the archive is just a warehouse for the past, and the past, as a wise and insufferable bird once told me, is only worth keeping if it buys you a braver tomorrow.*

*This book is the first entry. It is not the king's tale, though the king is in it, changed and changing. It is not the thief's tale either, though the thief carried it, turn by turn, across the dark. It is ours — the exiles and the ward-breakers and the girl with the shard in her chest and the crow who paid the toll — and it belongs to anyone who walks through that door having lost something, which is to say, everyone.*

*My name is Rosalind Thackeray. I remember nothing, and I know everything, and I am keeping the difference safe for you.*

I signed it. I blotted it. My hand shook, a little, and I let it.

* * *

Dominic found me at dusk, standing in the long gallery with the finished manuscript in both hands, looking at the shelf where it would live. First case, first shelf, first book. Behind it, ten thousand cases of returned memories glowed faintly in the lamplight — kitchens and coronations, first kisses and last arguments, the whole scattered hoard of a kingdom's forgotten years, coming home one case at a time.

"Well?" he said.

"It needs a title."

"You've had a year."

"Titles are load-bearing." I turned the manuscript over, looked at the blank cover, and laughed — suddenly, helplessly, at the obviousness of it. Of course. Of course it had been the title all along, sitting there through every draft, patient as a star waiting for its sky.

I wrote it on the cover in my best hand, the one Tam hadn't gotten to yet:

**DOMINIC AND THE SIGIL**

"Two names," Dominic said quietly, reading it upside down. "Yours isn't on it."

"It's in it. Every page." I slotted the book into its place. It fit the way true things fit — snug, inevitable, a little heavy. "Besides, the title's honest. Not the king's tale alone. Not the thief's either. Both of us, and the thing between us that broke."

He stood beside me a long moment, shoulder to shoulder, two men made of halves. Down in the cataloguing hall, I could hear Odile arguing with a volunteer about alphabetical order, and Tam laughing somewhere, and the small endless industry of people rebuilding a world one labeled case at a time.

Then the bell above the archive door rang.

We both turned. A traveler stood in the entrance, shaking snow from her cloak — road-worn, wary, with the particular hungry look I recognized from the border road, from mirrors, from every person who has lost something and doesn't yet know it can be found. She clutched a bundle to her chest, and from inside the bundle came a sound that stopped my breath and started my heart:

A voice. Small, dry, sardonic, unmistakable.

"—and put me *down*, I can walk, I have been walking since before your grandmother's grandmother was an egg—"

Dominic's hand closed on my arm. The traveler looked up at us, at the shelves, at the first book glowing on the first shelf, and asked the question I had been waiting my whole unlived life to answer:

"Is this the place? Where they keep what everyone forgot?"

I smiled, and reached for a fresh pen.

"Yes," I said. "Come in. Tell us everything."
