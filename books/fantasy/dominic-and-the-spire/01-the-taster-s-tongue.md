## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's supper arrived at the tasting table under a silver dome and a guard's bored stare, and Lena Hartwell greeted it the way she greeted all things: with attention.

She had been the royal taster for six years now, long enough that her tongue had become a kind of library, every flavor filed and cross-referenced against a hundred dinners, a thousand sauces, one memorable poisoning that had nearly ended her career along with her pulse. The kitchen staff called her *the Ledger* behind her back — she'd heard them — because she could tell you what a dish contained the way an accountant could tell you what a purse contained: to the coin.

Tonight's first course was pheasant in a dark sauce of elderberries and something warmer beneath it. She took a small spoonful, let it sit on her tongue like a question, and waited.

Cinnamon. No — cassia, cheaper, cut with something to stretch it; the palace cook had been skimming again. Elderberry, properly reduced. And underneath both, faint as a whisper through a wall, a bitterness shaped like almonds but wrong, almond's poorer cousin, the taste of—

*Apricot kernels.* Cyanide's rustic grandmother. Not enough to kill. Enough to sicken, over weeks, in a way no physician would trace to a dinner plate.

Lena set down the spoon, rinsed her mouth with the water provided — bread and water only, nothing that might mask or compound — and made a note in her leather book with a small pencil stub worn round as a river stone. Her handwriting was precise without being beautiful, each letter standing where it was put, like soldiers on parade. *Course one: mild toxicity. Source: apricot kernel oil in sauce. Intent unclear — possibly kitchen economy, possibly slow work. Recommend substitution of stock sauce.*

The second course was soup, white as moonlight, made of almonds.

She almost laughed. Someone in that kitchen was either very careless or very clever, layering the same poison family across two courses so that if one was caught, the other might pass unnoticed. But the soup held a second secret, subtler than the first: a mineral edge, cold and metallic, that sat at the back of her throat like a coin held between the teeth.

Hemlock water. Diluted past detection by any tongue less disciplined than hers — and hers was not merely disciplined, it was *trained*, years of guild exercises teaching her to separate flavors the way a jeweler separates gravel from gemstones. The dose was small. The intent was not.

*Course two: hemlock infusion, low concentration. This is deliberate. Flag to Captain Verel, quietly.*

The third course — candied figs, honeyed cheese, a little pastry swan whose wings were spun sugar — appeared innocent. Lena tasted it anyway, methodically, and found nothing lethal at all, which after two poisons felt like its own kind of message. What she found instead, folded into the honey of the cheese, was a ghost of bitterness so familiar it stopped her breath for half a second.

Foxglove. A single grain's worth. Not enough to harm a king. Exactly enough to be noticed by a taster who knew what foxglove tasted like, which was everyone in this palace, which meant it wasn't meant for the taster to catch at all.

It was meant to be found. A plant. Someone wanted the kitchen searched, a servant accused, a scapegoat led away in irons while the real hand stayed clean.

Lena knew how scapegoats worked. She had very nearly been one herself, ten years ago, before a stranger with ink-stained fingers had appeared at her trial with documents that turned the whole proceeding inside out. She did not think about that often. She thought about it now, looking at the pastry swan with its ridiculous sugar wings, and wrote in her book: *Course three: staged contamination, foxglove, trace. Do not report. Investigate.*

Then she ate the whole serving anyway, because the law of her office required it, and spent the following hour feeling her heart stumble and quicken under the foxglove's small cruelty, sitting perfectly still with her hands folded until it passed, as it always passed, as everything passed except the things that didn't.

* * *

By the time the king had eaten — heartily, praising the sauce, oblivious — the palace lamps were burning low and Lena was free to go home, if you could call it that.

Home was the guildhall, which stood in the crook of a street that most maps forgot to draw, behind a door with no sign, up stairs that creaked in a pattern she knew by heart: step wide on the third, skip the seventh entirely. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its premises shabby on purpose. Grandeur invited inspection, and inspection was death to a business whose entire inventory consisted of things that officially could not exist.

She let herself in through the kitchen entrance, as always, and the smell of the place wrapped around her like a coat: old paper, candle wax, dried lavender hung against moths, and beneath it all a faint sweetness that no visitor ever identified — the smell of bottled last words, three hundred jars of them shelved in the west room, each one humming very slightly, like bees dreaming.

The main hall was empty. It usually was, at this hour. Wren came and went like weather; Odile worked nights and slept days and answered questions only when paid to. And Dominic —

Dominic was wherever Dominic was, which tonight meant his archive, down the corridor, behind a door he'd fitted with a bell that rang only when he wanted it to, which was a piece of impossible craftsmanship nobody discussed too loudly.

Lena hung her cloak, lit a lamp at the ledger desk — her desk, the great scarred oak table that dominated the hall's center — and opened the night's accounts.

This was the part of the day she loved best, though she'd have denied it under torture. The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things were unlike any other books in the kingdom. In their pages, debts were recorded in units most treasurers would find nonsensical: one borrowed luck, repaid with interest of two borrowed lucks; one honest lie, sold twice, which should have been impossible and therefore fit the inventory perfectly; six hours of someone else's courage, withdrawn from stock and returned depleted, requiring replenishment. Every entry balanced, because everything balanced, because balance was the only law Lena had never seen broken — not by kings, not by courts, not even by the strange subterranean powers the guild traded with when trade became necessary.

Her pencil moved. *Jar 214 (last words, fisherman, saltwater dialect) — returned to shelf, seal intact, minor condensation noted. Stock: luck, common grade — withdrawn 2 measures by W., purpose unstated — W., if you are reading this, state your purpose or return the luck. Honest lie, unsold — note: Odile attempted to purchase for third time this month. Answer remains no. Some things should not be duplicated.*

She paused over that last line, pencil hovering. The honest lie was a curiosity even by guild standards: a single falsehood, bottled, that remained true while being untrue, and which everyone agreed must never be sold again lest it breed. Lena had appraised it once and refused to name a price. Some entries in the ledger existed only to remind you that value and price were different animals.

Outside, rain began against the high windows, soft as fingertips. The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the west room, jar 89 hummed a half-tone higher than the others, as it did every night at this hour, and Lena had long ago stopped finding it unsettling and started finding it company.

That was the truth she carried home every evening and unpacked nowhere: the guildhall was full of voices and she was alone in it. The jars spoke. The ledgers spoke, in their dry way. Wren and Odile and Dominic passed through like comets, bright and brief. But the desk was hers, and the hours after midnight were hers, and no entry in any book, in any hand, in any age, had ever once said *Lena was here.*

She was the keeper of records. She was in none of them.

She told herself this was professionalism. Tasters survived by being forgettable — a famous taster was a dead taster, since poisoners plan around reputations. And guild archivists survived by discretion; the fastest way to lose a client was to appear in the historical record beside their purchases. Her absence from every account was a discipline, a craft, a wall built brick by brick over ten years.

She believed that, mostly, right up until the moments she didn't — the moments like now, when the rain got louder and the lamp burned lower and she caught herself reading back through a decade of immaculate entries, searching for some trace of the woman who'd written them, and finding nothing but the writing itself. As if the words had simply condensed out of the air, ownerless, like frost on glass.

"You're doing the thing again."

Lena didn't jump. Six years of tasting other people's malice had burned the startle reflex out of her. She looked up instead, unhurried, and found Dominic leaning in the doorway to his archive with a cup of tea in each hand and the particular expression he wore when he'd been watching her longer than politeness allowed.

"What thing," she said.

"The thing where you read your own ledger like it's going to confess something." He crossed the hall — he moved quietly for a big man, another item on the long list of things about him that didn't add up — and set one cup on the desk within her reach. Tea, weak, exactly the way she took it. He'd known that for years. Neither of them remarked on it. "It won't, you know. Ledgers only confess to people who aren't looking."

"That's philosophically incoherent."

"I'm a smuggler-archivist. Coherence is for priests." He settled onto the corner of the desk, which he knew she hated, which was why he did it, and blew steam off his own cup. "Bad supper?"

"Three poisons." She pulled the tea toward her and let its warmth reach her fingers before drinking. "One probably accidental, one definitely not, and one that was only there to be found."

He raised his eyebrows. "A plant."

"A plant. Someone wants a body in chains by week's end, and they're not particular whose." She said it flatly, the way she reported all unpleasant things, and watched him absorb it. His face did something complicated — a flicker, quickly smoothed, of an emotion she couldn't file. Concern, but angled strangely, as if aimed slightly past her at something she couldn't see.

"You'll stop it," he said. Not a question.

"I'll try."

"You'll succeed," he corrected, with a certainty she hadn't asked for and couldn't account for. He was like that sometimes — certain of her in ways that outran the evidence, as though he'd read her ending in some book she'd never been shown. It should have been irritating. Mostly it was. Tonight, with the rain coming down and the foxglove still fizzing faintly in her blood, it was also — she admitted it only to herself, in the privacy of her own skull — a small warmth, like the tea.

"Drink your tea," she said, to end the moment.

"I'm drinking my tea."

They drank their tea. Rain on glass. Jar 89 humming. The lamp making a small gold room in the middle of all that dark.

Dominic set down his cup and nodded at the open ledger. "Anything interesting in there tonight? Beyond the usual impossibilities?"

"Wren withdrew two measures of luck and left no purpose."

"Two measures?" He winced theatrically. "Whatever they're planning, either it's enormous or it'll fail spectacularly. Possibly both."

"And Odile tried to buy the honest lie again."

"Third time." He clicked his tongue. "Persistent woman. You have to admire it."

"I don't have to do anything of the sort." But she was smiling slightly, and he saw it, and she saw him see it, and neither of them made anything of it, which was its own long-running conversation between them, conducted entirely in things unsaid.

He leaned over then, casual as anything, and glanced at the page — a liberty, but the ledgers weren't secret from him; he'd taught her half the cataloguing system when she first came to the guild, back when she was a condemned girl with nothing but a talented tongue and a talent for survival. His eyes moved down the columns. Then he straightened, and his voice went light in a way she'd learned to distrust.

"You know what I've never understood," he said, tapping the margin with one finger, "is why there's no entry for you."

"There's no entry for anyone's meals. I don't catalogue my own—"

"No, I mean it." He wasn't smiling anymore, quite. "Ten years of these books. Thousands of entries. Bottled words, borrowed luck, smuggled miracles, the lot. And nowhere — not one line, not one marginal note, not so much as a coffee stain in the shape of your initial — is there any record of Lena Hartwell. The woman who balances impossibilities doesn't exist in any of them." He cocked his head. "Doesn't that strike you as odd?"

"It strikes me as professional."

"It strikes me as a hole," he said, "in a very well-kept book. And I don't trust holes." He picked up his tea again, and the lightness came back into his voice like a curtain drawn shut. "Someone ought to write you in somewhere. Before the ink dries on the rest of it."

"That's morbid."

"That's archival." He drained his cup and stood, stretching, and for just a moment his eyes went to the window — to the rain, to the dark beyond it, to the mountain whose roots the city grew from — and whatever crossed his face then he took away with him, folded up small, the way he took everything. "Finish your balancing. Don't stay up all night arguing with the past. It cheats."

He padded back down the corridor. The bell above his archive door gave its single private chime, and then the hall was hers again: the lamp, the ledgers, the jars murmuring their three hundred small farewells in the west room.

Lena looked down at the open page, at the neat columns of her own handwriting, and found, absurdly, that her pencil had stopped working.

*A hole in a very well-kept book.*

She dipped the pencil, meaning to resume with the next entry — stock count, luck, common grade — and instead, before she quite decided to, she wrote in the margin, small and slanted:

*L.H., present. Midnight. Rain.*

Then she sat and stared at it, feeling exposed, as though she'd spoken aloud in an empty church. Ten years of silence, broken by five words. It changed nothing. It proved nothing. It was a drop of ink in an ocean of entries.

But it was there. She was there. And tomorrow — she told herself this firmly, the way one tells oneself things one intends to believe — tomorrow she would shelve the new folios that had come in from the estate of a dead collector, folios of prophecy and old law that needed cataloguing, and she would do it precisely, and thoroughly, and she would not think about holes, or margins, or the shape of her own initials anywhere near them.

Tomorrow, as it happened, the date would already be circled.

She didn't know that yet. She blew out the lamp, and went to bed, and slept better than she would ever sleep again for a very long time.
