## 2. A Date in Old Ink

The prophecy folios lived on the top shelf of the archive, which was the guild's way of saying that nobody wanted to deal with them.

Lena Hartwell had been dealing with them for two hours.

It was not, strictly speaking, her task. The shelving rota — which she maintained herself, in a small leather-bound book she kept inside another book, because redundancy was a virtue and the guild's memory was a sieve — assigned folio-duty to Pip Merrow, who had been assigned it every month for three years and had avoided it every month for three years with an artistry Lena almost admired. Tonight he'd outdone himself: a note pinned to the ladder claiming a sick grandmother, when Pip's grandmother had died before Lena joined the guild. He'd used the same note eleven times. He never even changed the ink.

So Lena climbed the ladder instead, because someone had to, because the archive did not forgive disorder, and because — though she would have denied it under oath — because the alternative was going home to her narrow room above the taster's quarters and lying awake cataloguing the aftertastes of the king's supper until sleep took pity on her.

The guildhall at night was a different building. By day it clattered and argued and smelled of tallow and pepper and Odile's iron-gall ink; by night it settled into itself like an old animal, creaking along its joints, breathing through the gaps in the shutters. Candlelight turned the archive into a canyon of spines. Somewhere below, Dominic was supposed to be re-cataloguing the bottled last words, but she'd heard him stop working an hour ago, and the silence that followed had the specific quality of a man reading something he didn't want to be interrupted during.

She shelved. That was the whole of it, the honest sum of the evening: she took folios from the return-cart and put them where the rota said they belonged, and if her hands were steadier than most people's and her eye for misplacement sharper than any clerk in the city, well. A taster learned precision or she learned nothing. You did not survive three poisons by being approximate.

The king's supper sat in her still, in fact, catalogued in her head the way everything eventually was. Quail in vine leaves — clean, nothing but fat and thyme. The honeyed pears — faint bitterness at the root of the sweetness, almond-adjacent, and therefore arsenic, and therefore already reported, and therefore one kitchen boy currently contemplating a career change. And the wine — the wine had been interesting, a whisper of something vegetal under the black cherry that she'd spent four sips chasing before naming it: fool's parsley, prepared badly, by someone who wanted it found. An insult-poison. A message-poison. Someone at court was saying something to someone else, and the king's supper was the page they wrote it on, and Lena was the only literate person at the table.

That was her life's arithmetic. Days measured in aftertastes, nights measured in folios, and between them a guild full of people she loved in the way you love a coat that fits — gratefully, daily, without examining it too closely, because examination might reveal how much of you would show through if it were taken away.

She reached the top shelf near midnight.

The prophecy folios were bound in grey calf, forty-one of them, spanning a hundred years of recorded impossibility. Not prophecies in the popular sense — no raving seers, no star-charts, no cryptic rhymes about ravens. The guild had bought them at auction decades ago from the estate of a court archivist, and what they contained was worse than prophecy: they were *records*. Entries written as though from afterward. Dates given as accomplished fact for events that hadn't happened yet. The early ones were nonsense, mostly — floods that never came, kings who didn't die. Later entries grew strange in a different direction: accurate, specific, dated, and always written in the same hand, a hand no member of the guild had ever identified.

Lena had read perhaps a dozen entries in five years, always with the professional detachment she brought to poison ledgers. Interesting specimen. No known antidote. File accordingly.

Tonight, reshelving volume thirty-eight — the oldest, its spine cracked like riverbed clay — a loose leaf slipped free and drifted down past her shoulder, turning over twice in the candle-draft, and landed open on the cart below.

She almost left it for morning. She would think about that later, the almost, the width of a breath between leaving it and picking it up. But the leaf had fallen face-up, and her eye — trained on small print, trained to find the one wrong thing in a plate of food meant to kill a king — caught the date first, the way her eye always caught things first and told her mind second.

Tomorrow's date.

Not approximately tomorrow. Not a date that might be made to fit with generous rounding. Tomorrow. Day, month, year, all present, all exact, circled once in brown-black ink gone the color of dried blood, in a hand that was not the archivist's, was not any hand she knew from the guild's hundred years of correspondence.

And beside it, a name.

*Hartwell.*

She stood very still on the ladder for the length of four heartbeats, doing what she always did with a discrepancy: ruling out the mundane explanations in order of probability. Forgery first — this was a guild of forgers, after all; half the membership could produce a century-old document before breakfast. Prank second — Pip's grandmother was dead, but Pip himself was very much alive and constitutionally incapable of resisting a joke that required effort. Coincidence third — a name is a small thing; there were other Hartwells, surely, somewhere in the census rolls, other people who shared her surname the way strangers share a birthday.

Forgery remained the favorite. She came down the ladder with the loose leaf held by its edges, the way she handled suspect glassware, and carried it to the long table beneath the window where the light was best, and began the work of proving it fake.

This was the part she was good at. This was, if she were honest, the only part of anything she was good at: taking a thing apart to see where the lie lived.

Ink first. She tilted the leaf against the candle. Iron-gall, obviously — the brown-black bloom of it, the way it had bitten into the paper fibers and gone brittle at the edges of each stroke. Fresh forgery in old style would use tea-staining or diluted modern ink laid over a burnished surface; it sits *on* paper, it doesn't sink. This had sunk. This had been eaten into the page so thoroughly that the letters had become part of the paper's body, the way a scar becomes part of skin. Under her magnifying lens — carried everywhere, a habit her fellow tasters had mocked and then adopted — she found what forgers always missed: the micro-cracks in the ink line where the paper had flexed over decades, tiny fault-lines running through every stroke like frost across a pane. Ink cracks with age. It cannot be hurried. There were tricks, she knew — Odile could age ink with heat and vinegar in six hours well enough to fool an appraiser — but the cracks were wrong for artificial aging. Too fine. Too even. Too *old*.

Paper next. Rag-laid, watermarked faintly with a mill-stamp she recognized from the guild's own oldest holdings — a mill that had burned down sixty years ago. The deckle edge was genuine wear, not torn-to-look-worn; genuine deckle softens, forgery tears.

Conclusion: the leaf was old. A hundred years old, give or take a decade. The ink had been set before her grandmother's grandmother owned a name.

Which left the handwriting.

Here she slowed down, because here was where forgers failed even when they fooled the material tests. Hands betray their era — letterforms drift like accents, a *g* loops differently in one decade than the next, and a forger copying an old hand copies the letters but not the rhythm. Rhythm was the tell. The speed of a stroke, the hesitation points, whether the writer lifted the pen mid-word or powered through.

She laid the leaf flat and looked at the entry properly for the first time.

It was short. Most of the prophecy entries were short — the archivist's voice was terse as a ledger. But this wasn't the archivist's hand. This was a different script entirely, smaller, faster, slanting slightly rightward with a peculiar efficiency about it, letters that leaned forward like a runner leaning into wind. Beside the circled date it said:

*Collection due. The Spire takes what was promised. Name: Hartwell. See contract, vault ledger, tenth year.*

And then, beneath, in the same hand, smaller, almost an afterthought, the way you annotate your own grocery list:

*Do not let him sign anything else.*

She read it three times. On the third reading, her mind performed the small quiet betrayal it sometimes performed — the one her taster's training had built without her permission — and stopped seeing the words and started seeing the *writing*.

The crossbar of the *t*, placed low and struck fast, leftward-flicked. The *H* of Hartwell with its second stem shortened, nearly swallowed, a laziness she had been scolded for in school. The way the double-*l* finished with a single connecting stroke rather than two, because — she knew this, she had always known this, the way you know your own gait — two strokes wasted time, and time mattered, and she had been writing fast since she was nine years old and balancing her mother's market accounts while the market shouted around her.

She picked up a blank sheet. Her hand was steady — her hands were always steady, that was the point of her — and she wrote: *Hartwell.* Then she set it beside the leaf.

The match was not close. It was total. Every idiosyncrasy, every shortcut, every lazy elegance of her own hand, reproduced stroke for stroke in ink a hundred years old, on paper from a mill that had burned before her birth.

Lena Hartwell did not scream. Screaming was imprecise. What she did was stand up, walk to the shelf, take down the water jug, pour a cup, drink it, and discover she could not taste it at all — which frightened her more than anything else that night, because taste was the one sense she trusted above all others, and it had simply closed, like a shop pulling in its shutters.

She sat back down. She forced herself to do the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the floor she stood on when everything else gave way.

Fact: the leaf was genuinely old. Fact: the hand was hers beyond any reasonable doubt — she had examined enough forged hands to know the difference between imitation and identity. Fact: the date was tomorrow. Fact: the annotation referenced a contract in the vault ledger's tenth year — ten years ago, the year she had come to the guild, the year of the trial, the year she had been pulled out of a condemned cell by a smuggler-archivist with quick fingers and a smile that never explained itself.

Fear arrived then, and it arrived the way she should have known it would — not as panic, not as the cold flood the ballads described, but as an accounting error. A column that refused to balance. She ran the sums again and again and they would not close: *I am twenty-six years old. The ink is a hundred years old. I cannot have written this. Therefore someone wrote it for me, perfectly, in a hand that exists nowhere but my own muscles. Therefore either the world has committed fraud, or—*

Or the column balanced somewhere she couldn't see, in a ledger kept in a book she hadn't read yet, in a year she hadn't lived.

There was a fourth possibility, of course, and she made herself look at it straight, because looking away from a poison was how people died. The fourth possibility was that the entry was true, exactly as written, and that some version of herself — older, otherwiser, writing backward down the years the way the guild's strangest stock seemed to move — had left her a note the way you leave a note for a colleague coming on shift: *here is what happens tonight; here is where the record lives; do not let him sign anything else.*

*Him.* The pronoun sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. The guild had many men. But there was only one man whose signing things was a known hazard, a standing risk itemized in her private mental ledger alongside arsenic and bad shellfish. Only one man who had once, drunk on cheap wine and feeling expansive, said: *You know the trouble with you, Hartwell? You never write yourself into anything. Ledgers, rosters, records — you file the whole world and leave yourself off the page. Somebody ought to enter you somewhere, just so the universe knows you exist.*

She had laughed and called him a sentimentalist and changed the subject, and filed the moment away in the place where she kept the moments that were too warm to examine.

Now the universe had entered her. In ink. A hundred years in advance.

The candle guttered. Downstairs, the silence where Dominic should have been working went on being silent, and for the first time it sounded less like a man reading and more like a man waiting — the particular held-breath quality of someone who knows something is coming and has decided, in advance, to lie about it smoothly when asked.

Lena looked at the leaf a long moment. Then she did the thing her training demanded and her dread resisted: she copied it. Full transcription, exact facsimile, every crack in every stroke noted, into her private book inside its book. Original evidence stays untouched; the copy travels. If the leaf vanished tonight, the record would survive.

Then she folded the original back into volume thirty-eight, shelved it precisely where the rota said it belonged, and stood at the bottom of the ladder with her hand on the rail, listening to the guildhall breathe.

One question remained, and it was the question that would not balance, and she was already descending the stairs toward the vault before she fully admitted she was asking it:

If the prophecy named her —

what, exactly, had Dominic signed?
