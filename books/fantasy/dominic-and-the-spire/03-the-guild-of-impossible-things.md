## 3. The Guild of Impossible Things

Dominic Vane had a theory about doors.

A door, he liked to say — usually to apprentices who hadn't asked, and once to a magistrate's clerk who had very much not asked — was just a wall that had learned to negotiate. Every door in the Guild of Impossible Things proved the point. The front entrance was a butcher's shop on Candle Lane that sold excellent mutton and no impossibilities whatsoever; you walked past the hanging carcasses, through a cold room that smelled of sawdust and iron, and pressed your palm against the third tile from the left, which was warm for reasons no one had ever satisfactorily explained. Then the wall negotiated. It always did. Walls, in Dominic's experience, were far more reasonable than people.

He came through it that evening with rain in his collar and a lie already warming up in his chest like a swallowed coal.

The guildhall opened around him the way it always did: lamplight the color of weak honey, shelves climbing into shadow until the eye gave up counting, and the particular hush of a building full of things that were listening. Not alive, exactly. Attentive. A jar of bottled last words murmured to itself on the second shelf the way an old man mutters at a window. Somewhere above, the borrowed luck shifted in its crates with a sound like dice rolling over in their sleep. And beneath everything, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the vault hummed — the deep ledger-room where the guild kept its heaviest inventory.

"Vane." Pell's voice came from behind the intake desk, where the guild's oldest member sat sorting buttons by the weight of the grief attached to them. Pell was ninety if she was a day, and she claimed to remember the founding, though she also claimed to have invented tea, so her testimony was best taken with salt. "You're dripping on the manifest."

"The manifest is waterproof."

"The manifest is *sentimental*," Pell said, "and you know it." She held up a button between finger and thumb. "Widow brought this in. Her husband's coat. Says when she holds it she can hear him humming. Wants to trade it for one good night's sleep."

"What did you offer?"

"A year of borrowed luck and a bottle of honest dreams."

"And?"

"She took it. They always take it." Pell set the button down among its fellows, and Dominic heard — or thought he heard, or chose to hear, which in this building amounted to the same thing — a faint thread of humming settle into the pile like a coin dropping into water. "That's the whole business, isn't it. We don't sell magic. We sell *terms*."

Dominic hung his coat on a hook that had once belonged to a hangman and had opinions about what deserved hanging. "Terms are all anyone ever sells," he said. "Kings sell terms. Priests sell terms. We're just honest about the interest."

He said it lightly. He said most things lightly; it was a professional skill, like lockpicking or handwriting analysis, and he'd practiced it longer than either. But the words snagged on something on the way out, and he felt the snag all the way down, because tonight of all nights the guild's economy felt less like a joke he'd perfected and more like a bill coming due.

Tonight. Or tomorrow, depending on how you counted, and the prophecy book counted by the old calendar, which turned at dusk, which meant—

Which meant nothing. He made himself think it twice. Which meant *nothing*, because Lena hadn't found the entry yet, and if she never found the entry, then the date was just a date, and dates were only dangerous to men who owed things.

He owed things.

* * *

The reading room occupied the guildhall's eastern spine, a long gallery of desks lit by lamps that burned without oil — a minor impossibility the founder had purchased at auction and never explained, on the principle that explained wonders depreciate. Dominic passed through it toward the folio stacks, and that was where he found her.

Lena Hartwell stood on the fourth step of the ladder with a prophecy folio open across her forearm, and the lamplight had gotten into her hair the way lamplight does when it has decided to be unfair. She was frowning at the page in the particular way she frowned at unbalanced columns: not angry, not afraid, just *confronting*. He had watched her confront poisons the same way. Most people met danger with their whole body. Lena met it with her eyes first, catalogued it, filed it, and only then allowed herself the luxury of a racing heart — and even then, quietly, somewhere private, the way other people wept.

She hadn't seen him yet. That was rare enough to be worth savoring. Ten years of working three desks apart, and he could still count on one hand the times he'd watched her without being watched back.

He thought about the folio in his coat pocket. He thought about the signature on it, in ink gone brown as old blood, and about the clause underneath the signature, and about the fact that he had read that clause every night for ten years the way other men read letters from home.

Then Lena looked up, and Dominic Vane did what he did best.

"You're shelving the prophecies out of order," he said. "That's a Corvane-cycle folio. It goes after the Salt Kings material, not before. Honestly, Hartwell, I expect better chaos from you."

"I'm *cross-referencing*," she said, "which is more than you've done with the Ashfall ledgers since midsummer. And don't loom. You loom like a debt collector."

"Looming is half my job description. The other half is tea." He set both hands on the ladder's rail and looked up at her, and let his face do the thing it did — the easy crooked thing, the thing that had talked customs officers out of searching his cargo and magistrates out of asking follow-up questions. "Speaking of which. You've been up there two hours. Your hands are going to be cold enough to crack the folio spines, and then Odile will resurrect herself from whatever disgrace she's currently enjoying purely to yell at us both."

Lena came down the ladder instead of arguing, which meant she was tired, which meant the finding had shaken her more than her face admitted. She landed beside him close enough that he caught the smell of her — lamp oil, paper dust, and underneath it the faint bitter green of the taster's tonics she chewed daily to keep her tongue sharp. Ten years and his pulse still did something unprofessional about it.

"You're in a strange mood," she said.

"I'm always in a strange mood. You've simply never learned to read me."

"I read everyone. It's the job." She tucked the folio under her arm. "You came in wet, went straight past your desk without sitting, checked the vault door twice — I heard the hinges — and now you're performing cheerfulness at volume. Either you've lost money, or you've found something."

There it was. There was the reason he'd spent a decade keeping secrets from the most dangerous mind in the city: not because she was cruel with them, but because she was *kind* with them, and kindness would go looking for the wound, and the wound was not hers to carry.

He had planned the lie on the walk over. He'd drafted three versions, tested each against his memory of her, discarded two. The surviving version was simple, specific, and boring — the holy trinity of a good forgery.

"Money," he said. "I found money. Well — the shape of money. There's a collector in Harrowgate who wants the bottled last words from the Ferryman lot, all forty-one jars, and he's offering enough to keep the guild fed for two winters. But he wants provenance papers, and the original seller's paperwork is—" he waved a hand, "—creative. So I spent the evening drafting creative-adjacent documentation, and then I remembered I hate lying to dead men's widows, and here I am, avoiding my desk, looming at you."

Lena studied him. He let her. The trick — the whole trick, the only trick — was that a lie survives inspection best when you stand still for it. Liars flinch; forgers hold the line.

"That's not it," she said finally.

"It's entirely it."

"You checked the vault door *twice*."

"I check the vault door twice every time it rains. The hinges swell. I have a relationship with those hinges."

Something moved behind her eyes — the accounting look, the one that tallied a man's tells in columns and carried the remainder forward. He watched her decide, deliberately, to trust the sum. It was the closest thing to a gift she knew how to give, and receiving it felt like swallowing glass wrapped in velvet.

"All right," she said. "But if the Harrowgate collector is fictional, Dominic, I want it noted that I called it."

"Noted," he said gravely. "In the record. Witnessed by the lamps."

And because the cruelest thing he could do was linger, he didn't linger. He touched her elbow — one second, no more, a touch calibrated to pass as collegial — and walked away down the gallery with his stride easy and his heart doing arithmetic it hated.

* * *

His desk sat in the alcove behind the map cabinet, a nest of paper and contraband that the guild had long ago stopped trying to reform. Dominic sat, did not light the lamp — darkness was a colleague — and took the folio from his coat.

It was small, as prophecy books went. Calfskin gone soft as glove leather, corners worn round by a century of anxious thumbs. The court beneath the mountain had produced exactly nine copies of the *Ledger of Dates* before the binding trade collapsed — the court did everything in nines, a superstition Dominic had never bothered to understand — and eight of the nine had been accounted for. This was the ninth. He had bought it eleven years ago from a drowning man, honestly, at fair price, and had told himself for a decade that owning a thing was different from reading a thing.

He opened it to the page he knew better than his own hand.

The old ink was brown and certain. A date, circled twice: tomorrow, by the old reckoning. Beside it, in the same century-dead hand, a single word that had stopped being a word to him long ago and become a sound instead, like a bell heard through a wall:

*Hartwell.*

Below the date, below the name, the entry continued in the flat administrative voice the court used for everything, funerals and festivals alike:

*The Spire claims its due. One soul, freely contracted. Collection at dusk.*

Freely contracted. He almost laughed. Four syllables of legal silk laid over the ugliest night of his life. He had been twenty-three and terrified and standing in a corridor of polished black stone while a magistrate with kind eyes explained, patiently, the way you'd explain arithmetic to a child, exactly what a soul cost and exactly what it bought. He had signed knowing. That was the part no one understood, the part that let him sleep: he had *known*. He'd watched his mother forget his face a year earlier — a bad trade, a desperate one, her own signature on her own contract — and he had learned precisely what memory weighed when it left a person. He had signed anyway. Fair price. Final sale.

The girl they were going to hang had been nineteen years old and guilty of nothing but a king's indigestion. The pardon had cost one soul, payable on demand, date of the court's choosing. The court had chosen generously. Ten years. A decade in which Lena Hartwell had lived, argued, shelved books out of order, and never once written herself into any record — and he had loved her in the specific, disciplined way of a man guarding a debt.

And now the date had come around like a hinge swelling in the rain.

Dominic closed the folio. His hands were steady. He noticed they were steady and was briefly, absurdly proud of them.

There was a solution. There was always a solution; that was the guild's entire theology. Contracts could be voided — the court itself taught that, taught it in its velvet corridors, because precedent was the only god the magistrate worshipped. Void a contract and you paid something of equal weight. Equal weight. He turned the phrase over and felt its edges. What equaled a soul? Another soul, presumably, but he would sooner let the Spire take him twice than put Lena's name on any line. Years? He had perhaps forty left and would sign away thirty-nine without blinking, except the court didn't deal in years; the court dealt in *weight*, and years were light as ash compared to what they wanted.

Memories, maybe. Memories had weight. He knew it firsthand.

But that road ended the same place his mother's had: in a chair by a window, looking at a familiar face with perfect politeness and no recognition. He had buried her with that face still turned to him. He would not survive watching it happen again, in reverse, wearing Lena's eyes.

No. There was one clean move left, and it was the oldest move in his repertoire: misdirection. If Lena never found the entry, the date stayed a date. If the folio vanished — reshelved, misplaced, mis-catalogued into the Salt Kings section where no sane person would look — then the prophecy slept another decade, and he'd spend the decade finding a better answer, and maybe there wasn't one, but maybe was the currency the whole guild ran on.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow he would lift the folio from wherever she'd shelved it, and forge a replacement page — he could fake that dead hand in his sleep, had practiced it, God help him, *had practiced it* — and the record would show nothing, and the nothing would keep her safe.

It was a good plan. It was a plan built from the same materials as every plan he'd ever made: competence, secrecy, and the quiet arrogance of a man who believed he could carry anything alone.

He slid the folio into the false bottom of his desk drawer, next to the forged passports and the vial of one honest lie — the guild's rarest stock, a liquid truth so potent it could be spent only once per lifetime, and his was still full, and some nights that felt less like thrift than like cowardice.

From the main hall came the sounds of the guild settling: Pell banking the lamps, the luck shifting in its crates, the last words murmuring themselves toward sleep. And under it, fainter, the vault humming its low patient note, the way it always hummed around heavy inventory.

Around *him*, he corrected himself. Around him. He was heavy inventory now. He had been for ten years, and the vault had known it before he did.

Dominic rested his hand flat on the drawer that held the folio, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you intended, eventually, to betray.

"One more day," he told it quietly. "One more day, and then I'll fix it. I always fix it."

The drawer said nothing. Drawers, unlike walls, had never learned to negotiate.

Above him, on the ladder, in the lamplight, Lena Hartwell was cross-referencing a shelf he had rearranged that morning — and Dominic Vane, who could forge any hand in the kingdom, who had lied to kings and courts and corpses, sat in the dark and discovered that the hardest document he would ever falsify was the one written on his own face.

He just hoped — with the specific, disciplined hope of a man guarding a debt — that she wouldn't find the entry before he could make it disappear.

He should have known better. Hope, in this building, was inventory too.

And inventory always found its buyer.
