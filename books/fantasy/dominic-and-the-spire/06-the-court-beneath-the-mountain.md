## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Wren had been below long enough to forget the color green.

Not the word — the word survived, filed away with *amber* and *dawn* and other luxuries of the upper air — but the thing itself, the living fact of it, had gone out of them like a candle in a draft. Down here everything was salt and stone and lamplight the color of old honey, and the only growing things were the corridors themselves, which the court insisted on calling gardens.

They were not gardens. They were veins. The Spire did not rise above the city; that was the joke everyone above ground believed. It grew downward, a tower inverted, root-first into the mountain's heart, and every corridor was a ring of its trunk, and every door was a knot where something had been swallowed and kept.

Wren walked those knots every day, and every day they counted the doors, because counting was how you stayed sane below, and because the count was worth money to people who had never seen this place and never would.

Four hundred and eleven doors between the salt gate and the magistrate's chamber. Wren knew because they had counted them four hundred and eleven times, which was either devotion or madness, and after six years as the court's debtor-spy, Wren had stopped pretending there was a difference.

Tonight the doors felt closer together than usual. Tonight the whole mountain seemed to be leaning in.

* * *

The summons had come at the change of shift, delivered not by an usher — ushers handled the living public, such as it was — but by a clerk of the second rank, a gray person made entirely of gray habits, who pressed a gray envelope into Wren's hands and said nothing at all. The envelope was warm. Everything down here was warm; the mountain's blood ran close beneath the stone, and the court had learned to bank its fires in the walls the way misers banked coin.

The summons said one line, in handwriting so beautiful it looked machine-made:

*The magistrate will hear the Hartwell matter at the ninth bell. Attendance is not optional. Attendance is never optional.*

Hartwell. Wren stood very still in their narrow cell — cells below were called *accommodations*, another of the court's jokes, though no one laughed — and let the name settle over them like cold water.

Everyone below knew the ledger of the owed. It was not secret; secrecy was an above-ground vice. The court kept its accounts open precisely because open accounts were honest ones, and honesty, properly weaponized, was more binding than any chain. Somewhere in the registry heart was a page bearing the name *Dominic Vael*, sold ten years past for the price of one girl's pardon, due for collection on a date circled in a prophecy book a century old.

And somewhere above, in a city that smelled of rain and frying onions and horse, that girl — Hartwell, Lena Hartwell, taster to the king, keeper of ledgers she didn't know were prophecies — was presumably going about her evening, tasting soup, balancing columns, unaware that the date on the page had teeth.

Or not unaware. That was the question, wasn't it. That was why the ninth bell.

Wren buttoned their coat, checked the three hidden things they always checked — the stub of pencil, the folded map drawn from memory onto rice paper thin enough to swallow, the small brass token stamped with a debt number that was not their own — and went up the corridor toward the chamber, counting doors against their will, because the habit counted them whether Wren consented or not.

* * *

The magistrate's chamber was the only room below with a ceiling high enough to feel like sky, and the court had built it that way on purpose, because the magistrate understood longing the way engineers understood load-bearing walls. You put the desperate under a vault of black stone shot through with veins of glowing salt, and you let them look up and think of stars, and you watched them understand, all at once, exactly what they had traded away.

The magistrate sat at the far end beneath the false stars, behind a desk of black wood that had been grown, not cut — you could see the grain curling like fern fronds, still faintly alive, still faintly reaching. Papers moved across it of their own accord, patient as beetles. Two clerks flanked the desk like bookends. An usher stood by the door with the particular stillness of someone paid to pretend not to listen.

"Sit," said the magistrate, without looking up.

Wren sat. The chair was comfortable, which was worse than uncomfortable. Comfort below was a courtesy extended to people whose cooperation mattered, and Wren had spent six years learning to distrust comfort the way sailors distrusted calm.

The magistrate finished a line, signed it with a flourish that took slightly too long, and then — only then — lifted their head.

Wren had never gotten used to the magistrate's face, and suspected no one did. It was a kind face. That was the horror of it. Round-cheeked, silver-haired, with laugh lines worn deep and genuine, the face of somebody's favorite aunt or a village schoolmaster retired to tend bees. The court had not chosen this face. The court had simply discovered, centuries ago, that cruelty worked better wearing kindness, and had bred itself accordingly, the way orchardists bred sweeter fruit.

"You've been counting my doors again," the magistrate said warmly.

"I count everything," Wren said. "It's cheaper than thinking."

"It is indeed." The magistrate slid a folder across the black desk. "You know why you're here."

"The Hartwell matter."

"The Hartwell matter." The magistrate opened the folder with two fingers, delicately, the way one opened a pastry box. Inside lay pages Wren recognized on sight, because everyone below recognized them: facsimiles of the prophecy folio, the century-old ink, the circled date. And beside the date, a name. *Vael.*

"Do you know what I love about prophecy?" the magistrate asked, in the tone of a man beginning a favorite anecdote at dinner. "Everyone above ground thinks it's about fate. Fate! As if the universe were a playwright with opinions. But a contract written a hundred years in advance, witnessed and sealed and filed — that isn't fate, Wren. That's *bookkeeping*. The universe doesn't care whether Dominic Vael comes below on the appointed night. The *ledger* cares. And ledgers," the magistrate tapped the folder, "are the closest thing this sad world has to morality."

"You summoned me to discuss morality."

"I summoned you because you have eyes above and I have need of them." The magistrate rose, and the clerks rose with the magistrate, in unison, like furniture with opinions. "Walk with me. I find I think better in the galleries, and tonight I want to think beautifully."

* * *

They walked. The galleries of the Spire's lower rings were carved from living salt, and the lamplight turned the walls to amber, and if you squinted — Wren refused to squint — it might almost have been beautiful, in the way a jewel is beautiful inside a toad.

"The trouble with souls," the magistrate said conversationally, hands clasped behind their back, footsteps soft on the salt floor, "is that people believe they're priceless. Priceless! Do you know what a soul costs, Wren? On average? Across our holdings?"

"No."

"Neither do I, precisely, because the prices vary so absurdly. A pardon here. A harvest there. One man sold his for a bottle of wine and regretted it before he'd finished the bottle. Another held out for his daughter's life and got fair value and never regretted a day." The magistrate gestured at the walls, at the doors, at the four hundred and eleven knots of swallowed things. "Priceless things cannot be traded. Only priced things can. And so every soul we hold is a soul someone, somewhere, decided had a number on it. We don't make the numbers, Wren. We honor them."

"You're collecting a man in three days," Wren said. "That sounds like making a number."

"We're collecting a man whose number came due. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire foundation of civilization." The magistrate paused before a door — door two hundred and six, Wren noted, hating themselves for noting it — and rested one kind hand on the salt frame. "Do you know what happens if we fail to collect? If contracts become suggestions? If a debt can be waved away because someone upstairs found it *sad*?"

"Everything unravels," Wren said, because it was the answer the court wanted, and also, annoyingly, true.

"Everything unravels," the magistrate agreed happily. "Every pardon bought with a soul becomes void — do you imagine the girl pardoned ten years ago would enjoy being re-condemned? Every price ever agreed loses meaning. The guild above — that charming little den of impossible things — trades in certainty. Borrowed luck only works because luck can be owed. Take away enforcement and you don't get freedom. You get noise." The magistrate smiled, and the smile reached all the way to the laugh lines, and Wren felt ill. "I am not a cruel creature, Wren. I am a *structural* one. Cruelty is what people call structure when it inconveniences them."

"And the girl? Hartwell?"

"Ah." The magistrate resumed walking, and Wren fell into step, and the amber light slid over them both like syrup. "The girl is the interesting variable. She shouldn't know. By every reasonable projection, she doesn't know — the folio is misfiled, the relevant page buried under forty years of dust, and the guild's archivist has been very carefully not telling her for a decade. Very careful work, that. Almost admirable." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "And yet."

"And yet?"

"And yet yesterday, a clerk reviewing the salt-gate manifests recorded an anomaly. Nothing dramatic. A request for historical shipping routes through the under-mountain. Old ones. Sealed ones." The magistrate's voice remained warm, unhurried, terrible. "Requested by a calligrapher. Disgraced. Formerly of the court. Odile Vasse."

Wren kept their face still. Six years of practice, and it held — barely. Odile. So the pieces were already moving above, faster than anyone had projected, faster than *Wren* had reported, because Wren had reported nothing, because Wren had spent six years below perfecting the art of reporting just enough to stay trusted and just little enough to stay clean, and now the bill for that artistry was coming due with interest, the way everything below came due.

"A calligrapher looking at old maps," Wren said. "Forgers study old documents. It's practically vocational."

"Mm." The magistrate stopped walking. Above them, the false stars pulsed gently, banking their glow for the night cycle, and in the dimming light the magistrate's kind face became something older and less assuring. "Here is what happens next. In three days, the collection proceeds. Vael comes below — willingly, unwillingly, carried, it makes no structural difference. The contract holds. The precedent holds. The world continues to have a floor beneath it."

"And if the girl interferes?"

"Then the girl learns what every debtor eventually learns: that interference is also a transaction. It has a price. It gets entered." The magistrate turned, and the warmth in their face did not diminish, which was the worst part; the warmth was total, bottomless, a well you could fall into. "But I would prefer no interference. Interference is untidy. So you are going above, Wren. Tomorrow. You'll resume your old circuits — the debtors' taverns, the guild quarter, the usual listening. And you will tell me whether the taster knows. Whether the forger is forging. Whether anyone above is foolish enough to stand between a soul and its number."

"And if they are?"

"Then you will report it," the magistrate said, "and we will handle it politely, and no one will need to be cruel." A beat. The lamplight hummed. "Unless, of course, you'd rather not go. Debts can be renegotiated, Wren. Yours has been generous these six years. Six years of service against a debt of—" the magistrate consulted the air, consulting some inner ledger with perfect recall, "—considerable size. One might say you've nearly earned your freedom. One might say a few more years of good conduct would see you walking out of here a free creature, debt discharged, name returned."

The trap was beautiful. Wren had to admire it the way one admires a well-made lock even while it closes around you. Go above, spy, betray — and buy freedom with the betrayal. Refuse — and remain below forever, watching the collection happen anyway, having accomplished nothing except the loss of whatever slim access to the surface might someday, somehow, be turned to use.

"There's no version," Wren said slowly, "where I refuse and it matters."

"There never is," the magistrate agreed cheerfully. "That's what refusal is. Now — questions? Concerns? A last cup of tea before the climb? We have excellent tea. It's in the budget."

* * *

Wren walked back through the four hundred and eleven doors alone, and this time counted them aloud, under their breath, because the counting was the only prayer they had left.

Door two hundred and six again. Door one hundred. Door nine, where the air began to taste of salt and iron and the deep mineral patience of the mountain's heart. Behind each door, something owed. Behind most of them, something that had stopped hoping so long ago that hope would have been an intrusion, a rude knock at a settled house.

At door one, the salt gate, Wren stopped and pressed a palm flat against the cold brass hinge.

Three days. In three days the court would come up the stairs like a tide, polite and punctual and utterly without malice, and take a smuggler-archivist who had once, in a fit of magnificent stupidity, sold the only thing he owned to save a girl who hadn't asked him to. And the court would call it justice, and by the court's arithmetic it would even *be* justice, and the world would keep its floor.

Unless the floor could be argued with. Unless somewhere above, a woman with a taster's tongue and a ledger-keeper's mind was already finding the error in the column — because there was always an error, Wren thought, there was always an error, that was the one thing six years below had taught them that the court would never admit:

Perfect books were a myth. Someone, sometime, always wrote in the margins.

Wren pulled their coat tight, shouldered their pack, and started the long climb toward the smell of rain.

Behind them, far below, in a chamber lit by false stars, the magistrate opened the prophecy folio to its marked page and read the coming date aloud — softly, savoringly, the way one reads the first item on a menu when one intends to order everything.
