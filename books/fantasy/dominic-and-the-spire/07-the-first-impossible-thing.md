## 7. The First Impossible Thing

The guildhall at dawn smelled of cold tea and candle smoke, the way it always did when the night shift of impossibilities had run long. Lena came down the back stairs before anyone else was awake, as she did every morning, and stood in the doorway of the ledger room for a moment longer than she needed to, letting the silence settle around her like a coat she hadn't asked to wear.

The prophecy folio still sat on the corner of her desk where she had left it, face-down, as if a book could be shamed into forgetting what it said. She had not slept. She had tried, in the manner of people who have discovered their own handwriting in a hundred-year-old book — which is to say, she had lain in the dark cataloguing her own breathing and finding errors in it.

*Hartwell. Due this night.*

No. Not *this* night anymore. Tonight. One day closer than it had been yesterday, and tomorrow it would be one day closer still, and the arithmetic of it was the worst kind — the kind that never balanced, only diminished.

She turned the folio over. The circled date looked up at her in her own hand, patient as a debt.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone she used for ledgers that lied to her. "Let's find out what you cost."

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its knowledge the way it kept everything: badly, in theory, and perfectly, in fact. The charter shelf in the reading room held forty years of precedent — disputes adjudicated, trades unwound, one famous case in which a man had tried to sell his shadow twice and been made to stand in both buyers' doorways on alternating nights until the shame resolved the matter. Lena had read all of it twice in her first year. She had never once imagined she would be reading it for herself.

She started with the index. *Soul — see Contracts, Irrevocable. Contracts, Irrevocable — see Equal Weight. Equal Weight — see* and here the index simply said, *you already know.*

"That's not an index entry," she told it. "That's editorializing."

But it was correct, which was the guild's whole character in a single line of ink. She already knew. Everyone who had ever worked the ledgers knew. The guild's economy ran on one law the way a river runs on gravity: *nothing is taken, only traded, and nothing trades except for its own weight.* Bottled last words weighed what they weighed. Borrowed luck came back with interest or not at all. The one honest lie in the vault — a small glass vial of it, humming faintly on the second shelf — had cost its seller exactly the weight of every other lie he'd ever told, which was why he'd wept at the desk and thanked her after.

So: a soul-contract could be voided. The precedents said so, in the dry voice of clerks long dead. Case after case, the same finding, the same phrase repeated down the decades like a bell rung at intervals: *the instrument may be voided upon payment of equal weight.*

She read until her eyes ached and the candles burned to stubs, and the answer never changed. It didn't matter what she wanted to pay, or what the court beneath the mountain wanted to take. The scales decided. The scales had been deciding since before the city had a name.

Equal weight for a soul.

Lena sat back in her chair and did what she did best, the thing that had kept her alive through nine years of tasting a king's supper: she made a list.

* * *

By the time the guild began to stir — Wren first, always, slipping in through the coal chute with the dawn like a rumor returning from a bad neighborhood — she had filled three pages.

The first candidate was the obvious one. *My taster's immunity.*

It was, on paper, the most valuable thing she owned. Nine years ago the crown's physicians had fed her a graduated course of every poison in the royal pharmacopoeia, in doses small enough to teach and large enough to scar, until her body had learned the alphabet of death the way other children learned their letters. It was not magic. It was worse than magic; it was *paid for*, drop by drop, in a childhood of vomiting and fever and one long week when they'd all assumed she'd die and she had lain there doing sums to stay awake. The immunity had a market value. The guild's own assessors had priced it once, as an exercise — enough, they'd said, to buy back a condemned name, or a burned bridge, or a small country's worth of grudges.

But a soul?

She held her hand over the candle flame, an old habit, watching the heat approach her skin like a polite question. The immunity would not help her here. The court beneath the mountain did not deal in bodies. It dealt in what the body *carried*, and the immunity was a property of flesh, and flesh was the cheapest currency in the under-mountain's books. She had read the court's tariff schedules — Wren had copied them out for her two nights ago, in a hand that shook only slightly — and the tariff schedules were explicit. *Physical endowments: assessed at material value only.*

Her body had survived nine years of poison so that she could live long enough to lose the thing the poisons had never touched.

She crossed it out. The line through *immunity* was straight and clean, because she was a professional.

* * *

The second candidate took longer, and it hurt in a stranger place.

*My guild membership.*

It looked small written down — a name on a charter, a key to the coal chute, a desk with a good lamp — but Lena knew better than anyone what it actually weighed. Guild membership was the only reason a condemned taster's scapegoat had survived her pardon. It was the roof over the vault, the law that said the court's writ ran thin above ground, the network of debtors and forgers and exiles who would, without being asked, hide a stranger in a cellar because the ledger said the stranger was *one of ours*. She had spent nine years believing she had earned her place here. She had learned, four days ago, that Dominic had bought it — no. That wasn't what the ledger said, and she was a ledger-keeper, and she would not falsify even her own grief. What the ledger said was that Dominic had bought her *life*, and the guild had given her the *place*, and she had filled the place so thoroughly for nine years that the difference had stopped mattering.

Could she trade it? The precedents said membership could be surrendered — there was a form for it, form eleven, in triplicate, and the guild loved its triplicate the way the under-court loved its own. But membership wasn't hers alone to weigh. It was a shared thing, a *held* thing, like a rope in a tug-of-war; letting go of her end didn't remove the rope. The scales, she suspected, would look at her half of it and find it light. A soul was the whole weight of a person, every hour they'd ever lived and ever would. Half a rope against that was a pebble on the wrong pan.

And there was a colder arithmetic underneath, the kind she did not let herself write down: if she surrendered her membership, she lost the guildhall, the coal chute, the charter shelf, the debtors' table — every tool she would need to actually *reach* the court and make the payment. Trading the membership to void the contract was like burning the bridge to pay the toll for crossing it.

She crossed it out more slowly. The line wavered.

* * *

The guild woke around her while she worked. Wren passed through the reading room without a word, but Lena saw their eyes flick to the folio on her desk, and to her, and away — the quick, guilty look of a person carrying a secret they'd been ordered to keep and were bad at keeping. Wren had been like that for four days. Everyone had. The whole guild moved around her now with the exaggerated casualness of people who have decided, by unspoken vote, that she is not to be told the thing she already knows.

She let them have it. She was not ready for the conversation the knowing would force, and neither, clearly, were they.

Odile Vasse had not yet arrived; the forger kept court hours even in exile, rising at noon and complaining about it until dusk. But her workspace stood open at the far end of the reading room, and Lena found her gaze drifting to it the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Odile could forge anything. That was the rumor, and like most rumors in this building it was an understatement. If a contract's provenance could be falsified — if the court's claim on Dominic could be made to look *mistaken*, misfiled, a clerical error a century deep — then perhaps the scales would never need to weigh anything at all.

It was a thought with a hook in it. She wrote *forgery?* in the margin of her list and underlined it twice, and told herself she was only being thorough.

* * *

Midmorning brought the question to the guild itself, because she had promised herself she would ask it aloud — that she would not do what she had done at fifteen, at the trial, which was to sit quietly and be sentenced by other people's arithmetic.

She called it a general inquiry, which in guild terms meant anyone could answer and no one had to. She stood at the debtors' table with her list in front of her, and the morning's collection of strays gathered the way they always did: Wren by the door with their back to the wall; Marta of the bottled last words, who was eighty and deaf in one ear and missed nothing; a pair of luck-brokers who owed the guild more than they liked to discuss; young Pell, who swept the floors and listened to everything.

"If a soul-contract exists," Lena said, "and it's valid, and it's come due — how is it voided?"

The silence had a texture. She had heard silences like it before, in the throne room, in the moments between the tasting and the king's first bite, when every courtier in the room was calculating whether the taster would live through the next sixty seconds.

"Equal weight," said Marta, gently, because Marta was the only one who wouldn't dress it up. "You know that, girl. You've weighed half the trades in this building."

"Equal weight," Lena agreed. "So the question becomes: what weighs as much as a soul?"

"Nothing," said one of the luck-brokers, and then, catching her expression, added, "Nothing that's *cheap*, is what I mean."

"Nothing that's *yours alone*," said Wren, from the door. It was the first thing they'd said to her in two days, and it had the sound of a warning being delivered by someone who hated delivering it. "That's the trap in it. People hear *equal weight* and start pricing their possessions. But the scales don't weigh things, Hartwell. They weigh what things *cost you*. A crown weighs what it cost the head that wore it. A secret weighs what it cost to keep. You want to void a soul-contract, you don't bring the scales an object." Their eyes went, briefly, to the folio on her desk. "You bring them a piece of yourself, and it had better be a load-bearing piece."

The room did its quiet math. Lena watched them do it — watched them all arrive, one by one, at the same sum, and look away from it.

"Thank you," she said, and folded her list, and went back to her desk, and unfolded it again the moment the room had emptied.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon. The list grew, and shrank, and grew again, the way honest lists do.

*My name.* No — a name was a legal instrument, and hers was already entangled; the court had held it once, in a sense, when it held her sentence. Trading a name the court had already tasted was like paying a debt with a coin the creditor had bitten. Crossed out.

*My hands.* The taster's precision, the ledger-keeper's neatness — but these were flesh again, and flesh was cheap below the mountain. Crossed out, with a small frustrated blot.

*My guild membership.* Already crossed out, but she found herself returning to it, because Wren's words had changed its weight. *A load-bearing piece.* Was membership load-bearing? It was the frame the whole house stood in. But it was shared weight, and shared weight divided, and a soul did not divide.

*My memories.*

She wrote it the way you write a word you've been circling for days without admitting it. And then she sat very still, because the moment it was on the page it stopped being one candidate among five and became the only entry the other entries had been politely pretending not to be.

She made herself do the arithmetic properly. That was her discipline; that was the whole of who she was. She took the thought apart and weighed it like anything else that crossed her desk.

What did her memories of the last ten years weigh?

They weighed the guildhall in every season. They weighed the first time she'd balanced a ledger of traded impossibilities and felt the strange, clean joy of it — a whole economy built on the principle that nothing precious is ever free, and nothing free is ever precious, and yet the people in this building kept giving each other things anyway, small things, impossible things, a cup of tea left warm on a night-shift desk. They weighed nine years of a man leaving the lamp lit for her when she came home late, and never once saying why, and her never once asking, because the not-saying was its own kind of saying and they had both agreed, wordlessly, across a decade, to let it stand.

They weighed him. That was the sum of it. The memories weighed *Dominic* — his voice doing the thing it did when he was about to lie to her badly, his hands on the folios, his laugh, low and unwilling, the one sound in the world she had never been able to catalogue because cataloguing it would have meant admitting she listened for it.

And what did the scales care what the memories *contained*? The scales weighed cost. And the cost of ten years was the whole of the person she had become in them. Not the flesh — the flesh was cheap. Not the name — the name was entangled. The *self*. The self was made of remembered things, and if you took the last decade out of her, what remained would be a woman who could taste poison and keep books and did not know why she flinched, some evenings, at the sound of low laughter in an empty room.

Equal weight. The scales would take it. She knew it the way she knew a poison by its aftertaste — not by proof but by the body's terrible certainty.

She looked at the word on the page. Her handwriting looked back at her, neat and upright and exact, the handwriting the court's own clerks would one day match against a prophecy book, the handwriting that had already written *Hartwell* beside a circled date in ink a century old.

She picked up her pen. She drew a circle around *memories* — a slow, deliberate, unbroken circle, the kind of mark a clerk makes when an entry is final — and she hated her own handwriting with a completeness she had never managed to feel toward any poison, any king, any court. It was such a *tidy* circle. Even now, even doing this, her hand refused to tremble. She wanted it to tremble. She wanted the mark to look like what it was, which was a woman pricing her own heart for a market that had never once, in a hundred years of precedent, given anything back.

The circle came out perfect. Of course it did.

She set down the pen, and pressed her palms flat against the desk, and made herself look at the folio beside her — the circled date, the patient debt, the name in her hand. Tomorrow. It was due tomorrow.

Which meant she had one night, and one circle on a page, and no plan at all — unless the forger's empty chair at the end of the room counted as a plan, and the rumor attached to its owner, and the price that owner would certainly demand.

Lena blew out the lamp, and in the dark she could still see the circle, faint and shining, like a ring the scales had already closed.

She went to find her coat. Odile Vasse kept court hours, which meant she was awake now, and drunk, and — if the rumors held — the only person in the city who could make a lie look older than the truth.

It was time to go shopping for a forgery.
