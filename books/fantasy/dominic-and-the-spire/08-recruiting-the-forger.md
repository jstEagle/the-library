## 8. Recruiting the Forger

The address was wrong on purpose.

Lena had learned long ago that in this city, a correct address was an invitation and a wrong one was a test. The scrap of paper Wren had pressed into her hand at the debtors' table read *Vasse — 14 Rue Cendre* in a clerk's careful hand, but when she stood before number fourteen she found a shuttered dye-house with a rusted chain across its door and no name on any lintel. She stood in the gray morning light doing what she did best: cataloguing.

Chain: old, but oiled recently. Shutters: closed, yet the dust along their edges had been disturbed by hands that knew to close them quietly. The smell drifting from under the door: ink. Not the cheap tannin stink of scribes' shops but true gall-ink, iron and oak-apple, the kind that outlived the men who wrote it.

Someone lived here who wanted very much not to be found, and had left exactly enough traces for someone worth finding.

She knocked four times, paused, then twice more — the rhythm of a food-taster announcing herself to a kitchen, a courtesy older than the court itself. Nothing. She waited. Somewhere inside, a chair scraped against stone.

"I'm not buying," said a voice through the wood, low and dry as paper left too near a fire. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling," Lena said. "I'm trading."

A long silence, the kind that weighed her through the door. Then the chain slid loose with a sound like a small bone breaking.

* * *

Odile Vasse was smaller than her reputation and older than her wanted anyone to know. Her hair was silver at the temples in a way that looked deliberate, like everything else about her, and her hands — Lena noticed hands first, always; it was a professional deformity — were stained to the second knuckle with forty years of pigment. The room behind her was a calligrapher's tomb: reams of vellum stacked like grave-goods, jars of ground lapis worth more than the building, and on every surface, examples of handwriting that belonged to other people.

That was the thing about Odile Vasse, Lena thought. Most forgers copied hands. Odile *became* them. On the desk lay a royal decree so convincing that Lena's stomach tightened before her mind caught up — she'd seen that seal, that flourish, that particular cruelty in the spacing of the king's titles, every day of her working life. It was perfect. It was also, presumably, worthless, because it had come from this room and not the chancery.

"You're the Hartwell girl," Odile said. "The taster." She said it the way one identifies a mushroom — edible or not, and how certain am I.

"Lena Hartwell. Guild of Impossible Things."

"I know what you are. The question is what you want, and I'll warn you now—" she turned back toward her desk, dismissing Lena with her spine, "—I don't do love letters anymore. Or death warrants. A woman learns."

"I need a contract forged," Lena said. "Or rather, un-forged. I need a document's provenance falsified so thoroughly that a court that has existed for centuries believes it never signed what it signed."

Odile's shoulders went still. It was a small stillness, but Lena had spent ten years reading bodies across supper tables, waiting for the twitch that meant poison, and she recognized the quality of it. Fear, held the way one holds a full cup across a crowded room.

"Sit down, girl," Odile said without turning. "And tell me whose soul we're talking about."

* * *

Lena told her almost all of it. Dominic, the sale, the collection clause, the date circled in century-old ink in her own handwriting. She left out only the part that made no sense even to her — the margins, the future hand, the growing suspicion that the prophecy book was less a record than a draft. Some things you didn't say aloud until you could survive saying them.

Odile listened while grinding pigment, which Lena understood to be a compliment; the woman worked with her hands when her mind was fully engaged, and the pestle never once stuttered.

"So the plan is forgery," Odile said at last. "You want me to produce a document showing the contract was voided at signing — some clerical defect, a witness improperly sworn, a seal applied out of order. Something the court must honor because the court honors nothing else."

"Exactly. Contracts are the only law they have. So we break the contract with a contract."

"And when they audit it? And they will audit it, Hartwell, because a magistrate who has held a soul for ten years does not let it go over a smudged signature. Every document I make has a lineage. Paper from a mill, ink from a batch, a hand trained in a specific scriptorium in a specific decade. To forge a document old enough to predate their claim, I need materials old enough, and I need—" she set down the pestle, and her voice went quiet in a way that was worse than shouting, "—I need my name."

Lena waited. She was good at waiting. It was most of her job.

"They took it," Odile said. "Twenty-two years ago. I forged a coronation oath — the real one, mind you, the king's father couldn't read his own title and someone had to make the ceremony work — and when the old king died and the new one needed someone to blame for every irregularity of the last reign, they blamed the pen instead of the crown. They confiscated my name. Do you understand what that means? Not my freedom. My *name*. It's written in the registry beneath the Spire, in the heart of that tower, on a page in a ledger, and until it comes off that page I cannot sign anything as myself. Every document I've produced in twenty-two years has been someone else's. I have been a hundred hands and no person." She laughed, short and terrible. "They say I can forge anything. It's true. Everything except forgiveness, and I've stopped taking commissions for that."

"The registry," Lena said slowly. "In the Spire."

"In the Spire. Which opens for no one. Which has opened for no one living in three hundred years, except the owed and their collectors." Odile finally turned and looked at her straight on, and her eyes were the pale gray of paper left in sun. "So here is my price, taster, and you may find it steep. You get me your contract's false provenance, and in exchange, you go into that registry and you steal my name back. The whole page, if it can be managed. I don't want a copy. Copies can be disputed. I want the original in my hand, so that somewhere in their perfect ledgers there is a hole shaped exactly like me."

Lena sat with that. Around them the room smelled of iron-gall and lapis and old paper, and somewhere below the city, she supposed, the court beneath the mountain was sharpening its claim on a smuggler-archivist who had once been foolish enough to love her in units of souls.

"That's not a price," Lena said finally. "That's a suicide note with extra steps."

"Yes," Odile agreed pleasantly. "Most worthwhile prices look like that."

* * *

They argued for two hours. Lena would remember afterward that it was the first argument she'd enjoyed since the one with Dominic — the same shape, oddly, two proud people circling a thing neither would name, except this time the unnamed thing was hope rather than whatever she and Dominic kept not saying over cold tea.

Odile's objections were professional and devastating. Lena had no plan for entering the Spire. Lena had no map. Lena had, as far as Odile could determine, a guild of eccentrics, a debtor-spy of uncertain loyalty, and a deadline measured in days. Forging the provenance without knowing whether the theft could succeed was malpractice; Odile had survived twenty-two years by never forging anything she wasn't certain of.

"And if I refuse?" Lena asked at one point. "If I just take my business elsewhere?"

"To whom? There are perhaps four hands in this city capable of what you need, and two are dead, one works exclusively for the chancery, and the fourth is drinking himself blind in the Salt Quarter and hasn't held a steady line since the plague year." Odile refilled both their cups — tea, Lena noted, brewed strong and bitter, a working woman's tea. "You came here because there was nowhere else. That's not an insult, girl. It's how the best bargains start. Nobody trades from abundance."

It was true, and Lena hated it, and she respected Odile more for saying it than she would have for any kindness.

But it was Lena who found the flaw, in the end, because finding flaws was her trade. She set down her cup with the deliberate click she used when she wanted a table's attention.

"You're lying to yourself," she said. "Not to me — I'd taste it anyway. To yourself. You say you want the original page because copies can be disputed. But a copy of your name, authenticated well enough to satisfy even you, would free you just as cleanly. What you actually want is for them to *lose* it. To open their ledger one morning and find a hole where Odile Vasse used to be, and no way to prove she ever belonged to them." She leaned forward. "That's not a fee. That's revenge. And revenge is the one commission you told me you don't take."

The silence that followed had texture. Dust moved in the light from the high window. Odile's hands, resting on the desk among the graves of other people's handwriting, curled slightly inward.

"You are wasted on soup," the forger said at last.

"I'm wasted on nothing. I catalogue. It's what I'm for." Lena held her gaze. "So let's be honest, both of us, since honesty seems to be the rarest material in either of our trades. You want to help me because helping me gets you inside the greatest heist this city has ever seen, wearing the face of a favor. And I need you because there is genuinely no one else. We are both pretending this is commerce. Shall we continue pretending, or shall we strike the bargain and get to work?"

Odile Vasse smiled, and it transformed her face the way a seal transforms blank wax — suddenly official, suddenly dangerous.

"Terms," she said. "One: I begin the provenance forgery tonight, but I deliver nothing until your people bring me my page. Two: I choose the clerical defect we exploit, because you clearly don't understand contract law and I clearly do. Three—" she raised a stained finger, and something in her expression shifted, softened by a degree so small anyone else would have missed it; Lena didn't. "Three: when you go down there, into that registry, and you see the pages and pages of names — thousands of them, sold cheap, sold young, sold by desperate people to desperate courts — you will want to steal all of them. You won't be able to. No one can. And you will carry that out of the mountain with you, the weight of every name you leave behind, and I want you to know that weight is coming before you agree. Because I've carried it for twenty-two years, and the least I can do is warn the next fool."

Lena thought of the vault ledgers at the guildhall, columns of traded impossibilities in Dominic's slanted hand. She thought of a boy ten years ago, condemned as a scapegoat, whose pardon had cost someone everything. She thought of a page numbered 412 that she hadn't seen yet but already dreaded.

"I accept," she said. "All three terms."

They shook on it, hand to stained hand, and Odile's grip was stronger than her size promised.

"One more thing," Odile said as Lena rose to leave. "This Dominic. The one who sold his soul for a stranger's pardon." She was already turning back to her desk, already selecting a pen with the tenderness other people reserved for lovers. "Does he know you're doing this?"

"He forbade it."

"Mm." The nib whispered against a whetstone. "Then he knows you at all, does he?"

Lena didn't answer. At the door, she paused.

"Why did you really agree?" she asked. "The honest answer. Not the commerce."

Odile didn't look up from her pens. "Because twenty-two years ago, a girl was condemned for holding a pen, and everyone who could have spoken for her calculated the cost and stayed silent." The whetstone stilled. "I've done my own accounting since, taster. It never balanced. Consider this a correction entry."

Lena stepped out into the gray street, and behind her the shutters closed softly, and somewhere beneath the mountain a ledger waited with a hole in it shaped like a woman, soon to be joined — if they were quick, if they were lucky, if impossible things went their way — by a second hole shaped like a man.

Three days. She had three days to learn how to steal a name.

She was still calculating when she heard the bells above the palace quarter begin to ring wrong — fast, arrhythmic, the pattern the kitchens called *poison in the palace* — and broke into a run.
