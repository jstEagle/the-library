## 9. The Debtors' Table

Wren had betrayed the court beneath the mountain on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were audit days down there — days when the ledgers were opened and every debt was read aloud in a voice like wet slate — and if you were going to commit treason, you might as well do it while the paperwork was already out.

The climb up through the salt galleries took eleven hours. Eleven hours of narrow chimneys and brine-slick handholds and the particular silence of a place that had never known weather. Wren counted the hours because counting was how they kept from thinking about what waited above: a guildhall full of strangers who would have every reason to feed them to the magistrate's clerks, and one map inked into the lining of a coat that had cost Wren everything they had left.

By the time they hauled themselves out of the disused well behind the tannery district, dawn was doing its gray work over the rooftops, and Wren's fingers had gone past numb into something philosophical.

The guildhall of the Guild of Impossible Things stood where it always stood, wedged between a chandler's and a building that insisted it was a bakery but had never once produced bread. Its sign hung crooked. Its windows glowed with the particular amber of people awake far too late or far too early. Wren stood in the alley for a long moment, salt crusted white along their sleeves, and rehearsed the speech.

*I am Wren. I was the court's eyes above ground for nine years. I am here to defect, and I have brought you the mountain.*

It sounded better in rehearsal. Most things did.

* * *

They let Wren in because Odile Vasse recognized the coat.

"Salt-mine issue," she said, before Wren had gotten three words into the speech. She was sitting at the long table near the hearth with her feet up on a chair, a cup of something dark steaming beside a scatter of practice scripts — forged signatures, each more confident than the last. "Court surplus. They stopped making them eleven years ago after the tannery scandal, so either you're very old or you've been below." Her eyes, quick and unkind in the way of someone who had been flayed by institutions and had learned to flay first, went to Wren's hands. "And you're very old for a courier."

"I'm not a courier," Wren said.

"No," said a voice from the doorway to the ledger room, and Lena Hartwell came out drying her hands on a cloth, and Wren felt the strange vertigo of seeing in person a face they had only ever studied across a magistrate's desk. "You're the court's listener. You're the reason three of our members moved houses last spring without telling anyone." Lena set the cloth down with the deliberate care of a woman who handled poison daily and had opinions about sudden movements. "Why are you here?"

"Because I'd rather be a traitor than an instrument," Wren said. It wasn't the rehearsed line. The rehearsed line had been longer and cleverer. This one just fell out, plain as a dropped coin, and it seemed to land better than anything they'd practiced.

Odile looked at Lena. Lena looked at the window, at the gray light, at some arithmetic only she could see running behind her eyes. Then she pulled out a chair.

"Sit," she said. "Talk slowly. I'll be checking everything you say against the ledgers afterward, so don't embellish."

"That," said Wren, sitting, "is the most reassuring thing anyone has said to me in nine years."

* * *

They spread the map on the debtors' table.

It was the table everyone called the debtors' table because that was where the guild conducted its strangest business — the trades that couldn't be shelved, the debts that couldn't be balanced, the negotiations conducted over cold tea by people who owed each other things no coin could cover. Its surface was scarred with candle burns and ring-stains and, somewhere under the current layer of varnish, the ghost of a contract written in wine. Wren had heard about this table for years from the other side of the mountain. Sitting at it felt like putting a hand into a story.

The map itself was drawn on the inside of the coat lining, in ink mixed with salt water so it would survive damp. Wren peeled the lining back and pinned it flat, and the room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet when a thing becomes real.

"There," Wren said, and their finger found the shape of it. "That's the Spire. Everyone above ground thinks it's a tower. It isn't. It's a root. It grows downward — has been growing downward since before the city had a name. What you see above is the taproot's tip. Everything else is under the mountain." Their finger traced the descending spiral. "Velvet corridors. Waiting rooms. Offices. The whole apparatus of a court, except the court's only business is holding what it holds. And here—" the finger stopped at the lowest chamber, the deepest point, a small dense circle the cartographer-self in Wren had inked twice for emphasis, "—is the registry heart. Every name ever sold is written there. Every soul the court holds is a line in that book. Burn the book, you burn the holdings. But you can't burn the book, because the book is the mountain. So instead—"

"You strike the entry," Odile said softly. She had leaned forward without seeming to notice she'd done it, and her eyes had gone bright and terrible. "You amend the record."

"You amend the record," Wren agreed. "The court runs on precedent. Contracts must hold or everything unravels — that's not cruelty, that's their entire theology. Which means their strength is also their cage. If an entry can be shown to be voided — properly voided, with provenance, with paperwork — even the magistrate has to honor it. He'd hate it. He'd do it anyway. That's the crack in the wall."

Lena had not sat down. She stood at the map's edge with her arms folded, and Wren watched her read it the way she presumably read everything: completely, once, with total retention.

"The corridors are guarded how?" she asked.

"They aren't, mostly. Guards imply intruders are possible, and the court's founding assumption is that nothing enters that isn't owed. There are ushers. Clerks. Things that check credentials. The credential-checking is the danger. Everything else is furniture and patience." Wren hesitated, then made themselves say the rest, because they hadn't climbed eleven hours of salt chimney to deliver half a warning. "But there's a second thing. The reason I left tonight and not next month. Three days ago the magistrate convened a private session — no clerks, no record. I wasn't meant to know. I listen; knowing is my function. He read the prophecy aloud. The old folio, the one with tomorrow's date in it. And then he said—" Wren closed their eyes to get the cadence right; the magistrate's voice was a thing you didn't paraphrase, you reproduced, "—'Interference is anticipated. Interference will be weighed. Let it come, and let it be entered.' He *wants* you to try. Or he wants whoever tries to be legible to him. Either way, he suspects, he expects, and he has had a century to prepare the paperwork."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a bottle of borrowed luck shifted on its shelf with a sound like a sigh.

"He read the prophecy," Lena said at last, very evenly. "Aloud. Like a menu."

"Yes."

"And my name was on it."

Wren met her eyes and did not lie, partly from principle and partly because lying to this woman seemed like a category error. "Your name was the dish."

* * *

The argument started within the hour, once the others had been woken and gathered — the guild's full strange complement, the debtors and exiles and one retired curse-broker who mostly attended meetings for the biscuits. Wren sat at the edge of it all, drinking tea that was too strong, watching a family argue like a family, which was to say: with history.

"We go in quiet," Odile was saying, tapping the map. "Two people. Credentials forged clean, corridor knowledge from our defector, in and out before the day-turn. A crowd is a confession."

"A crowd is a diversion," countered Marlowe-the-curse-broker's-nephew, whose actual specialty was locks and whose actual personality was contrarianism. "Send four. Two visible, two not. The court watches the visible ones and misses the rest."

"The court doesn't *miss* anything," Wren said. "That's rather the problem. You cannot sneak past omniscient bookkeeping. You can only give it something accurate to record."

"Then we give it something false," said Odile.

"It doesn't accept false. It accepts *legal*. Learn the difference and we live."

Lena spoke rarely during the argument, but each time she did the argument reorganized around her words like iron filings around a magnet. *What does the registry require to strike an entry?* — and Wren explained the forms, the witnesses, the seal. *How long does an audit take?* — and Wren described the sweep, the polite irrevocable escorts. *If we're caught, what happens to the ones who aren't me?* — and Wren had to admit they didn't know, because the court had never caught anyone who mattered, and the uncertainty sat on the table like a fifth guest.

It was nearly noon, and the plan had been built and half-dismantled and rebuilt twice, when the door opened without a knock.

Dominic Vael stood in it, rain-wet, travel-cloaked, with the look of a man who had walked a long way specifically to be inconvenient.

Wren had seen portraits. The court kept records of everything it held, and though souls couldn't be sketched, faces could, and Dominic's face was filed in the annex under *V, collateral interests.* The portrait showed a younger man with a gambler's grin. The man in the doorway was older, and the grin was still there, but it had been re-forged into something with edges.

"Well," he said, to the room in general. "This looks like a conspiracy. Someone fetch me a chair before I start feeling excluded from my own funeral."

"Dominic." Lena's voice did something complicated — relief and fury braided into one syllable. "You were supposed to be in Coldharbor until month's end."

"I was. I left. Coldharbor's dull and everyone there owes me money, which stops being fun when you realize collecting is the only conversation available." He came into the room the way weather comes in, unhurried, inevitable, and took the chair Odile kicked toward him without looking. His eyes swept the map, the salt-stained stranger, the scattered notes, and Wren watched him assemble the entire situation in perhaps four seconds. "So. You've found my contract, you've recruited a court listener, and you're planning a burglary of the afterlife. Have I got the shape of it?"

Nobody answered, which was answer enough.

"Good," said Dominic. "Now here's the part where I save everyone time. No."

"No?" said Marlowe's nephew.

"No. Nobody is rescuing me. I want that minuted. Odile, you keep a minute-book, minute it: *the asset declines rescue.*" He said it lightly, but Wren — who had spent nine years listening to people say true things in light voices — heard the steel underneath. "I signed. I knew the terms. A trade is a trade, and the guild's whole economy rests on trades holding. If we start unwinding deals because someone's feelings got involved, we're not a guild anymore, we're a mob with stationery."

"That is the court's argument," Wren said quietly. "Almost word for word."

"Then the court and I agree on something. Mark the date." But his eyes, when they flicked to Wren, weren't joking at all. They were measuring. *The court's listener,* the look said. *Sitting at our table. How convenient.*

Lena stepped forward. "Dominic—"

"Don't." Just the one word, and the lightness dropped away entirely, and for a moment the room saw the thing underneath the smuggler-archivist: ten years of a secret carried alone, worn smooth as river stone. "Lena. Don't. Whatever you've built, whatever this brave ridiculous scheme is — I won't be the reason any of you go under that mountain. I have *seen* the waiting room. I signed anyway, because the alternative was watching you hang for a king's bad supper, and I'd sign again, and that's the end of the arithmetic."

"It isn't arithmetic," Lena said. "That's exactly what's wrong with it. You did sums with your soul and never once asked the variable."

"The variable was dying, Lena."

"The variable," she said, and her voice cracked precisely once, in the middle, like ice underfoot, "was never asked."

The silence afterward had texture. Wren sat in it, an intruder at a family's worst moment, and understood suddenly why the magistrate wanted interference anticipated rather than prevented: because this — love expressed as stubbornness, both parties too proud to say the plain true thing — was predictable. Was *prophesied*, possibly. Was the kind of human mess that made excellent precedent.

Dominic stood. "Minute it," he repeated, softer now, to Odile, who was looking at him with an expression Wren couldn't parse — something old and sad and fond. "The asset declines rescue." He pulled his cloak back on. "I'll be in the archive. Where I should have stayed."

The door shut behind him with terrible gentleness.

* * *

Afterward, the plan lay on the table in pieces, and nobody moved to rebuild it.

Odile spoke first, staring at the door. "He hid it for ten years. The contract. From all of us." Her voice was flat in the way of someone pressing down on something that wanted to rise. "I forge documents for a living. I know what hiding costs. Ten years." She picked up her pen, put it down again. "We're going anyway. All of us. Whether he consents or not."

"He'll fight it," said Marlowe's nephew.

"He'll fight it *from inside the plan*," Odile said. "Watch. He'll refuse to be rescued and then he'll improve our escape route, because he can't help himself. That man has never once left a lock unexamined."

Wren turned their teacup slowly on its saucer, and thought about the magistrate's voice saying *let it come, and let it be entered,* and decided — quietly, without yet saying it aloud — that there was one more thing they needed to confess. Not the maps. Not the warning. The offer. The one that hadn't been made yet, but would be; Wren could feel it coming down the mountain like weather, because the magistrate never spent a listener without first trying to buy one back.

Three days, probably. The magistrate liked three-day deadlines; they appeared reasonable and left no time to think.

"Wren." Lena was looking at them across the ruined plan, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "You've been listening to that court for nine years. Tell me honestly. When we come for him — what will it cost?"

Wren set down the cup.

"More than you've budgeted," they said. "And I think I need to tell you about the letter I'm going to receive."

Outside, the rain kept falling on the crooked sign, and below the city, patient as furniture, the court began to write.
