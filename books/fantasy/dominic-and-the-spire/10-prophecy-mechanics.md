## 10. Prophecy Mechanics

Odile Vasse had spent eleven years forging documents for the crown of Veymar, and in all that time she had learned one truth above the others: a hand betrays its owner the way a face cannot. A face can be schooled. A face can lie. But the way a person curls the tail of a *g*, the pressure they lean into a downstroke when they are angry, the little hitch where they lift the pen to think — these things confess. She could copy any hand in the kingdom because she understood that every hand was an autobiography written small.

Which was why, on the third night of studying the prophecy book, she set down her magnifying lens and said, out loud, to the empty scriptorium: "Oh, you foolish, magnificent girl."

The guildhall had gone quiet around midnight. Somewhere below, Wren's boots creaked on the stairs as they made their rounds — a habit from the court beneath, where sleeping sentries did not stay employed long. The lamp over Odile's desk burned low and amber, and the prophecy book lay open before her like a patient under examination.

It was not a beautiful book. That had been her first disappointment. She had expected a grimoire — blackened vellum, iron clasps, letters that crawled. Instead it was a ledger-bound folio, practical as a customs record, its spine repaired twice with different thread, its pages soft at the edges from a century of thumbs. Whoever had compiled it had cared about accuracy, not theater. Odile respected that immediately. Forgers always respect the honest work; it is the only kind they cannot do.

The body of the text was in a formal secretary hand, old-fashioned even for its age — prophecies entered by date, each one brief, most of them maddeningly vague. *The river will remember its name.* *A door that opens only for the owed.* *On this day, the Spire takes what was promised.* Entries spanning ninety years, each dated with the confidence of someone who had never once been wrong about a calendar.

But it was the margins that kept her up past three nights running.

Marginalia was her specialty. Anyone could forge a document; only a master forged the *conversation* a document had with itself — the corrections, the doubts, the little arguments between a writer and their own earlier self. And the margins of this book were crowded with such conversations, in a quick, slanted, unlovely hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the page behind it.

She had assumed the annotator was some later archivist. Guilds accumulated hands the way ships accumulated barnacles. She had catalogued four distinct marginal scripts so far: a fussy clerical italic, a heavy merchant's block, a spidery scholarly cursive — and this fourth one, the pressing one, the urgent one, which corrected the others as though it had authority over all of them.

*Wrong. Not the river — the aqueduct. See 14 March.*

*This entry is off by two days. The court counts from descent, not announcement.*

*He signs with his left thumbprint, not his name. Note for p. 212.*

Small notes. Practical notes. Notes with the tone of a woman balancing books she had not been given the ledger for.

And then, tonight, working by lamplight with her lens, Odile had done the thing she should have done the first evening: she had checked the dates. Not the dates of the prophecies. The dates *beside* the annotations.

The pressing hand had annotated an entry from sixty-one years ago. The annotation was dated forty years from now.

She turned the page. Another note in the same hand, correcting a prophecy from the reign of the old king's grandfather — dated thirty-three years ahead of tonight. Another, dated fifty-one years ahead. Another, dated ahead of that. The annotations marched backward through the book and forward through time, as if the annotator were reading history in one direction and living in the other.

Odile sat very still, the way she did when a forgery was about to come apart in her hands and she needed to decide whether to run or to finish it better.

Then she did what any professional does with an impossible document. She tested it against everything she knew.

* * *

The first test was ink. Time changed ink the way time changed people — iron gall darkened and then ate into paper, leaving those telltale halos, the brown corrosion rings around every letter like frost around a window. Fresh ink sat *on* the page; old ink sank *through* it. There was no faking the chemistry. She had tried, early in her career, aging ink with heat and vinegar, and the result had fooled a customs officer but never another calligrapher. Old ink had a texture grief had. You couldn't counterfeit it convincingly.

She held the lamp close and examined the annotations from sixty-one years ago, written in the pressing hand.

The ink was corroded. Deeply, honestly, irreproducibly corroded. Sixty years of corrosion, at least. Whatever chemistry had gone onto that page had gone onto it decades before Lena Hartwell was born.

And yet the hand —

Odile pulled open her drawer of reference samples. This was the part she had been avoiding, the part that had made her keep the lens down and her eyes on safer things for two nights. Because she knew handwriting the way musicians knew voices, and she had spent an afternoon a week ago watching Lena Hartwell balance ledgers across the table from her, and she had thought, with the idle appreciation of a craftsman noticing another craftsman: *she presses too hard, that one. She writes like she's afraid the page will forget.*

She laid Lena's ledger page beside the prophecy margin.

The tail of the *g*: identical, that impatient downward flick that didn't quite finish. The crossbar of the *t*: placed slightly right of center, a habit of someone writing fast while thinking slow. The numerals: the *4* closed at the top, the *7* crossed twice, always twice, a superstition or a signature. The pressure: deep, insistent, embossing — the pen of a woman who did not trust paper to hold what she wrote on it.

Identical. Not similar. Not *a family resemblance of hands*. Identical, down to the tiny hesitation before every capital letter, that millisecond lift where the writer checked herself — a hesitation Odile had seen in exactly one other hand in her life, her own, back when she still signed things with her true name.

She sat back. The lamp guttered. Down in the hall, Wren's footsteps paused outside the scriptorium door, considered, and moved on. Even the building seemed to be giving her room to think.

"Backdated," she said aloud, testing the word. It didn't fit. Backdating required the forger to exist before the date. These annotations were *forward*-dated — dated decades beyond the corrosion of their own ink, which meant either that someone had invented a method of aging ink backward through time, or —

Or the book was written backward. Or the woman who wrote the margins lived forward of the book the way a reader lives forward of a letter. Or —

Or Lena Hartwell, who was twenty-nine years old and downstairs asleep in a narrow bed above the vaults, had annotated prophecies that occurred before her grandmother's birth, in ink that had dried before her mother learned to write.

Odile pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations.

* * *

She worked until dawn because work was the only shape her fear took. Panic, in Odile's experience, was a luxury of people whose mistakes stayed put. Her own fear had always converted instantly into precision: when the world became unbearable, she made lists. So she made lists.

Item: the ink was old wherever the dates said it should be old. Verified on nine annotations across six decades. No pattern of forgery she knew of could produce this.

Item: the hand was Lena's, without deviation, across the entire span. Hands drifted over decades. People injured themselves, grew old, changed pens, changed minds. This hand did not drift. Twenty-nine-year-old Lena and sixty-year-old Lena and, presumably, seventy-one-year-old Lena wrote with a single unbroken identity. As if the hand existed outside of time and merely visited pages.

Item — and this was the item that made her stop listing, because the list was leading somewhere with teeth:

Item: the annotations were *corrections*. Not predictions. Corrections. *Wrong, not the river — the aqueduct.* *Off by two days.* You did not correct a prophecy unless you knew how it actually went. You did not know how it actually went unless you had watched it happen. Every note in that margin was the note of someone standing at the far end of events, looking back, tidying the record for the benefit of whoever stood at the near end.

For the benefit of her. Of now-her. Of the her that was sitting at this desk.

Odile looked down at the open book, at the entry for the eleventh of the month — tomorrow's date, the date circled in century-old ink beside a name, *Hartwell*, the discovery that had started all of this — and forced herself to read the margin beside it slowly, word by word, the way she read a contract she intended to break.

There were three notes in the pressing hand.

The first: *The court will offer you the obvious trade. Take the meeting anyway. You need to see the scales.*

The second: *Page 412. Do not sign anything until you have seen page 412.*

The third was smaller, squeezed into the corner, written — she would have sworn it — more gently than the others, with the pressure eased, the haste abandoned:

*I'm sorry I couldn't warn you sooner. The margins are narrow. So is the road.*

Odile read it four times. On the fourth reading she noticed what a forger notices last and best: the apology was in present tense. *I'm sorry.* Not *I was sorry.* Not *I will be sorry.* Present tense, from a hand dated forty years away, about a night that hadn't happened yet.

Free will, she thought, with the cold clarity that had gotten her exiled from court. Free will is a question of margins. The text is fixed — a century of prophecy, entered in advance, unalterable, the main body of the book as immutable as any statute. But the margins — the margins were where a hand could reach in and argue. The margins were where someone had already fought this battle, note by note, correction by correction, and lost or won in spaces barely wide enough for a sentence.

Was Lena free? Was she walking toward tomorrow, or was she walking through a book that had already recorded her walk — with her own hand holding the pen?

And worse, the question underneath, the one that made Odile's own fingers curl involuntarily, the question of a woman who had spent her life making false things true: if future-Lena had written the margins, then future-Lena had *chosen* what to correct and what to leave alone. She had left the circling of the date untouched. She had left *Hartwell* in the text. Whatever happened tomorrow, whatever price was paid at the Spire — the woman who knew best had read that line and let it stand.

Either she couldn't change it.

Or she wouldn't.

Odile closed the book softly, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and pressed her palm flat against its cover as though taking a pulse.

"There you are," she murmured — to future-Lena, to the stranger wearing her friend's hand. "I don't know yet whether you're our map or our cage. But I'm going to find out. And if there's a forgery anywhere in you — if some version of you wrote yourself into history to trap a scared girl into a noble sacrifice —"

She stopped. Swallowed. Finished it anyway, because a threat half-spoken is a lie, and Odile Vasse had retired from lying in her own voice.

"— then I'll find the seam. There's always a seam."

Outside the arrow-slit window, the sky was going grey at the edges, the particular grey of the hour when night admits it has lost. In two hours, Lena would come down to make tea, and Odile would have to look at her — at the quick unlovely handwriting of her, at the pressing hand, at the woman who might be the author of everyone's doom or its diligent editor — and decide how much to say.

That was the trouble with reading other people's hands. Sooner or later you read something you wished you could unlearn.

Odile banked the lamp, gathered her samples, and paused at the door with one last glance at the closed folio on the desk. Tomorrow's date waited inside it, circled in old ink, patient as a debt.

She went upstairs to wake the household early. Some news did not keep until breakfast — and besides, she wanted to watch Lena's face when she showed her the margins.

Because there was one more thing, one small thing, that Odile had not yet said aloud, even to herself, even in the privacy of her own skull:

In forty years, the hand in the margins stopped. The final annotation was dated four decades hence, a single line, and after it — nothing. A century of prophecy, and the correcting hand simply ceased, mid-conversation, as if the woman writing it had finally run out of road.

The last note read: *If you're reading this, then the forgetting worked.*

Odile did not intend to let it.
