## 11. The King's Poisoner

The soup arrived at the king's table the color of a storm deciding whether to break.

Lena stood at her station beside the serving door, tasting spoon in hand, and let the first mouthful rest on her tongue the way she'd been trained to: flat, patient, attentive. Ten years of this work had taught her that poisons rarely announced themselves. They arrived as small disagreements between what a thing looked like and what it tasted like. Almond where there should be no almond. A sweetness sitting wrong in a savory broth. The faint metallic ghost of a metal that had no business touching food.

She catalogued the storm-colored soup. Leek. Cream. A whisper of thyme. Underneath it all — nothing. That was the problem.

Nothing was never truly nothing.

She swallowed, set the spoon down with a soft click, and waited.

* * *

The waiting was its own art. A taster who spat and gasped told everyone in the hall exactly which dish had been tampered with; a taster who died quietly told no one anything, which was worse for everyone except the poisoner. So Lena had built herself a repertoire of small delays — the pause to consider, the sip of water, the murmured consultation with the undercook about whether the cream had been scalded properly — each one buying her body time to decide whether it intended to keep living.

Three minutes past the swallow, she felt the first wrongness arrive. Not in her throat. In her fingertips. A coldness there, precise as a struck bell, spreading up through her knuckles into the bones of her hands.

Her heart did not race. She had trained that out of it years ago, back when she'd been a frightened girl standing trial for a poisoning she hadn't committed, watching a courtroom decide whether her death would be convenient. Fear, she had learned, was an expense. She budgeted it.

Cold fingers. She moved down the line of dishes with her tasting spoon, working methodically while the king watched her with the mild, hungry patience he brought to all his meals. The roast lamb: clean. The buttered greens: clean, if oversalted. The honey cake: clean, though someone had been generous with the saffron, and saffron was worth more per ounce than the cook earned in a month. She noted that separately. Generosity in a royal kitchen was either theft or message.

Then she returned to the soup, dipped the spoon a second time, and took a second taste — smaller, held longer against the roof of her mouth, where certain compounds confessed themselves only after a slow surrender.

There. Beneath the leek and the cream and the thyme, so faint she might have imagined it: a bitterness shaped like winter. Like biting the air off a frozen lake. It dissolved before she could name it, leaving only the certainty that something had been there and the equally strong certainty that she could not have said what.

Lena lowered the spoon.

"Your Majesty." Her voice came out level, which she counted as a professional triumph. "I'd recommend against the soup tonight."

A murmur ran the length of the table. The king frowned at the bowl as though it had insulted his mother. "Reason?"

"The cream has turned," Lena said smoothly, because *I tasted a bitterness like frozen air and my fingers have gone cold* was not a sentence that ended well for anyone, least of all the cook. "Bitterness at the finish. It may be harmless. I don't recommend finding out."

The king waved the soup away with the grandeur of a man who had never once been poisoned successfully, and the meal proceeded without it. Lena stood through the remaining courses with her hands folded behind her back, feeling the cold retreat from her fingertips one joint at a time, and thought, with the particular clarity that fear-budgeting allowed:

Someone had put something in that soup that she had never encountered before. And in ten years of tasting, she had encountered everything the city's apothecaries could brew, every bitter widow's recipe, every jealous rival's import from across the sea. Her tongue was a ledger. Every entry was accounted for.

This one wasn't in any book she owned.

Which meant it had come from somewhere else. Somewhere beneath the ordinary world. Somewhere with a magistrate and velvet corridors and a date circled in old ink.

* * *

She did not go straight home. Home, tonight, meant the guildhall, meant Dominic, meant the conversation they were still not having — the one that lived between them like a splinter neither of them would dig out. Instead she walked the long way, through the servants' corridors and down the cold stair that smelled of tallow and stone, to the chamber beneath the kitchens where the palace kept its antidote cabinet.

It was not really a cabinet. It was a room, floor to ceiling, shelved with jars and vials and stoppered gourds, organized by a system only three people alive understood, two of whom were dead. Lena had spent five years quietly reorganizing it in her head, because a taster who couldn't find the antidote in time might as well not have one.

She lit the lamp and began pulling references. *Velen's Compendium of Bitter Things.* The apothecaries' guild registry, copied in a hand so cramped it might have been a cipher. Her own notebook — ten years of aftertastes, catalogued by sensation, season, and suspected source. She worked by lamplight with the patience of a woman balancing a ledger, matching the ghost-bitterness of frozen air against entry after entry.

Nothing.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the shelves, and the cold in her fingertips seemed suddenly very far away and very close at once.

There were two explanations, and she made herself hold both in her mind the way she held a tasting spoon, turning them over.

One: a new poison existed, above ground, that had simply escaped her notice. Unlikely. Her network of cooks and cupbearers and disgraced physicians was better than most spymasters', and new poisons left ripples — sudden deaths, quiet funerals, apothecaries selling strange quantities of odd reagents. The ripples weren't there.

Two: the poison didn't come from above ground at all.

Lena closed her eyes. In the dark behind her lids she saw the prophecy folio, tomorrow's — today's now, almost — date circled in ink that matched her own handwriting, and beside it a name. Hartwell. She saw Dominic's face doing the thing it did when he lied well, which was to say it did nothing at all, which was how she knew.

The court beneath the mountain had come to collect. And the collection clause said *due when the date arrives.* But contracts, she had learned from the guild's stranger ledgers, were living things. They could be hurried. They could be *encouraged.*

If the soul owed belonged to Dominic, then killing the person who might interfere —

No. She stopped herself. Think it through properly. If the court wanted Dominic's soul collected cleanly, why harm her? Unless someone below suspected interference. Unless someone below had decided that the taster who kept impossible ledgers and asked impossible questions was a complication to be removed before the date, quietly, deniably, by an agent above ground who wanted no fingerprints.

Or — and this possibility she turned over slowly, tasting it like the soup — unless the point wasn't to kill her at all. Unless the point was to make her afraid. To make her spend the last days before the date looking over her shoulder instead of preparing. Fear as strategy. Pressure wearing poison's clothes.

Both possibilities fit. Both were unacceptable.

Lena banked the lamp, locked the antidote room, and climbed back up into the palace proper, where the torches burned warm and the court laughed at jokes she no longer heard.

* * *

The second attempt came two nights later, and it was cleverer.

She noticed it first as an absence. The king's evening wine — a heavy red from the southern valleys that Lena tasted nightly and trusted implicitly — arrived already poured, decanted at the butler's pantry rather than at the table. Small change. Invisible change. The kind of change that existed precisely so that no one would look twice.

But Lena looked twice at everything. That was the whole of her profession.

She tasted the wine. Clean. Rich. Perfectly itself. She tasted it again, slower. Still clean. And it was the third sip — taken not because procedure demanded it but because something in the wine's *confidence* bothered her — that caught it.

Under the plum and the smoke and the valley dust: a thread of hazelnut.

There were no hazelnuts in the southern valleys' winemaking. There never had been. Which meant the thread hadn't come from the wine. It had come from somewhere else — from the glass, perhaps, or the decanter, or the cloth that had wiped it — and it was so faint, so perfectly dosed, that only a tongue calibrated over ten years of deliberate practice would ever find it.

Lena set down the glass and did not drink again, and no one at the table noticed, because the glass remained full and full glasses attract no attention. She smiled at the right moments. She passed the salt. All the while her mind ran the problem like a ledger running totals.

Hazelnut. She knew of exactly one compound that announced itself as hazelnut and killed quietly: a distillation the old texts called *kernel-veil*, rare enough that Velen's Compendium gave it half a page and a warning that no reliable antidote existed. Rare enough that whoever had obtained it had resources. Patient enough that whoever had dosed the glass had chosen a vector that would kill hours later, far from the palace, with the taster's own supper as the presumed culprit.

And here was the beautiful, terrible part: if Lena had drunk that wine, she would have died tomorrow morning in her bed at the guildhall, and every inquiry would have found nothing in the palace at all. No scene. No accusation. Just a guild member dead in her sleep, unremarkable, unmourned by anyone whose mourning mattered to the court.

Except that the timing was wrong for accident and right for schedule. Two attempts in three nights, escalating in sophistication, both aimed at her and only her, both in the narrow window before a certain circled date. This was not a poisoner's pattern. Poisoners spread their work. This was a deadline's pattern.

Someone above ground was working for the court beneath it. And they had a calendar.

* * *

She survived the third attempt by refusing to attend it.

On the fifth night before the date — she had begun counting down the way sailors count distance — a summons arrived requesting the taster's presence at a private supper in the west wing, hosted by Lord Cassian Merle, Keeper of the King's cellars, a man whose smile Lena had never once seen reach his eyes. The summons specified that the taster would dine *with* the company rather than serve it. Flattery. Honor. A seat at the table instead of beside it.

It was, of course, a trap. Not even a subtle one. A taster removed from her station, seated among strangers, served food she had no hand in vetting, in a wing of the palace where the antidote cabinet was forty minutes' walk away. If she refused, she insulted a powerful man and marked herself as fearful. If she accepted, she dined on whatever he chose to put in front of her.

Lena read the summons four times. Then she went to find the palace physician, Master Ellery, a stooped man with kind eyes and a lifelong debt to her — she had once identified the ergot in his bread supply before his whole household sickened, and he had never forgotten it.

"I need you to write me an allergy," she said.

Ellery blinked. "An allergy."

"A documented one. Severe. Hazelnut and related kernel oils — violent reaction, throat swelling, the sort of thing that makes hosts terrified to serve me so much as a crumb that's touched a nut. Backdate it into my file. Make it old. Make it boring."

He studied her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him ask no questions, which was why she had chosen him. He wrote the note in his own crabbed hand, dated it six years back, filed it, and said only, "Whatever this is, girl, eat nothing you haven't tasted yourself."

"I never do," Lena said, and it was the truest sentence she'd spoken all week.

At Lord Merle's supper, when the seventh course arrived — a delicate pastry glazed with something amber and glistening — Lena pressed the back of her hand to her throat, went pale with theatrical precision, and produced Master Ellery's note. There was a moment, just one, where Lord Merle's face performed concern while his eyes performed arithmetic, and Lena watched the arithmetic fail. The glaze was hazelnut. Of course it was. He could hardly insist now without explaining how he'd known there'd be nothing to worry about.

She ate bread she'd brought herself, drank water from a sealed flask, and toasted the host's health with a smile that said *I know, and you know I know, and neither of us can prove anything.*

Walking back through the cold courtyards afterward, breath fogging, she let herself feel the shape of what had happened. Three attempts. Escalating care. An agent inside the palace with access to cellars, pantries, and private suppers — someone with rank, patience, and a reason to want a food-taster dead within the week.

The court below the mountain did not send assassins. The court sent clerks. Contracts, precedents, forms in triplicate — that was the grammar of the place, if Dominic's careful silences and the guild's oldest ledgers were to be believed. But every bureaucracy, however subterranean, required hands above ground. Agents. Correspondents. People who signed things.

Which meant that somewhere in these warm, torchlit halls, someone had signed on with the court beneath the mountain. Someone whose signature Lena might even have seen — in cellar inventories, in provisioning records, in the endless paperwork of a palace that ran on paper the way the Spire ran on souls.

She stopped in the middle of the courtyard and stood very still in the falling cold.

Tomorrow was the date. Tomorrow the prophecy's century-old ink came due, and the court would come to collect a soul named in a contract signed ten years ago by a man who lied beautifully and loved stupidly and would never, ever ask for help. And someone up here meant to make sure that when the collection happened, the one person stubborn enough to stand in the way would already be dead, tidily, of natural causes, with a full stomach.

Well.

Lena pulled her cloak tighter and turned toward the guildhall, toward the ledgers, toward the argument she and Dominic were going to finish whether he liked it or not. Let them send their agents. Let them dose their wines and glaze their pastries. She had survived a scapegoat's trial at sixteen by keeping her mouth shut and her wits sharp, and she had spent every year since sharpening both.

They had made one mistake, whoever they were. They had tried to kill a woman whose entire profession was noticing what didn't belong.

By dawn, she would know exactly which signature didn't belong — and by the night after, the court beneath the mountain was going to learn what happened when a taster decided to spit back.

She pushed open the guildhall door, and found the lamps still burning, and Dominic asleep at the ledger desk with a contract hidden under his arm — and the seal on it was one she recognized from Lord Merle's supper invitations.
