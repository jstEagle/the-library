## 12. Ten Years Ago

The trial of Lena Hartwell lasted eleven minutes.

Dominic counted. He was good at counting — it was the archivist in him, the part that catalogued everything because cataloguing felt like control — and he stood at the back of the King's Assize Hall with his hood up and his heart going like a drum in a procession, and he counted every second of the farce as it unfolded beneath the stained glass saints.

Eleven minutes for a girl of nineteen to be condemned.

She stood on the raised platform in a borrowed grey dress that wasn't hers — nothing about her situation was hers, that was the whole obscene architecture of it — and she did not cry. That was the first thing he noticed, before the evidence, before the verdict. The royal taster's apprentice had been accused of poisoning the king's soup, and while the prosecutor unrolled his list — the almond note in the broth, the bitter finish, the trace of gallowroot found in her locker, *planted*, everyone in the gallery who mattered knew it was planted — Lena Hartwell stood with her hands folded and her chin level and listened to her own death being scheduled like a delivery.

She corrected them twice. That was the second thing he noticed.

"The gallowroot would have left an aftertaste of copper," she said, in a voice pitched exactly for the record, not for mercy. "The soup tasted of almonds only. Whoever poisoned His Majesty wanted me blamed and wanted the king dead, but they didn't know enough poisons to make the two stories agree."

The magistrate's clerk wrote it down without looking up. No one asked her how she knew what gallowroot tasted like. She was the taster; tasting was her crime and her testimony both, and the court had no use for expertise that complicated its conclusions.

Dominic had come to watch a formality. Everyone had. The Guild of Impossible Things took its coffee that morning with the resigned air of people attending a funeral they'd already paid for — the scapegoat trial was a court tradition older than the guild, older than the mountain itself: when poison entered the palace, someone poor and foreign and recently hired must answer for it, and the real poisoner, whoever they were, went home to supper. It was not even corruption anymore. It was liturgy.

But then she'd corrected them. Twice. With precision like a knife laid flat on a table.

And something in Dominic's chest, which had been closed for business since his mother's last winter, unlocked one drawer.

He should have let it stay locked. He knew that then; he knew it now, ten years downstream of this moment, standing in the vault of the guildhall with a contract in his hands and Lena's name in his ears. But he was twenty-three years old and stupid in the specific way that clever men are stupid — he believed that because he could see the trap clearly, he wouldn't step in it.

* * *

The descent began at midnight, three days before the execution date, from a door behind the tanneries that most people walked past without seeing, because the door was shy.

Everyone in the guild knew about the court beneath the mountain. You didn't join the Guild of Impossible Things without learning the geography of debt. Above ground, impossibility was commerce — bottled last words, borrowed luck, one honest lie kept in stock like vintage wine. Below ground, impossibility was *law*. The court under the mountain held every contract the surface world was too squeamish to enforce. Souls sold. Names surrendered. Futures pawned by desperate fathers and redeemed or forfeited by their grandchildren. The court did not advertise. It did not need to. Desperation had excellent hearing, and doors that were shy always opened for the owed.

Dominic brought collateral. That was the first rule, and he'd spent the three days assembling it: his mother's ring, which was worth nothing and everything; the deed to a boat he no longer owned, honestly transferred, honestly worthless; and the thing that mattered — a ledger page, torn out with permission, proving that he, Dominic Vael, junior archivist of the guild, had once located a lost heirloom for a countess and had been paid in favors rather than coin. Favors accrued interest below. The court liked paper. Paper was patient.

He told himself he was going down to negotiate. To haggle. Archivists haggled; it was practically breathing. A pardon for a scapegoat couldn't cost more than — he ran figures in his head the whole way down the salt galleries, figures that got worse with every turning — more than his savings, more than the boat, more than ten years of favor-debt called in all at once.

The ushers met him at the third gate. They were polite in a way that made his skin crawl, the way furniture is polite, the way a well-run waiting room is polite. One of them carried his collateral on a velvet cushion, as if it were a canapé.

"Master Vael," the usher said, though Dominic had never given his name. "The magistrate will see you. You're expected."

"I've never been here before."

"No," the usher agreed pleasantly, and opened the door.

* * *

The magistrate's chamber smelled of old paper and lamp oil and, faintly, of tea — which unsettled him more than anything else, because it was such a human smell for so deep a place. The room was round and low-ceilinged, grown rather than built, the walls ribbed pale stone like the inside of a shell. Books lined every curve of it, shelved floor to vault, and the shelves went up farther than the lamplight reached. Somewhere above the visible dark, pages turned. No footsteps accompanied the sound.

The magistrate sat at a desk of black wood, small and neat, with a cup of tea steaming at her elbow. She looked like someone's grandmother. Silver hair pinned with two plain combs. Spectacles on a chain. Hands folded on a ledger open to a blank page.

"Sit down, Master Vael," she said, and gestured to the chair across from her, and the chair was warm, as if someone had just left it, as if many someones had just left it, one after another, for a hundred years.

"You know why I've come," Dominic said.

"I know why you've come." She poured a second cup without asking and pushed it toward him. "You've come to buy a girl's life with whatever you're carrying in that coat, and you've come believing you'll talk me down to a price you can afford. Would you like me to spare us both the performance? You can't. Nothing you own covers it."

His stomach performed a slow, professional collapse. "Then I'll leave—"

"You won't." She smiled over her spectacles, and it was a kind smile, which was the worst thing in the room. "You'll sit and drink your tea, because you have nowhere else to go with it, and we both know you did the arithmetic on the stairs. Sit."

He sat. He drank the tea. It was excellent, and he hated it.

"Now." The magistrate turned her ledger around so he could see it. On the blank page, in ink that gleamed wet and fresh, was written: *Lena Hartwell — condemnation commuted, sentence voided, record expunged.* Beneath it, a line waited empty, labeled simply: *Consideration.*

"The crown wants a guilty party," the magistrate said. "It has one. I can make her innocent — truly innocent, retroactively innocent, so clean that the trial becomes a clerical error and the clerks themselves forget holding it. The king lives, the poisoner goes uncaught, and the girl walks out of your guildhall in a week wondering why she feels like she's been given back something she never missed. That is the service. Here is the price."

She tapped the Consideration line.

"Not money," Dominic said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "You said nothing I own covers it."

"Money never covers it. Money is just the currency people prefer because it's cheap." She removed her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, unhurried, giving him time to understand that the polishing was not for the spectacles. "The court trades in weight, Master Vael. Equal weight for equal weight. A life weighs—" she paused, weighing it, professionally, "—considerably. What do you have that heavy?"

"I don't know," he said. "Tell me what you buy."

"We buy futures, usually. Ten years of a man's tomorrows makes a fine payment, and he barely notices losing them — days are slippery things. We buy names; a man without a name can be rewritten by anyone who holds his paperwork, which suits some people wonderfully. And we buy souls." She said it the way a fishmonger says *haddock*. "Souls are the heaviest coin in circulation. Not because of any theological nonsense — I leave theology to the chapels upstairs. A soul is heavy because a soul is *memory*. Everything you are is a story you tell yourself, and the soul is the telling. Take the telling, take the self. Very clean. Very final. And very much against guild policy, I'm told, which is why the guild sends no one, and yet here you are."

The lamplight hummed. Somewhere overhead, a page turned.

"What does a soul weigh?" Dominic asked. "In trade terms."

"For a full pardon, record expunged, poisoner's trail cold forever?" The magistrate consulted nothing, needed to consult nothing. "All of it. The whole account."

"And what's left?"

"That depends entirely on what you ask me to leave."

* * *

Here is the thing about Dominic Vael that only two people ever learned, and one of them was dead: he had already watched memory get taken.

His mother, Maren Vael, had sold her memories when he was sixteen — not to the court, to a flesh-and-blood creditor above ground, a charm-broker off Salt Row who dealt in gentler thefts. She'd sold them in installments, the way the poor sell everything: first the bad years, then the indifferent ones, keeping the good ones longest because the broker paid best for joy. Dominic had watched her sell the winter his father died and feel *relief*. Watched her sell her wedding day and laugh about it, genuinely laugh, saying *it must have rained, I remember rain, why do I remember rain if it was lovely?* Watched her, in the last month, look at her son with perfect politeness and say, "You have kind eyes. Do I know your mother?"

He had answered, "I'll take you to her," because it was easier than the truth, and she'd smiled and said, "She must be proud of you."

That was what memory cost. Not the forgetting — the forgetting was painless, the broker saw to that, the forgetting came with warm tea and a comfortable chair. What it cost was everyone around you, holding the whole story alone.

So when the magistrate slid the pen across the desk, Dominic did not ask what would be left. He already knew the shape of the empty rooms. He signed knowing exactly what memory costs, and he signed anyway, because eleven minutes of trial had shown him a woman who corrected her own murderers with the precision of a knife laid flat, and he had thought — with the arrogance of twenty-three, with the arithmetic of a man who believes he can see the whole ledger —

*I'll remember her. Someone has to hold the story. I'll hold it, and she'll never owe me, and that's the fair version.*

The pen was cold. The ink was red-black, like wine held up to no light.

"One amendment," the magistrate said, when he'd finished the signature — his own true name, in his own true hand, the hand she would keep longer than he would. "A standard clause. Collection occurs upon the date specified in prophecy. The court collects on schedule; it keeps better books than grief does." She turned the ledger so he could read the date, and the date meant nothing to him, a day ten years distant, abstract as weather. "Until then, Master Vael, the consideration remains yours. Live richly. Remember thoroughly. Memory appreciates, right up until it doesn't."

"And her?" His voice cracked on the single syllable and he pretended it hadn't. "She'll never know?"

"She'll never know," the magistrate agreed, "unless you tell her. Which you won't." She gathered his collateral onto the cushion, the worthless ring, the worthless deed, the worth-something favor-page, and set them all aside as if clearing the table for the actual meal. "Not out of cowardice. Out of protection. I see it in the signatories, year after year — they always think they're protecting someone. It's the most common lie buyers tell themselves." Her eyes, behind the spectacles, were not unkind. That was somehow the cruelest thing of all. "You may be right, of course. Occasionally someone is. Go home, boy. Sleep. Tomorrow you start carrying the whole story alone, and I promise you — I promise — it gets heavier every year, and lighter all at once, the day we collect."

* * *

The pardon arrived at the guildhall four days later as a writ with six seals, and the trial became a clerical error, and the clerks forgot holding it, and the king's soup scandal dissolved into rumor, and the real poisoner — a pantry steward bribed by a merchant house, though no one ever proved it — retired comfortably to the coast.

Lena Hartwell came back to work.

Dominic watched her shelve folios and balance ledgers and taste the king's supper with her precise, unsentimental tongue, and she did not know — could not know — that her life had been purchased at a pawnshop under the mountain, and that the pawn ticket was a soul, and that the soul was his, and that the collection date circled in century-old prophecy was coming for him the way tide comes for a sandcastle: slowly, patiently, on a schedule better kept than grief.

He told himself the secrecy was protection. He told himself it every year, on the anniversary, over cold tea at the ledger desk while she complained about the guild's filing system and he teased her about never writing herself into any record. *If she knew, she'd try to fix it,* he told himself. *She'd go down there with her lists and her equal weights and she'd pay something worse. This way she loses nothing. This way the whole story stays mine to carry.*

He was an archivist. Carrying the whole story was, he told himself, the one thing he was built for.

Ten years later, standing in the vault with the contract trembling in his hands and her footsteps approaching along the corridor — quick, precise footsteps, the walk of a woman who had found something in the ledgers that refused to balance — Dominic Vael folded the contract closed and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly which lie the magistrate had watched him tell himself from the beginning.

The door to the vault opened behind him.
