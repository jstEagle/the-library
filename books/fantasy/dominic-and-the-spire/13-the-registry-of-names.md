## 13. The Registry of Names

Wren had drawn maps for people who intended to die on them. That was the trade — a debtor-spy's cartography was never neutral; every line was a promise made to someone desperate enough to believe it. So when they unrolled the salt-stiff paper across the debtors' table and pinned its corners with a teacup, a bottle of last words, Odile's penknife, and Dominic's ring, they felt the old weight settle into their shoulders like a coat that had never been returned.

"Before anyone gets sentimental," Wren said, "understand what this is. This is not the mountain. The mountain is just where it lives."

The guildhall's back room was crowded past comfort. Lena sat nearest the lamp, because she always did, her ledger open to a fresh page already ruled into columns. Odile perched on the windowsill with her boots hooked on the frame, as though she might need to leave through the glass at speed — a habit, Wren suspected, from years of court calligraphy done under eyes that punished errors in ways ink could not erase. Dominic leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had been invited to his own funeral and found the flowers insufficient.

And at the far end of the table, silent as furniture, sat the old woman from the waiting room — no, not yet. Not here. Wren corrected themself: at the far end sat a chair nobody used, which was almost the same thing, and would be until they went down and came back.

"The Spire," Wren said, and touched the center of the diagram.

It looked wrong on paper, and Wren let it look wrong, because accuracy here required a kind of lie. Anyone drawing the Spire from memory drew a tower. A tower points up. But the Spire had never pointed up; it had grown down, the way teeth grow, the way roots grow toward something rotten or sweet. From the city above you saw only its crown — a needle of grey stone rising from the mountain's shoulder, thin enough that birds refused to land on it. Everything below, everything that mattered, descended into the dark in widening rings.

"It grows," Wren said. "That's the first thing people get wrong. It isn't built. It was seeded, centuries ago, and it has been growing ever since, downward, ring by ring. Every time someone signs away something the court considers valuable — a soul, a name, a voice, a shadow, forty years of Sundays — the Spire adds a chamber to hold the record of it." They tapped the outermost ring. "The oldest contracts are deepest. Some of those chambers haven't been opened since before the city had walls."

Lena's pen had stopped moving. "How many records?"

"No one counts. Counting is an act of acknowledgment, and acknowledgment is how things become precedent, and the court runs on precedent the way we run on tea." Wren glanced up. "But if you want a number that will keep you honest: the waiting room alone held four hundred souls when I left. Four hundred people sitting in velvet chairs, patient as heirlooms, waiting to be collected. Most of them sold cheaply, generations back, by ancestors who needed rain or revenge."

The lamp flame bent slightly, though there was no draft. Nobody remarked on it. In this guildhall, small impossibilities were permitted to pass unremarked, the way one permits a cat on the table.

* * *

Odile spoke first, because Odile always spoke first when the silence began to feel like sympathy. "Show us the registry. The actual heart, not the architecture lecture."

Wren turned the paper ninety degrees and drew three concentric lines inward with their thumbnail. "Here. The Registry of Names. It sits at the geometric center of the whole descent — not because the court loves symmetry, but because every contract, no matter how deep it's filed, must be legible from the heart. Think of it as the root. Everything above is growth; this is the point from which all of it was written."

They sketched as they talked: a vaulted chamber, round, its walls faced floor-to-ceiling in narrow drawers of pale wood — thousands upon thousands of them, each drawer holding a single leaf. And in the center, on a pedestal of black stone under a bell jar of glass so clear it seemed merely a suggestion, the master ledger itself.

"Every name the court holds is written twice," Wren said. "Once in a drawer, on its individual leaf — that's the working copy, the one clerks consult, annotate, transfer between departments. And once in the master ledger, which never leaves the pedestal. The master entry is the true one. Strike the leaf and the court still owns you. Strike the ledger line and the leaf goes blank on its own within the hour."

"And striking the ledger line," Odile said flatly, "is impossible."

"Nothing is impossible," said Dominic from the doorframe. "Some things are merely expensive. We're a guild of the distinction."

Wren gave him a look that had ended arguments in darker rooms than this. "The ledger can only be written by the hand that holds authority over the contract. For a soul-contract like yours, that means the magistrate, or the magistrate's seal acting through a clerk. There are exactly two other ways a line in the master ledger changes." They raised fingers. "One: the debt is paid in full, by equal weight, witnessed. Two: the entry is proven false — provenance broken. If the contract underlying the entry can be shown to be forged, coerced beyond legal bounds, or executed by an unauthorized party, the entry doesn't get struck. It gets *voided*, and voiding is worse than striking, from the court's perspective. A struck entry is a closed account. A voided entry is a scandal."

Lena looked up slowly. "You're saying forgery isn't just a way in. It might be the weapon."

"I'm saying," Wren said, "that I spent six years below learning their law so I could survive it, and their law has exactly one load-bearing wall: contracts hold. Which means contracts can also *break*, loudly, in front of everyone, if the flaw is real. The court fears nothing more than a bad document. Their entire world is paper. Bad paper is heresy."

Odile had gone very still on the windowsill — the stillness of a professional hearing her own trade named as blasphemy, and finding she didn't mind. "Then we don't steal your friend's page," she said. "We forge the *provenance*. We make Dominic's contract illegitimate after the fact. Retroactive fraud." She smiled the way a knife smiles. "I've never forged backward before. How novel."

"That's theft number two," Wren said. "But we're getting ahead. First theft first."

* * *

They gathered closer over the diagram, five heads — four heads, Wren corrected again, and hated the arithmetic — around a map of a place that ate people politely.

"To reach the registry," Wren said, "you descend. The public way is the Salt Stair, nine hundred steps cut through the mine workings, ending at the Door That Only Opens for the Owed. It reads you when you arrive. If you're owed anything — if the court holds a claim on you, however stale — it opens. If you owe nothing, it stays stone, and you can knock until your knuckles are bone."

"I've been owed before," Lena said quietly. "Everyone in this room has been owed something by somebody. Will it read debts generally? Or only theirs?"

"Only theirs. The court doesn't care about your landlord." Wren met her eyes. "Which brings us to the ugly part. None of you will be admitted. You have no entries. You are, legally speaking beneath the mountain, *nobody* — and the Door does not open for nobodies. It opens for the owed, and it opens for those carrying writs, credentials, or cargo consigned to a department below."

"So we go as paperwork," Odile said. "Credentials. I can forge credentials that would pass a bishop's inspection."

"They won't pass the second inspection," Wren said. "There are two checkpoints on the descent. The first is the Door itself — mechanical, if you like, a lock that tastes your legal existence. The second is the audit corridor, halfway down, where clerks verify writs against the registry index in real time. A forgery buys you past the Door. At the audit corridor, they compare your papers against the index of names currently expected below. Your name won't be on any list."

"Unless," Lena said, and Wren watched her mind do the thing it always did — turn the problem sideways until it lay flat — "the list is wrong. Or rather, unless the list is right about someone else." She pulled the diagram toward her. "Who's expected below right now? On the date the prophecy names?"

Wren felt the answer arrive in their stomach before their mouth. "Dominic. His collection clause comes due. He'll be expected at the registry heart, at midnight, for formal weighing. There'll be an escort detail, an usher, a clerk of transfers, and a line in the index reading *Sandoval, D. — conveyance to heart, hour of the bell.*"

Silence took the room the way water takes a lung.

"You want to send me down as myself," Dominic said at last, "as *cargo*."

"As the owed," Wren said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the plan. You walk in the front door because the front door exists specifically for you. No forged credential can be questioned, because you aren't carrying one — you're carrying a claim. The court's own machinery delivers you to the registry heart, which is where we need someone to be anyway. Meanwhile—" they tapped the audit corridor "—the rest come down behind you as a consignment. Odile forges a writ of transfer: documents being conveyed to the Department of Records for filing. Paper going to the paper place. Clerks wave paper through; it's the most natural traffic below. The writ carries three names as bearers. By the time anyone checks whether the Department of Records requested anything—"

"—we're already at the heart," Lena finished.

"Or already caught," said Dominic. "Because here's what none of you seem to grasp: I don't *want* to be delivered. I've been declining delivery for ten years. The entire point of my declining was that no one else pays my freight." He pushed off the doorframe and came to the table, and for a moment the lamplight caught the scar on his knuckles that Wren knew he'd earned signing his name. "Strike my entry. Fine. Void my contract. Wonderful. But if the price is one hair on any of your heads, I'll tear the plan apart with my hands."

"You'll do no such thing," said Odile, "because tearing plans apart is my prerogative, and because you signed a contract once without consulting anyone, and turnabout is the only justice this guild recognizes."

"That was different—"

"It was exactly the same," Lena said, not looking up from her columns, "and we settled this at the ledger desk, and we'll settle it again at the debtors' table, and apparently we'll settle it a third time now, so let me save us the evening: the argument is over. You lost. Sit down."

He sat. It was, Wren thought, the most intimate thing he'd done all night.

* * *

The details took another hour, and the hour behaved the way planning hours do — stretching, snagging, unraveling, being re-woven. Wren walked them through it ring by ring.

The Salt Stair first: nine hundred steps, lit at intervals by lamps that burned without oil — the court considered fuel an expense and darkness a courtesy. The stair passed through the old mine galleries, and Wren marked the two junctions where a person could step off the official route and vanish into the salt, if vanishing became necessary. It would become necessary. Plans were prayers with diagrams attached.

Then the audit corridor: a hall of green marble where clerks sat at brass desks behind low rails, checking writs with the unhurried thoroughness of people who had literally forever. Wren taught them the rhythm of inspection — the clerk reads the seal, then the bearer-list, then looks up and compares faces to names, then stamps. "The stamp is the danger moment. Everything before it is theater; the stamp is commitment. If Odile's writ survives the stamp, the corridor forgets you exist. Clerks below are forbidden to remember what passes their desks — memory is a liability in an institution built on records. It's the one mercy the place extends, and it's structural, not kind."

Then the rings themselves: the archive spirals, each deeper ring older and stranger. Wren described corridors where the drawers grew into the walls like honeycomb, where some leaves had been written so long ago the language had died and the souls in them were held in words no living mouth could pronounce. They described the ushers — grey-robed, faceless in the way that well-trained servants learn to be — and the sound of the place, which was the worst part: a vast, dry whisper, thousands of pages turning somewhere out of sight, always, eternally, the sound of the court reading itself.

And finally the heart. Wren drew it last and drew it smallest, because the truth of the heart was small: a round room, a pedestal, a book, a bell jar. Two clerks of the inner office, the only souls below permitted to touch the master ledger, and they touched it with tongs. The magistrate's presence was not constant but was always imminent; the room behaved accordingly.

"Two thefts, one night," Wren said, and laid it out plain, because they had promised themself they would say the whole shape of it aloud and let the room decide if it was madness.

"Theft one: Dominic's line in the master ledger. We void it — provenance broken, contract shown flawed. That requires evidence, which requires the original contract, which the court keeps filed in the third ring, Department of Origins. Odile examines it under pretext of filing, confirms the flaw or finds one worth manufacturing, and we present it. To whom? The magistrate, ideally. Voiding happens above the clerks' pay grade."

"Theft two: Odile's name. Confiscated eleven years ago when the court broke her for the Vellum Scandal — her name taken as surety against further crimes, filed like a hostage." Wren nodded at her. "Her leaf sits in a drawer in the second ring. Here's the cruelty of it: we cannot strike it, because her name isn't a debt — it's collateral, held not owed. Collateral returns only when the court releases it, or—" they paused "—or when the page is stolen whole. A leaf removed intact, with its wax seal unbroken, is presumed misfiled, not stolen. Misfilings happen constantly below. The court loses things the way rivers lose silt. If the page simply ceases to be findable, the collateral lapses within a season, unclaimed, and she walks out owning herself."

"A season," Odile said. "I'd rather walk out owning myself tonight."

"Tonight you walk out with the page in your coat. Ownership follows the paperwork. Paperwork takes a season to catch up. I'm sorry. The court moves at the speed of its own filing, and its filing is eternal."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then: "A season. Fine. I've waited eleven years; I can wait twelve months. But I want it noted that I'm doing this properly, and that proper is the hardest way."

"Noted," Lena said, writing it down, because she wrote everything down, and Wren understood suddenly that the ledgers weren't habit for her — they were armor. A thing recorded could be argued with later. A thing unrecorded simply happened to you.

* * *

It was Lena who saw the flaw, near midnight, when the lamp had been refilled twice and the teacup pinning the map's corner had gone cold.

She set down her pen and looked at the diagram for a long time — long enough that Wren, who had learned to read silences the way other people read weather, braced.

"There are two thefts," she said, "but only one distraction. When Dominic's weighing begins at the heart, every eye in the mountain turns to it — that's our cover, that's the whole architecture of the plan. But the weighing *is* the emergency. The moment his entry is contested, the court doesn't look away from the heart. It looks *harder*. We're counting on the ceremony to blind them at the exact moment it would mobilize them." She tapped the heart, then the second ring, then the heart again. "Someone has to pull the court's attention *away* during the critical minutes. Someone has to be loud somewhere else."

The room considered this, and the room's conclusion arrived in four separate silences, all pointing the same direction.

"I'll do it," Wren said, before anyone else could volunteer, because it was their map, their mountain, their six years below, and because a debtor knows best what a creditor's attention costs. "I know their procedures better than their clerks do. I'll file something — an objection, an audit challenge, a procedural appeal — something that forces a review, something that makes three departments argue with each other in writing. Nothing pulls a bureaucracy apart like a well-formed complaint. I'll be loud on paper while you're quiet in person."

"That puts you in their hands," Lena said.

"Wren," Dominic said, and his voice had changed — stripped of its performance, down to whatever lived underneath, "they offered you reinstatement once already. If they take you—"

"Then they take me," Wren said, "and I will be the single most obstructive employee in the history of the subterranean court. There are forms, Dominic. I will file *so many forms*." But their hand, resting on the map, had gone white at the knuckle, and everyone saw it, and everyone was polite enough to look elsewhere.

* * *

Near the end, when the candles had burned to stubs and the plan had been committed to memory by all parties — nothing written, nothing carried, nothing to confiscate — Odile asked the question that hadn't been asked yet.

"The prophecy," she said, turning her pen end over end. "The date. Tomorrow, now. The book named tonight before any of us knew what it meant. If the court wrote that prophecy—"

"The court didn't write it," Wren said. "That's the thing I keep circling and can't land. The court reads prophecy; it doesn't author it. Authorship implies power over events, and the court's whole theology is that it has none — only claims. Something *else* wrote that book. Something that knew the date, and the name, and—" they hesitated, because the next part had been bothering them for days, like a splinter worked under the skin "—and the handwriting. Has anyone actually confirmed the hand matches?"

Lena went still in a way Wren had seen once before, at the ledger desk, when the word *soul* first entered the argument.

"It matches," she said. "It's mine. I checked letter by letter. The pressure, the slant, the way I cross my sevens. It's mine, Wren, and it's a century old, and I don't know what to do with that except carry it."

Nobody spoke. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, attentive, as if it too were listening.

"Then we add it to the list," Odile said briskly, because briskness was her mercy. "Impossible things pending: void a soul-contract, steal a name, survive a weighing, and explain a book written in a dead woman's living hand. Any others? Excellent. Sleep. Tomorrow we rehearse."

They dispersed by ones and twos, the way conspirators do — never together, never goodbye. Dominic lingered at the table, looking down at the diagram of the place that owned him, and Wren watched him trace the route from the Door to the heart with one finger, slowly, like a man memorizing the road home to a house that had burned.

At the doorway he stopped. "Wren. The audit corridor. The clerks check faces against the index." He didn't turn around. "My face is going to be in that index by tomorrow night. Conveyance to heart. If they look at you too closely — if they remember your face from before —"

"Then I'll remind them," Wren said, "that remembering is against policy."

His laugh was short and had no joy in it, only admiration, which between the two of them amounted to the same thing. He went out into the dark of the guildhall, and his footsteps faded toward the archives, where a certain folio waited on a certain shelf with a certain date circled in century-old ink.

Wren stayed a moment longer, alone with the map. Then, carefully, they folded it along its old creases — the creases it had carried through six years and one defection — and slid it inside their coat, over the heart, where a spy keeps the only honest thing they own.

Tomorrow they would rehearse.

Tomorrow night, they would go down.

And somewhere below, in a room full of turning pages, Wren would have sworn the whisper changed pitch when they thought of the date — as if the mountain, too, had circled it in ink.
