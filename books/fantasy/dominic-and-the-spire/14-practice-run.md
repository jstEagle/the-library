## 14. Practice Run

The guildhall at midnight smelled of candlewax, old paper, and the particular staleness of rooms where too many people had argued too long about things that could not be argued. Lena had blown out most of the lamps, keeping only two, because Odile had insisted that the Spire's corridors were lit the way a miser lights his house — barely, and with malice — and if they were going to rehearse, they would rehearse in honest darkness.

Odile Vasse stood in the middle of the great room with a stick of chalk in her fingers the way a surgeon holds a blade. She had been a court calligrapher for nineteen years before the court took her name back, and it showed in everything: the way she held her spine, the way she measured distance with her eyes before committing her hand, the way she looked at the chalk-dust settling on the floorboards as though it were a personal insult she intended to correct.

"Again," she said.

"Again," Lena agreed, and picked up her own chalk.

They had been at this for four hours. The floor of the guildhall was now a map — or rather, a map's ghost, since Wren's diagrams of the under-mountain were drawn from memory and memory, Wren had warned them, was the least reliable currency the court dealt in. Chalk lines marked the salt galleries. Chalk boxes marked the checkpoints, the ushers' stations, the three doors Wren knew about and the fourth Wren suspected. In the center of it all, a chalk rectangle labeled in Odile's immaculate hand: REGISTRY ANNEX — DO NOT CHALK OVER THIS AGAIN, LENA.

Lena had chalked over it twice. It had felt necessary both times.

"Walk it," Odile said. "From the top. You are a courier with credentials. I am your hired scribe. We are bored, we are cold, we are unremarkable. Say the line."

Lena squared her shoulders and produced, from somewhere in her chest, the flat, faintly put-upon voice of a woman who had carried documents underground for a decade and hated every stair. "Registry delivery, annex four. It's overdue and it's not my fault, and if you make me wait until the second bell I'm filing a complaint with whoever files complaints."

Odile's mouth twitched. "Better. Last time you sounded like a duchess apologizing to a footman. No one apologizes underground. Apology is a surface vice." She gestured down the chalk corridor. "First checkpoint. What do they ask?"

"Name."

"Give it?"

"The forged one. Marta Vell." Lena had been drilling the name until it sat in her mouth like her own. "They ask for the countersignature, I show the seal, Odile — *you* — sigh about your hands being cold, and we move on."

"And if they ask the question Wren says the clever ones ask?"

Lena hesitated. This was the hinge, the place the rehearsal kept breaking. "'What is the weight of the document?'"

"And the answer?"

"Whatever it weighs," Lena said. "Literally. You weigh it in your hands, you frown, you say a number under a pound. Because no one underground trusts a document that claims importance, but everyone trusts a document that claims to be heavy with ink and nothing else."

"Because?"

"Because the court believes everything has a weight," Lena said, "and it finds honesty about small weights more convincing than lies about large ones."

Odile nodded slowly, and for a moment her professional mask slipped and something like respect showed through. "You'd have survived down there," she said. "That's not a compliment I hand out."

* * *

From Odile's side of the room, the rehearsal looked different than it did from Lena's. Odile watched the way she read manuscripts — not for what was written but for what had been altered, the tremor in a stroke, the ink laid over ink. And what she saw, watching Lena Hartwell walk a chalk corridor at midnight, was a woman performing a role she had been rehearsing her whole life without knowing it.

The taster. Of course. Lena had spent years training her body to accept small deaths and name them precisely. Odile had spent years training her hand to commit small forgeries and deny them beautifully. They were, Odile reflected, the same profession with different instruments.

The trouble was the third member of their party, who was not present, and whose absence sat in the room like a fourth person.

"Again," Odile said, "and this time we use the luck."

Lena grimaced. "Do we have to?"

"You want to walk into the Spire without a rehearsal that includes failure? The Spire will provide failure whether we practice it or not. Better it happens here, where the worst consequence is chalk on my boots." Odile crossed to the stockroom door and unlocked it with the guild's second key — the first key was Dominic's, and Dominic had not been invited tonight, because Dominic had made it abundantly clear at the debtors' table that he did not wish to be rescued, and Lena had made it equally clear that his wishes on this subject were noted, filed, and disregarded.

The stockroom of the Guild of Impossible Things was organized with a librarian's madness. Bottled last words, sorted by bitterness. Borrowed luck in graduated vintages — the good luck was old, the great luck was older, and the legendary luck was kept in a locked case because it had a tendency to curdle into catastrophe if handled by the undeserving. Odile took down a mid-shelf bottle, the kind a gambler might pawn, and held it to the lamp. The liquid inside moved against the tilt, slow and golden and reluctant.

"Half a dose each," she said. "Enough to smooth a stumble, not enough to make us brave. Bravery down there would be fatal."

They drank. It tasted like the moment before a coin lands — that suspended, weightless instant stretched over the tongue. Lena felt the room sharpen. The candle flames stood very still.

"Again," Odile said.

They walked it again, and this time it went wrong immediately and comprehensively. Lena tripped over a chalk line that had not been there a moment before — or had it? — and knocked over the chair that stood in for the first usher, and the chair's legs caught Odile's ankle, and Odile went down with a curse in three languages, two of which Lena suspected she had invented, and the bottle of borrowed luck, which had been sitting on the ledger desk, chose that precise moment to roll off the edge and shatter, dousing the chalk map in secondhand fortune.

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then the candle nearest the window guttered, righted itself, and the draft that had caused it — a draft from a window that was shut — died away. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse stopped running. The luck, such as it was, settled over the room like snow.

"Well," Odile said from the floor. "That was the rehearsal."

"That was a catastrophe."

"That was a rehearsal *of* a catastrophe, which is the only kind worth having." She pulled herself up, inspected her ankle, and pronounced it bruised but functional. "Note the lessons. One: we do not carry open luck. Two: the chair stays where the chair is, and we plan around the chair, because there is always a chair. Three—" she paused, looking down at the luck soaking into the floorboards, directly into the chalk rectangle that read DO NOT CHALK OVER THIS AGAIN, "—three, we may have just made the guildhall floor the luckiest surface in the city, and I want it noted that this is your fault."

"My fault?"

"You chalked over my lettering. The luck knew. Luck is very literate."

Lena laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her, the first in days — and the sound did something to the room's weight. Odile watched it happen and thought: *there it is.* The thing Dominic had sold a soul for. Not the taster's tongue, not the ledger-keeper's precision. The laugh. Though of course Odile did not know exactly what Dominic had loved; she only knew what she herself was beginning to, reluctantly, against all her trained cynicism, love: the specific quality of this woman's attention, which made even a chalk floor feel like a document worth signing.

"Again," Lena said, wiping her eyes. "From the top."

* * *

It almost worked, the fifth time.

That was the terrible part, Lena thought afterward. The first four failures had been almost comfortable — tripping, cursing, the chair, the luck, the comedy of two competent women reduced to floundering in candlelight. But the fifth time, the corridor assembled itself around them. The forged credentials passed under Odile's steady hand. The countersignature held. The question about weight came and was answered — *under a pound, mostly ink* — and the imaginary usher, a chalk outline with a chalk frown, let them through. They reached the annex rectangle. They stood before it. And for three full seconds the guildhall was not the guildhall; it was the under-mountain, cold and vast, and the rectangle on the floor was a door, and behind the door was a registry where names were kept like coins in a vault, and one of those names was Dominic's, and the page it was written on could be struck, or stolen, or — if Odile's art was equal to the night — rewritten.

Then Odile said, quietly, "And what do we do when it works?" and the spell broke, because neither of them had an answer for that. Success was the one scene they hadn't rehearsed.

They stood in the chalk annex, breathing, and the candles had burned down to stubs, and the hour was that dangerous hour past midnight when the body is tired enough to tell the truth.

Odile began gathering the chalk sticks, not because they needed gathering but because her hands wanted occupation. And she asked it the way she did everything — with the precision of someone who had forged ten thousand signatures and knew that the dangerous line in any document is the one written in haste.

"Why the memories?"

Lena didn't pretend not to understand. "You know why. Equal weight. Nothing else I own weighs enough. The taster's immunity — the court priced it once, it's not enough. My guild membership — sentimental value only, the court doesn't trade in sentiment. My memories of him—" She stopped. Started again, in her ledger voice. "The court's own assessors valued what Dominic purchased with his soul. A life. Ten years of a life, mine. The only asset I hold of comparable weight is ten years of him."

"That's the accounting," Odile said. "I asked why."

The chalk dust hung in the lamplight. Somewhere above them, the city slept, and above the city the king's poisoner was presumably awake, and above everything the date was coming down the mountain toward them like weather.

"I don't—" Lena began, and found the sentence had no floor under it.

She was, by training and by nature, a woman who could name any poison by its aftertaste, who could balance a ledger in the dark, who had once identified seven frauds in a single page of guild accounts before breakfast. She was not a woman who lacked answers. And yet here, in the space where the answer should have been, there was only a blank — not an empty blank but a *full* one, the way page 412 was blank, the way certain documents are blank because everything they have to say is too heavy for ink.

"I would like to say it's duty," she said at last. "He bought my life; I'm repaying the debt; the guild honors its accounts. That's the version I'd file."

"And the true version?"

"The true version is that I have tried to write it down four times." Her voice had gone quiet, and steady, the way it went steady right before she swallowed something that might kill her. "For the ledger. For myself. *I am doing this because—* And every time, the sentence stops. Not because I don't know. Because everything I know about it is the same word wearing different clothes, and the word is—" She looked at the chalk rectangle, at the door that stood for the place where his name was kept, and finished, simply, because there was no other word and there never had been:

"Love. The answer is love. It was always going to be love. I just thought I'd have something cleverer to say when I finally admitted it."

Odile set down the chalk she was holding. For a long moment she said nothing, which from Odile was a eulogy, a coronation, and a standing ovation all at once. Then: "For what it's worth — and my opinion has been confiscated twice, so it isn't worth much — I have forged love letters for ambassadors and kings, and I can tell you that no one has ever written the true version, because the true version is always the same word, and kings would rather lose the war."

Lena laughed again, softer this time. Then she crouched, and with the last of the chalk, beside the annex rectangle, she drew one small line — a tally mark, a taster's mark, the kind she chalked on the king's supper trays to mean *tested, safe, proceed.*

"Tomorrow," she said, "Wren brings the final route. And then we stop rehearsing."

She did not know, as she said it, that Wren was at that very hour standing in a velvet corridor beneath the mountain, being offered a clean and reasonable price. She did not know that loyalty was about to arrive at the guildhall carrying a stone in each hand. She only knew that the chalk map lay finished on the floor, that the luck had soaked into the boards, and that somewhere in the dark above the city, the date kept coming.
