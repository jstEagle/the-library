## 15. The Magistrate's Offer

The summons came the way Wren had always known it would: folded into the seam of a salt shipment, written on paper the color of old teeth, in a hand so beautiful it felt like being touched.

*The Magistrate requests the pleasure of your return. Bring nothing. Owe nothing new. Come as you were.*

Wren read it three times in the dark of the counting room, by the light of a lamp turned down to a coal, because that was how you read things you did not want to exist. The words did not change. The paper did not crumble. The wax seal — a scale, balanced, rendered in green wax that smelled faintly of lilies — sat unbroken and patient as a closed eye.

They had been on the surface for eleven days. Eleven days of honest air and honest bread and the guildhall's crooked stairs, and in all that time Wren had almost managed to forget that the court beneath the mountain did not forget. It kept accounts the way glaciers kept valleys: slowly, totally, without malice and without mercy.

*Come as you were.*

Wren folded the letter along its original creases, slid it into the lining of a coat they no longer wore, and went to find out what time the salt carts left for the mines.

* * *

The descent was familiar in the way a scar is familiar. Wren had made it forty times as a spy for the court, carrying maps and names and small betrayals wrapped in oilcloth, and had made it exactly once in the other direction, carrying nothing but the decision to stop. The tunnels narrowed as they went down. The air thickened. Somewhere past the third checkpoint the rock stopped being rock and started being architecture — walls dressed smooth, corridors hung with lamps that burned without oil, and everywhere the quiet, courteous hum of a place that was busy being inevitable.

The ushers met Wren at the last gate, as they always had. Two of them, gray-robed, faces like well-kept furniture. Neither reached for a weapon. Neither needed to. One simply inclined his head and said, "The Magistrate is pleased you came," and Wren felt the old machinery of obedience try to turn over in their chest, gears rusted but recognizable.

"I came because I was asked," Wren said. "That's not the same as pleased."

"Everything is the same as everything, down here," the usher said pleasantly. "It's one of the advantages."

They walked. The corridors of the court were not cruel — that was the thing surface-dwellers never understood, the thing Wren had tried and failed to explain to Odile Vasse over a stolen bottle of borrowed luck. The court was not a dungeon. It was a waiting room the size of a country. Everything in it was polite. The lamps were polite. The doors opened before you knocked. The very stone seemed to apologize for the inconvenience of existing beneath you. And underneath all that courtesy, like water under ice, ran the simple understanding that you would do what was asked, because what was asked was always reasonable, and because the alternative was to be unreasonable in a place that had all the time in the world to reason with you.

The Magistrate's chamber was at the heart of the oldest gallery, a room Wren had entered perhaps six times in a decade of service, because the Magistrate did not waste audiences on small matters. The door was oak, which was ostentatious down here — everything else was salt and slate — and it swung open without a sound.

The Magistrate sat behind a desk that was also a scale. That was not a metaphor. The desk had two pans, one at each end, and on the left pan sat a stack of contracts tied with green ribbon, and on the right pan sat nothing at all, and the whole apparatus hung in perfect, insulting balance. The Magistrate themselves was small. That was the other thing no one believed. A person of no particular height, wrapped in gray, with a face like a question that had been asked so many times it had stopped expecting answers. Their eyes were the color of the lamps.

"Wren," the Magistrate said, and their voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was always warm. "Sit. Would you like tea? It's the same tea they drink up there. We are not monsters."

"I know what you are," Wren said, and sat, because refusing the chair would have been a statement, and statements were currency, and Wren had come to spend nothing.

"Good. That's why I sent for you and not someone else. You know what we are, and you stayed eleven days anyway." The Magistrate poured the tea themselves — two cups, no ceremony, steam rising between them like a small ghost. "Eleven days. You could have run. There are ports. There are mountains with no mines under them. You didn't run. Why?"

Wren took the cup. It was hot and honest and tasted exactly like the tea Lena Hartwell made in the guildhall kitchen, which meant the court had sources everywhere, or the court had taste, or both. "Because running makes you guilty," Wren said. "I'm not guilty. I quit. Quitting is legal."

"Is it?" The Magistrate smiled, and it was not a cruel smile, which somehow made it worse. "Let's find out. Tell me about your eleven days. Not the spying — I don't care about the spying, you were terrible at it, you always were, that's why I liked you. Tell me about the people."

Wren drank the tea instead of answering. It bought four seconds.

"You've fallen in with them," the Magistrate continued, gently, as if Wren had confirmed something pleasant. "The forger. The taster. The smuggler-archivist." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Dominic."

"There it is," Wren said.

"There it is," the Magistrate agreed. They rose, and walked to the desk-scale, and rested one small hand on the empty pan. "Wren. I want to tell you a story, and I want you to listen to it the way you used to listen to everything: as a professional. Not as a friend. As a professional."

The lamps hummed. Somewhere far off, in the endless polite corridors, a clerk's pen scratched.

"A hundred years ago," the Magistrate said, "a woman sold her voice to us to buy her son a pardon. The contract was clean. The voice was weighed, priced, and filed. Forty years later her granddaughter came down and asked us to give it back, and we said: bring something of equal weight. She brought her granddaughter's future — a whole unwritten life, weighed and priced and filed. Clean. Sixty years after that, a man sold his mother's name to buy a boat, and the boat sank, and he came back to complain, and we showed him his signature, and he wept, and we let him weep, because weeping is free." The Magistrate turned. "Do you know what happened to the granddaughter? The one who paid her future?"

"No," Wren said, though they suspected.

"Nothing happened to her. That's the point. She grew old down here, in a chair, in a corridor, waiting for a future she no longer owned. She is in the Waiting Room of the Owed at this moment. She has been there sixty years. She is patient. She is *furniture*, Wren, because that is what a contract makes of a person when it is honored." The Magistrate's voice did not rise. It never rose. "And do you know why I am telling you this? Not to frighten you. To show you what we are *for*. Every contract we hold is a wall in a dam. Tear one out — just one, one soul, one signature, one Dominic — and the water asks why the others are still standing. And water, Wren, is very persuasive."

"You're saying you can't let him go."

"I'm saying the opposite of what you think I'm saying." The Magistrate came around the desk and sat on its edge, close, familiar, the posture of a superior about to offer a promotion. "I am saying I would *prefer* not to collect him. Collection is work. Paperwork, escorts, weighing, filing. I have been doing this for two hundred years and I am tired in ways you cannot imagine. If there were a way to void the contract cleanly — legally, precedentially, with the dam intact — I would take it in a heartbeat."

Wren looked at the empty pan of the scale. "There isn't."

"There is." The Magistrate let the silence do a full turn of the room. "Equal weight. That is the only law we have, and it is a good law, and I will not bend it. But weight is not the same as *identity*. The contract says: one soul, belonging to Dominic, due on the prophesied date. It does not say the weight must be *his* soul. It says the weight must be equal." The small gray hands folded. "And we have weighed, over the centuries, nearly everything. And do you know what we have found weighs the same as a soul? Almost nothing. Almost nothing in this world. But not quite nothing."

The tea had gone cold in Wren's hands. They hadn't noticed drinking it. "Say it."

"Lena Hartwell," the Magistrate said, "is preparing to pay for him with her memories of him. She has begun listing them. She has begun circling. We have sources, Wren; you know we have sources; the guildhall leaks like a basket." The Magistrate's voice remained warm, remained kind, remained the voice of someone explaining arithmetic to a child. "And her memories of him — ten years of them, freely given, freely surrendered — weigh, on our scales, *almost* exactly what his soul weighs. Almost. Within tolerance. It would be the cleanest voiding in a century. No dam broken. No precedent harmed. One woman loses a decade of love, one man keeps his soul, the court keeps its law, and everyone goes home."

"That's her choice to make," Wren said. "Not yours. Not mine."

"Entirely," the Magistrate agreed. "If she comes down and offers it, we will accept, and the scales will sing, and I will sleep well for the first time in a decade. But she won't come down alone, will she? She'll bring the forger. She'll bring you. And the forger will try to forge, and you will try to *Wren* your way through my corridors, and somewhere in all that noble noise the clean transaction will become a theft, and a theft voids nothing — it merely converts a debt into a grudge, and grudges, Wren, accrue interest." The Magistrate leaned forward. "So here is my offer. Not a threat. An offer, with a clean price, because I know you respect clean prices."

The lamps hummed. The empty pan swayed, a fraction, and stilled.

"Deliver her to me before the date. Not harmed. Not tricked — *delivered*, walking, knowing, having chosen, or at least having been given every chance to choose. Bring her to the registry heart on the appointed night and place her in front of the scales, and let her make the trade she is already planning to make, without the forger's forgeries and without the smuggler's heroics muddying the weight. Do that, and I will tear up your resignation. Reinstatement. Full standing. Your old rooms, your old salary, your old name in the court's rolls — unburned, unmarked. And—" the Magistrate held up one finger, and the finger was the whole world for a moment, "—Dominic's contract, torn. Not voided by her sacrifice alone. Torn by *me*, as a fee for services rendered. He walks free either way. She pays either way. The only question is whether the paying happens cleanly, or in a riot of forgery and broken stone that ends with all of you filed in the Waiting Room beside the granddaughter."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will assume you have chosen your new family, and I will respect it, and I will do what courts do when contracts are about to be stolen: I will prepare. And Wren — I am very good at preparing. I have had a hundred years to prepare for this exact date. The prophecy is not a surprise to me. It is an *appointment*."

Wren set down the cold cup. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; inside, something was being weighed on scales of its own, and they could hear the pans creak.

"Three days," Wren said.

The Magistrate raised an eyebrow — the first genuine expression of the evening.

"I don't decide things in one sitting. You taught me that. You said a spy who decides fast is a spy who decides for someone else." Wren stood. "Give me three days. I'll come back up to the surface, I'll look at them — at her — and I'll give you an answer on the second night before the date. Clean. No tricks. You have my word, and my word used to be worth something down here."

The Magistrate studied them for a long moment, and Wren had the uncomfortable sense of being read the way Odile read handwriting — every stroke, every hesitation, every place where the pen had pressed too hard.

"Three days," the Magistrate said at last. "Not because I doubt you. Because I remember being you." They extended the small gray hand, and Wren shook it, and the handshake was dry and light and final as a signature. "The ushers will show you out. Wren — one more thing."

Wren paused at the door.

"She is going to pay regardless. You know that. I know that. *She* knows that — she's been circling the word *memories* in her own hand for a week. The only variable left in this equation is whether it happens with dignity or without it. Think about which one you'd want, if it were your decade being weighed."

Wren did not answer. The door closed behind them without a sound, and the polite corridors folded around them like hands.

* * *

The surface, when Wren came up into it, was unbearable.

That was the truth of it, the thing they could not have said to the Magistrate and could not now say to anyone: the surface was unbearable, because the surface was *specific*. Down below, everything was gray and general and easy. Up here the evening light came through the guildhall windows the color of honey, and it lay across the ledger desk in a long warm bar, and Lena Hartwell was sitting in it with her sleeves rolled up, arguing with Odile Vasse about the correct way to forge a customs seal, and Dominic was on the stairs eating an apple he had absolutely stolen from the kitchen, and the whole scene was so precisely, so *irreplaceably* itself that Wren stood in the doorway with the Magistrate's offer sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone and thought: *this is the thing they want me to weigh. Not her memories. This.*

"—and I'm telling you, the clerk in the customs house presses too hard on the downstroke, he's been doing it for twenty years, if you give me a light hand he'll know it's wrong *because it's better than his*," Odile was saying, her ink-stained fingers sketching a letterform in the air.

"So make it worse," Dominic said, around the apple. "Forge his mistakes. That's the trick, isn't it? Forgery isn't making a perfect thing. It's making a perfectly *flawed* thing."

"It's adorable that you think you understand my craft."

"I understand *smuggling*, and it's the same craft with worse handwriting."

Lena laughed — a short, real laugh, the kind Wren had learned, over eleven days, to catalog the way they'd once cataloged guard rotations — and then she saw Wren in the doorway, and the laugh changed. Not died. Changed. Lena Hartwell had a taster's tongue and a ledger-keeper's eye, and she read faces the way she read aftertastes: three poisons, named by their traces.

"You've been down there," she said. Not a question.

"Salt shipment had a seam in it," Wren said. "Seams get letters."

Odile set down her pen. Dominic stopped on the stairs, apple halfway to his mouth, and for a moment the guildhall was so quiet Wren could hear the lamp burning.

"Well?" Odile said.

And here was the moment. Here was the clean, reasonable price, sitting on the scale. Wren could lie — they were good at it, they had been *trained* in it, and the training did not wear off like a resignation did. They could say the Magistrate had threatened them, which was even true, in the way that all the Magistrate's kindnesses were threats. They could buy three days of clean conscience with a small forgery of their own, and spend those three days deciding in private, and no one would ever know.

But the Magistrate had said *delivered, knowing*, and had said it about Lena, and the words had gone into Wren like a splinter, because Wren had understood, standing in that warm terrible chamber, that the Magistrate had not been describing a betrayal. The Magistrate had been describing the truth: that Lena was going to pay regardless, and the only question was the manner of the paying. And if Wren lied to her now, then whatever happened on the appointed night, Wren would be one more thing in the weighing — one more small dishonest weight on a pan that needed to be clean.

"I was offered a job," Wren said.

Dominic came down two more stairs. "What kind of job."

"Reinstatement. My old name. My old rooms." Wren crossed the room and sat down at the ledger desk, because their legs had decided the matter without consulting them. "And Dominic's contract, torn up by the Magistrate's own hand. Free. Clean. Precedent intact."

The silence rearranged itself.

"And the price," Lena said quietly. She was not stupid. She had never once been stupid. "What did the scales want in exchange?"

Wren looked at her. At the honey-colored light. At the ink on her fingers and the ledger open in front of her with its neat columns of impossible things, and — there, in the margin of the current page, half-hidden under her wrist — a small circled word she thought no one had seen. *Memories.*

"You," Wren said. "Delivered. Knowing. To the registry heart, on the date, so the trade can happen cleanly, without forgeries, without heroics, without any of us ending up filed in the waiting room. That was the offer. Three days to decide. I bought three days."

Odile swore, softly, comprehensively, in court French.

Dominic set the apple down on the stairs with great care, as if it might go off. "And what did you say?"

"I said I'd think." Wren's voice came out lower than they intended. "And then I came up here, and I looked through that window, and I saw the four of us in one room, and I understood that the Magistrate knew exactly what they were doing when they set the price. Because it's not a price, is it? It's a *mirror*. They're asking me which pan I stand on. And the horrible thing — the genuinely horrible thing —" Wren's hands were flat on the ledger desk now, pressing down as if the room might tip, "— is that the offer is *reasonable*. That's the trap. It's clean. It's fair. She pays what she already intends to pay. He goes free either way. Nobody bleeds. All I have to do is make sure it happens without a riot, and I get my whole life back as a tip."

"Then why are your hands shaking?" Lena asked.

Wren looked down. They were. Slightly. The taster's eye, the ledger-keeper's precision, missing nothing, as always.

"Because," Wren said, "I spent ten years learning that the court is always reasonable, and I spent eleven days learning that you people are not. And I don't yet know which one I want to be when I grow up." They straightened. "Three days. I bought us three days, and I'm spending them honestly: I don't know what I'm going to do. But I'll tell you this for free, Magistrate's training, no charge — she's right about the customs clerk. Odile, forge his mistakes. And Dominic—"

Dominic waited, apple forgotten, face carefully, deliberately blank in the way of a man who had already sold something once and knew exactly what the scales felt like.

"—the Magistrate said she's been circling the word *memories* in her own hand for a week," Wren said. "So before we spend those three days arguing about me, maybe the two of you should spend them arguing about that. Because I don't think the offer was really for me at all. I think I was just the delivery route." They stood, legs steady now, the stone still sitting in their chest, heavy, clean, and reasonable. "And I think the Magistrate already knows what she's going to choose."

Lena's hand had moved, very quietly, to cover the circled word in the margin.

Outside, the first rain of the season came down the mountain, and somewhere beneath them — patient, polite, precise — the court began, with a hundred years of practice, to prepare.
