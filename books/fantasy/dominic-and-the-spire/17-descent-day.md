## 17. Descent Day

The date arrived the way debts do: quietly, and then all at once.

Lena woke before dawn in her narrow room above the guildhall, as she had every morning for ten years, and lay still for a long moment with her eyes on the ceiling, waiting to feel different. She had imagined this day so many times in the last month — imagined waking with her heart hammering, with some animal certainty pressing down on her chest like a hand. But there was nothing. Only the gray light seeping through the shutter slats, only the smell of salt dust and old paper that lived in the walls of the guildhall the way damp lived in the walls of everything else in this city.

Only a ledger page folded into quarters on her desk, where she had left it, with tomorrow's date circled in ink that was a century old and a handwriting that was entirely hers.

*Today,* she thought, correcting herself. *Today's date.*

She got up, dressed, and did the thing she always did first, because precision was a kind of prayer and she had never learned another: she counted. Six vials of antidote in the case at her belt. One forged set of court credentials, sealed in wax that Odile had aged with smoke and patience until it looked older than the clerk who would inspect it. One knife, small, mostly for opening crates. One contract of sale, copied out in Dominic's hand from the original in the vault — because Wren had insisted they bring proof of what they were owed, and because reading it one more time felt, horribly, like reading a love letter written by an idiot.

She did not count the things she couldn't count. The weight of what she'd already decided to pay. That ledger stayed shut.

Downstairs, the guildhall smelled of tea and tallow and the particular anxiety of five people who had agreed to do something impossible and were now standing around trying not to look at each other.

* * *

Odile Vasse had laid out her forgeries on the long table like a card sharp showing a winning hand, which, Lena supposed, was exactly what they were.

"Three documents," Odile said, tapping each in turn with one lacquered nail. "A writ of conveyance authorizing the transport of bonded goods to the registry annex. A clerk's pass under the name of Maren Doss, who has been dead four years and whose signature I have been practicing since I was nineteen." She said this without shame; shame, along with forgiveness, was among the things Odile could no longer produce. "And a supplemental inventory sheet listing the cargo. Read it."

Lena read it. *One (1) item, soul-bearing, contracted, due.* Then, beneath it, in smaller script: *Handle with indifference.*

"Dominic wrote that part," Odile said.

"I can see his handwriting."

"He insisted it be accurate. He says courts trust pedants." Odile's mouth did something at the corner that was almost a smile. "He is not wrong. I spent eleven years forging royal decrees, and the ones that passed inspection were never the beautiful ones. They were the ones with the clerical smudges and the petty corrections. Nobody suspects a document that looks annoyed with itself."

Wren stood apart from the table, arms crossed, wearing the flat gray livery of a court usher over their own clothes, which made them look like a blade wrapped in a towel. They had not slept. Lena could tell by the way they kept touching the maps folded against their chest — checking, again, that the salt galleries were where they had left them.

"Say it again," Wren said. It was the fourth time they had said it themselves, and now they wanted it said back.

"The mine road descends through the old salt workings for six miles," Lena recited. "At the third junction there's a sealed gallery marked with a white X — that's yours. From there the corridors are court-cut, dressed stone, lit by lamps nobody remembers lighting. Two checkpoints before the annex. You talk us past them, or we're finished."

"And if I'm taken—"

"You won't be."

"If I am *taken*," Wren repeated, with the patience of someone who had spent years being reasonable underground, "you go on without me, you do not come back for me, and you do not improvise. The court's whole strength is procedure. Our whole weakness is that none of us have any."

"We have one procedure," said a voice from the doorway. "We don't leave each other."

Dominic stood there with a crate strapped to his back.

* * *

It had been his idea, of course. Everything ridiculous was his idea; that was how you knew it was his.

The court below collected what it was owed, but it collected *cargo* without ceremony — goods moved down the mountain constantly, souls sold cheap generations ago, bottled last words, confiscated names, all of it hauled by contractors who were paid not to look. A person walking into the Spire drew attention. A crate being delivered did not. So Dominic had spent three days having himself packed: a shipping crate sized to a curled man, lined with wool, vented through knotholes disguised as woodworm, its paperwork impeccable because Odile had made it impeccable and its occupant insufferable because he was himself.

"You'll be carrying me," he'd explained to Wren with the delight of a child presenting a frog. "Which means that for the first time in your life, your burden will be legally documented."

Now he stood in the doorway of the guildhall, the empty crate propped behind him like a coffin taking a bow, and looked at Lena.

He had shaved. He wore the plain dark clothes of a man going somewhere serious, and he had done something to his hair, and Lena understood suddenly, with a lurch, that he had dressed for this the way people dress for weddings. He thought he might be buried today and he wanted the court to see him well-turned-out when they took the receipt.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't look at me like it's the last time. If you look at me like it's the last time I will turn this entire operation around and march us all back to bed."

"That," Dominic said gravely, "is the most tempting sabotage anyone has ever proposed to me."

"It wasn't a proposal."

"Everything is a proposal if the other party is desperate enough. Ask the magistrate." He came to the table and began, with maddening calm, to help himself to tea. His hands were steady. Lena noticed hands for a living — she watched for tremors, for the fine shake that betrayed a poisoner, a liar, a frightened man — and his were perfectly steady, and she hated how much it cost him to make them so. "Tea?" he offered.

"It's cold."

"All my favorite conversations start over cold tea."

*That's chapter sixteen,* Lena thought, and didn't know why the phrase surfaced, only that it arrived with a strange ache attached, like a bruise on an organ she couldn't locate. She poured anyway.

They drank standing up, all four of them, in the gray half-light, and nobody made a speech, because speeches were for funerals and this was, everyone insisted, a theft.

* * *

The salt mines opened in the hillside east of the city like a wound that had healed into a doorway. By the time they reached it the sun was up, thin and white, doing its best. The mine road was busy — carts of rock salt going up, empty carts going down, the great slow respiration of the city's oldest industry — and no one gave a second glance to an usher, a clerk, and two porters guiding a heavy crate on a handcart.

No one except the man by the gatehouse.

Lena saw him the way she saw everything: peripherally first, catalogued second. A courtier's cut of coat, wrong for the mines. Hands too clean. And the stillness — that was what snagged her. Everyone else at the minehead was in motion, bent into their work, but this man stood against the wall with his arms folded, watching the road with the patient attention of someone counting. Not carts. People.

She knew the type. She had survived two poisonings and one staged allergy by knowing the type. The king's poisoner — though officially he held some title about cellars — did not chase. He confirmed. Somewhere above, someone wanted to know whether the taster and her smuggler would run on the appointed day, and this man had drawn the errand of finding out.

Let him confirm, then.

Lena shifted her grip on the cart handle, angled her body the way porters do, spine curved, face slack, and became furniture. Beside her, Odile did the same, and did it better — Odile could become anything with a signature. Wren walked ahead with the bored authority of a man late for a shift. And Dominic rode inside a crate between them, and Lena found herself thinking, absurdly, *he's the best-disguised of all of us, because he isn't pretending to be anything.*

The watcher by the gatehouse did not move. But as they passed — close enough that Lena caught his scent, ink and clove oil, a court scent — he inclined his head. Barely. A courtesy. A *we'll meet again.*

She did not look back. Looking back was how people died in stories, and also, she suspected, in ledgers.

* * *

The dark came on gradually and then completely.

Six miles of salt. That was what the map said, and the map was right, and the rightness of it was somehow worse than error would have been — every landmark arriving exactly as promised, the third junction, the brine-stink fading, the air drying out until Lena's lips cracked and her voice, when she finally used it, came out sanded.

"Water break."

"Approved," said Wren, who had appointed themselves the keeper of approvals and was exercising the office mercilessly.

They stopped in a widening of the tunnel where old miners had carved their initials a century deep. Salt walls glowed faintly where Wren's lamp touched them, a dim amber like light through honey, and the ceiling was hung with crystals that had grown in the dark for longer than the kingdom had existed. Lena tipped water into her mouth and let the silence press on her ears.

From inside the crate, muffled and cheerful: "Is anyone going to comment on the architecture, or shall I? Because from in here the acoustics are extraordinary. Every footstep sounds like judgment."

"You're supposed to be conserving air," Odile said.

"I'm conserving dignity. There's less of it, so it goes faster."

"Conserve both."

There was a pause. Then, quieter, the voice losing its performance the way a coat slides off a shoulder: "Lena."

She crouched by the crate. Through the knothole she could see very little — a slice of lamplight, the shadow of his eye. It should have been comical. It wasn't. It was the most intimate conversation they had ever had through a wall of pine, and she had once argued with him through a locked door about the proper classification of borrowed luck.

"When we get down there," he said, "whatever happens at the registry — whatever the magistrate offers you, whatever forms appear — remember that the court cannot invent prices. It can only weigh what you put on the scale. So put nothing on the scale you aren't prepared to lose."

"You've been talking to Wren."

"Wren and I correspond extensively through three inches of wood."

"Dominic." She put her palm flat on the crate lid. She meant it as steadying. It came out as something else, and she heard it land as something else, and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it was the one procedure they had never developed. "I know what I'm carrying. We settled this."

"We settled that you'd settle it. Those are different motions."

"Overruled," said Wren, without turning around. "Both of you. We move."

* * *

The white X was exactly where the map promised, chalked on a sealed gallery door so old the chalk had become part of the stone.

Beyond it, the world changed.

The rough-hewn salt ended at a seam as clean as a paper cut, and past the seam the corridors were dressed stone, black and polished, fitted so precisely that the joints were thinner than a hair. Lamps burned in niches at perfect intervals — flames that did not flicker in air that did not move. No dust. No echo. Their footsteps went forward and simply stopped, absorbed, as if the mountain itself were listening politely and declining to repeat anything.

Wren walked point with their lamp held low and their shoulders set in a way Lena recognized from the debtors' table: the posture of a person returning to the place that owned a piece of them. Twice already Wren had paused at junctions not to consult the map but to *remember*, and each remembering had added a new gray tone to their face.

"How far?" Lena asked.

"Checkpoint in four hundred paces. Then the long gallery. Then—" Wren stopped.

Ahead, the corridor divided around a free-standing arch, and in the arch stood a door.

It was small — smaller than it had any right to be, given the grandeur of the approach, a modest door of pale unvarnished wood in a wall of polished black stone, the way a plain sentence sits in the middle of a legal document, load-bearing. No lock. No handle. No hinge visible. Only a shallow recess at its center, shaped like nothing so much as an open palm, and carved above it in letters worn soft by centuries:

**THE OWED MAY ENTER. ALL OTHERS, WAIT.**

Nobody spoke. The lamps burned without flickering. Somewhere far off — impossibly far off, or possibly very near, sound worked strangely here — came the dry whisper of a page turning.

Wren checked the documents in their breast pocket. Odile straightened her clerk's collar. Lena reached back and rested her hand on the crate one final time, feeling through the pine the faint warmth of the man inside, alive, breathing, owed.

"Well," said Dominic's voice through the wood, very softly, all the humor gone out of it. "Knock knock."

Lena pressed her palm into the recess.

The door, without sound, without effort, swung open onto a velvet corridor and a light the color of candlelight through wine — and a voice, courteous as a clerk's, warm as a creditor's, said:

"Hartwell. And freight. Come in. You've been expected for a hundred years."
