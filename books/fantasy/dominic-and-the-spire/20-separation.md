## 20. Separation

The audit came at the worst possible hour, which Wren would later insist was proof that the court had a sense of humor — a cruel one, filed and stamped like everything else down there.

It began as a sound. Not an alarm; the court beneath the mountain did not raise its voice. It was the sound of many soft shoes on stone, moving in unison, and behind them the whisper of paper — thousands of pages turning in perfect synchrony, like wind through a wheat field made of ledgers.

Wren heard it first, three corridors ahead of the others, and felt the old reflexes wake in their spine like dogs remembering they were wolves. Twelve years in the court's service had taught them the difference between an inspection and an audit. An inspection wanted to find nothing. An audit had already found something and was coming to confirm it.

"Move," Wren said, not loudly, and the word carried the way a dropped knife carries.

* * *

They were in the Gallery of Minor Holdings when it happened — a long vaulted corridor lined with glass cases containing the court's smaller acquisitions: a knight's courage, bottled; a composer's second-best melody; forty years of a baker's dreams, pressed flat as flowers between panes of smoked glass. Odile had stopped at one case despite herself, her breath fogging the glass, because inside it lay a single sheet of vellum bearing a name written in a hand she knew better than her own — hers, confiscated eleven years ago, ink gone the brownish color of old bruises.

Lena stood beside her, counting ushers. Dominic, disguised as cargo under a tarp of salt-cloth stamped with the seal of a salt-merchant who did not exist, was being pushed along on a handcart by Wren, who had spent the descent perfecting the slouch and dead eyes of a laborer.

That was when the shoes began.

"Audit," Wren said again, sharper. "Full sweep. They're checking provenance on everything moving through the corridors tonight."

"How can they know?" Odile whispered. Her forged credentials sat against her ribs like a coal. Twice they had passed inspection already — twice! — and each time she had walked away with the particular nausea of the forger whose work is praised by its victim.

"They don't know," Wren said. "Audits don't know things. Audits suspect things. That's worse." They were already moving, tilting the cart into a side alcove, pulling the tarp loose. "Listen. The plan doesn't change. The plan was always: if we're separated, everyone keeps moving toward the registry heart and trusts the others are doing the same."

"And if someone gets taken?" Lena asked.

"Then someone else becomes the diversion." Wren's face did something complicated and settled on grim. "Everyone here is the diversion. That was always true. I just didn't say it out loud before."

Dominic threw back the tarp, salt dust silvering his hair. He looked at Lena first — he always looked at Lena first, though none of them remarked on it anymore; it had become a fact of the group's architecture, like a load-bearing wall. "You remember the waiting room?" he said. "The old woman. If you're taken—"

"I know. Names, dates, the clause language. I've been reciting it in my sleep for a week."

"That wasn't what I was going to say."

Lena looked at him. The corridor filled slowly with the sound of paper.

"What were you going to say?"

But there was no time for it, and both of them knew there never was, and perhaps that had been the design of their whole acquaintance — a conversation perpetually interrupted by circumstance, so that neither of them ever had to finish a sentence dangerous enough to mean something.

The ushers came around the corner like a tide of gray coats.

* * *

Wren ran.

Not away — that was the artistry of it. Wren ran *toward*, straight down the Salt Gallery, past the case of bottled last words, and deliberately knocked over a display stand of collection notices, sending ten thousand official papers avalanching across the polished floor in a blizzard of bureaucratic snow.

"CLERICAL ERROR!" Wren bellowed, in the voice of a person having a professional crisis. "THERE'S BEEN A CLERICAL ERROR IN THE THIRD GALLERY AND I DEMAND A SUPERVISOR!"

Nothing draws auditors like the accusation of error. Within moments, six gray coats broke off from the sweep and converged on Wren, quills already drawn, forms already materializing from nowhere the way court paperwork always did — spontaneously, like mold.

An usher with spectacles and the patient expression of a man who had processed ten thousand crises into triplicate seized Wren's elbow. "Name and function?"

Wren gave him a false name and a false function, and then, because the best lies are scaffolds built of truth, gave him a real complaint: the collection notices in Gallery Three were, in fact, misdated. Wren knew this because Wren had misdated them personally, years ago, as a small act of sabotage no one had ever noticed. Watching four auditors kneel in the paper snow to verify the error, watching the machinery of the court grind its attention entirely onto a typo, Wren felt something dangerously close to pride.

*Good,* Wren thought. *Look at me. Only look at me.*

They let themselves be marched toward the holding rooms, dragging their feet exactly enough, complaining about overtime, buying minutes the way other people spent money — carefully, and all at once.

In the back of their mind, where the court could not audit, Wren counted the others. *Odile has the annex key. Lena has the clause memorized. Dominic is cargo.* And underneath the counting, quieter, the thing Wren had carried up from the magistrate's chamber like a stone in the mouth: *the offer was refused hours ago, and I chose this table, and I will choose it again in whatever room they put me in next.*

A gray coat took their other arm. Wren smiled at him with genuine warmth.

"Mind the paperwork," they said. "It's worth more than I am."

* * *

Odile went the other way.

She had rehearsed this corridor in chalk a dozen times in the guildhall, walking its outline on the floorboards while Lena timed her and Dominic heckled from the stairs, and now the real corridor unfolded itself around her almost familiarly — same proportions, colder air, the walls veined with salt that glittered like frost that had given up trying to be beautiful.

Left at the case of dreams. Right at the fountain that dispensed not water but silence — an actual pooled silence, black and still, that swallowed the sound of footsteps within three feet of its rim. Odile skirted it carefully. In the court beneath the mountain even the fountains were clauses.

Her forgery passed inspection twice already, and the third checkpoint was the annex door itself, where a clerk sat behind a desk of black wood, stamping documents with the serenity of a monk.

"Provenance?" the clerk asked.

Odile presented the credentials Odile Vasse had spent thirty years learning to build: flawless hand, flawless seals, flawless story. A junior archivist of the Upper Registry, come to cross-reference holdings. The clerk examined the papers for a long time. Long enough for Odile to notice the sweat she was not producing, because fear had long ago burned through every ordinary symptom and left only clarity.

"These are excellent," the clerk said at last, and there was something in his tone — a connoisseur's tone. "May I ask who trained you?"

"The court did," Odile said. "Before it discarded me."

Something flickered across the clerk's face — recognition, or its bureaucratic cousin, filing. He stamped the credentials. "The annex is open to authorized persons. Mind the shelves marked *Pending*. Those souls have not yet finished becoming documents, and it is unkind to interrupt them mid-sentence."

"I know something about unfinished sentences," Odile said, and went through.

The annex was smaller than she'd imagined and vaster than the room suggested — shelves receding into dark that geometry refused to explain, and everywhere the faint rustle of names. She walked the aisles with her finger trailing the spines until she found the drawer labeled with the year of her disgrace, and inside it, the folder with her name.

She lifted it.

Somewhere far off, a bell that was not a bell began to ring — a sound like a ledger closing.

Alarms. Not for her, she realized with a cold drop of understanding. Not yet. Something else had tripped the sweep. But the clerk outside would stiffen, the corridors would thicken with gray coats, and every second she lingered now cost double.

*Copy, don't steal,* she told herself — the plan's own words, and also, conveniently, the only thing a forger had ever wanted to hear. She uncapped her ink, laid her own blank vellum beside the confiscated page, and began to write herself back into existence, stroke by stroke, in a hand identical to the one the court had taken.

Halfway through the second line, she found the annotation beside another entry in the drawer — an entry she had not been looking for and could not stop reading:

**Dominic Vael — soul, whole. Collected per contract, due date as prophesied.**

And beneath it, in handwriting Odile knew intimately because she had spent weeks studying its loops and pressures in a prophecy book:

*voided, see p. 412.*

Odile sat very still in the aisle of stolen names, her pen dripping one black bead onto the stone floor, and understood that the woman upstairs-in-reverse — the taster, the ledger-keeper, the stubborn kind fool — had written something in this registry decades before she'd ever descended into it.

Or would write it. Or had always written it.

"Damn margins," Odile whispered, and kept copying.

* * *

Lena was escorted.

That was the word the usher used, and he used it gently, the way one uses a word that has been sanded smooth by centuries of use. *"You'll be escorted."* Not arrested, not summoned, not seized. Escorted, as if she were a guest whose host had simply arrived early.

She had been three steps from the registry heart's antechamber when the gray coats folded around her, and she had done the arithmetic instantly — Wren's noise echoing from the east galleries, Odile's absence already a fact rather than a hope, Dominic somewhere behind her under salt-cloth — and she had understood her role in the night's design before anyone announced it.

Each of them believed they were the diversion now. That was either the plan working perfectly or failing perfectly, and there was no time to determine which.

"This way, mistress Hartwell," said the senior usher. He knew her name. Of course he did. Her name had been in the prophecy book for a hundred years; it had probably been in the registry longer.

"I'm not going anywhere," Lena said, with dignity, while going.

"No one ever is," the usher agreed. "That's rather the point of the court. Everything stays. We're very good at keeping."

They walked her through corridors she hadn't seen on Wren's maps — velvet-hung passages where the torchlight was warm and the air smelled of cedar and old promises, hallways designed, she realized, to make the walked feel honored. Every door they passed bore a brass plate with a date. Dates of sales, of collections, of contracts sealed. A museum of dues.

"You've been expecting me," Lena said.

"We've been expecting this evening since before your grandmother learned to write," the usher said pleasantly. "Though I confess the staff had wagers on whether you'd come armed. You came with paperwork instead. The magistrate found that delightful. He does love a well-documented opponent."

"He's read my file."

"Mistress Hartwell, the court wrote your file. We merely lent it to the future for safekeeping."

They turned the last corner, and the doors of the magistrate's chamber opened ahead of them without being touched — two leaves of pale wood swinging inward on air that smelled, absurdly, of chamomile.

Lena thought of Dominic, wherever he was, under his tarp or out of it, moving toward her or away from her, believing — she hoped — that she was drawing the court's eyes so he could slip free. She thought of the clause language she'd recited into her pillow. She thought of page 412, which she had not yet seen and which her own handwriting insisted existed.

Then she thought of nothing tactical at all, only of a man who had sold his soul without asking her, and stepped through the doors as if she owned them.

Inside, a fire burned low. Behind a desk of the same black wood as the annex, the magistrate rose to greet her, courteous as winter sunlight.

"Mistress Hartwell," he said. "At last. Do sit down. We have so much to settle, and you've brought such interesting marginalia."

Behind her, the doors closed with the soft, final sound of a ledger closing — and somewhere above, in the world of weather and bread and unregulated light, dawn was still hours away.

* * *

In the Salt Gallery, pinned beneath three auditors and a form labeled *Irregularity 9,004-C*, Wren heard the distant chime of the registry bell and grinned through a split lip.

"Diversion's holding," they told the auditor sitting on their legs. "You're welcome."

The auditor, to his credit, stamped the form anyway.
