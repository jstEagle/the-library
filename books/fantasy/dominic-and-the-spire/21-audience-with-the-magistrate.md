## 21. Audience with the Magistrate

The usher was polite about it, which was somehow worse than chains would have been.

"This way, Mistress Hartwell. The magistrate will see you now." As if she had asked for an appointment. As if she had sent a card ahead by courier and been received with pleasure into a busy schedule. The corridor smelled of beeswax and old paper and something faintly mineral, like the inside of a geode, and the lamps along its length burned without flickering in air that did not move at all.

Lena walked because her legs decided to before her mind could argue. Behind her, somewhere back among the velvet corridors and waiting rooms, Odile was alone with the registry annex, and Wren was — Wren was drawing them off, or captured, or dead, and there was nothing Lena could do about any of it except keep walking and keep her face arranged into the expression she used for tasting the king's soup: attentive, neutral, giving away nothing.

*Catalogue,* she told herself, the way she always did when fear arrived. *Name what is in front of you.*

Corridor: green marble veined with white, polished to a wet shine. Lamps: glass, oilless, burning on something that cast no heat. Sound: their two sets of footsteps, hers and the usher's, and beneath them a low hum like a held note, as if the whole mountain were humming to itself the way a man hums while he works. Doors: none visible, which meant they were all hidden, which meant every wall might open.

She catalogued until the catalogue ran out, and then there was only the door at the end of the corridor, tall and dark and already opening before the usher touched it.

"Magistrate," the usher said, and stepped aside, and Lena went through because the alternative was to be a person who hesitated at doors, and she had never once hesitated at a door in her life. You didn't last long as a royal taster if you flinched at thresholds.

The chamber beyond was not what she expected, though she couldn't have said what she'd expected. A throne, probably. Darkness, certainly. Something with teeth.

Instead: a study. A warm, lamplit, book-lined study, with a fire in a low grate and a desk of black wood scattered with papers in tidy stacks, and a teapot under a knitted cozy, and a cat asleep on the hearthstone, orange and enormous and utterly indifferent to her. The magistrate stood at a shelf with his back to her, returning a slim volume to its place, and he was — small. That was the second wrong thing. He was a narrow, silver-haired man in a grey robe, spectacles pushed up onto his forehead, with the stooped shoulders of someone who had spent a lifetime bent over ledgers, and when he turned to greet her his face was lined and mild and almost kind.

"Mistress Hartwell." He inclined his head. "You're earlier than I expected. Do sit down. The tea has just drawn."

"I haven't come for tea," Lena said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You've come to steal from my registry. But you've been walking for hours and you've eaten nothing since dawn, and I find negotiations go poorly on empty stomachs. Sit. Please." He lifted the cozy off the pot and poured two cups without asking whether she wanted one, and set one on the near side of the desk, precisely where a guest's cup would go, and then he sat down across from her and folded his hands and waited.

It was, she realized with a cold sliding feeling, exactly the kind of courtesy Dominic would have recognized instantly. The court beneath the mountain did not snarl. It *hosted.*

She sat. She did not touch the tea. "Where are my friends?"

"The calligrapher is where you left her, doing better than she knows. Your debtor-spy is currently reciting clause forty-one of the Salt Galleries Accession at three pursuers who very much wish they hadn't been assigned this shift." A small dry smile. "And your smuggler-archivist is in a holding room drinking tea he says is terrible, which is flattering, because it's the same blend I'm offering you. He has opinions about tea. It's in his file."

"You have a file on him."

"I have files on everyone who owes me anything, which is everyone." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, aligning handle to rim, and the gesture was so familiar it hurt — she did that; Dominic did that; half the guild did that, the little compulsions of people who lived by precision. "But we aren't here to discuss him yet. First, I want to say that I admire your work. The taster's tongue is a remarkable instrument. Three poisons identified by aftertaste at the king's own table, and never once a false alarm in ten years. Do you know how rare that is? Most tasters die of nerves before they die of arsenic."

"How do you know about the king's table?"

"Mistress Hartwell." He removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, replaced them. "I am the court beneath the mountain. Everything owed passes through my hands eventually. And everything above is owed to something." He gestured gently at the room, the shelves, the sleeping cat. "Now. Shall we discuss why you're really here? Or shall we perform the pretense a while longer? I'm content either way. I have a great deal of time. It's practically all I have."

Lena looked at the tea. Looked at the man. Thought of Wren's warning — *the magistrate doesn't want cruelty, he wants precedent* — and thought of Odile's voice in the guildhall: *a forgery only fails when the truth underneath it is wrong.* She had walked into this chamber carrying a plan like a blade under her coat, and the plan had been simple: get to the magistrate, stall, negotiate, survive until Odile struck the entry. Every hour she kept him talking was an hour Odile worked.

So she would talk. She folded her hands the way he had folded his, and gave him her best court smile, the one that had fooled a paranoid king for a decade.

"Then let's not pretend," she said. "I want Dominic's contract voided."

"Naturally." He said it the way a shopkeeper agrees that yes, of course you'd like to buy the bread. "And you've come prepared to pay. Equal weight. That's the law, and it's a good law, and I won't break it even for a guest in my study." He opened a drawer — she heard paper slide, precise and unhurried — and drew out a single sheet and laid it on the desk between them, facing her.

She looked down at it, and the cold that had been sliding through her all evening finally stopped moving and simply settled, like a stone dropped into a well.

It was a trade agreement. Properly drafted, properly witnessed, clauses numbered in a fine antique hand. Her name at the top. His name below it. And in the column where payment was itemized, in her own handwriting — she knew it the way you know your own reflection, the slight forward slant, the way her *g*s looped too far — were written the words:

*All memories of Dominic Vale, itemized and weighed.*

"That's not possible," she said quietly. "I haven't written anything down. I made the list in my head. I circled *memories* in my notebook and then I burned the page."

"Burned pages still burn somewhere," the magistrate said mildly. "Besides, I didn't write this tonight. This draft is eleven days old."

"Eleven days ago I hadn't—"

"Hadn't decided. No. But decisions are just conclusions, and conclusions leave drafts behind them in places you don't think to look." He tapped the sheet once, gently, with one finger. "You've been composing this offer since the day you learned what he sold. You wrote it in the way you look at him when he isn't watching. In the way you stopped taking sugar in your tea, because sugar was *his* habit first and you wanted to give it back. The court reads debts the way you read poisons, Mistress Hartwell. By aftertaste. And your aftertaste has been legible for years."

She should have felt horror. She registered, distantly, that horror was the appropriate response, the way she knew arsenic was in the wine by the almond ghost at the back of the throat. But what she actually felt was something worse and quieter: recognition. Because he was right. She had been drafting this trade since chapter and verse, since the vault ledger, since the moment she read *collection due* beside a date and understood whose soul it named. The list of candidates — immunity, membership, memories — she had made it in ink and burned it, but she had known, circling that word, that she wasn't choosing. She was confirming.

"And the prophecy," she said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, in a distant, useless way. "The date. Tonight. That's in the book too."

"It is." He rose, crossed to the shelf, and took down a volume she knew — she had shelved it herself a hundred times, or its twin; the guild's folios and the court's archives were cousins, she saw now, grown from the same root. He laid it open beside the trade agreement, and there was the date, circled in century-old ink, and beside it, in the margin, in handwriting that was hers and was not hers and was older than she was:

*Paid in full. Remember to forget.*

"You see the difficulty," the magistrate said, sitting again, and now his kindness was unmistakable and unbearable, because it was real. "I am not your enemy in this. I am the clerk of a law older than both of us, and the law says a debt must be paid, and the prophecy — which your own hand wrote, in a tense I won't pretend to understand — says how it gets paid. You came down here to sacrifice yourself. I am merely offering to formalize the sacrifice. Same terms. Same weight. My scales, which are honest, and my witnesses, who are required. You lose your memories of him. He walks out of my mountain with his soul in his own chest, free and clear, contract dissolved, precedent honored. Everyone gets what they came for." He spread his hands. "Where, precisely, is the cruelty?"

"In the expecting," Lena said.

He tilted his head, inviting her to continue, and she hated how much he seemed to genuinely want to hear it.

"A trap is cruel because it closes. But this—" she touched the edge of the agreement, not picking it up, "—this was open before I ever walked in. You knew what I'd offer before I offered it. You priced my sacrifice before I decided to make it. That means it was never a choice. It means I'm a line in a ledger that balanced itself a hundred years before I was born, and you're just... collecting the arithmetic." She looked up at him. "That's the cruelty. Not the losing. The *expecting.*"

For a long moment the magistrate said nothing. The fire ticked. The cat stretched one paw, sheathed it again. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, she imagined she could hear the city going about its night, unaware.

"That," he said at last, "is the most intelligent objection anyone has raised in this chamber in sixty years." He sounded — she searched his face for the lie and found, appallingly, none — he sounded moved. "You're right. Of course you're right. Expected sacrifices are not sacrifices; they're deliveries. And yet." He turned his cup that quarter-turn again. "The law does not care whether a payment is chosen or prophesied. Only that it balances. If I release him without equal weight, every contract I hold unravels — do you understand what that means? There are souls in my waiting room sold for a sack of flour by grandfathers dead three generations. If precedent breaks, those sales break, and those souls go... where? To owners who bought in bad faith? Into the dark with no registry at all? The law is cruel, Mistress Hartwell, but the lawlessness beneath it is crueller. I hold the line because someone must, and I hold it politely because terror is inefficient."

"So you'll take my memories because the paperwork requires it."

"I'll take your memories because the paperwork permits it," he corrected gently. "There's a difference, and the difference is you. Sign, and it's done tonight, cleanly, and he goes home whole. Refuse, and the collection proceeds as contracted at dawn — his soul, taken entire, and your memories worth nothing to anyone." He slid the pen across the desk. It was a good pen, black lacquer, well-balanced. Even his instruments were courteous. "I would rather you sign. For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much to you — I would rather it were otherwise entirely. You are the finest argument against my office I have encountered in a century. Argue with me again sometime, when it isn't costing you everything."

Lena looked at the pen. At the agreement in her own handwriting. At the margin note in the book that was hers and older than her: *remember to forget.*

Somewhere below or above or behind the walls, Odile was working. Wren was buying minutes with clause forty-one. Dominic was insulting the tea in a holding room, alive, owed, and hers to save. Every hour she kept this polite, terrible man talking was an hour purchased with nothing but conversation, and conversation was the one commodity she had never run short of with anyone she loved.

She could sign. She could end it now, cleanly, and never taste another meal with him across the table, never balance another ledger with his elbow bumping hers, never—

No. Not yet. Not while stalling was still worth more than signing.

She put her hand flat over the agreement, covering her own name, and met the magistrate's eyes.

"I'll sign," she said. "But not tonight, and not in five minutes, and not because you slid a pen at me like a man settling a bar tab. I want time. An hour. Alone, with the terms, read properly — I'm a ledger-keeper, Magistrate; I don't sign anything I haven't audited. You of all people should respect that."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, she thought, genuine and quickly mastered — and then he smiled, slow and rueful, like a chess player watching an opponent find a move he'd honestly hoped she wouldn't.

"An hour," he said. "Granted. The usher will bring you fresh tea and the full instrument — every clause, every witness line, unredacted. Audit away." He rose, and walked her to the door himself, and paused with his hand on the frame. "Mistress Hartwell. One courtesy, since you've earned it. When you audit — check the clause on *interest.*"

"Interest?"

"What is freely given," he said, "may sometimes be reclaimed. It's an obscure provision. Almost no one invokes it." His eyes, mild behind the spectacles, held hers a moment longer than courtesy required. "Almost no one thinks to. But you strike me as someone who reads footnotes."

Then the door closed behind her, and the usher was waiting with a lamp and a fresh pot, and Lena walked back down the green-marble corridor with the word *interest* turning over in her mind like a coin she couldn't identify, and behind her, in the warm study, the magistrate returned to his desk and looked for a long time at the unsigned agreement, and at the prophecy book lying open beside it, and at the margin note in a hand that was Lena Hartwell's and a hundred years old:

*Paid in full. Remember to forget.*

And beneath it, in ink so fresh it had not yet dried, in a hand no one in the chamber had seen arrive:

*Not yet. See page 412.*

* * *

The usher installed her in a small reading room — a lamp, a table, the full instrument of the agreement delivered on a tray with the promised fresh tea — and withdrew with a bow, and Lena sat for a full minute doing nothing but breathing and cataloguing.

Room: stone, dry, one door, no windows. Tea: the same blend Dominic had called terrible, which meant the magistrate had been telling the truth about that, which meant — she filed the thought away — the magistrate lied about almost nothing, which was its own kind of terrifying. Document: present. Pen: absent. They had taken the pen back. Of course they had. Nothing signed in this mountain happened by accident.

She unfolded the agreement and began to audit.

It was beautiful work. Clauses clean, definitions tight, no traps in the margins — she checked twice, three times, running her taster's discipline over the language the way she'd run it over the king's wine, hunting for the almond ghost, the bitter note, the thing that wasn't what it claimed to be. There wasn't one. That was the horror of it, still, layered under the other horrors: the document was *fair.* Equal weight, honestly weighed, witnesses required, dissolution complete upon payment. The court had drafted her sacrifice more carefully than she had.

Clause nine covered the itemization. Memories of Dominic Vale, enumerated: the guildhall, the ledgers, the teas, the arguments, the laugh, the ten years, the sale itself — all of it, weighed and surrendered. She read each item the way you read a list of the dead. *Item the fourth: the subject's habit of aligning his cup to his saucer.* They had noticed that. The court had watched them both for years, pricing the small things, and the small things weighed as much as the large ones. More. The scales here were honest, and honesty meant the ordinary moments counted full value.

Her eyes blurred. She let them, briefly, and then wiped them with the heel of her hand and kept auditing, because that was who she was: a woman who finished the inventory.

Clause fourteen. *Interest.*

*What is freely given may be reclaimed, per precedent of the Second Registry, provided the reclamation be claimed in kind, by the giver, within a term not exceeding—*

She read it four times. Then she set the page down flat on the table and pressed both palms against it, as if it might otherwise drift away, and stared at the lamp until her vision cleared.

Freely given. Reclaimed. In kind.

Her memories, given — could be *returned.* Not voided. Not undone. Reclaimed, by her, if she claimed them. The magistrate hadn't warned her out of kindness. Or not only kindness. He had handed her the flaw in his own perfect document, the footnote that undid the arithmetic — or completed it, she couldn't tell which, not yet, not alone in a stone room with a lamp and a cooling pot of terrible tea.

And beneath that thought, arriving slowly, the way dawn arrives under a door: *page 412.* The fresh ink she'd glimpsed. The note that hadn't existed when she walked into the study and existed when she walked out. Someone was writing in that book *now.* Someone with her handwriting, ahead of her, behind her, around her — future-Lena, past-Lena, some version of her leaving instructions like breadcrumbs in a forest she was still walking into.

*See page 412.*

She looked at the tray. At the absent pen. At the door, and the polite world of the court beyond it, and the hour the magistrate had granted her ticking away like water into sand.

An hour. She had asked for an hour to audit a document, and she had gotten it, and somewhere in the green corridors Odile was alone with the registry, and somewhere in the salt galleries Wren was spending favors like a gambler's last coins, and somewhere in a holding room Dominic was drinking terrible tea and refusing, absolutely refusing, to be rescued.

One hour. One footnote. One page, somewhere in this mountain, numbered 412.

Lena stood up, folded the agreement into her coat, and went to the door to see how politely the court would take being robbed of its schedule.
