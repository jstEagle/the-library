## 23. Page 412

The magistrate's chamber smelled of warm stone and old paper, which was the most dangerous thing about it.

Lena had expected sulfur. She had expected cold, and chains, and the particular dread that the surface world imagined for the court beneath the mountain — all the folklore of a childhood spent above ground, where mothers said *behave or the under-court will hear of it* as casually as they said *eat your greens*. What she had found instead was a room like a library married to a countinghouse: shelves rising into the dark of the vaulted ceiling, each one crowded with ledgers bound in leather gone soft with handling, and at the center of it all a desk so wide and so polished that it seemed to float on its own reflection, black as still water.

The magistrate sat behind it. Not looming. *Seated*, the way an accountant is seated, the way a physician is seated — someone whose power lay entirely in what they knew and how patiently they were willing to explain it to you.

"Take your time," the magistrate had said, when Lena asked for a moment before signing. And then, when she had taken rather more than a moment: "Though I should note that patience is not the same as indecision, Mistress Hartwell. The contract matures at moonrise."

"I understand," Lena said. "I'd like to see the master ledger first."

Something moved behind the magistrate's eyes. It was very small, and very fast, and if Lena had not spent ten years reading poison in the micro-expressions of kings' cooks she would have missed it entirely. But she had not missed it, and she catalogued it the way she catalogued everything: *bitterness, almond, a trace of iron — surprise.*

"You've been told about the master ledger," the magistrate said.

"I was told there was a book in which everything is written." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used when assuring a nervous king that his supper would not kill him. "I'm told your court honors precedent above all things. If precedent lives anywhere, it lives in that book. I won't sign away my memories on the strength of a verbal summary. I want to read the terms."

The magistrate regarded her for a long moment. The lamplight — actual lamps, oil-fed, their flames burning a shade too steadily, as though even fire down here was on its best behavior — caught the edge of a face that might have been any age between forty and four hundred. Smooth skin. Tired eyes. The eyes of someone who has read every book in the room, including the ones that were never meant to be read.

"A taster's caution," the magistrate said at last. "I have always found it admirable. In others." A hand lifted, pale fingers beckoning once, and from somewhere beyond the shelves came the soft scuff of feet. An usher appeared — one of the polite ones Odile had described, grey-robed, faceless in the sense that their face was simply unremarkable, forgettable within seconds of looking away. The magistrate spoke a string of syllables too low for Lena to catch, and the usher bowed and vanished between the stacks.

"You will find," the magistrate said, folding pale hands on the desk's dark shine, "that our records are impeccable."

"I know," Lena said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

While they waited, the magistrate made conversation.

This was, Lena suspected, a tactic. She recognized tactics; she used them herself across negotiating tables less terrible than this one. Make the counterparty comfortable. Make them talk. Let the silence do half the work of persuasion, because people abhorred silence and would fill it with their own doubts, given room enough.

So she did not fill it. She let it sit, and examined the chamber instead, the way she had learned to examine rooms — exits first, then sightlines, then the small betrayals of objects out of place. There were two doors she could see: the great one through which she had been escorted, and a low one behind the magistrate's desk, barely taller than a child, its wood worn pale around the handle by centuries of hands. There were no windows. There was no clock, but there was a shadow along the eastern wall that lengthened, almost imperceptibly, as the lamps burned — the only clock this place needed, marking time toward her moonrise deadline.

On the desk itself, beside the contract awaiting her signature, sat three objects arranged with deliberate precision: a scale, small and brass and old enough that its pans had gone green at the rims. A pen. And a single dried flower, white, flattened as if pressed between pages for a hundred years.

Lena looked at the flower longer than she meant to.

"My predecessor kept it," the magistrate said, following her gaze without turning. "A bribe, technically. From a petitioner who hoped to soften the terms of her husband's contract. It didn't work. But my predecessor kept it anyway, as a reminder."

"A reminder of what?"

"That everyone who sits where you're sitting believes they are the exception." The magistrate's voice was not cruel. That was the worst part, Lena thought. It was not cruel, it was not smug, it was not even cold. It was the voice of a person stating the weather. "And that the law does not bend for exceptions. It bends for nothing. That is its entire virtue, and the whole reason anyone trusts it — including, eventually, you."

"Do you believe that?" Lena asked. "Or do you recite it?"

For the second time, something flickered behind those tired eyes. This one lasted longer. This one looked, absurdly, like pain.

"I am the court," the magistrate said quietly. "There is no meaningful difference between what I believe and what it requires."

Before Lena could answer — before she could decide whether she wanted to answer, whether pity for a creature like this was wisdom or a trap — the usher returned, carrying a book.

It took two of them, actually; a second usher materialized from the stacks to help bear it, and together they set it on the desk with a reverence that bordered on liturgy. The master ledger. It was enormous — wider than her armspan, thick as a fallen log, its covers strapped shut with three buckles of tarnished silver. Its spine bore no title. It did not need one. Everything was written inside it.

"Buckle the straps again when you finish," the magistrate said. "Page numbers run continuous from the founding. Ask the ushers if you require assistance locating anything." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Anything *relevant.*"

"Everything in it is relevant," Lena said. "That's what a ledger *is.*"

She unbuckled the first strap.

* * *

The paper inside was cream-colored and fine as skin, and the handwriting changed constantly, era to era, clerk to clerk, century to century — a river of scripts flowing past her eyes as she turned the early pages. Contracts. All contracts. Every soul ever sold beneath the mountain, recorded in ink that did not fade and did not forgive. Some entries were short: a name, a price, a date. Some ran pages, itemizing what had been traded — *one talent for luck, renewable annually; the memory of a daughter's face, held in escrow until the debt clears; the seller's capacity for joy, partial, being the joy of rain specifically* — and some were annotated in margins in hands that had clearly believed themselves important, magistrates dead these hundreds of years arguing with their successors in furious marginal script about valuations and clauses.

Lena turned pages slowly. Deliberately slowly. She had asked for page 412, yes — but the number she had spoken aloud in the waiting room, the number Wren had risked everything to whisper through a keyhole before the audit scattered them — was a number she had gotten from nowhere she could name. It had simply arrived in her mind, complete and certain, the way the taste of bitter almond arrives on the tongue before the conscious mind knows what it means.

She did not trust it. She trusted nothing tonight, least of all her own head.

But she knew how to stall, and stalling was a discipline like any other, and so she read. She read the sale of a miller's shadow in the year the city gates were raised. She read the purchase of a soldier's courage, sold by his own captain, priced at eleven years of good harvests. She read, on a page near the front, an entry in a shaking hand recording the sale of *all memory of my son, he will be better off,* and she had to stop for a moment and press her palms flat against the cool paper until her breathing steadied.

Somewhere above her, moonrise was approaching. Somewhere in the corridors, Wren was leading pursuers through salt galleries on legs that had not fully healed from the last time. Somewhere alone in the registry annex, Odile was committing forgery against the most meticulous institution in existence, armed with nothing but genius and nerve. Dominic was in a holding cell being polite at his guards, which was somehow the most frightening image of all.

She turned another page. Another. The numbers climbed. Three hundred. Three hundred fifty. The handwriting grew more modern, tighter, more bureaucratic, and the entries grew shorter, as though the court had learned over the centuries to say more with less, the way a blade sharpens itself by wearing away.

Four hundred.

Four hundred and five.

Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. She noted it, catalogued it — *fear, presentation: cardiac, flavor: copper* — and turned the page.

Four hundred and eleven. A contract of no interest whatsoever. She read it twice anyway, savoring the delay, hating herself for savoring it.

Then she turned to page 412.

* * *

It was blank.

Of course it was blank, she thought — relief flooding her so fast and so hard that she nearly laughed aloud, nearly gave the game away right there, because whatever Wren had seen or dreamed or half-remembered, it had been wrong, the page was empty, there was nothing—

Except.

Not entirely blank.

One line. Centered vertically on the page, as though it had been written not as an entry among entries but as a verdict upon the whole sheet. One line of text, in ink that was neither brown nor black nor any color she could name — an ink that seemed, when she tilted her head, to be the exact color of the moment just before remembering something.

And the handwriting was hers.

Not similar. Hers. The specific hers — the slight forward slant she'd never trained out of herself, the way her capital H opened like a door left ajar, the little hook she put on the descender of a *g* that a calligrapher would call sloppy and that Odile had once, drunk on stolen wine, declared *charmingly unfixable.* She had written grocery lists in this hand. She had balanced guild ledgers in this hand. She had, three nights ago, circled the word *memories* in a list of things she might pay, and hated her own handwriting for how steady it had been.

The line read:

***Paid in full. Remember to forget.***

No date. No signature — it needed none; the hand was the signature. No entry of what had been paid, or to whom, or for whom. Just those six words sitting on the cream-colored page like a stone at the bottom of a clear stream.

Lena did not touch it. Some instinct older than thought held her fingers hovering a hair's breadth above the paper. You did not taste an unknown poison straight. You tested it against silver, against flame, against the inside of your own wrist. And this — this was the same. This was a substance of unknown potency, and she was looking at it, and it was looking back.

"Ah," said the magistrate.

Lena had not heard the chair move, but the magistrate was standing now, come around the desk's dark water, gazing down at the page with an expression she could not parse. Not triumph. Not confusion. Something older and wearier than either — the look of a person who checks a calculation for the hundredth time and finds, yet again, that it balances.

"So it exists," the magistrate said softly. "I confess I wondered. My predecessors wrote of it. A page entered in advance. A settlement recorded before the debt comes due." Tired eyes lifted to hers. "You have seen this before, haven't you, Mistress Hartwell?"

"No," Lena said truthfully.

"But you recognize the hand."

"Yes," Lena said, also truthfully, because lying to this creature suddenly felt like spitting into a well she'd have to drink from later.

"And whose hand is it?"

The chamber was very quiet. The lamps burned their too-steady burn. Somewhere far off, in the salt galleries, she imagined she could hear the faint percussion of running feet, though that was probably her own pulse.

"It's mine," she said.

The word *mine* hung in the air like smoke. The magistrate studied her — long, thorough, the way she herself studied a dish, searching for the poison hidden in the sauce — and then nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something already suspected, which was somehow worse than shock.

"The prophecy book names this night," the magistrate said. "You know this. It named this night a century ago, and it named you, and it named him, and every attempt to alter what it foretold has failed or fed it. And now we find, in the master ledger of record, a settlement page bearing your hand — undated, unwitnessed, uncovenanted — declaring the very exchange you sit here tonight contemplating to be *already complete.*" The magistrate spread pale hands. "Do you understand what the law must conclude from such a document? It cannot conclude that the page is forged. Forgeries can be proven. It cannot conclude that the page is irrelevant, for the ledger admits no irrelevancies. The law must conclude that the payment occurred — or will occur — and that this page stands as its receipt."

"Receipts require transactions," Lena said. Her voice sounded steadier than she felt, which was the entire point of a lifetime of practice. "Show me the transaction. Show me where my memories were weighed. Show me the scales' record, the witnesses, the—"

"Mistress Hartwell." The magistrate's voice gentled, and the gentleness was terrible, was the velvet glove drawn snug. "I am offering you exactly what you came to take. Your memories of Dominic Vael — itemized, weighed, exchanged — in return for the soul the court holds. You planned this. You listed the candidates in your own quarters; you circled the word yourself; do not trouble to deny it, the walls of guildhalls are thinner than guildsmen believe. I am prepared to formalize it tonight, cleanly, legally, with every courtesy. And now I find the ledger already bears your mark upon the outcome." A pause. "Tell me — when you wrote that line, whenever, wherever, in whatever year your future self keeps her accounts — did she strike you as coerced?"

Lena looked down at the page. At the six words in her own hand. *Paid in full. Remember to forget.*

Remember to forget.

Not *remember me.* Not *it was worth it.* Not *tell him.* Whoever she became — whoever wrote this, decades from now or centuries, at the bottom of whatever life waited on the other side of tonight — that woman had chosen these words with a taster's precision. Every word a dose. *Remember:* the memory existed once, was real, was loved. *To forget:* the cost was paid knowingly, freely. And underneath it all, folded into the phrasing like arsenic folded into honey, an instruction aimed forward through time at no one and everyone: *this is not a tragedy. This is an accounting. Do not weep at arithmetic.*

"She struck me," Lena said carefully, "as someone who had done the math."

"And the math favored the trade."

"The math," Lena said, "always favors keeping Dominic alive. That's not math. That's axioms."

The magistrate exhaled — a sound almost, almost like laughter, worn smooth by centuries of disuse. Then the pale hand extended toward the page, toward the line of impossible ink, and stopped just short of touching it, the way one stops short of a flame.

"The law requires me to honor a settled account," the magistrate said. "If this page is genuine, the debt is discharged, the contract dissolves, and the soul returns — and I am bound to permit it, however the timing offends me. If this page is false, it is the most exquisite forgery ever committed against this court, and I will spend a hundred years finding the hand that made it, and I will enjoy every year." The tired eyes rose to Lena's. "Which is it, Mistress Hartwell? Because there remains one party whose testimony the law permits on a matter of self-authentication, and that is the hand itself."

"What does that mean?" Lena asked. "Practically. What are you asking?"

"I am asking you to claim it," the magistrate said. "Stand before the scales, place your hand upon this page, and declare: *this is my writing, and the account it records is true.* If you speak true, the ledger accepts you and the contract dies at moonrise without your signature on a single new term. If you speak falsely—" the pause was surgical, "—the ledger will know, and the court will know precisely what your memory is worth, and we shall weigh it ourselves. Either way, Mistress Hartwell, the transaction completes tonight. The only question left to you — the last free question — is whether you are its author or merely its instrument."

The lamps burned. The shadow on the eastern wall crept. Somewhere overhead, the moon was climbing toward the hour that had been circled in old ink a hundred years before she was born.

Lena looked at the line in her own handwriting one last time.

Then she rolled up her sleeve, and reached for the page — and stopped, fingers trembling a hair's breadth from the ink, because a thought had arrived, sudden and cold as a coin dropped down a well:

*If it's already paid — if some version of me already did this, already forgot him, already lived through losing him —*

*then why is the prophecy book still predicting tonight at all?*

Unless the loop wasn't closed.

Unless it was only closed *if she claimed the page* —

and somewhere below her, in the registry annex, a woman who could forge anything except forgiveness was at this very moment copying an entry that bore an annotation in the same impossible hand: *voided, see p. 412.*

Lena drew a slow breath, placed her palm flat upon the cream-colored paper, and felt the ledger wake beneath her hand like something ancient turning over in its sleep.

"I'll claim it," she said. "But not as author. As *editor.*"

The magistrate's eyebrows rose — the first genuinely unguarded expression she had seen on that face all night.

"There's a correction," Lena said, "in the margin."
