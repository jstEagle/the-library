## 24. Wren's Stand

The salt galleries ran beneath the city like the veins of something long dead, and Wren had memorized every one of them in the years when memorizing them had been their job.

They knew this particular branch intimately. Gallery Nine, the one the miners called the Throat, because it narrowed and narrowed until a grown person had to turn sideways to pass. The walls here wept brine that dried in white rills, and the lamplight turned everything the color of old bone. Somewhere behind them — three galleries back, maybe four — the pursuit party was moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the map.

Wren leaned against the salt wall and did the arithmetic they'd been doing since the audit horn first sounded: how many minutes could be bought, and what each minute would cost.

Behind them, down the branching dark, Odile Vasse was running toward the registry annex with a forged page against her heart. Ahead of them, somewhere between here and the magistrate's chamber, Lena Hartwell was being escorted by polite ushers who would not be refused. And Dominic — Dominic was under guard with the others, disguised as cargo, which meant that if the court caught up to the cargo, everything ended here in the salt and the dark.

So Wren stood in the Throat and waited to be caught, because being caught slowly was the only gift left to give.

* * *

They heard the pursuers before they saw them: the particular cadence of court boots on salt-crusted stone, two beats and a pause, two beats and a pause. Court soldiers walked like clerks. Everything the subterranean court did, it did in the rhythm of paperwork.

Wren counted them into the lamplight. Six. A file leader with the silver pin of an auditor-at-arms at his collar, four line soldiers, and — Wren's stomach dropped with something almost like affection — Clerk Second-Class Merrin, carrying the summons ledger under her arm like a sleeping cat.

Merrin. Of course it was Merrin. The court never sent muscle alone; it sent muscle *and* documentation, because an arrest without a properly witnessed summons was, by its own lights, not an arrest at all but a kidnapping, and the court was very proud of never once having kidnapped anyone.

"Wren of the Surface Detail," the file leader said. His voice echoed flatly off the salt. "You are named in Audit Order 88-C as absent without leave, consorting with debtors, and interfering with a lawful collection. You will come with us."

"May I see the order?" Wren asked.

The file leader blinked. In eleven years of service, Wren had learned that the single most effective weapon against the court was courtesy. Rage gave them leverage. Fear gave them speed. But a debtor who asked politely to see the paperwork stopped the whole machine like a stone in a gear.

"I have it here," Merrin said quickly, already untying the ledger strings. She was young, round-faced, and had once spent an entire shift explaining to Wren why she'd joined the court — *someone has to make sure it's done correctly,* she'd said, as though correctness were a kind of mercy. Maybe, Wren thought now, watching her fumble the knot, for some people it was.

Merrin read the order aloud. It was thorough. It was, Wren noted with professional appreciation, beautifully drafted — the clause about consorting with debtors even cited the specific subsection Wren had violated, which was more care than most surface magistrates bothered with.

"I contest the third charge," Wren said.

"You—what?" said the file leader.

"The third charge. Interfering with a lawful collection." Wren folded their hands, the way the magistrate did when he wanted a room to feel smaller. "A collection is lawful only if the contract it enforces is valid, current, and unencumbered. I am formally notifying you that I intend to file an encumbrance claim against the Dominic contract before any collection proceeds. Under Standing Rule Twelve, a collection cannot proceed while an encumbrance claim is pending."

Silence. One of the line soldiers shifted his weight, and the salt grit under his boot made a sound like a small betrayal.

"That's—" The file leader looked at Merrin. "Is that true?"

"It's true," Merrin said reluctantly, "that Standing Rule Twelve exists."

"And does Rule Twelve specify a processing time?"

"Three days," Merrin whispered. "Minimum."

Wren smiled, and it cost them nothing, because it wasn't even a lie. This was the thing the surface had never understood about the court beneath the mountain: it was not cruel. Cruelty was inefficient. The court was *procedural*, and procedure was a wall with no top to it, and Wren had spent years learning to climb it from the inside.

"Then I file," Wren said. "Here. Now. Witnessed."

* * *

It took forty minutes.

Forty minutes, in the Throat of Gallery Nine, standing in bone-colored light while Merrin transcribed the encumbrance claim in triplicate — the court required triplicate; the court required triplicate the way other institutions required breathing — and Wren dictated it slowly, savoring every clause.

The claim itself was nonsense, and Wren knew it, and Merrin probably knew it. Wren asserted that the Dominic contract might contain an undisclosed co-signatory, and that no collection could proceed until all signatories were identified. There was no co-signatory. They both knew there was no co-signatory. But the claim was *plausible*, and plausibility was the only currency the court honored above gold.

The file leader paced. Twice he suggested they simply carry Wren along and finish the filing at the magistrate's chamber, and twice Wren pointed out — gently, helpfully, like a colleague — that a contested collection moved to the slow docket, and the slow docket required the claimant's presence at every stage, and dragging the claimant would constitute duress, which would void the claim, which would restart the clock.

By the time Merrin signed the last copy and blew on the ink, the light in the gallery had gone from bone-colored to the gray of exhausted lamps.

"Three days," Wren said, taking their copy with a small bow. "You see how it works."

"We'll see how it works," the file leader said, and there was something new in his voice now, something that had been there since roughly the twenty-minute mark: respect, sour and unwelcome, the kind a soldier develops for an enemy who fights fair. "But the audit order stands regardless. You're still absent without leave. That part isn't contested?"

"No," Wren said. "That part I'll wear."

Because that was the trade, and Wren had made it the moment the horn sounded. The encumbrance claim bought three days for the *contract*. The arrest bought minutes for the *people*. Odile needed minutes. Lena needed minutes. The cargo that was not cargo needed minutes. And Wren had exactly one self to spend, so they spent it where it purchased the most.

They put their wrists out.

Merrin hesitated with the binding-cord, and for a moment her round face did something complicated. "You could run," she said quietly. "Gallery Nine connects to Seven through the crawlway. I've seen the maps. I could write down that you escaped."

"And then it would say I escaped," Wren said. "In your hand. In the record. Forever." They shook their head. "I spent six years below learning what the record does to people, Merrin. I'm not going to let it say something false about me just because the truth is inconvenient. Write it plain: Wren of the Surface Detail surrendered at Gallery Nine, filed a claim, and went quietly. Write it plain, and someday, when you're the one holding the pen over someone's whole life, you'll remember what plain costs."

Merrin wrote it plain. Her handwriting shook, but it was legible, and legibility, in the court beneath the mountain, was next to godliness.

* * *

They marched Wren down through the galleries, and Wren used the march the way a miser uses coins.

Every corridor offered a procedure. At the junction with Gallery Eight, Wren requested a rest period under the Fatigue Provision, and got ten minutes. At the flooded stretch near the old brine cistern, Wren observed that the escort route deviated from the standard prisoner-transfer path by eleven paces, and the resulting debate — conducted with total seriousness, in the dark, ankle-deep in salt water — consumed another quarter hour while Merrin measured the deviation with a knotted cord. When a rat crossed their path, Wren formally noted it as an omen requiring interpretation under the Old Statutes, and the file leader, to his visible horror, discovered that the Old Statutes had never been repealed and that an uninterpreted omen rendered a transfer procedurally incomplete.

"You're doing this on purpose," the file leader said at last, somewhere around Gallery Five.

"Of course I'm doing it on purpose," Wren said. "That's what makes it legal."

He almost laughed. Wren saw it happen — saw the laugh start and get strangled by duty, saw him look away and press his lips together, and understood that this man, too, had once been someone who laughed in salt galleries, before the court finished making him into a function. Pity was useless. But recognition, Wren found, was not. Recognition was the one thing Wren could give these people that didn't cost anything and couldn't be confiscated.

At the doors of the magistrate's chamber — doors of black wood banded in iron, polished by a century of hands — Wren stopped and straightened their collar. Salt dust covered them from crown to boot. Their knees ached. Their lamp had died two galleries ago. None of it mattered. What mattered was the last thing they would ever say inside this mountain, and they intended to say it standing up.

"Ready?" asked the file leader, and his voice had gentled despite himself.

"I've been ready since the day I took their coin," Wren said. "Open it."

* * *

The magistrate received them in the long chamber with the hanging lamps, seated behind the desk that was older than the city above, and he rose when Wren entered, because the court was courteous to everyone, especially the condemned. That courtesy had frightened Wren once. Tonight it just looked like what it was: a machine so well-made it had learned to imitate a soul.

"Wren." The magistrate's voice filled the chamber like water filling a cup. "Audit Order 88-C. Absent without leave. Consorting with debtors. And now—" he lifted Merrin's triplicate, read it with genuine interest, "—an encumbrance claim alleging an undisclosed co-signatory. Bold. Poorly founded, but bold. Do you wish to withdraw it before I rule it frivolous?"

"No, Magistrate. Let it stand. Frivolous claims are still claims. You taught me that."

"I taught you that," the magistrate agreed, "and I have regretted the lesson several times tonight, I suspect, though I won't know precisely how much until my clerks report." He sat, and folded his hands, and regarded Wren across the desk with those patient, bottomless eyes. "Where is the taster, Wren? Where is the archivist? My ushers report that Miss Hartwell accepted an escort with suspicious grace, and that your companions' cargo crate has been unattended for some time. Tell me where they are, and the offer I made you three days ago remains open. Reinstatement. Your name restored to the rolls. The Dominic contract torn up before your eyes. All of it, for one sentence spoken plainly."

And there it was. The clean price, held out one last time, gleaming.

Wren had carried that offer up from this very chamber like a stone in the chest, and had set it down on a table over cold tea, and had watched Lena go white and Dominic go quiet, and had watched the two of them do the thing that had finally, after all the fractures and the stubbornness and the years of unsaid things, made Wren certain: they had chosen each other, out loud, together, whatever it cost either.

So the answer was already old news. It deserved better than old news. It deserved to be said plainly, in the plain hand, in the plain room, on the record.

"The offer was refused," Wren said. "Hours ago. Before we ever came down the salt stairs. You should know that your price was never in doubt — I told them within the hour, because keeping secrets from people you're about to risk your life beside is just theft with extra steps. They refused it. Both of them. Together." Wren lifted their chin. "Lena Hartwell said, and I quote her exactly because I know you value exactness: 'Tell him thank you, and tell him no.' Dominic said—" and here Wren smiled, because the memory was worth the smile, "—Dominic said, 'Tell him he can tear up the contract whenever he likes. Paper doesn't scare me. I've read his filing system.'"

Something moved behind the magistrate's eyes. Not anger. The court did not do anger. It was closer to the look of a man finding an error in a ledger he had personally balanced — surprise, and beneath the surprise, faintly, unwillingly, delight.

"They refused," the magistrate repeated.

"With prejudice," said Wren. "Witnessed by tea."

For a long moment the chamber was silent except for the hiss of the lamps. Then the magistrate inclined his head, a small precise motion, the acknowledgment of one professional to another.

"Then the offer dies with your silence, and I find I respect the manner of its dying." He gestured, and the file leader stepped forward with the binding-cord already looped. "Take them to holding. Note in the record that the prisoner cooperated fully, filed proper claims, and spoke truthfully under direct question. Full cooperation. Plain hand."

"Yes, Magistrate," said Merrin, and her voice cracked only a little on the word *plain*.

As they turned Wren toward the door, the magistrate spoke once more, softly, almost to himself — but the chamber was built to carry a magistrate's voice, and Wren heard every word.

"A refusal given freely, before the collection, with witnesses. Hmm. That has weight, Wren. Freely given things always do." A pause, and the sound of a page turning. "It may complicate the scales."

They marched Wren out. Down the black-banded doors, into the corridors of polite ushers and waiting rooms full of patient souls, toward holding, with salt dust in their hair and their wrists bound and their spine straight as a ruled line.

And behind them — muffled at first, then rolling up through the galleries like the mountain clearing its throat — the alarm began.

Not the audit horn. The other one. The deep one, the old one, the one Wren had heard only once in six years of service and had hoped never to hear again: the registry alarm, the sound the Spire's heart made when someone touched a page.

Wren smiled all the way down the corridor, bound hands and all.

Odile was in.
