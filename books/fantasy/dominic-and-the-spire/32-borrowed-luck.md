## 32. Borrowed Luck

The first gift arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing in particular except that Tuesdays were when Lena scrubbed the tasting spoons and therefore came upstairs late and tired and not entirely observant.

She noticed it anyway. A taster notices.

Her quarters at the guildhall were two rooms above the ledger office — a bedroom with a window that faced the salt-weathered east wall, and a sitting room where she kept her few luxuries arranged with the precision of a pharmacopoeia: three books, one chair, a kettle, a tin of good tea hidden behind the bad tea because Wren had once drunk the good stuff without apology and called it *inventory management*. Everything had its place. Everything had been in its place when she left that morning.

Now there was a bottle on her table.

It was small — the size of a plum — dark glass, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. No label. No note. It sat in the exact center of the table, which was somehow worse than if it had been shoved carelessly against the inkwell, because center-of-table said *I was placed here*, and placed things implied placers.

Lena did not touch it.

She circled it instead, the way she circled an unfamiliar dish before the king's supper: reading the vessel first, then the contents, then the intention behind both. The glass was old — older than guild stock; there was a bubble near the shoulder of it, a flaw no modern blower would leave. The wax was unbroken but had been broken once and resealed, which took either great skill or great arrogance. And there was something inside. Not liquid. She tilted the bottle toward the lamp and caught the shape of it: a curling wisp, pale as breath on cold glass, that moved when she moved the bottle. Moved like it was watching her move it.

A bottle of last words. She knew what they were the way every guild member knew — stock item, category four, *fragile even after bottling*. Someone's final sentence, captured by a deathbed-catcher before it could scatter, kept in suspension until someone paid for the privilege of hearing it. They were expensive. They were never given as gifts, because last words were the one commodity in the guild's entire economy that could not be returned.

Lena set it down very carefully and checked her door. Locked from outside, as always. Her window — third floor, shuttered, latch painted shut since the reign of the previous king's previous king. No dust disturbed on the sill. Whoever had left the bottle had done it the way everything in this building got done: impossibly, quietly, and without leaving a receipt.

She slept with the lights burning and told herself it was because she'd forgotten to snuff them.

* * *

In the morning the coin was under her pillow.

Not on the nightstand, not on the floor where it might have rolled. Under the pillow, directly beneath the hollow where her head had lain, so that she must have slept all night with her skull an inch from it and never felt a thing — which was either sleight of hand beyond mortal skill or a message about how thoroughly she could be reached while she dreamed.

It was a copper penny, worn nearly smooth, and it should have been nothing. Except that Lena, who tested everything, tested it, and the coin did something coins do not do.

She stood at the edge of the stairwell and flipped it. Heads. She flipped it again. Heads. Eleven times, over the rail, off her thumb, catching it flat or letting it strike the boards — heads, heads, heads. On the twelfth flip she let it fall past her hand entirely, and it bounced off the banister post, rolled along the corridor, dropped down the staircase, ricocheted off Wren's abandoned boot, and came to rest at her feet.

Heads.

"That," Wren said, coming around the corner with a stack of requisition forms and an expression of deep professional suspicion, "is a kind coin."

"I know what it is."

"Someone gave you a kind coin." Wren looked at her the way they looked at a ledger that refused to balance. "Kind coins are worth more than the building. More than two buildings. There are debtors in the under-city who'd sell a year of their lives for a coin that lands kindly, and somebody put one under your pillow and didn't even ask for a trade?"

"Apparently."

Wren crouched, examined the penny without touching it, then examined Lena. "You don't know who."

"No."

"You're not frightened."

Lena turned the coin over in her fingers. It was warm. It had been warm under the pillow too, though the room went cold at night and copper holds no heat for long. "I'm a royal food-taster," she said. "I'm frightened professionally. This doesn't frighten me. That's what frightens me."

Wren stood, brushed off their knees, and delivered the verdict with the flat certainty of someone who had spent years reading a subterranean court's moods. "Somebody's courting you."

"Somebody is *haunting* me."

"In my experience," Wren said, handing her the requisition forms she hadn't asked for, "those are frequently the same thing done with different levels of paperwork."

* * *

The letter came on Friday.

It was waiting on her desk in the ledger office, squared neatly against the blotter's edge, sealed with plain wax and no mark — and the moment she saw the direction written across its face, Lena stopped walking.

Because the handwriting was hers.

Not similar. Not a good imitation, and she would know a forgery; she'd spent a decade learning to spot them in the ledgers, and Odile Vasse had personally taught her every trick of the trade, the lifted nibs and the measured slants and the way forgers always pressed too hard on the letters they feared most. This was none of that. This was her own hand — the slight rightward lean she'd never managed to correct, the way her capital H opened like a door left ajar, the little tick she made after a word when her thoughts ran ahead of her pen. Nobody alive knew that tick. She barely knew it herself; it lived below the level of intention.

She sat down. She broke the seal. She read.

*If you are reading this, then you have already decided not to remember, and I am writing to the woman you became rather than the woman I am — which feels strange, like posting a letter to one's own future address.*

*There is a man you will meet, or have met, or are currently failing to avoid. He is tall in the way of people who stoop out of courtesy. He argues like an accountant and laughs like a boy getting away with something. He sold the most valuable thing he owned to save your life and has never once mentioned it, because he believes mentioning it would make the saving into a debt, and he refuses — absolutely refuses — to let love arrive looking like arithmetic.*

*You will test him the way you test everything. You will find no poison in him. This will annoy you considerably.*

*His name is Dominic. I am not telling you what he looks like, or where to find him, because you already know both, and because some things should be recognized rather than reported.*

*One more thing. When the memories come back — and I believe they come back, because otherwise I would not be writing this letter, and hope is the one ingredient I refuse to cut — when they come back, you will want to apologize. Don't. He hates apologies. Bring tea instead. Two cups. He'll understand.*

*L.H.*

Lena read it four times. Then she did what she always did with anything that entered her orbit claiming to be safe: she began to test it.

The paper was guild stock, the ordinary foolscap from the third drawer — but the age of it was wrong. Fresh paper, yet the ink had settled into the fibers the way ink does only after years, sunk in deep and slightly bloomed at the edges, as though the letter had been written long ago and delivered now, or written somewhere time behaved differently. She held it over the lamp. No invisible second layer rose in the heat. She wetted one corner and touched it to her tongue — an instinct she'd had trained into her by kings and poisoners alike — and tasted nothing but paper, sizing, and a faint ghost of chamomile.

Chamomile. She drank chamomile every evening. Her own ink never smelled of it, but her *hands* did, after her nightly cup, and anyone who had ever watched her write at close range —

Anyone who had watched her write at close range.

She set the letter down and pressed her palms flat against the desk until the wood bit back.

* * *

Odile examined the handwriting that afternoon, in the annex of the guildhall that still smelled faintly of the Spire's registry — chalk dust and old vellum and, beneath it, the mineral breath of the mountain itself.

She held the letter up to the light for a long time. Odile Vasse, who could forge a king's hand so perfectly that the king had once been unable to sign his own pardon, who had copied her own confiscated name out of a court registry and walked out owning herself — Odile, whose eye was the finest instrument in the city for detecting a lie written in ink, finally lowered the page.

"It's yours."

"It can't be mine. I've never written it."

"Lena." Odile set the letter down with the delicacy she reserved for documents worth more than the person holding them. "I authenticated my own forgery in front of a magistrate's clerk and slept fine afterward. I am telling you, with every technique I possess, including three that are technically crimes: this is your hand. Your hand, your pressure, your habits. Written by you, or by something that knows your hand better than you do."

"The date on the seal wax —"

"Is eleven days ago. Which is possible. Barely. If you wrote it eleven days ago and don't remember—" Odile stopped herself, and Lena watched the thought land on her face like a bird on a wire. "Ah," Odile said quietly. "Yes. Of course."

"Say it anyway."

"There's precedent. In your own file, so to speak." Odile tapped the letter, right over the signature. "*When the memories come back.* You paid for a man's soul with your memories of him. The court's scales accepted the payment. But nobody — and I include the magistrate, who is many things but thorough above all — nobody ever asked where the memories *went*. Payment doesn't mean destruction. It means transfer. The court holds them. Or—" and here Odile smiled, thin and strange and not entirely comfortable, "—or they're finding their own way home. Interest, Lena. Somebody filed an interest clause."

Lena stared at the letter. *He sold the most valuable thing he owned to save your life.* She had no memory of it. She had no memory of him, not truly — she had a colleague named Dominic who worked the far side of the ledger office, courteous and precise, who brought her reports when she was ill and argued with her about filing conventions with a warmth she'd privately filed under *guild camaraderie*. She had looked at him for weeks and felt nothing but professional regard, and had told herself that the occasional hitch in her chest when he laughed was indigestion, because Lena Hartwell did not grieve things she could not name.

And yet. Somewhere in her, in some drawer of herself she couldn't open, a version of her had written this letter and trusted her to receive it.

"He's doing this," Lena said. "The bottle, the coin, the letter. It's him."

"Obviously it's him. Who else would break into a locked room on the third floor to leave a kind coin under a taster's pillow?" Odile gathered her pens. "The question isn't who. The question is why now, and why this way — and whether he knows what he's spending. Because those items aren't gifts, Lena. They're installments. He's paying you back one impossible thing at a time, exactly like he said he would, and knowing Dominic, he hasn't priced a single piece of it properly."

* * *

That evening Lena conducted the closest thing she had to a controlled experiment.

She brewed two cups of chamomile. She carried them to the ledger office, where the lamps burned low and Dominic sat cataloguing a crate of bottled fog that had come in from the coast, and she set one cup beside his elbow without a word.

He looked at the cup. Then at her. Something moved through his face — quickly, carefully contained, the way a man contains a thing he has practiced containing for weeks — and he said, "Thank you," in the voice of someone accepting a gift whose full weight only he could feel.

"You take chamomile?" she asked. Testing. Always testing.

"Every evening," he said. "For years. I picked up the habit from—" and there he paused, a man standing at the edge of a cliff he had dug himself, "—from someone. She was very precise about steeping times."

Four minutes, Lena thought. She steeped hers four minutes and everyone else in the guild found that bitter and she had never told a soul.

"I'm precise," Lena said.

"I know," said Dominic, and drank his tea, and looked at her across the ledger desk with an expression she could not catalogue — patient and grieving and hopeful all at once, like a man watching a lock he'd made slowly, slowly begin to turn.

* * *

The gifts continued.

A ribbon that untied any knot it touched, left coiled on her windowsill like a sleeping snake. A thimble that fit her finger exactly despite being sized for someone with broader hands. A single pressed flower she had no memory of pressing, from a garden she had no memory of visiting, with a water stain on the petal shaped — she checked, because checking was what she did — shaped precisely like a laughing mouth.

Each one she tested. Each one passed. That was the unbearable part: whatever phantom hand was leaving these things in her locked rooms, it left only true things. Guild-grade impossibilities, authentic and unpoisoned, each one chosen with the intimate accuracy of someone who had watched her for years and loved what he saw.

She started leaving the window latch unpainted-shut. Just slightly. Just enough.

On the ninth night she found a new entry in her own ledger, written in her own hand in her own ink, dated that morning — a morning she had spent entirely in the tasting chamber. The entry was brief, and it was not about accounts:

*Borrowed luck, returned with interest: today the coin landed heads fourteen times and I laughed out loud in the stairwell and a man on the landing heard me and pretended he didn't, and his pretending was the kindest sound I've heard all year.*

Lena read it standing in the dark of her sitting room, the lamp unlit, the bottle of last words gleaming on the table where it had lived for three weeks now because she could not bring herself to open it and could not bring herself to move it.

Then she lit the lamp, took out pen and paper, and wrote beneath the entry, in her present hand, deliberately, so that whoever was reading her ledgers from wherever he was reading them would see:

*To the author of these gifts: I am a taster. I identify things by their effects. And I have identified you — not by name, but by weight. Whatever you are paying, you are paying alone, and I have stood on the other side of that transaction before, and I know how it ends.*

*Come argue with me in person. I'm told I'm difficult to win an argument against. Prove it.*

She signed it, sealed it, and left it on her table next to the bottle — addressed to no one, directed at everyone, a summons issued to a ghost.

In the morning the note was gone. In its place lay a single line on guild foolscap, in a hand she now knew almost as well as her own, steady except at the very end where the pen had pressed too hard on the letters a forger fears most:

*Tomorrow night. The debtors' table. Bring the coin — you'll want luck where we're going.*

*And Lena — steep it four minutes.*

Below the mountain, something ancient stirred in its registry and turned, with the patience of centuries, to a fresh page.
