## 33. The Court's Audit

Wren had spent eleven years learning the court's rhythms the way a diver learns currents: by feel, by pressure, by the particular silence that preceded a shift. So when the salt galleries began to hum — a low, mineral vibration that set the hanging lamps swaying in unison, all down the corridor, like a congregation bowing its heads — Wren knew before any clerk said a word.

An audit.

Not the ordinary kind, either. The ordinary kind was a clerk with a slate and a bad attitude, checking whether the waiting room's tea ration had been properly deducted from the owed. Wren had watched those audits pass a hundred times, had even been pressed into service as a runner once, carrying sealed ledgers from office to office while ushers pretended not to see. The ordinary kind touched nothing. The ordinary kind existed so that the court could say it had been audited.

This was not that.

This was the deep kind. The kind that came up from the foundations, from the registry heart itself, where every name ever written into the court's keeping sat in its row like a patient in a ward. When the heart audited itself, the whole mountain knew. Doors that had been locked for a century swung open for inventory. Clerks who had not spoken in decades were summoned to account for entries made before their grandparents were born. And the magistrate — the velvet-voiced, patient, terribly reasonable magistrate who had once offered Wren reinstatement in exchange for a taster's life — the magistrate was seen walking the corridors personally, reading spines.

Wren stood in the gallery above the Great Waiting Room and watched it happen.

The room itself had changed since the exchange. That was the first thing everyone noticed and the last thing anyone said aloud. The old woman — Mother Sable, though the court had never recorded her name, only her debt — was gone, freed by Odile Vasse's clerk's stamp and a straight face. And where she had sat for sixty years, patient as furniture, the bench was empty. But the emptiness had weight now. The other owed souls had begun, in the weeks since, to shift in their seats. To look up when footsteps passed. One of them, a man sold cheaply by a gambling grandfather four generations back, had asked an usher — *asked*, in words — whether reweighing was a thing that could be requested.

The usher had not known the answer. That was the trouble. Nobody below the mountain knew the answer, because the answer had never existed before, and the court was a machine built entirely of precedent, running now on fuel it had never been designed to burn.

*The exchange was legal.* Wren had heard the phrase four times in the past month, always in the same tone: a clerk's tone, defensive, the tone of a person repeating a wall's inscription to prove the wall was still standing. The exchange was legal. The memories had been itemized, weighed, accepted. The scales had balanced. The contract had dissolved. Dominic's soul had come home with an annotation in the margin of the prophecy book — *returned, with interest* — and the court had honored its own precedents and let him go, because the magistrate was bound by law above malice, and the law had been cornered by its own handwriting.

But *with interest* was still sitting in that margin. Wren had seen it. Wren, in the confused hours after the alarm, had been marched past the registry annex and had looked, with a debtor's reflexive hunger, at the open book on the clerk's stand. Two lines in Lena Hartwell's hand: *paid in full, remember to forget.* And beneath, in Dominic's furious, triumphant scrawl: *returned, with interest.*

Open-ended. That was the phrase the clerks kept using, in the tone of people describing a leak in a cellar wall. Open-ended. An interest clause with no stated rate, no term, no ceiling. Dominic had written it as a demand — *give him back or honor the debt with everything it grew* — and the court, cornered, had honored it. But a clause that open cut both ways. That was what the audit was for. That was what the humming was about.

Wren leaned on the gallery rail and watched the clerks below carry ledgers in both arms, like pallbearers.

* * *

The summons came on the third day of the audit, delivered not by usher but by a child.

That was new, and it was meant to be noticed. The court's ushers were its politeness made visible; to bypass them was to say, *this message has no courtesy in it, only weight.* The child was perhaps nine, salt-pale, dressed in the grey of the registry's junior scribes, and she stood in the doorway of Wren's assigned cell in the staff gallery holding a folded paper with both hands, the way one carries something hot.

"You're Wren," the child said. Not a question. "Formerly of the collection office. Currently of nothing, which is why I was told to find you. Nobody currently of something would take this."

Wren took the paper. The child watched, with the frank appraisal of someone trained early to witness, then turned and walked away without waiting for thanks. The court was teaching its children to be witnesses now. That was new too.

The paper was brief. It was also, Wren noted with a professional chill, in the magistrate's own hand — the same velvet hand that had written the offer of reinstatement, the same hand that had read the prophecy aloud like a menu, item by item, price by price.

*Wren. The audit continues. Your testimony in the matter of the Hartwell exchange remains the only firsthand account not yet entered into the record. You will attend tomorrow, ninth bell. You will not be paid. You will not be punished. You will be heard. These are not the same thing, and you know it. — M.*

Wren read it three times. *You will be heard.* In the court beneath the mountain, that was the most dangerous sentence in the language. Being heard meant being entered. Being entered meant becoming precedent. And precedent, once set, belonged to the court forever — it could be cited by anyone, for anything, in any direction. Wren had spent eleven years in the collection office learning exactly how a person's own words could be taken down, indexed, and used to weigh them later.

But Wren had also spent the last months learning something else. Something the court had not taught.

Wren thought about the debtors' table above ground, the arguments, the maps, the night Dominic had arrived uninvited and refused to be rescued. Thought about Mother Sable's empty bench. Thought about the man with the gambling grandfather, asking his one careful question. Thought about the fact that Wren had defected, had burned favors, had fought in the salt galleries not to escape but to buy minutes, and had told the magistrate the truth with dignity while being captured — *the offer was refused hours ago* — and had somehow, impossibly, not been destroyed for it.

The court did not destroy useful precedents. And Wren, it appeared, had become one: the spy who was offered a clean, reasonable price and refused it. The court had never had one of those before. The clerks didn't know what to do with Wren, and their not-knowing had, so far, been the safest place in the mountain.

*You will be heard.*

"All right," Wren said to the empty doorway, to the child already gone, to the humming walls. "Let's hear each other."

* * *

The hearing room was not the magistrate's chamber. That mattered too. The magistrate's chamber was velvet and courtesy and forms in triplicate; this was the old room, the deep room, the one Wren had only seen in diagrams — a rough oval of salt and black stone with the registry heart's pulse audible through the floor, a slow tidal beat like something enormous sleeping and dreaming of arithmetic.

The magistrate sat at the room's center, not on a throne — the court beneath the mountain did not do thrones, it did *desks* — but behind a desk of black stone so old its edges had gone soft. The magistrate looked, as always, like a person who had never once raised their voice and never once needed to. Grey-robed. Ageless in the way of well-kept furniture. Eyes like an auditor's: kind, and totaling.

"Wren." The magistrate gestured to the single chair across the desk. "Sit. You will notice there is no oath. The court has learned, recently, that oaths given under old assumptions may not hold under new ones. We prefer, now, to simply listen and see what is true."

"That's a change," Wren said, sitting.

"That is the audit." The magistrate folded their hands. "You are wondering why you are here. I will tell you before you spend the whole hour guessing, because guessing wastes both our time and I know you budget yours like a debtor. The exchange of Hartwell's memories for Dominic's soul was legal. It was weighed, itemized, and accepted by the scales. I want you to understand that I say this without resentment. The court does not traffic in resentment. Resentment implies the outcome was wrong. The outcome was not wrong. It was *precedent*, and precedent is the only thing we love."

"And the audit?"

"The audit is because precedent behaves like water." The magistrate's voice never changed register; it simply went on, level as a scale arm. "It finds every crack. When the soul of Dominic was returned *with interest*, an interest clause entered the law of this court with no rate, no term, and no ceiling. My clerks have spent a month trying to close it and cannot. It is not a flaw. It is a door. And through that door, the audit has found—" the magistrate paused, and for the first time Wren saw the magistrate pause deliberately, a courtesy, a courtesy that was also a weapon, "—one hundred and forty souls sold below their weighed value. Sold cheaply. Sold in grief, in haste, in ignorance of their worth, by people who did not understand what they were signing. The waiting room is being reweighed, Wren. Bench by bench. It will take years. It has already begun."

Wren kept their face still. Inside, something enormous turned over. *One hundred and forty.* The old woman's bench had not been an anomaly. It had been the first stone of an avalanche the court itself was now obliged to trigger, because the court loved precedent above everything, and the precedent said: *what was sold unfairly may be reclaimed.*

"And me?" Wren asked. "Why does the audit need a defected spy?"

"Because of the clause." The magistrate drew a single sheet of paper from the desk — one sheet, and Wren's debtor's eye catalogued it instantly: heavy stock, registry watermark, the kind of paper on which things became true. "The interest clause. *Returned, with interest.* Dominic claimed back his soul and wrote that the debt had grown. The court honored it. But interest, Wren, is a two-sided instrument. If the debt grew while held, then what was paid to release it may also have grown. The memories Hartwell paid — itemized, weighed, accepted — have been *held* by her for months now. They were returned to her in the hearing, yes, minus the one afternoon she kept paid forever. But the court's scales record something the hearing did not address." The magistrate slid the paper across the desk. "They record that what came back is not what left."

Wren read it. It was a weighing certificate — the court's driest, most bureaucratic document, and therefore its most terrifying. The figures meant nothing to Wren, but the summary line was written in plain words, the way the court wrote things when it wanted them undeniable:

*Returned memories exhibit variance from original weight. Variance consistent with growth. Growth, per the interest clause, remains claimable in kind.*

"In kind," Wren said carefully.

"Memories for memories. What her remembering has become since it returned — the new weight it has gained by being lived in, loved in, argued over — that increase is, by the letter of the clause, open to claim." The magistrate watched Wren with those totaling eyes. "By the court. Or by any party with standing. And here is why you are here, Wren, and why you will be heard: you are the only person in this mountain who has stood on both sides of a scale. You were offered Lena Hartwell and refused. You know what she is worth, in the only currency we trade. I am required by audit to ask a witness: is a thing that grows, when it is loved, still the same thing that was sold? Answer slowly. Whatever you say will be entered."

The heart pulsed through the floor. Somewhere above, Wren knew, it was morning on the surface — Lena Hartwell tasting the king's supper, cataloguing poisons by aftertaste; Dominic at the ledger desk, keeping his private book of a life he refused to let be forgotten; Odile teaching calligraphy to children who would never know what her own hand had bought back. A whole small civilization of people who had learned that debts could be argued with.

And the magistrate, patient as stone, had just asked Wren to hand the court a knife and tell it where to cut — or to say the words that would become the next precedent, the one that might protect them all, or undo them.

Wren thought: *whatever I say will be entered.*

Wren thought: *so make it worth entering.*

"Before I answer," Wren said, "I have a question of my own. You'll want to enter that too."

The magistrate inclined their head, a degree of curiosity that, from the court beneath the mountain, was practically astonishment.

"The variance," Wren said. "The growth. When you weighed what came back — did the scales say it grew *heavier*?" Wren leaned forward. "Or did they say it grew *lighter*? Because I've carried both, magistrate. I carried a debt for eleven years and it got heavier every year. And I've watched that woman carry her memories back, and I'm telling you as a witness: she carries them like they weigh nothing at all. So before you decide what can be claimed in kind — you should find out what kind of thing it is that gets lighter the more it's loved. Because I don't think your scales have a category for it. And I think," Wren said, standing, "that's why you really called me. Not to testify. To find out if the audit has already found something the court can't file."

The magistrate did not answer. The magistrate looked down at the weighing certificate, and for the first time in eleven years of watching, Wren saw the court beneath the mountain look — briefly, terribly — *uncertain*.

"Nine days," the magistrate said at last, quietly. "The audit concludes in nine days. Tell them above. Tell them the clause is open. Tell them—" and here the velvet voice, for one syllable, was not velvet at all, "—tell them we do not know what we are owed."

Wren walked out through the humming corridors, past the reweighed benches, past the man with the gambling grandfather who looked up as Wren passed — and Wren, who had spent a lifetime carrying messages like stones, carried this one up toward the light at a dead run.
