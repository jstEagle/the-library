## 34. Interest Due

The ledger did not sleep, and neither, lately, did Lena.

She had learned its habits the way she'd once learned the king's palate: by attention, by repetition, by the small betrayals of ink. Ledgers lied the way people lied — not outright, but in omissions, in margins, in the careful spacing between entries where a truth had been scraped away. And this one was lying to her now, or rather it was telling her something she did not want to hear, which amounted to the same discipline.

Three nights she had sat at the guildhall's long desk with a candle guttering and the salt smell of the mountain seeping up through the floorboards, and three nights the trail had led her downward instead of backward. The interest clause. *Returned, with interest.* Four words in Dominic's hand, written in the prophecy book's margin under pressure of the moment, witnessed by a magistrate who had no choice but to honor precedent — and left open-ended, because Dominic had been fighting for his soul and had not paused to define his terms.

Interest, in the court beneath the mountain, was never a courtesy. It was an obligation. She had read enough of their contracts by now to know that. Every debt in that velvet bureaucracy accrued; nothing stood still. A soul sold cheaply for a hundred years became a soul owed richly for a hundred more. The court did not forgive. It compounded.

Which meant that when the contract dissolved — when her memories of Dominic were weighed out item by item on those patient brass scales and accepted as payment — the payment itself had entered the court's economy. It had become an asset. And assets held by that court did not sit idle.

Her memories were earning interest somewhere in the dark.

She found the confirmation on the third night, in a folio Wren had smuggled up before the reforms began, a sampling of standard instruments used by the registry annex: *Held payments accrue at the discretion of the magistrate, per the doctrine of continuing weight.* Continuing weight. She read the phrase until it stopped looking like words. Somewhere below the salt, in a vault or a shelf or a drawer lined with green felt, there was a container — she imagined something like the guild's bottled last words, stoppered and labeled — holding ten years of teas and arguments and rain and his laugh, and every day it sat there it grew heavier, and every day it grew heavier it grew more valuable, and every day it grew more valuable the court had less reason to ever let it go.

Dominic didn't know yet. She had decided that much already, sitting alone with the candle. He would want to march down there and argue, and arguing was exactly wrong; she had watched him do it in the hearing chamber and seen how close the scales came to tipping against them out of sheer procedural spite. No. This claim had to come from her. It was hers. The memories had been taken from *her* ledger, out of *her* column, and if anyone was going to stand in that polite terrible waiting room and demand them back, it was the woman whose handwriting started all of it.

Besides — and here she allowed herself the small dishonesty she permitted herself perhaps twice a year — she missed him. Not the man beside her at the desk; the courteous colleague Dominic was easy to be around, pleasant as a well-kept archive. She missed the *knowing*. She missed opening a door and having her chest do the thing it apparently used to do. She had read her own letter describing a man worth remembering, tested the coin that landed kindly, drunk from the bottle of last words with the reverence of a taster approaching an unknown vintage — and every gift had only sharpened the absence, the way a beautifully written menu sharpens hunger in a fasting man.

The ledger trail pointed down. So down she would go.

* * *

Wren met her at the mouth of the old salt gallery two days later, at the hour when the city above was still arguing about breakfast and the mountain below had not yet begun its day either. Wren looked tired in the particular way of someone whose job had become reforming the institution they'd once spied on — a burden, Lena suspected, that Wren carried with more satisfaction than they'd ever admit but no less exhaustion.

"You've thought this through," Wren said. It wasn't a question. They studied her the way they studied corridor maps, checking for weaknesses.

"I've written it out." She patted the satchel at her hip, where the pages sat in their oilcloth wrap, drafted seven times, each draft tighter than the last. "Claim of right, citation of the interest clause, citation of my own exchange record, request for hearing. Three pages. Odile authenticated my handwriting, which felt absurd, given whose handwriting started everything."

"Did you tell him?"

"No."

"Lena."

"He'll try to come with me," she said. "And he can't. If we both stand in that chamber it becomes a negotiation between the guild and the court, and negotiations have middle grounds, and I don't want a middle ground. I want my memories. This is a single claimant filing a single claim. That's how their law works best — I've read it. One person, one debt, clean terms. He muddies it by existing."

"He'd say you're doing to him exactly what he did to you," Wren said quietly. "Buying something without asking."

The words landed where Wren had aimed them, dead center, and Lena let them sit there for a moment before answering. "Yes," she said finally. "He would say that. And he'd be nearly right, except for one difference." She adjusted the satchel strap. "When he bought my life, I never got a vote and never got a bill. When I get my memories back, he gets both. He'll find the receipt on the kitchen table with a full accounting. Let him be furious about the method. But he doesn't get to decide what's owed to me any more than I got to decide what was owed to him." She paused. "That's the whole argument of the last year, isn't it? Written small?"

Wren's mouth twitched — the almost-smile they rationed like hard currency. "You've spent too long around lawyers."

"I've spent too long around magistrates. It's worse." She turned toward the dark mouth of the gallery, where the salt glittered faintly like frost that had given up on being weather. "Walk me to the door?"

"The door opens for the owed," Wren said, falling into step beside her, lantern swinging. "It always has. Question is whether it opens for the owed *voluntary*." A beat. "We're about to find out."

* * *

It opened for her.

Not grandly — nothing in the court was grand, that was the trick of it. The door simply ceased to resist, the way a clerk ceases to resist when presented with correctly completed forms, and Lena stepped through into the warm mineral dark with her heart beating in the steady rhythm she kept for poison tastings: even, catalogued, unimpressed by danger.

The corridors were as Odile had described them, and as Lena remembered from the one other time she'd descended — politely terrible. Lamps of pale glass. Ushers in grey livery who bowed at precisely the angle that communicated respect without warmth. Waiting rooms furnished in deep quiet, occupied by souls who had learned patience the way stone learns water. An usher materialized at her elbow before she'd gone twenty paces.

"Mistress Hartwell." Her name, pronounced perfectly, filed perfectly. Of course they knew her. She was, after everything, a famous entry. "How may the court serve you?"

"I'm here to file a claim," she said. "A financial claim, arising under an interest clause. I'll need the appropriate form."

Something flickered across the usher's face — not surprise exactly; she doubted the court's staff were capable of surprise — but something adjacent to it. Recategorization. Visitors came down the mountain to plead, to bargain, to beg. They did not come to file.

"This way, mistress."

* * *

The claims office was a small round room with a desk, a chair for the clerk, and a stool for the petitioner — the stool deliberately lower, she noted, and deliberately uncomfortable, a piece of architectural rhetoric she refused to be moved by. She sat anyway, spine straight, and slid her three pages across the desk.

The clerk — a narrow person of indeterminate age with ink-stained cuffs and eyes like ledger lines — read. Lena watched them read. She had spent eleven years watching faces while tongues catalogued aftertastes, and she knew the taxonomy of a reader: the flicker at the first citation, the slow blink at the second, the stillness that meant *this is better drafted than I expected and I resent it.*

"You are invoking the doctrine of continuing weight," the clerk said at last.

"I am citing it. Whether it applies is what the hearing will determine." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used with the king's steward when the soup was fine and she wanted him to know she knew. "My memories of one Dominic Vael were tendered as payment under a formalized exchange, witnessed by your magistrate, recorded in your master ledger. Payment accepted. Under the doctrine, held payments accrue. Accrued value belongs to the debtor of record — in this case, me — unless the instrument specifies otherwise. I have read the exchange record. It specifies nothing."

"The court holds many payments."

"And returns none of them, which is why your waiting rooms are full of furniture that used to be people." She let that sit. Then, softer: "I'm not asking you to empty the waiting room. Not today. I'm asking about one asset, one clause, one line of one contract your own magistrate enforced to the letter six months ago. You bound yourselves to precedent then. I'm only asking you to be bound by it again."

The clerk looked at her for a long moment. Then they pulled a fresh form toward themselves, dipped a pen, and wrote in a hand so beautiful it could only have been trained here, in the school of velvet corridors. "Claim of Hartwell, Lena. Nature of claim: return of tendered payment plus accrued interest." The pen paused. "You understand that if the hearing finds for you, the court must weigh what has grown. Ten years of held memory compounds, mistress. What went down as payment may come back up as considerably more than payment. Scales require balance. Something may be demanded of you in return."

"I assumed as much," Lena said. "I brought my own scale, in a sense." She tapped the satchel. "I've itemized what I'm prepared to weigh against it. I'm a taster by trade. Precision is the whole of my craft."

The clerk finished writing, blotted the page, and stamped it — the sound small and final, like a bone setting. "A hearing is granted," they said. "Third bell tomorrow, the chamber of audience. You will stand where debtors stand." A fractional hesitation, the pen still hovering over its log. "May I ask — the party whose memory comprises the payment. Does he know you've come?"

"He'll know by tomorrow," Lena said. "There's a note on his kitchen table. Full accounting. He does hate an unexplained figure in a ledger."

The clerk almost smiled. Almost. In this court, she was learning, that was the equivalent of open laughter.

* * *

They gave her a waiting room for the night, because the mountain did not send petitioners back up in the dark — that would have been rude, and rudeness, in this place, was the only true crime. The room was clean and warm and had a window that looked out onto nothing, onto salt and lamplight and the slow breathing of the deep galleries. There was tea. She tasted it out of habit: chamomile, honey, a trace of something floral she couldn't name, no poisons, no pressures. Just tea, offered by a court that wanted its paperwork impeccable.

She slept badly, which was unusual for her, and dreamed, which was rarer. In the dream she was at the guildhall desk balancing columns and one of the columns was a river and Dominic was standing in it up to his knees, laughing, saying something she couldn't hear, and she woke with wet eyes and no idea why, which was — she was beginning to understand — the whole condition of her life now. Grief with the object filed separately.

In the morning an usher came for her, grey-liveried and punctual, and walked her through corridors she half-remembered from the audit-day chaos, except that now they were calm, orderly, reformed in the small ways Wren's office had managed and unreformed in all the ways that mattered. They passed the registry annex. They passed a door behind which, she was nearly certain, the old woman's garden decree was being implemented in some bureaucratic fashion — she heard trowels. The mountain adapted. That was its genius and its menace.

And then the doors of the chamber of audience, and the magistrate beyond them, seated, robed in that soft grey that was neither color nor absence of color, hands folded, face composed into an expression Lena had last seen worn like kindness and understood now to be something older than kindness: the patience of an institution that had outlasted everyone who ever hated it.

"Hartwell," the magistrate said. "Twice now you've stood in my chambers. Once as a thief of names." A pause, weighted, deliberate. "Today you stand as a creditor. The court notes the promotion."

"The court taught me the vocabulary," Lena said. "I only borrowed it."

"Indeed." The magistrate gestured to the debtor's mark on the floor — a small worn circle of paler stone, polished by centuries of standing feet, and Lena's stomach turned as she recognized it, because she had read the transcripts, because she knew whose boots had worn that shine first, ten years ago, a young man selling the only thing he had that the market valued. She stepped onto it anyway. It was warm. Everything down here was warm; the mountain's heat rose through the floors like a held breath.

"State your claim," said the magistrate.

Lena unfolded her pages. Her voice, when it came, was steady — the taster's voice, the ledger-keeper's voice, the voice of a woman who had catalogued three poisons in a single supper and never once let her hands shake in front of a king.

"My memories of Dominic Vael were tendered as payment and accepted by this court. Under your own doctrine of continuing weight, held payments accrue. The accrual belongs to me. I claim return of the principal — the memories entire — together with whatever interest the scales assess." She looked up. "And I claim it not as a plea. As a debt. You of all courts should understand the difference."

Silence, in the velvet corridors, had texture. This silence had weight, and grain, and the magistrate wore it comfortably.

"A bold framing," the magistrate said at last. "And — I will grant it this — a correct one. The clause was left open. Open clauses are the court's favorite food, and we have eaten very well these six months." Something moved behind the grey eyes that might, in a younger face, have been called appetite. "Very well, Mistress Hartwell. A hearing is granted — a full one, with testimony, with witnesses, with scales. You will have three days to gather your proofs and your speakers." The folded hands opened slightly, a gesture like a door. "But understand what you have asked for. If you win, the court pays. And this court has not paid anything in four hundred years. Whatever precedent you set will not stay contained to one jar of memories." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Every soul in my waiting room will hear that the scales tipped. Choose your witnesses carefully, creditor. You may find they choose to speak of more than you brought them here to say."

Lena bowed — the exact angle the ushers used, respect without warmth — and turned for the doors, her mind already moving faster than her feet, drafting lists, summoning names: Dominic and his reconstructed ledger, Odile and her authenticating eye, Wren and their armory of citations, the old woman and her sixty years of patient furniture-silence.

Behind her, unhurried, the magistrate added one sentence more, pitched to follow her out like a shadow:

"And do bring the archivist. I find myself curious what a returned soul has to say about interest."

The doors closed on the words, and Lena stood alone in the lamplit corridor, heart hammering the steady poison-taster's rhythm, understanding at last what she had done: she hadn't filed a claim.

She'd opened a trial.
