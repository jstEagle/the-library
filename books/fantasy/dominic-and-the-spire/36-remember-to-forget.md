## 36. Remember to Forget

The Hearing of Hartwell had run for six hours, and by the end of it the chamber beneath the mountain had stopped feeling like a courtroom and started feeling like something older and stranger: a kitchen where an argument had gone on too long, everyone still seated because no one wanted to be the first to leave.

Lena felt it in her hands first. They had gone steady — steadier than they'd been in weeks — the way they always did when she finished a tasting. There was a moment after every plate, after every cup, when the verdict was made and there was nothing left to do but say it plainly. She lived for that plainness. She had built two lives out of it.

The magistrate sat at the high bench with the master ledger open before them, page 412 turned so the whole court could see it: *paid in full, remember to forget*, in her own handwriting, dated in ink older than she was. Beside it lay Dominic's reconstructed ledger — thirty-two years of tea and arguments and small kindnesses, itemized by a man who refused to let forgetting stand — authenticated twice over by Odile Vasse, who had testified in the flat, unimpressed voice of someone who could forge anything except forgiveness and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

Wren stood at the counsel's rail, having cited precedent from four centuries of subterranean law until the clerks stopped checking and started nodding. The old woman from the waiting room — Maren, sixty years sold, patient as furniture no longer — had spoken last, and her voice was still hanging in the air like smoke that wouldn't rise.

*"I waited,"* she had said. *"Sixty years, I waited, and no one ever argued for me. If you let this one go back empty-handed, then waiting is all any of us were ever worth."*

Now the magistrate folded their hands — long grey hands, courteous even now — and looked down at Lena as though she were the last course of a very long meal.

"The court has heard the claim," they said. "Memory freely given. Memory freely reclaimed. The guild argues this sets new law." A pause, precise as a knife laid flat on a table. "The court agrees."

Lena's breath went out of her all at once, and beside her she heard Dominic make a sound he would deny making later — half laugh, half something broken finally setting right.

"However," said the magistrate.

There it was. Lena had known there would be a however since she was nine years old, standing on a stool to reach the king's supper tray, learning that nothing in either of her worlds was given without a counterweight. She straightened her spine and gave the bench her full attention, the way she did when a dish arrived whose smell she didn't recognize.

"The scales demand balance," the magistrate continued. "You ask us to return what was paid. Very well. But payment was made, and payment cannot simply vanish — the ledger will not permit it, and neither will we. If your memories come home, then something of equal weight must remain behind. Forever."

"Equal weight," Lena repeated. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that later, though at the time she barely noticed. "You're asking me to choose which memory stays paid."

"I am asking you to choose which memory stays *gone*," the magistrate corrected gently, which was worse. "One. A single entry. The scales are not greedy; they have never been greedy. But they are exact."

The chamber stirred. Wren started forward and Odile caught their sleeve. At the rail, Dominic had gone very still, and Lena knew without looking that his jaw was set in the particular way it set whenever he was about to do something noble and unforgivable, like offer himself again.

She spoke before he could. "I want to understand the terms exactly."

"Of course." The magistrate almost smiled. They were the kind of creature, Lena thought, who respected a well-argued claim more than any plea, and she intended to give them a claim worth respecting. "You will walk the registry heart. Every memory the court holds of you — those traded, those weighed, those witnessed aloud during the exchange — will be laid open to you, as a taster lays out a meal. You will examine each one. And you will select one to remain here, struck from you permanently, its weight satisfying the balance. Choose well. The scales will know if you try to cheat them with something worthless, and the whole return will void."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Dominic's soul returns anyway." The magistrate spread their hands. "That debt was dissolved; precedent binds us. But your memories stay ours, held in trust against a balance that never comes due. You would live as you lived these past months — courteous, competent, complete, and missing him." Their grey eyes flicked, briefly, to Dominic. "We do not recommend it. We have watched you both for some time. It does not suit either of you."

*They've watched us,* Lena thought, and found, oddly, that she wasn't angry. Of course they had. The court beneath the mountain did not want cruelty; it wanted contracts, and contracts required witnesses. Somewhere in this tower grown downward into stone, there were probably shelves of observations about her, filed in triplicate, and she decided she would rather not know.

"I'll need time," she said.

"You may have until the scales cool," said the magistrate, and gestured toward the far wall, where the great brass scales of the registry hung from chains thick as her wrist. The pans were still swaying faintly from the morning's weighing, and Lena understood that this was the court's idea of mercy: a deadline measured in metal settling into stillness. Perhaps twenty minutes. Perhaps less.

* * *

They gave her a small antechamber off the registry heart — a room clearly designed for people about to sign things, with a table, two chairs, and a lamp that burned without flame. Dominic closed the door behind them, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

Then he said, "Take one of mine."

"No."

"Lena—"

"You don't get to pay for me twice." She said it quietly, which somehow made it land harder; she watched it hit him like a hand laid flat on the chest. "Ten years ago you bought my life without asking. Three days ago you told Wren to take the magistrate's offer. I have spent a decade being protected by your decisions, Dominic Sandoval, and I am done with it. This one is mine. Sit down."

He sat. He was smiling a little, she noticed — helplessly, the way he smiled when she balanced a ledger to the copper or named three poisons by aftertaste. "You've changed," he said.

"I've remembered," she corrected. "There's a difference."

And there was. That was the thing no one had warned her about — that the memories coming back would not arrive like water filling a vessel but like a city relighting, district by district, street by street, lamps she hadn't known were dark coming up one after another until she was standing inside her own life again. The tea, cold between them, the night of Wren's confession. The practice run, chalk outlines and borrowed luck, laughing until the chalk dust got in everything. Ten years ago, the trial, the pardon, the girl condemned as a scapegoat who never knew why she lived until a ledger told her. His mother, forgetting him. The velvet terms. Page 412.

*Paid in full, remember to forget.*

She had written that. Some version of herself, standing where she stood now, had weighed this exact choice and left herself a note across a century of ink. And the note said: remember to forget.

Not *choose wisely*. Not *save yourself*. Remember to forget. As if future-Lena had known that present-Lena would need reminding that letting go was permitted — that it was, in fact, part of the work.

"Dominic," she said. "Tell me about the small things."

He blinked. "The small things?"

"When you rebuild the ledgers — you told me, in the hearing, that you reconstructed our history item by item. What did you write down? Not the big entries. Not the sale, not the descent, not page 412." She leaned forward. "What were the small things?"

He considered her for a moment, and then, because he was Dominic and had never once been able to refuse her a straight question, he began to recite. "The afternoon you catalogued the entire stock by smell with your eyes shut and got forty-one out of forty-five right, and argued about the four for an hour. The time the roof leaked over the vault and we moved the folios at midnight and you made me hold the lamp and criticized my lamp-holding. The day you discovered the bottled last words from the widow on Candlemaker Row and cried, and denied crying, and I never mentioned it again until now, for which I apologize in advance." He paused. "The ordinary Tuesdays. There were a lot of ordinary Tuesdays. I wrote down most of them."

"The ordinary Tuesdays," Lena repeated softly.

Something was assembling itself in her mind with the same clean click she felt when a poison announced itself in an aftertaste — bitter almond, or sweet rot, or the metallic whisper of nightshade dressed up in honey. The scales wanted equal weight. One memory, forever gone. Everyone in that chamber would expect her to hoard the treasures: the confession, the sale, the reunion-to-come. To guard the crown jewels of her own heart and surrender something she'd barely notice.

But she had spent her whole life knowing what she was actually tasting beneath what she seemed to be tasting. Pressure disguised as poison. Poison disguised as supper. And now, beneath the obvious answer, another flavor entirely.

If she kept only the grand moments, she would be a woman with a handful of jewels and no pockets — dramatic, incomplete, wrong. The self wasn't made of climaxes. The self was made of Tuesdays.

"I'm going to walk the registry," she said, standing. "Alone."

"Lena—"

"Dominic." She touched his face, briefly, and felt him lean into her palm the way he probably didn't know he did. "Whatever I choose, I need you to promise me you won't argue with it. Not afterward, not ever. Can you do that?"

His jaw worked. "That depends entirely on what you choose."

"It shouldn't. It should depend on whether you trust me."

A long silence. Then, with the expression of a man signing away something enormous: "I trust you."

"Good." She smiled. "Because I'm about to do something impossible, and I'd hate for you to ruin it."

* * *

The registry heart opened for her the way the outer door had opened for the owed — silently, inevitably, as if it had been expecting her for a century, which, she supposed, it had.

The memories of Lena Hartwell hung in the brass-and-stone light like bottles in the guildhall's deepest vault. Not literally — or not only literally; the court beneath the mountain had a bureaucratic genius for making metaphor into inventory — each one labeled, shelved, cross-referenced. She walked the aisles and read her own life in a clerk's handwriting that was also, impossibly, hers.

Here: the first time she tasted for the king, aged nine, terrified, precise. Here: her mother's voice, teaching her that a taster's tongue must never flinch. Here: the guildhall door, the first night, rain, laughter. Here: the exchange itself, itemized — *the guildhall, the tea, his laugh* — each memory she'd surrendered to buy Dominic's soul, returned now and awaiting her signature.

The grand ones were all present, glowing faintly with importance. She passed them with a taster's discipline. She was looking for something else.

She found it near the bottom shelf, unlabeled except with a date eleven years old.

An afternoon. An utterly ordinary afternoon. She took it down and it opened around her like a room:

The guildhall, late summer, dust in the light. No crisis. No prophecy. Dominic at the ledger desk arguing with a shipment manifest, and her at the window shelling peas — someone had talked her into buying fresh peas, an extravagance, and she'd regretted it immediately and loved it completely. He had read a line from the manifest wrong three times because he was watching her hands instead of the page. She had thrown a pea pod at him. He had caught it without looking, which was insufferable, and then said, without any warning at all, *"This. Whatever else happens — I want you to know I'd sign for this."*

And she had laughed at him and said, "Sign for peas? You're a fool, Sandoval," and he had said, "Yes, but a solvent one," and the light had moved across the floor, and the afternoon had ended, and neither of them had known it was anything.

It was nothing. It was everything. It was an ordinary Tuesday wearing summer clothes.

Lena stood in the aisle of her own soul and understood, at last, what future-Lena's note had meant. *Remember to forget.* Not the sale. Not the sacrifice. Those were debts, and debts could be discharged, renegotiated, appealed. But this — this small, unrepeatable, perfect nothing — this was the one thing she could never get back once she knew its weight. The moment you name an afternoon sacred, you've already lost the afternoon. It becomes an artifact instead of a life.

Someone had to keep it paid forever. Someone should.

"A self should keep one mystery," she said aloud, to the shelves, to the scales, to whatever version of her had written the note across a century. "Even from herself."

She carried the afternoon to the great brass scales and laid it in the pan with both hands, gently, the way she set down the king's final cup.

The scales considered. The chains hummed. And then — slowly, with the terrible courtesy of the court beneath the mountain — the pans settled into perfect balance, and the afternoon lifted from her hands like a bird deciding to leave, and was gone.

She felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth: a bright, strange gap, an ache shaped exactly like something that used to be there. She knew an afternoon existed. She knew she had chosen it. She could see its label in her mind — date, season, dust in the light — and beyond the label, nothing. Velvet dark. A locked door she had locked herself, smiling.

Her eyes were wet. She wiped them with her sleeve and found, when she checked, that she was grinning.

"Well?" said the magistrate's voice behind her, soft as filing paper. "How does it weigh?"

"Like enough," Lena said. "Like exactly enough."

* * *

They released Dominic's soul at sundown, underground sundown, the great lamps dimming to amber while clerks stamped things in triplicate and the machinery of a century-old contract unwound with a sound like a very large book closing. The annotation appeared on the registry page in real time — Odile swore she watched it happen — the ink blooming across the entry in a hand everyone recognized: *voided, see p. 412*, and beneath it, newer, bolder, in ink that smelled faintly of the guildhall's lamp oil:

*returned, with interest.*

Dominic read it upside-down across the clerk's desk, and Lena watched his face do several things in quick succession — disbelief, relief, and then a slow kindling she'd seen only twice before, the look he got when he spotted an impossible thing and started wondering whether it could be done.

"Interest," he murmured. "Open-ended clause."

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said it with your whole face."

Above them, the alarm that Wren's stand had bought — minutes, precious minutes, dignity in defeat — was winding down into silence, because there was nothing left to pursue. The contract was dead. The soul was home. In the Waiting Room of the Owed, somewhere below, Maren was walking out through doors that had never opened for her before, and clerks who had spent careers saying *no* were discovering, with visible discomfort, that precedent now obliged them to say *maybe*.

The five of them gathered at the foot of the ascent stairs — the smuggler-archivist with his soul back in its customary place behind his ribs; the forger holding a certified copy of her own name like a deed to a house; the debtor-spy gray-faced and triumphant, carrying a court reform commission they hadn't asked for; the old woman's blessing trailing them like light through salt. And Lena, complete, minus one afternoon she would never miss because she could never know it.

Dominic offered her his arm for the climb. She took it.

"Forty flights of salt stairs," he said. "Think of it as the last impossible thing of the day."

"The day isn't over," Lena said. "And I've decided something on the way down here, which I intend to tell you at the top, over tea, and you're going to argue with me about it, because that's what we do."

"That sounds ominous."

"It isn't. It's accounting." She smiled up at him, and the smile had the missing-afternoon ache in it and the joy too, braided together like everything worthwhile. "Equal weight, Sandoval. The scales taught me that much. Everything returned has to be matched by something kept — and I've been thinking about what *you* owe."

They climbed. Above them, thin as a coin, the first true daylight of the world showed through the mine shaft like a door left politely open — and Lena climbed toward it carrying one locked room in her heart, one mystery she had paid for gladly, and a question forming on her tongue that she would spend the rest of her life, and several cups of tea, learning how to ask.
