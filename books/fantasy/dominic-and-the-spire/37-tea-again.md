## 37. Tea, Again

The hearing ended the way avalanches end — not with a crash but with a settling, a long quiet in which everyone waited to see what would hold.

Lena walked out of the court beneath the mountain with her own name in one pocket and a debt in the other. The magistrate had been scrupulous to the last: the memories of Dominic Vane were returned to Hartwell, L., effective upon ascension, with one exception to be named by the claimant and held in perpetuity by the court. She had chosen before she left the registry heart. She had chosen smiling.

Now it was three days later, evening, rain on the guildhall roof, and the memories had not come back.

That was the part no one had warned her about. She had assumed — everyone had assumed — that restoration would be an event. A door opening. A flood. Instead there was only this: a man sitting across the ledger desk from her, pouring tea with the particular care of someone who had rehearsed the gesture, and inside Lena a silence where a decade should have been.

She knew *about* him the way you know about a country you've never visited. She had read his file. She had read her own marginalia. She had read, last night, the private ledger he'd kept — the reconstructed history of them, every tea and every argument copied out in his careful archivist's hand — and she had wept over it the way you weep at a stranger's funeral: sincerely, and from a slight distance.

"You don't have to do this," she said now, watching him pour. "The tea. You've made it four times this week."

"I'm calibrating," Dominic said. "Ten years of calibration. I refuse to be rushed."

"It's tea."

"It's *evidence*," he said. "Everything is evidence. That's rather the point of me." He slid the cup across the desk. "Chamomile cut with dried pear blossom, honey from the north-facing hives, and exactly one drop of brandy, because you once told me that tea without brandy is just warm water with opinions."

Lena looked down into the cup. Somewhere beneath her sternum, something shifted its weight — not memory, not yet, but the *shape* of a memory, like furniture moved under a sheet.

"That's absurd," she said slowly. "And it sounds like something I'd say."

"It is. You did. Page forty-one of my ledger, if you want citations."

"I read your ledger."

"I know. Odile told me you read it twice and took notes." He smiled — that crooked, unguarded thing she'd seen in sketches and descriptions and one surviving portrait Odile had drawn from recollection, all of which had prepared her not at all for how it landed when aimed directly at her. "Notes on your own life. Only you."

"Verification," said Lena. "I'm a taster. I don't swallow anything whole."

"No," he agreed softly. "You never did."

* * *

They talked the way two people talk when they are both pretending this is easy. Guild business first — the charter amendments, Wren's reports from the reform office below, the slow bureaucratic emptying of the waiting room, souls reclaimed one reweighed contract at a time. Then the smaller things: Odile teaching calligraphy to guild children who forged their homework; the old woman's garden coming up green above ground for the first time in sixty years, beans and marigolds and one stubborn rose she claimed had been sold to her soul's account and was therefore legally hers.

Lena listened. Lena answered. And underneath the conversation she kept waiting for herself to arrive.

Because here was the unbearable arithmetic of it: she could narrate their entire history back to him. She could say *you sold your soul in the winter of the scapegoat trial; you signed knowing what forgetting costs because you'd watched your mother lose you first.* She could say *page 412 says paid in full, remember to forget.* She had all the facts filed and cross-referenced, and none of it was hers, and all of it was hers, and the difference between those two states was the difference between reading a menu and tasting the food.

"You're doing it again," Dominic said.

"Doing what?"

"The accounting face. The one where you balance something that won't balance." He turned his cup a quarter-rotation on its saucer, precisely, the way she'd learned he did everything. "It hasn't come back yet. Has it."

It wasn't a question, so she didn't insult him with a lie. "No."

"And?"

"And nothing." She set her cup down harder than she meant to. "Dominic, I have read every record. I know what I'm supposed to feel when I look at you. I know the exact inventory — the guildhall, the tea, the argument about the trade, the ten years of — of whatever we were. It's all catalogued. It's all *true*. And when I look at you I feel—" She stopped. Started again, more honestly. "I feel polite."

Something crossed his face and was gone before she could name it. Not hurt. He'd had three days to spend his hurt already, and he'd apparently spent it somewhere she couldn't see.

"Good," he said.

"*Good?*"

"Polite is honest. Polite is the truth right now, and I'd rather have your truth than a performance of something warmer." He leaned back. "You told the court you wanted them back without theater. No dramatic reunion, you said, per your own marginalia. So we don't do theater. We do tea. We wait. Memory isn't a door, Lena — it's weather. It comes back when it comes back."

"When did I say that? About the door?"

"Page sixty-three. You were arguing with me about whether the sale was fair. You always argued about whether the sale was fair."

"Did I win?"

"Never once." His eyes crinkled. "You'll want to know that going in."

* * *

So they picked up the argument. Of course they did — it was the oldest thing between them, older than the ledger, older than the tea, and if her feelings hadn't survived the crossing then at least the argument had, waiting for her on the far side like a lit window.

"You bought my life without asking," Lena said. She had said it before, in another life, at this same desk. She knew that the way you know a road you've walked in dreams. "You went down into the dark and you traded yourself for a girl you barely knew, and you never asked whether I wanted to be owed."

"I didn't buy your life," Dominic said, in the tone of a man correcting a citation for the hundredth time and still not tired of it. "I purchased a pardon. Your life was never for sale. That was the whole point."

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean, and it's wrong, which is why I keep correcting it." He set down his cup. "You were nineteen. Condemned as a scapegoat for a poisoning you didn't commit, because the king needed a taster to blame and you were the cheapest name in the ledger. There was no version of events in which asking you was possible. You were in a cell. Asking you required a conversation, and conversations require both parties to be alive next week."

"So you decided for me."

"So I *acted*," he said, "in the only window that existed. Which is what you did, three weeks ago, in the registry heart. You traded your memories for my soul. Did you ask me first?"

"That's different."

"Why?"

"Because—" She reached for the distinction and found, infuriatingly, that he was already holding the other end of it. "Because I knew what it cost. Because you'd taught me—"

"You knew what it cost because I'd paid it first," he finished quietly. "Yes. That's the part I've never managed to regret loudly enough for you. Everything you spent, you spent knowing, because I'd shown you the price list. So either we're both guilty of the same mercy or neither of us is. Pick one. But pick it fairly, because I've had ten years to prepare my case and Odile has annotated my exhibits."

Despite everything — despite the politeness, despite the fog where a decade should be — Lena felt her mouth twitch. "You rehearsed this."

"Every day. In front of the vault mirror. Wren timed me."

"How long is your case?"

"Four hours, with intermission."

"I have tea service at dawn."

"Then we continue tomorrow." He said it lightly, but under the lightness was something steady and patient as masonry: *we have time; I built us time; I will outlast this fog brick by brick.*

And Lena looked at him across the desk — this man who was a stranger wearing a decade of intimacy like a coat that fit someone else better — and thought: *he is not waiting for me to love him. He is waiting for me to arrive.*

There was, she admitted, something in that. Something warm. Not memory. Just warmth, unattributed, like heat off a stone after the sun has gone.

"Fine," she said. "One hour tonight. Then I sleep."

"Agreed. Exhibit one." He opened the private ledger to a marked page and turned it toward her. "The night we met. You'll want to correct my version. Everyone does."

* * *

He read. She corrected. This, it turned out, was the shape of them: he supplied the record, she audited it, and somewhere in the friction the past came alive in a way the documents alone never managed.

"You wrote that I was calm at the trial."

"You *were* calm. Wren said you thanked the guards."

"I was terrified. Calm was the performance — the same trick I use with poison. You can't let the tongue know it's dying or the whole body panics." She pulled the ledger toward her, found a pencil among the pens (his small flinch of horror at graphite near vellum was, she suspected, also documented), and wrote in the margin: *not calm. acting.* "Note the correction."

"Noted." He watched her write, and something in his expression went soft and strange. "Your hand hasn't changed."

"Should it have?"

"No. It's just — I've spent three weeks looking at that handwriting in old books, telling myself it would come home eventually. And here it is, correcting my margins, exactly as arrogant as ever."

"It's not arrogance if I'm right."

"It's *always* both with you. That's page twelve."

She laughed — short, surprised at itself — and the laugh did something the facts hadn't. For half a second the guildhall smelled different: beeswax and rain and something herbal she couldn't name. For half a second she almost remembered being someone who laughed at this man regularly, who had a whole archive of laughs assigned to him.

Then it passed, and she was polite again, and he saw it pass and didn't pretend otherwise.

"There it was," he said gently.

"What?"

"You, for a moment. The other you. The one with the decade." He folded his hands. "It comes like that. Weather. Don't chase it."

"Dominic." She put the pencil down. "What if it doesn't come? What if the court returns everything except the part that matters — what if I get all ten years back and they're just... records? A biography instead of a life?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, even questions designed to wound him.

"Then I start again," he said. "New ledger. Volume two. I've done it before — I fell in love with you once across a ledger desk, with no prophecy and no court and no reason at all except that you balanced impossible accounts and drank tea with brandy in it and lied beautifully exactly once a year. I can do it again. I'm very thorough."

"That's not — you can't just *decide* to—"

"You can't?" He raised an eyebrow. "You decided to walk into the mountain and pay a decade of yourself for a man you'd already lost once to paperwork. Deciding seems to be our whole family tradition."

*We*, she noticed. He'd said *our family tradition.* As if the guild were a bloodline. As if he were certain — had perhaps always been certain — that she belonged to it, to him, regardless of what her memory carried across the border.

Lena looked into her cold tea and felt the strangest sensation: grief with no object, yes, but underneath it, stubborn as a root under pavement, something that refused to grieve. Something that kept insisting, without evidence, without citation, that the man across the desk was not a stranger she was studying but a room she'd lived in, and rooms remember their people even when the people forget the rooms.

"Tell me about the argument," she said. "The real one. The one we had before I went under."

His breath caught — a small thing, quickly mastered, but she was a taster; she caught things.

"You're sure? It wasn't kind."

"Neither am I, allegedly. Go on."

"We fought about whether the trade was fair," he said. "Same as always. You said I'd bought your life without asking. I said a pardon isn't a life. We went around it for an hour and got nowhere, because we're both too proud to say the true thing." He paused. "You left for the descent without us saying it."

"And the true thing was?"

"Mine was that I didn't sell my soul for you." His voice was very even, the way voices go when they're carrying too much weight to risk unevenness. "I sold it because there was no world worth keeping that had you dead in it at nineteen. Those aren't the same sentence. The first one makes you a debtor. The second one just makes me a man who knows what matters. I never said it because saying it would make the trade about my feelings instead of your freedom, and you'd have hated that worse than the trade."

Lena sat very still.

The fire ticked. Rain worked the roof. And under her ribs, under the fog, something enormous shifted its weight — not arriving, not yet, but leaning toward arrival the way a wave leans before it breaks.

"Say it now," she whispered.

"I just did."

"Say it again. Slower."

He smiled — that crooked, unguarded thing — and said, slower: "There is no world worth keeping that doesn't have you in it. There never was. The sale was never the point. You were always the point."

And Lena Hartwell, who had tasted three poisons in one supper and never flinched, whose precision was legend, whose whole self was built on cataloguing what entered her — felt something enter her that no ledger had ever held.

It came not as a flood but as recognition. The smell of beeswax sharpened until it was *this* guildhall, *their* guildhall. The taste of the tea bloomed backward through time — chamomile, pear blossom, one drop of brandy, *warm water with opinions* — and suddenly she was drinking it at this desk a thousand nights, and the desk knew her, and the chair knew her, and the man across from her was not a stranger wearing a decade but the decade itself, sitting patiently, waiting for her to finish her sentence.

Because there was a sentence. She could feel it — unfinished, ten years old, hanging in the air of some night she'd forgotten. He had started it. She had never answered.

"Dominic," she said, and her voice cracked open like ice on a river in spring. "Ten years ago. The night before the collection date. You started to tell me why you really did it, and I interrupted you, and you never finished. What were you going to say?"

He stared at her.

"Lena—"

"Finish it. Please. Finish it and let me answer, because I've been answering it in my bones for three days without knowing the question."

He set down his cup with hands that were not quite steady. When he spoke, it was barely above the rain.

"I was going to say that it wasn't fair," he said. "That it was never fair, that I knew it, that buying someone's life without asking is a theft no matter how good the reason — and that I'd do it again tomorrow, and that the only apology I had was to spend the rest of my life making sure the theft was worth it. That was the sentence. Ten years. I never got to the end of it."

The fog broke all at once, everywhere, like a dam giving way — the guildhall and the tea and the arguments and the vault ledgers and the night he came home smelling of the mountain and lied to her with smooth words while a contract burned a hole in his coat — all of it, all of it, *hers* —

And Lena finished it.

"—and it *wasn't* fair," she said, tears running freely now, laughing and crying in the same breath, "and neither was mine, and I'd do it again tomorrow too, so we're even, you impossible man, we are finally, *finally* even—"

She came around the desk and he stood and they held each other in the middle of the guildhall, among the shelves of bottled last words and borrowed luck, and it was not dramatic. It was quiet. It was two people setting down a weight they'd carried on opposite sides of the same road, and finding that their arms remembered the shape of each other perfectly.

"Page sixty-three," Dominic murmured into her hair. "You owe me a concession. The sale stands ruled unfair by both parties."

"The sale stands ruled unfair," she agreed, "by both parties, unanimously, forever." She pulled back just enough to look at him, and oh, there he was — the crooked smile, the ink-stained fingers, the eyes she'd been politely admiring for three days and *knowing* for ten years. "I kept one memory away, did you know? The court demanded a price that stays paid. One afternoon, forgotten permanently."

"Which one?"

"A small one. An ordinary afternoon. I chose it on purpose." She smiled through the tears. "A self should keep one mystery. Even from itself. Especially from itself."

"Which afternoon, though? I need to know what I'm not allowed to mourn."

Lena shook her head, and the smile turned sly, and it was entirely, completely her.

"That," she said, "is the mystery."

He groaned. She laughed. Outside the rain gentled, and on the desk between the cooling teapot and the open ledger, the prophecy book lay closed — its final margin note still drying in her present-tense hand — and beneath it, tucked halfway out of its cover, a single blank page waited, labeled in fresh ink:

*413.*

Lena saw it over his shoulder and went still.

"Dominic," she said. "Why is there a page 413?"

He turned. Looked. And his face did something complicated — surprise, and recognition, and the particular expression of an archivist who has just realized a book is not finished with them yet.

"I didn't put that there," he said.

"Neither did I."

From the shelf behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the prophecy book's pages turning — once — as if something had just been written, somewhere ahead of them, in a hand they wouldn't recognize for years.
