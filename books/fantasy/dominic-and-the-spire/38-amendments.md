## 38. Amendments

The guildhall smelled of ink and beeswax on the morning the charter was rewritten. Lena arrived early — she always arrived early; a taster's habit was not so easily unlearned — and found the long ledger table already cleared, its scarred surface polished for the first time in anyone's memory. Someone had set out seven chairs. She counted them twice, because counting twice was also a habit, and then a third time because the number kept being wrong.

Seven chairs. Six guild members. The seventh, she decided, was for the charter itself.

By the time the others came down the stairs — Dominic with his sleeves rolled and his hair still wet from the rain, Odile carrying her pen case like a reliquary, Wren with an armload of documents that rustled like nervous birds, and old Maren from the waiting room, who had refused to be left at home — the table had been arranged, the lamps lit, and the old charter laid out flat under glass.

It was a thin thing, the original charter. Three pages in a hand two centuries dead, all high-flown language about *the recovery of lost goods* and *the negotiation of improbable terms*. Nobody had touched it since. Guilds, like courts, preferred their laws dusty; dust made them feel permanent.

"Read it aloud," Maren said, settling into her chair with the groan of a woman who had spent sixty years underground and intended to enjoy every chair above it. "All of it. If we're going to change a law, we ought to hear what we're changing."

So Wren read. Their voice had changed since the salt galleries — there was a rasp in it now that hadn't been there before the capture, a scrape like stone on stone — but they read steadily, clause by clause, while the morning light climbed the windows and the fire ticked in the grate. And as they read, the room grew quieter, because everyone heard it at once: the old charter said nothing about souls. Nothing about memories. It spoke of goods, of services, of impossible things bought and bartered, and nowhere — not once — did it ask whether the thing traded could be *spared*.

"That's the hole," Odile said. Her fingers rested on the glass above the third page, light as a fly landing. "That's where Dominic fell through. Not malice. A gap wide enough for a man."

"Then we fill the gap," Lena said. "Clause by clause. And this time we write it in a hand that can't be mistaken for anyone else's."

She looked at Odile when she said it, and Odile looked back, and something passed between them that needed no ledger to record.

* * *

They worked through the morning, through noon, and well past the point where tea became supper. It was, everyone agreed later, the strangest drafting session in the guild's history: a food-taster who caught every poisoned phrase before it reached the page; a forger who could imitate any hand alive and insisted on using only her own; a debtor-spy who knew every loophole in subterranean contract law and took a vindictive pleasure in closing each one behind them; an archivist-smuggler who kept saying *we should keep one loophole, just in case* and was voted down every time, cheerfully, by acclamation.

"No soul-sales," Wren dictated, pen scratching. "*No member of the Guild shall sell, pledge, or otherwise encumber their soul, their name, or any faculty of the mind, whether to the court beneath the mountain or to any other party.*"

"Too narrow," Odile said. "Say *any part of themselves that cannot be regrown*. Otherwise some clever clerk will argue a soul is technically regrowable."

"Is it?" Maren asked.

"Ask me again after sixty years in a waiting room," Maren answered herself, and the room laughed, and the laughter went into the clause somehow — you could hear it in the phrasing, a warmth beneath the legalese.

The memory-trade article took longest. They argued about it until the candles guttered: whether trades should be forbidden outright, or permitted under witness, or priced by committee. It was Dominic, surprisingly, who settled it.

"Outright prohibition won't hold," he said. "People trade memories anyway. Grief does it. Love does it. We'd only drive it into back rooms." He turned his cup slowly on the table, watching the tea swirl. "Regulate it. Witness it. And require — this is the important part — require that both parties name aloud what they're giving up, before witnesses, *in their own words*. No forms. No summaries. If you can't say what you're losing, you're not ready to lose it."

Lena thought of the registry heart, of narrating her own memories as they lifted out of her, word by word, so that the words would remain when the feeling went. She thought: *he learned that from me.* Or perhaps she had learned it from him, ten years ago, in some conversation she now owned only on paper. Either way, the clause went in exactly as he said it, and when Wren read it back, no one amended a syllable.

The last article they wrote together, all six hands steadying the pen at once, which made the letters wobble terribly and which Odile declared *a forgery of competence* and signed anyway:

"*Nothing sold in error shall remain sold. Nothing given in love shall be counted as debt. The waiting room is closed.*"

Maren wept at that last line, briefly and without embarrassment, and then dried her eyes and asked whether the garden plot behind the guildhall was spoken for, because she had seen it from the window and it was a disgrace.

* * *

The court's copy of the amendments went down the mountain three days later, carried by Wren personally, in duplicate, with a receipt stamped in triplicate — because if there was one language the subterranean court respected above all others, it was paperwork done properly.

Wren had expected resistance. What they got instead was a summons.

The magistrate received them in the same velvet chamber where so many bargains had been struck, seated behind a desk that seemed carved from the mountain itself. Age had not softened the magistrate's face so much as organized it, the way water organizes stone. For a long moment neither spoke. Then the magistrate slid the amendments across the desk and tapped the fourth article with one finger.

"You've written law above ground that binds us below," the magistrate said. "You understand that."

"I understand that contracts must hold or everything unravels," Wren replied. "Your own doctrine. Precedent cuts both ways. You taught us that — expensively."

Something moved at the corner of the magistrate's mouth. In another face it might have been called a smile.

"The audit continues," the magistrate said. "Souls sold cheaply are being reweighed. Do you know how many entries in our registry were signed by people who didn't understand what they held? Who signed because a clerk told them it was customary? Because their parents signed?" The finger tapped again. "A century of holdings, Wren. And your little guild has made every one of them arguable."

"Good," said Wren.

"*Good,*" the magistrate repeated, tasting the word like a poisoner tasting a draft. And then, in a voice stripped suddenly of velvet, almost weary: "I have sat in this chamber for longer than your city has stood, and I have never once been thanked for a release. Every soul I have ever freed was freed by trickery, by loophole, by someone finding the crack in my law. Your guild found no crack. Your guild rewrote the wall." The magistrate looked down at the amendments, at the wobbling six-hand signature. "The reform office requires a director. Someone who knows our procedures from inside. Someone the debtors below will trust because they defected above."

Wren stared at him. "You're offering me a job."

"I am offering you a war," the magistrate said, "conducted entirely in triplicate. It is the only kind I know how to fight, and I find, lately, that I no longer wish to fight it alone."

Wren thought of the salt galleries, of buying minutes with procedure against procedure. They thought of the waiting room, patient as furniture. They thought — though they would deny it later — of a certain table above ground with seven chairs, and how one of them would sit empty most evenings now.

"I'll need clerks," Wren said. "Ones I choose myself."

"Naturally."

"And the reforms go in the registry's front matter. Not the annex. Front matter, where no one can claim they didn't see them."

The magistrate inclined his head, and somewhere far above, in a guildhall smelling of beeswax, a charter gained its first enforcement office.

* * *

Odile Vasse had never expected to teach. Teaching, in her experience, was what failures did when they ran out of things to forge — and yet here she was, three mornings a week, standing before eight students in the guildhall's back room with chalk dust on her cuffs and her pen case open like a surgeon's tray.

Her students were an unlikely congregation: two junior guild members who wanted legible ledgers; a salt-mine widow learning to sign her own name for the first time at fifty-three; a boy of twelve who had been sent by the reformed court itself, with official papers, because someone upstairs had decided that children born in the under-mountain should learn to write hands that belonged to no one but themselves.

"The state teaches you a hand so it can read you," Odile told them on the first morning, uncapping her pen. "Every clerk trained in the chancery style writes the same letter the same way. That's not efficiency. That's inventory." She wrote her own name on the slate — large, slanted, unmistakably hers, with the crossed t that had once gotten her arrested. "I spent twenty years making my writing disappear into other people's. This," she tapped the slate, "is the only hand I was ever guilty of. Learn to make yours. Guard it. When someone asks you to sign something, make sure the name on the line is a person who agreed to be there."

The widow cried a little, practicing her O's. The boy filled three sheets with a jagged alphabet entirely of his own invention, and when Odile examined it she pronounced it, gravely, *uncounterfeitable*, and the boy walked home like a king.

In the evenings, alone, Odile practiced the hardest lesson of all: her own forgiveness. She copied her confiscated name — the original still lived in the Spire's registry, page and seal intact — onto fresh paper, again and again, until the copying felt less like theft and more like handwriting. On the last night of the month she mailed the copies to the reformed court's front matter office with a note in her unmistakable hand: *Requesting amendment. Name returned by forgery; requesting return by law, so the forgery may retire.*

Wren's reply came back within the week, stamped in triplicate: *Granted. The original page will be delivered to you, whole, for whatever purpose you see fit.*

Odile looked at that reply for a long time. Then she framed it, hung it above her students' slates, and told anyone who asked that it was the finest piece of calligraphy she had ever authenticated.

* * *

The garden plot behind the guildhall had indeed been a disgrace — a rectangle of compacted clay where nothing had grown since before the current king's grandfather, littered with bottle shards and the fossilized remains of someone's ambitious compost experiment. Maren surveyed it the way a magistrate surveys a fraudulent account: thoroughly, mercilessly, with rising satisfaction.

"Sixty years underground," she said, kneeling despite her knees' loud objections and crumbling a fistful of clay between her fingers. "Do you know what grows down there? Nothing. Mushrooms, if you flatter them. I used to dream about dirt. Real dirt, with worms in it, that argued back." She spat on the clay, an old miner's blessing. "This will argue beautifully."

She commandeered help with the imperiousness of someone owed six decades of interest. Dominic hauled manure. Lena tested the soil the way she tested everything — a pinch on the tongue, a frown, a verdict (*alkaline; we'll want ash and patience*) — and the boy from Odile's class turned out to have a gift for coaxing seeds, though he claimed it was just luck and kept a coin in his pocket that always landed kindly.

By the first hard frost, the garden was a rumor of spring: garlic beds tucked under straw, a rose cutting from the palace gardens smuggled out in Lena's sleeve ("it was going to be pruned anyway"), and one crooked apple sapling that Maren planted in the center and named, without explanation, *Dominic's tree*, which everyone understood and no one discussed.

On fine evenings she sat out there in a borrowed chair with a blanket over her knees, hands in the dirt even when there was nothing to plant, watching the sky do what the sky does over a city — which is everything, endlessly, for free.

"First time in sixty years," she told Lena one dusk, nodding at the darkening blue. "Above ground for a sunset. I'd forgotten they take so long. Down below, day just — switches off. Up here it *lingers*. Like a guest who knows you don't want them to go." She patted the soil beside the apple tree. "Everything worth having lingers, girl. Remember that."

Lena remembered it. Lena, these days, remembered nearly everything — and guarded the remembering the way she once guarded the king's supper, cataloguing each recovered memory by aftertaste, checking for poison, finding none, only a sweetness so complete it sometimes frightened her more than fear had.

* * *

Winter deepened. The amendments were ratified — first by the guild, unanimously; then, in a formality nobody had expected to matter, by the crown, when the king's new food-taster (Lena had trained her replacement herself, patiently, passing on the tongue and the caution and the habit of counting twice) reported that His Majesty's supper remained unpoisoned and His Majesty, feeling generous, signed whatever the guild put in front of him.

The waiting room emptied the way a river empties — gradually, then all at once. Wren's office processed releases by the dozen, then the hundred. Some of the freed souls had nowhere to go and no one left to remember them; those ones tended to stay below awhile anyway, helping with the audits, because a waiting room full of the patient makes excellent auditors. Others came up into the light blinking, squinting, asking the price of bread in a currency forty years out of date. The guild fed all of them. It was, after all, in the charter now.

And on a grey afternoon near midwinter, Lena Hartwell sat at the ledger desk with the finished charter bound in green leather before her, reading it through one final time before it went to the shelf of records. She read every clause. She read the six-hand signature, wobbling like a heartbeat. She read the marginal notes — Odile's precise corrections, Wren's citations, Dominic's single attempted loophole, crossed out but preserved, because history should show its arguments.

Then she reached the end of the book, where the prophecy folio had been rebound into the same volume — past, present, and future stitched together at last — and found that the final pages were blank except for one.

Page 412, with its single line in her own hand: *paid in full, remember to forget.*

And beneath it, waiting, a page numbered 413.

Blank.

She sat looking at it for a long time. Outside, rain had begun against the windows, steady and unhurried. In the garden, Maren's straw blankets held the garlic safe. Somewhere below the city, Wren was stamping a release; somewhere above it, Odile was teaching a boy to guard his own hand. Everything settled. Everything paid.

Which meant, Lena thought, turning to a fresh leaf, dipping her pen, that the only question left in the world was the oldest one the guild knew:

What impossible thing comes next?

She wrote the date at the top of the page, poured two cups of tea, and called Dominic in from the rain.
