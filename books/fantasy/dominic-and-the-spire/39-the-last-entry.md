## 39. The Last Entry

The prophecy book had never been beautiful.

Lena had thought so once, in the first weeks after the descent, when she still caught herself touching its cover like a pilgrim at a shrine — the cracked leather gone soft as an old glove, the brass corners worn to the color of dull honey. But beauty was a thing that asked nothing of you, and this book had asked everything. It had named a date and taken a soul against it. It had written her hand before her hand knew how to move. It had cost her ten years of tea shared with a man whose name she'd spent six weeks unable to place, and then it had given them back, all but one small afternoon she had chosen — smiling, the scales watching — to keep paid forever.

No. Not beautiful. *Honest*, maybe, in the way ledgers were honest: indifferent, exact, waiting to be corrected.

She sat at the guildhall's long desk with the book open on her knees, rain ticking against the high windows in the rhythm of someone counting out coins one at a time. Below her, the great room was quiet in the way it only got on evenings when everyone had somewhere better to be — Odile upstairs teaching three apprentices the difference between a court hand and a guild hand (the difference, as far as Lena could tell, was that one of them lied beautifully and the other lied usefully), Wren off at the reform office they now kept inside the mountain itself, commuting like any civil servant, which Wren insisted was the funniest joke ever played on a bureaucracy. The old woman — Maren, though for sixty years the court had filed her under a number — was in the garden behind the hall, turning soil that hadn't seen daylight since before Lena was born, planting things whose names she recited aloud like blessings.

And Dominic was out in the rain, because of course he was. He'd said something about a delivery, about a bottle of last words that needed returning to a widow in the Salt Quarter, and he'd refused the umbrella with the particular stubbornness of a man who had once sold his soul and considered weather beneath negotiation.

Lena turned to the last written page.

The prophecy proper ended long ago — centuries ago, really, the final dated entry being some king's death and a comet and a harvest that failed, all in ink gone brown as dried blood. Everything after that was margins. Her margins. Decades of them, in her own hand, notes correcting earlier entries, arguing with a version of herself who had apparently known how the story went and had left instructions anyway, like a woman packing a trunk for a journey she couldn't remember taking.

*Voided, see p. 412.*

*Paid in full, remember to forget.*

*Returned, with interest* — no, that one wasn't hers. That one was Dominic's, scrawled in the registry heart while the scales were still settling, in handwriting that shook and didn't care. The court had honored it because the court honored nothing more fiercely than its own precedents, and that single act of bureaucratic obedience had turned out to be the crack through which everything else poured: the audit, the reweighings, the emptying of the waiting room, Maren walking up into daylight for the first time in sixty years carrying a clerk's stamp Odile had liberated and would deny liberating to her grave.

Lena dipped her pen.

The ink was good ink — Odile's standard now, black cut with a thread of salt from the mines, so that anything written with it could be authenticated by taste. Lena tasted everything. It was, after all, the one talent the whole impossible year had never managed to take from her or give back differently. She touched the nib to her tongue out of habit, found it bitter and clean, and wrote in the margin beside the last prophecy, in her present-tense hand, small and level:

*The date passed; we chose.*

Four words. She looked at them for a long time.

It seemed too little. A century of dread compressed into four words, a soul sold and returned, a memory traded away and argued back, a courtroom full of debtors and forgers and exiles standing up one by one to say *this is not how it has to be* — and all of it ending in a margin note shorter than most of her poison reports. But then, she thought, setting down the pen, that was rather the point. Prophecies ran long because they were afraid. Choices could be brief, because they were certain.

She let the ink dry, then blotted it with the corner of her sleeve, which Odile would have called vandalism and which Lena called provenance. Let some future reader know a real person had been here, hurried and human, smudging the record.

There was one thing left to do, and she had been putting it off for three days, which for Lena Hartwell constituted a scandal.

At the back of the book, bound in after the prophecies ended — Odile's stitching, flawless as always — waited a single blank page. At its head, in Odile's careful court hand, was a number: **413**.

Page 412 held one line, in her handwriting, in a tense she had never fully resolved: *paid in full, remember to forget.* Page 413 held nothing at all. It had been waiting for months now, patient as furniture, and every time Lena looked at it she felt the same vertigo she imagined astronomers felt staring into the part of the sky where a star used to be. The blankness wasn't empty. It was *owed*. Somewhere in the machinery of the world, an entry was pending.

She picked up the pen again.

Not to write on it. Not yet. She had learned that much — that you did not fill a page like this one casually, the way you did not swallow an unknown substance casually, the way you did not sign a contract beneath a mountain without reading the clause about collection. Page 413 would take whatever came next, whatever impossible thing the guild stumbled into or invented, and it deserved to be met deliberately, with a clear tongue and a steady hand.

But she did write, very lightly in pencil, at the bottom corner where a cautious accountant keeps her options open, two words:

*In progress.*

Then she closed the ledger.

The sound of it — leather meeting leather, brass corners clicking home — rang through the empty hall like a small verdict. Lena sat for a moment with both hands flat on the cover, feeling the weight of it, and realized she was breathing easily. That was new. For a year the book had sat in her chest like a swallowed stone; now it was just a book, heavy and finished, a thing with a past tense.

Outside, the rain thickened, drumming harder on the glass, and she thought of Dominic walking through it with a widow's bottle tucked inside his coat, getting soaked on principle, and felt the specific warmth that had come back to her in a courtroom in the mountain's heart — the memory of him testifying, voice cracking on the third page of his reconstructed ledger, reading aloud every cup of tea they had ever shared because the court required witnesses and he intended to be thorough. *He bought my life without asking,* she had told him once, furious. *Just as I planned to buy his.* They had settled it the way they settled everything: badly, honestly, over cold tea, and then properly, later, when the memories came flooding back mid-sentence and she had finished for him a sentence he'd started ten years ago, and they had both laughed until the ushers came to see what was wrong.

The trade had not been fair. They agreed on that now. Fairness was for contracts. What they had was better — it was *chosen*, amended and re-amended, argued over and signed anyway, which was the only kind of love the Guild of Impossible Things recognized as legal tender.

Lena rose, set the prophecy book on its shelf between the folios of traded luck and the register of debts forgiven, and went to make tea.

* * *

The guildhall kitchen had changed since the year began. Someone — Wren, almost certainly, though Wren denied it with the flat sincerity of a trained spy — had installed a second chair at the little table by the window, so that the room no longer looked like an afterthought but like a place where two people were expected. The kettle was copper, dented, older than the guild charter. The tea was the good mountain blend now, salt-cured, the kind the subterranean court had hoarded for generations until Wren's reform office opened the stores and Odile forged the requisition forms so smoothly that even the magistrate had written back to ask, politely, whether the guild might care to formalize the arrangement.

Lena measured the leaves by eye, the way she measured poison — precisely, respectfully, with full awareness that the difference between medicine and harm was a matter of grams and intent. She had catalogued three poisons in the king's supper the night all this began. She still took the tasting post; the king had offered her a title, a stipend, a wing of the palace, and she had declined all three on the grounds that precision was worth more than comfort and the crown's kitchen remained, in her professional opinion, a crime scene awaiting its next offense. Besides, the double life suited her. Taster by day, keeper of impossibilities by evening. She had stopped thinking of it as hiding. It was simply having two honest jobs instead of one dishonest one.

The kettle sang. She poured, and the steam rose up fragrant and dark, and through the window the rain came down in silver ropes across the courtyard, and there — crossing it at an unhurried walk, coat dark with water, hair plastered flat, grinning like a man who had never once lost an argument with the weather — was Dominic.

He had the widow's empty bottle wrapped in oilcloth under one arm and a paper parcel in the other hand that he was visibly failing to hide, and Lena watched him through the glass and felt the afternoon-memory stir in her, the one small gap the scales still held, the price she paid smiling. She knew there had been an afternoon. She knew it had mattered enough to keep. She did not know what happened in it, and that absence had stopped aching and become something else — a locked drawer in a house she otherwise owned outright, a mystery she had chosen the way you choose a favorite stone to carry in your pocket. A self should keep one mystery. She had said so to the magistrate, and the magistrate, who respected a well-argued claim above any plea, had inclined her velvet head and entered it into the record.

Dominic shouldered the door open, bringing the storm in with him in a gust of wet air and river-smell.

"You're dripping on the charter," Lena said.

"The charter's seen worse." He unwound the oilcloth, set the empty bottle on the shelf among its brothers — bottled last words, each labeled in Odile's hand, waiting to be delivered or traded or, in one memorable case, drunk by accident — and dropped the paper parcel on the table in front of her with the elaborate casualness of a man presenting evidence. "Delivery."

"I didn't order anything."

"No," he agreed, sitting down across from her, water pooling cheerfully off his coat onto Wren's floorboards. "That's rather the point."

She opened the parcel. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper, was a coin — old, worn nearly smooth, the face rubbed to a featureless shine — and a note in her own handwriting, which meant it had passed through Odile's forgery bench or through the strange loop of the prophecy book, and these days Lena had learned not to demand the difference. The note read: *A coin that lands kindly. Test it first. You'll want to.*

"Where did this come from?"

"Borrowed luck, restocked and re-bottled. There's a debtor in the Salt Quarter who owed the guild a favor from before your time, and I called it in." He wrapped his hands around the second cup she'd poured — she had made two without deciding to; the kitchen had opinions now — and watched her over the rim. "You're going to say it's sentimental."

"I'm going to say it's unbalanced." She turned the coin over. Warm already, though it had been sitting in wet paper in the rain. "A lucky coin is an asset. Assets go in the vault. Sentiment goes—" She paused.

"Go on," Dominic said, eyes bright with the particular patience of a man who has waited a year and a courtroom and a resurrection for this sentence.

"Sentiment goes on the desk," Lena finished, "where it can be audited daily." She set the coin beside the closed ledger, heads up, and pushed his tea toward him. "Drink that before it goes cold. We've history with cold tea."

"We do." He drank. Outside, the rain kept on, steady and generous, filling the gutters, feeding Maren's garden, running off the eaves of a guildhall that no longer contained a single soul owed to anyone. In the vault below, the ledgers of traded impossibilities balanced to the gram. On the shelf, the prophecy book sat closed, its last margin dry, its page 413 waiting with a pencil ghost in the corner — *in progress* — for whatever came next.

Lena lifted her cup, and paused, and looked at the man across the table whom she had sold her memories for and argued back from the dead, and found that the truest thing she could say was also the smallest.

"The date passed," she said. "We chose. And tomorrow—"

Tomorrow, the ledger would open again. Tomorrow, page 413. Tomorrow, whatever impossible thing came next, and the two of them to meet it, cups in hand, rain on the roof, the whole improbable arithmetic of their lives finally, gloriously, out of balance in exactly the right direction.

"—tomorrow we start a new page," Dominic said, and reached across the table, and turned the coin over. It landed kindly. It always would.
