## 1. The Charm Seller

The bells of Saint Vess's rang the hour before dawn, and Wren Zhou was already awake, because the bells were wrong and she had learned, over ten years in Corvane, to distrust anything that came too easily.

They rang seven strokes when it was closer to six. The bell-ringer, old Tam with his wine-flushed cheeks and his perpetual apology, had been off by an hour since midwinter. Nobody corrected him. In Corvane, you let small errors live, because correcting them meant noticing them, and being noticed was the one luxury Wren could not afford.

She lay in her narrow bed above the stall and listened to the city wake. Cart wheels on wet cobbles. A fishwife arguing with nobody in particular down on Brackish Row. Somewhere above, the gulls that nested in Saint Vess's spire — gulls in a landlocked city, which everyone agreed was odd and no one investigated, because investigating things was how you ended up on a gallows with your name chalked on a board beneath you.

*WREN ZHOU, TRINKETS,* said the sign she'd painted herself three years ago, the letters going brown at the edges like old teeth. *LUCKY CHARMS. HONEST GOODS.*

The *honest goods* had been Pip's suggestion, delivered through a grin while he leaned against her stall eating an apple he hadn't paid for. "People love honesty in a liar," he'd said. "It's the best lie there is."

He wasn't wrong. That was the trouble with Pip. He was rarely wrong, and never sorry.

Wren dressed in the dark by feel — wool stockings darned at both knees, a skirt the color of nothing in particular, a shawl the color of slightly more nothing. She checked her coin purse out of habit, though she'd counted it twice the night before: eleven coppers, two silvers, and the protection coin, wrapped separately in flannel like something sick.

Then she went down to open her stall, and to be, for another day, the least interesting person in Corvane.

* * *

By the time the sun cleared the rooftops of Tanner's Lane, Wren had her table laid out with the precision of a general deploying troops. It looked like chaos. That was the art of it. A real trinket-seller's table was chaos: ribbons tangled with bootlaces, tin whistles jumbled against horn buttons, charms hanging from nails hammered into the awning post in no order a sane person could discern.

Every charm was different. That mattered. Rabbits' feet — well, hares', actually; Wren had standards and rabbits were someone's livestock — hung beside knotted red thread, beside river stones with holes worn through them, beside little stamped-tin medallions she bought wholesale from a man who stamped them, presumably, in his sleep. Each one priced differently. Each one *looking* priced differently, as if Wren had assigned values according to some deep inscrutable logic rather than simply according to what each customer could bear to pay.

That was the first rule of selling luck: never let them see the system.

"Charms!" she called as the market filled, her voice pitched to carry exactly far enough — loud enough to be heard, quiet enough to be ignored. "Lucky charms! Protection for the road, fortune for the home! Honest goods, fair prices!"

An old woman stopped and fingered the knotted red threads. "These work?"

"Madam," Wren said gravely, "if they worked, I'd be rich, wouldn't I? I sell hope at a copper apiece. Cheaper than the priest charges, and mine doesn't come with a sermon."

The old woman laughed and bought two.

This was the dance, and Wren knew every step. She had learned it young, from Guildmaster Voss himself, back when the guild still took apprentices openly — back when the Hollow Lantern Guild was a name spoken with respect in certain narrow streets, before the Purges, before magic became a word you whispered only to accuse someone. Voss had taught her pricing, patter, the psychology of the pause. He'd taught her how to look poor without looking desperate, honest without looking naive. He'd taught her that the best hiding place in any city is a market stall, because no one remembers the face of a person trying to sell them something.

Ten years. Ten years since the guild had been broken, scattered, its members fled or dead or — worst of all — respectable. Ten years since Wren, aged nine, had been left on the guild's doorstep with nothing but the clothes she stood in and a name stitched into her collar. Ten years of being small and unnoticed and *careful*, and she had gotten so good at careful that careful had become the shape of her soul.

She just wished, sometimes — in the hour after closing, in the dark before sleep — that somebody, somewhere, remembered her face for a reason other than commerce.

But wishing was a charm that never sold, because nobody believed in it, including her.

* * *

Midmorning brought the patrols.

You could tell an Inquisitor patrol by the way a market went quiet. Not silent — silence drew attention. But the noise changed texture, the way water changes when a big fish passes under a boat. Conversations dropped half a tone. Laughter got shorter. And the sellers whose goods existed in the gray country between *trinket* and *evidence* began, very casually, covering things with cloth.

Wren had nothing to cover. That was the point of her inventory: knots and stones and tin, all of it dull, all of it explainable. Superstition was tolerated in Corvane the way damp was tolerated in cellars — omnipresent, mildly distasteful, impossible to fully stamp out. What the Inquisition hanged people for was not superstition but *result*. A charm that did nothing was a trinket. A charm that glowed was testimony.

Which was why Wren's heart performed a small, controlled stumble every time one of her own pieces warmed against her palm.

It didn't happen often. Maybe one charm in forty carried the faint heat — not warm the way a stone warmed by sun is warm, but warm from inside, warm the way a sleeping bird is warm, as if something lived curled within it. She'd felt it since childhood. Voss called it "seller's intuition" when other guild members were around, and called it something else entirely when they were alone, late, in the guildhall kitchen: *"A knack,"* he'd say. *"Don't chase it, don't feed it, and for the love of all the patient saints, girl, don't ever let it show."*

So she didn't. When a piece warmed, she moved it. Quietly, mid-patter, mid-transaction, her hand flowing naturally to rearrange stock — the warmed charm sliding beneath a scarf, behind a button jar, into the locked box under the table where the truly unsellable things lived. Then, at night, she carried the box down into the cellar and added its contents to the collection there.

She had never asked Voss why the guild kept a cellar full of warm charms. She had been nine years old and frightened, and by the time she was old enough to ask good questions, asking had become a habit she couldn't break.

The patrol came down Tanner's Lane at the pace men walk when they want to be seen walking. Three of them in gray-and-black livery, cudgels loose in their belts, and behind them — Wren's stomach tightened — a tithe-collector with a ledger book, which meant today was a *counting* day. Counting days were worse than searching days. Searching days ended quickly. Counting days ended with lists, and lists endured.

"Morning, mistress." The tithe-collector was young, with the earnest, apologetic cruelty of a man doing arithmetic on other people's lives. "Stall license?"

Wren produced it from the oilcloth sleeve pinned inside her awning, smooth as a card trick. "Paid through month's end."

"Mm." He consulted his ledger with the leisurely thoroughness of a man who has never once been in a hurry in his life. "Inventory declaration?"

"All declared." She gestured at the table with a seller's easy openness. "Thread, tin, stones, buttons. You're welcome to count."

Something flickered in his eyes — the temptation of a man invited to do exactly what he wanted. Behind him, one of the patrol soldiers drifted toward her stall with the loose-shouldered stroll of a bored predator.

And that was when Wren made her mistake.

Not a large mistake. A human one. The soldier's elbow caught the awning post as he passed, and the whole frame swayed, and her hand shot out on pure reflex to steady the hanging charms before they swung into view of anyone standing at the right angle — because one of them, third nail from the top, the little river-stone amulet she'd strung herself last week, was *warm*. Warm and swinging, and if it brushed the collector's hand or caught the light wrong—

Her fingers closed around it. She tucked it into her apron pocket in one motion so fluid it surprised even her, and smiled up at the collector as if nothing in the world were happening at all.

"Anything else, sir? I've a lovely whistle for your nephew."

The pause stretched. The collector looked at her. She held his gaze with the particular steadiness of a person who has practiced looking boring in a mirror until boring became native to her face.

"No nephew," he said finally, and wrote something in his ledger anyway, because writing things down was his entire personality, and moved along.

Wren exhaled only after they'd turned onto Brackish Row, and even then only halfway. Her hand, in her pocket, closed around the warm stone. It pulsed gently against her palm like a second heartbeat, faint and stubborn, and she thought — as she thought most days, in the privacy behind her own eyes — *one of these days this is going to get me killed, and it'll be such a stupid way to die.*

She didn't know yet how nearly right she was.

* * *

Noon brought lunch, which in Wren's case meant bread, hard cheese, and an audience.

"You look terrible," said Pip Ferrow, materializing on the empty crate beside her stall the way he always did — without visible approach, as if the space between *not there* and *there* were merely a door he declined to use properly.

"I look *thriving*," Wren said, tearing her bread in half and handing him the bigger piece, because she was a fool with soft habits. "Thriving people look tired. It's all the thriving."

"Mm." Pip bit into the cheese rind-first, which was a crime in four kingdoms. He was a lean, quick-eyed young man with hair the color of autumn oak gone slightly to seed, and if you looked at him in the right light — which almost no one did, because almost no one looked closely at Pip — you might notice that his ears came to points a shade too delicate, or that his eyes held green where green had no business being. Half-fae smugglers did not advertise. "Sold much hope today?"

"Three hopes, two fortunes, and a protection for the road. And nearly lost my neck to a counting-day."

Pip went still in the way he did, the way that made him seem suddenly older and stranger, like a fox deciding whether you're prey. "Ashgrave's men counting on Tanner's Lane?"

"Not Ashgrave himself. Just his arithmetic."

"That's worse." Pip swallowed. "Arithmetic is how they find things. Searches make noise. Numbers are quiet." He glanced at her — really glanced, for once — and Wren had the uncomfortable sensation of being seen by someone who usually made a profession of seeing nothing. "Your cellar's still—"

"None of your business."

"It became my business the day I started paying to keep it that way." He fished in his coat and set a small purse on her table, casual as a man leaving a tip. "Voss sent it. For the watch-captain on Gate Street. His discretion doesn't come cheap, and Voss says the cellar's contents are 'seasonally relevant.'"

"What does that mean?"

"If I knew, I'd be rich instead of charming." He flashed the grin that got him fed in half the taverns in Corvane and thrown out of the other half. "Take it to Gate Street before dusk. And Wren—" the grin faded, and underneath it was something almost like worry, which on Pip's face looked as unnatural as snow in July, "—the courier roads have been strange lately. My contacts aren't answering. All of them, all at once. When smugglers go quiet, it means either feast or famine, and I haven't heard any feasting."

"Maybe they finally arrested you all and the city's enjoying the peace."

"Pip Ferrow, arrested? They'd have to catch me first, and then convict me, and then believe their own paperwork." He hopped off the crate, stole an apple from her stock basket with the shameless fluency of long practice, and paused. "Gate Street. Before dusk. Don't forget."

"I never forget debts," Wren said. "I'm a merchant."

"You're a *hope* merchant," Pip said, walking backward down the lane. "Different thing entirely. Hope merchants always pay last."

* * *

Dusk found Wren walking Gate Street with the protection purse sewn into her shawl hem, past the watch-house with its lanterns already lit, to the doorway of Captain Dree's own cousin's tavern, where the captain himself held court nightly over a cup he never finished.

She hated this part. Not the danger — danger she could manage; danger was just weather, and she'd learned to dress for it. What she hated was the *transaction*: the smiling, the deferring, the performance of gratitude toward a man she despised, all to keep safe a cellar full of warm charms whose purpose she didn't understand and had never dared to ask about.

Captain Dree was a thick-necked man with a bureaucrat's hands and a hangman's patience. He counted the coin without appearing to touch it — a skill Wren privately suspected was the true qualification for promotion in the watch.

"The guild's affairs remain... unremarkable," he said, which was the closest he ever came to poetry.

"Unremarkable is what we sell best."

"And the girl who runs the trinket stall on Tanner's Lane?" His eyes settled on her with mild, professional interest. "She remains equally unremarkable?"

"She remains," Wren said, "the most boring woman in Corvane."

"Good." He turned back to his cup. "Keep it that way. There's talk from the Cathedral district. New commissions. New *attention*. The Inquisition's been given leave to widen its counts." He glanced at her sidelong, and for a moment the bureaucratic mask slipped and something almost like pity looked out. "Be smaller, mistress. Whatever you are, however small you think you've made yourself — be smaller."

Wren walked home through streets going blue with evening, past shuttered windows and early fires, and tried to imagine being smaller than invisible. She failed. Instead she found herself thinking about the courier roads gone silent, about Pip's worry wearing its disguise of jokes, about the captain's pity, about the warm stone still in her apron pocket, pulsing its slow, patient heat against her hip like a thing waiting to be asked a question.

Behind her, somewhere across the city, the cathedral bells began to ring.

Seven strokes.

Wren stopped in the middle of the street, her breath fogging in the cold, and frowned — because it was dusk, near enough to eight, and the bells were ringing seven, and old Tam was never *that* wrong.

Unless, some small voice said, deep in the place where a seller learns to notice what customers try to hide — unless it wasn't Tam at all.

Across the square, a figure in traveling leathers stumbled out of the shadow of the tanner's wall — mud-spattered, gray-faced, one arm pressed against his side in a way that made Wren's stomach clench with a recognition she didn't yet have words for. He looked around the square wildly, wildly, and then his eyes found hers.

Found hers, and fixed, and filled with something that looked terribly like relief.

He started toward her stall.
