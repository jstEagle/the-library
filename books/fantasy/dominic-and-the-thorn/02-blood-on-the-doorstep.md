## 2. Blood on the Doorstep

Wren Zhou had three rules for surviving Corvane, and she recited them every morning like a litany while she unpacked her stall.

One: never be interesting.

Two: never be seen being lucky.

Three: if the Inquisitors' gray cloaks come down Copperlane way, become furniture.

She was arranging her wares — the tin luck-knots, the braided red threads "blessed by a monk in Vail" (there were no monks in Vail; there hadn't been monks anywhere since the Purges), the little carved acorns that supposedly kept nightmares out of your pillow — when she noticed the morning felt wrong.

Not dangerous-wrong. Not yet. Wrong in the smaller, more personal way of a day that had decided to inconvenience her specifically. The light through Copperlane's crooked eaves came down too sharp, the kind of light that showed every scuff on her table and every frayed thread on her charms. Market days wanted soft light. Soft light forgave.

"Morning, Wren," called Old Bess from the fish stall two down, already elbow-deep in herring and opinions. "You look like something the cat refused."

"Good morning to you too, Bess."

"You eating? You've got cheeks like a debt collector."

"I'm eating beautifully." She wasn't, particularly, but that was between her and her ribs. "I've been invited to dinner tonight. Roast pheasant."

Bess squinted at her. "Whose dinner?"

"The pheasant's. I'm sure it'll be lovely."

The old woman laughed, which was the point, and turned back to her fish, and Wren let herself have one small private moment of warmth before tucking it away where it couldn't cost her anything. Ten years in Corvane taught you that warmth was fine, so long as you kept it rationed. A smile here, a joke there, nothing that made anyone look twice at you. Be the charm seller. Be nobody's business.

She set out the day's best piece last, as always: a glass lantern charm the size of a plum, swirled with blue and gold, that caught the light and threw little swimming shadows across the table. It was beautiful work. It was also — though only she knew this — genuinely warm to the touch, faintly, like a stone left near a hearth. Real. One of perhaps nine real things left in her entire inventory, buried among forty pieces of honest rubbish.

She'd stopped asking herself how they were real. That question led to places she didn't go anymore. Places with names like *why did Guildmaster Voss really send me out here* and *what happened to the other apprentices*, and those roads ended in hanging squares.

So instead she polished the lantern with her sleeve, set its price at an outrageous eight coppers, and settled in to be small.

* * *

The morning went the way good mornings went in Corvane: slowly, and with a lot of haggling.

A baker's boy bought a luck-thread for his mother's birthday and tried to pay in a bun, which Wren accepted because the bun was excellent and because a boy who loved his mother enough to haggle on her behalf deserved a discount he didn't know he'd gotten. A nervous young couple bought an acorn apiece for nightmares that were probably just each other's snoring. A man in a merchant's coat picked up the glass lantern, turned it over twice, put it down without asking the price, and moved on — and Wren exhaled a breath she hadn't known she was holding, because sometimes the best outcome for her finest work was that no one looked at it too closely.

It was nearly midday when she saw the gray cloaks.

Two of them, moving up the lane at market pace — not hurrying, not searching, just *present*, the way Inquisitors liked to be present, letting everyone feel them pass through like weather. Wren's hands went still on her counter. Rule three engaged. She became furniture. She became a slightly shabby woman interested in nothing but her threads.

They passed within ten feet of her stall. The taller one's gaze slid across her table, across her face, past her, gone. Her heart kept beating. It always surprised her a little when it did.

Then the screaming started.

No — not screaming. She corrected herself even as she flinched. Shouting, from the far end of Copperlane, near the fountain square. Running footsteps. And then a man came around the corner of the chandler's shop at a dead sprint, and Wren understood in one cold drop that this was what rule three actually existed for.

He was maybe thirty, lean, dressed as a courier — dusty riding leathers, a courier's satchel banging against his hip, one strap torn clean through. There was blood on him. Not much. Enough.

He ran straight at her stall.

There was nowhere else, she supposed. Copperlane bent like a hook at that corner, and her stall sat in the bend like bait, and the man took the hook with both hands. He hit her table hard enough to send luck-threads scattering like startled birds, grabbed her forearm with fingers that burned hot through her sleeve, and looked at her with eyes gone white-rimmed and desperate.

"You sell charms," he said. It wasn't a question. His voice was shredded, like he'd been talking or praying for hours. "Hollow Lantern. You're Hollow Lantern."

"I sell trinkets," Wren said, automatic, smooth, the lie worn so smooth it came out before thought. "Souvenirs. Nothing blessed, nothing—"

"He said find the charm seller." The man's grip tightened. His knees buckled; he sagged against her table, and the whole stall groaned. Up close she could see the blood now was more than enough — soaked dark along his side, glistening between his fingers where he pressed them to his ribs. "Voss's girl. He said — he described you. Small. Quick eyes. Charms that are *warm*—"

Everything in Wren went very quiet and very cold.

"Let go of me," she whispered. "Please. You're going to get us both—"

"They're coming." He wasn't listening. He was fumbling one-handed at the satchel, tearing at its buckles with fingers suddenly clumsy. "Listen to me. Listen. The Root is failing."

The words meant nothing. They landed in her like coins dropped down a well — she heard them, heard them splash somewhere deep, felt their weight, understood nothing. "What?"

"The covenant. The Root—" He got the satchel open. From inside he pulled a scroll case, black wax seal unbroken, and pressed it into her free hand with the whole desperate strength of a dying man, folding her fingers shut over it. "Take it to Voss. Tell him the anchors are dying. Tell him it's *her* turn, he'll understand — tell him—" His breath hitched wetly. His eyes found hers again, and what was in them wasn't fear anymore. It was worse. It was relief. "Thank you. Oh, thank the green, someone finally—"

"Gray cloaks!" Bess's voice, high and cracked, from two stalls down. "Boy, they're coming back—"

The courier shoved off her table, spun to face the lane, and planted himself between Wren and the world like a door closing.

"No," Wren said. "No, wait—"

The two Inquisitors rounded the chandler's corner at a walk.

That was the worst part, later. That they walked. No sprinting, no drawn steel, no fury. Just two gray figures advancing at the pace of men collecting a debt, and the crowd of Copperlane flowing away from them like water around stones, and Bess hauling her fish baskets back with shaking hands and refusing to look, and Wren frozen behind her own stall with a sealed scroll case burning against her palm.

"Hold," said the tall Inquisitor, to the courier, mildly. Almost kindly. "That's all. Just hold."

"I didn't do anything." The courier's voice shook. "I'm a courier. I carry letters. Check my satchel, it's letters, it's all letters—"

"It's all letters," agreed the second Inquisitor. He was younger, with a broad flat face and patient eyes. "Most couriers say that."

The tall one raised his hand, palm out, fingers spread — and Wren felt it happen. Felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm, a wrongness sliding through the air like a fish under ice. Some sense she'd never known she had opened one reluctant eye.

The Inquisitor was tasting the air. Tasting it for magic.

And every real charm on her table — nine of them, warm as banked coals beneath her scattered stock — began, very softly, to sing.

She didn't hear sound, exactly. But the lantern charm threw its swimming blue-gold shadows harder, faster. The acorns trembled in their tray. Somewhere under her counter, in the locked strongbox where she kept the truly dangerous stock, something hummed against the wood like a trapped bee.

The tall Inquisitor's head turned. Degree by degree. Toward her stall.

Toward *her*.

"Run," the courier breathed — and then he moved. Not at the Inquisitors. Sideways, into the younger one, arms wide, buying nothing but seconds, shouting nonsense about letters and rights of passage and the courier's guild charter, and the tall Inquisitor sighed the sigh of a tired official and drew his blade, and Wren's body made the decision her mind was too slow for.

She ran.

She snatched the strongbox on instinct — ten years of habit, that box held everything, that box was *hers* — clutched the scroll case in her fist, and bolted through the gap behind her stall into the tanner's yard, and the last thing she saw of Copperlane market was the courier turning his head to check that she'd gone, and smiling.

Then the alley swallowed her, and the sounds started behind her, and she ran faster.

* * *

She didn't stop running until she was four streets gone and her lungs were full of knives.

Copperlane spilled into Weaver's Stair, a lane of crooked houses stacked like drunkards, and Wren wedged herself into the alcove beneath the third stairwell — a spot she'd used since she was twelve, deep enough in shadow that the watch walked past it daily without seeing her — and folded up small, arms around the strongbox, forehead on her knees, and shook.

She counted. That helped. Counting was the first thing Voss had ever taught her: *when the fear comes, girl, count the stitches in your sleeve. Fear can't count.*

Forty-one stitches. Forty-two. The scroll case dug into her palm. She became aware of it gradually, the way you become aware of a bruise, and lifted her hand and looked at it.

Black wax. Unbroken. Stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize — a circle crossed by seven lines, like a wheel with too many spokes. The case itself was dark wood, old, worn smooth at the grip by years of some other hand.

*The Root is failing.* Whatever that meant. *Tell Voss the anchors are dying. Tell him it's her turn.*

Her turn. Her turn for what?

Above her, through the stairwell slats, boots passed. Two sets. Gray cloaks didn't run in Weaver's Stair — too many turns, too many doors — but they'd spread out, she could feel it, a net drawing across the city with her name written somewhere in its center. Because a dying courier had gripped her arm in front of witnesses. Because Bess had shouted. Because half of Copperlane had watched a stranger press something into the charm seller's hand right before the Inquisitors arrived, and half of Copperlane would sell that story for the reward on informants' bounties.

"They'll ask who I am," she whispered to the dark. "They'll describe me. Small, quick eyes. *Charms that are warm.*"

Oh, he'd described her. He'd known her name. *Voss's girl.* Which meant Voss had given it to him. Which meant Voss had sent this man — sent him running and bleeding through the gates of Corvane carrying something worth killing for, carrying it *to her*, and had told him exactly where to find her, exactly what she looked like, without one word of warning to her.

The thought arrived whole, fully formed, and terrible.

Guildmaster Odell Voss had fed her to this morning on purpose.

"No," she said out loud, because saying it made it ridiculous. Saying it made it the paranoia of a frightened woman under a staircase. Voss had pulled her off the streets at eleven years old. Voss had taught her to read, to count, to haggle, to survive. Voss had held her hair back when she had fever at fourteen and sat up all night doing it. He was a hard old man with ledger-soul and a voice like gravel in a sack, but he was —

He was the only family she had.

And he had told a stranger her description and her trade and let him run bleeding through a city crawling with Inquisitors, straight to her stall, without telling her a single thing.

Wren pressed the scroll case against her sternum and made herself breathe. In. Out. Forty-three stitches. The shaking wouldn't stop, so she let it shake and thought anyway, because thinking was the other thing Voss had taught her, and it seemed only fair to use his lessons to decide what to do about him.

Option one: burn the case. Drop it in a cookfire, forget the morning, move her stall to Saltrow, start over. Except Inquisitors didn't chase you across a city for something you could burn, and the courier hadn't died for something she could forget, and — she flexed her fingers — the case was warm. Faintly. Like the lantern charm. Like the nine true things.

Like all the things she'd spent ten years not asking questions about.

Option two: run. Out of Corvane entirely, north road, new name. She had coin hidden, not enough, and legs that could carry her maybe thirty miles before the net closed, and no idea what waited beyond the walls except stories — and lately, if the tavern talk was true, bad ones. Villages going quiet. Woods standing closer than surveyors' maps said they should.

Option three.

Option three was climbing the stairs to the guild house on Lantern Row, putting the sealed case on the scarred oak desk of the man who had raised her, and saying: *Tell me what this is. Tell me what I am. All of it this time, old man, or I swear by every god they haven't banned yet, I will open it myself and read whatever falls out.*

Her heart said option three, stupid and loyal as a dog. Her survival instincts — honed ten years sharp, rule one, never be interesting — said option one, or better, option zero: never have existed.

The courier had smiled when he saw her run. Relief. *Someone finally.*

Someone finally what?

Wren Zhou closed her hand around the warm wax seal, and beneath the stairwell, in the dark, she made her choice — or rather, she admitted the choice she'd already made, because the moment his blood touched her sleeve, some part of her had known.

She stood up, tucked the strongbox under her arm, and started toward Lantern Row.

Behind her, far off across the city, a bell began to ring — the market bell, tolling the hour, ordinary and oblivious — and Wren walked toward the one truth she'd spent a decade avoiding, carrying a dead man's message in her fist.

*It's her turn,* he'd said. *He'll understand.*

She intended to make Voss explain it. Every word.

And if he wouldn't — well. She still had a knife, a strongbox, and, gods help her, a seal to break.

The bell rang on. Somewhere behind her, faint and getting fainter, she thought she heard the gray cloaks calling her description through the lanes.

Small. Quick eyes. Warm hands.

They weren't wrong, she thought grimly, and climbed.
