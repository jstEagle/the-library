## 3. What the Guild Knew

The guildhall smelled the way it always had: beeswax, old paper, and the faint scorched sweetness of soldered tin. Ten years of that smell. It was the smell of home, or what Wren had learned to call home, and tonight it made her stomach turn.

She had run the whole way from the market, map crushed against her ribs beneath her coat, weaving through the evening crowds with the particular talent for invisibility she'd spent a decade perfecting. Small steps. Downcast eyes. The walk of a girl carrying nothing worth stealing. She'd slipped through the guild's back door on Copperlane Street the way she'd slipped out of it ten thousand mornings — quick, quiet, unremarkable.

Except now her hands were shaking so badly she could barely work the latch.

"You're late." Odell Voss stood at the long workbench in the main hall, sleeves rolled to his elbows, silvering hair tied back with the same leather cord he'd used since before she could remember. He didn't look up from the charm he was wiring — a luck-knot, copper and river glass, the kind they sold by the dozen to sailors' wives. "Brannigan took your corner today, I hear. You'll want to be earlier tomorrow, or he'll have it by spring."

"Voss." Her voice came out wrong. Thin. He glanced up then, and whatever he saw in her face made him set down his pliers very slowly.

"Wren?"

"There was a man." The words tumbled out before she could arrange them into something safer. "At my stall. He was bleeding, Voss, there was blood all down his side, and he grabbed me and — he gave me this." She pulled the map from inside her coat and held it out, and hated how her hand trembled. "He said the Root is failing. Those words exactly. And then the Inquisitors came and they — they killed him. Right there. In the street. In front of everyone."

Voss went still in a way she had never seen him go still. Not thoughtful-still, not tired-still. *Afraid*-still. For three heartbeats he did nothing but look at the wax seal, dark and cracked, on the roll of vellum in her outstretched hand.

Then he crossed the room in four strides and snatched it from her fingers.

"Where else has this been? Who saw him give it to you?"

"I — nobody, I don't think, the crowd scattered when—"

"The Inquisitors. Did they see you take it?"

"They were cutting him down, Voss, I don't think they were—" She stopped. Breathed. Started over, the way he'd taught her to do when a customer got difficult. "I ran. Like you always say. Be small, be quick, be gone."

"Good girl." But he didn't say it warmly. He said it the way you'd praise a soldier for keeping her head down, and that was wrong too, because Voss never talked like that, Voss talked about profit margins and festival season and whether Pip had watered down the glue again.

He turned toward the hearth with the map in both hands.

"What are you doing?"

"Burning it."

The word hit her like cold water. "What? No — Voss, a man *died* for that. He bled out against my stall trying to get it to someone, and maybe he was mad, but maybe he wasn't, and you're just going to—"

"Yes." He was already kneeling at the fire, feeding the map's edge toward the coals. "That is precisely what I'm going to do, and you're going to help me forget we ever discussed it."

Wren crossed the hall faster than she'd meant to and caught his wrist. His arm, under her grip, felt thinner than she remembered. Old. When had he gotten old?

"Tell me what it is," she said. "You looked at it and you *knew* it. I've watched you appraise everything from fake dragonbone to a countess's forged will, and you looked at that scrap of vellum for one second and your face went gray. So tell me."

"Wren—"

"*Tell me.*"

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he hadn't since she was small enough to sit on his workbench and sort glass beads by color — and something in his face shifted. Resignation, maybe. Or grief. Or the particular exhaustion of a man who has been holding a door shut for thirty years and has just heard the first splinter crack.

"You were always too clever for the trade," he said quietly. "Too clever, and too stubborn, and far kinder than is safe." He drew the map back from the coals. "Sit down, child."

"I'd rather stand."

"I'd rather you sat." A ghost of his usual dry humor flickered and died. "It's a long story, and my knees are seventy-two years old."

She sat. He did not burn the map. Instead he laid it flat on the workbench, weighting the corners with tin punches, and stood looking down at it for a long moment — an old man studying a letter from someone he'd hoped was dead.

It was a map of Corvane. That much she could see even upside-down. But not the Corvane of any chart she'd ever seen sold — no ward boundaries, no tax districts, no tidy streets. This was older, ink faded to the brown of dried blood, and threaded across the city were lines. Hundreds of lines, fine as spider silk, connecting point after point after point. Market stalls. Doorways. Chimneys. Wells. A line ended at a little drawn square on Copperlane Street, and beside it, in cramped antique script, someone had written a single word:

*Anchor.*

Wren's mouth went dry. She knew that square. She had slept above it for ten years.

"That's our cellar," she said. "Our storeroom. Why does this map say—"

"Because it is," Voss said. "And it is." He lowered himself into the chair across from her with the care of a man whose body had begun negotiating terms with gravity. "How many charms do you suppose we've sold in ten years, Wren? Roughly."

"I don't know. Thousands? We do good business at festival time—"

"Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six." He said it without checking anything. "Give or take the ones Pip miscounted, which is most of them. Forty-one thousand charms, each one placed in a specific hand, sold at a specific price, in a specific part of the city. You thought that was commerce."

"It *was* commerce. It paid for the roof and your ridiculous tea habit—"

"It paid for the roof," Voss agreed. "And it kept every soul in this city alive, which I grant you is better business." He pushed himself up, joints cracking, and went to the great iron-banded chest behind his desk — the one she'd been told, since childhood, held the tax records and was therefore the most boring object in the world. He unlocked it with a key from around his neck.

Inside were no tax records.

Inside were books. Dozens of them, leather-bound, spines cracked, arranged in careful chronological order. Voss lifted the newest one — its cover barely worn — and set it on the bench in front of her.

"This is the ledger," he said. "The real one."

Wren opened it. The pages were dense with entries in Voss's precise hand, and for a moment she thought it was ordinary bookkeeping — dates, items, sums. Then she read closer.

*Third of Harvestmonth. Luck-knot, copper/glass, sold to Maren Ostler, fishwife, Harborside. Anchor 212. Holding steady. Warmth: faint.*

*Fifth of Harvestmonth. Warding bead, iron/rowan, gifted to child (unnamed), Tanner's Row. Anchor 198. Fading. Recommend replacement within the year. Warmth: trace only.*

*Ninth of Harvestmonth. Hearth-charm, silver/thornwood, sold to Alderman Creel. Anchor 77. **Failed.** Replacement dispatched. Warmth: none.*

Warmth.

Her skin prickled. Because she knew about warmth. Every charm they made carried a faint warmth if you held it long enough — real warmth, not the trick of a candle-warmed coin she used to demonstrate the fakes. She'd discovered it as a child and assumed it was some harmless quirk of Voss's craft, some residual heat from the soldering iron. She'd hidden it from customers for years, because warmth suggested magic, and magic suggested gallows.

"How many anchors?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away. "In the city. Total."

"When the ledger began, six hundred and twelve." Voss turned the pages backward, backward, backward — decades of entries scrolling past, the handwriting changing, aging, becoming another man's entirely, and then another's, generations of it. "Today? Two hundred and nine hold. The rest have failed or are failing. The charm dies, the anchor dies, and the line goes slack." He tapped the map, where dozens of the fine silk-lines had been carefully crossed out in red ink. Crossed out in what looked, up close, disturbingly like blood.

"And what happens when a line goes slack?"

Voss was quiet for a moment. Outside, somewhere on Copperlane, a cart rattled past and someone laughed, ordinary and oblivious.

"You've heard the well water going bitter in Lowbridge," he said finally. "You've heard the stories about the woods past the walls — how the treeline seems nearer than it was, how folk swear the hedges lean in at night. People blame bad wells and drunk eyes." He met her gaze. "It's the covenant, Wren. The old binding between this world and the Thornwood. It was anchored here — anchored *everywhere*, once, in a thousand little objects like ours, tended by a hundred guilds like ours. And the covenant is failing, and every anchor we lose lets a little more of the wild leak through, and a little more of the ordered world drain away. The courier was right. The Root is failing. Has been failing since before you were born. We are simply the last fools still minding the shop."

Wren looked down at the ledger. At forty thousand entries in her family's handwriting, each one a small warm thing pressed into a stranger's palm, each one a stitch holding a dying world together.

"We sold them for coppers," she whispered. "We charged *two bits* for the end of the world."

"We charged what people could pay," Voss said, with a flicker of his old pride. "A guild that charges fairly keeps its customers, and customers who come back keep their charms close. Sentiment, Wren, is the finest preservative ever devised. Half those anchors have lasted fifty years because some grandmother refused to throw away her lucky knot." He almost smiled. Almost. "But that isn't why you're angry."

No. It wasn't.

"Ten years." She stood, and her chair scraped loud in the quiet hall. "Ten years I've worked this bench. I've soldered, I've hawked, I've paid protection coin, I've memorized patrol routes and lied to Inquisitors with a smile on my face. And the whole time — the *whole time* — you knew. All of it. The warmth, the anchors, the covenant, whatever the Root is. You let me believe I was selling *trinkets*. You let me believe I was *small*."

"You *were* small. That was the entire point." Voss rose too, and for the first time she noticed how he had to brace his hand on the bench to do it. "Do you know what happened to the other guilds? There were eleven in this city when I was your age. Eleven. The Lantern Guilds of the old compact, sworn to tend the anchors in silence. The Inquisition found the Glassmakers in '41. Found the Chandlers in '44. They burned the guildhalls with everyone inside and called it plague year. The only reason we survived, the only reason *any* of us survived, is because we became invisible. Harmless. A shabby shop selling lucky rubbish to gullible fishermen." His voice roughened. "Small kept you alive, girl. Small kept *me* alive. And yes, I lied to you, every day of your life, because a lie that keeps a child off the pyre is a lie I'll carry gladly into whatever hell is owed me."

"You could have told me when I was grown!"

"Told you *what*? That the world rests on trinkets and the trinkets are failing and there is nothing anyone can do?" He laughed, short and bitter. "Knowledge is weight, Wren. I watched your face tonight when you told me about that courier. You already carry his death like a stone. I promised myself you'd carry nothing heavier. Call me a coward. Everyone I've ever loved eventually does."

The word landed before she could stop it — *loved* — and it disarmed her more thoroughly than any argument. Because that was the unbearable part, wasn't it. He wasn't a monster. He was Voss: stingy with praise, extravagant with tea, who had taught her letters and lock-picking and how to fall without hurting herself, who had found a screaming infant abandoned on his doorstep in the dead of winter and — 

Wren went very still.

"Voss," she said slowly. "When you found me. On the doorstep."

His face closed like a shutter.

"What did I have with me? What was wrapped around me? Was there anything — a token, a cloth, a—"

"*Wren.*"

"—something that came from the other side of the veil?" She hadn't known she was going to say it until it was out. But the map was still lying between them, and the ledger was still open, and the courier's blood was still on her memory, and suddenly ten years of small oddities were rearranging themselves into a pattern she couldn't unsee: the way stray cats wouldn't come near her; the way hedges in the garden grew crooked toward her window; the way Voss had flinched, only once, when she'd asked — aged nine — whether she'd been born in Corvane.

Voss looked at her for a long, long moment. And she watched him make a decision, and she watched the decision cost him, and she understood with sick clarity that whatever he decided, she was about to stop being the person she had been this morning.

"No," he said at last, gently. Terribly. "Nothing from the other side. Only you." He gathered the map, the ledger, and began closing the chest, movements suddenly brisk, businesslike, final. "Now listen to me, because we haven't much time before you must be gone from this city. Tomorrow morning you'll take the river barge to your aunt's in Millbrook — yes, the aunt who doesn't exist, don't interrupt — and you'll stay there until I send for you. Take nothing. Tell no one. And Wren—"

"Wait. Gone? I'm not going anywhere, there's a dying covenant, there's a *map*—"

"There is nothing you can do." He said it flatly, and it was the cruelest thing he'd ever said to her, because he said it as fact. "You are an apprentice charm-seller with clever hands and a kind heart, and neither of those things can hold back what's coming. I have spent thirty years failing at it with considerably more experience. Go to Millbrook. Live. Grow old. Forget the Root, forget the ledger, forget—" his voice snagged, barely, "—forget you ever read the word *anchor*. Promise me."

And Wren, who had promised this man a thousand small obediences across ten years — promise me you'll count the change twice, promise me you won't sell to the Ostler boy on credit again, promise me you'll be small, be quick, be gone —

looked at the open ledger, at the entry dated the day she'd arrived on his doorstep, at the notation beside her own name in his younger handwriting, and felt the floor of her whole life tilt.

Because there, in faded ink, in a column headed *Provenance*, was written:

*Delivered through the veil. Intact. Both realms claim her. Insurance.*

Both realms.

*Insurance.*

"Voss," she said, and her voice did not sound like her own. "What am I insurance *for*?"

Before he could answer — before he could lie, or confess, or burn the page — there came a knock at the guildhall's front door. Three slow knocks, evenly spaced, patient as a headsman.

Neither of them moved.

Then a voice, mild and courteous and utterly without warmth, spoke through the oak:

"Guildmaster Voss. Inquisitor Ashgrave. Might I trouble you for a moment of your time? I have questions about a charm stall on Cooper's Row."

* * *

Voss blew out the lamp in a single motion.

"Back stairs," he breathed, pressing the map into her hands and folding her fingers closed over it. "Cellar, then the coal chute, then the tanner's yard. Don't run — walking fast. And Wren—" For one instant his hands framed her face, the way they had when she was seven and feverish, and his thumbs were rough and warm and shaking. "Whatever you learn about yourself, wherever you end up — you were wanted. Whatever else was true, that was always true."

The knocking came again. Still patient. Still courteous.

Wren went down into the dark.
