## 4. Ashgrave Comes Calling

Inquisitor Aldous Ashgrave rose before dawn, as he did every morning, and spent a quarter hour sharpening nothing in particular.

The ritual mattered more than the blade. He kept a whetstone on his desk beside the ledgers, and each morning he drew it along the edge of a knife that was, by any practical measure, already sharp. The sound — a long, patient *shhh* of steel on stone — organized his thoughts the way bells organize monks. Somewhere in Corvane, someone was practicing magic. The sound reminded him that it was his business to find them.

He was not, he reflected, an unkind man. People said otherwise, of course. People said it behind their hands at the fish market and in the confessional booths, and once — memorably — to his face, a dye-shop widow whose son had been taken to the pyre. *You're not cruel,* she had told him, weeping, gripping his coat with both fists as though shaking sense into him. *That's what makes it worse.*

He had thought about that sentence for eleven years and never found the flaw in it.

Cruelty implied appetite. Cruelty was the butcher who liked the work, the magistrate who lingered over sentencing. Ashgrave took no pleasure in burnings. He attended every one — attendance was required by statute — and he watched with the same expression he wore for tax audits: attentive, sympathetic, faintly sorrowful. When the condemned screamed, he mouthed the words of the Litany of Cleansing along with the presiding priest, not out of piety exactly, but because it seemed rude to stand there silent while a human being died partly because of him.

But magic killed. This was not doctrine; it was arithmetic. He had grown up in Millhaven, downstream of the Sundering, when the covenant's edges had frayed and something green and hungry had come through the veil into the river country. He remembered the year the wells went sweet-then-bitter, the crops standing black in the fields like burnt matches. He remembered his sister Lark, seven years old, who had been fond of a hedge-witch's charms — harmless things, everyone said, lucky knots and dried lavender, *harmless* — and who had walked into the river one night in her sleep with her eyes open, smiling, and had not come back out.

Harmless. Everyone had said it right up until the end.

So no, Aldous Ashgrave was not cruel. He was a man doing sums, and the sums always came out the same. Every charm burned was a well that stayed clean, a child who woke up in her own bed, a border that held. He would carry the dye-shop widow's grief and Lark's wet ghost to his own grave, and he would keep counting anyway. Someone had to.

* * *

The market at Corvane's Saltgate opened with the smell of frying onions and the sound of forty people lying about the freshness of their fish, and Ashgrave moved through it the way he moved through everything: slowly, thoroughly, without apology.

"Morning, Inquisitor," called the eel-seller, who had learned that greeting first and asking forgiveness second was the cheaper order of operations.

"Mistress Hobb." He paused at her stall, admired the eels without wanting any, and asked after her daughter's fever. Better, she said. He noted it. He noted everything; it was the whole of his method. An Inquisitor who shouted and kicked doors found nothing but broken doors. An Inquisitor who remembered that Mistress Hobb's daughter had a fever, and that the fever had been bad last month, might one day notice that the fever was suddenly, impossibly gone — and might ask, gently, who she'd bought the charm from.

Not that he suspected Hobb. Her luck ran honest and bad, like most people's.

He worked the rows. He let a pie vendor overcharge him for a pie he didn't eat, because paying inflated prices made vendors chatty. He listened to two porters argue about a dropped crate and filed away, without quite knowing why yet, the fact that the crate had come off a cart from the Thornward road. He complimented a child's chalk drawing on the cobbles — a lopsided sun with a face — and felt the small warm pang he always felt around children, which he had learned to treat the way a man treats a sore tooth: acknowledged, endured, never indulged.

It was near midmorning, in the row of stalls along the old guild wall, that the day turned.

He smelled it before he saw anything. Not magic itself — magic rarely announced itself to the nose — but the absence under it. A stillness, like the hush in a room where a clock has just stopped. Ten years of hunting had taught his senses to notice the shape of that hush, and now it hung over a narrow stall wedged against the wall: a folding table, a bolt of faded cloth, and a modest array of trinkets laid out with more care than such trinkets usually received.

Charms. Lucky knots, painted beads, little woven stars, a jar of river-stones labeled *FOR SAFE TRAVEL* in a careful hand. The kind of inventory a hundred sellers in Corvane carried, all equally fraudulent, all equally legal.

Except this table hummed.

Not aloud. Anyone else walking past would have seen junk. But Ashgrave stood very still, hat in hand, rain-damp hair combed flat, and let the sensation wash over him the way a diver lets cold water close over his head. Residue. Faint, old, disciplined — the kind of trace left not by a burst of power but by power held carefully in check for years. Like standing in a cellar where wine was once stored. The wine was gone. The cellar remembered.

And beneath the residue, newer, sharper: blood. Old blood, hours old, scrubbed at but not vanquished, soaked somewhere below the level of the cobbles.

"Well," said Ashgrave pleasantly, to no one in particular. "Hello."

The stall was unmanned. A stool sat empty. A half-eaten apple on a saucer had gone brown at the bite — abandoned in haste, or simply forgotten by a seller stepping away? He looked at the arrangement of goods and noted the small professional details: prices chalked low enough to be humble, high enough to suggest quality; the good pieces angled toward the foot traffic from the gate; the cheap ones up front as decoys. Whoever ran this stall understood people.

People were easy. It was the residue that interested him.

"Inquisitor?" A city watchman had drifted over, the way watchmen drift toward trouble the way gulls drift toward fish carts. "Trouble?"

"Whose stall is this?"

The watchman squinted. "The charm girl. Wren somebody. Zhou, I think. Been here years — sells knick-knacks. Harmless."

*Harmless.* Ashgrave smiled the smile he kept for that word, the one that made the watchman take half a step back without knowing why. "Where does she lodge?"

"Honest, sir, nobody knows. She's careful. Pays the lamplighters extra to sweep her corner first thing, if you follow me." The watchman lowered his voice, conspiratorial in the manner of men who gossip professionally. "There's talk she's got a patron. Somebody big. Nobody's ever seen her scared, is the thing. Ten years in Saltgate market and never once robbed. You don't stay untouched that long on luck alone."

"No," Ashgrave agreed softly. "You really don't."

He circled the stall twice, unhurried, cataloguing. The cloth was dyed with madder root — common. The trinkets were wood and glass and river-stone — inert, or meant to seem so. But when he held his palm flat above the table, six inches off, the way he'd been taught by old Inquisitor Brandt (dead now, God rest a hard man), he felt it again: that cellar-hush, that stopped-clock stillness, layered so deep and so evenly that it could only be the work of long habit. Someone had been dampening real power at this table for years. Damping it down to nothing. Smothering it daily, patiently, the way you'd keep a fire banked in a stove you couldn't put out.

Which raised a question he found genuinely interesting: why would a fraud bother?

A fraud needed no damping. Only a true practitioner needed to hide warmth under cold ash — and only a very disciplined one could hold the mask this seamless. His heart did the thing it always did at moments like this, a slow heavy knock, grief and purpose arriving together like twins. He thought of Lark. He thought of the hedge-witch's lavender sachets. He thought of clean wells and children waking in their beds, and he set his hat back on his head with the care of a man settling a yoke onto his own shoulders.

"Leave the stall as it is," he told the watchman. "Touch nothing. If she returns, be pleasant. Be boring. And send word to the Bastion before she's finished selling her first charm."

"And if she runs?"

Ashgrave looked down the length of the market row, past the eels and the pies and the chalk sun with its smiling face, toward the warren of alleys where a careful person might vanish for years at a stretch. He felt the net in his mind draw closed, strand by patient strand, and experienced the closest thing to joy his temperament allowed: the satisfaction of arithmetic resolving.

"They always run eventually," he said. "Running is how they tell you where the door is."

* * *

He spent the afternoon being kind to people, which was, in his experience, far more frightening than the alternative.

He bought tea for the lamplighters' guild foreman and asked, idly, about protection coin — who paid it, who collected it, which corners cost more than others. The foreman, flattered by the attention and three cups deep, mentioned almost immediately the charm girl's corner by the guild wall. *Pays double, always on time, never haggles. Funny, that. Everyone haggles.*

Funny indeed. Protection coin wasn't tribute to bullies; done properly, it purchased silence and swept floors and averted eyes. A girl paying double for invisibility wasn't hiding from thieves. She was hiding from precisely the sort of man who bought tea for lamplighters.

He interviewed the fishwife adjacent to the empty stall, who remembered the girl as polite and quiet and — here the fishwife leaned in, savoring the morsel — *oddly sad*, the way some pretty things are sad. He interviewed the pie vendor, who remembered a courier, yesterday evening, gray-faced, stumbling through the rows like a drunk, asking after charm-sellers. He interviewed the boy who drew chalk suns, who had seen "a lady runned" the night before, fast, toward the river lanes, "with her face all scrinched up."

Scrinched. Frightened, then. Running. Good. Fear made people clumsy, and clumsy people left the residue he could read like tracks in snow.

By dusk he stood in the Bastion's map room, a tall chamber smelling of tallow and cold stone, moving wooden markers across a chart of Corvane while two junior inquisitors watched with the anxious devotion of dogs hoping to be praised.

"She has three refuges available to her." He placed a marker on the river lanes. "The smugglers' warrens under Gull Street — but smugglers sell to whoever pays, and fear pays better than coin." A second marker, in the tenement district. "Rented rooms change hands weekly; someone will remember a new face." A third marker went down at the edge of the map, where the inked streets gave way to blank parchment and, beyond it, the hatched border of the Thornwood. He rested his finger there a moment longer than necessary.

"The woods," said the younger inquisitor, whose name was Pell and who was eager in the way that got eager people hurt. "She wouldn't. No one goes to the Thornwood."

"People go to the Thornwood constantly," Ashgrave said mildly. "They simply don't come back, and we've agreed as an institution not to find that interesting." He straightened. "Post watchers at the river gates and the Gull Street mouths. Quiet ones. And pull every record we hold on the charm-and-trinket trade going back fifteen years. Licenses, confiscations, complaints, market taxes. If our charm girl has been careful for ten years, then somewhere in ten years of paperwork she has been careless once."

"And when we find her, sir?" Pell again, hand drifting toward the iron cuff-box on the shelf, hungry for procedure.

Ashgrave considered the question honestly, because he considered all questions honestly; it was the only form of prayer he trusted.

"When we find her," he said, "we will be gentle, and we will be thorough, and we will ask her one question above all others. Not *did you practice* — they all lie about that, it means nothing. We will ask her what her charms were *for*. Because residue like what I felt today doesn't gather by accident. That much discipline, that many years — she wasn't playing at magic, Pell. She was *maintaining* something." He looked at the marker resting on the blank edge of the map, at the hatched line of the Thornwood behind it. "And I want very much to know what."

The juniors bowed and withdrew. Alone, Ashgrave took out his whetstone and drew it once along his knife, the long patient *shhh* filling the empty map room like a bell tolling for one.

He thought of Lark, asleep and smiling, walking into the dark water. He thought of clean wells. He told himself, as he told himself every night, that tomorrow's work was mercy wearing armor, that the fire cleansed rather than punished, that a world without magic was simply a world where children stayed children.

Most nights the telling held.

Tonight, for reasons he declined to examine, the telling wobbled — and in the wobble he heard, absurdly, the memory of a chalk sun drawn on wet cobbles, smiling its crooked smile up at him, already beginning to run in the rain.

He snuffed the lamp and went to bed, and dreamed, as he always did, of the river.

* * *

Word came at dawn, as word does: the charm girl had surfaced. Not in the river lanes, not in Gull Street, not in any rented room. She had been seen at the Bastion's own front step, pale as milk, asking — of all things — for an audience with Guildmaster Odell Voss of the Hollow Lantern Guild.

Ashgrave stood in his office with the report in hand and felt the strange pleasure of a puzzle rearranging itself into a sharper shape. The Hollow Lantern. The guild whose charter predated the purges. The guild that sold charms openly, licensed and taxed and dismissed as harmless by three generations of magistrates — the one loophole in all of Corvane's law, grandfathered in by some ancient treaty nobody could produce anymore.

*Harmless,* everyone said about the Hollow Lantern.

Everyone said it right up until the end.

He took up his coat and his knife and his whetstone, and he sent a runner ahead with instructions to seal the guildhouse doors, quietly, before anyone inside thought to leave. Then he stepped out into the grey morning light of Corvane, a kind man with clean arithmetic, and began — unhurried, thorough, without apology — to walk toward the net.
