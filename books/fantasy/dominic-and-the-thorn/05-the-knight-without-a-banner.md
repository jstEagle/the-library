## 5. The Knight Without a Banner

The trouble with being small and unnoticed for ten years, Wren thought as she pressed herself into the gap between a cooper's shed and a rain barrel, was that eventually you got very good at noticing when it stopped working.

She had heard the patrol before she saw them — the particular rhythm of Inquisitor boots, not the loose shuffle of city watch but the metronome click of men who had been taught to walk in step with their own certainty. Four of them, maybe five, moving up Tanner's Row in a line that left no gaps. Behind her, the mouth of the alley was already filling with grey cloaks.

They weren't looking for a charm seller. Wren knew that. Charm sellers were beneath the Inquisition's notice; that was the entire architecture of her survival. They were looking for whoever the dead courier had spoken to last, and the dead courier had bled out against her stall with his fingers closed around hers, and Ashgrave's people were nothing if not thorough.

*Left is blocked. Right is blocked.* She took inventory the way she took inventory of her stock: quickly, without sentiment. The cooper's yard had one exit and it opened onto the Row. The wall behind her was eight feet of good oak staves, too high to climb and too honest to bribe.

"Well," she said quietly, to nobody. "This is stupid."

It was the truest thing she'd said all week.

She was still calculating the odds of talking her way past four Inquisitors — low, historically — when the door beside her opened.

Not burst open. *Opened*, with the mild creak of hinges that needed oiling, and a woman stepped out carrying a bucket of wash water as though she had every right to it and no interest at all in Wren whatsoever. Tall. Grey-streaked hair cut short as a soldier's. A long coat that had once been fine and now was merely clean, and hands that were wrong for laundry work — scarred across the knuckles, calloused in the pattern of a sword grip rather than a scrub brush.

The woman glanced at Wren. Glanced at the patrol coming up the Row. Something in her face did the same arithmetic Wren's had just done, only faster, and arrived at a more generous answer.

"In here," she said. "Quickly, if you'd rather not hang."

Wren did not need telling twice. She slipped through the doorway, and the woman followed, and the door shut behind them with a soft click that sounded, to Wren's straining ears, like the loudest thing in Corvane.

* * *

The room beyond was a single bare chamber above the cooper's yard: a cot, a chair, a basin, a window overlooking nothing in particular. It had the look of a place where someone lived who had decided at some point that living was mostly a matter of keeping things flat and square.

The woman set down the bucket, which turned out to contain actual wash water, because apparently even dramatic rescues benefited from props.

"Name," she said.

"Wren." Then, because lying on top of a lie gets heavy: "Just Wren."

"Ser Ilsa Marrow." She said it flatly, the way you'd say a sentence you'd been made to repeat until it stopped meaning anything. "Though the 'Ser' is decorative these days. Sit down. You look like a rabbit that's read a book about hawks."

Wren sat on the cot, which creaked. Through the wall she could hear the patrol passing — boots, voices, the clipped cadence of an officer reading a description aloud. *Woman, small, dark-haired, market stall near the Bellows Gate—*

"They have my stall," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was its own small miracle. "They're going to turn my stall inside out."

"Yes."

"Some of those charms are—" She stopped. Rewound. "Some of those charms are worth money."

Ilsa Marrow looked at her for a long moment. It was not an unkind look, exactly. It was the look of someone assessing whether a bridge would hold her weight, and Wren had been assessed by better liars than this and knew precisely what was being weighed.

"The courier," Ilsa said. "The one who died on the Bellows Road yesterday evening. He spoke to you."

It wasn't a question, so Wren didn't answer it. She was proud of that. Her face, she suspected, answered it comprehensively.

"I watched him die," Ilsa said, turning to the window, watching the empty street below with the patience of someone used to waiting for things that took years. "From the far side of the road. There was nothing I could do — five of them, and me without—" Her hand moved briefly toward her hip, toward the absence of something. "He ran past me first. He said one word. He said *Marrow*. As if I were supposed to know what to do about it."

"Do you?" Wren asked. "Know what to do about it?"

"No." Ilsa turned back. "But I know what he was carrying, or I can guess, and I know what the Inquisition does to people who are guessed to be carrying it. So. Here is what happens next. In an hour, when they've finished pulling apart your little table of lies, they will start asking the neighbors questions. Neighbors are wonderful people, but they fold like wet paper. By nightfall they'll have your face. By tomorrow they'll have your name. And by the day after, if you're still in Corvane, you'll be in a cell learning things about yourself you never wanted to know."

"That's a very detailed forecast."

"I used to do this for a living," Ilsa said. "The other side of it."

Wren studied her. The coat. The hands. The word *Marrow* spoken by a dying man as though it were a key. Ten years of surviving Corvane had taught her that the most dangerous thing in any room was usually the person who wasn't trying to take anything from you yet.

"You're the knight," Wren said slowly. "The one from the stories the old guild members tell when they've had too much plum wine. Ser Ilsa Marrow, who was sworn to guard the covenant, and when it failed ten years ago they stripped her oath and put her out of the order." She paused. "They say you cried at your own disgrace."

"They say a great many things." Ilsa's expression didn't change, but something behind it went very still, the way a pond goes still when a stone has just gone through it. "Did they also tell you why the covenant failed?"

"No."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "Neither did anyone else. That's rather the shape of the whole story." She crossed to the basin, poured water, drank like it was a duty. "You have a map. Wax-sealed. Bloodstained. And whatever Voss told you about it, I promise you the truth is worse."

Wren came off the cot before she'd decided to. "You know Odell Voss?"

"I know *of* him. We've never met. But his guild kept the western anchors, and mine kept the roads between them, and neither of us was ever told what we were actually holding together." She set down the cup. "Until ten years ago, when mine broke."

Something cold walked down Wren's spine. "The courier said the Root is failing."

"I heard." For the first time, Ilsa Marrow's composure cracked — not much, just a hairline, just enough to see the older fracture underneath. She sat down heavily on the chair, and suddenly she looked less like a rescuing knight and more like a tired woman in her forties whose knees hurt. "Do you know what I spent ten years believing, charm seller? That I failed. That the covenant fell because I was slow, or cowardly, or insufficient, pick your flavor, the order offered all three at my trial. I built my whole life on top of that belief the way you build a house on a foundation. And yesterday a dying man ran past me saying *Marrow*, and today the Inquisition mobilizes half its city strength over a map, and I am beginning to suspect the foundation was never there at all."

"You want to fix it," Wren said. "Your failure."

"I want the truth," Ilsa said. "The fixing, if there is any, comes after. Usually does." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at Wren directly, and there was nothing warm in it, but there was nothing false either, and after the week Wren had had, nothing-false felt almost like tenderness. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it once. I can get you out of Corvane tonight. Not through the gates — those are watched, and you're already painted on them. Through the old ways, the ones the order used to use, that the Inquisition thinks are bricked up. I know the Thornwood roads better than any living person, which is a small distinction since most of the living people who knew them are dead. I will take you to the Worldroot, or to whatever's left of it. In exchange, you tell me everything the courier said, everything Voss knows, and you don't lie to me. Not once. I've had my fill of institutions that lie beautifully. I want one person in this whole rotten arrangement who doesn't."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll wish you luck, finish my laundry, and read about you in the Inquisitor's broadsheets next week." She shrugged. "I'm not your mother. I'm barely your ally. I'm a woman with a route and a grudge, and you happen to be standing on both."

Wren looked at her for a long time.

Every instinct she owned — every instinct that had kept her alive through ten years of Corvane winters, protection coin, and Odell Voss's loving, lying household — was screaming at her that this was how it always went. Someone strong offers you safety. Someone strong asks for trust. And then somewhere down the road the bill arrives, and it's always larger than quoted.

But her other instinct — the newer one, two days old, born in blood on her doorstep — pointed out that staying in Corvane was death with extra steps, and that Voss had ordered her to burn the only honest thing anyone had handed her in a decade.

"One condition," Wren said.

Isla raised an eyebrow.

"We go by way of the Guildhall cellar first. There's something there I need — the ledger, the real one, the one Voss doesn't know I've seen. If we're going to the Root, I want to bring the anchors' records. Whatever's failing, maybe the answer's written down somewhere, and I refuse to walk into a forest full of walking trees armed with nothing but a map and personality."

A sound escaped Ilsa Marrow that took Wren a moment to identify as a laugh — short, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from cover.

"Personality," she repeated. "Gods help the Thornwood." She stood, and the tiredness fell away from her the way a cloak falls, revealing the soldier underneath, and Wren understood suddenly why dying couriers trusted this woman with their last words. "Ledger, map, and personality. Fine. But we move at dusk, we move quiet, and if I say run, you run, and if I say leave something behind, you *leave it*." She held out her hand. "Do we have terms?"

Wren looked at the hand. Scarred knuckles, sword calluses, steady as a doorframe.

Ten years of trusting no one, and here she was, about to bet her life on a disgraced knight she'd known for the length of one cup of water.

"Terms," she said, and shook it.

* * *

Dusk came down over Corvane like a lid.

They went out through the cooper's yard, Ilsa carrying the wash bucket as though it were the most natural thing in the world, Wren wrapped in a borrowed shawl two sizes too big with the map sewn flat against her ribs under her shift, where no search would casually find it. Ilsa moved through the back lanes the way water moves through cracks — no wasted motion, no hesitation, an unerring sense of which shadows were safe and which ones had eyes.

Twice they froze in doorways while patrols passed close enough to touch. Once a watchman looked directly at them, and Ilsa produced from somewhere a laundry token and an expression of such profound, aggressive boredom that the man apologized to *her* and moved along.

"He apologized," Wren whispered, when they were clear.

"Boredom is armor," Ilsa murmured back. "Never forget it. Nobody robs a woman who looks like Tuesdays disappoint her."

And despite everything — the dead courier, the burning map, the family that had lied to her since infancy — Wren felt something in her chest that took her a moment to name, because it had been so long since she'd felt it.

It was almost fun.

They reached the mouth of the old storm culvert behind the tanneries as the last light died. Ilsa pried loose a grate that had allegedly been rusted shut for a decade, revealing steps going down into breathing darkness.

"After you," Ilsa said.

"Why me?"

"Because if there's something down there with teeth, it should eat the smaller one first. It's only fair." And there it was again, that rusty almost-laugh, and Wren found herself smiling into the dark as she descended.

Behind them, above, the bells of the Inquisitors' quarter began to ring — not the hour, but the other peal, the one every child in Corvane learned to fear before they learned their letters. The hunt bell.

"They've found the stall," Wren said.

"They've found the stall," Ilsa agreed, swinging the grate shut behind them with a clang that echoed down into the deep places under the city. "Which means by morning they'll have found the trail to the tanneries, which means we have perhaps six hours' lead, and the man we need to meet at the floodgate keeps hours like a cat keeps promises." She struck a small hooded lantern alight. Its glow caught her face, grim and faintly amused. "So let's hope the smuggler owes you a better debt than he owes me."

"The smuggler?"

"Pip Fenwick. Half-fae, wholly unreliable, owes Voss more money than either of them will admit aloud." Ilsa started down the steps. "And the only soul alive who knows the way under the river."

Wren stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the hunt bell fade above, feeling the map press against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Somewhere ahead, in the wet black under Corvane, a smuggler was waiting. Somewhere beyond that, a forest full of walking trees. Somewhere behind her, everything she had ever called home, burning quietly down to its foundations.

"Right," she said to no one, and followed the lantern down.
