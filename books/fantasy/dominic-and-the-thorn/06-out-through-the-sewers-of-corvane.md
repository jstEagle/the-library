## 6. Out Through the Sewers of Corvane

The grate behind the tannery smelled like everything I'd ever tried to forget about Corvane.

"Charming," Ser Ilsa Marrow said, peering down into the dark. She said it the way she said most things — flat as a paving stone — but her hand was already on the iron ring, hauling it up with a scrape that made my teeth ache. "I've besieged fortresses with better manners than this city."

"You've *besieged* fortresses?" Pip asked from the shadow of the rain barrel, where he'd been doing something complicated to a lantern with his thumbs. He was a small man, quick-made, with ears that came to subtle points he hid under a wool cap and eyes the color of pond water at dusk. "See, this is why I don't work with soldiers. Soldiers think 'escape' means 'toward the fighting.'"

"I'm not a soldier anymore," Ilsa said. "I'm nothing anymore. It's very restful."

I crouched at the mouth of the alley and watched the street. Somewhere past the fishmongers' row, an Inquisitor patrol was moving door to door, and every door they knocked on opened too fast, too politely, the way doors do when everyone in Corvane has learned what questions smell like. The courier's blood had come off my hands two washings ago; the memory of it hadn't come off at all.

"Pip," I said. "How long?"

"How long to get us through?" He tucked the lantern under his arm. It gave off a light that was somehow *apologetic*, a gray-green glow that wouldn't carry past ten feet. Fae-work, obviously, though he'd deny it with his dying breath. "Through the Undercorv, past the Weep, up through the old pilgrim gate? If nobody screams, if nobody drowns, and if His Worshipfulness Voss's directions are actually worth the debt I owe him — half a night."

"And if somebody screams?"

Pip smiled. It was a nice smile. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Then we find out how fast a disgraced knight can run in armor."

"I can run," Ilsa said, with dignity, "in armor."

* * *

The ladder went down farther than I liked. That was the thing about Corvane: the city you walked was only the top layer, like scum on soup. Beneath it were the cellars of cellars, the foundations of foundations, and beneath those the Undercorv — the old drainage ways the founders had cut when Corvane was young and arrogant and believed it would never need to hide from anything.

We dropped into standing water ankle-deep, and the apologetic lantern turned the black brick walls the color of old bruises.

"Left here," Pip said.

"Are you sure?" Ilsa asked.

"No," Pip said cheerfully. "But Voss's letter says left, and I have decided to trust him exactly as far as his coin is heavy and no further." He patted his coat pocket, where a folded paper rustled. "You want to know the worst part, charm-girl? It's not the smell. It's not the rats, though I see we're going to be popular with the rats. It's that I *like* him. Twenty years I've known Odell Voss, and he's saved my neck twice, and I hate owing him because a man like Voss always collects. You know what I mean?"

I did know. I'd been collecting interest on Voss's kindness since I was seven years old and shivering on his doorstep. But I said, "He raised me."

"Mm," said Pip, in the tone of a man filing something away for later.

Ilsa brought up the rear without being asked. That was the kind of woman she was — she took the dangerous position by instinct, the way other people reached for a door handle. Her armor was plain steel gone dull with years of neglect, no banner, no sigil, just a knight-shaped absence where her honor used to be. She'd told me almost nothing about herself in the two days since she'd pulled me out from between two patrols. Only that she'd once been sworn to guard something called the covenant, and that when it failed — when the Thornwood sealed itself and the last of the guild's true charms began to gutter — she'd been exiled for asking too loudly whose fault it was.

She kept looking at me sideways, though. Like I was a question she hadn't worked out how to ask.

The water deepened. The ceiling lowered. Twice we waded through side-channels where the dark pressed close enough to taste, and Pip hummed under his breath — some tuneless little thing, half lullaby, half sea shanty — and I realized after a while that the rats were keeping pace with us but never crossing into the lantern's light.

"That's a charm," I said quietly. "Your humming."

"It's a *song*," Pip said, wounded.

"It's a fae song. Your mother's people."

His humming stopped. For three steps the only sound was water and Ilsa's measured breathing, and then Pip said, lightly, so lightly it barely held its own weight: "My mother sang it. Before your precious Inquisitors decided half-bloods were contraband. So yes, charm-girl, it keeps the rats polite. Anything else you'd like to inventory while we're fleeing for our lives?"

"I didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant." He shrugged, quick and birdlike. "Everyone means well. It's the meaning-well that gets people hanged."

Ilsa said, from behind us, "In my experience it's usually the not-meaning-anything that does it."

"Oh, wonderful," Pip muttered. "The knight has opinions. Did the exile come with a refund?"

And that was how it went, for the first hour. Bickering. The three of us picking at each other like gulls over one fish, because it was easier than being afraid, and because none of us knew yet whether we trusted the others enough to be afraid *out loud*. I kept thinking: this is what a family sounds like when it's still deciding. I'd had a family once, or the shape of one — Voss, the other apprentices, the cellar full of charms that hummed warm when I walked past. And now the shape had a hole in it the exact size of a bloodstained map.

* * *

The Weep was where the Undercorv broke open into a cavern the founders had never bothered to roof, because who would ever come down here? Water fell somewhere above us in a hundred silver threads, catching even our dim lantern-light, and the sound filled everything — a soft, ceaseless rushing, like the city whispering to itself.

It was also, Pip explained, the only place in the Undercorv where someone could follow us without our hearing their footsteps.

"So we move fast and we don't talk," Ilsa said.

"We've been talking the whole—"

"*Don't. Talk.*"

We moved fast. The causeway across the Weep was a spine of wet stone barely wider than my shoulders, slick with spray, dropping away on either side into water so black it looked solid. I went second, behind Pip, one hand on the wall. Halfway across, my boot skated, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world — and then Ilsa's gauntleted fist closed on the back of my collar and set me right again without a word, without even breaking stride, the way you'd steady a table.

"Thank you," I breathed.

"Don't mention it," she said. "Seriously. Don't. I have a reputation."

"What reputation? You're *nothing*, remember."

"Nothing with excellent reflexes."

We were nearly across when the light came.

It swept the far gallery first — a hard white beam, nothing apologetic about it, the kind of lantern the Inquisitors carried, blessed glass that showed magic the way a butcher's window shows meat. Voices followed it. At least six. Maybe more, swallowed by the rush of falling water.

"They found the tannery grate," Ilsa said. No panic in it. Just fact.

"Or they're following *us*," Pip said, and his face had gone sharp and strange in the white glare bouncing off the far wall. "Which means either Voss sold us, or Voss was sold, or—" He stopped. His eyes flicked to my coat, where the map rode against my ribs. "Or they don't need to follow anyone. They just need to follow *that*."

The beam swung toward our gallery.

"Off the causeway," Ilsa said. "Now. There'll be a maintenance landing."

"There is absolutely not a maintenance—"

"*Pip.*"

There was a maintenance landing. A rotted plank platform jutting over the black water, wide enough for two people and a grudge. We crowded onto it, pressed against the dripping wall, and killed the lantern, and stood in the dark listening to boots splash closer along the gallery above.

Six men. White lantern. And among the voices, one that made the skin of my arms tighten — calm, unhurried, almost gentle. I couldn't hear the words over the Weep, but I knew the cadence. Everyone in Corvane knew the cadence. Ashgrave did not shout. Ashgrave *asked*, and things confessed.

"He's not searching," I whispered. "Listen. He's telling them where to look. He already knows."

"Then he knows about the map," Ilsa murmured back, "or he wants us to think he does. Either way, that copy in your coat is worth more than all three of our lives right now, and he knows it, which means we cannot let him take it alive off your body."

"Cheerful," Pip whispered.

"Practical."

I put my hand inside my coat. The map was there, wax seal cracked, the courier's dried blood flaking off the corner onto my fingers. And underneath it — because I'd spent a sleepless night with candle stub and a steady hand, because Voss taught me that every good lie needs a twin — was the decoy. An identical sheet, aged with tea, stained with chicken blood, bearing a route that led nowhere except into the arms of whoever chased it hardest.

An idea arrived. It was a bad idea. Most of mine were, lately.

"The gallery forks ahead of them," I whispered. "Past the fork there's an old sluice channel — I saw it on the guild's drainage charts, it runs up toward the tannery district. Dry half the year." I worked the decoy free of the real map's fold, careful, careful, my heart hammering against both papers. "If the decoy were lying somewhere obvious — somewhere they'd *find* it —"

"They'd chase the wrong map," Ilsa finished slowly. "Into the wrong quarter. Buying us hours." She looked at me in the dark. Even in near-blackness I could feel the weight of the look. "It's a risk. If they catch whoever carries it—"

"Nobody carries it. We leave it. They find it abandoned." My throat was dry. "A dropped map reads like a panicked fugitive. Which, for the record, is accurate."

"And if Ashgrave sees through it?"

"Then we're caught anyway, and the question's moot."

Pip made a small sound. When I glanced over, he was grinning in the gloom, that quick fox grin. "Oh, I *like* her. Charm-girl, remind me to teach you smuggling. You've got the temperament — all conscience on the outside, larceny underneath."

"Just throw the map," Ilsa said.

I waited until the boots reached the fork — until the white beam swung left, away, toward the deeper galleries — and then I leaned out over the black water and threw. The decoy spun once, pale as a moth, and landed in the muck of the lower channel where any searcher with a lantern would spot the gleam of wax within minutes. Then we pressed back against the wall and became stones.

The searchers passed below us. Close enough that I could hear the creak of leather, the slosh of boots, the calm voice saying something I couldn't catch — and then stopping. Then a low whistle. Then the scramble of men climbing down, and the triumphant cry: "*Here! Seal's broken — blood on it —*"

The white light swept away, chasing a lie I had made with candle stub and chicken blood, and I pressed my forehead against the cold wet brick and shook, quietly, the way you shake when the fear arrives late and wants its full payment.

A hand settled on my shoulder. Ilsa's. It stayed there maybe four seconds, which from her was practically an embrace.

"Well played," she said.

* * *

We climbed for a long time after that. Up the sluice channel, up a rusted ladder, up through a hatch Pip swore on his mother's grave he could open — and then, when that failed, swore on his *own* grave, which apparently worked better, because the lock surrendered with a clank that echoed like a church bell.

We came out through the floor of a ruined pilgrim chapel on the city's western edge, into air that tasted like rain and freedom and, faintly, still, tanneries. Dawn was a gray rumor through the broken windows. Outside, the old pilgrim road ran west toward hills, toward the border marches, toward the Thornwood that everyone said was only trees.

Pip sat down heavily on a fallen pew and put his head in his hands.

"For the record," he said, muffled, "I want it noted that I have been paid nothing for tonight. Nothing. I have been paid in *debt relief*, which is not a currency, it's a leash with better stitching. Voss calls in twenty years of favors and what do I get? Damp boots. Rats who respect me but don't *like* me. A knight who threatens me with her reputation—"

"You don't have a reputation," Ilsa said, settling onto the opposite pew with a groan of armor. "You have a *record*."

"—and a charm-girl who throws maps into sewage." He lifted his head. Pond-water eyes, tired and bright at once. "Honestly? Best night I've had in years. Isn't that pathetic."

"A little," I said.

"Thank you." He fished a flask from his coat, drank, offered it around. Ilsa took a swallow like medicine. I took one like a sacrament. It burned all the way down and lit a small stubborn lamp in my chest.

We sat in the ruined chapel while the gray brightened, three people who owed each other nothing and everything, and I thought about the courier dying in the market with his hand pressed flat against mine, passing me a weight I hadn't asked for. About Voss's face when he saw the map — not surprise. *Recognition.* About the ledger in the cellar with its columns of anchors, each one a charm I'd sold with a smile, each one quietly holding up a piece of the sky.

I was tired of being small and unnoticed. I was also, I admitted to myself, tired of being alone, and the two tirednesses had finally started pulling in different directions.

"Pip," I said. "When this is done — whatever's at the end of that road — your debt's paid. Whatever Voss holds over you, it ends. I'll see to it."

He blinked at me. "Will you. And how does a trinket-seller see to anything with Odell Voss?"

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "But I'm carrying a map to the root of the world, a knight owes me her second rescue, and I throw a decent decoy. I'm working on it."

Pip laughed — a real one, surprised out of him. Ilsa almost smiled, which for her was a religious experience. And outside, the sun came up over Corvane, gilding the rooftops of the city that wanted me dead, and somewhere behind us a bell began to ring the alarm, and somewhere ahead of us the road went west.

Ilsa stood first, rolling her shoulders like a woman taking up a burden she'd already agreed to carry.

"Rest later," she said. "Walk now. The Thornwood won't wait, and neither will whatever's following us." She paused at the chapel door, silhouetted against the gray-gold light, and added, quieter: "Because something is following us. I'd bet my name on it. What's left of it."

I touched the real map through my coat, warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat, and followed her out.
