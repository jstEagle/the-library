## 7. The Road That Remembers

Pip had three rules for the pilgrim road, and he'd broken all of them by noon.

Rule one: never travel with people who argue. Rule two: never travel with anyone the Inquisition wants badly enough to post notices about. Rule three — and this was the sacred one, carved into his bones alongside his mother's lullabies and every con he'd ever run — never, under any circumstances, travel with someone who smells like home if you don't have a home.

Wren Zhou smelled like home.

Not Corvane-home, not the wet-stone-and-tallow stink of the city he'd spent half his life slipping through. Something older. Green and dark and sweet, like the inside of a loaf that had been baked in a forest, which made no sense whatsoever, and Pip had been quietly losing his mind about it for two days.

"You're staring again," Ilsa said without turning around.

"I'm observing. It's different. Staring is rude; observing is professional."

"Observing what?"

"That," Pip said, "is an excellent question, and I'll thank you not to ask it."

Ilsa Marrow walked like a woman who expected the road to apologize to her. Broad shoulders, sword wrapped in sacking across her back because a knight's blade on the old pilgrim road drew eyes they couldn't afford, and a face that could have been carved from a church door — stern, weathered, faintly disapproving even in sleep, though Pip had yet to catch her asleep. She'd been exiled from her order ten years ago, she'd said, and had volunteered nothing since except directions and grudging silence, both of which Pip found deeply suspicious. Honest people volunteered things. He'd built a career on it.

Ahead of them, Wren walked with her hood up and her shoulders hunched, the way small people walk when they've learned that being noticed is the first step toward being dead. She kept touching the map through her coat, Pip noticed. Little pat-pat-pat against her ribs, like checking on a sleeping animal. The courier's blood had dried brown on its wax seal, and Wren hadn't let it out of arm's reach since the sewers.

The road itself didn't help his nerves. This stretch of the old pilgrim way hadn't been used properly in a generation — the stones were sunk in turf, the waymarkers worn to anonymous teeth — but it remembered being walked. Roads like this did. You could feel it under your boots, that patient, bruised memory of ten thousand feet, and most days Pip ignored it the way you ignore an old scar.

Except today the road wasn't just remembering.

Today it was *responding*.

He'd noticed it at dawn, when they'd passed a blackthorn hedge gone mad with blossom. Blackthorn flowers in early spring, harsh and white before its leaves come in. It was high summer, and this hedge was so heavy with bloom it looked like snowfall had gotten lost and decided to stay. He'd told himself it was a freak of weather. Then they'd passed a rowan doing the same. Then a bank of wild roses blooming so thickly the scent hit like a wall, and a stand of hazel dropping ripe nuts out of season, and a foxglove patch standing at attention like it had heard royalty was coming.

All of it trailing behind Wren like the wake of a boat.

*Don't think about it,* Pip told himself, for the hundredth time. *You're a smuggler. Smugglers don't think about things. Thinking about things is how smugglers end up in the river.*

But the other half of him — the half that came from his mother, the half he kept folded up small and hidden the way he kept everything valuable — that half was singing. That half knew exactly what blooming-out-of-season meant, knew it the way you know a lullaby, and it was whispering: *you know what she is, little one, you've known since the market, why do you think the charms in her stall warmed when she got close, why do you think the pigeons follow her—*

"Pip." Wren had stopped. "Is that normal?"

She was pointing at a waymarker. It was one of the old ones, a waist-high stone carved with a cup-shaped hollow at the top where pilgrims once left offerings. Except the hollow wasn't empty. It was full of water — clear, bright water, in a stone that hadn't held rain in years, if the moss was anything to go by — and floating on the water was a single white flower.

"It's an offering," Pip said carefully. "Old custom. Travelers leave gifts for safe passage."

"We haven't passed another traveler in two days."

"Mm."

"And nobody's been here to put a flower in it."

"Mm-hm."

"So who put the flower in it?"

Pip looked at the flower. The flower, being a flower, offered no comment. But the road around it — the turf, the hedges, the whole green breathing length of the pilgrim way — seemed to lean toward Wren the way a dog leans toward the person holding the bread.

"Maybe," Pip said, "the road likes you."

"The road *likes* me."

"Roads are sentimental. All that walking. It builds up."

Wren gave him the look she'd apparently developed specifically for him, the one that said *I survived Inquisitors and sewers for this.* Then she crouched, touched the water — and Pip watched, with the particular horror of a man watching a candle flame bend toward him, as the water *brightened*. Just slightly. Just enough. Like a coal breathed on.

Wren snatched her hand back. "Did you see that?"

"See what? I see nothing. I am famously unobservant."

"Pip."

"Water does that sometimes. Reflections. Light angles. Very technical."

"Pip, the water glowed when I touched it."

"Then I'd stop touching it!" His voice came out higher than intended. He coughed, lowered it, arranged himself into an expression of breezy unconcern, which fooled absolutely no one including possibly himself. "Look — it's a long road, we're all tired, tired people see glowing water. Let's make camp while there's light. There's a hollow ahead, past the third marker. Good walls, bad views, excellent drainage. A smuggler's five-star establishment."

Ilsa had said nothing through all of this, which was her way, but her eyes had gone from the waymarker to Wren and stayed there, thoughtful and heavy as millstones. Pip caught it and filed it away. The knight knew something too. Or suspected something. Everyone suspected something; that was the trouble with traveling with people instead of cargo.

* * *

The hollow was everything he'd promised — a fold in the earth walled with old fieldstone, sheltered, defensible, and currently hosting more wildflowers than Pip had seen outside of a funeral wreath shop. They carpeted the floor of the hollow. Violets, mostly, out of season by a full season, blooming in a rough circle about the size of a bedroll.

A bedroll-sized circle.

Pip stood at the edge of it and did math he very much did not want to do.

"That's new," Wren said behind him. "Was that there?"

"Nope."

"Are you sure?"

"Extremely. I scouted this hollow last spring, running goods for a man named Ferro who is now in prison, and I remember it distinctly as a dirt-floored hole with ambitions. It had no flowers. It had no *anything*. It had ambition and mud." He turned. "Wren. Where did you sleep last night?"

"Here. Why?"

"And the night before?"

"The hedgerow by the ford." Her voice had gone careful. Flat. The voice of someone assembling a picture from pieces they'd been avoiding looking at directly. "There were primroses there too. I thought — I thought it was just a good spot for primroses."

"Wren."

"What."

"Wherever you sleep, things bloom."

The silence that followed had texture. Ilsa stopped unwrapping her sword and looked up. Somewhere above the hollow, a bird started singing — some thrush, going at it like the fate of nations depended on the quality of its performance — and Pip realized with a sinking feeling that the birds had been doing that whenever Wren was near. Singing louder. Gathering closer. Two days of birdsong that had seemed charming and now seemed like a congregation.

"That's not possible," Wren said. "I'm not — I'm nobody. I sell charms. Fake charms. Well." A pause. "Allegedly fake charms. Allegedly."

"Allegedly," Pip agreed.

"I would know. If I were — whatever you're implying. Wouldn't I know?"

Wouldn't you, Pip thought. And there it was, the question underneath the question, the one he'd been circling for two days like a moth around a lamp he suspected was hot. Because he did know. Not the specifics — the specifics lived on the other side of the veil, in the country of his mother's people, and he'd spent thirty years keeping that door shut and bolted — but the shape of it. Half-bloods learned to read the signs early, or got eaten by them. Blooming out of season. Water that brightened. Birds that gathered. Bees, gods help him, bees — he'd watched a bee land on Wren's knuckle yesterday morning and just *sit there*, sunning itself like a cat, and Wren had laughed and called it friendly, and Pip had nearly walked into a ditch.

"She doesn't know," Ilsa said quietly. It wasn't a question either.

"How would I know?" Wren's voice cracked upward. "I grew up in a cellar in Corvane. The most magical thing that ever happened to me was Odell teaching me sleight of hand. I'm a charm seller. I'm a *fraud*. I have been a fraud for ten years and I would like to continue being one, because frauds don't get burned at the stake!"

Nobody said anything. The thrush sang on, oblivious, gorgeous, insufferable.

Pip sat down on a stone that wasn't blooming — he checked first — and tried to figure out how much truth a person could hand over before it became a weapon. He'd spent his whole life dealing in small truths, priced individually, refundable. This one wasn't small. This one had roots.

"Half-bloods know things," he said finally. "It's not knowledge, exactly. More like... weather sense. You grow up with one foot on each side of the veil, you learn what the other side feels like when it leans on you." He rubbed the back of his neck. "My mother was fae. River folk, the kind that live under fords and take tolls in memories. My father was a lock-smith from Corvane who thought he was courting a very unusual woman. Which, technically, he was."

Wren blinked. "You've never told us that."

"There's a lot I've never told you. We met four days ago. I owe your guildmaster money, which is a whole separate conversation, and I'd like to keep having it never." He waved a hand at the violets. "But I know what it looks like when the other side takes an interest. And Wren — the other side isn't taking an interest in you. The other side thinks you're *theirs*."

"I'm from Corvane."

"You're from a doorstep," Pip said gently. "We all are, one way or another."

That landed wrong — he saw it hit, saw her flinch — and he wished immediately he'd phrased it better, but there was no taking it back, and honestly it needed saying. Voss had told them nothing useful about where Wren came from beyond *left on the step, standard practice*, and Pip had believed it the way you believe a merchant's invoice: because checking was expensive.

Except now the road was checking for him. The road was checking enthusiastically. The road had filled an offering-cup with fresh water and left a flower in it, and roads did not do that for charm sellers from cellars.

"Here's what I know," Pip went on, counting on his fingers because counting helped him not run away. "One: wherever you sleep, things bloom out of season. Two: animals act like you're the interesting thing at the fair. Three: your allegedly fake charms actually work, which we established when that warding knot of yours kept Ashgrave's tracker-hound confused for a full minute in the sewer, and I have sold warding knots my entire career and none of mine have ever done that. Four—" he hesitated, then committed, because lying to her now felt like theft from someone already robbed, "—four, the land remembers you. That's the only way I can put it. Old roads, old stones, old hedges. They remember you like you're somebody who used to live here."

"I've never been here."

"Yeah," Pip said. "That's the part I don't like."

* * *

They ate cold supper by a fire Ilsa built small and shielded, because she was that kind of careful, and the talk thinned out after that the way talk does when everyone's thinking too loudly. Wren washed the pans — she'd claimed the chore on day one with the fierce competence of someone who'd grown up doing it, and nobody argued — and Pip lay back on his bedroll, deliberately placed outside the violet circle, and stared up at the strip of sky between the hedges.

Stars. Ordinary stars. Thank all the merciful powers for ordinary stars.

Except even the stars were conspiring, because the longer he lay there the more he noticed the hedges leaning. Not physically — probably not physically — but there was a *tension* in them, a green attention, the whole living architecture of the road bent slightly inward toward the hollow where Wren slept. Toward the girl scrubbing pans and complaining under her breath about Ilsa's scorched stew pot.

*What are you?* Pip thought at her, uselessly. *What did Voss leave on that doorstep?*

Because it occurred to him — it had been occurring to him with increasing insistence since the waymarker — that the courier hadn't chosen Wren at random. Dying men don't choose randomly. Dying men choose the nearest door, yes, but dying men who carry maps sealed in blood choose doors that will *hold*, and something in that courier had looked at a charm stall in a Corvane market and seen a keeper.

And it further occurred to him that if the covenant binding the mortal realm to the Thornwood was failing — if the Root was failing, if the woods were walking closer, if wells were going bitter in villages they hadn't reached yet — then the thing the failing covenant wanted most in all the world might be exactly the kind of creature who made hedges bloom by sleeping on them. A bridge. A hinge. A child belonging fully to neither realm and therefore able to touch both.

And things that can touch both realms are, historically, things that both realms want to *use*.

Pip had watched his mother's folk bargain. He knew what they did with hinges and bridges and doors. They didn't break them. They *walked through them*, until there was nothing left in the middle.

Across the fire, Wren finished the pans, wiped her hands, and settled into her bedroll inside the ring of violets. Within minutes her breathing slowed. Within minutes the violets — he swore it, he would swear it in any court that would have him — seemed to open a little wider, like hands relaxing.

Ilsa sat watch, sword across her knees, and caught Pip looking.

"You know what she is," the knight said. Low, under the thrush's late song.

"I know what she *isn't*," Pip said. "She isn't a charm seller."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Pip agreed. "It's a down payment. I'll tell you the rest when I understand it myself." He pulled his blanket up. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me somewhere the hedges aren't listening."

Isla's mouth did something that on a less severe face might have been a smile. "You think the hedges are listening."

"Petroc Marrow's ghost, Ilsa, look at them."

She looked. She was quiet a long moment. "When I served the covenant," she said at last, "there were stories. About anchors. Living ones. The old knights called them *keystones* — souls set into the wall of the world, holding the arch up. They were supposed to be a myth. A comfort story for young soldiers on cold nights." Her thumb moved along the wrapped hilt of her sword, slow, like touching an old wound. "The night my order fell, the Grandmaster said the keystone had been lost. Hidden. Kept somewhere safe until it was needed."

The fire popped. The violets breathed. Wren slept on, unaware, her hand curled loosely against her ribs where the map lay — pat, pat, pat, even in sleep, checking on it, or maybe it checking on her.

"And now the covenant's failing," Pip said, "and a map falls into her hands, and the road starts leaving her flowers."

"Yes," Ilsa said.

Neither of them said the rest. Neither of them had to. The rest sat between them, huge and obvious and terrible: *if she's the keystone, then everyone who wants the covenant reforged needs her — and keystones don't get asked. They get set.*

Above the hollow, the thrush finally went quiet. In the silence, far off down the remembered road, Pip heard — or thought he heard, or hoped he only thought he heard — something answering the birdsong. A voice, thin and sweet and many-throated, coming from the direction of the Thornwood.

Singing, very softly, a lullaby.

His mother's lullaby.

Pip lay very still and did not sleep, and the hedges leaned closer all night, and in the morning there were primroses growing in the shape of a door.
