## 8. Voss's Confession

The waystation had once been a pilgrim chapel, back when pilgrims were allowed to exist without paying a tithe to the Inquisition for the privilege of walking. Now it was a roof, three walls, and a hearth that smoked more than it burned, tucked into the shoulder of the old road like a beggar against a warm wall.

Ilsa built the fire with the grim efficiency of a woman who had done it ten thousand times and resented every one of them. Pip went out to check the snares and came back with one rabbit and an expression of profound betrayal, as though the rabbit had personally wronged him by being catchable.

Wren sat on the broken altar step with the courier's map unrolled across her knees, not reading it. She had been not-reading it for two hours.

"You're going to wear a hole in it," Pip said, dropping cross-legged beside her and offering her the better half of the rabbit on principle, because he was generous that way and also because she'd made him a charm against nightmares back in Corvane and he still felt vaguely indebted. "Staring at it won't make it confess."

"Everything confesses eventually," Ilsa said from across the fire. "Maps. Men. Me, if you ask nicely enough times." She did not say this like a joke. She never quite did.

It was Pip who found the letter.

Later — much later, after the rabbit was bones and the fire had settled into its evening mutter — he went digging through his coat for a twist of pipe-weed and instead produced a folded square of heavy paper, sealed with grey wax, that neither of them had ever seen before.

He turned it over twice, frowning. "This isn't mine."

"Let me see." Wren held out her hand.

The seal bore the mark of the Hollow Lantern Guild: a lantern with no flame inside it. Her stomach performed a slow, deliberate turn, like a cat settling into a spot it knows it shouldn't be in.

"That's Voss's hand," she said. The wax was already broken. Someone had opened it and resealed it badly — or perhaps the seal had simply given up, the way everything old gave up eventually. "When did you—"

"I bought a coat off a dead man's brother in Corvane," Pip said slowly. "Three days before we left. Paid too much for it, if you can believe that." He looked up at her, and for once there was nothing flippant in his face. "Wren. It's addressed to *you*."

She didn't take it. He set it in her hands anyway, because that was the kind of man he was when he remembered to be.

The paper was thick and expensive, the kind guildmasters used for contracts. The handwriting inside was Odell Voss's — she knew it the way she knew the creak of the third stair in the guildhouse, the smell of his pipe, the particular way he cleared his throat before lying to a customer about a charm's provenance. She had spent ten years learning that handwriting. Ledger entries. Inventory lists. Once, a birthday note that said only *Another year. Keep your head down. — O.V.*

This was longer.

"Read it aloud," Ilsa said quietly. She had moved closer to the fire without appearing to move at all, the way she did everything. "If it's what I think it is, you shouldn't be alone with it."

Wren almost laughed. "You don't know what it is."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "But I know what letters from old men to lost girls usually are, and none of them should be read in silence."

So Wren cleared her throat, which had gone strangely small, and began.

---

*Wren.*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead, or I have failed you so thoroughly that you have run, and in my experience the second tends to become the first within the fortnight. I have written this letter eleven times. This is the twelfth draft. The first eleven are in my hearth. I mention this not for sympathy but so you understand that what follows is the best cowardice I could manage, and even that took twelve attempts.*

*You will want to burn this. I would, in your place. But you have never been me, thank every power worth naming, and so I am going to trust you to be better than I was, which has never been a difficult standard to meet.*

*You were not abandoned.*

*I need you to sit with that sentence before I give you the rest of it, because I know what I told you. I know the story we gave you — left on the doorstep, winter night, no note, poor mite. We told it well because we told it often. Lies get strong the same way charms do: repetition, and a little warmth behind them.*

*Here is the truth, as plainly as I am able, which is not very.*

*On the ninth of Frostmonth, twenty-two years ago, a door opened in the cellar of the Hollow Lantern Guild. Not a door of wood. A door of the other kind — the kind the Inquisitors hang people for drawing pictures of. And through it came a woman carrying a bundle, and the bundle was you.*

*She was fae, Wren. Not half, not touched, not glamoured. Fae, whole and entire, though she wore a mortal shape to pass through the city above us. She came through the veil itself, which no one does, which no one *can*, except — as we learned that night — someone carrying something the veil is willing to part around.*

*She carried you the way one carries a lit candle through a dry field. Carefully. Like the world depended on it.*

*Because it did.*

*Your mother — and I use the word with all its weight, knowing I have no right to — was of the Thornwood court, of the line that tends the Worldroot. You will not know what that means yet. Ilsa Marrow might, if she is with you, and if she is not, find her, because she is the last person alive who swore an oath to what I am about to describe.*

*The covenant. The old bargain that holds the Thornwood apart from the mortal realm and holds both of them together besides. Mortals forget it exists. Fae pretend they've outgrown it. But it is the frame the house stands in, child, and for a thousand years it has been fed the only food it accepts: a soul that belongs fully to both realms, given willingly, at the appointed hour.*

*There has always been such a soul. That was the covenant's cruelty and its genius — it did not take. It waited. It let each generation believe the price was far away, or someone else's, until the hour came round and some poor bridging creature walked to the Worldroot of their own free feet.*

*Your mother saw the last of those walk. She told us — that night in our cellar, while the door stood open behind her and the lanterns burned blue — that the previous bridge had been her own sister. And that when the sister walked, something in the covenant had gone wrong, or hungry, or tired, and the veil had begun to fray at the edges like old cloth.*

*So she did the arithmetic no mother should ever have to do. She looked at her infant daughter — born of the Thornwood's blood, fathered by a mortal man from the border villages, belonging wholly to neither realm and therefore wholly to both — and she understood what you were. What you were *for*.*

*A living bridge. Insurance, if you like. A coin saved against a debt not yet due.*

*She could have kept you hidden in the Thornwood. She said the court would have raised you sweetly and lied to you prettily and delivered you to the Root at the proper hour with flowers in your hair. Instead she brought you to us. To a guild of trinket-sellers and liars, in a city where your nature is a hanging offense, because — she said — mortals lie badly but they lie *small*, and she wanted you raised among small lies rather than beautiful ones.*

*She wanted you to have a childhood before you had a purpose. She paid for it. Oh, how she paid for it — the toll of passing through the veil with you nearly unmade her; I watched it take the color from her eyes and leave silver there instead. And then she went back through, and the door closed, and that was the last any of us saw of her.*

*We named you Wren because she asked us to. She said the wren builds her nest in the thornbush, where no hawk will follow. I have thought about that sentence for twenty-two years and I still don't know whether it was a blessing or an instruction manual.*

*Now the part that will hurt, and I will not dress it up because you have earned better than dressing-up.*

*We kept you, Wren, because keeping you was useful. Every charm this guild ever sold — every luck-knot and hearth-warmth and traveler's-blessing — was an anchor point, a thread in the great net that holds the veil taut. Your presence in Corvane, your magic sleeping under your skin, your simple *existence* on the mortal side of the line — it strengthened every thread within a hundred miles. The guild did not raise a daughter. The guild maintained an asset, and called it a family because the calling made the work easier to sleep beside.*

*I tell myself we loved you anyway. Some nights I believe it. Your room was cold one winter and I carried the stove-coals up myself, three flights, burning my hands through the cloth, and I remember thinking: this is not maintenance. This is just a man who cannot bear for the girl to shiver.*

*But then I remember the ledger, and the columns, and the entry from your seventh year where I recorded — in my own hand, in my own ledger — a notation of your magic's growth alongside the market value of the anchors it sustained. As though you were stock. As though you were grain in a cellar.*

*I burned that page. I want you to know I burned it. And I want you to know that burning it changed nothing, because the page was not the sin. The sin was writing it.*

*The anchors are dying now. You may have seen it — the courier who found you, if he found you, was carrying word of it. The threads are going dark one by one, from the borders inward, and the veil is sagging like a wet rope, and things are coming through the gaps that were never meant to see daylight. The covenant is failing because the bridges are gone — all of them but you.*

*They will come for you. The Inquisition, because you are what they say you are. The Thornwood court, because you are what they need you to be. And the covenant itself, patient as winter, because you are the coin it has been waiting twenty-two years to spend.*

*I taught you to be small, Wren. I taught you to hide the warmth in your hands and keep your head down and survive by being unnoticed. I told myself it was protection. It was also, I see now, training. I was fattening the calf without ever saying the word slaughter aloud, and hating myself in a way I mistook for virtue, because hatred is so much cheaper than action.*

*There is a road east of Corvane, the old pilgrim road, and along it a waystation chapel with a crooked chimney. If you are reading this there, then my messenger did his work, and I am glad, and I am sorry, and I do not expect those two things to fit together in your heart. They do not fit together in mine.*

*Go to the Worldroot. See it with your own eyes before anyone tells you what it wants. And then — and here is the only request I will make of you, the only one I have any right to — decide for yourself. Not as the guild's asset. Not as your mother's insurance. Not as the covenant's coin.*

*As Wren. Whoever that turns out to be. I genuinely do not know, and for once in my life, the not-knowing feels less like failure and more like hope.*

*The stove-coals are cold now. The guildhouse is quiet. I keep setting out two cups at breakfast out of habit and drinking both, which Pip would tell you is the saddest magic in the world, and he'd be right.*

*Keep your head down, I used to say. I take it back. Hold your head up. Whatever you choose at the Root — hold it up while you choose.*

*With a love I have no right to and refuse to return,*

*O.V.*

*P.S. — The third stair creaks. I never fixed it because you laughed at it when you were four. I am telling you this so that at least one thing in your childhood was real, and it was.*

---

The fire had burned down to a red murmur by the time she finished. Nobody spoke. Somewhere outside, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which seemed about right.

Wren became aware, distantly, that tears had been falling onto the letter for some time, and that the expensive paper was drinking them in without complaint, the way it had probably drunk ink and candle-grease and Voss's twelve drafts of cowardice.

Her whole childhood rearranged itself behind her eyes. Not shattered — worse than shattered. *Re-sorted.* Every kindness re-filed under *maintenance*. Every scolding under *asset management*. The stove-coals. The unfixed stair. The birthday notes. Which ones were love, and which ones were upkeep? Was there a difference? Had there ever been?

"A custody arrangement," she said finally. Her voice came out flat and strange. "That's what I was. Ten years of — of *being raised*, and it was a custody arrangement with a kill clause."

"Wren—" Pip started.

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Not yet. I'm — give me a moment to be angry at him before you make me feel sorry for him. He doesn't get to skip the queue."

Pip closed his mouth. Wise, for once.

It was Ilsa who spoke, after the silence had gone on long enough to ache. She was staring into the fire, and her face had the look of a woman hearing her own confession read in someone else's voice.

"The line that tends the Worldroot," she said quietly. "He wrote that. Your mother's line." Her jaw worked. "I knew a guardian of the Root, ten years ago. The last one. When the order came down to let her die, I thought the covenant would simply... find another. They always had before." She looked up, and her eyes found Wren's with terrible precision. "They did find another. They just had to wait twenty-two years for her to grow."

The wind moved through the broken chapel, and the fire guttered, and far off to the east — too far to see, close enough to feel — something old and patient shifted in its sleep beneath the roots of the world.

Wren folded the letter along its worn creases, very carefully, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where the charms used to sit warm.

"We walk at dawn," she said. "And Voss is right about one thing. Whatever's waiting at that Root gets *me*. Not the guild's asset. Not anybody's coin."

She looked at the dark eastern sky, where the Thornwood waited, and her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"*Me.* And it had better be ready for that."

* * *

Behind them, unseen in the deepening dark, a rider on the old road paused at the edge of the waystation's firelight — a tall figure in a grey coat, whose shadow fell the wrong way — and did not approach.

Not yet.
