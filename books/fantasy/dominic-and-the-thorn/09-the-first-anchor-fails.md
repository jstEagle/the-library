## 9. The First Anchor Fails

The well at Millbrook had been dug four hundred years ago by a woman named Aldith Crane, who — according to the carving on the coping stone — had bargained with something in the earth to make the water sweet. Nobody in Millbrook thought much about that bargain. That was rather the point of it.

Tam Osgood was the first to notice, on a grey morning that smelled of rain that never came. He lowered his bucket the way he had every morning for thirty years, and when he drew it up and drank from his cupped hand, he spat it out so fast he nearly went over the edge himself.

"Bile," he said. "The well's gone to *bile*."

His wife tasted it. His neighbor tasted it. The miller's boy, who would drink anything, tasted it and made a face like a folded sock. By noon half the village had stood at the well's mouth peering down into the dark as if the water might explain itself, and by evening they had all arrived at the same uneasy conclusion: the water wasn't spoiled. Spoiled water smelled of rot. This smelled of *grief* — if grief could be said to have a smell, which apparently it could, and the smell was iron and old leaves and something faintly sweet underneath, like flowers left too long on a grave.

Old Marta Crane — no relation to Aldith that anyone could prove, though she claimed otherwise whenever it got her free bread — put her ear to the well stone and listened for a long while.

"It's not the water that's sick," she announced. "It's whatever was holding the water."

"Which is what?" Tam demanded.

Marta looked east, toward the treeline, where the Thornwood stood on its ancient horizon like a wall that had learned patience.

"I don't know," she said. "But I think it just died."

* * *

Two days' ride south, in the wheat country around Harrow Fen, the crops began to blacken.

Not all at once. That was the horror of it. A field would be gold at dawn and by dusk there'd be a patch of grey-black stalks in the middle of it, shaped like nothing at all, or like something — the shape changed depending on who looked, which nobody wanted to discuss. The blight didn't spread the way blight spreads. It appeared where it pleased, here, then there, then back again, like a stain soaking through cloth from the far side.

Dagny Holt walked her family's fields with a lantern on the third night and found the black stalks warm. Not sun-warm. *Hearth*-warm, the way a sleeping dog is warm, and when she knelt and pressed her palm flat against the soil she felt — or dreamed she felt, and would spend the rest of her life deciding which — a slow pulse beneath the earth. One beat. Then another. Then a long, long pause, like a heart forgetting what it was for.

She ran home and told her father, and her father told the headman, and the headman sent a rider to the provincial garrison, and the garrison sent back a form and a pencil and instructions to report further instances of *agricultural irregularity* in triplicate.

Dagny's little brother, who was six and had not yet learned which thoughts were safe to say aloud, asked at supper: "Is the world dying?"

"Hush," said their mother.

But everyone at the table had been thinking it, and nobody corrected him, and the silence where the correction should have been was the loudest thing in the house.

* * *

On the pilgrim road, three days east of Corvane, Wren Zhou woke to find the hedge blooming over her head.

This had been happening for two days now, and Pip had stopped pretending it was normal, which for Pip was a significant concession.

"Morning," he said, crouched beside her bedroll with a wild rose stem in his fingers, turning it over like a jeweler assessing a forgery. "You should know you've done this." He gestured upward. The hedge above them — bare, thorned, aggressively dormant when they'd made camp — was covered in white blossom. Out of season. Out of reason. "Third night running. You're a one-woman spring festival."

Wren sat up too fast and paid for it with a headache. "I don't *do* anything. I sleep. Sleeping isn't magic."

"Tell that to the hedge." Pip let the rose stem fall. He'd been quieter since the letter — Voss's letter, the one that had turned Wren's whole childhood inside out like a pocket — and his quiet had a watchful quality, the way a man watches a river he's about to have to swim. "In the Hollows we had a word for things that made green grow where green shouldn't. We didn't use it near them, either."

"Pip."

"I'm not saying anything. I'm saying a word exists." He stood, brushing off his knees, and raised his voice toward the horses. "Ilsa! Your apprentice is photosynthesizing!"

"I'm not his apprentice," Wren told the hedge. The hedge, being a hedge, declined to confirm or deny.

Ser Ilsa Marrow came around the cart leading both animals, and as always her first act upon waking-adjacent existence was to scan the road behind them. Old habit, Wren guessed. A knight without a banner checking for banners. There were none — just the long brown road unspooling west toward Corvane and everything they'd fled — but Ilsa kept checking anyway, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

"East," she said instead of good morning, and pointed.

They all looked. The Thornwood was visible from here even in daylight, a blue-green darkness lying along the horizon, and Wren had been telling herself for three days that it looked exactly as it always had.

It did not look exactly as it always had.

It was closer.

Not dramatically — not a forest lunging across the countryside, whatever the ballads might do with such an image — but closer the way a tide is closer between one glance and the next. The treeline that had sat a hard day's ride off now sat perhaps half that. Between the last farms and the trees lay a strip of meadow that Wren would have sworn, on their first morning out of Corvane, had been wider.

"The woods are walking closer," Pip said. He said it lightly, the way he said most true things, which was how Wren had learned to hear the weight under his voice. "Refugees coming up the road yesterday were saying the same. Whole villages from the fen country. They think the forest's invading." He shook his head slowly. "It's not invading. It's *leaning*. Like a drunk against a door. The thing holding it upright is going, and it's starting to list."

"The covenant," Wren said.

"The covenant." Ilsa swung up into the saddle in one motion, armor clinking, a sound Wren had come to think of as the group's heartbeat. "Then we stop talking about it and start riding toward it. Every hour we spend admiring the problem, the problem gets bigger. Literally." She looked down at Wren, and her expression did the thing it sometimes did — the thing where the exiled knight fell away and something older and wearier showed through, something that had once sworn oaths to the very thing they were riding to save. "Eat while you walk. Both of you. If the world's ending, I refuse to let it catch us hungry."

"That's the most inspiring thing anyone's ever said to me," said Pip.

"I have others."

* * *

They reached the crossroads shrine at midafternoon, and that was where the refugees found them.

There were dozens of them, maybe a hundred, spread along the verges — families with carts, farmers with sacks, a whole apiary's worth of beehives lashed to a wagon driven by a woman with smoke-stained hands. They were heading west, away from the Thornwood, which meant they were flowing past the trio like a river past three stones.

An old man with a bandaged arm stopped at their fire that evening, drawn by the smell of Pip's barley stew, and stayed to talk because Pip had the kind of face people told things to.

"Fen country?" Ilsa asked him.

"Aye. And before that the river villages." The old man held out his bowl for more, and Pip gave it, because Pip grumbled about sharing food and then always shared it. "Well went bitter at Millbrook first. Then the crops. Then—" He paused, and lowered his voice, and glanced at the eastern dark as if it might be listening. "Then the other thing."

"What other thing?" Wren asked.

The old man looked at her — really looked, for the first time, and something flickered across his face that Wren had seen before on the faces of people meeting her, some small animal recognition they never explained. "The hedges," he said. "The standing stones. The old boundary markers. They're *moving*. Not walking about, nothing like your fairy stories. But a marker my grandfather set on our north field — granite, two tons if it was a pound — we found it forty yards south. No tracks. No hole where it had stood. Just moved, like a chess piece, when nobody was looking." He shivered. "Everything old is shifting. Everything that used to sit still and mind the lines. As if the lines themselves are going slack."

Wren's hand had gone, without her permission, to her coat, to the letter over her heart.

*Every charm sold was an anchor,* Voss had written. *And the anchors are dying, because the thing they anchor to is dying, and I am sorry, child, for every year I let you believe you were only a girl.*

"How many of you are there?" Ilsa asked the old man. "Heading west?"

"More every day. And more behind us." He ate a moment in silence. "Here's the thing that keeps me up nights, knight. We're all running from the woods. But my wife's cousin lives out past Dominic's Ford, on the far side, and she says the fae folk are leaving the deep forest too. Coming west. Coming *out*." He shook his head. "Both peoples running from the middle. Like rats—" He caught himself, embarrassed. "Beg pardon. Like everyone's leaving the same burning house, only nobody can see the fire."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightjar called, and Wren noticed — filed away, uneasily — that it called in a rhythm almost like speech.

Pip broke the quiet, because Pip always did. "So what happens," he said, "when the people running west meet the fae running west? On roads this narrow, with wells going bitter and tempers going shorter?" He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Everyone at the fire finished it privately, in their own heads, and nobody liked the answer.

"We get there first," Wren said quietly. "That's what happens next. We get to the Worldroot before the middle burns through."

The old man blinked at her. "Worldroot? Girl, that's a story for children."

"So's a hedge that blooms overnight," said Pip. "We live in interesting times."

* * *

Wren took first watch, because she'd stopped sleeping properly anyway.

She sat with her back against the cart wheel and watched the refugee fires strung along the road like a fallen constellation, and she thought about anchors.

Her whole life she'd sold charms — luck-knots, hearth-blessings, traveler's pieces — and told herself they were trinkets, harmless brass and thread, the guild's respectable cover business. Ten years of believing she was small. A shopkeeper of nothing. And all the while, every charm had been a stitch in a garment vast enough to hold two worlds together, and the garment was unraveling, and the unraveling had a name, and the name was hers.

A shape came out of the dark between the fires.

Wren was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, a charm — a real one, warmth crawling up her wrist — clenched in her fist, and then the shape resolved into a child, a girl of six or seven in an oversized coat, dragging a blanket, staring at Wren with enormous solemn eyes.

"You're the flower girl," the child said.

"...What?"

"The hedges bloom where you sleep. Mam saw. She says you're bad news or good news and she can't decide which." The girl considered Wren with the frank appraisal only children and cats can manage. "I think you're good news. But mam says the woods are eating the sky, so."

"The woods aren't eating the sky."

"They're eating *something*." The girl hugged her blanket. "My da says the old lady in our village talked to the well before it went bitter. She said the well was crying. Wells can't cry." A pause. "Can they?"

Wren crouched down to the child's level. Behind her she heard Ilsa shift, awake and listening, and pretend not to be.

"No," Wren said gently. "Wells can't cry."

"Then why is everything sad?" the girl asked. "The grown-ups won't say. They just pack faster."

And there it was — the whole collapsing covenant distilled to a child's question, asked on a dark road a hundred miles from anywhere. Why is everything sad. Because something ancient is dying, Wren thought. Because the bargain that held the sadness back is failing. Because the price of the bargain was always someone, and the someones have run out, and the only person left who belongs to both sides of the veil is sitting right here failing to answer a six-year-old.

"Because something old is hurt," Wren said at last. "And we're going to help it. That's why we're going the wrong way. Somebody has to."

The girl studied her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and produced, with great ceremony, a button — horn, worn smooth, clearly prized — and pressed it into Wren's palm.

"For luck," she said. "It works better than buying it."

She padded back into the dark between the fires, and Wren sat back down against the wheel and closed her hand around the button, and found she couldn't speak for a moment.

"Word travels fast among refugees," Ilsa murmured from her bedroll, not opening her eyes. "By tomorrow half this road will know the flower girl's face. Including, possibly, anyone riding behind us with a grey coat and a shadow that falls wrong."

"I know."

"And you gave her hope anyway."

"She gave *me* a button," Wren said. "I'm just the middleman."

Ilsa huffed — the closest thing to a laugh she issued before full daylight — and rolled over. But a minute later her voice came again, low, stripped of armor:

"When I was sworn to the covenant, thirty years ago, this is what I imagined it protected. Fires on a road. Children asleep. I never saw it, back then. I saw treaties and borders and the Root as a duty." A pause. "I see it now. Funny, what it takes."

Wren looked east. The Thornwood lay against the stars, closer than yesterday, a darkness leaning on the world. Tomorrow they would reach the last farmsteads. The day after, the veil itself — the place where the air went strange and the paths forgot where they led, where her magic, her terrible inheritance, was waiting to wake.

Somewhere behind them, she was nearly certain, a tall figure in a grey coat rode the same road, patient as winter.

Somewhere ahead, something old was crying into its well.

Wren closed her fist around the button until the horn bit warm into her palm, and made herself a promise with no witnesses but the blooming hedge:

*I'm coming. Hold on.*

Out in the dark, very softly, the nightjar called again — and this time, unmistakably, it called her name.
