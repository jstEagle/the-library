## 10. Into the Thornwood

The veil did not look like anything. That was the first disappointment.

Wren had spent three days on the road imagining it — a shimmering wall, a door of light, at the very least a dramatic arch of standing stones with some carved warning in a dead language. Instead, Ilsa stopped at a mossy boulder shaped rather like a sleeping pig and said, "Here," in the tone of someone announcing a rest stop.

"Here," Wren repeated.

"Here." Ilsa set down her pack and rolled her shoulders. "The veil runs along the old pilgrim road for about half a mile. Any point will do. It's thinner where people used to cross often. Pilgrims, mostly, before the covenant went quiet."

"Before it went *quiet*," Pip said, "or before your lot started hanging anyone who so much as sneezed sideways?"

"Both are true," Ilsa said, "and both are somebody's fault, and we can litigate it on the other side."

Wren knelt and pressed her palm flat against the moss. The stone was cold, ordinary, utterly unremarkable — and beneath that, so faint she might have invented it, something pulsed. Like pressing an ear to a door and hearing, very far away, someone breathing.

"It's warm," she said.

"That'll be you," said Pip. He was crouched nearby, turning his hat in his hands — he'd been doing that all morning, which for Pip meant nerves, since Pip treated his hat the way other men treated their mothers. "Been getting warmer every mile, if you ask me. Which nobody does, but I'm going to keep saying it."

"You've been saying it since the waystation."

"And I'll say it until one of us admits what it means." He jammed the hat back on. "You're not carrying a map into that forest, love. You're carrying yourself. There's a difference, and it's the sort of difference that gets people eaten."

Ilsa gave him a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces. "If you'd rather stay—"

"I'd rather complain. It's different from leaving." But his voice had lost its flippancy, and when he glanced at the boulder again, Wren saw him do something she'd never seen him do: he touched two fingers briefly to his own chest, the way sailors touch iron. A warding gesture. An old one.

"Pip," she said quietly. "What happens on the other side?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. For once in their entire acquaintance, Pip had nothing to sell.

"The paths move," Ilsa answered instead. She was checking her sword belt with the methodical care of a woman who expected to die and wanted her affairs in order first. "Time pools in the low places. You can walk a day and come out an hour behind yourself, or walk an hour and come out ten years late. The wood decides what you need more than you do." She paused. "It decided things about me, once. We didn't part friends."

"You were its guard," Wren said. "Ser Ilsa Marrow, Shield of the Covenant. They don't give titles like that to people the forest dislikes."

"They gave it to me because my family had held the border for six generations, and because I was obedient, and those turned out to be the same qualification." Ilsa's jaw tightened. She looked at the boulder the way people look at graves. "Enough. When we cross, stay within arm's reach of each other. Don't eat anything offered. Don't answer if something calls your name in a voice you know — especially not a voice you know. And Wren."

"Yes."

"When your magic wakes — and it's going to wake, the moment you're through — don't fight it and don't chase it. Just breathe. Can you do that? Breathe?"

"I've had practice," Wren said. "Being small and unnoticed is mostly breathing exercises."

Something almost like a smile crossed Ilsa's face and died there. "On three."

There was no three. There wasn't even a two. Wren stepped around the boulder expecting a wall and found — grass. The same grey road-grass, the same overcast Corvane sky pressing down like a lid.

And then the lid came off.

She felt it happen rather than saw it: a seam splitting along the world, quiet as paper tearing. One step she stood under Corvane's smoky ceiling of cloud; the next, light poured through her as though she'd walked out of a cellar into noon. The air changed taste — green, resinous, sweet with something flowering somewhere out of season. The silence changed too; it wasn't empty anymore but *occupied*, the hush of a room full of people holding their breath.

And inside her chest, something that had slept her whole life sat up and screamed awake.

Wren dropped to her knees.

It wasn't pain exactly, or it was pain the way birth is pain — everything too much and too bright and arriving all at once. Every charm she had ever made, every trinket she'd sold for coppers with a wink and a lie, flared in her memory and burned. Because they hadn't been lies. Not one of them. Ten years of harmless little charms, and every single one had been *true*, and she had carried that truth in her hands daily without ever feeling it, the way you carry a coal wrapped in so many rags you forget it's hot.

Now the rags were off.

Light ran through her veins. She could feel the road beneath her thinking. She could feel the trees — not nearby trees, but trees miles deep in the green dark, each one a slow burning lamp — and every one of them turned toward her at once, the way a crowd turns toward a door when someone familiar walks in.

"*Oh,*" she heard herself say, and it came out wrong, layered, her voice and something older underneath it saying the same word.

"Wren." Ilsa's hand on her shoulder, solid, human, callused. "Breathe. In through the nose."

"In through the nose," Wren gasped.

"Out through the mouth. Again."

Pip had gone very still and very pale, which on Pip's complexion meant he'd gone slightly greenish. He was staring at her the way he stared at uncut gems — hunger and fear fighting for the same chair. "Her eyes," he said. "Ilsa. Her eyes have gone — there's no white left. It's all — like sunlight through honey."

"Don't describe it," Ilsa said sharply. "Naming draws attention. Help me up."

Between them they got Wren to her feet, and only then did she actually *see* the Thornwood.

Words failed her the way words fail in dreams, but she was a charm-seller and a liar by trade, so she tried anyway. The trees were vast — not merely tall but vast, the way cathedrals are vast, built out of centuries instead of stone. Their trunks were silver-grey and ribbed, and their leaves caught the light and held it, so that the whole forest seemed lit from within, a green lantern the size of the sky. Moss grew in cushions deep enough to swallow a boot. Flowers bloomed along the path in ranks — autumn flowers and spring flowers side by side, snowdrops nodding next to marigolds, the seasons arguing politely about whose turn it was.

And everywhere, everywhere, the sense of being *known*. Of walking into a room where her name had been spoken a thousand times before she arrived.

"It's beautiful," she whispered.

"Aye," said Pip, in the voice of a man agreeing that the sea is wet. "Beautiful things here bite. Rule of thumb: if it's prettier than you, don't touch it."

"That rules out most of the forest and all of you," Ilsa said, and moved them forward.

* * *

They walked, and the walking was strange.

Wren noticed it first as a rhythm problem. Her steps kept landing slightly wrong — not stumbling, just *off*, as if the ground were arriving a heartbeat early or late. Then she realized the light was doing it too. Sunlight slanted through the canopy, and between one step and the next the angle shifted by hours, golden afternoon to long amber evening and back again, like a lantern-flame guttering through a day.

"Time pools," Ilsa had said. Now Wren understood. Time didn't flow here; it *puddled*. They waded through patches of afternoon, forded an evening, climbed out onto a dry bank of eternal morning. Once they passed through a stretch where frost silvered the bracken and their breath smoked, fifty paces of winter wedged between two summers, and Pip walked through it with his arms crossed and an expression of profound personal grievance.

"I hate this place," he announced, stepping back into warmth. "No offense intended toward any listening vegetation, but I hate it. Nothing keeps. You buy a round of drinks in there—" he jerked his chin at a hollow where dusk pooled like spilled wine, "—and come out owing money you spent twenty years ago."

"You've been in before," Wren said.

"Everyone in my line has been in before. Question is how much of you comes back out." He glanced at her sidelong. "You're taking this remarkably well, for a woman who fainted an hour ago."

"I didn't faint. I knelt dramatically."

"You wept, is what you did."

"I had something in my eye. Several somethings. All of them magic."

But the truth was stranger than either fainting or weeping, and Wren was beginning to suspect she couldn't hide it even from herself, let alone from these two. Because the forest wasn't frightening her. That was the thing. It should have been — everything Ilsa warned of, everything Pip feared — and instead, with every mile, a feeling was opening in her chest like a fist unclenching. A feeling she had no honest name for except one word she refused to say aloud, because saying it would make it real, and real things could be taken away.

The word was *home*.

She had never been here. She had grown up in a cellar in Corvane smelling of tallow and mildew, learning to smile at strangers and count coins in the dark. This forest owed her nothing and she owed it less.

So why did the sight of a moss-covered waystone, carved with a symbol she somehow knew meant *rest and be welcome*, make her throat close up? Why did she know, without being told, that the stream to their left ran backward on Thursdays? Why did the smell of the blue flowers — some nameless bell-shaped thing glowing faintly at dusk — hit her with grief, actual grief, homesickness sharp as a stitch pulled from a wound?

For a home she had never seen.

"Stop," she said.

They stopped. Ilsa's hand went to her sword; Pip's went to his knife. Old habits, both.

"I know that flower," Wren said. Her voice sounded strange to her. "I've never seen it and I know it. It grows over doors on the far side. It means — it means *come back safe*." She stared at the small glowing bells swaying in the windless dark. "How do I know that?"

Neither of them answered. The silence stretched, and in it Wren heard — not with her ears, with whatever new sense had cracked open in her chest — the forest listening. Patiently. Fondly. The way a parent listens outside a child's door, waiting for them to stop crying so it can pretend not to have heard.

"We should camp," Ilsa said at last, gently, which was unlike her. "You've been bleeding magic since the crossing. It needs grounding or it'll ground itself."

"What does that mean?"

"Last time someone crossed who woke up like you did," Pip said, "the next morning every bird within a mile was singing her name. Took the court a week to decide whether that was flattering or an omen. They settled on both."

*The court.* The words landed in Wren's stomach like stones. Chapter eleven of her life, waiting ahead of them in the deep green, and everyone in this forest apparently already knew her lines.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a fallen giant — a tree so broad that even lying dead it sheltered them like a hall, its roots rearing overhead in wooden arches. Ilsa built a fire with the brisk competence of a woman performing surgery, and Pip produced supper from his coat with the same professional pride: hard bread, hard cheese, dried apples, and a bottle of something he claimed was elderflower wine and Wren suspected was regret fermented in a boot.

"To crossing over," Pip said, raising the bottle.

"To not dying yet," Ilsa amended, and drank.

Wren took a swallow. It tasted like summer, which was suspicious, and warmed her like a lie, which was familiar.

Night came strangely here — not falling but *settling*, like dust. The glowing flowers brightened. Somewhere far off, something sang: three notes, rising, patient. Not threatening. Inviting. Everything here was inviting. That was the horror and the comfort of it, wound together.

She sat with her knees drawn up and watched the fire and felt her magic pool in her chest like the time pooled in the hollows — slow, deep, learning her shape. And gradually, as the others drowsed, she let herself look at the truth she'd been refusing all day.

She belonged here.

Not the way Pip belonged, half-and-half, welcome nowhere fully. Not the way Ilsa belonged, bound by oath and exile. She belonged the way a river belongs to its bed. Something in her had been *made* here, or made *for* here, and had been sent away so young it had forgotten its own shape — and now, standing in its bed again after ten years in the wrong country, it remembered.

Homesickness, she thought, was the wrong word. Homesickness was missing a place. What she felt was the place missing *her* — a pull so gentle and so vast it made her bones ache. The forest had waited for her. It had kept her seat.

"Can't sleep either?" Pip's voice, soft, from across the fire.

"No."

He scooted closer, and for once didn't joke. He studied her face in the firelight with those mismatched fae eyes — one brown, one the exact green of the canopy — and whatever he saw made him sigh.

"You feel it," he said. Not a question. "The pull."

"Is it always like this? Coming back?"

"For fae-born, coming home? No." He poked the fire. Sparks rose and hung, unwilling to fall, tiny orange stars. "For us it's like going back to a house you were thrown out of. Doors slammed, locks changed, the dog remembers you wrong. What you've got—" he shook his head slowly, "—what you've got, I've never seen. That's not a house wanting you back, love. That's a house that never stopped setting your place at the table."

The words went through her like cold water. "Then why was I sent away?"

"Now *that*," Pip said, "is the right question. And I'll tell you what I know, which isn't much, and most of it I'd charge for under normal circumstances." He glanced at Ilsa, asleep sitting up against the root-arch with her sword across her knees even now — a woman who trusted nothing, not even sleep, not even peace. He lowered his voice anyway.

"Courts talk. Smugglers listen. Ten years ago — maybe eleven, I was young, everything was further away then — there was a night the veil *tore*. Proper tear, not a thin patch. Both sides panicked. And in the middle of the panic, a package came through. Small package. Screaming package."

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "Me."

"An infant, delivered through a torn veil, straight into mortal lands, on the worst night either realm had seen in a century." Pip met her eyes. "Babies don't tear veils, Wren. Babies don't cross alone. Somebody put you through. Somebody on *this* side sent you out — fast, secret, scared. And everybody who might know why has spent ten years making sure nobody asks."

The fire crackled. The three-note song came again from the deep woods, nearer now, or perhaps the same distance and simply more insistent. Above them, through the root-arches, unfamiliar stars wheeled in patterns Wren's new-woken blood read like letters.

"Tomorrow," Ilsa said quietly, without opening her eyes. She hadn't been asleep at all. "Tomorrow we reach the court of brambles. And they will know why you were sent away, Wren. They've known all along." Her eyes opened, grey and grim. "The question is whether knowing will help us — or whether that's exactly what they want. Courts don't give answers for free. Remember that tomorrow, whatever they offer you. Whatever they *welcome* you with."

From the darkness beyond the firelight, softly, in a voice like leaves settling:

"*Wren.*"

Her own name — but not in her mother's voice, or Voss's, or anyone she knew. In a voice older than all of them, warm as a hearth and vast as weather.

"Especially that," Ilsa said, and drew her sword.
