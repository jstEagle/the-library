## 11. Court of Brambles

I've been robbed by dukes, cheated by archbishops, and once swindled out of my boots by a grandmother who then fed me soup, so believe me when I say I know a bad bargain when it smiles at me.

The trouble was that this one wasn't smiling. Not yet.

We'd been walking in the Thornwood for — well, that's the thing about the Thornwood, you can't say *for how long*, because time in there pools like rainwater in hoofprints. Sometimes deep, sometimes gone by morning. My half of the blood remembers it, and my half still flinches. The paths rearranged themselves whenever Wren stopped paying attention to them, which was constantly, because she kept stopping to stare at trees like they were letters from home she'd never gotten. Ilsa walked behind us with her hand on her sword hilt the whole way, which in fae country is roughly the equivalent of walking through a dinner party announcing you hate everyone's cooking.

Then the trees opened, all at once, like a curtain drawn by hands we couldn't see, and there it was.

The Court of Brambles doesn't sit *in* the forest so much as the forest agrees to be its walls. Thorn hedges rose higher than Corvane's cathedral spires, woven into arches and galleries and stairways that went up into green shadow, and everywhere — everywhere — there were lights. Little drifting motes of gold and blue-green, some of them moths, some of them definitely not moths. Music came from somewhere above us, played on instruments I couldn't name, in a rhythm that made my heart want to keep their time instead of its own. That's the first trick, if you're wondering. Don't let your heart take requests.

And down the long aisle of thorns, between rows of the Fair Folk come to look at us, walked a lady in a gown of living bramble, roses opening along her sleeves as she moved.

"Ah," I said, under my breath. "Company."

"Is that—" Ilsa started.

"The Bramble Queen herself, or one of her sisters, or her herald wearing her face, or possibly a very committed hedge." I spread my hands. "In my experience it's best not to ask questions you're not prepared to have answered literally."

Wren wasn't listening to either of us. She was staring at the court the way a starving woman stares through a bakery window, and I felt something cold settle behind my ribs, because I'd seen that look before — on my own face, in mirrors, forty years ago, when I was small enough and foolish enough to think the other side of my blood was a door instead of a debt.

*She's going to want it,* I thought. *Whatever they offer, she's going to want it.*

And they were going to offer.

* * *

The herald-queen-or-hedge stopped ten paces away, which is polite, because closer than ten paces and the Fair Folk start counting your breaths as gifts. Her face was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say it had no interest whatsoever in being kind.

"Wren Zhou," she said, and her voice was leaves moving. "Daughter of two doors. The wood has been singing your name since you crossed the veil."

"It has?" Wren said. And oh, that was the thing about Wren — she had no defenses against sincerity. Ten years of selling fake luck in Corvane had taught her every kind of lie a human could tell, but nobody had ever taught her what to do when something vast and ancient said her name like it meant it.

"The paths bent toward you while you slept," the queen said. "The hedges bloomed out of season. The river ran sweet for a day and a night." She tilted her head, and roses shivered along her shoulders. "You are welcome here, child of both realms. More than welcome. You are *looked for*."

Beside me, Ilsa made a sound low in her throat, the sound of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"I'm looking for someone too," Wren said, and her voice only shook a little, which I counted as heroism. "The Worldroot. Its guardian. We need to reforge the covenant before it fails completely. Before—" She gestured vaguely eastward, back toward the mortal lands where wells were going bitter and crops were blackening. "Before everything on the other side comes apart."

"Mm," said the queen.

That single syllable contained more condescension than an entire season of Corvane tax collectors, and I found myself warming to her despite everything, which is another trick, and also don't fall for it.

"You would reforge the covenant," the queen said. "The old chain. The old leash." Something moved behind her beauty then — old anger, banked but alive, the kind that keeps a hearth warm for centuries. "My people remember how it was forged. We remember what was traded away to make it. But yes. If it must be reforged, we will help. Our strength against yours, our knowledge against your ignorance. The court will stand beside you at the Worldroot."

Wren actually smiled. Actually took half a step forward. And I watched Ilsa's hand tighten on her sword until the knuckles went white as bone.

"There is one matter," the queen said, "to be settled first."

And there it was.

"A bridge must choose which shore it stands on." The queen's gaze settled on Wren, gentle as frost forming. "You belong to both realms, and so you are claimed by neither, and so the covenant cannot anchor in you — not as you are. But that is mended easily enough. Renounce your mortal ties. Your human blood, your human grief, your little city and its smoke and its hanging magistrates. Give them up freely, wholly, and the court will claim you, and through you we will remake the covenant, and both worlds will be saved."

The music overhead had gone quiet. Every light in the court had drifted lower, closer, a thousand small lanterns leaning in to hear the answer.

"Renounce," Wren repeated.

"Your mother's side of you," the queen said softly. "It is only half a life, child. You would lose so little. And gain everything."

* * *

Now. Here's what nobody tells you about being half-fae: you spend your whole childhood waiting for exactly this conversation. Some night, some glade, some beautiful voice explaining that you could be whole, complete, *chosen* — if you'd just agree to amputate the half of yourself that hurts.

My mother was human. A fisherman's daughter from the coast, laughed too loud, sang worse, died when I was nine, and I loved her the way you love a fire in winter — desperately and knowing it wouldn't last. When the fair folk finally came to make me the offer, when I was nineteen and hungry and tired of being too strange for the docks and too strange for the hollow hills, I asked them one question: "Will I remember her?"

They said memory fades, of course. Grief is heavy, and the Fair Folk have no use for luggage.

So I told them no. I've regretted it maybe twice. Both times on birthdays.

I looked at Wren now and saw the arithmetic happening behind her eyes, and I knew — I *knew* — that if she answered alone, in this moment, with the lights leaning in and the queen's voice like honey poured over a blade, she might say yes. Not because she wanted to abandon humanity. Because nobody had ever, in her whole small unloved life, offered her a place anywhere, and here was a place with her name already sung into the woodwork.

"No," said Ser Ilsa Marrow.

She hadn't drawn her sword. To her credit — and I'll admit her credit cost me something, we hadn't been getting along — she hadn't drawn it. But her hand was resting on the hilt in a way that was less *resting* and more *introducing them*. She stepped forward until she stood at Wren's shoulder, armor clinking, absolutely refusing to be impressed by several hundred immortal beings and their lighting arrangements.

"She's not making any bargains today," Ilsa said. "Not with you. Not standing in your hall, under your lights, with your music in her ears."

"Our hall is open—"

"Your hall is a snare with better manners than most." Ilsa's voice was flat and even, the voice of a woman reciting inventory. "I guarded the old covenant for eleven years. I watched what claiming does. I watched what happens to those caught between courts and called it loyalty. So I'll say it plainly, madam, whatever you are: she goes to the Worldroot whole, or she doesn't go at all."

Silence. The dangerous kind. Somewhere in the hedges above, something with too many joints shifted its weight.

And then — because I'm a smuggler, and smuggling is ninety percent knowing when to talk and ten percent knowing when to run — I stepped in.

"What she means," I said brightly, "is that we're honored. Deeply honored. Overwhelmingly honored, honestly, it's a bit much, all these lights, my eyes are watering. But surely the court knows better than anyone that a bargain made under pressure isn't freely given? And the covenant, as I understand it — and I've carried goods past it for thirty years — runs on free consent. Willing sacrifice, willing oath, willing everything. Wouldn't a renunciation extracted tonight, before she's even seen the Root, before she's spoken with its guardian, be..." I let my hands describe a delicate shape in the air. "...fruit with a worm already in it?"

The queen turned her attention to me. All of it. It was like being looked at by a landslide.

"Pip Thistlewick," she said. "Half-blood. Smuggler. Debtor." Oh, lovely, word travels fast in the brambles. "You speak boldly for one whose own price remains unpaid."

"My prices are always paid eventually," I said. "It's sort of my whole tragic brand."

More silence. Then the queen did something I truly did not expect.

She laughed.

It wasn't a kind laugh, but it wasn't cruel either — it was the laugh of someone who has been bored for three hundred years and has just been handed something interesting. The lights drifted upward again. The music resumed, somewhere far off, in a gentler key.

"Very well," she said. "Go to the Worldroot. Speak with what remains of its guardian. Hear the truth of the old bargain from the thing it was built upon." Her gaze returned to Wren, and softened — or arranged itself into something resembling softness, which in the Thornwood may be the same thing. "But hear me, Wren Zhou, daughter of two doors. When the guardian tells you its price, remember that we offered you a different one. Remember that there are two ways to belong to the Thornwood."

"And what are they?" Wren asked quietly.

"To be claimed," said the queen, "or to be welcomed. One costs you everything you are. The other costs everyone else something." She drew back, and the roses along her sleeves closed one by one, like fists. "Choose slowly. The wood has waited a long time. It can wait a little longer."

The hedges parted. Just like that, an aisle opened behind us, leading away into green dusk — and I got the distinct impression the path had been there the entire time, and had simply declined to exist until the court approved of our departure.

* * *

We walked. Nobody spoke for the first hundred yards, which for me is approximately a lifetime.

It was Wren who broke it, and her voice came out small. "Was she lying?"

"About what part?" I asked.

"Any of it. All of it. About — about belonging. About being looked for." She hugged the courier's map against her chest like a shield. "Because it felt true. It felt more true than anything anyone's said to me in ten years, and I *hated* that I wanted to say yes, and I don't understand why I wanted to say yes to giving up — " Her breath hitched. "Corvane is awful. Corvane smells like tanneries and hangs people. Why would I miss *that*?"

"Because it's yours," Ilsa said.

We both looked at her. Ilsa Marrow giving comfort is like watching a bear attempt needlework — you admire the effort, you fear for everyone involved.

"When they exiled me," she went on, after a moment, staring straight ahead, "I missed the worst kingdom in the world. Cold barracks. Bad ale. A king who lied to me. Eleven years of my life given to a lie." Her jaw worked. "Still mine. Still missed it. That's what ties are. They're not a reward for good service. They're just — yours."

Wren blinked rapidly and looked away, at the blooming hedges, at the drifting lights. "Thank you," she managed.

"Don't mention it," said Ilsa, with enormous relief.

I let them have their moment. Somebody had to be professional about this, and clearly it wasn't going to be either of them.

But here's the thing I didn't say. Couldn't say. As we walked out of the court's light and into the pooling dark between the trees, I kept turning over the queen's last words — *the other costs everyone else something* — and the way she'd looked at me when she said *debtor*.

Because Voss owed the river-fae. That was the debt Pip Thistlewick carried out of his old life, the favor called in that put him on this road, and I'd been carefully not thinking about what the river-fae might want in return for opening the sealed path to the Worldroot. Carefully not thinking about it for six days. I was planning on seven.

Ahead, the trees began to whisper, and beneath the whispering — faint, patient, unmistakable — I heard water.

The river was close. And rivers, in the Thornwood, always collect.
