## 12. Ilsa's Broken Oath

Ilsa Marrow had learned, over ten years of exile, that guilt made a poor bedfellow and an excellent whetstone. She sharpened her sword the way other people prayed — every night, same hour, same slow strokes — because a blade in motion left no room for thinking, and thinking was where the rot lived.

Tonight there was no blade to sharpen. The Thornwood had eaten their whetstone somewhere around the third rearrangement of the path, along with half their rations and, briefly, Pip's dignity. So she sat with her back against a root the size of a chapel wall, oiling the steel instead, and watched Wren sleep.

The girl slept like someone who had never once been allowed to sleep deeply. One hand curled near her pack, where the bloodstained map lived. Her brow furrowed even in rest, as if she were still haggling with someone.

*You look like her,* Ilsa thought, and then hated herself for it, because it wasn't true, and because the lie was the whole architecture of her shame.

She looked away. She oiled the steel.

Ten years ago tonight — or close enough; the seasons ran strange out here, and stranger still in memory — she had stood at the mouth of the Worldroot's chamber with rain running off her pauldrons and an order in her ear that she could still recite word for word, the way you can recite a thing only if you have never once been permitted to forget it.

*"Stand down, Ser Marrow. That is not a request."*

Captain Aldery Vane had said it gently. That was the worst part. Not shouted, not snarled — *gentle*, the way you speak to a horse you're about to lead into fire, because gentleness is cheaper than argument and works nearly as well.

Behind Vane, down the long throat of the root-hollow, the guardian had been dying. Ilsa had seen it. That was her post; seeing was the whole of her post. Ten years of standing watch over a being most of the realm insisted was a story, and she had learned its breathing the way a midwife learns a laboring mother's — the long pull, the catch, the settle. That night the breathing had gone wrong. Shallow. Ragged. A sound like wind through a cracked window.

And she had known. Not suspected. *Known*, the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop lands, because your bones are older than your training.

"Ser Marrow." Vane's hand on her shoulder, light as ash. "The covenant has provisions for this. The guardian fades, the anchor holds until a successor is found. It is handled."

"It is not handled," she had said. "It's dying *now*. If we go in — if I go in —"

"If you go in," Vane said, "you will touch it, and it will know a knight's oath is near, and it will fight to live. And a guardian that fights to live past its appointed hour tears loose from the root when it goes. You've read what happens then."

She had read what happened then. Everyone in the Order had read what happened then, in a ledger so old the ink had gone brown: *a guardian that dies struggling takes the vale with it.* Fields. Rivers. The border villages. Perhaps more.

It might even have been true.

That was the sentence she had turned over for ten years, polishing it like a stone, looking for the crack in it. *It might even have been true.* Because the order hadn't come from Vane alone. It had come down the chain, sealed and countersigned, from men whose names she'd never learned, and the seal had been the crown's, and the logic had been airtight, and airtight things, she had learned since, are usually coffins.

So she had stood down. She had stood at her post, which was the cruelest joke of it — she had *kept her oath*, technically, scrupulously, to the letter — and she had listened to the guardian die through four hours of rain, and when the breathing stopped she had felt something pass through the hollow like a door closing in a house she loved.

Three days later the Order declared the covenant failed and dissolved itself. Six months later she was exiled — not for disobeying, but for asking, once, in writing, why no successor had ever been sought. The question had been answered with a gate, a stripped banner, and a road east.

She had spent ten years telling herself she had obeyed because obedience was duty and duty was all she had. It had calcified nicely. It had almost become true.

Then a charm-seller with tired eyes and a dead man's map had walked into her life, and the stone had cracked straight down the middle.

* * *

Wren woke near dawn — or what passed for dawn under a canopy that kept its own hours — and found Ilsa already up, already armored, already radiating the particular cheerlessness of a woman who had slept badly and intended everyone to know it.

"You're staring," Wren said, sitting up, hair full of leaf-rot and twigs.

"I'm keeping watch."

"At me."

"The enemy keeps watch at things too," Ilsa said. "It's a respected tactic."

Pip, from his bedroll, without opening his eyes: "She's been doing it all night. I counted. Forty-one times I woke up and she was glaring at you like you owed her money."

"I don't glare."

"You absolutely glare," Wren said. "You have a face like a closed ledger."

"A closed ledger is an honest ledger."

"It's a ledger nobody can *read*, Ilsa."

Ilsa decided this was the moment to check the edge of her sword, which did not need checking. But Wren didn't drop it — the girl never dropped anything, which Ilsa was beginning to understand was both her greatest virtue and the reason Inquisitors wanted her head — and after a silence that stretched long enough to have its own weather, Wren said, quietly:

"You've been different since the river-fae. Since Pip came back with that gap behind his eyes and wouldn't say what he'd paid. You keep looking at me like you're rehearsing something."

"I don't rehearse."

"You do. Your jaw moves."

Ilsa put the sword down. That was the trouble with this girl; she noticed everything, catalogued everything, filed everything away in that exhausted little head of hers, and sooner or later she was going to notice the one thing Ilsa had spent a decade burying under drill and discipline and the careful, load-bearing fiction that she had done nothing wrong.

"We should wake Pip," Ilsa said. "We lose the light—"

"There is no light. There's never any light. The forest makes its own schedule." Wren pulled her knees up. "Sit down, Ser Marrow. Whatever it is, it's been eating you since Corvane, and I'd rather hear it now than watch you chew it for another hundred miles."

From the bedroll, muffled: "For the record, I also want to hear it. I'm just staying horizontal out of solidarity."

"Pip—"

"Solidarity," Pip repeated firmly, eyes still shut. "Also my feet hurt."

Ilsa sat down. It was astonishing how heavy sitting down could be, when you'd been carrying a thing for ten years.

"When I was sworn," she began, and her voice came out level, parade-ground level, because that was the only voice she trusted with this, "the Order of the Verdant March guarded the Worldroot. Twelve knights, one post, one purpose. We didn't guard against the fae. Half the stories have that wrong. We guarded *for* them — for the crossing, for the balance. The guardian at the root was the hinge of it all. An old creature, older than the crown, older than the war. It didn't speak, mostly. It sang, sometimes, at dusk. Badly. Like a cat in a sack. Pip would have hated it."

A small, wounded sound came from the bedroll. "I'm listening," Pip said, "and I'm awake now, and I resent both."

"The guardian was dying when I was twenty-six. Not suddenly. Fading, the way a lamp fades. And I reported it, and the report went up the chain, and what came back down was an order." She heard her own voice flatten further, the way it always did. "*Stand down. Do not approach. Do not ease its passing. The covenant provides.*"

Wren had gone very still. "Provides what?"

"That's what I asked. In writing. Afterward." Ilsa looked at her hands. Knight's hands, scarred, kept clean by habit rather than vanity. "They exiled me for the question, not the answer. I never got the answer. But I got ten years to think, and here's what thinking gets you: I stood at my post and I let it die alone, because men with a crown's seal told me that approaching it would make things worse, and I believed them, because believing is easier than walking into a hollow and holding a dying god's hand while the world tells you you're committing treason."

The words came out faster now, the dam cracking in the middle the way dams do — not dramatically, but all at once, everywhere.

"And it might have been true. That's the part I need you to understand, Wren. It might genuinely have been true. If I'd touched it, if it had clung to life past its hour, maybe the vale would have torn open and drowned three villages. Maybe I saved them. Or maybe—" and here her voice finally broke its parade-ground line, just slightly, just enough that Wren leaned forward — "maybe they knew the guardian was failing because the covenant itself was rotten, and letting it die quietly was cheaper than letting anyone see how. Maybe 'the covenant provides' meant *provides a successor*, and the successor they had in mind was never born yet. Or was still a child. Or was—"

She stopped.

Wren was looking at her with an expression Ilsa had no name for. Not horror. Worse than horror — recognition.

"When was this," Wren said. "Exactly."

"Ten years ago. Autumn."

"I'm nineteen," Wren said. "I was left on the guild's doorstep nine years ago. A year after."

"I know," Ilsa said. "I did the arithmetic the first night you told me about yourself. I've been doing it every night since. That's why I stare."

The forest made a sound around them — not threatening, just present, the way it was always present, the way it had been leaning closer to Wren since the pilgrim road. Somewhere above, leaves moved without wind.

"So either it's coincidence," Ilsa said, "and I've spent ten years breaking myself over nothing. Or it isn't, and the order I obeyed — the order that let the guardian die — was the first move in whatever game put you on a doorstep in Corvane. And either way, Wren, I'm the woman who held the door for it. I kept my oath so perfectly that I betrayed the one thing the oath was actually for. That's what a broken oath looks like when it's done properly. Nobody punishes you. They promote you, or they exile you, and either way you carry it."

Silence. Pip had sat up at some point without anyone noticing, which for Pip was roughly equivalent to standing at attention.

Wren reached out and took Ilsa's gauntleted hand — an absurd gesture, a child's gesture, her fingers barely wrapping around two of Ilsa's — and said, "You didn't know."

"Knowing is my job. Knowing was the entire post."

"You didn't know," Wren repeated, stubborn as a root. "And you've spent ten years paying for a decision made by men whose names you don't even know. I've met that trick before. Voss used it on me my whole childhood — make the kid feel responsible so the adults never have to." Her grip tightened. "But here's the difference between you and them, Ilsa. They never once lost sleep. You haven't slept properly in a decade. That's not guilt. That's a conscience that outlived a bad order."

Ilsa discovered, to her private fury, that her eyes were wet. She blamed the canopy. Canopies dripped.

"If we reach the Worldroot," she said, when she could trust her voice, "and the truth is what I think it is — that the covenant was never a treaty, that everything I swore to defend was a cage with good manners — then my whole life was service to a lie. I need you to understand that I may not be able to help what I do next. Duty doesn't die easy. It just changes masters."

"Then we'll find it new work," Wren said simply. "Duty's like a charm, Ilsa. It matters what it's anchored to."

And that — that ridiculous, market-stall wisdom, delivered with leaf-mold in her hair — was the moment Ilsa Marrow understood she would follow this girl to the root of the world and back, orders be damned.

She was still deciding whether to say so when Pip, who had been uncharacteristically quiet, spoke from the edge of the camp, very softly, in the tone he reserved for genuine emergencies:

"Ladies. Lovely moment. Truly. But the trees have moved again, and I don't remember that path being there, and it's glowing."

Down the newly opened track, between two roots like cathedral pillars, something pale flickered — steady, patient, unmistakably a lantern-light, though no lantern hung there.

And from deep beneath their feet, faint as a heartbeat heard through a floor, the ground pulsed once.

Twice.

Like something enormous, turning over in its sleep.
