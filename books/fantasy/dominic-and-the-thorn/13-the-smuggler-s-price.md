## 13. The Smuggler's Price

Pip had stolen a great many things in his life. A silver candlestick from the Bishop of Corvane's own table. A kiss from a duchess's daughter, which had cost him three teeth and taught him that nobility bit. An entire barge once, though technically it had been sinking at the time and he'd only been redirecting its fate.

He had never before stolen from himself.

That was how it felt, standing on the mossy bank of the river the fae called *Sorrow's Sister* and everyone else called nothing at all, because mortal maps simply refused to hold still long enough to name it. The water ran green-gold in the Thornwood's perpetual dusk, slow as honey, and where it passed over stones it made no sound whatsoever. That was the worst part, Pip thought. Rivers were supposed to make noise. A silent river was a river listening.

And it was listening to him.

"You're up early," said a voice behind him, and Pip's heart performed a small acrobatic maneuver before he recognized Ilsa's gravel-and-iron tones. The knight stood at the edge of the trees, already armored — she slept in half her plate, which Pip considered either admirable or deeply sad — with her sword across her back like a spine of honest steel.

"Couldn't sleep," Pip said, which was true, and also a lie, because he hadn't even tried.

Ilsa grunted. She was not a woman who wasted words, which Pip normally appreciated, since it left more conversational room for him. This morning, though, he found himself wishing she'd chatter. He found himself wishing anyone would chatter, so he wouldn't have to hear the river.

"Wren's still out," Ilsa said. "The forest kept her up again. Singing, she says." Her jaw worked around something. "It sings to me too. Mostly insults."

"It has opinions about your sword arm."

"It told me my sword arm is 'adequate.'" Ilsa looked genuinely offended. "Twenty years of drill, and a hedge calls me adequate."

Pip laughed, and the laugh came out easier than anything else had come out all night, and for one bright moment he almost forgot why he was standing at the bank of a listening river with his pockets full of things he meant to give away.

Almost.

"I'm going to scout downstream," he said. "See if there's a crossing."

"The guardian said the path seals at midday."

"The guardian," Pip said, "says a lot of things in a voice like a landslide. I like to verify."

Ilsa studied him for a moment with those gray eyes that missed very little, and Pip felt the particular terror of being seen by an honest person. But she only nodded. "Shout if you need steel."

"Always do."

She turned back toward camp, and Pip watched her go, and thought: *she'd stop me*. Not out of cruelty. Out of that stubborn, mule-headed decency she carried around like her armor, the kind that had gotten her exiled because she'd finally stopped obeying and started caring too late. If Ilsa knew what he intended, she'd put herself between Pip and the river, and she'd lose, because you couldn't fight a bargain with a blade, but she'd try, and then there'd be two people broken instead of one.

So. He wouldn't tell her. Or Wren. Especially not Wren, who already carried enough names for other people's dead, who lay awake at night doing sums of guilt that never came out fair. Pip had watched her do it by firelight. He wasn't going to add a line to her ledger.

He waited until the camp sounds faded — Ilsa's whetstone, Wren's uneven breathing, the forest's low muttering — and then he walked down along the bank until the trees grew close and old and the moss came up past his ankles like green snow.

The river-fae were waiting. Of course they were. They'd been waiting since the moment he'd first dreamed of water three nights ago, dreaming in a language he didn't speak but understood anyway, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

They rose out of the current without disturbing it.

There were three of them, or perhaps one of them wearing three shapes, tall and reedy as drowned willows, their skin the pale green of light through leaves, their eyes the color of deep water over deep stone. Their faces were beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say you admired them right up until you froze to death.

"Pip of Nowhere," said the middle one, in a voice like water finding a crack in stone. "Smuggler. Debtor. Half-blood."

"Just Pip is fine."

"You come to trade."

It wasn't a question. The river had known since the dream. Pip had hoped, stupidly, childishly, that maybe he could talk his way past — charm them, bluff them, find some loophole in the wording. That was his whole art, loopholes. He'd built a life on the space between what was said and what was meant.

But looking at the river-fae now, he understood that there would be no loopholes here. The river did not deal in gaps. It dealt in weight.

"Yeah," he said. His mouth was dry as August. "Yeah, I've come to trade."

"The path to the Worldroot is sealed," the fae said. "Sealed against the Inquisitor who follows you, sealed against the court that would use you, sealed against all who come with empty hands. It opens for price alone." Its head tilted, willow-slow. "You carry coin. Coin is nothing here. You carry trinkets. Trinkets are less than nothing. What will you pay?"

Pip had rehearsed this. Standing watch while the others slept, staring into the fire, he had rehearsed the words until they were smooth as river stones. And now, when it mattered, they all fell out of his head at once, and what remained was just a boy from a fen-edge village twenty-nine years ago, standing in a doorway watching his mother pack.

"You know what I have," he said quietly. "You've been in my head. You know what I have."

"We know what you have," the fae agreed. "We wish to hear you say it. Price must be named by the payer. That is the oldest law older than us."

Pip closed his eyes.

His mother's name had been Maren. She'd been a fisherman's daughter with forearms like rope and a laugh that scared crows off the marsh, and she'd sung — badly, gloriously badly, off-key with total conviction — songs about eels and unfaithful husbands and the moon being a woman who owed her money. His father had been the fae side, a river-man, a thing of riddles and cold hands, gone before Pip could walk, leaving nothing but the pointed ears and the hunger for horizons. The village hadn't blamed Maren, exactly. They'd pitied her, loudly, in ways designed to be overheard. And when Pip was six, when the tithe-collectors came and the whispers got teeth, she'd sat him down on their one good chair and held his face in her rough hands and said:

*"You're going on an adventure, little fish. And I'm going to be so proud of you the whole time, and don't you dare look back, because if you look back I'll cry, and then you'll cry, and neither of us wants the neighbors seeing that."*

And he hadn't looked back. Six years old, walking down the lane with a bundle on his stick, and he hadn't looked back, because she'd asked, and because he'd been a good boy, and because looking back would have killed them both.

He'd spent twenty-three years telling himself it had been an adventure.

"There's a memory," Pip said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Of my mother. Of the day she sent me away. All of it. Her hands. Her voice. The door." He opened his eyes. "That's the price."

The river-fae went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"That is a heavy price," it said, and there was — Pip would swear later, though he'd have no way to check — something almost like reluctance in it. "Memories of love weigh more than memories of pain. Pain fades. Love does not. Are you certain?"

"No," Pip said honestly. "But certainty's for priests and knights. I'm a smuggler. I work with what's in the boat."

"Why?" the fae asked. "You owe Guildmaster Voss a debt you despise. You could let the girl fail. Let the knight turn back. Walk away whole, with all your cargo." Its deep-water eyes fixed on him. "Why pay?"

And Pip, who had an answer for everything, who had talked his way past customs men and cutpurses and one memorable dragonfly-sprite with a badge complex, found that the true answer was embarrassingly simple, and that lying to the river was impossible anyway.

"Because she sent me away so I'd live," he said. "And I've been alive twenty-three years without ever once doing anything worth the sending. Wren's going to stand under that root and pay more than any of us can imagine. The least I can do is open the door." He managed a crooked grin, mostly for himself. "Besides. My mother liked brave idiots. Said they were the only entertainment worth having."

The river-fae regarded him for a long moment.

Then it extended a hand — long-fingered, translucent at the edges, dripping light instead of water.

"Then pay, Pip of Nowhere. And know this: what we take, we keep. What we keep, we sometimes sing. Your mother's memory will live in this river, and rivers remember kindly. On quiet nights, if you pass water anywhere in the world, listen. You may hear her laughing."

"That's either a comfort or a curse," Pip said.

"Yes," said the fae.

Pip took the hand.

* * *

It didn't hurt. That was the cruelest part. He'd braced for pain, for tearing, for some dramatic rending worthy of a ballad. Instead it felt like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying — a lightness, a loosening, a warmth flowing out of him through his palm and into the green-gold water.

He saw it go. That was the strange mercy and the strange horror both: for one last instant, the memory flared bright as it left him. Maren's kitchen. Eel stew burning because she'd been singing instead of stirring. Her hands on his face, callused and warm. *Don't you dare look back, little fish.* The door. The lane. The morning light.

*Goodbye, little fish,* said the memory, in her voice, and then it was in the river, and the river took it down into the deep places where such treasures sleep, and Pip stood on the bank of Sorrow's Sister with a hole in his chest shaped exactly like a woman's laugh.

He tested the edges of the hole, the way you test a missing tooth with your tongue.

He knew he'd had a mother. The fact remained, filed and factual. Fisherman's daughter. Fen village. Sent him away at six. He knew all of it the way you know the capital's population or the number of days in autumn — true, useful, and utterly without warmth. He reached for her face and found a smooth blank wall where a face should be, and behind the wall, faintly, impossibly, he thought he heard singing. Badly. Off-key. About eels.

"Oh," Pip said aloud, to nobody, to the trees, to the hole. "Oh, that's — yeah. Okay. That's fine. That's absolutely—"

His knees decided independently that sitting down was happening now. The moss received him. Somewhere above, a bird sang something mocking and lovely.

He sat there for a long time. Long enough that the light changed, though in the Thornwood light changing meant little. He catalogued himself, the way he catalogued cargo after a bad crossing. Hands: present, shaking slightly. Humor: damaged but salvageable. Heart: holed below the waterline, pumps still working, jury-rigged, seaworthy-ish.

Alive. Still alive. Still himself, minus one item.

"One item," he whispered. "Just one item off the manifest."

The river said nothing, but further downstream, faintly, he heard laughter. A woman's laugh, scaring crows off the marsh. It might have been the water over stones. There were no sounds of water over stones. It might have been nothing at all.

Pip laughed too — wet, cracked, real — and got up, and brushed the moss off his coat, and straightened his collar, because a smuggler's dignity was the last cargo he had left and he'd be damned if he'd abandon that too.

* * *

By the time he got back to camp, the path was open.

He felt it before he saw it — a pull in his chest, gentle as a hook set behind the ribs, drawing him toward a place in the hillside where the roots of an enormous tree had woven themselves into an arch. Yesterday the arch had been solid wood, impassable, ancient. Now the heart of it glowed faintly, like a lantern behind a curtain, and the air that breathed out of it smelled of loam and starlight and something older than either.

Wren was on her feet, eyes wide. "Pip! The seal — it just opened, we heard it groan, Ilsa nearly put her sword through it — how did you—"

"Negotiated," Pip said. He tipped an imaginary hat. "Smuggler's arts. Never reveal the terms; it weakens your position in future dealings."

Ilsa's gray eyes narrowed. She looked at him the way she looked at maps that didn't quite match the terrain. "What did it cost?"

"A favor," Pip said smoothly. "To be named later. Standard arrangement. Very boring. You'd hate the details."

"You're pale."

"I'm always pale. Half-fae. We're essentially decorative."

"Pip." Wren stepped closer, and her eyes — her strange, bridge-between-worlds eyes — went soft and sharp at once, and Pip realized with a lurch that she was doing the thing she did, the seeing-people thing, the thing that made her dangerous to liars. "What did you give them?"

And here was the moment. Here was the exact moment where he could have told her, where the truth sat right there behind his teeth like a coin under the tongue, and all he had to do was open his mouth.

But he looked at her — at the shadows under her eyes, at the way she flinched when the forest sang, at the enormous terrible weight she was carrying toward the Worldroot step by step — and he thought about ledgers, and fairness, and what his mother (a fact, a file, a woman whose face was a locked door) would have wanted.

"An old debt," he said gently. "One I've been carrying a long time. Consider it paid, and consider me lighter for it." He smiled, and it barely shook at all. "Now. Are we going to stand here admiring architecture, or are we going to see what's at the bottom of the world?"

Wren searched his face a moment longer. Then, mercifully, she let it go — filed it, he could tell, in that relentless ledger of hers, next to all the other debts she tracked for other people.

"All right," she said. "Together, then."

"Together," Ilsa rumbled, and drew her sword — not threatening, just company, the way some men traveled with dogs.

Pip looked at the archway, at the glow beyond it, at the dark throat of the path that led down to the Worldroot and whatever ending waited there. The hook behind his ribs tugged, gentle and patient. The path wanted him to walk it. The river had opened it for him specifically, priced to the payer, and every step down would be a step he'd bought with a woman's laugh.

*Don't you dare look back, little fish.*

He didn't know why those words surfaced, or why his eyes stung when they did. Some cargo you carry without knowing the manifest.

"Well," Pip said briskly, clapping his hands together, "after you, ladies. History's not going to make itself."

And he followed Wren and Ilsa into the dark, humming a tune he didn't recognize — something about eels, maybe, or unfaithful husbands, or a woman who owed the moon money — and behind him, far behind, in a green-gold river, something sang along.
