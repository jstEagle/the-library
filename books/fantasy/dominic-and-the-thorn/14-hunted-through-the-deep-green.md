## 14. Hunted Through the Deep Green

The Thornwood had stopped pretending to be a forest three days ago.

Wren had grown up in Corvane, where a street was a street and a door was a door and nothing rearranged itself out of spite. Here, the paths had opinions. The trail they'd followed at dawn had, by midmorning, become a different trail entirely — one that looped them past the same split boulder twice and then, insultingly, a third time, as if the wood were checking whether they'd learned their lesson yet.

"Left," Pip said for the fourth time that hour, pointing at a gap between two birches that had definitely not been there before.

"You said left last time," Ilsa said. "Last time left took us back to the river."

"The river moved."

"The river *moved*."

"It's fae country," Pip said, with the weary tone of a man explaining taxes to a child. "Everything moves. Rivers, roads, grudges. My grandmother used to say the only fixed point in the Thornwood is a debt."

"And you would know," Ilsa said.

"I *do* know, which is why I'm the navigator, Ser Marrow, if it's all the same to you."

It was not, visibly, all the same to her. But Ilsa let him lead, because she was wise enough to know when her sword arm was more useful than her opinion, and Wren trailed behind them both, one hand resting on the strap of her satchel, feeling the map through the leather like a heartbeat that wasn't hers.

Three days since the ambush at the waystation. Three days since Ashgrave's arrows had come out of the dark and turned their camp into splinters and smoke. They'd run barefoot through bracken with half their supplies still burning behind them, and they hadn't stopped running long enough to feel safe since.

Wren kept telling herself he couldn't have followed them through the veil. The veil was dying, yes, but crossing it required power, or blood, or —

Or the blood of someone who already had.

She remembered the courier's blood on her own palms, tacky and cooling, the night he died against her stall. She remembered wiping her hands on her apron because there hadn't been time for anything else, and wearing that apron for two more days, and folding it into her pack because she was poor and waste was sin.

*Oh,* she thought, not for the first time. *Oh, you absolute fool.*

"Pip," she said. "How does someone cross the veil without magic?"

Pip glanced back, eyebrows climbing. "Blood of a crossed-over, mostly. Why?"

"No reason."

"Wren."

"A hypothetical reason."

"Wren." He stopped walking entirely, which in the Thornwood was its own small act of courage, and looked at her with those mismatched eyes — one brown, one the pale gold of cheap honey. "You're telling me the Inquisitor has been tracking us by your laundry."

"My *apron*—"

"Your blood-soaked murder-apron."

"It wasn't murder, he was already dying—"

"You've been carrying a compass made of dead man pointing directly at us for three days." Pip pressed both hands over his face. "I traded my mother's face to a river for this path. I could have just given him the apron."

"We don't talk about the river," Ilsa said quietly.

"We're talking about the apron now, apparently, so the dam has broken generally—"

"*Both of you.*" Wren heard her own voice crack and hated it. "Burning it won't help now, will it? He's got what he needs. So we keep moving, and we stop wasting breath."

They kept moving. But something had shifted in the space between the three of them, some small seam pulled loose, and Wren felt it snagging at her with every step. Fear did that. Fear was a thorn; it caught on everything.

By late afternoon the light had gone strange and green-gold, filtering down through a canopy so dense it seemed less like sky and more like the memory of sky. And Wren began, very quietly, to notice the silence.

Not the good kind of silence. Not the hush of deep woods. This was the silence of held breath. No birdsong. No insect hum. Even the wind had gone somewhere else and left no note.

Ilsa noticed it too. Wren saw her hand drift to her sword hilt — not drawing, just resting, the way a person touches a wall in the dark.

"How long," Ilsa murmured, "has it been quiet?"

"Since the second boulder," Wren whispered.

"That was an hour ago."

"Yes."

Pip had gone very still ahead of them, ears — slightly pointed, slightly mobile, a family inheritance he complained about constantly — swiveled backward like a cat's. "He's not following our trail anymore," he breathed. "He doesn't need to. He knows exactly where we are. He's choosing his ground."

"Then we choose ours first," Ilsa said. "Find me somewhere narrow. Somewhere he can't ring us."

"There's a ravine—" Pip started.

The first arrow took him in the shoulder.

*

Later, Wren would try to reconstruct those minutes and fail. Memory came back to her in pieces, the way shattered glass comes back: a shape, a flash, a sound.

Pip spinning, the arrow buried high in his left shoulder, his cry more outrage than pain — *that's my bow arm, you absolute bureaucrat* — and then Ilsa moving, faster than Wren had ever seen a woman in armor move, hauling Pip behind a fallen trunk as a second arrow buried itself in the bark where his head had been.

"DOWN," Ilsa roared, and Wren went down, cheek in the moss, heart slamming, and crawled.

The trees had betrayed them. Of course they had. The narrow ravine Pip had been about to name opened instead onto a wide clearing — soft ground, open sightlines, a killing field dressed up as a shortcut. The Thornwood had led them here. Or something in the Thornwood had been paid to.

Ashgrave stepped out from between two oaks.

He didn't rush. That was the worst part, the part that lived under Wren's skin afterward. He walked, unhurried, crossbow lowered but ready, his grey cloak beaded with mist, his face carrying the mild patience of a man arriving slightly early for an appointment.

"Wren Zhou," he said. His voice was quiet, almost kind. "And the knight. And the smuggler. You've cost me six days and two good horses."

"You'll want to add a third horse," Pip called from behind the log, voice strained, hand clamped over his shoulder. "The one you fed to the bog outside Tarnholt. She was lovely."

"She bit me."

"She bites everyone. It's how she shows affection."

"Master Pip." Ashgrave tilted his head. "You are bleeding. Hold still and this ends quickly."

"See, that's the thing about offers like that," Pip said. "Historically, when you say 'quickly,' you mean for *you*."

Wren's mind was racing, cold and clear in the way it only went when things were truly bad. Ten years of market stalls. Ten years of reading crowds, spotting the cutpurse, knowing when the patrol was about to turn the corner. Ashgrave stood between them and the treeline on the far side. Two men-at-arms flanked him — no, three, one drifting wide to close the circle. Crossbows. Open ground. Maybe forty paces.

Forty paces might as well have been forty miles.

*Ilsa can't reach him. Pip can't shoot. I can't do anything.*

Except.

The warmth in her palms.

It had been growing for days — ever since the veil, ever since the forest started greeting her like a returning child. A faint heat, banked-coal faint, that she'd been ignoring the way you ignore a toothache. Her guild charms had never been fake. She was guild-raised, guild-blooded, whatever that meant, and the covenant was failing, and the magic that should have been flowing through ten thousand little anchors across the mortal realm was backing up somewhere, and apparently *somewhere* was her.

She didn't know what she could do. She had never once, in her life, done anything on purpose.

But she knew what fear looked like on a man, even a calm one. And Ashgrave's eyes had flicked — just once, just briefly — toward her hands.

So she raised them.

"Stop," she said, and poured every scrap of that banked heat into her voice, into the air, into the space between them, with no idea at all what she was doing.

The clearing answered.

Every thornbush at its edge bloomed at once — white flowers bursting open out of season, out of order, out of any sane botany — and the brambles themselves *moved*, a slow groaning ripple, like a dog shifting in its sleep. Not attacking. Just... waking. Just making it known that they were there, and large, and awake.

One of the men-at-arms broke. He simply turned and ran, crossbow and all, crashing into the undergrowth with the sound of a man renegotiating his career.

Ashgrave didn't move. But Wren saw it — the fractional pause, the recalculation. He looked at the blooming thorns. He looked at her. And for the first time since the market square, Inquisitor Ashgrave looked uncertain.

"What are you?" he asked. It wasn't rhetoric. It was a genuine question, and it frightened her more than the crossbow had.

"Nobody," Wren said. "That's the whole trick. Nobody special."

"Nobody special doesn't wake the wood."

"Well." Her voice shook. She let it. Let him hear the terror underneath, because it was true, and true things land harder than bluffs. "Maybe the wood disagrees. Maybe you should ask yourself why everything in this forest keeps deciding I'm worth protecting, Inquisitor, and whether you really want to find out what happens next."

A long moment. The thorns rustled. Somewhere overhead, a bird resumed singing, tentatively, like a musician rejoining after a wrong note.

Ashgrave lowered the crossbow an inch. Then another inch.

"To be continued, then," he said, and stepped backward into the shade, and was gone — taking his remaining men with him, unhurried even in retreat, which somehow made it worse. He wasn't leaving. He was *withdrawing*. There was a difference, and the difference was that he'd learned something today, and so had she:

She had shown him her one card. And he now knew she couldn't aim it.

*

They ran anyway. You didn't stay in a clearing after a thing like that; that was rule one of everywhere.

They ran until the light thickened to amber and Pip's jokes ran out and finally Ilsa called a halt in the hollow beneath a yew tree old enough to remember better centuries. Wren built a fire — small, shielded, technically against every survival instinct she owned, but Pip was shivering and grey-lipped and stubbornly insisting he was fine.

"Hold still," she told him, cutting the sleeve away from the arrow wound.

"It's fine."

"It's a hole in you."

"It's a *small* hole in me. I've had bigger. There was an incident with a harpoon in '41 that I don't discuss in mixed company."

"You're discussing it right now."

"With mixed company, yes. You're both very mixed." He winced as she cleaned the gash. The arrowhead had punched through clean, thank every merciful star — a graze by an inch, though an inch was the whole world sometimes. Ilsa bound it with strips of shirt and a paste of yarrow that Pip identified and then complained tasted like regret.

For a while, none of them spoke. The fire crackled. The yew tree dripped. Somewhere far off, the Thornwood went on being enormous and indifferent and, lately, worryingly attentive.

"He'll come at dusk tomorrow," Ilsa said at last. "Or dawn after. He won't rush again. Today taught him our shape."

"Today taught him *my* shape," Wren said bitterly. "Flowers and theatrics. That's all I've got. One bloom and a strong talking-to."

"It saved our lives."

"For today." She stared into the fire. "Ilsa, I felt it. When I pushed. There isn't much. It's like... like a purse with three coins in it, and I just spent one in front of the man hunting us. What happens when the purse is empty? What happens when he stops being impressed?"

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. Firelight moved across the scarred line of her jaw.

"Then we do what soldiers have always done when the magic runs out," she said. "We get cleverer, or we get buried. Preferably in that order."

"Glad someone's keeping track of the order," Pip mumbled. He was fading fast, the blood loss and the yarrow pulling him under. "Wake me if the forest tries anything. ...Also if it brings breakfast."

His eyes closed. Within minutes his breathing went slow and even, one hand still curled near his ruined shoulder like a child guarding a toy.

Wren watched him sleep and felt the guilt settle in beside her like an old friend. *He traded his mother's face for this path. Ilsa broke her whole life for a covenant everyone said had already failed. And what have I brought? An apron full of breadcrumbs leading straight to us.*

"I can hear you blaming yourself," Ilsa said quietly. "It's very loud."

"Somebody has to do it properly."

"No." Ilsa's voice was iron wrapped in wool. "Blame is a luxury, Wren. I spent ten years marinating in mine and it fed nothing and no one. Save it for after. If we live, you may have seconds."

Despite everything — the ache in her hands, the hole in her friend, the Inquisitor somewhere out in the dark learning patience — Wren almost smiled. "Seconds of blame."

"You strike me as a girl who's skipped a lot of meals."

The fire burned low. Wren took first watch, sitting with her back against the yew's vast root, the map heavy in her lap, the forest breathing around her. And it was in the deepest hour of the night, when even Ilsa's breathing had gone slow, that she felt it.

Not danger. Something stranger.

The warmth in her palms — the dwindling, three-coin warmth — pulsed once. Twice. Like a knock.

And from the darkness beyond the firelight, soft as falling petals, came a smell she had no business recognizing and recognized instantly: beeswax and lamp-oil and old paper.

Hollow Lantern Guild seal-wax. The exact scent of Voss's study, of the ledger room, of home.

Wren rose without waking the others and followed it, twenty paces into the dark, to where a single lantern hung from a low branch — a plain glass-and-brass lantern of a make she'd sold a hundred imitations of — burning with a flame that cast no heat.

Tucked beneath it, weighted with a stone, a letter. Sealed in green wax. The guild's mark pressed deep and deliberate.

Her hands shook as she broke it. The handwriting was unmistakable — Voss's careful, cramped script, the script that had corrected her letters when she was seven and signed her first sales ledger when she was twelve.

*Wren. If you are reading this, then the wood has carried it to you, which means the wood still loves you, which means there is still time.*

*Come to the Worldroot alone. Do not trust the knight's plan, and do not trust mine. Trust only what you find beneath the Root — because I am no longer certain that what I was told, all those years ago, was the truth.*

*Your father sends his love. He always did. That was the problem.*

*— V.O.*

Wren read it three times. The lantern flame guttered, steadied, and went out.

Behind her, back at the dying fire, she heard Ilsa sit up sharply — and the whisper of steel leaving its sheath.
