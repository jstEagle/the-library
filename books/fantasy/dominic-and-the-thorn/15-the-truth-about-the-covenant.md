## 15. The Truth About the Covenant

The last mile to the Worldroot was the kind of quiet that made Wren's teeth ache.

Not silence — the Thornwood never managed true silence — but a hush, as though the forest had drawn its breath and was holding it, waiting to see what they would do. The trees here grew enormous and strange, their trunks spiraled like honey-sticks, their leaves silver on one side and green on the other so that every breeze turned them over like coins being counted. Pip said it was because the fae had argued about which color they preferred and refused to compromise. Ilsa said it was because the forest didn't care what anyone preferred. Both of them sounded certain. Neither of them looked at each other when they said it.

Three days since the ambush. Three days since Ashgrave's men had burned their supplies and very nearly Ilsa along with them, and the knight still walked with her left arm bound tight against her ribs, refusing help with the particular ferocity of someone who considered being carried an insult on par with treason.

"You're favoring it," Wren said for the fourth time that morning.

"I'm *respecting* it," Ilsa said. "There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

"In Corvane there isn't. Here there is." Ilsa nodded ahead, where the trees had begun to lean inward, forming something between a corridor and a congregation. "Everything means more here. That's rather the problem."

Wren wanted to ask what she meant by that, but the path chose that moment to end.

It ended the way dreams end — not with a wall but with a change. One step she was walking through silver-green light; the next, the light came from below. They stood at the rim of a vast hollow, a bowl sunk into the earth wide enough to swallow Corvane's market district whole, and rising from its center, so tall it seemed less grown than *declared*, was a root. A single root, pale as bone and broad as a keep, climbing out of the dark and up into a canopy that vanished into cloud. The Worldroot. She'd heard the name her whole life in Odell Voss's careful voice, always lowercase, always abstract, the way you say *the crown* or *the law*. This was not abstract. This was a thing with weight and shadow and, she realized as she stood there with her heart hammering, a heartbeat.

She could feel it through the soles of her boots. Slow. Enormous. And wrong — stuttering, like a lamp running low on oil.

"Oh," Pip said softly, beside her. All his usual patter had drained away, leaving just a thin young man with mud on his boots staring at something too big for him. "Oh, that's not good. That's not good at all. It's supposed to be — I mean, my mother used to sing about it, and even the songs made it sound—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It sounds like it's dying."

"It is dying," said a voice from below. "Thank you for noticing. Most visitors take considerably longer."

Wren nearly jumped out of her skin.

At the base of the root, half-hidden by ferns the size of rowboats, sat a figure she had taken at first glance for a fallen branch. It resolved slowly, the way things did here — reluctantly, as if the forest only showed you what it decided you could bear. An old woman, or something shaped like one, seated against the pale bark with her legs stretched out before her like roots themselves. Her skin was gray-brown and fissured, moss growing in the creases of her knuckles. Her eyes were closed. When she spoke again, her lips barely moved, and yet her voice arrived everywhere at once, from the ferns and the hollow walls and somewhere behind Wren's sternum.

"Well," the guardian said. "The guild finally sent someone. Only took them ten years past due."

* * *

Getting down into the hollow took the better part of an hour, the path switchbacking down the bowl's inner wall in long spirals that seemed designed to give visitors time to reconsider. Wren spent all of it rehearsing questions and discarding them. *Are you the one who's been keeping us alive? What do you need? Can we save you?* Each one felt smaller than the last, like bringing a thimble to bail out a sinking ship.

Ilsa walked differently on the descent. Slower. Her jaw set in a way Wren had learned to read as old pain. At one point she murmured, almost to herself, "It's smaller than I remembered."

"Was it bigger?" Wren asked.

"No." Ilsa's hand found the pommel of her sword and stayed there, not gripping, just resting, the way you touch a doorframe of a house you grew up in. "I was."

They reached the floor of the hollow as the light failed, though *failed* wasn't right either — the glow coming off the root itself deepened instead, amber now, pooling around the guardian like spilled honey. Up close she was worse and better than she'd seemed from above: worse because the cracks in her skin were real cracks, deep ones, weeping a sap that smelled of autumn; better because when she opened her eyes they were warm, brown, human-warm, and full of a tired kind of amusement.

"So," the guardian said, looking Wren over with unhurried attention. "The bridge-child. You have your mother's chin. Your father's terrible posture."

"I— what?" Wren's mouth went dry. "You knew my parents?"

"I knew everyone's parents. That was rather the job." The guardian shifted, and the sound she made was the sound of an entire forest settling after a storm. "Sit. All three of you. If you've come to reforge the covenant, you'll want to know what you're reforging, and that conversation goes easier sitting down. It goes easier drunk, frankly, but I don't suppose any of you thought to bring wine."

Pip, who absolutely had brought wine — two bottles, hoarded through the ambush like a dragon with a grudge — produced them with the flourish of a man who had been waiting his whole life for this exact cue.

The guardian drank from the bottle Pip offered with surprising delicacy, holding it in both gnarled hands, and something in her shoulders loosened. "Better. Right. Ask me your questions, then. Or don't, and I'll simply talk, which is what I'd do anyway. Ten years I've had no one to talk to but beetles, and beetles are dreadful conversationalists. All appetite, no curiosity."

"What are you?" Wren asked. It came out ruder than she intended, and she winced. "Sorry. I mean—"

"I'm the hinge." The guardian said it plainly, without self-pity. "When the covenant was forged, something had to hold it. Something that belonged to neither side entirely, so that neither side could claim it. They made me for the purpose — grown, not born, part mortal wood and part fae dream, planted here at the Root where the veil runs thinnest. I am the knot in the middle of the rope. And knots, child, wear."

"How long?"

"Long enough that I remember when your kind still remembered why this place frightens them." Her brown eyes glinted. "Which brings us to the question you actually came to ask, whether you know it or not. So let's not waste the wine. You want to know how to fix the covenant. Before I tell you anything about fixing it, you deserve to know what it *is*."

She paused. Somewhere far above, wind moved through the canopy, and the sound that came down was almost like breathing.

"Every story you've been told says the covenant is a treaty," the guardian said. "A peace. Two peoples who fought a war and agreed to stay on their own sides of the hedge. That's the version in your songs. It's even mostly true — there *was* a war, and it was terrible, and afterward both sides were desperate for it to end. But treaties are made by equals sitting at a table. Nobody sat at a table." She took another slow drink. "Your ancestors won. Decisively. And the victors, being frightened men who had seen what the fae could do and could not unsee it, did not build a peace. They built a cage. The covenant is not a wall between two houses. It is a muzzle and a chain, dressed up in treaty language so the generations after could sleep at night."

Wren felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs and sit there, heavy.

"A cage," Ilsa said quietly. It wasn't a question. It was the sound of a woman hearing a thing she had half-known for twenty years and never let herself say aloud.

"The anchors your guild sold," the guardian went on, nodding at Wren, "the charms, the little iron-and-hawthorn trinkets Voss's people have been peddling for centuries — those aren't protections. They're lockpins. Every charm sold in Corvane is another rivet in the cage, distributed so widely that no one remembers what they're for. Clever, really. The mortals made their prison self-maintaining. The fae buy charms against bad luck, and every coin they spend tightens their own chains." Her mouth curved, without humor. "And the Worldroot is the central post. I am the keeper of the post. And the post is failing, because nothing caged forever stays strong forever — not the cage, not the keeper, and not the lie that holds it all together."

The hollow was very quiet. Even the root's stuttering pulse seemed to have slowed, listening.

"But if it fails," Wren said, and her voice came out small, "the veil tears. Both realms bleed into each other. People die. We watched the wells go bitter, the crops blacken, the woods walk closer to villages that never did anything to anyone—"

"Yes." The guardian's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "That's the trap, dear one. That's the whole architecture of it. The cage was built so that letting it go looks exactly like ending the world. Whoever forged it understood something important about people: they will forgive you almost anything if you make the alternative terrifying enough. Fear is the cheapest mortar there is. It's held this thing together for nine hundred years."

Pip set his bottle down. For once he had nothing funny to offer, and the absence sat on him visibly, like a coat too heavy. "So what do we do?" he asked. "Reforge it? Let it break? There has to be a third—"

"There is a third," the guardian said. "But you won't like it either, and I'm an old woman who has earned the right to be honest, so I'll tell you the truth about the truth: I don't know if it works. No one does. It has never been tried. Nine hundred years, and in all that time, every keeper before me, every guildmaster, every king and queen and court on both sides of the veil, every single one of them looked at the failing cage and reached for the same tools. Repair the bars. Renew the oath. Find a soul that belongs to both sides and feed it to the post, because the post must be fed, and a willing meal strengthens the binding longer than an unwilling one." Her eyes settled on Wren, and Wren felt seen straight through, all the way down to the marrow. "They will tell you it's noble. Some of them will even believe it. But look at what it is, child. Look at it clearly. Nine hundred years of feeding children to a lock so that one people can keep another people afraid."

Ilsa stood up. She hadn't seemed to decide to; her body simply rose, the way it probably had a thousand times in drill yards and battlefields, and she stood there facing the root with her bound arm pressed to her side and her face doing something complicated that Wren couldn't parse and didn't try to.

"I swore an oath to this," Ilsa said at last. Her voice was flat, controlled, the control costing her everything. "Ten years ago I was ordered to stand aside while the last guardian died, and I obeyed, and I told myself it was duty. And now you're telling me the duty itself was—" She stopped. Started again. "What was the war even *about*? If I'm going to lose my whole life's meaning in one evening, I'd like the details."

The guardian smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and crooked. "Borders. Tribute. A stolen bride on one side and a stolen cauldron on the other, depending on whose grandmother tells it. Petty things. Wars usually are. The terror came later, and the terror was real — the fae do not fight the way mortals fight, and mortals saw things in that war that broke something permanent in them. So they built the cage not out of justice but out of nightmare. And then they raised their children on stories until the nightmare felt like history." She looked up at the great pale column of the root, at the amber light guttering along its length. "I have kept this post for four hundred years. Do you know what I have learned, in four hundred years, watching both sides through the bars?"

Nobody answered.

"They miss each other." The guardian said it simply, and the simplicity was devastating. "Mortals leave offerings at hedgerows and call it superstition. Fae leave gifts on doorsteps and call it mischief. Both sides grieve the ones who crossed over and never came back. Both sides tell stories about the other that are half longing and half fear. I have watched nine hundred years of mutual heartbreak, enforced by mutual terror, maintained by a lie, and fed — fed — with the lives of anyone unlucky enough to belong to both worlds." Her cracked hand rested flat against the root's pale bark, tenderly, the way you touch a sleeping child. "The covenant doesn't prevent grief. It *is* the grief. Formalized. Weaponized. Made permanent."

Wren discovered she was crying. She hadn't noticed it start. The tears just arrived, the way they had on the road when the hedges bloomed, the way they had reading Voss's letter — her body knowing things before her mind consented to them. She wiped her face with her sleeve and hated herself a little for it and kept wiping anyway.

"So if we reforge it," she said, "we're saving the world by saving the cage."

"You're saving the world by saving the lie," the guardian corrected gently. "Though I'll grant the world is included in the package. That's the vertigo, isn't it? That's the design. There is no clean option. There is only the broken thing you know, and the untried thing you don't, and the fact that the broken thing is currently eating people." She tipped the bottle back and found it empty and handed it to Pip with genuine appreciation. "Good vintage. Mortal wine. Tastes like sunlight and poor decisions."

Pip laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, but a laugh, and the sound loosened something in all of them.

"The third option," Wren said. "You said there was one. Tell me."

The guardian studied her for a long moment. The amber light moved across her fissured face, and Wren thought: *she's deciding how much of this I can survive.* Which was, she was beginning to understand, the oldest kindness in the world and also the cruelest.

"Not tonight," the guardian said. "Tonight you should eat, and sleep, and argue with each other, because you will argue — I can see it already, the knight with her oaths and the smuggler with his debts and you, bridge-child, with that stubborn set to your jaw that I'd wager a forest you inherited from someone specific. Tonight, rest. Tomorrow I'll show you the heart of the covenant. The actual mechanism. The price it names, and the shape of the souls it has taken, and what remains, at the bottom of everything, when you strip the treaty language away." She closed her eyes again, settling back against the bark. "And then you'll choose. Not me. Not the courts. Not the guildmasters and their ledgers. You. That's the other thing nobody tells you about hinges — eventually, the whole door swings on the smallest part."

They made camp in the lee of a root-flare the size of a barn door. Ilsa built a fire with her one good arm and refused commentary. Pip produced hardtack and dried plums and, when he thought no one was looking, quietly poured a splash of his second bottle onto the ground at the base of the root — an offering, Wren realized, to a dying stranger, from a boy who owed debts to everyone and still gave things away.

Wren lay awake a long time, watching the amber light breathe along the Worldroot's height, feeling its failing pulse come up through the ground and into her spine.

*A cage,* she thought. *All those charms. All those years of Voss's careful hands and the ledger's neat columns. I was selling lockpins. I was the lockpin.*

And underneath the horror, quieter, more dangerous, a second thought kept surfacing, patient as water:

*Nine hundred years of feeding children to a lock.*

*How many?*

*And what were their names?*

She slept eventually, and dreamed of a garden she had never visited, and woke with dirt under her fingernails that hadn't been there when she lay down.

In the morning, the guardian was waiting for them at the base of the root, standing — which she clearly did rarely, and at cost, her legs groaning like ship's timbers. Behind her, where the great trunk met the earth, a seam had opened in the pale bark: a doorway, narrow and dark, breathing out air that smelled of rain on hot stone.

"The heart of the covenant," the guardian said. "Come and meet it. And bridge-child—" her brown eyes found Wren's, and held, and were suddenly very old and very sorry, "—when you see what it asks for, remember that you are allowed to say no. No one in nine hundred years has believed that. It's time somebody did."

Then she turned, and led them into the dark, and the seam closed behind them like a wound deciding to heal.
