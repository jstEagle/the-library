## 16. Loyalty vs. Truth

They made camp in the hollow beneath a fallen oak so vast that Wren first mistook it for architecture — a roof of mossed wood, walls of root, a floor carpeted in needles soft as any featherbed she'd ever sold. The Thornwood had carried them here without being asked, which Pip said was either hospitality or herding, and that in his experience the two were separated by nothing but intent.

The guardian had gone on ahead, alone, toward the Worldroot. *Rest tonight,* she'd said. *Tomorrow you'll want your legs and your wits, and I promise you'll only get to choose one.*

So they rested. Ilsa built the fire with the grim economy of a woman who had done it ten thousand times in ten thousand bad places, stacking wet wood until it caught anyway through what Wren suspected was sheer moral disapproval. Pip produced three fish from somewhere inside his coat — "the river owed me" — and skewered them with a thorn he claimed was ceremonial.

Wren sat closest to the flames and tried not to think about the morning.

It didn't work.

"She knew," she said finally, to no one. "The guardian. She knew what it asks before we got there. She's known for nine hundred years, apparently."

"That's the job," Pip said around a fish bone. "Guardians know things. It's why they're insufferable."

"What do you think it will ask?"

Pip went still in the particular way fae-blooded people could go still, like a cat deciding whether the moving thing was prey or weather. He looked at the fire instead of at her. "Bridge-child," he said gently, using the name the forest used, the one that made her skin itch every time, "you already know. You've known since the waystation letter. You just haven't let yourself finish the sentence."

"I finished the sentence."

"You finished half of it." He pointed the ceremonial thorn at her. "The sentence is: someone who belongs to both realms has to die. The other half is: who do you know that belongs to both realms? Because I've met exactly one, and she's sitting too close to my fish."

"Pip," Ilsa said.

"He asked— she asked!"

"She didn't ask anything," Wren said. "I asked."

"Well." He shrugged, elaborate and unconvincing, and retreated into his coat collar. Wren loved him a little for the cruelty of it and hated him more.

* * *

Ilsa waited until Pip had curled up against the root wall and gone into the shallow, watchful sleep of smugglers. Then she fed the fire another branch and sat forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. Ten years older than the knight Wren might have imagined from stories; ten years harder than the one the stories would have kept.

"You should sleep," Ilsa said.

"So should you."

"I don't, much." A pause. "You've been turning something over since the threshold. Since the guardian told us to rest. Say it, or it'll say itself at the worst possible time. That's how truth works. It waits for audiences."

Wren pulled her knees up. "What happens tomorrow. When it names its price. What are you going to do?"

Ilsa didn't answer immediately, and Wren realized she was watching her hands, those scarred, steady hands, as if the answer were written there and she needed to read it before speaking it.

"My oath was to the covenant," Ilsa said at last. "Not to Corvane, not to the crown, not even to the Worldroot itself. To the covenant — the thing that keeps the veil standing and the woods out of the wheat fields and the wells sweet. I swore it at nineteen. I meant it at nineteen. I mean it now, or some stubborn piece of me does." She looked up. "If reforging it requires a price, then the price gets paid. That's order, Wren. That's the whole of it. Somebody pays so everyone else doesn't have to."

"And if the somebody is me?"

"Then I hate it," Ilsa said simply, "and I stand there while you do it, because I have watched what happens when nobody stands anywhere. I held a dying man's post for six hours once because the man who should have relieved me deserted, and I can tell you exactly what the village downriver looked like after the veil frayed over their fields. Black stalks to the horizon. A well that smelled like grief. Children—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Order is not justice. I know that better than most. But order is the floor under everything, and when the floor goes, justice falls with it."

"It's a leash." Wren heard her own voice come out flat and hard. "You heard the guardian. Nine hundred years ago mortals lost a war they barely survived, so they didn't make peace — they made a cage and called it a treaty. Every charm I ever sold was a link in that chain. My whole life, I thought I was keeping a lamp lit. I was keeping a lock oiled." She shook her head. "And you want to forge a new chain, and pay for it in blood, and call the bleeding 'order.'"

"I want there to be a tomorrow where Pip can sell his river-fish and you can sell your charms and neither of you hangs for it." Ilsa's voice stayed level, but Wren saw the effort of the levelness, the way a wall stays standing by refusing to admit the wind exists. "Justice is a fine cathedral, girl. I've never worshipped in one. I've slept in ditches outside them. Give me a crooked hut that stands over a palace that's falling."

"A peace built on chains isn't a hut. It's a dungeon with good acoustics."

"Then build me a better one by morning," Ilsa said, "because tomorrow the guardian is going to name a price, and you're going to be the coin, and I need to know that when I put my hand on your shoulder and walk you forward, it's because there was no other way — not because I wanted the world tidy." Her voice cracked, just once, hairline-thin. "Because I did that once already. Stood aside. Let a thing die because the order required it. And I will tell you, Wren Zhou, that the order did not thank me. It never does. It just takes, and takes, and calls it necessary."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the oak's dead crown, something small moved — a bird, or a leaf deciding to be a bird, out here it was hard to say.

"I'm not going to be necessary," Wren said quietly. "I'm going to be chosen. There's a difference, and I intend to find out what it is before anyone walks me anywhere."

Isla looked at her a long moment. Something in the knight's shoulders came down half an inch — not surrender, but the end of an argument she hadn't wanted to win.

"All right," she said. "Chosen. Find out what that means. And if it turns out it means walking forward—" 

"Then you'll hate it and stand there. I know. You're very clear."

A ghost of warmth crossed the ruined landscape of Ilsa Marrow's face. "One of us has to be."

* * *

Sleep came for Wren in pieces, and between the pieces, the forest talked. Not in words — in warmth. The ground beneath her held heat like a hand; the roots overhead creaked in rhythms almost like rocking. The Thornwood was glad she was here. That was somehow the worst part. The whole vast green dark of it curled around her like a dog around a returned owner, and it had no idea — or worse, every idea — about what tomorrow cost.

She woke before dawn to the smell of bread.

That was wrong. There was no bread within a hundred miles. There was certainly no bread being baked by the figure now standing at the edge of the firelight, wrapped in a road-dusted traveling cloak, holding a cloth bundle out before him like an offering at a shrine.

Guildmaster Odell Voss had aged ten years in ten days. His beard had gone fully white at the edges; his hands, always so precise with ledger and ladle, trembled around the bundle. But his eyes found Wren across the ashes of the fire with the same terrible gentleness they'd had when he'd pulled her off a doorstep at age four and said, *well then, little bird, I suppose you're ours.*

"No," Wren said.

"I brought breakfast," Voss said. "You always liked the honey ones. I remember."

"You followed us through the veil. Through Ashgrave. Through all of it." She stood slowly, and felt Ilsa rise behind her, hand drifting toward steel. Pip was awake too — she could feel him listening with his whole body. "How?"

"The courier's blood opens doors," Voss said. "I'm sorry. I took it from his coat before they burned him. I've been three days behind you, eating badly and apologizing to trees." He attempted a smile; it collapsed. "The trees accepted the apologies. They're very gracious. It's unsettling."

"Why are you here?" Ilsa's voice was iron.

"Because I owe a confession I couldn't put in a letter, and because—" His voice broke and reassembled. "Because tomorrow the covenant will ask for her, and I am the only person alive who knows the full design, and I need her to say yes."

The word dropped into the hollow like a stone into a well.

"You wrote me a letter," Wren said. Each word placed carefully, the way you set down something that might explode. "You told me the truth. Delivered through the veil. Living bridge. Raised as insurance. Do you remember what I did with the letter?"

"You read it aloud. Then you didn't burn it. You folded it small and put it next to your heart, which nearly killed me, because I'd hoped you'd burn it."

"And now you've walked into the last free night of my life to ask me to die for a leash."

"To die for the world." Voss set the bread down on a stone, deliberately, like a man disarming himself. "Wren. Little bird. Sit. Please. Hear the whole design before you decide I'm a monster, and hear it knowing that I have loved you since you bit me at four years old for trying to teach you letters."

"I was right to bite you."

"You were. I've thought so for years." He sat down heavily on the far side of the dead fire, uninvited, and Ilsa's hand closed on nothing because striking him seemed suddenly beside the point. "Here is the design, then. The covenant was forged by nine mortals and seven fae who were tired of war. Yes, it became a leash — the guardian told you true. But leashes were all they could agree on, because both sides were grieving, and grieving creatures bargain badly. For nine hundred years the leash held, and under its protection, mortals forgot the war and fae forgot the sky. Generations were born who never knew there'd ever been a choice. That's not nothing, Wren. That's the floor Ilsa talks about. Crooked, ugly, but standing."

"And the anchors."

"Dying. All of them. Your charms, the boundary stones, the pilgrim roads. The covenant was designed to renew itself through sacrifice at long intervals, and the intervals shortened each time, because each sacrifice was less willing than the last, and unwilling payment buys less. The founders knew this would happen eventually. They built one final provision." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet and steady and unbearable. "A bridge-child. Born of one realm, delivered wholly into the other. Someone whose belonging was never divided, only displaced. Such a soul, given freely, could renew the covenant for a thousand years. A real thousand years. Long enough for the world to grow wise enough to find a better way."

"There is no better way coming!" The words tore out of her before she knew they were loaded. "Voss — Odell — you raised me on lies and sold me a childhood made of cellar dust and protection coin, and now you want me to believe the same institution that hid the truth for nine hundred years has a plan that ends in wisdom? It ends in me. It has always ended in me. You named me insurance when I was an infant!"

"Yes." He didn't flinch. That was the worst thing — he took it full in the face, unblinking. "Yes, I did. And I have hated myself in seventeen languages and zero comforts for twenty years. But hear this much, because it's the only clean thing I own: I fought it. Year after year, I delayed. I sold anchors slow. I let the covenant fray rather than rush you toward the altar. Every year of your life was a year I stole from the design, and I stole them gladly, because I wanted you to have birthdays, not purpose." His hands flattened on his knees. "But the fraying is past stealing-from now. The wells are bitter. The woods walk. In a season, maybe two, the veil tears wide, and everything on either side of it pours into everything else, and it will not be wonder, Wren. I've seen a tear. I saw one, once, as a young man. I still sleep with a lamp lit."

The hollow was silent. Even the forest seemed to lean in.

"If I refuse," Wren said, "what do you do?"

"I grieve you," Voss said, "for whatever years the tearing leaves me. And I keep selling charms, and I start telling the truth about them, and I spend what's left of my life teaching Corvane that magic was never the crime — cowardice was. That's the vow I made on the road here. Whatever you choose, I'll serve the choosing." He smiled, wrecked and honest. "I'm not asking you to obey me anymore, little bird. I'm asking you to save the world. It's the same request I've made since you were four. You bit me then, too."

Above the hollow, dawn began to move through the canopy — not light yet, but the green hush before it, the forest holding its breath.

And out of that hush, from beyond the fallen oak, came a voice old and brown-eyed and sorry:

"Come, then, all of you. The Root is waking early. It smelled an argument."

* * *

They climbed out of the hollow into a morning that wasn't quite morning, following the guardian along a path that assembled itself ahead of them out of bracken and apology. Voss walked beside Wren, close enough to touch, not touching. Behind them, Ilsa's boots kept soldier-time, steady as a drum. Pip drifted at the rear humming something in a language with no honest translation.

"Wren," Voss murmured. "Whatever you decide in there — the honey buns are yours regardless. Consider it a bribe with no conditions. The last gift I'll ever give you as guildmaster."

"And the first as what?"

He considered the question with the seriousness he'd once applied to teaching her letters, back when biting him was still on the table.

"As whatever you decide I am," he said. "That's rather the point of today, isn't it?"

Ahead, the trees opened like a gate, and the light of the Worldroot rose through the ground — flickering, guttering, alive — and Wren felt her own blood answer it, bridge and bell, key and door.

The guardian stopped at the threshold and turned.

"It knows you're here," she said. "All of you. It's been waiting a long time for this conversation." Her ancient eyes moved from face to face, and settled last on Wren. "Remember what I told you. No one in nine hundred years has believed they were allowed to say no."

She stepped aside, leaving the path bare.

"Be the first."
