## 17. Ashgrave's Choice

Inquisitor Tomas Ashgrave had spent thirty-one years believing in exactly three things: that the Ledger of Corvane was honest, that the pyres kept people safe, and that magic was a debt that always came due — and when it came due, it came due in blood.

He had watched the first belief die in a market square two weeks ago, when a courier named Ellery Vance had bled out on the cobbles rather than give up the name of the person he'd passed a map to. Ashgrave had turned out Vance's pockets himself afterward. He had found a locket with his daughter's portrait in it, a half-eaten apple, and nothing else. No payment. No contract. A man who had run himself to death for no reason Ashgrave could find in any ledger.

That was the trouble with magic. It made people do things that made no sense. That was what he had always told himself.

The second belief had died somewhere on the pilgrim road, in the dark, while he pressed a cloth soaked in a dead man's blood against his own palm and felt the veil part like a curtain that had been waiting for him all his life.

The third belief was dying now, here, at the threshold of the Worldroot, and it was taking everything else down with it.

* * *

He had followed them through the Thornwood for six days. He knew this because he had counted the nights by the way the sky refused to darken properly — the forest kept its own hours here, a perpetual amber dusk, like the inside of a lantern. He had eaten the last of his rations on the third day. On the fourth, he had found a spring and drunk from it, and then stood there for a long moment waiting to feel cursed or changed or dead, and had felt only less thirsty.

That was the other thing nobody had ever told him about the Thornwood. It didn't attack you. It just *let* you in, which was somehow worse.

On the fifth night, something had brought him bread. He never learned what. He'd woken at dawn with a loaf on his bedroll of moss, still warm, and he had sat holding it like a live coal before eating every crumb. His mother would have called it witchcraft. His mother had also once blamed a bad harvest on a neighbor's cow looking at her funny, so perhaps his lineage of rigorous thinking had limits.

And now, on the sixth day, the trees simply opened, and there they were.

The threshold was not what he expected. He had imagined — he did not know what. An altar. A wound. Something that matched thirty years of woodcut illustrations showing snarling beasts and writhing shadows. Instead there was a hollow in the earth like an amphitheater of roots, and above it arched the largest living thing Ashgrave had ever seen: the Worldroot itself, a trunk wide as a cathedral, its bark silver-grey and peeling in long curls, its canopy lost in the amber light above.

It was dying. That much was unmistakable even to a man who had never seen a tree larger than an orchard apple. Whole limbs hung bare and grey. The bark near its base was cracked, and through the cracks seeped a light that guttered — actually guttered, like a candle in a draft — pale gold one moment, nearly dark the next.

Kneeling at its base was the guardian.

Ashgrave had read about guardians in confiscated texts. Old things, bound to their charges, sustained by the covenant. The illustrations showed them as majestic. This one looked like a woman who had been kneeling for ten years, because she had been. Her skin was the grey of the peeling bark. Her hair had gone white at the temples though her face was young. When she lifted her head and saw him, she did not cry out or bare teeth. She said, in a voice worn thin as old paper:

"Another one. Come to see."

And Ashgrave felt something in his chest turn over, because he recognized that voice. He had heard it in the mouths of prisoners. He had heard it, God help him, coming out of his own reflection some mornings.

* * *

"Wren Zhou." He stepped into the amphitheater of roots, hand nowhere near his sword yet, because that was how you did it — calm first, steel later, and mostly you never needed the steel if your voice held steady. "You'll come with me. The others too. There are cells in Corvane with better ventilation than most, and I have it within my discretion—"

"You followed us through the veil alone," said the knight, Ilsa Marrow, rising with the unhurried economy of a woman who had done violence professionally for twenty years and saw no reason to rush it now. "Without backup. Without a writ. Into fae territory."

"I don't need a writ to arrest a charm-seller and her accomplices."

"No," Marrow agreed pleasantly. "But you might need more than one of you."

"Ashgrave." Wren Zhou got to her feet. Up close she was smaller than her file suggested. Twenty-two years old, five foot nothing in muddy boots, ink stains on her fingers, a face that had learned to be unremarkable so thoroughly it had forgotten how to be anything else. Only her eyes gave him trouble. They were frightened, yes — good, proper, sensible fear — but underneath the fear was something worse. Pity.

People did not look at Inquisitor Ashgrave with pity. They looked at him with fear or hatred or, in the case of the Corvane constabulary, relief that someone else was willing to do the necessary work.

"Why is she chained to that tree?" Wren asked.

"She isn't chained. She's bound. It's different."

"How?"

"The covenant sustains her, and she sustains the covenant. It's in the old texts. The guardian serves willingly—"

"Do I?" said the guardian, mildly, as if asking whether it might rain.

Ashgrave stopped.

"The texts say—" he began.

"Yes," said the guardian. "I know what the texts say. My husband wrote some of them. Lovely penmanship, Aldous. Terrible habit of leaving out the parts where people scream." She settled back against the root. "Come here, Inquisitor. You've walked six days through my wood and eaten my bread. The least I can do is show you what you've come to save."

"This is a trick," Ashgrave said. He said it loudly, to the whole clearing, because saying it loudly helped. "Guardians are bound by oath and covenant. This is fae theater. They show you suffering to loosen your resolve—"

"Marrow," said the guardian, "hit him lightly and drag him over, would you? Some men need to be dragged."

"I'm not going to hit him," Marrow said. "Probably."

"He's going to kill us all, Ilsa."

"After," Marrow said, and there was something in her voice Ashgrave hadn't heard from her before — something almost gentle. "Let him look first. You wanted someone to look. So did I, once."

* * *

They did not drag him, in the end. He went on his own feet, which he would think about for a long time afterward — that his legs carried him forward when his doctrine screamed at him to stand fast. Perhaps it was the guttering light. Perhaps it was the bread. Perhaps it was simply that thirty-one years is a long time to hold a lamp against the wind, and a man gets tired, and tiredness looks a great deal like doubt from the outside.

She put his hand on the crack in the bark.

That was all. She guided his palm — rough bark, warm bark, bark with a pulse under it, slow and arrhythmic, like a heart forgetting its own count — and she put his hand there, and she let go.

"What am I feeling," he said. Not a question. A demand for contradiction. Give me a trick, a glamour, a wire under the bark, anything, and I will thank you for it and burn this place and sleep tonight.

"You're feeling a leash," said the guardian, "on a very old neck."

Beneath his palm, the light pulsed, and with each pulse he felt — not saw, *felt*, the way you feel a sob in your own throat — the shape of it. The covenant wasn't threads woven between realms, as the diagrams showed. It was a collar of thorns driven into the root of a living world, and the Worldroot wore it, and the guardian wore the Worldroot, and every anchor charm sold in every market of Corvane was a link in the chain, renewed by mortal hands, generation after generation, by people who mostly believed they were selling luck.

"We fought a war," the guardian said. Her voice had gone distant, recitation-soft, the cadence of grief told so often it has worn smooth. "A long time ago. Mortals and fae both did unforgivable things; that is generally how wars go. And when it ended, your ancestors were afraid. Fear is honest, Inquisitor. I don't blame them for being afraid. But they took their fear and they forged it into iron and they called it peace." The light guttered. "Every hundred years, the collar needs tightening. Every tightening needs a soul that belongs to both sides of the wall they built. Do you know how many of those children there have been?"

"How many," Ashgrave said. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"Nine," said the guardian. "Wren would be the tenth. Each one delivered over as an infant by one side or the other, raised in secret, taught to be small, taught to be grateful, and led here at last to kneel where I kneel. Willingly. It must be willing. That was the cruelty dressed up as mercy — they made certain the children *believed*. Gave them purpose. Called it an honor." She paused. "I was not one of them. I was simply standing too close, a thousand years ago, and I said yes to something without reading the terms. Which is also generally how wars go."

Behind him, Ashgrave heard Wren Zhou say quietly, "You don't have to watch this," and realized she was talking to him. Offering *him* mercy. Standing at the edge of the thing designed to kill her, and offering the man sent to arrest her a chance to look away.

His certainty did not crack all at once. Certainties never do; that would be cleaner. It flaked, the way bark flakes, in long curling strips.

* * *

He thought of Ellery Vance's apple.

That was the thing that finished it, in the end — not the dying tree, not the ancient chained woman, but a half-eaten apple in a dead courier's pocket. Because Ashgrave had built thirty years of doctrine on the premise that magic was a corruption, that everyone touched by it was either predator or prey, that the ledger always balanced. And Vance had died for a stranger with no payment, no contract, no sense at all — which meant the ledger didn't balance, had never balanced, and the arithmetic he'd used to sign nine death warrants in his career was wrong at the root.

Or — the colder, kinder thought, the one that arrived like dawn and hurt considerably more — the arithmetic was right, but it had been counting the wrong currency. Magic didn't create blood debts. People did, and then blamed the magic, the way his mother had blamed the cow.

"Inquisitor." Wren Zhou again. She had come around to stand between him and the guardian, arms folded, chin up, doing her best impression of someone brave, which was exactly as convincing as his own had ever been. "Say what happens next. Whatever it is, say it plainly. I've had enough of people deciding my life in whispers."

He looked at her. Then at Marrow, whose hand rested on her sword in a manner that managed to be simultaneously threatening and apologetic. Then at the smuggler — Pip — perched on a root, watching Ashgrave with the frank professional assessment of a man pricing his options, several of which appeared to involve running.

Then at the guardian, who was smiling at him. Not kindly. Knowing. The smile of someone who had watched ten centuries of men arrive at crossroads.

"My orders," Ashgrave said slowly, "are to take you back to Corvane alive. Dead is acceptable. Alive is preferred." He drew a breath. "My orders assume the covenant is a treaty worth preserving at the cost of one girl. I can no longer certify that assumption. And an Inquisitor who cannot certify his assumptions..." He sheathed his sword, and the sound of it was louder than he expected. "An Inquisitor who cannot certify his assumptions has no authority to do anything at all except step aside."

"You're lying," Pip said. "No offense. It's just, we've been running from you for a month, and people don't stop being the thing they've been for thirty years because of one sad tree."

"Pip," said Marrow.

"No, it's a fair question," Ashgrave said. He found, to his surprise, that he almost smiled. "It's a fair question and the answer is: I don't know. Perhaps I'll wake tomorrow and be myself again. Perhaps I'll write it all down in my report and burn it. But I find I cannot lead you back in chains past a woman chained to a root and tell myself the difference is that mine is legal." He moved aside, off the path to the threshold, and gestured at it with something that was not quite a bow. "Go. All of you. If anyone asks, I was overcome by fae sorcery. It's what I'd report anyway. It's tidier."

Wren Zhou looked at him for a long moment. "Why?" she asked. Not accusing. Genuinely asking.

"Because thirty-one years ago I watched my father die of a fever, and a hedge-witch offered to help, and my mother turned her away at the door, and I have spent my entire life since then building a philosophy out of that closed door." He shrugged, uncomfortable. "It seemed very solid at the time."

* * *

He watched them descend toward the threshold. He heard Wren speak to the covenant's heart, heard the terms laid out, heard the price named — a willing soul belonging to both realms — and he understood with terrible clarity what was about to happen, and he understood that she understood, and he stood aside anyway, because that had been the choice, and choices have edges.

What he did not anticipate was the covenant noticing.

The light in the cracks flared — white, hard, furious, nothing like the warm guttering gold — and the whole amphitheater shuddered, roots groaning, bark splitting, and a voice that was not a voice but a pressure, a verdict, rolled up through the ground and through his boots and into his bones:

**THE LEASH DOES NOT LOOSEN. THE LEASH DOES NOT CHOOSE.**

"It chose," Ashgrave said through his teeth. "I chose."

**THEN BE CHOSEN FOR.**

He felt it take hold of the mercy in him — literally felt it, a cold hook behind his sternum, hauling on the exact place where he had softened — and he had time for exactly one coherent thought, which was not of Corvane, or the Ledger, or the pyres, or even his mother and her cow, but of a warm loaf of bread on a bedroll of moss, and how he had never once, in thirty-one years, been given anything by the world he had sworn to protect.

"Ashgrave!" Wren's voice, sharp with horror.

He waved her off — toward the threshold, downward, onward — with the last strength in his arm, and he was distantly pleased to see Marrow bodily hauling the girl back, because someone had to keep the girl moving, and it was gratifying to know the exiled knight still remembered her actual oath.

The light rose around him like water. It did not hurt, precisely. It was more like being unwritten — like a clerk drawing a line through a name, tidy and final, and Ashgrave, who had spent his life on the side of the pen, discovered with a strange, cold wonder what it felt like to be the page.

*The last thing he saw* was Wren Zhou turning at the threshold's mouth, tears cutting through the dirt on her face, screaming his name — his actual name, Tomas, which almost no one living had used in years —

*and the last thing he thought* was that she should stop wasting breath on him and go make the world honest,

*and the last thing the world heard him say* was:

"Tell them the door was closed. Tell them I opened it."

Then the covenant's judgment fell, and the amber light of the Thornwood went out like a pinched wick —

— and in the sudden dark, beneath the Worldroot, something vast and old and desperate began, very softly, to laugh.

Because for the first time in a thousand years, it had felt a hand on the collar that was not trying to tighten it.

And it had remembered what hands were *for*.
