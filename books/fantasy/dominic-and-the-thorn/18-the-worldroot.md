## 18. The Worldroot

The path down was not a path. It was more of an invitation.

The Thornwood had spent three days rearranging itself around them — trees sliding their roots like old men shifting in chairs, trails unspooling ahead and rolling up behind — but here, at the lip of the great hollow, it simply stopped pretending. The ground opened beneath the Worldroot like a throat, and a stair of living wood spiraled down into a light that wasn't sunlight and wasn't torchlight but something older and quieter, the color of honey held up to a dying fire.

Wren stood at the edge and looked down, and her stomach did the thing it always did before she haggled with someone bigger than her: a cold little flip, quickly smothered.

"Well," said Pip at her shoulder, "on the bright side, if this goes badly, we won't have to walk back up."

"That is not the bright side," Ilsa said.

"It's *a* side."

Wren laughed. It came out thin and strange, but it came out, and both of them looked at her like they'd been waiting to hear it. Three days of silence and thorns and Ashgrave's blood still drying on the leaves somewhere above, and here she was laughing at Pip's terrible jokes. Grief did that, she was learning. It made room for absurdity the way a full cellar makes room for nothing else.

She went first. Of course she went first. She'd been going first since she was nine years old, when she learned that if you walked into a room confidently enough, people assumed you belonged there and didn't ask questions. Ten years of being small and unnoticed had taught her every trick of seeming larger than she was.

The stair took longer than it should have. Time pooled in the Thornwood — Pip had warned them, and Pip had been right about everything so far, which Wren found deeply annoying — so that each step felt like a week and also no time at all. By the twentieth step she had forgotten the sun. By the fortieth she had forgotten Corvane's smell, which was smoke and fish and fear, and could only remember that she had once hated it, without being able to recall the shape of the hatred.

By the last step, she remembered everything at once, all pressed together: Voss's hands steadying hers over a carving knife when she was seven. The courier's blood soaking through the wax seal. Her own name, spoken by the forest as she crossed the veil — *wren* — like it was reading a ledger it had kept for nineteen years.

The hollow at the bottom was vast and round, ribbed with root-arches that rose out of sight into darkness. And at its center, beneath the great root itself — thick as a cathedral pillar, ancient beyond sense, its bark silvered and split — hung the covenant's heart.

It looked, Wren thought, exactly like a lamp about to gutter.

It was a knot of light suspended in a cage of thorn, no bigger than a lantern, and it flickered. That was the wrong word for it, she realized as she drew closer; a flame flickers because it is fighting the air. This light stuttered because it was forgetting how to burn. Between one pulse and the next there was a darkness that felt like held breath, and then the light would remember itself, weakly, the way a tired man remembers his own name when someone shouts it.

"You're dying," she said aloud. Her voice fell into the hollow and did not echo. The hollow swallowed it, politely.

*We are,* said the heart, and the voice was not a voice. It arrived behind her eyes, in the marrow of her teeth, in the place where she had always felt her charms warming against her palm. It sounded exhausted. It sounded, absurdly, like Voss on a bad night, tallying accounts he knew were short. *We have been dying for ten years. You have been carrying our last anchors in your pockets and selling them for coppers.*

"I sold them for *fair* prices," Wren said automatically, and then heard herself, and wanted to sit down. Somewhere above, faint as memory, she thought she heard Pip snort.

Footsteps behind her. Ilsa came down the last steps with her sword drawn — of course it was drawn; Ilsa's sword had been drawn for approximately a decade — and planted herself at Wren's left. Pip drifted to her right, hands conspicuously empty, which for Pip meant he was terrified or planning something, possibly both.

"Is that it, then?" Ilsa asked quietly. "The covenant."

*It is what remains of it.* The light pulsed. In its stutter, Wren saw things in the dark between beats — she couldn't say whether they were visions or memories leaking from a failing thing. A war. Brambles marching on a city of white walls. A treaty signed in blood that was supposed to be ink. Chains shaped like promises.

"Tell us the price," Ilsa said. She said it like a soldier asking for orders, jaw set, eyes forward. Wren heard the decade of guilt underneath it. *I was ordered to let the guardian die and I obeyed.* She'd confessed it three nights ago by a fire that burned green, and Wren had not known what to say, and Ilsa hadn't expected her to. Some things you carried aloud so someone else knew the weight.

*You know the price,* the heart said gently. *You have always known. It is why you walked ahead of her the whole way down.*

Ilsa's knuckles whitened on the hilt. "That isn't—"

*A willing soul,* the covenant said, ignoring her, or perhaps being kind to her, Wren couldn't tell. *One who belongs fully to both realms. One foot in each world, given freely, so that the door may be barred from both sides. This has been the price since the first forging. There is no other door, and no other key.*

The words settled over the hollow like snow.

Wren found that she was not surprised. She had been not-surprised since the waystation, since Voss's letter read aloud in Pip's flat smuggler's cadence while her whole childhood rearranged itself around her ears. *Not abandoned — delivered.* A living bridge. Insurance. She had wept until she had no water left in her and then she had gotten up in the morning and walked, because that was what she did. She survived by being small and moving forward.

And now the covenant had finally said it out loud, and the saying of it landed on her not like a blade but like a receipt. An account come due.

"Me," she said. "You want me."

*We do not want,* the heart said. *Wanting is for the living. We require. There is a difference, and it is the entire tragedy of us.*

Pip made a sound beside her — half laugh, half choke. "Did the world's oldest magical contract just get *sardonic* at us?"

"It's dying," Wren said. "Dying things stop wasting words." She knew this. She had watched the courier die. Dying men don't waste breath on comfort.

She took a step forward.

She heard Ilsa move — heard the scrape of armor, the sharp inhale, the beginning of *Wren, wait* — and she raised one hand, not looking back, and to her lasting astonishment Ilsa stopped. Perhaps because the hand was steady. Wren's hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook, privately, where no one could bill her for it.

Another step. The light grew. Its warmth touched her face, and it felt like the warmth of her charms — every charm she had ever sold, every coin she'd ever palmed, every morning she'd stood in a cold market telling lies with a smile. It felt like home, which was the cruelest thing it could have done, because home had never once been hers. It had been rented. Custody. An arrangement.

One more step and she would be close enough to touch the thorn-cage. Close enough to say yes.

She stopped.

It wasn't courage that stopped her, and it wasn't fear. It was something she'd learned at a market stall at fourteen, haggling with a fat wool merchant who tried the oldest trick in Corvane: bury the real price under a pile of generous-sounding numbers. *Free delivery, madam, a gift with purchase, loyalty discount* — and folded small into the middle of it, the actual cost, tripled.

Wren had walked away from that sale. Voss had been furious. *You don't walk away from coin,* he'd said, and she'd said, *You don't buy anything until you've counted what's actually in your purse,* and she had been right, and he had hated being reminded.

She counted now.

She looked at the guttering heart, and she made herself see past its warmth the way she'd made herself see past the merchant's smile, and there it was. Folded small in the middle of all that generous light.

Every sacrifice before her.

She didn't know how she saw it. Maybe it was the bridge in her blood; maybe the dying covenant had no strength left to hide its ledgers. But she saw — clearly, terribly, the way you see a room by lightning-flash — the ones who had come before. Not names. Shapes. A girl with river-reeds braided in her hair, centuries ago, stepping into this same light with this same terrible calm. A boy who had been *both*, whatever *both* had meant then, walking down these stairs while his mothers were told it was an honor. Another. Another. Each one belonging to two worlds and therefore owned by neither. Each one told the price was noble, necessary, the only key. Each one willing — oh, they had been willing, that was the horror of it, the covenant only accepted the willing, so it had become very good, over a very long time, at making children willing.

And every single time, Wren understood now, standing in the cold math of it — every single time, the door had been barred again. The leash reforged. The cage rebuilt with a new lock, keyed to a new child, and the worlds set apart again, and the grief set aside again, unpaid, compounding like debt in a hidden ledger, until here they were. Ten years of dying anchors. A forest walking closer to villages. A well gone bitter. A courier cut down in a market street with a map in his hands.

Ten years ago, a knight had been ordered to let a guardian die, and had obeyed, because the covenant demanded obedience even unto its own collapse. The system ate its keepers too. It always had.

"Wren?" Ilsa's voice, very quiet. Careful, the way you speak near a spooked animal. Or near a woman deciding something.

"I'm fine," Wren said. "I'm counting."

"Counting?"

"In my purse." She almost laughed. Of all the moments for Voss's market lessons to save the world. "There's a trick to it. When someone tells you the price is simple, that's when you count twice."

She turned, slowly, to look at the two of them — Ilsa rigid with dread, Pip pale and gripping a knife he must have stolen from her pack, the absolute rat — and she saw, laid over their faces, the same pattern she'd just seen in the covenant's memory. Everyone in her life, arranging themselves around a sacrifice. Voss raising her as insurance. The fae court offering welcome with teeth. Ilsa obeying a monstrous order ten years ago because the order came wrapped in necessity. Even Ashgrave, burning witches out of sincere belief that magic killed — even he was part of it, the great machine that solved the problem of two frightened worlds by finding a child who belonged to both and spending her.

Everyone had always been perfectly willing to let her pay. They'd just wanted her to volunteer.

"I know what you're going to say," Wren said to Ilsa. "You're going to say it has to be done. Order over justice. Bar the door, keep the peace, one life against two worlds. I've heard the argument. I've *made* the argument, in my head, on the road, every night since the letter. It's a good argument. It's the best argument anyone ever made to a girl with reeds in her hair, and it worked, for six hundred years, it worked every time—"

Her voice cracked. She breathed.

"—and I am not going to be the next bead on that string."

The heart's light stuttered, and in the darkness between pulses, Wren felt it listening.

*The alternative is the end of both worlds,* it said, and there was something in its tone she hadn't heard before. Not threat. Hope, maybe, or the memory of hope — the way you mention a thing you gave up believing in, to see who flinches.

"Then I'll find another way," Wren said. "Or I'll die trying, which — fair warning — is a distinct possibility, I've read the plan and honestly the plan is bad."

"The plan is *terrible*," Pip confirmed hoarsely.

"But it will be *my* plan," Wren said, turning back to the light, "and it won't be paid for by a child who was delivered through a veil and raised in a cellar and told her whole life that she was spare change. Six hundred years of sacrifices bought you six hundred years of borrowed time, and look at the interest. No. I'm done paying your prices. From here on, we negotiate."

Silence in the hollow. Real silence — the kind that comes after a thing hears something it has not heard in six centuries.

Then, softly, terribly, the covenant began to laugh. It was not a cruel sound. It was the sound of a very old, very tired merchant realizing, at last, that the customer had read the ledger.

*Negotiate,* it repeated. *With what, bridge-child? You stand at my threshold with a broken knight, a thief, and a spark of borrowed fire. What could you possibly offer that outweighs the end of everything?*

Wren reached into her coat.

Her fingers found the familiar shape — worn smooth, warm even now, warm always, warmer than any trinket had a right to be. The first charm she had ever made, at seven years old, with Voss's hands over hers. He'd told her it was a luck charm. She'd believed him for thirteen years. She had sold hundreds like it, and kept this one, and never known why she couldn't let it go.

She knew now. It wasn't an anchor. It had never been an anchor.

It was a promise — the oldest kind, the kind made before either world taught anyone to lie — and it hummed against her palm like something waking.

"I'll offer you the one thing your covenant was built to make sure nobody ever offered," Wren Zhou said, and stepped forward, not toward the sacrifice, but past it, straight up to the thorn-cage's edge, chin up, the way you walk into a room when you intend to belong there.

"I'll offer you the truth."

Above them, far above, at the mouth of the stair, the darkness began to fill with the sound of measured footsteps, and cold light, and the slow, deliberate breathing of a man who had followed them all this way and had just seen the mercy in his own heart — and the covenant felt him coming, and its laughter died.

*Too late,* it whispered. *He is here. Choose quickly, Wren. Everything ends at dawn — or sooner.*
