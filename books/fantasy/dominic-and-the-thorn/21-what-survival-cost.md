## 21. What Survival Cost

### I. Wren

The first thing Wren learned about being ordinary was that mornings still came.

She had expected, in the fevered days after the Worldroot, that the world might simply stop — that a girl who had poured ten years of borrowed magic into breaking an ancient covenant would be permitted some grand cessation, some curtain. But no. The sun rose over Dominic's Ford, thin and gray and indifferent, and her hands were just hands, and when she reached for the place inside her where the warmth had lived, she found only quiet. A hearth swept cold.

She missed it the way you miss a tooth. Not constantly. Just at odd moments — reaching for bread, or laughing at something Pip said, or watching Ilsa sharpen a knife by firelight with the particular concentration of a woman who had once sworn oaths with that same focus. Her fingers would twitch toward the hollow, and find nothing, and she would breathe through it like a cramp.

"You're staring at your hands again," Pip said. He was sprawled on the inn's bench with his boots up, all elbows and fox-colored hair, looking as though he'd never carried grief anywhere in his life. This was a lie so well-constructed that Wren sometimes envied it.

"I'm admiring them," Wren said. "They're very clean. Do you know how long it's been since my hands were this clean? Ten years of charm-grime, gone in a fortnight."

"Tragic. You were becoming one with the grime."

"I was *at peace* with the grime."

Ilsa snorted from the corner without looking up from her whetstone. That was the other thing about being ordinary: you noticed how much of your life was people making small sounds at you to say they were there.

* * *

The fae court came for her on the ninth morning.

Not all of it — a delegation, three tall figures in woven green who walked into the Ford as if the town had been built around their arrival rather than the reverse. The mortals of Dominic's Ford bowed and scraped and got out of the way, because everyone knew what these three were, and everyone remembered — vividly, recently — what happened when the veil tore.

Wren stood up from her breakfast and felt every inch the fraud: a magic-less woman in a patched coat, facing down the Court of Brambles' finest.

The tallest of them, a woman whose antlers were hung with tiny bells that made no sound at all, looked at Wren for a long moment. Then she knelt.

The whole square went silent. Ilsa's hand went to her sword. Pip made a sound like a kettle deciding whether to scream.

"Rise," Wren said faintly. "Please. Everyone stop kneeling, this is — I sold *charms*. Bad ones. Mostly."

"We know what you are," the antlered woman said, rising. "You are the one who unmade the cage. You are owed a debt the Court does not enjoy owing." Her mouth curved, showing teeth that were slightly too many. "So we pay it in the only coin we have. Guest-right, Wren Zhou. Under branch and bramble, under moon and mushroom-ring: any door of the Thornwood opens to you. Any table feeds you. Any fire warms you. For as long as you live, and a day beyond if you ask nicely."

The words settled over Wren like a cloak she hadn't asked for but couldn't refuse. She felt them take hold — not magic, exactly, nothing so simple. More like a promise being written somewhere permanent.

"That's generous," she managed.

"It's humiliating," the fae woman agreed cheerfully. "We prefer it that way. It keeps us honest." She turned to go, then paused. "The river-fae send a message. They say the smuggler paid twice, and they are not accustomed to honesty, and it unsettled them. They return what they can."

And she pressed into Wren's palm a single river-stone, smooth and pale, and left.

* * *

"What did she mean, 'paid twice'?" Wren asked that night.

Pip examined the ceiling with tremendous interest.

"Pip."

"He told you not to tell you," Pip said. "Which is difficult grammar, but the point stands."

"*Pip.*"

He sighed, sat up, and pulled his boot off, and from inside the boot — because he was Pip and of course it was in the boot — drew out a small folded scrap of paper covered in cramped handwriting.

"Before we crossed back," he said, "the river gave me a choice. My mother's memory — the real one, whole, restored — or the memory of you. Of us. The road, the sewers, the terrible singing, Ilsa's cooking. All of it. Couldn't have both. River-fae math." He turned the paper over. "So I wrote down everything I could remember about her before I traded it away. Every detail. So she wouldn't be entirely gone."

Wren took the paper with hands that weren't quite steady. It was four pages long. He had written down the shape of a laugh. The smell of bread. The way she'd said his name when he was small — *Fenwick*, not Pip, Fenwick, and Wren understood suddenly why he never used it.

"You chose us," she said. "Over your own mother."

"I chose the person I am now," Pip corrected. "Different thing. She made me; you lot *made me make sense*." He shrugged, and his grin flickered, and beneath it for half a heartbeat was something raw and young. "Besides. I've got four pages. Some smugglers keep worse ledgers."

Wren hugged him, hard, and he let her, which from Pip was worth more than guest-right.

### II. Ilsa

Ser Ilsa Marrow had broken one oath in her life, and it had cost her a banner, a king's favor, and ten years of drinking in towns whose names she'd tried to forget.

She did not intend to break another. She intended, in fact, to do something considerably more alarming.

She waited until evening, until the inn's common room emptied and Wren was mending a coat sleeve by the fire with the fierce concentration of a woman pretending not to be nervous, and then Ilsa knelt. On stone floor, in plain clothes, with no banner and no blade drawn — blade laid flat across both palms instead.

"Oh no," said Wren. "Oh no, get up, we talked about this—"

"We talked about you hating ceremony," Ilsa agreed. "Hear me anyway." Her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. Ten years of calcified shame, and here it was, cracking open like ice in spring. "I held an oath once, to guard the covenant, and when it asked me to do a monstrous thing, I obeyed, because obedience felt like safety. I let a guardian die. I told myself it was duty." She swallowed. "It was cowardice wearing duty's coat. And I have been wearing that same coat ever since."

"Ilsa—"

"So this is my new oath, freely given, badly spoken, witnessed by a smuggler who is absolutely going to mock me later." Behind them, Pip raised a hand in cheerful confirmation. "No crown, no order, no covenant asks it of me. I choose it. I choose *you*. My sword is yours, my judgment is yours to question — and it should be questioned, I was wrong once and silence made me wronger — and my death, if it comes to that, is yours to spend wiser than I spent my last oath."

The fire popped. Wren was staring at her with wet eyes and an expression of utter exasperation, which Ilsa had learned to read as deep affection.

"You're a knight without a banner swearing herself to a woman with no magic, no country, and no table manners," Wren said.

"Yes."

"And you're doing it in an inn."

"The carpet's cleaner than most chapels."

Wren put down the sleeve, crossed the room, and laid her hand flat on the blade between Ilsa's palms. "Then I accept," she said formally, and then ruined it completely by adding, "and the first order of my knight is to stop calling my cooking a war crime in public."

"No oath binds me to lies, my lady."

"*Get up.*"

Ilsa got up. Something in her chest that had been clenched for ten years — she could not have named it, only felt its absence now, like a hand unclenching — sat loose and easy. Disgrace, it turned out, had been heavier than the sword ever was.

### III. Voss

Guildmaster Odell Voss returned to Corvane in autumn, when the smoke of the Inquisition's pyres had finally stopped rising and the new magistrates — terrified, pragmatic men — had repealed the hanging laws with the haste of people apologizing to a storm.

The Hollow Lantern Guildhall was exactly as he'd left it: dusty, half-looted, its cellar sealed under wards that had died with the covenant. He stood in the doorway a long while, an old man in an old coat, holding a ledger.

Every charm sold. Every anchor placed. Every name of every child the guild had sheltered, and beside each name, in his own handwriting, the words *insurance* or *asset* or, in Wren's case, *bridge*. Forty years of bookkeeping on human beings.

He did not burn it. Burning felt like mercy, and mercy was not his to give himself.

Instead he cleared the main hall, set out a table, and opened the guild's doors with a new sign in the window:

**CHARMS SOLD HONESTLY. LIES SOLD CHEAPER. MAGIC TAUGHT FREE.**

The first week, no one came. The second week, a boy arrived with a hedge-witch's talent and no teacher, and Voss sat him down and began, at last, to tell the truth — starting with the worst of it. His own part. The ledger. The courier whose name he spoke aloud every morning, because the dead deserved better than anonymity.

Some nights, teaching, he caught himself almost happy, and hated himself for it, and then decided — slowly, like frost thawing — that perhaps grief and usefulness were allowed to share a house. Perhaps that was the only honest way anything got rebuilt.

He wrote letters he never sent. One went thus:

*To Wren. The guild was never a home. I mistook custody for love, and you paid the difference. I will not ask forgiveness; asking would be another withdrawal from an account I have overdrawn. Only this: the doors are open, the ledger is public, and every child who crosses our threshold will be told the truth about where they came from — including, when they're old enough, about you. Not the legend. The girl who refused. May your hands stay warm by ordinary fires.*

He kept that one in his pocket. Some debts you carry rather than repay.

### IV. Wren

Winter came to Dominic's Ford, and with it, the market.

Wren had a stall again. Of course she had a stall again; some things were less a profession than a spine. But the charms she sold now needed no disguise and no lie — little braided cords and river-stones and bits of Thornwood holly that glowed honestly, gently, sold openly to mortals and fae alike who crossed the border town's muddy high street trading fish for starlight and wool for songs.

Her hands stayed empty of magic. But her stall stood at the exact midpoint of the Ford, where the veil ran thin as honest breath, and things grew near it whether she willed it or not — her potted rose bloomed in December, her tea never went cold, and once a lost fae child had wandered out of the mist straight to her counter, because children of both realms, apparently, could still feel where the bridge had been. Even burned-out bridges leave a warm spot in the bank.

"You're the one who broke the world," said the child, on a gray afternoon, chewing a honey stick Wren had given her.

"Fixed it, mostly."

"Mama says the thorn rose walks."

Wren glanced toward the treeline, where the Thornwood stood at the town's edge — closer than it used to be, tamed by treaty but never tame, its border hedges blooming stubbornly out of season wherever a certain former apprentice happened to sleep.

"It does," Wren said. "When it's needed."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Are you needed?"

Wren looked at her hands — clean, ordinary, empty. At Ilsa across the street, arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger, her new oath worn as lightly as an old coat. At Pip, who appeared from nowhere to steal an apple and leave a coin, because he was relearning honesty one apple at a time. At the letter-shaped lump in her pocket that she'd read forty times and answered, at last, just yesterday: *Come see the guild, Odell. Bring the ledger. We'll start with page one.*

"Ask me again in spring," Wren said, and smiled.
