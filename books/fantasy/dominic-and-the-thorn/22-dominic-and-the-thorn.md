## 22. Dominic and the Thorn

The market at Dominic's Ford smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and frying apples, which Wren considered a considerable improvement over the old Corvane smell of fear, sweat, and Inquisitor boot-leather.

She had set up her stall in the same spot for six years now — the corner where the pilgrim road met the river crossing, under the leaning eave of the ferry-house, where the morning sun came through the gaps in the shingles and laid stripes of gold across her wares. The charms hung from pegs and strings: knotted hazel, river-glass on twine, little woven circles of thorn-wood that she sold at cost because they made themselves faster than she could carve them.

They were real charms now. That was still strange, some mornings. She would hold one up to check the knot and feel it hum faintly against her palm like a sleeping bee, and she would have to stand there a moment, blinking, before she could go on with her day.

"Charm for safe travel!" she called to the crowd ambling past. "Charms for good luck, honest sleep, and butter that won't burn! I cannot charm your mother-in-law into kindness — I've tried, and the results were disturbing."

An old woman by the cheese stall laughed so hard she had to sit down.

Wren grinned and turned back to her work. Her hands knew it all without thinking: re-knotting the hazel cords, sorting the river-glass into blues and greens, straightening the little hand-lettered sign Pip had made her as a joke and that she had kept because it was true:

*ALL CHARMS GENUINE. NO REFUNDS ON MIRACLES.*

Six years ago, selling a genuine charm in public would have meant a hanging. Now a farmer's wife haggled her down two coppers for a knot against storms, paid with a smile, and asked after her knee. An actual fae woman — tall, green-veined, wearing a coat of woven reeds — bought three charms for her children's names-day, counted out mortal coin carefully because fae arithmetic was a menace, and thanked Wren in a voice like water over stones.

The veil at the Ford was thin enough to step through if you knew the spot, and most people did. Mortal traders crossed to sell wool and iron nails in the bramble courts; fae came through to buy honey and shoes and, inexplicably, corsets. There were arguments. There were always arguments — about tariffs, about whether a riddle was a fair substitute for payment, about whose fault it had been when somebody's goat got swapped for a very smug hedgehog. But there was a border office now, jointly run. There was a ledger with two columns. There was a bell you rang when something went wrong, and both sides answered it.

That had been the whole point. That had been the price.

Wren touched the place below her sternum where her magic used to live — an old habit, like pressing a tongue to the socket of a missing tooth. Nothing there. Just Wren. Just a woman with bad knees and a good stall.

It should have hurt more than it did. Some days it did. On those days she walked out to the river at dusk, where the veil shimmered faint as heat-haze over the water, and stood there until the homesickness passed through her and out the other side. She belonged to neither realm; that had been the bargain's fine print. The Thornwood no longer sang to her blood. Corvane had never sung at all.

But Dominic's Ford — Dominic's Ford was hers. Not inherited. Not assigned. Chosen, stall by stall, friend by friend, year by year.

* * *

The trouble arrived, as trouble usually did at her stall, in the form of a child.

This one was perhaps seven, gap-toothed, freckled, with the particular stubborn set of the shoulders that Wren recognized because she had worn it herself for ten years in Corvane: the posture of someone who has been told to stop asking questions and has decided to make that everyone else's problem.

"You're the charm witch," the child said. Not a question.

"I'm a charm seller," Wren corrected, sorting river-glass. "The distinction is important. A witch charges more."

"My da says you broke the world."

"Your da," said Wren, "sounds charming. Did he also tell you I ate a knight?"

"No." The child frowned, recalibrating. "He says you *knew* a knight. Ser Marrow. He says she once threw an Inquisitor into a canal."

"That's true, actually. It was a Tuesday." Wren held up a blue bead to the light, discarded it. "Your da is better informed than most."

The child leaned on the stall, entirely ignoring the social contract. "And you walked into the Thornwood and talked to the big root under the world, and you wouldn't do what it said, and everything almost ended, except it didn't, because of you."

"That is a version of events."

"What's the other version?"

"The other version involves a smuggler who cried twice and denies both times, and a lot of running." Wren set the bead down and gave the child her full attention, which was, she had learned, the most valuable thing she could give anyone. "Why are you asking?"

The child pointed across the square — past the border office with its two flags, past the fae woman lacing herself into her alarming purchase, past the ferry landing — to the ridge above the town, where the Thornwood began.

Or rather, where the Thornwood's edge began: a single line of blackthorn, dense and gleaming, running along the hilltop like a wall that had decided to be polite about it. The locals called it the Hedgerow. The fae called it the Long Courtesy. It marked where the veil ran now — thinner than it used to be, honest, tended. And every few miles along it, breaking the line like punctuation, stood the thorn roses.

Except "stood" wasn't quite the word people used.

"They walk," the child said, in the hushed, thrilled voice of someone reporting a crime. "The thorn roses. My cousin saw one move. It went along the Hedgerow in the night, all by itself, and where it stopped the brambles were different in the morning. Da says it's nonsense. Cousin says otherwise. Which is it?"

Wren looked at the ridge. At this distance the nearest rose was just a crown of dark red against the grey-green thorn, motionless, beautiful, patient.

She remembered the first time she'd seen one move — the night the new covenant took root, when the whole Hedgerow had stirred like a sleeper turning over, and the roses had begun their slow patrol along the borders of two worlds. She remembered what the Worldroot's guardian had told her, in its voice like wind through a hollow: *A leash breaks. A tended fence must be walked.*

"It's not nonsense," Wren said. "They walk."

The child's eyes went enormous. "*Really?*"

"Really. They're wardens. They keep the border honest — mend the thin spots, mark the crossings, remind both sides that the line belongs to everyone." She leaned in conspiratorially. "Between us? They're also terrible gossips. If you want to know anything that happened along the Hedgerow, bring a rose one cup of honey and ask nicely."

"Do they talk?"

"Not in words. More in... impressions. Strong ones." She thought of the message the roses had carried her two springs ago, when a band of poachers had tried to drag a fae child across the veil: a feeling like *thorns, urgent, south field, now.* She thought of how Ilsa had read it off the brambles' angle in under a minute and been saddling her horse before Wren finished translating. "But they get their point across."

The child absorbed this with the gravity of a scholar. Then, because children are never done: "Did you really pay for the whole world with your magic?"

Ah. There it was. The question underneath the questions, the one the ballads had gotten fat on. *The Witch Who Paid Everything*, Pip called the song, when he wasn't demanding royalties for his depiction as "the roguish and devastatingly handsome third verse."

Wren considered lying. She considered the softer version, the tidy one, the one where the sacrifice had been clean and worth it and painless forever after.

"I paid," she said instead. "My magic. My — my place in both worlds, sort of. The fae can't claim me, and neither can the mortals. I'm not anybody's daughter or anybody's bridge anymore. I'm just mine." She shrugged. "Some days that costs more than others."

The child studied her with unsettling directness. "Was it worth it?"

And there it was — the question Wren had asked herself on bad nights, standing at the river, pressing her tongue to the empty socket. The nights when the Thornwood's music reached the edge of her hearing and stopped, like a song cut off mid-phrase, and she grieved something she could barely remember.

She looked at the square. At the fae woman laughing with the cheese-seller over the corset situation. At the border office, where a mortal clerk and a bramble-court steward were arguing cheerfully about tariffs and sharing a meat pie anyway. At the Hedgerow on its ridge, tended, walked, alive. At the ferry landing, where —

— where a broad-shouldered woman in a plain knight's traveling cloak was dismounting, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had somewhere she was expected. Grey threaded Ilsa Marrow's temples now, and the scar through her eyebrow had faded to silver, but she still scanned a crowd the way she'd been taught to scan battlefields: corners first, exits second, then the people who mattered.

Ilsa found Wren immediately. She always did. Something in her face loosened — only slightly, only for Wren to see, because knights without banners learn to ration their softness — and she began leading her horse toward the stall.

"Worth it?" Wren repeated. She smiled. "Ask me again tonight. I'm having dinner with the answer."

* * *

Dinner was at Wren's cottage above the ferry-house, because it always was, because Ilsa had a room at the inn and used it exactly often enough to maintain plausible deniability, which was to say, rarely.

Pip arrived through the window, because he always did, despite owning a perfectly good front door and knowing perfectly well where it was.

"Honeyed pears," he announced, materializing in the kitchen in a swirl of river-damp cloak and self-congratulation. "From the far bank. Traded fairly, mostly. You're welcome."

"You climbed in through the window to deliver fruit," Ilsa said from the table, not looking up from sharpening her knife, which she did during all conversations the way other people fidgeted.

"I entered *dramatically.* There's a difference. Also the door was locked."

"It was not locked," Wren called from the stove. "You just enjoy climbing in through windows because you think it makes you mysterious."

"It does make me mysterious."

"It makes you a cat, Pip."

"A devastatingly handsome cat." He dropped into the fourth chair — the one nobody else ever sat in, because some things were simply true — and helped himself to bread. Then, more quietly, in the tone he saved for things that mattered: "Border office sent word ahead. The steward wanted me to tell you the western stretch of the Hedgerow flowered last week. Out of season. Full bloom, overnight."

Wren paused, ladle in hand. Flowering out of season was the roses' way of celebrating. Or mourning. Or, occasionally, of being insufferably pleased about something.

"What are they pleased about?"

"They wouldn't say." Pip's grin turned sly. "But they sent a bloom. With your name on it. Well — not your name. Fae don't write names down, terrible idea, don't ask. But it's yours. It's on the mantel. Go look."

On the mantel, in a chipped clay cup, sat a single thorn rose: deep red, impossibly fresh, petals edged in a faint gold that no mortal rose had ever managed. Wren picked it up and felt — not magic, she couldn't feel magic anymore, that door was shut — but memory, which is its own kind of enchantment. Six years ago. A guttering lamp beneath the world. A choice made with shaking hands.

"She remembers," Ilsa said behind her. Not a question either. Ilsa rarely needed them.

"She remembers." Wren set the rose back carefully. "They all do. That was the whole point."

The covenant they'd built wasn't grand. That had been its genius, if Wren was allowed to call it genius, and she had decided long ago that she was. No great sacrifice anchored it. No leash held it taut. It ran on something smaller and harder-won: exchange. Grief acknowledged on both sides — the fae mourning what mortals had taken in the old war, the mortals mourning what the fae had taken in retaliation, the whole long ugly ledger finally read aloud, line by line, wept over, forgiven-not-forgotten. In exchange for the telling of it, the veil held. Thinner than before. Honest. Crossed freely by anyone willing to be known on the other side.

Tended jointly, by mortal hands and fae hands, and by the slow walking patience of the thorn roses.

Nobody was sacrificed. Nobody was caged. When the covenant flickered — and it flickered sometimes, borders were living things — the fix was never blood. It was a meeting. It was tea, insultingly often. It was Ilsa riding the Hedgerow with a fae warden at her side, arguing about proper fence etiquette until the veil steadied out of what Pip insisted was sheer embarrassment.

Voss wrote every month from Corvane, where the rebuilt Hollow Lantern Guild taught openly now — taught craft, history, and a mandatory course titled *Ethics of Anchors, or: How Not to Repeat My Mistakes,* which Voss delivered personally, grimly, and without sparing himself. His letters always closed the same way: *The cellar is a classroom. The ledger hangs framed on the wall, both columns showing. Come visit when you can bear to. Your debt here was paid long ago; your chair is waiting regardless.*

She would go. Someday soon. She'd been meaning to for six years, and Pip mocked her for it annually, and she always said next spring, and he always said "next spring" in a voice that understood completely. Some doors you had to approach slowly. Some families you forgave in installments.

"Pears are getting cold," Pip announced. "Cold pears are a tragedy. Sit."

They sat. The four chairs were full — well, three and a half; Pip's chair didn't count fully since he occupied it sideways with his boots on the rung. Outside, the evening settled over the Ford: lamplight kindling window by window, the river muttering its endless commentary, and up on the darkening ridge, the Hedgerow keeping its watch.

Halfway through dinner, Ilsa said, without preamble, "There's movement on the western reach. The roses shifted position this week — three of them, walking north together. The stewards think it's nothing. New growth, maybe."

"But you don't."

"I've watched that border for six years. Roses walk alone or in pairs. Three abreast means they're going somewhere with purpose." She met Wren's eyes. "North. Toward the deep crossing. Toward—" She stopped, and her jaw tightened around whatever the end of that sentence was.

"Toward the old battlefield," Wren finished quietly. Where the leash had been forged. Where the grief ran oldest and deepest, and where the new covenant's roots were youngest and least tested.

Pip had gone still, which for Pip was roughly equivalent to another man screaming.

"Well," Wren said. She refilled their cups, unhurried, because panic was a luxury and dinner was nearly ready. "Good thing the border town has a charm seller who owes the roses several favors. And a knight who knows every mile of the Hedgerow. And a smuggler who claims he's retired but keeps a packed bag under his bed."

"I keep TWO packed bags," Pip said. "One's for dramatic exits."

Ilsa allowed herself one small, rare smile. "Tomorrow, then. At dawn."

"At dawn," Wren agreed.

Up on the ridge, in the last light, the nearest thorn rose turned — slow as a season, deliberate as a promise — to face north.

Wren raised her cup to it across the distance, an old greeting between old friends, and drank.
