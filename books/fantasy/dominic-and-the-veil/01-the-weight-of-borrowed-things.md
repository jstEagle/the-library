## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The soldier was dying in the alley behind the Tallowman's Rest, and he wanted to talk about summer.

Yara Joubert found him there a little past midnight, propped against a wall slick with rain-grease, one hand pressed to his belly as if holding something in — which, Yara saw when he crouched, he was. The wound had come from a knife rather than a blade duel; the blood was the same either way. In Corvane it usually was.

"You're not a physician," the soldier said. His voice was steady the way a rope is steady just before it frays through. "I can tell. Physicians look at the wound. You're looking at me."

"I'm looking at your options," Yara said. It was true. He tallied them quickly, out of long habit: no surgeon would come to the Tallowman's Rest for less than a crown and a name, and this man had neither. The watch would come only to roll him into the river by morning. There was exactly one thing of value left in him, and it wasn't his purse, which Yara could already see was empty.

The soldier laughed, and it cost him. "Options. That's a good word for them." He tilted his head back against the wall. Sweat had carved clean channels through the grime on his face. He was young — twenty-four, twenty-five — with the sunburn line of a helmet across his forehead, which meant border service, which meant the eastern garrisons, which meant Yara already knew roughly what summer lived in him before he ever touched it.

"Here is what I'll offer," Yara said. "It isn't charity, and I won't pretend otherwise. I can take something from you. Something you won't be needing much longer."

"Take my boots while you're at it."

"Your memories are worth more than your boots. One of them. Your choice which." Yara kept his voice flat, unhurried, the voice of a man discussing the price of fish. This mattered. Fear curdles memory; grief burns it. If the man panicked or wept, the taking would come out scorched and thin, good for nothing but the cheapest kind of charm. "You'd feel it leave. Most say it's like setting down a pack you forgot you were carrying. And in exchange—"

"In exchange for what? I'm dying. What could you possibly give me?"

Yara considered lying, then didn't. Lies sat poorly on a dying man's ears; they slid off. "A few hours without pain. A warm room upstairs if you can climb the stairs, a bucket of water if you can't. Someone who knows your last day happened."

That last part was not part of the trade. Yara added it because it was true, and because truth, in his experience, loosened memory like warm water loosens a jar's lid. But he did not fool himself that it made him kind. Kindness was a technique here, the way a lockpick is a tool. He had learned that lesson so thoroughly he could no longer locate the place where the lesson had been learned — which was, in a sense, its own proof of how well it had been taught.

The soldier looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere above them, a shutter banged in the wind off the Graywash, and the smell of the tallow works drifted down — hot fat and lime, the smell that gave the street its name and its bad dreams.

"The fair," the soldier said. "Two years back. Not the war summer — the one after, when I had leave. My sister married a cooper from Merrow's Ford, and the whole town came out, and there was a bear that danced. An actual dancing bear, half-starved and miserable, and everyone cheered it, and my sister laughed until she cried, and I thought—" He stopped. His hand slipped on his belly and came away red. "I've been thinking about that day all night. Like my mind's packing a bag. Is that strange?"

"No," Yara said quietly. "That's very common, actually."

It was the truest thing he'd said all year.

* * *

The taking took four minutes and required two hands, a quiet corner of the mind, and consent — though only the first two were strictly physical requirements. Consent was craft. Without it, a stolen memory came away ragged, full of holes where the owner had gripped it; with it, the whole thing slid loose intact, every sense threaded through it like color through cloth.

Yara set his fingertips against the soldier's temple, light as a fly landing, and said, "Think of the bear. Just the bear. What did its fur smell like?"

"Wet dog," the soldier said, and closed his eyes, and laughed again, softer. "Wet dog and hay. God, it was awful. It was wonderful."

Yara went in.

He had done this perhaps nine hundred times, and it never felt like anything, and that was the point. The mind under another's touch was not a room, whatever the poets said. It was a country seen from very high up — fields of light, rivers of sound, and there, near the surface where recent and beloved things glowed like hearth-fires, the fair. He could see it whole: dust gold in afternoon light, a fiddle somewhere, a woman in a green dress laughing with her head thrown back — the sister, the bride — and the bear shuffling on its hind legs, swaying, wretched and magnificent, and the crowd's roar, and cider, and the specific ache of a young man standing in the middle of his own happiness knowing, without knowing he knew, that it would not come again.

Yara gathered it the way you gather water in cupped hands, and pulled.

The soldier exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down that forgotten pack. His face smoothed. The pain was still there — Yara could do nothing about pain, only about what pain meant — but for a moment it was only pain, unaccompanied, without a past or a future attached to it.

"Is she safe?" the soldier asked. His eyes stayed closed. "The memory. Wherever you keep them."

"It goes somewhere it will be loved," Yara said. Which was also true, in the way that a coin is loved by a miser.

"Good," the soldier said. "Good." And then, after a minute, in a smaller voice: "I can't see her face now. The sister. I know there was a wedding. I know I loved her. I can't—" His breath hitched once, and steadied. "No. No, it's better. Keep it. Tell someone a soldier from the eastern garrisons danced with his sister at a fair, once, and was happy. Will you do that?"

"I will," said Yara Joubert, who told no one anything.

He stayed with the soldier until the end, which came an hour later, quietly, mid-sentence, the man talking about the cooper's speech and whether bears could truly be trained or only starved into it. Yara answered him the whole time. It changed nothing. He had learned that too: it changed nothing, and he stayed anyway, and he had stopped asking himself what the staying was for.

Then he took the boots after all. The dead have no use for them, and Yara had use for everything.

* * *

Corvane's night market opened when the lawful city closed, spilling down Cutpurse Stair from the Lantern Gate to the Graywash Docks in a cascade of paper lamps and illegal light. Magic was punishable by unmaking — the Wardens' writ ran through every street in the kingdom, and the king's edicts against the gifted arts had teeth older than anyone living could remember — but a memory-charm carried no resonance a Warden's instruments could catch, once properly bound. It was simply a small glass bead, warm to the touch, humming faintly with somebody else's best day. The crown couldn't punish what it couldn't find, and so Corvane, being Corvane, had built an entire economy in the dark between the edicts.

Yara had a stall — a plank on two barrels — halfway down the Stair, between Old Sefa's curtain of salvaged lace and a woman who sold luck, which was to say pigeon bones and confidence. His stock lay ranked on black velvet in rows of beads, each one tagged with a slip of paper in his careful hand:

*A summer fair. A dancing bear. A bride laughing.*
*First snow on a roof, seen from inside, aged seven.*
*Your mother's hands, kneading bread. Warm flour.*

The tags were Sefa's invention. "Nobody buys a mystery, boy," she'd told him his first month in the trade. "They buy a door, and you make them believe what's behind it is better than their house." She took a fifth of his earnings for the pitch and the protection, and Yara paid it without complaint, because in Corvane a fifth was cheap for a woman whose curse-words could blister paint and whose reach ran, somehow, even into the Warden barracks.

Business was good. Business was always good, because Corvane was a city that ran on hunger of every kind, and the hungriest people in it were not poor. They were the merchants' wives with their arranged marriages and their forty years of Tuesdays; the guildmasters who had climbed so high they'd forgotten what their lives were for; the young men about to take ship east to the garrisons who bought, at scandalous prices, other men's homecomings to carry against their hearts like talismans.

"Bear fair, is it?" said a voice, and Sefa's gray head appeared over the lace curtain, eyes sharp as tacks in a face like a crumpled letter. "Word travels. The Tallowman's girl heard you came in at midnight smelling of blood and river."

"The river comes to me these days. I don't go to it." Yara held up the bead. It caught the lamplight and held it, deep inside, where a golden afternoon turned slowly like something suspended in honey. "One owner. Clean death, clean taking. Best quality."

Sefa picked it up and rolled it between finger and thumb, her old thief's trick, reading weight the way a banker reads a coin's ring. Her expression did something complicated and then put itself away.

"Border boy," she said. It wasn't a question. She could taste provenance the way he could, forty years of it in her thumbs. "Garrisons are full of these lately. Full of boys dying stupid deaths in alleys with empty purses." She set the bead down with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "Full of summers nobody will remember by spring."

"That's why the trade exists," Yara said.

"Mm." Sefa looked at him a moment longer than she needed to, and Yara felt the look land somewhere and stick, though he couldn't have said where. "There's talk at the docks. Warden skiffs at anchor, doing sweeps. Resonance readings, they're calling it. New instruments from the capital. Nothing found yet, they say." She smiled without warmth. "Yet is a word I've never liked."

"If they had something, we'd know."

"We knew the plague was coming too." She drew the curtain shut. "Sell your beads, boy. And sell the bright ones first."

* * *

He sold the bear fair within the hour, to a wool merchant's widow who listened to his pitch with her gloved hands folded and her face perfectly composed, and then, when he pressed the bead into her palm, closed her eyes and swayed as though struck.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, it's *loud*, this one."

"They're all loud, madam. Life is loud. We've just gotten good at not listening."

She paid without haggling — the rich never haggled over feelings, only over goods — and walked away holding the bead against her sternum like a reliquary, and Yara watched her go and felt precisely nothing, which was his finest skill and the one no one ever paid him for.

Numbness, Vess had taught him, was not the absence of feeling. That was what amateurs believed, and it made them sloppy, because absence leaks. True numbness was a discipline, a held note, a muscle kept flexed for years. You did not stop feeling what you took; you built a room for it, and locked the room, and eventually you forgot you owned the key. Vess had built his rooms for him personally, the way a mason builds, and she had taken payment in a coin he couldn't remember counting out. Three years in the trade and he knew the shape of his own emptiness the way a man knows a missing tooth — by the tongue's constant return to the gap.

His rules, chalked on the underside of his stall where no customer would ever read them:

*Never take from a child. Never take love of the living from the living. Never take more than the seller can survive losing. Always stay until the end.*

Four rules. He had broken none of them, and told himself, in the flat voice he used for such accounting, that the ledger balanced. A dying man's last summer, preserved forever in glass, loved by a stranger — weighed against a soldier who died remembering a wedding instead of a wound. Who had lost, there? Show him the harm. Point to it with one finger.

The trouble was that harm in his trade never stood still long enough to be pointed at. It dispersed. It became a widow's warm evenings and a merchant's son braver on a ship and a guildmaster weeping gently over a mother's hands he'd never had. The harm was a fog, and Yara lived in it comfortably, the way fish live in water, and the only moments it condensed enough to drown him were moments like this one: the market winding down, the lamps guttering, his velvet nearly bare, his pockets full, and the whole glittering Stair silent enough that he could hear, underneath the paper lanterns' crackle, the exact weight of what he was.

He was folding the velvet when the first bead cracked.

Not dropped — he would have felt the impact. It split along its seam with a sound like ice on a pond, and the memory inside it did not spill out as light, as it should have. It spilled out as *sound*. A voice, high and thin and childish, speaking words Yara did not know, except that some animal floor of him knew them completely, knew them the way you know falling.

The language moved through the market like cold air through a broken window. Down the Stair, other charms began to whisper — he could hear them, dozens of them, his whole inventory and Sefa's and the luck-seller's pigeon bones rattling — all of them murmuring in that same small voice, saying the same unknowable syllables, over and over, patient as tide.

And beneath the whispering, faint as a knock through a thick wall, Yara heard something else.

Someone was knocking. Not at his stall.

At his door.

Three streets up, through the lawful dark, in the little rented room where Yara Joubert kept his ledgers and his locked rooms and his carefully forgotten life — someone small was knocking, and the knocking, impossibly, was keeping time with the whispers, and every knock landed in his chest like a fist on the far side of his ribs.

He stood very still among his ruined stock, a man who had made peace with everything discovering what his peace had been resting on.

Then he ran.
