## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The knock came at the hour when even Corvane's night market had gone home to count its coin and curse its debts — three strokes on Yara Joubert's door, spaced like a heartbeat that had forgotten how to hurry.

He knew every knock in his district by sound. The fat baker two doors down knocked with the flat of his hand, apologetic even in wood. The Warden patrols didn't knock at all; they announced themselves with boot-heels and the creak of unmaking-irons. Debt collectors knocked fast, twice, like they were ashamed of it.

This was none of those. This was a knock that waited.

Yara sat up in his narrow bed above the charm shop and listened to the rain working its way through Corvane's gutters, and he told himself, as he told himself most nights when something went wrong, that whatever it was could be someone else's problem until morning. He had rules. Rule one: never open your door after the lamps gutter low. Rule two: never open your door for anyone who knocks like they have all of eternity to wait for you.

The knock came again. Three strokes. Patient as tide.

"Go away," he called, because rule three, unwritten, was that sometimes people left if you sounded annoyed enough. Fear attracted predators; irritation bored them. He'd learned that from Vess, back when he still had lessons to learn. *Never look hungry, boy. The whole city is a mouth, and everything in it is deciding what to eat.*

Silence. Then, small and clear through the door, a voice said something in a language Yara did not know — and yet, horribly, knew.

It wasn't the knowing of vocabulary. It was deeper than that, the way you know the shape of a word you heard once in a fever dream. The syllables were old. They had weight, the way stones have weight, and they landed somewhere behind Yara's sternum and *sank*, and along the walls of his little shop, forty glass vials of stolen memory began, very quietly, to sing.

Not sing. That was too gentle. They *cracked* — hairline fissures racing up the glass like frost blooming across a window, each vial leaking a thin silver breath of somebody else's summer, somebody else's grief, into air that had no business holding them.

Yara was off the bed and moving before he'd decided to move. Three years of trade had taught him exactly what a cracked vial meant: a dead sale at best, a haunting at worst. Memories leaked like that didn't fade politely. They *stuck*. You could find yourself weeping over a stranger's drowned brother for a week before you figured out whose sorrow had soaked into your floorboards.

"By the Veiled Saint," he muttered — an oath that would have gotten him hanged if any pious ear heard it, which was precisely why he used it; blasphemy kept a man honest about whom he actually served, and Yara served himself — "by the Veiled Saint and her nine bleeding fingers, *what is that*."

He lit the lamp. He checked the seams of his shutters out of habit, though nothing so mundane as a burglar made glass weep. And then he stood in the middle of his own shop, barefoot on cold boards, listening to forty memories hum in their broken bottles, and realized that the sound had a shape.

They were all humming the same note.

That had never happened. Not once in three years. He had lifted a soldier's last summer on Tuesday — a good haul, sun-yellowed, the taste of peaches in it — and he had a fishwife's memory of her daughter's wedding, and a scholar's recollection of a library now burned, and not one of those things shared anything except that they had all passed through his hands. His inventory was a hundred different lives. It should have been a cacophony.

Instead it was a choir. And under the choir, under the cracked-glass shimmer, he could hear the words the voice outside his door had spoken, repeated inside every vial like something trying to get out.

Yara's hands were shaking. He noticed this with a strange professional detachment, the same detachment he brought to all his work: *observe the symptom, price the damage, decide whether to run.* His hands shook. His inventory sang. Someone was knocking at his door at the dead hour with a language older than the crown, and every instinct he owned was screaming at him in a register he hadn't heard since he was small enough to still have a childhood to remember.

Which was, itself, impossible. Yara did not remember being small. That was the arrangement. That was the price he'd paid Vess Corhane for learning the trade, and he had signed it gladly, and in three years he had not once missed what he couldn't name.

But his body remembered. His body was remembering right now, sweat gathering at his hairline, his knees wanting to fold, some animal part of him insisting that this language was the sound of the worst thing that had ever happened to him, and that it had happened *here*, or somewhere, long ago, behind a door just like this one.

The knock came a third time.

And Yara Joubert — thief of memories, survivor of the city that ate magicians, author of rules — opened the door.

Later, he would not be able to explain why. He would tell himself it was curiosity, or greed, or the simple fact that a cracked inventory was already ruined and he might as well see the face of the thing that ruined it. All lies, or close enough. The truth was simpler and worse: the voice had said something in that old tongue, and some buried room in Yara had understood it to mean *please*, and he had never in his life been able to leave a *please* unanswered, because he suspected — without evidence, without memory — that no one had answered his.

The child on his doorstep was six years old, perhaps seven. Small for the age, rain-soaked, barefoot on the wet stone. Hair the color of wet ash, eyes the gray of a sky that has given up on clearing. A child of the kind Corvane produced by the thousand and lost by the hundred — war orphan, gutter-foundling, the sort of child the city chewed without noticing the taste.

Except that the child looked up at Yara with those gray eyes and spoke again, and the words went through Yara like a blade finding the gap in armor.

He didn't understand them. He *understood* them. Both at once, and the contradiction hurt. The syllables were the ones from his dreamless dreams, the ones his cracked vials were singing, and they meant — he was almost sure, in the way you are almost sure of a thing that will ruin you — *I looked for you.*

"No," Yara said. "No, absolutely not. Whatever game this is, whoever sent you — Sefa? Is this Sefa's idea of a joke? Go around front, come back at market hours, buy a charm like everyone else."

The child tilted its head. Rain ran off its chin. Behind it, the alley was empty the way alleys are never empty — no beggars, no rats, no drunks, just rain and dark, as if the night itself had stepped back to give them privacy.

"I don't take foundlings," Yara said. "I don't take apprentices. I don't take strays. I sell charms. Little ones. Harmless ones. First love, first snow, your grandmother's kitchen. That's the whole of my ambition, and none of it involves—" he gestured at the child, at the alley, at the entire unfolding catastrophe "—whatever this is."

The child reached out and touched his hand.

Yara had been touched by strangers before. It came with the trade; clients grabbed your wrist, pressed your palm to their temple, begged you to *take it, please, take it out of me.* He had felt grief, lust, terror, the sticky residue of other people's sins. He had a catalog of human touch as thorough as any physician's.

Nothing in the catalog covered this.

The world went white and then very deep, the way a well looks black until you're falling down it. He saw —

— a hall vast beyond sense, pillars like the legs of drowned gods, and in the middle of it a crowned figure kneeling. Kneeling the way men kneel when their legs have been taken from them rather than offered. The crown was old work, heavy gold, older than any king Yara had ever seen process through Corvane's streets, and the figure wore it the way a drowning man wears water. Because there *was* water, everywhere, rising — no, not water. Shadow with the texture of water, shadow with an appetite in it, climbing the crowned figure's body inch by patient inch, and where it touched, the man beneath the crown became less. Yara watched his hands go translucent. Watched his mouth open in a scream that made no sound because sound required a self to make it, and the shadow was eating the self first.

And the crowned figure turned his head — the last motion he had left, the last territory not yet swallowed — and looked directly at Yara across that impossible distance, and his eyes were not afraid.

They were *asking*. Asking permission, or forgiveness, or simply asking to be witnessed — *see me, whoever you are, see me before there's nothing left to see* — and Yara, who had built a life on the principle that seeing was a commodity and compassion a luxury, felt something crack in his chest with the same fine sound as his vials.

Then the child let go, and Yara was on his knees on his own doorstep, rain hitting the back of his neck, gasping like a man hauled out of deep water.

The child crouched beside him. Close up, its face was wrong in ways he couldn't itemize — too symmetrical, maybe, or too calm, or the eyes held a patience no six-year-old had ever survived learning. It spoke again in the old tongue, gently, the way you speak to a spooked animal, and this time Yara caught the meaning the way you catch a scent: *You saw him. You saw the king.*

"He's not the king," Yara rasped. His voice came out wrecked. "The king is — Aldric IV, he's fat and he waves from balconies and he opened the harvest festival last year wearing purple. That wasn't — that thing in the crown was drowning."

The child reached up and touched his cheek, feather-light, and this time there was no vision — only a feeling, cold and enormous, of a door. A door set into a hill, green turf over ancient stone, and behind it something that had waited so long that waiting had become its religion.

And underneath that, unmistakable now, coming from inside Yara's own shop, from the forty cracked vials on his shelves: the choir had resolved into words. Every stolen memory he owned — the soldier's summer, the fishwife's wedding, the scholar's burning library, three years of harmless charms — every single one contained, in some corner of it, the same image, dreamed by a hundred sleepers who had never met: *a door in a hill, and a king kneeling before it.*

"Oh," Yara said. Very quietly. To himself, mostly. "Oh, no."

He had rules. Rule four: when the job goes wrong, drop the goods and walk. Goods can be replaced. Thieves who hesitate end up in Warden irons, and Warden irons don't kill you — they *unmake* you, slowly, publicly, to the sound of applause.

The child was shivering now. Ordinary shivering, a six-year-old's shivering, blue lips and chattering teeth, and whatever the thing was — omen, weapon, key, trap — it was also, undeniably, soaked to the bone on his doorstep at the hour when Corvane ate its children.

Yara looked at the empty alley. Looked at his cracked inventory singing its one terrible note. Thought about wardens, and unmaking, and the comfortable numb life he had mortgaged his childhood to buy.

"You," he informed the child, hauling it inside by the collar and slamming the door on the rain, "are going to be the death of me. I want you to know that I know that. I am a professional, and I can tell."

The child looked up at him, dripping on his floorboards, and said something soft in the First Tongue.

Yara didn't need the translation. Some part of him had already provided it, unasked, from whatever sealed room Vess had left inside him:

*Thank you for opening the door.*

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, a bell began to ring — not the hour bell, not the alarm. The resonance bell. The one the Wardens rang when something old woke in the city.

And in Yara's shaking hands, every one of his charms began, softly, to glow.
