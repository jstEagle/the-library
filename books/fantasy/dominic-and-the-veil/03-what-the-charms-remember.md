## 3. What the Charms Remember

The child slept in Yara's bed like something poured there, limbs arranged with the boneless indifference of the very young, and Yara sat across the room with a lamp turned low and his entire inventory spread on the table, counting.

It was what he did when the world misbehaved. Count. Catalogue. Reduce chaos to columns. Forty-one charms, each one a stolen hour or a stolen afternoon, each one sealed in wax and glass and harmless as a river stone. He knew them the way a miser knows coins. The soldier's last summer, third shelf, wrapped in blue silk. A fishwife's wedding morning. A drunkard's memory of his mother's hands kneading bread, sold twice over because the drunkard wouldn't miss it and the buyers wept every time.

Forty-one. He counted them twice because the first count had come out forty-two, and that was the kind of small wrongness that had been accumulating all evening, like water finding cracks.

The child breathed. Outside, Corvane's bells rang the third hour, and somewhere below, the night market was packing its lies into crates.

Yara picked up the soldier's summer.

He had lifted it four days ago from a man named Aldric Vane in the charity ward of Saint Ossuin's, a spearman with a wound gone rotten and no family to sit with him, and Yara had traded him a warm hour for a warm hour — given him a charm of a harbor at dusk in exchange for the last summer of his life, the one with the orchard and the girl named Merrit and the afternoon they'd stolen a boat. The man had smiled. They always smiled. That was the trick of it, the mercy of it, the thing Yara told himself at night when the trade sat wrong in his stomach: he took only what people were finished with, or nearly finished with, and he left something soft in the hole.

He turned the charm in the lamplight. Blue silk, wax seal intact, the memory inside coiled like a sleeping adder.

The seal was intact.

The seal was intact, and the charm was whispering.

Not aloud — he would have preferred aloud, because aloud could be explained. The whisper was inside the wax, inside the glass, inside his own skull, a thin voice threading up through the bones of his hand the way cold threads up through a boot in winter. It was the child's voice. He knew it after one day and one night, the way you know a hook that's caught you: the strange liquid vowels, the words that bent in places words shouldn't bend.

He set the charm down. The whisper stopped. He picked it up. The whisper resumed, and now, with the lamp close and his breath held, he could almost make out the shape of what it said.

Not words. Images, worn soft, like a coin rubbed between fingers for a hundred years.

A hill. Green, and then not green — older than green, the hill of some season before seasons had names. And in the hill, a door. Not built into the hill. Part of it. The way a mouth is part of a face. And before the door, a figure on its knees, crowned, its head bowed so low its forehead nearly touched the grass, and the door was open a crack, and out of the crack came a darkness that was not the absence of light but a hunger for it—

Yara dropped the charm.

It rolled to the table's edge and he caught it on reflex, and the whisper flared — *kneeling, kneeling, the king kneels, the door remembers* — and he flung it into the drawer and slammed it shut and stood there in his own kitchen with his heart going like a fist on a door.

*The king kneels.* He had never put a king in a charm. He didn't traffic in kings. Kings were the reason the Wardens existed, the reason the First Law was written in fire on every courthouse in Corvane: *no craft of the mind, no craft of the soul, on pain of unmaking.* He stole summers and wedding mornings and the taste of a dead mother's bread. He was a pickpocket of the heart, not a — whatever this was.

The child shifted in the bed and made a small sound, and Yara went very still, the way he'd gone still all day whenever the child moved, as if quiet were a wall between him and the thing he'd let across his threshold.

The drawer whispered on.

He could hear it now even with his hands over his ears, faint as a fly in another room. He opened the drawer, took out the charm, and did the thing his three years in the trade told him never to do with sealed stock: he cracked it.

Wax split. Glass breathed. The memory uncoiled into the room like smoke finding its shape, and for a moment his kitchen was an orchard in high summer, was a girl named Merrit laughing with her sleeves rolled, was a boat drifting out on a green river — and underneath the summer, threaded through it like a dark wire through honey, the dream. The hill. The door. The crowned figure kneeling, and the darkness in the crack of the door leaning toward the kneeling man like a listener leaning toward a secret.

The dream was *in* the memory. Not beside it. Inside it, the way a flaw is inside a gem — and Yara understood with a lurch that it had been there all along, that he had sold this summer four days ago to a clerk with a taste for pastoral idylls, and the clerk had bought a king kneeling before a hungry door and never known it, because the flaw only showed when you held the stone up to a particular light.

The child's light. The child's voice.

Yara pulled the rest of his inventory out of the drawers and laid all forty-one charms on the table in rows, and one by one, with hands that had gone from steady to something else, he cracked them.

* * *

The fishwife's wedding morning: bells, a white dress gone grey at the hem from being remembered too often, a groom with flour on his collar — and beneath it, faint as a watermark, the hill, the door, the crown. The drunkard's mother kneading bread: flour again, warm dough, a kitchen smelling of yeast — and beneath, the hill, darker here, the door open wider, the darkness in the gap like a tongue. A ferryman's crossing, a mason's first wall, a widow's one afternoon of joy in thirty years of grief: hill, door, crown. Hill, door, crown.

Every charm. Every single one, all three years of his trade, every memory he had ever lifted from every mind in Corvane and its gray surrounding farms — the dream was in all of them, and it was *growing*. The oldest charms, the ones from his first year, held the dream like a rumor: a green smudge, a suggestion of stone. The newest, the soldier's summer, held it like a shout. Whatever the dream was, it was getting closer. It was climbing toward the surface of the world through the only cracks it had found.

Through him. Through the little holes he left in people when he took their soft hours out.

Yara sat down slowly on his one good chair. The lamp hissed. Forty-one broken charms lay in their rows like forty-one opened graves, and the room smelled of summer orchards and bread and river water and, beneath all of it, something older, a smell he had no name for, the smell of a hill before seasons.

He had thought himself a small criminal. A pickpocket. He had built his whole moral architecture on the smallness — *I take only what's finished, I leave something soft in the hole, I hurt no one a man doesn't already hurt himself* — and now the architecture was showing its foundation, and the foundation went down farther than any building he could have imagined, down to a door in a hill and a king on his knees before it, and he had been carrying pieces of it through the streets of Corvane in his coat pockets for three years. Selling it. Selling it to clerks and widows and lonely old soldiers of the crown, one harmless charm at a time, seeding the city with the edges of something enormous.

A courier. He had been a courier and never known who signed his letters.

In the bed, the child said something.

Yara turned. The child was still asleep — he was almost sure the child was still asleep — but the mouth was moving, and the sounds coming out of it were the sounds from the drawer, from the charms, the liquid bent vowels of the First Tongue, and the lamp flame leaned toward the bed as if listening, and every hair on Yara's arms stood up and saluted.

*The door remembers. The door remembers. The door—*

The child's brow furrowed in sleep. The whispering charms fell silent all at once, the way birds go silent before a storm, and the silence was worse.

* * *

— and in the child's sleep there is a hill, and the hill is not a dream because the child has never dreamed anything else, the hill is where the child was before the child was, the hill is the inside of the child's own name said in a language that has no word for *I* —

*keleth-va-oren, keleth-va-oren,* the door says, which means *the one who kneels, the one who kneels,* and the child knows this the way the child knows blood is red, without learning, and the child also knows — this is newer, this arrived with the smell of lamp oil and a thin man's fear — that the thin man is afraid of the wrong thing —

the child is not the door's key. the child is what was left outside the door when it closed. the child is the shape of the keyhole, cut away and given breath so that no one would ever find the key again, and this was mercy once, long ago, and mercy left in a hill for two hundred years goes strange, goes lonely, goes *hungry for the hand that cut it* —

there is a man in the city who carries pieces of the door in his pockets. the child felt them the moment the thin man opened his own door: forty-one small dark openings, forty-one little keyholes walking around in wax, and through each one the child can see a sliver of the hill, and the slivers are widening, the wax is thinning, the city is becoming a sieve and the hill is pouring through —

the child does not want the hill to pour through. the child wants — and here the sleep tangles, because the child wants what the door was made to hold, which was a hand, which was a voice, which was the man with the crown kneeling in the grass saying *I am sorry, I am sorry, I am sorry* in the First Tongue, and the child has been waiting two hundred years for the sentence to finish —

the thin man is cracking the charms. the child can feel each one open, a small cold breath, a widening. he should stop. the tall dark one behind the door says the child should stop him, the tall dark one who wears the crown now and wears it wrong, the glove wearing the hand —

but the child is tired, and the bed is soft, and the thin man's fear has a warm human smell underneath it, like bread, like the bread the child dreamed once of a mother kneading, and the child decides — with the vast, slow, terrible patience of a thing two hundred years old wearing the shape of six — that the thin man may keep opening the little doors a while longer.

the child decides to see what the thin man does when he understands.

* * *

Yara did not sleep. He sat in the chair with a poker across his knees like a sword he didn't believe in, and watched the child sleep, and the child slept like any child, fist curled, breath whistling faintly, and that was somehow the worst of it — that the vessel of all this could look so ordinary, so breakable, so *young*.

Toward dawn he gave up on vigilance and did what he always did with a problem too large to hold: he made it smaller. He took out the ledger he kept in his head and began to list what he actually knew.

One: the dream was in every memory he had stolen, growing stronger in the newer ones. Two: the child's voice woke it. Three: the child had appeared at his door out of a rainstorm with no name, no history, and a language that predated the kingdom, and had touched him and shown him a crowned man drowning in shadow. Four: the child had looked at Yara's coat pockets — at his *inventory* — with an expression he had taken for a beggar's hunger and now understood had been recognition.

Five: he had sold forty-one copies of the edge of this thing into the city of Corvane, and charms, once cracked, did not reseal. The memories passed into their buyers and stayed there. The dream was in forty-one heads in Corvane tonight, and growing, and he had no way to take it back, because taking it back was the one thing his craft could not do. He could lift a whole memory out. He could not reach into a memory and pull one thread from the weave. No one could. It was the first thing Vess had ever taught him—

Vess.

The name arrived in his mind and sat there like a stone dropped in still water, and the ripples went out and out. Vess Corhane, who had taught him to slip past the door of a mind, who had taken his childhood as her fee and left him a man with no before, who had not spoken to him in three years and who was the only person alive who might know what a dream was doing inside other people's memories — because if it was in every charm he'd ever cut, it had been in every charm *she'd* ever cut first, and Vess had been cutting charms for forty years.

Forty years of charms. Forty years of the dream, seeded through the city, through the country, through every mind that had ever bought comfort in a wax seal.

Yara looked at the child, and the child opened its eyes.

Not the slow surfacing of a waking child. The eyes simply opened, dark and calm and ancient in the small face, and fixed on him across the lamplit room, and the child said — in Corvanese, in clear, accented, perfectly ordinary Corvanese, the first words Yara had heard it speak in anything but the First Tongue:

"You opened them all."

It was not a question. Yara's mouth was dry. "They were whispering."

"I know." The child sat up. The blanket pooled at its waist. In the low light its shadow on the wall was too large, or perhaps Yara was too tired, or perhaps neither. "You put them in people. The little doors. Three years of little doors."

"I sold charms," Yara said. "Charms. Comforts. I didn't know there was anything in them but what I took."

"No," the child agreed, with terrible gentleness. "You never know what you carry. That's what makes you useful."

The lamp flame stood very straight. Somewhere below, in the gray before the market opened, a cart rattled past, and Yara heard — or thought he heard, or told himself he only thought he heard — the cart driver humming, and the tune was wrong, bent in the vowels, liquid in the places words shouldn't bend, and it was not a tune the man could have learned from anyone living.

The child tilted its head, listening to the same thing, listening through the floor, through the walls, through the whole waking city, and said:

"It's getting louder. You should take me to whoever taught you. Before the ones in the masks hear it too."

Yara stood very still. "What ones in the masks?"

The child looked at him with pity, which no six-year-old should be able to do, and said nothing, and outside, faint but unmistakable, from the direction of the river and the Warden barracks beyond it, a bell began to ring — not the market bell, not the hour bell, but the third bell, the one that had not been rung in Yara's lifetime, the one every child in Corvane learned in the cradle and prayed never to hear.

The unmaking bell.

Yara began, without quite deciding to, to pack his inventory.
