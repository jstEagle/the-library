## 4. The Unmaking Wardens

The third bell rang nine times before it stopped.

Yara knew the count because he counted everything now — coins, charms, exits, the seconds it took a customer's eyes to glaze when the memory took hold. Nine rings. In the cradle-songs they said the bell rang once for each Warden ship crossing the river, and that was a lie, like most things sung to children, but it was useful: nine ships meant every Warden in Corvane was coming, and they were not coming for pickpockets.

He worked fast. The good charms — the soldier's summer, the baker's wedding morning, a fisherman's one perfect storm survived — went into the oilskin roll sewn into his coat lining. The cheap ones, the diluted memories cut with nothing but warm air, he swept into the stove. They burned with a smell like rain on hot stone, and the child watched from the corner with that terrible stillness of his, hands folded, as if attending a lesson.

"You're frightened," the child said.

"I'm efficient." Yara doused the stove. "There's a difference."

"There isn't." The child tilted his head. "In the First Tongue there's no word for *efficient*. Only words for *afraid* and *pretending*."

"Then it's a poor language, and I'm glad I don't speak it."

But even as he said it, Yara felt the ache again — the deep, bruised ache behind his eyes that had started the night the child knocked. His charms were whispering. He could feel them through the oilskin, faint as flies against glass, murmuring in that old syllabic music, and beneath the murmur something else: the dream. The king kneeling at the door in the hill. It had been in his inventory for three years, folded into other people's memories like rot folded into bread, and he had carried it to market and sold it and never known.

*Courier.* That was what he had been. A man who thought himself a smuggler of small sins, and all the while his coat had been full of the end of the world.

The bell stopped. In its place came the sound Yara had heard only twice in his life and hoped never to hear a third time: the Warden drums, slow, spaced, patient. Not a chase rhythm. A sweep rhythm. They were going street by street, and the night market — the Lantern Stair, the tallow-lit warren under the aqueduct where Yara had sold his stolen summers for three uneventful years — sat directly between them and the river.

"We're leaving," he said.

"Through the floor," the child said. It wasn't a question. The boy looked at the flagstones the way other children looked at picture books. "There's a door under your house. It's old. Older than the house. It wants to be opened."

Yara stared at him. "How do you know that?"

The child smiled, and for a moment — just a moment — the smile was too wide, too knowing, and Yara saw again the crowned figure drowning in shadow, felt the cold of deep water in his own lungs. Then it passed, and the boy was only a tired child in a borrowed shirt.

"I listen," he said simply. "You taught me that listening is a kind of trade."

"I never taught you anything."

"You will."

---

The tunnels under Corvane belonged to no one and everyone, which meant they belonged to the memory-thieves. Three generations of Yara's trade had honeycombed the city's bones: smugglers' runs beneath the tanneries, drainage cuts older than the aqueduct, and — oldest of all, though few thieves used them — passages no one had dug at all, doors that opened in stone where no door should be, left over from whatever the founding king had sealed and the city had built itself upon without asking questions.

Yara did not use those. Yara's rule, rule number one, written nowhere and obeyed always: *touch nothing old.*

Tonight he broke it within twenty minutes.

The main run under the Tanners' Row was already compromised. He heard them before he saw them — boots, many boots, and the particular wet-hiss sound of a null-lantern burning, the light that ate resonance, that made a charm go dead and dumb in your pocket. Wardens moved in fives. He counted six sets of boots, which meant either a bad count or an oversized patrol, and both possibilities were bad.

"This way," the child whispered, and pulled him left, toward a seam in the tunnel wall that Yara's lantern showed as solid brick.

"That's a wall."

"It's a door that forgot." The boy pressed his palm flat against the brick. "Doors forget when nobody knocks. You have to remind them." And he spoke — two syllables of the First Tongue, soft, almost tender, and the brick shivered like a horse shaking off flies, and a crack opened where no crack had been, breathing out air that smelled of rain and iron and something older underneath, something like a hill in springtime.

Yara's whole body said *no*. Rule one. Touch nothing old. But behind them the null-lanterns hissed closer, and ahead was a door that wanted opening, and he had learned long ago that survival was mostly the art of choosing which rules to break first.

"In," he said. "Quickly. And if anything in there asks us our names, we lie."

"Everything in there already knows my name," the child said, and stepped through.

---

They came out into a passage that was not a passage.

That was the only way Yara could describe it, walking with his lantern held high and his skin crawling. The walls were stone, but the stone had grain, like wood; the ceiling arched in curves no mason would choose; and the air moved past them steadily in one direction, as if the corridor were breathing. Old work. Founding-era work. The kind of thing the crown paid Wardens to find and fill with fire.

The child walked it easily, barefoot — Yara had forgotten shoes again; the boy seemed to shed them the way other children shed attention — and touched the walls as he went, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

"They're memories," the child said. "The walls. Can't you hear them?"

"No. And I don't want to."

"You do want to. You just think wanting is dangerous." The boy glanced back at him. "It is. That's why it matters."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Talk like a priest. You're seven."

"I might be older than seven." The child considered this. "I might be much older. I don't remember. Isn't that funny? I know every word of a dead language and I don't know how many birthdays I've had."

Yara did not laugh. Something in the phrasing snagged in him like a hook — *I don't remember* — because he understood it from the inside. There was a hole in Yara Joubert where his childhood should have been, smooth-edged and painless, the way Vess had promised it would be. Before nine years old: nothing. A blank, clean as fresh paper. He had told himself for fifteen years that the blank was freedom.

Walking through a corridor made of someone else's remembering, with a child who spoke the language of kings, the blank felt less like freedom and more like a debt.

The passage ended at a ladder, and the ladder ended at a trapdoor, and the trapdoor opened onto the back room of Old Sefa's shop on Candlemakers' Lane, among hanging bundles of dried lavender and the sweet dust smell of her trade. Yara dropped down first, knife out, then relaxed by degrees: the shop was dark, the front shutters barred, and from the little room behind the counter came the scratch of Sefa's pen and the mutter of a woman doing sums she didn't trust.

"Sefa," he called softly. "It's me. I need—"

The pen stopped.

"Yara Joubert." Her voice was dry as her lavender. "Come out where I can see you, and bring whoever's breathing behind you."

She was seventy if she was a day, small as a sparrow and twice as quick, with white braids coiled tight and ink stains to the second knuckle. She'd fenced his charms since his first year in the trade. She never asked where he got them. She never asked why he flinched when customers mentioned their mothers. She took thirty percent and gave him coin, silence, and — though neither of them ever named it — the closest thing to kindness his life contained.

Her eyes went to the child and narrowed.

"You brought *that* into my shop. During a sweep. With the third bell still ringing in my ears." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Sit down, both of you, and don't touch the ledger."

"Sefa, the Wardens are following First Tongue resonance. They'll trace it to the market, then outward. Your shop is four streets from the Lantern Stair. You need to close tonight and come with us."

"Come with you *where*?"

"Away. Through the tunnels. There are old ones — real ones —"

"I know about the old ones, boy, I fenced charms before your mother was a rumor." She was already moving, though, pulling her ledger from its drawer, wrapping it in oilcloth with the brisk economy of a woman who had packed to flee before. "And I know what a Third Bell sweep means. They're not hunting a thief. They're hunting a *source*." She looked at the child a long moment. "And here he sits, eating my biscuits."

The child was, indeed, eating her biscuits.

"You can't stay," Yara said.

"I'm coming, aren't I? Give an old woman a moment to say goodbye to her shop." But her hands slowed on the ledger, and her voice dropped. "Yara. Listen to me. If they take me — not if they chase, if they *take* — you don't come back. You don't bargain, you don't offer trades, you don't stand in a square holding a sign. You take the boy and you go to ground and you stay gone. Swear it."

"Why would they take you? You're a fence. Fences get fines."

"Fences get fines." She tied the oilcloth. "Traitors get unmade. And whatever that child is carrying, the crown has wanted it found for longer than either of us has been alive. I've seen the old notices, boy. Sealed orders. My grandmother fenced for a woman who read them aloud once, drunk, and the woman was gone by morning and so was the notice and so was everyone who'd heard it." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and very tired. "This isn't a sweep. It's a harvest. Now help me move the shelf."

The shelf hid the cellar hatch, and the cellar hid another tunnel mouth, one of Sefa's own — newer, hers, dug with bribed masons across ten careful years. They got the shelf half-moved when the knock came.

Not a knock. A *pounding*, iron-shod, on the front shutters, and then a voice like a file drawn across iron:

"Open in the name of the Crown. Resonance has been traced to this address. Open, or we open it for you, and the opening is charged to your account."

Sefa went very still. Then, quietly, with terrible calm, she began putting things in her pockets. Ledger pages. A knife. A small tin Yara recognized — charm-blanks, empty vessels, worth nothing to anyone but a thief.

"Back stairs," she murmured. "Behind the lavender. Go now."

"Sefa—"

"*Now.*" And then, louder, in a cracked old-woman voice pitched perfectly for fear: "Coming! Mercy, coming, don't break my shutters, I'm only an old woman!"

Yara grabbed the child's hand. The boy resisted — turned, looked at Sefa with those pitiless pitying eyes, and said something in the First Tongue, low and urgent. Sefa's face changed. Whatever he said, it landed on her like a blow, and for a heartbeat she looked younger and more afraid than Yara had ever seen her.

Then she smiled at him, patted his head with her ink-stained hand, and said, in plain Corvanish, "Go on, love. Mind the tall one. He pretends he doesn't care, but he counts everything, and you only count what you can't bear to lose."

The back stairs. The lavender. Behind them the front shutters splintered inward with a sound like a tree falling, and boots poured in, and a null-lantern's hiss filled the shop, and Yara heard Sefa's voice rise into performance — indignation, confusion, the comedy of a harmless old woman — buying them seconds with the only currency she had left.

He climbed. At the top of the stairs, in the dark among the drying bundles, he made the mistake of looking back.

He saw, through the gap in the floorboards, the circle of grey masks tilt toward Sefa like a field of blind flowers. He saw her hold up her hands, still talking, still fast. He saw a Warden — tall, unhurried, its mask featureless except for a single vertical seam where a mouth should be — reach out and touch her forehead with two gloved fingers.

Sefa stopped talking mid-word.

She didn't fall. That was the worst of it. She just *stopped*, standing upright in her own shop, hands still raised, face emptied like a bowl wiped clean, and the tall Warden said in its file-voice: "Resonance confirmed. This one remembers something. Take her for the burning."

*Remembers something.* As if memory were contraband. As if her seventy years of sums and lavender and fence-work and the one time, Yara suddenly understood, she had covered for him with a Warden sergeant and never once mentioned it after — as if all of it were cargo to be seized and burned in a square for the crowd's entertainment.

The child's hand tightened around his. "We can't save her," the boy said quietly. "Not tonight. Tonight we can only not waste it."

Yara had spent three years perfecting the art of not caring. He had thought himself quite good at it. Standing in the dark above Sefa's shop, listening to the boots carry her out into the street, he discovered the art had limits, and the limit had a name, and he had just watched it being led away in chains.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out rough and strange, "is the person who taught me my trade?"

The child looked up at him. Somewhere below, the null-lantern hissed; somewhere above, the drums resumed their slow, patient sweep.

"The one whose name you won't say," the child said. "Vess. The one who took your childhood for payment." He smiled, small and sad. "I know where she lives. I've always known. She dreams about you, you know. Every night. Guilt keeps better hours than love."

The drums grew louder. Yara pulled his hood up, gathered the child against his side, and stepped off the stair into the dark — toward the one person in Corvane he had sworn never to see again, carrying the one thing in the world the Wardens wanted, owing his life to an old woman who had taught him, in her last free hour, exactly what his counting had always meant.
