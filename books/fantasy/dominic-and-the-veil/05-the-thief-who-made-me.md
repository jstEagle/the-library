## 5. The Thief Who Made Me

The house stood where the map said it would and nowhere else.

That was the first thing to understand about Vess Corhane: her home existed only for people who already knew how to find it. Yara had walked this street in the Saltrow district a hundred times in the years since he'd last come here, and the house had never been there — just a blank stretch of wall between a chandler's shop and an abandoned counting-house, gray stone, gray shutters, the kind of architecture the eye slid off like rain off oiled cloth. Tonight it was there because he needed it to be there, and because he still carried the key she'd left in his mind nine years ago: a memory of a green door that opened on nothing.

He stood across the street with the child's hand swallowed in his own and felt his heart doing something it had not done since he was young enough to have no name for fear. The child — Tam, he'd started calling him, though the boy had never confirmed or denied the name — looked up at him with those eyes too old for a six-year-old face.

"You're frightened," the boy said. Not in Corvani. In that other tongue, the First Tongue, the one that made Yara's teeth ache and his charms whisper in their wrappings.

"I'm irritated," Yara said. "There's a difference. Frightened people run. Irritated people knock."

"Which are you?"

Yara didn't answer. He crossed the street.

The door wasn't green anymore; it was the color of old bone. But when his knuckles touched the wood, he felt the latch recognize him before he heard it turn — felt something reach through the grain of the door and rifle through him the way a customs officer rifles a traveler's pack. It lingered on the child. It stayed there a long moment, long enough that Yara's palm went damp against the wood.

Then the door opened on darkness that smelled of tallow smoke, dried lavender, and something older underneath, like a cellar that had once held wine and now held secrets.

Vess Corhane stood in the doorway, small and straight-backed as a fence post, her white braid coiled at the nape of her neck like a sleeping adder. She was past sixty now. Her hands had been beautiful once; they were still beautiful the way a well-used knife is beautiful. Her eyes went from Yara to the child, and Yara watched — with a satisfaction so bitter it nearly choked him — the great Vess Corhane, the thief who had emptied half the noble houses of Corvane without ever being named in a Warden report, go pale as tallow.

"Well," she said. "You'd better come in. Both of you. Quickly."

* * *

Inside, the house was exactly as he remembered and nothing like he remembered. The same low ceilings, the same shelves crammed with objects that were not what they appeared — a brass compass that pointed at regrets, a jar of river water that remembered being ocean, a child's shoe that had outlived its owner by forty years and still turned to face the door each night. But there were more wards now. He counted seven layers woven into the entryway alone, and the air tasted of copper, which meant blood had been spent here recently. Vess had never been one to pay in blood if coin would do. Blood meant she was afraid.

She sat them at a table lit by a single candle and did not offer tea, which frightened Yara more than the wards. Vess Corhane performed hospitality the way other thieves performed alibis. Skipping it meant she thought the performance was wasted on what was coming.

"Say it again," she told the child.

Tam looked at Yara. Yara nodded. It cost him something, giving permission — everything about this house cost him something — but the boy spoke anyway, softly, in that language that made the candle flame stand up straight and attentive:

*"The door remembers what was buried. The hill keeps the king. The king keeps nothing."*

Vess closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were wet, and Yara realized he had never in his life seen Vess Corhane cry, not even the day the Wardens burned her sister's name out of her in the courtyard of the Assize Hall, and she had watched it happen with her chin up and her hands folded like a woman at prayer.

"That's not a spell," she said. "It's not a charm. It's not even a language, properly speaking. It's older than languages. We call it the First Tongue because we have nothing older to call it." She turned to Yara. "Where did you find him?"

"He found me. My doorstep, eleven nights ago. No name, no origin. He speaks it in his sleep."

"And your charms?"

"Cracking. All of them. And lately—" He hesitated. He had sworn off telling anyone anything, but that oath had died in Sefa's empty stall three nights ago, along with whatever safety Sefa might have found in the Warden cells, if such a thing existed. "Lately they've been whispering. Every memory I've lifted in three years has a piece of the same dream in it. A crowned man kneeling at a door in a hill. I checked. I checked every charm in my inventory. It's there. Buried deep, like sediment. Like it came with the ground they were dug from."

Vess was quiet for a long time. The candle flame leaned toward Tam, then away, as if uncertain which of them it should be afraid of.

"Show me," she said. "Your inventory."

* * *

Yara laid the charms out on her table in rows, wrapped in their silk like the dead laid out for viewing, which was not far from what they were. Each one was a stolen summer, a stolen grief, a stolen first kiss — pressed flat, sealed, sold as harmless things. Luck-charms. Sleep-charms. A widow in Tanners' Row wore her late husband's last morning around her neck and called it comfort.

Vess bent over them and did not touch them. Instead she hovered her hand above each in turn, reading them the way a blind woman reads faces.

"Dream residue," she murmured. "Of course. You lifted these memories secondhand — from people who'd been near the palace, near the court, near *him*. The dream leaks. It's been leaking for ten years, and every thief in this city has been carrying drops of it in their stock without knowing." She straightened. "Do you know what you are, Yara?"

"A thief," he said. "You made very sure I knew that."

"No." She tapped the table beside the charms. "*This.* You're a courier. Three years of running errands you never agreed to, delivering pieces of a single message to every corner of this city. And now the message has grown legs and knocked on your door." She looked at Tam. "Because it recognized its courier."

Yara's mouth had gone dry. "What message?"

Vess pulled out a chair and sat down slowly, like a woman whose knees had suddenly remembered her age. For a moment she looked less like the legend who had trained him and more like what she was: an old criminal in a room full of dangerous antiques, afraid of something for the first time in twenty years.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "I took a job. The kind of job I stopped taking after that night. No broker, no intermediary. A man in a gray cloak met me in the salt fog on the Lantern Stair and paid me triple my rate in crown mint. Fresh coin. He wanted one memory taken, cleanly, whole, no residue. And he wanted me to carry it out of the city and lose it somewhere it could never be found again." Her eyes flicked to Tam. "The memory belonged to the king."

The candle guttered. Neither Yara nor Vess moved to shield it.

"The king," Yara repeated. "Old Aldric? He's been on the throne—"

"Not Aldric. Aldric's father. King Maerin the Second, may the Veil keep him, which it apparently has." Vess laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the sound of a lock breaking. "I went up through the servant passages into the royal bedchamber — I was younger then, thinner, better at hating myself — and I stood over a sleeping king and reached into his head. And do you know what I found? Not state secrets. Not the location of treasury gold. I found a dream. The same dream, worn smooth from repetition. A crowned man kneeling at a door set into a hill, and behind the door, something breathing. Something patient. And the king in the dream wasn't kneeling because he was forced to." Her voice dropped. "He was kneeling because he was grateful."

Tam made a small sound. Yara looked over. The boy had gone very still, the way children go still when they hear something they almost understand, and his hand had crept across the table until it rested against one of Yara's wrapped charms. The silk beneath his fingers was glowing faintly, like coal remembering fire.

"What did you do with it?" Yara asked. "The memory. You were supposed to lose it."

"I tried." Vess's hands, the beautiful knife-hands, were trembling. "I carried it north for six days. I buried it under a cairn in the Graywater hills. I came back to the city and told myself it was done. And then, two years later, I'm working a job in the Temple Quarter and I reach into some canon's mind and there it *is* — the door, the hill, the kneeling king. It had crawled back. Memories taken from a king don't stay lost, Yara. They go home. Or they try." She looked at the boy, and her face did something complicated and terrible. "Or they wait until someone gives them a body to walk around in."

"You think he's—" Yara couldn't say it. Saying it would make it real, and it was already too real; it had been real since the night the knocking woke him.

"I don't think anything," Vess said. "Thinking is for people with time. I *know* that the First Tongue hasn't been spoken aloud in Corvane since the founding, because speaking it is the one crime the Wardens unmake you for without record, without trial, without ash. And I know that a child is sitting at my table speaking it fluently, and that the king's dream has been seeping into every memory lifted in this city for years." She stood, abruptly, and began pulling books from her shelves with a violence Yara had never seen in her. "And I know that ten years ago, King Maerin's son stopped aging. Stopped changing. The court calls it piety. The physicians call it constitution. I call it a glove with nothing inside it but hunger."

The words landed in the room like dropped glass. Tam's hand was still on the charm. The glow had spread to the silk beside it, and then the next, and Yara watched his entire inventory light up row by row like a city catching fire, and from within the silk, faintly, all of them began to whisper — dozens of stolen voices, murmuring in unison, in the First Tongue:

*The hill keeps the king. The king keeps nothing.*

"Stop it," Yara said hoarsely. "Make him stop."

"You don't stop that," Vess snapped. "You *listen*." But her hands shook as she swept the charms back into their box, and when she turned to face Yara fully, he saw that she had already moved past fear into calculation, which was worse, because Vess calculating meant Vess about to spend something irreplaceable.

"There's a place," she said. "North, past the Graywaters, where the founding records say the first bargain was struck. I've avoided it my whole life the way you avoid the edge of a roof in a dream. If that door is real, and if this child came out of anywhere, he came out of there — or he was made to be a key to it. Either way, the Wardens will trace the resonance eventually. They'll trace it here, to me, to you, and they will not bother with trials. So." She began stuffing provisions into a satchel with quick, brutal economy. "We leave tonight. All three of us. And on the road, you can tell me why you really came here, Yara, because it wasn't for the history lesson. You knew that before you knocked."

Yara said nothing.

"Oh, I know you," Vess said, softer now, and that softness was the cruelest thing she'd done all evening. "I carved the person out of you myself, remember? I know exactly what's left. You came here because you needed to look at me. Because the boy scares you, and the whispers scare you, and the only person alive who understands what it costs to hollow yourself out and keep walking—" she slung the satchel over her shoulder, "—is the one who did it to you. Isn't that right?"

The candle flame had gone still. Tam was watching Yara with those old, old eyes, waiting, it seemed, for his answer as much as Vess was.

"It's right," Yara said finally. "Now teach me how to unmake what you made. Before the Wardens arrive. Because they're coming, Vess. They followed the resonance to Sefa, and they'll follow it here, and this time there won't be tunnels to run through."

Something flickered across Vess's face at Sefa's name — grief, quickly filed away, like a coin palmed by a pickpocket.

"Then we have perhaps an hour," she said, and blew out the candle. "And Yara — the first thing you need to learn about the First Tongue is that it doesn't take words. It takes payment. Everything spoken in it costs the speaker something true." In the dark, her voice was almost gentle. "So pray you still have something left to spend."

Outside, far off toward the Assize Hall, a bell began to ring the alarm pattern — three strokes, pause, three strokes — and in the silence after it, Yara could swear he heard the charms whispering still, patiently, from inside their box:

*The hill keeps the king.*

*The king keeps nothing.*
