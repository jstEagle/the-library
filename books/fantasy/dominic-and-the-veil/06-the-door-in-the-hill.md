## 6. The Door in the Hill

Vess Corhane's house stood where the river forgot itself — a crooked stone place at the edge of the Sump district, where the water went stagnant and the city's maps simply gave up. Yara had not been there in three years. He had sworn he never would be again.

The child's hand was warm in his. That was the strange thing. Everything else about the night was cold — the fog off the river, the silence where the market's noise should have been, the way the charms in his satchel had gone quiet as drowned men since they'd left the tunnels. But the child's hand was warm, and it held his the way a small hand holds a large one: completely, without any awareness that holding could ever be a thing done halfway.

"You said she was cruel," the child said. He spoke Corvanish now, mostly. He'd learned it in three days, the way other children learned songs.

"I said she was the best thief in the city," Yara said. "Cruel is a word people add on their own."

"Is it true, what she added?"

Yara didn't answer. The door of the house was black oak, older than the district around it, and it opened before he knocked — because of course it did. Vess had always known when he was coming. It was one of the hundred small violences of being raised by a memory-thief: she had been inside his head, once, deeply enough that some part of her still lived there, keeping watch.

"Come in before the fog does," Vess said from the dark of the doorway. She was smaller than he remembered. That was the other thing time did to the people who had hollowed you — it made them small.

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath it, something Yara had never smelled there before: fear. Vess Corhane did not fear things. She had once picked a memory out of a Warden captain's mind while he held her wrist, and she had laughed about it for a year afterward.

Now she stood with her back to a cold hearth, and she did not look at the child. That was the first sign. Everyone looked at the child. He was six or seven and had appeared out of nowhere speaking a dead language; he was the kind of thing eyes went to whether they wanted to or not.

Vess was looking at Yara instead, and her face was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Say it again," she said.

"He speaks the First Tongue. The charms started whispering. Every memory I've taken in three years has a piece of the same dream in it — a king, kneeling, a door in a hill. And when the Wardens came, they were following resonance. Real resonance. Sefa's in a cell because of it, and I—" He stopped. His voice had started climbing. He brought it down. "You went pale when I told you what he speaks. You. I've seen you pick a lock with a Warden's own fingers. So say it again, whatever it was you said before I knocked. Say it where he can hear it."

Vess's eyes went, finally, to the child. Something moved through her face — not softness. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing a debt walk through her door with warm hands and a patient expression.

"Sit down, Yara."

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit." She pulled a chair from the table herself, turned it, set it down with a gentleness that was worse than shouting. "Because I'm going to tell you what the First Tongue is, and what it was for, and why every thief in this city who ever touched a king's dream is already dead or running. And then you're going to understand that the small ugly life you've been so busy regretting is a thread in something that started before Corvane had a name. And your knees will want the chair."

Yara sat. The child climbed into his lap without asking, the way children do when they have decided a lap is theirs, and Yara found his arms had closed around the boy before his mind agreed to anything.

Vess began to speak.

* * *

"Every kingdom is built on a lie," she said. "That's not cynicism. It's engineering. You can't make ten thousand people obey one man without a lie somewhere in the foundation. Most kingdoms, the lie is small. A false father. A stolen crown. A god who never answered.

"Corvane's lie is older than that. Corvane's lie is *hungry*."

She crossed to a shelf, and Yara watched her hands — those hands, the first hands that had ever reached into his mind, that had taken his childhood out of him like a splinter and left him smooth and new and empty at nine years old. She took down a book. Not a book — a ledger, bound in grey leather, swollen with inserted pages, and Yara's stomach dropped, because he knew what a memory-thief's ledger was. He kept one himself. Everyone in the trade did. It was the only confession the law allowed them.

She didn't open it. She just held it, the way you'd hold a wound closed.

"Four hundred years ago," Vess said, "there was no Corvane. There were the hill clans, and the river clans, and they were at each other's throats the way clans are. And there was a man — we'd call him a warlord; the histories call him Aldric the Founding, which tells you who wrote the histories. Aldric was losing. He was clever and he was brave and he was losing, because clever and brave only carry you so far against more spears.

"And Aldric went into the hills, alone, to a place the clans wouldn't hunt. And there was a door."

The fire had gone out hours ago, but the room got colder. Yara told himself it was the fog coming off the river. The child in his lap had gone very still — not asleep. Listening the way stone listens.

"A door in the side of a hill," Vess said, "older than the hill, maybe. Older than hills. And behind it, something that had been there since before there was anything to be hungry *in*. The old stories call it the Veil, because that's what it showed them — a curtain between what is and what isn't, and behind the curtain, an appetite. It doesn't want gold. It doesn't want blood, particularly. Blood is cheap. It wants *memory*. The thing a person is, the whole of it — every summer and every grief and every name. It eats what you were, and what's left is a body that walks and smiles and pays its taxes."

"And Aldric made a bargain," Yara said. His mouth was dry.

"Aldric made a bargain." Vess set the ledger on the table. "He gave it memories — not his own; he wasn't that stupid, not at first — and in exchange it taught him the First Tongue. The language the thing speaks. The language you use to *bind* it, because it can't be killed, only bound, and the binding is a sentence spoken in a grammar no human mouth was built for. Aldric spoke the binding. He shut the door. He came down out of the hills, and within five years every clan banner in the river valley was burned or bowed, and Corvane was founded on the one law that has never once been broken since: *magic is unmaking.*"

"Because—" Yara started.

"Because a king who got his crown from a thing behind a door cannot afford competition." Vess's voice was flat. "Every hedge-witch and charm-seller and memory-thief since the founding has been burned or unmade not because magic threatens the realm, Yara. Because magic *recognizes* magic. A Warden's craft and a Veil's craft are sisters, and the crown has spent four hundred years making sure no one learns the family resemblance."

The child shifted in Yara's lap. "He's wrong," the boy said quietly.

Vess stopped. Yara felt the boy's heart beating against his own, quick and small.

"What did you say?" Vess asked, and her voice had changed — the thief's voice, the careful one.

"The binding isn't a sentence," the child said. He was looking at the middle distance, at something none of them could see, or that only he could see. "It's a *song*. And a song needs a voice. And the voice gets tired." He looked up at Vess with those eyes that were too old and too young at once. "The voice got tired."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Yara to hear the river, far off, moving its stagnant water.

"Ten years ago," Vess said at last, and now she did open the ledger, and her hands were not steady, "the old king died. The one before the one on the throne now. And his son was crowned — King Maren, fourth of that name. I was at the coronation. Not invited. Working. A coronation is a harvest, if you're a thief; every noble in the realm in one room, drunk on ceremony, their memories loose as purses.

"And I stole from the king. I know how that sounds. I stole from *everyone*; his crown just made him one purse among two hundred. I took a memory off the surface of him — a small one, the kind you take when you want to be paid and gone. A dream. A king kneeling before a door in a hill."

Yara's arms tightened around the child. "The dream. The one in my charms."

"The one in your charms, yes, and in the charms of every thief who's worked this city in the last decade, because it's not one dream, it's a *leak*. The binding is failing, and when a binding fails, the thing it holds starts dreaming outward, and everyone downstream gets wet." She turned a page. Her finger found a line. "I took that memory twenty years— ten years ago, and I sold it, and I thought nothing of it, because a dream is a dream. And then, three years later, I took a job. A private commission. Someone in the palace wanted a rival's mind softened, and the pay was obscene, and I got inside the king's own chambers to do it, and I saw him up close."

"What did you see?"

"I saw a man whose eyes reflected nothing." Vess looked at Yara, and made him look back. "You've seen it. Don't lie to me, boy, I can see it in your face that you've seen it."

He had. He hadn't. He'd seen the crowned figure drowning in shadow when the child touched him — a glimpse, a nightmare given to him by a small warm hand — and he had told himself it was a vision, a metaphor, the kind of thing magic did when it wanted your attention. But *eyes that reflect nothing* — he had heard that phrase before, from his own mouth, describing the king at a distance, at a procession, and had thought: *tired man. Kings are tired.*

"I saw a man wearing a crown the way a glove wears a hand," Vess said. "From the inside. The Veil didn't conquer Corvane, Yara. It didn't need to. The binding is a song, and the song needs a voice, and the voice got tired, and the thing behind the door has spent ten years *singing through him*. The king on the throne is not a man. He's a mouth."

The child made a small sound. Not a word. Yara looked down and the boy was pressing his fist against his own chest, hard, as if something in there ached.

"And the Wardens," Yara said. "The raids. The resonance."

"Are the Veil's immune system." Vess closed the ledger. "It can feel the First Tongue the way a spider feels the web. Anything that speaks it, anything that carries it, anything that *dreams it* — it sends its Wardens, and the Wardens unmake, and the crown calls it law, and everyone applauds. Your charms have been leaking king-dreams into the night market for three years. You haven't been selling memories, Yara. You've been *seeding* it. You've been the courier of the thing that's eating the realm, and you didn't know, because that's what it does. It doesn't need your loyalty. It only needs your smallness. It needs every person in this city to be exactly as small as you've worked so hard to be."

Yara stood up so fast the child slid from his lap and caught himself against the chair, and Yara didn't reach for him, and hated himself for not reaching, and reached.

"No," he said. "No. I steal *trinkets*. Last summers. First kisses. I have rules—"

"You have rules." Vess's voice cracked like ice. "I taught you your rules, boy. I wrote them. *Never take a name. Never take a grief. Never take more than the owner can live without.* And do you know why I wrote them? Not mercy. *Scale.* Because I stole from the king, once, one small dream, and ten years later the whole city is whispering in a dead language. There is no small theft from a thing like that. There is no *small*. There's only the appetite, and what you fed it, and how long it takes to notice."

The child had come to stand beside Yara now, his hand finding Yara's again, and when he spoke, he spoke in the First Tongue — three words, soft, that Yara felt in his teeth and in the cracked charms of his satchel and somewhere older than both.

Vess went white.

"What did he say?" Yara demanded. "What did he say, Vess?"

She was looking at the child the way you look at a door you thought was painted on a wall.

"He said," Vess said slowly, "*the door remembers me.*" She swallowed. "And he's right. Because the binding needs a key, Yara. Every binding does. Aldric bound the Veil with the First Tongue, but a spoken binding frays — it needs something living to hold the shape of it, something made *of* the binding, something that *is* the door's memory of being shut. The old kings kept it. Fed it. Hid it. And when the Veil took the throne, the key was lost, and the binding started to fail, and the dreams started to leak."

She looked at the small warm hand closed around Yara's fingers.

"And now the key has walked out of wherever they kept it, and knocked on the door of a charm-thief, and is standing in my kitchen speaking the founding language like it's his mother's lullaby. Which means one of two things, Yara. Either the Veil lost him—"

"Or it let him go," Yara said.

"Or it let him go," Vess agreed. "Because a key that's hidden can't be followed. But a key that runs—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The fog pressed at the windows. Somewhere out in the Sump, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then stopped all at once, the way dogs stop when a door somewhere opens that shouldn't.

Yara looked down at the child, who was looking back up at him with perfect, terrible trust.

"Can it find him here?" Yara asked.

"It can find him anywhere," Vess said. "The question isn't where we hide. The question is the door in the hill. Because there's only one way this ends, boy, and it's not hiding, and it's not running, and it's certainly not selling charms. The binding can be *renewed* — spoken whole, at the door, by a voice that carries it. And there are exactly two voices in this city that touch the First Tongue." Her eyes went from the child to Yara, and Yara felt the floor of the last three years of his life tilt beneath him. "One of them is six years old. The other one has been leaking king-dreams through his own merchandise and doesn't know a word of the grammar. So you're going to learn, Yara. From him. Starting now. Because the Wardens took Sefa tonight, and they will have found her ledger, and her ledger will have your name in it, and by morning there won't be a hole in this city deep enough."

Outside, the fog had begun to glow. Faintly. From below — from under the streets, under the river, under everything — a light the color of a held breath, rising.

The child squeezed Yara's hand and said, in Corvanish, very quietly: "It's already looking."
