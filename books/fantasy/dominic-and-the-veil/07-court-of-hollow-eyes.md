## 7. Court of Hollow Eyes

The palace of Corvane admitted strangers the way a mouth admits food: slowly, with much tasting.

Yara learned this over four days of waiting in the queue for licensed charm-sellers, a line of forty-odd men and women huddled under the Colonnade of Beneficence with their wares laid out on velvet scraps — luck-knots, sleep-smooths, grief-easers, all of them legal, all of them frauds. Real magic got you unmade. Fake magic got you invited to court. The distinction struck him, as he stood stamping feeling into his boots against the cold, as the single funniest thing about the kingdom, and he did not laugh.

His papers were perfect. He had made them himself, which was why they were perfect: a guild license in the name of Master Ambrose Fenn, charm-wright, purveyor to the households of the northern lords; letters of character from three minor nobles whose faces he had borrowed and whose signatures he had *improved*. Vess would have approved. Vess would also have told him he was an idiot, and she would have been right, but Sefa was in a Warden cell and the child was hidden under a floor in the tunnels with three days of bread and strict instructions not to speak a single word of anything, and Yara had run out of ways to be careful.

The First Tongue had left a mark on the palace's attention. That was the whole reason he was doing something this stupid. Wardens had come to his street, had walked his market, had stood outside Sefa's shop with instruments like tuning forks carved from bone, listening for echoes. If they could trace the resonance here, then so could he — backwards, following the same thread, to whatever it was at the other end that had taught a language to a child who said she'd been no one before.

*It's already looking.* The child's words had kept him awake since the tunnels. Not *it will look.* Already. Looking.

"Master Fenn."

A steward in silver-grey held open a door tall enough to drive a cart through. Beyond it: warmth, beeswax, the smell of roast quail and lilies, and beneath both, faint as a bad memory, something cold and mineral, like the air off a deep well.

Yara picked up his case of charms and walked into the mouth.

* * *

The Court of Corvane in winter was a machine for pretending, and Yara — professional pretender — hated how good it was.

He had expected decadence. What he found was order. Every lord and lady moved through the Hall of Audiences along grooves worn invisible into the marble, bowing at depths calibrated to the millimeter, laughing at intervals that felt scheduled. The music never stopped and never changed key. Servants glided with trays of wine no one drank. And everywhere, woven through the beauty like wire through a cake, were the Wardens — unarmed, unhelmed, dressed as secretaries and ladies-in-waiting and one extraordinarily bored-looking harpist — each of them, Yara realized within an hour, positioned exactly where they could watch everyone else.

He sold charms. That was his cover and his craft, and he worked it honestly, because the best lies are mostly true. A countess with liver-spotted hands bought a sleep-smooth and paid triple because he told her it had been blessed by a hermit. A young lord bought a courage-charm before what was clearly a very frightening marriage negotiation. Yara gave them all the same warm, empty patter, and they drank it down, and he thought: *this is what I am to these people even when I'm not lying. Furniture that talks.*

He was cataloguing exits — seven visible, two plausible, one that involved the wine cellar and a long swim — when the horns sounded.

Everyone stopped. Not startled-stopped. *Stopped*, the way clocks stop, all at once, mid-gesture, mid-sentence, a hundred people frozen between one breath and the next. Even the music ceased, the harpist's hands hovering above the strings.

Then the doors at the far end opened, and the King of Corvane came in to hold his evening court, and every frozen body thawed into motion again, smoother than before.

Yara had seen the king twice before in his life, both times from half a mile away at public ceremonies: a distant gilt figure on a balcony, a horseman behind a screen of lancers. He had assumed, without ever thinking about it, that the man was large. Kings usually were, in the mind's eye. This one was not. Aldric IV was a middle-sized man going soft, perhaps fifty, with thinning hair combed carefully over a pink scalp and the slightly stooped shoulders of someone who had spent thirty years sitting on thrones. His face was pleasant. His robes were magnificent without being absurd. He smiled as he entered, and the smile was so perfectly calibrated — warm but not familiar, regal but not cold — that Yara felt his own face begin to answer it before his brain caught up and clamped down.

Then the king passed within ten feet of him, and Yara looked into his eyes, and everything in Yara went quiet the way the forest goes quiet before a landslide.

They reflected nothing.

Not *little*. Not *dull*. Nothing. Yara had spent three years looking into eyes at the moment memory left them; he knew every kind of emptiness eyes could hold — the blank of exhaustion, the white-out of grief, the flat shine of the dying. This was none of those. Those were rooms with the lights out. This was a room that had never been a room. The king's eyes caught the thousand candles of the hall and gave back only darkness, smooth and total, and Yara understood with animal certainty that whatever used those eyes was not looking *out* of them. It was looking *through* them at something behind Yara, or below him, or — the thought arrived with a taste like iron — *inside* him.

The king paused. Half a step. His head turned by a degree, toward Yara, toward the little table of charms, and the pleasant smile did not waver and the nothing eyes rested on him for the length of one heartbeat.

Yara had faced knives. He had once been thrown out of Sefa's back door into an alley by a debtor with a fish-gutting hook, and he had handled that. This was worse. This was being *read*, the way he read others — turned inside out, thumbed through, assessed for value — and finding nothing there to read, nothing the thing wanted, and understanding that this was the only reason he was still standing: he was beneath its appetite. A crumb too small to eat.

The king moved on. The court breathed.

"First presentation, Master Fenn?" said a voice at his elbow. A steward, silver-grey, smiling professionally. "You've gone rather pale."

"The honor of it," Yara managed. "I hadn't expected to be so close."

"No one does," said the steward, and there was something strange in his voice, some sediment of meaning Yara couldn't parse, and then the man was gone, gliding, and Yara made himself smile at the next customer and sell her a charm against nightmares that cost him more to make than she would ever know.

* * *

Court proceeded. Yara watched the king the way a gambler watches a marked deck.

Public warmth. That was the phrase he settled on, and it fit like a key in a lock. The king received petitioners with evident delight, asked after their children by name, wept — actually wept, a single dignified tear — at the story of a widow whose sons had died in the river floods. He granted boons, settled disputes, joked with the Lord Marshal about his hunting record. And every gesture was flawless, and every gesture was empty, and once you saw it you could not stop seeing it: the warmth sat on him like paint sits on glass. It ran a fraction late, always, arriving a half-second after the moment called for it, like a translation spoken by someone consulting the original text.

And in the pauses — the tiny dead spaces between petitions, when the hall's attention slackened — the mask didn't slip. It simply *stopped*, and for those few seconds the king stood motionless among his court like a coat on a hook, and no one else seemed to notice, and Yara pressed his thumbnail into his palm until it bled to keep from screaming.

Because he had seen this before. Three nights ago, in a glimpse delivered through a child's touch: a crowned figure drowning in shadow, mouth open, screaming without sound. He had thought then that it was a warning. Standing in the Hall of Audiences with his heart hammering against his ribs, Yara understood it had been a portrait. The drowning man was real. He was right there, waving and smiling and granting boons, and whatever wore him was drinking something through his ruined insides, and the court applauded the flavor.

The evening wore on. Petitions thinned. Yara was packing his case, planning his route to the wine cellar, when the hall's rhythm changed.

She came forward in a rustle of grey — a servant girl, nineteen or twenty maybe, laundry by her raw knuckles, escorted by two chamber officials who looked deeply embarrassed about something. She was crying already. The sound she was making wasn't loud. It was the crying of someone trying very hard not to cry, which is the worst crying there is.

"Your Majesty." The senior official bowed. "Forgive the interruption. There's been a — a discrepancy. Mara here has been found keeping contraband. Personal effects. Unregistered keepsakes." He said it like a man reciting a tax code, and Yara heard the true words underneath, heard them the way you hear a scream under floorboards: *memories. Unregistered memories.*

The law was old and stupid and absolute. Servants of the crown surrendered their pasts at the palace gate — not all of them, officially just *sensitive* material, family matters, matters of sentiment — and the surrender was administered by the Wardens, cleanly, for everyone's protection. Everyone knew this. No one spoke of it. Yara had known it for years the way you know where the graveyard is: as geography, not as horror.

Mara had kept something back. That was all. A scrap of her own life, smuggled in a locket of the skull, hidden for years.

"I'm sorry," she was saying, over and over, to the king, of all people, as if the king were a person who could forgive. "I'm sorry, I only — it's my mother. She died when I was eleven and I keep her voice, I only kept her voice, singing, it's only the one song, please, it isn't worth anything to anyone, it's only—"

"Hush now," said the king.

And the whole hall hushed, because the king had spoken, and the king stepped down from the dais — he *never* stepped down from the dais, Yara saw the courtiers' shock ripple outward like a stone in a pond — and crossed the marble to where the girl stood shaking, and took her hands in his, gently, like a father.

"You poor child," said the king, and his voice was warm as fresh bread. "Grief is a heavy thing to carry alone. You should never have had to hide it. Come. Give it to me."

Yara saw it happen. He was twenty feet away and he saw everything, and for the rest of his life he would see it.

The girl's face changed first. Relief, briefly — blessed, terrible relief, the relief of a hunted thing finally letting itself be found — and then confusion, and then a kind of slow astonishment, as if she were watching her own hands from a great distance. Her grip tightened on the king's fingers. Her mouth opened.

"There," said the king softly. "There. Wasn't that easy?"

She swayed. The officials steadied her arms, though their faces said they hadn't been instructed to, though their faces said they were as frightened as Yara was. And the girl — Mara — blinked up at the king with wet, bewildered eyes and said, in a voice gone strangely light, like a room swept out:

"I'm sorry. Have we met? Am I in trouble?"

"You're a good girl," said the king, patting her cheek. "Go back to your work."

She went. She walked back down the hall between the officials, upright, docile, wiping her cheeks with her apron, and halfway to the door she paused and frowned faintly, like someone trying to remember why they'd entered a room, and then kept walking, and Yara knew — knew in his teeth, in the thief's sense that lived in his hands — that she would spend the rest of her life with a hole in her shaped exactly like a woman singing, and never learn the shape of it, and never grieve properly either, because you cannot mourn what you don't know you lost.

And the king turned back to the court, smiling, ascending the dais, and as he climbed the steps Yara saw him do the thing he would remember on his deathbed, if he was permitted a deathbed:

The king closed his eyes. Just for a moment. Just for the length of a swallow. And his expression — the only crack in the entire evening, gone so fast most people would have missed it entirely, would have filed it under nothing — his expression was the expression of a man who has eaten well and knows it, and is ashamed, and finds the shame delicious.

*Appetite.* That was the word. Not hunger — appetite, with its implication of refinement, of courses, of a palate. The Veil did not feed the way beasts fed, tearing and gulping. It dined. It had arranged this — the discovery, the confession, the little ceremony of mercy — because memory tasted better given freely. Because fear salted it. Because the girl's love for her mother, offered up in the belief that the crown was a father's hand instead of an open throat, must have been the finest thing that thing had tasted in weeks, and it had wanted to savor the moment, walk down from the dais, take her hands, feel it pulse in her wrists while it drank.

The hall resumed its murmur. Wine circulated. The harpist began a new piece in the same key as the last.

Yara stood very still beside his table of harmless frauds and felt something break loose in his chest and settle somewhere lower, colder, permanent. He had spent three years telling himself a story about his trade: that he took only surplus, only pain, only things people were better off without. That numbness was a mercy he dealt in. Watching the king, he finally saw the story from outside, and it was small, and it was filthy, and it was the same story written in smaller handwriting. The difference between Yara Joubert and the thing on the throne was scale, and permission, and the fact that no one had yet handed Yara a kingdom to eat from.

He picked up his case. Seven exits, two plausible, one involving a cellar and a long cold swim.

He had gotten four steps toward the least plausible one when the steward in silver-grey materialized at his elbow again, smiling, holding a folded card sealed with the royal crest.

"Master Fenn. An unexpected honor." The steward's voice was professionally delighted. His eyes were professionally terrified. "Their Majesty requests your presence. Tomorrow, after evening court. A private commission — the Queen Mother suffers from dreams, and His Majesty understands you are a craftsman of unusual... sensitivity."

The seal on the card was black wax, and Yara, who noticed everything about seals, saw that it had been stamped with a signet ring worn on the wrong hand.

"Tell His Majesty," Yara heard himself say, "that I would be honored."

The steward bowed and glided away, and Yara stood alone in the beautiful hall with the card burning in his pocket like a coal, and understood that he had just been invited to dinner by the meal.

Behind him, faintly, from the direction of the servants' stair, someone was humming — a girl with raw knuckles, walking back to her laundry, trying and failing to place a tune she no longer owned.

It was a lovely song. He could hear, even now, that it had been lovely.

He walked out through the Colonnade of Beneficence into the fog, and the fog glowed faintly from below, the color of a held breath, and this time he did not need a child to tell him what it meant.

It was already looking. And tomorrow, it had decided, it would look at him.
