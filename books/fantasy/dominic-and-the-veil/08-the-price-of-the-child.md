## 8. The Price of the Child

They had been running for two days when Yara made his mistake.

Not the running itself — he was good at running, had been good at it since before he could remember being anything else, which was its own kind of joke. The mistake was the well at Cresset's Cross, a hamlet of nine houses folded into a fold of hills where no one would think to look. He had been thirsty. The child had been thirsty. And thirst makes a man stupid in the specific way that comfort makes a man stupid: it lets him believe the world owes him one clean drink.

The well stood in the middle of the hamlet's green, ringed in old stones furred with moss, and the water came up cold and tasting of iron and deep places. The child drank with both hands cupped, water running down his chin, and for a moment — one stupid, mortal moment — he looked like exactly what he appeared to be: a boy of six or seven, exhausted, his shoes worn through at the toes, his eyes too old for his face but still, underneath the oldness, a child's eyes.

Yara was watching him drink when he felt the resonance.

It came through his teeth first, the way First Tongue always did, a hum in the jaw like a struck tuning fork buried in his skull. Then through the charms in his satchel — the ones that hadn't shattered in the raid, the ones he hadn't had the heart to throw away — a faint chorus of whispers, memories stirring in their glass like insects under a stone. And then, fainter, from the road below the hamlet: the particular cold signature of warded iron.

Three riders. No — four. He counted heartbeats of silence between the hoofbeats and made it four, maybe five, moving at a walk, which was worse than a gallop. Men who gallop are chasing. Men who walk are herding.

"Tam," he said, and used the child's cover-name gently, the way you'd touch a bird. "We're leaving."

The child looked up, water on his lips. "The gray men?"

"The gray men." The Wardens wore gray the way a knife wears a whetstone — not as color but as function. "Back through the houses. Don't run. Running is a story they know how to read."

They went through the hamlet's single lane, past a woman splitting kindling who didn't look up, past a dog that did look up and then, wisely, decided against barking. Yara's mind was already laying the route out behind his eyes: the drainage cut behind the last house, up through the bracken, over the spine of the hill, down into the trees where the memory-thieves' tunnels — the few that ran this far from Corvane — might still be open. He'd used this route twice before. It was good. It was safe.

It was a lie, and the lie had teeth, and the teeth were waiting at the drainage cut.

* * *

There were six of them, not five.

They came out of the cut's shadow the way smoke comes out of a banked fire, all at once and everywhere, and Yara understood with a lurch that the riders on the road had been the loud part of the trap, the part meant to be felt. These six had been here since before dawn, sitting in the cold among the nettles, warded so thoroughly that they read to his senses as nothing at all — as absence, as a hole in the world shaped like men.

The one in front was a woman with a face like a folded letter: closed, official, nothing to be read without breaking the seal. Her warden's iron hung at her belt in a fan of slender rods, each one etched with script Yara could not read but which his stolen memories flinched away from anyway. Unmaking rods. He had seen them used once, from a rooftop, across a market square. He had told himself afterward that he hadn't seen.

"Yara Joubert," the woman said. His name in her mouth sounded like an inventory entry. "Licensed charm-seller, unlicensed practitioner. You're charged with resonance crimes under the Statute of Unmaking, first and second degree. You'll come with us. The boy comes with us."

"The boy," Yara said, "is a hamlet child I gave water to."

The woman's eyes went to Tam and something moved behind them, the way a fish moves under ice. "The boy resonates in a dead language," she said. "The boy is the reason we've burned three days of the crown's iron getting here. The boy is not a hamlet child." She held out one hand, palm up, patient as a tax collector. "You can make this easy. Easy is a thing we offer. It's not a thing we are."

Yara's mind went through its old catalog the way a gambler's hand goes through a worn deck. Bribe — no coin in Corvane bought back a Warden once the iron was drawn. Run — the cut behind them, the hill, the trees, and six pairs of legs fresher than his, and Tam's small legs freshest of all and least able to use them. Talk — he was talking; it was accomplishing the precise nothing that talking always accomplished.

And underneath the catalog, quiet as sediment, was the other thing. The thing he had been carrying since the night market, since Sefa's scream cut off in the middle, since he had run with a child's hand in his and told himself that running *was* doing something.

The thing was: he had one weapon, and it was a monstrous one, and he had sworn — not out loud, never out loud, but sworn the way a scar is sworn — that he would never use it on a person whole.

"Yara," Tam said. Small voice. He wasn't looking at the Wardens. He was looking at Yara's hands, which had gone, without his permission, to the satchel's clasp.

"Don't," the child said.

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when he would lie awake cataloguing himself the way Vess had once catalogued him — Yara would try to reconstruct the decision and find that there wasn't one. That was the horror of it. There was no moment where he weighed the man he'd been against the thing he was about to do. There was only the woman's hand still open, palm up, patient; only Tam's shoulder under his own hand, thin as a sparrow's; only the arithmetic of one life against six, done so fast and so far beneath thought that it felt less like a choice than like a reflex. A flinch. The body protecting what it had decided mattered.

He opened the satchel and took out a memory.

Not a charm. A raw one — the soldier's summer, the first theft the reader had ever witnessed, the dying man's last warm afternoon lifted whole from a mind going cold. He had carried it for three years the way a man carries a stone he can't explain. He held it now between two fingers, and it glowed the way stolen things glow: softly, guiltily, like a window lit in a house whose owners are away.

"Last chance for easy," the woman said.

"It's a strange thing," Yara said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to, "how much of a person is just — weather. A few good afternoons. Take those, and see what's left standing."

He spoke the First Tongue.

He only knew four words of it — Tam had been teaching him in the stolen hours of the last two days, patiently, the way you'd teach a child, because that was what Tam was — but the language did not seem to care about fluency. The words came out of him sounding older than his mouth, and the memory in his hand flared white, and he *pushed*.

He had stolen from one mind at a time, always. One mind was a door; you knocked, you slipped the latch, you took what was owed. Six minds at once was not six doors. It was a wall coming down.

The soldier's summer poured out of him and into them.

He felt it go — felt the warm afternoon, the river, the girl with the flour on her arms, the taste of stolen plums, all of it leaving him like blood leaving a wound, and he *aimed* it, spread it, drove it into six minds at once with a force that made his nose run red and his vision spark. And because a memory cannot enter a mind empty-handed, because taking and giving are the same act in the First Tongue, the language reached back into the Wardens as it poured in and pulled.

It pulled everything loose.

The woman with the folded-letter face went first. He watched her whole self come apart like a knot untied from the inside — twenty years of warden's discipline, a father's funeral, a dog she'd had at nine, the taste of the tea she drank every morning of her adult life, all of it lifting out of her in a silver-gray mist that the soldier's summer settled into like rain into a plowed field. She swayed. She smiled. It was the most terrible thing Yara had ever seen: a smile arriving on a face that had forgotten what it was for, spreading slow and vacant and *happy*, the happiness of a man who has just set down a weight he cannot remember carrying.

The other five went the same way, in a stagger, like dominoes falling in honey. Their iron rods clattered to the nettles. One of them sat down where he stood, in the wet grass, and laughed — a small, wondering laugh, the laugh of someone seeing sunlight for the first time. Another began humming. None of them reached for a weapon. There was no one left in there to reach.

Six smiling blanks, standing and sitting in a drainage cut, wearing the bodies of the crown's most feared hunters, and in each of them now nothing but one borrowed summer: a river, a girl, plums, sun.

Yara stood in the middle of what he had done with blood on his lip and his heart going like a trapped bird, and he waited to feel victorious.

What he felt instead was the silence of the child.

* * *

Tam had not run. Tam had not screamed. Tam had stood exactly where he'd been standing, three feet behind Yara's left shoulder, and watched everything — and Yara, turning now, found the boy's face and understood that he had been wrong about the worst thing in the world. He had thought the worst thing would be the Wardens taking the child. It was not. The worst thing was the child's face as he looked up at Yara, and the expression on it.

It was not the expression people wore when they saw magic. Corvane's poor saw magic all the time, in the cracks, in the dark — it was the city's open secret, punished and practiced in the same alleys. No. The expression Tam wore was older and more specific than fear of the forbidden. It was recognition.

"You did what the gray king does," the child said.

The words landed like a blade going in flat, no edge, all weight.

"No," Yara said. "No, listen — they were going to take you. They were going to unmake you, do you understand what that is? What they did to Sefa? What they'd have done to—"

"You took their *afternoons*," Tam said. His voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; the child spoke the way a judge speaks, quietly, from a bench that had been built long before either of them was born. "The river. The plums. The girl with the flour." He had heard it all, Yara realized, heard it in the First Tongue's passing, the way you hear the shape of water through a wall. "They were smiling. They didn't know their own names and they were *smiling*, and you made them smile, and that was worse than if they'd cried."

Yara opened his mouth and found that every defense he owned was made of the same rotten wood. *They would have killed you. It was them or you. I had no choice.* All true. All beside the point. The Veil in the king's body would have said the same things, in the same order, in the same reasonable voice — he had heard it, that day in the palace, feeding on a servant's memory of her mother, and the thing that had frightened him most had not been the hunger but the *tidiness* of it, the way appetite dressed itself in necessity.

"I'm not them," he said, and heard how it sounded. A man insisting he is not a thing, to a child, standing in the thing's aftermath.

Tam looked at the six of them. The woman with the folded-letter face had knelt down in the grass and was tracing the river with one finger on the air, following a current only she could see, her mouth curved in that borrowed, vacant joy. The humming one had begun to hum a second song, harmonizing with himself.

"They'll die like that," Tam said. Not a question. "Won't they. People can't live on one afternoon. The rest of them is gone, and it doesn't grow back."

Yara didn't answer, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie, and the child had spent his whole short impossible life being lied to by kings.

"I stole to live," Yara said finally, and the words came out of him like stones pulled from a wound, one at a time. "I told myself that every time. I stole from a dying man and told myself he wasn't using it anymore. I stole from grief and told myself I was selling comfort. I built a whole—" his hand made a shape in the air, a cage, a ledger, a life, "—I built a whole cathedral of *telling myself*, and I believed it, right up until the moment I didn't have to believe it anymore, and then I did this." He looked at his own hands. They were just hands. That was the obscenity of it. They should have been different hands now. "You're right to be afraid of me. I want you to know that. I want you to know that I know."

The child was quiet for a long time. Somewhere up the hill, a wood pigeon called, indifferent, ordinary, obscene in its ordinariness.

"You gave the summer back," Tam said at last. "The soldier's. You didn't keep it. You spent it." He frowned, working it through, and Yara saw the oldness in him again — the remnant, the thing Vess had tried to name and failed, the piece of something vast wearing a small boy like a coat. "Spending isn't stealing. Spending is — " he reached for the First Tongue and gave Yara a word he didn't know yet, a word that tasted like *burning a candle to see by*, like *paying*, and then he translated it badly into ordinary speech: "Spending is what the founding king wouldn't do. He kept everything. He kept everything and it all rotted."

He looked up at Yara, and the fear in his face had not gone — Yara never wanted it to go, not entirely, not if there was any justice in the world — but it had made room beside it for something else. Not forgiveness. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Something more useful than forgiveness.

"Never again," Tam said. It wasn't a plea. It was a term, the way terms are laid down before a treaty between exhausted powers. "Not on people. Not whole people. If you do it again, I'll — " and here the child faltered, because what could he do, what threat could a six-year-old remnant of a dead king's humanity make that meant anything — and then his chin came up, and he found it. "I'll remember it. Forever. I'm the one who remembers. That's what I'm *for*. And you'll have to live with being remembered."

Yara laughed — a cracked, awful, grateful sound, half sob, the laugh of a man who has just been sentenced by the only court whose verdict he fears. "Deal," he said.

* * *

They left the Wardens in the cut, because there was nothing else to do with them. Yara dragged the iron rods into the nettles and took the patrol's orders from the woman's saddlebag — a folded sheet of warded paper that read like a sentence of death written in bureaucratic ink, and which he read three times before the words stopped swimming.

*Subject Joubert to be taken alive where feasible. The child is not to be taken alive at all. The child is to be unmade on site, and the unmaking witnessed, and the ash sealed. By order of the Crown. By order of —*

The signature was not a name. It was a single glyph in the First Tongue, and Yara recognized it now, the way you recognize a face in a nightmare after you've woken: the sign for *door*.

The order had come from the king. From the thing wearing the king. And it meant the Veil knew where they were — not roughly, not by rumor, but *precisely*, because there was only one person alive who could have told it the route they'd take out of Corvane, the safehouses, the tunnels, the name Yara used in the hill country.

Vess.

Vess, who had taught him everything. Vess, who had erased his childhood and kept it, forty years of theft folded into a ledger somewhere in the dark of the city. Vess, who had gone pale at the sound of the child's language and said, in a voice Yara had never heard her use, *I stole that memory myself*.

Either she had sold them. Or she had been taken, and had sold them the only way the taken ever can — by being unable not to. And either way, the map of their flight was in the enemy's hands, and every tunnel, every safehouse, every name was a room with the door already open.

Yara folded the order and put it inside his shirt, against his heart, where the cold of it could do the most good.

"Tam," he said. "We're not going to the tunnels. There's a woman we have to see. And after her—" he looked up the spine of the hill, toward the deep country, toward the old shrine roads, toward the door in the hill that haunted every stolen memory in his satchel, "—after her, I think we're going where all of this started."

The child took his hand. It was, Yara noted, the hand that had held the memory. The child took it anyway.

They walked north, and behind them, in the drainage cut, six smiling people hummed a dead soldier's summer to each other in the grass.
