## 10. The Language of Doors

The child taught him the way water teaches stone: patiently, without malice, and with the absolute certainty that it would win.

They sat on the floor of Vess Corhane's back room, which was less a room than a cavity between two walls of the old city, lit by a single oil lamp that had been burning — Yara suspected — since before either of them was born. The child sat cross-legged with his small hands flat on his knees, and he said the first word again.

"*Senneth.*"

The lamp flame leaned toward him like a listener.

"Say it," the child said, in Corvanese. He still spoke Corvanese the way a foreigner does, each word set down carefully, as if checking whether the floor would hold it. "Not with your mouth. With the place your mouth comes from."

"That's not an instruction. That's a riddle."

The child looked at him. Seven years old, maybe eight, with eyes the grey of rain about to decide something, and he did not blink, and he did not laugh, and Yara felt the familiar vertigo of being measured by someone who had been alive in some sense for centuries.

"Fine," Yara said. "*Senneth.*"

The word left his mouth and the room changed.

It was nothing dramatic. The lamp did not gutter; the walls did not bleed. But the air acquired a texture, a grain, and every charm in Vess's inventory — three years of Yara's work, hundreds of little glass vials and knotted cords and wax-sealed lozenges, each one holding a stolen summer or a first kiss or a soldier's last morning — every one of them *turned*. He felt them turn the way you feel a room full of people turn to look at you. They were listening. They had always been listening. He had simply never known what language they listened *in*.

"Stop," Yara said, and his voice came out wrong, scraped. "What did I just say?"

"You said *senneth*." The child tilted his head. "You said *open*. A door doesn't care who opens it. That is why they made laws." He said this without judgment, the way you'd report the weather. "Again."

"I don't think—"

"Again. You are bad at it. Being bad at it is safe. For now."

Yara laughed — a short, ugly sound — because the alternative was to admit his hands were shaking. Three years he had worked memory like a pickpocket works pockets: quick fingers, no eye contact, never look at what you lift. And here was a child telling him that all along he'd been handling the raw material of a language older than the crown, older than the law that would unmake him for so much as humming it, and that the only thing keeping him alive was incompetence.

"Again," the child said.

So Yara said it again. And again. And the night went on, and the lamp burned, and somewhere below them Corvane closed its shutters against the Wardens' curfew bells, and Yara Joubert learned to open doors he had spent his whole adult life pretending were walls.

* * *

The first thing that came back belonged to a baker's wife named Maren Ott, and it came back at two in the morning while Yara was trying to sleep on Vess's floor.

He had spoken *senneth* perhaps forty times that day, and the child had added a second word — *velloth*, *close*, though the child insisted the truer meaning was *keep* — and a third whose sound Yara could not hold onto, a word like a stairway descending into dark. He fell asleep exhausted, hollowed in a new way, and dreamed of a kitchen.

Not his kitchen. He knew that immediately, the way you know a borrowed coat. It was a wide kitchen with flour handprints on the doorframe at the height of a child's reach, and a woman with strong forearms and a burn scar on her wrist was pulling loaves from an oven, and she turned and saw him standing there and her face did something no face had ever done to him before.

She *recognized* him.

"Yara," she said. "Oh — oh, you've brought it back. You've brought my mornings back."

And the dream poured into him like water into a cracked vessel: kneading dough at dawn, twenty years of dawns, the weight of her daughter asleep against her shoulder, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke, her husband's cough from the room above, the whole warm ordinary architecture of a life — and underneath it, faint as a watermark, the moment Yara had lifted it out of her, three winters ago, in the night market, when she'd sold her happiest season for six coppers because her husband's lung-fever needed a physician and physicians needed coin.

He woke with tears on his face that were hers, tasting bread he had never baked, loving a child he had never met.

He lay on the cold floorboards and understood, with the slow horrible clarity of a man watching floodwater find every crack in his foundations, what the child had done. What the First Tongue *did*. It was not a weapon and not a spell, not exactly. It was a *returning*. Every word of it moved things toward their origin. Speak it near a stolen memory and the memory remembered where it belonged, and strained toward home like a dog hearing its name across a field.

His inventory was full of homes.

"Vess," he said into the dark. "Vess, wake up. We have a problem."

From the other cot, dry as old paper: "We have several. Rank them."

"The charms are going home."

Silence. Then the creak of her sitting up, and the scratch of flint, and lamplight, and Vess Corhane's face above him — sixty-odd years of thievery carved into it, eyes like coins worn smooth by handling. She looked, for once, genuinely unsettled.

"All of them?"

"Maren Ott's whole summer came back to me in a dream. Her *dream*, Vess. I was in it. She thanked me." He sat up, dragged both hands through his hair. "If speaking the Tongue returns what we take — then everything I've lifted in three years is a debt coming due. Every charm I sold. Every buyer. The memories will start walking back to their owners and those owners are going to remember selling them, remember *me*, remember—"

"Yes," Vess said. "That's what returning means. It's why none of us ever learned the Tongue, boy. Not because it's forbidden." She smiled, and it was the saddest expression he had ever seen on her. "Because it's honest."

* * *

By the third day, the back room sounded like a tide pool.

The charms whispered. Not in the child's voice anymore — in their own voices, in the voices of the people they'd been taken from, murmuring fragments of themselves through the glass and wax: *the harbor at dawn, the harbor at dawn, my brother's wedding, don't let go of the rope, don't let go.* The child walked among the shelves sometimes, touching nothing, listening, and once Yara caught him with his palm flat against a vial containing a fisherman's memory of his father teaching him knots, and the child's lips were moving, following the words like a boy learning a song.

"It wants to go back," the child said, catching Yara watching. "All of them want to go back. Stolen things are very patient. But they are not *content*."

"Is that how you know the Tongue?" Yara asked. "Were you — are you made of it?"

The child considered this with the seriousness he gave all questions, which unnerved Yara more than any answer could have.

"I am what was left over," he said finally. "When everything else was spent."

Yara didn't ask what that meant. He was learning, slowly, that the child answered true questions and deflected frightened ones, and Yara wasn't sure he wanted the answer yet. Instead he sat down across from him and held out his hands, palms up, the way the child had shown him.

"Teach me the fourth word."

The fourth word was *hareth*, and it meant *who*, but the way a key means a lock. The child placed his own small hands over Yara's and said it once, slowly, shaping Yara's mouth with two fingers the way a music teacher corrects a student's embouchure, and Yara repeated it —

— and the floor of the world opened.

* * *

He was nine years old and it was raining and there was a door.

Not a grand door. A tenement door, green paint peeling, in a street called Tanner's Row that smelled of lye and wet wool, and he was standing in front of it and his fists were too small to knock properly and he was crying, though he couldn't have said why, only that something had happened upstairs, something with boots and shouting and his mother's voice saying a word he now heard again after twenty years:

"*Hareth*," his mother had said. Not the word itself — but the shape of it, the grammar of it, woven into the last thing she ever said to him, which was: *"Run, and whatever asks you who you are, tell it nothing."*

He was nine years old and his name was not Yara Joubert.

He was nine years old and his mother's hands smelled of ink and tallow because she wrote charms, real ones, First-Tongue ones, and his father drew the ward-diagrams and laughed too loudly and kept a ledger hidden under a floorboard, and they were thieves, both of them, memory-thieves of the old school, the kind who took only from the rich and gave half back to the poor neighborhoods in charms against grief — and the crown had come for them on a Tuesday, four Wardens and a priest of the Unmaking, and Yara had been sent down the stairs with his mother's palm pressed flat against his chest and that word-shaped warning in his ears—

—and then the memory tore.

Not ended. *Tore*, like cloth caught on a nail, and beyond the tear there was nothing, a white absence shaped exactly like the next eleven years, and Yara came back to himself on Vess's floor with blood in his mouth where he'd bitten his tongue and the child kneeling over him, gripping his shoulders, saying "*velloth, velloth*" — *keep, keep* — trying to close what had been opened.

"What did you see?" the child demanded.

Yara couldn't speak. His chest was a bell that had been struck and was still ringing. Twenty years he had believed his emptiness was nature — that he had simply been born without a childhood, the way some men are born without an ear for music. A flaw. An absence. Something to build a trade on: a man with no past has nothing to lose by taking other people's.

It wasn't nature. It was *surgery*. Someone had cut him, cleanly, at the age of nine, on the stairs of a house on Tanner's Row, and left the wound dressed in white silence.

"Who did this to me?" he whispered.

The child's grey eyes held his, and for the first time since the knock at his door, the child looked away first.

"Ask her," he said. "The woman who owns this room."

* * *

He found Vess in the front of the shop, doing what she did every evening: burning the day's ledger pages in the stove, feeding the paper in strip by strip so the names curled and blackened individually. Forty years of clients reduced to ash, one line at a time. She didn't look up when he came in.

"Tanner's Row," Yara said.

The strips stopped.

"A green door. Rain. My mother's voice telling me to run." His voice was steady, which surprised him; somewhere inside, a man was screaming, and it seemed to be happening to someone slightly adjacent to him. "My parents were thieves, Vess. Real ones. Your school. Your *generation*." He crossed the room and took the remaining ledger page out of her hand — gently; he was surprised by his own gentleness — and read the top line. It was dated twenty years back. It said: *J., Tanner's Row. Full extraction, juvenile. Payment deferred.*

"Payment deferred," Yara read aloud. "For what? Who paid? Who *ordered* it?"

Vess sat down. He had never seen her sit so heavily, as if the chair were a confession booth and she'd been standing in it for twenty years.

"The crown paid," she said. "There was a purge, that winter. Memory-thieves who dealt in the First Tongue — not charming trinkets like yours, boy, real workings, the old craft. The Wardens burned a dozen households in a month. Your parents were the last." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, hard, as if trying to grind something out from behind them. "The order came down to erase the child rather than unmake him. Some magistrate's mercy. Or his accounting — a dead thief makes martyrs, but a child who remembers nothing makes *nothing*."

"And you took the contract."

"I took the contract." Flat. Unflinching. She had always been brave in exactly the ways that hurt most. "I climbed the stairs. Your mother was already gone — taken. Your father was holding the door. He asked me for one thing." Her voice did not waver, and somehow that was worse than weeping. "He said: *leave him enough to grieve us. Don't leave him nothing.* And I told him I would. And then I stood in front of a nine-year-old boy on those stairs, and I looked at what he was carrying — the fear, the running, the last sight of his mother's face — and I thought: this much pain will break him. This much pain will make him one of *them*, one of the hollow ones, or dead in a gutter by fifteen. So I took all of it. Everything. I left him clean."

"Clean," Yara repeated.

"Empty," she corrected herself, and closed her eyes. "I left you empty. And I told myself it was mercy for twenty years, and then I watched you grow up into the finest natural thief I ever trained, and I told myself it was talent, and it was never talent, Yara. It was the wound. You steal because you were stolen from. You deal in other people's memories because yours were taken, and every charm you sell is a little revenge you don't even feel." She opened her eyes and looked at him, and there it was, the thing she had carried alone for two decades, finally set down between them like a corpse on a table. "You asked me once why I trained you when I refuse everyone. Now you know. Guilt is a leash. I've been holding it so long I mistook it for love."

The stove ticked. Somewhere above, the curfew bells rang the second hour, and neither of them moved.

Yara discovered that he was not furious. He had expected fury — had perhaps been saving it, the way a man saves a coin for a specific purchase. But what rose in him instead was colder and stranger: recognition. Because he had done it too. He had stood in the night market a hundred times, looking at grieving buyers, and told himself that taking their best summers was mercy — that people were lighter without their happiness, easier to carry through hard lives. He knew that arithmetic. He had invented it independently, the way wounds teach all their children the same language.

"We're the same," he said quietly.

"No." Vess shook her head. "You still think what you took can go back. That's the difference. That's the only difference, and right now it's everything." She nodded toward the back room, toward the shelves of whispering glass, toward the child sleeping — if such a creature slept — among the murmuring inventory. "The Tongue returns things. You've felt it. Which means every theft either of us ever committed is reversible. Except—" and here her voice finally frayed, just at the edge, a thread pulling loose, "—except mine on you. Because I didn't keep what I took, boy. The contract required destruction. I burned your childhood in a Warden brazier the same night I took it, and ash doesn't come home."

The words landed and Yara waited for the grief to arrive, the great wave of it, the loss of a mother's face and a father's laughter and a name — *his name, he'd had another name, his mother had used it in the rain* —

But what arrived instead was something else. Something with structure. Because the memory on the stairs had torn rather than ended, and torn things leave threads, and threads can be followed. His mother's voice lived in him still, folded into the Fourth Word. The smell of ink and tallow. The green door. The shape of a warning: *whatever asks you who you are, tell it nothing.*

They hadn't destroyed everything. They couldn't have. Some part of him had hidden his parents inside the deepest chamber of his emptiness, the way the child had been hidden inside the binding, the way the founding king's humanity had been hidden inside the Door in the hill.

Hidden things wanted to come home. That was the whole grammar of the Tongue.

"Vess," he said. "The ledger. The full ledger. Every job you ever ran, every contract, every name — especially anything touching the crown, the palace, the founding era. I need to see it."

Something flickered across her face — fast, quickly buried, but Yara had spent three years reading faces for a living and he caught it. Fear. Not of him. Of the ledger.

"Why?"

"Because my parents died in a purge ordered by the crown, and the child on your floor speaks the language the crown forbids, and the king's eyes reflect nothing, and I am done being a man things happen *to*." He heard his own voice and barely recognized it; it sounded like his mother's, saying *run* and meaning *fight*. "Show me the ledger, Vess."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the bells finished striking, and in the silence after, from the back room, faintly, the charms began to whisper — dozens of voices, dozens of stolen lives, all murmuring the same word in the old tongue, over and over, soft as rain on a green door.

"*Hareth. Hareth. Hareth.*"

*Who. Who. Who.*

Vess rose, took the lamp, and led him toward the cellar stairs.

"There's something in the ledger you need to see," she said. "About Sefa. About why the Wardens took *her* and not you."
