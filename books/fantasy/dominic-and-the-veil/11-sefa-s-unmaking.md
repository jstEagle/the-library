## 11. Sefa's Unmaking

The Warden cells were not where the law kept people.

Yara had known that since he was old enough to know anything at all — since the years Vess carved out of him and left a smooth blank wall where his childhood should have been. The cells beneath the Hall of Judgment were for debtors and drunkards, men who'd stolen bread or struck their wives. People who would be seen again. The other place — the place where the unmade went — was called the Lantern House by those who spoke of it at all, and it stood on the far side of the Sere Canal, in a district of government granaries and record offices where no one lived and no one lingered. It was called that because of what they did in its courtyard every seventh evening: they burned memories in public, and the light of them was said to be beautiful.

Yara had never gone to watch. That had been a point of pride, once. He told himself it meant something, that he refused the spectacle while he fed the trade that filled it — that there was a line between selling a dying soldier's last summer as a charm and standing in a crowd to watch the Wardens peel a woman open like fruit. He had held that line carefully, the way you hold a rope over a drop, and he had told himself for three years that holding it was the same as being good.

He crossed the canal at dusk with the child's hand in his and understood, finally, that the line had never held anything but his own reflection.

* * *

They had taken Old Sefa nine days ago. Yara knew the shape of it from the tunnels — word moved through the memory-thieves' warren faster than it moved through the streets above, carried in whispers and chalk marks and the particular silence of a stall that would never open again. Sefa had been his fence for three years. She had bought his first charm when he was nobody, haggled him down to nothing, and paid him anyway, because she said a thief who starved in his first winter made a poor investment. She had gray dreadlocks bound with copper wire, hands like tree roots, and a laugh that came out of her sideways, as if she were embarrassed to be caught enjoying anything. She had hidden the child for two nights in her cellar while Wardens walked the street above, and she had pressed a bag of dried figs into the boy's hands and told him, in the flat trade-tongue he almost understood, *eat slow, little king, sweet things are for remembering.*

Nine days ago the Wardens had come for her with resonance-irons and a writ signed in the Lord Protector's name, and the tunnel network had closed around her absence like water closing over a stone.

Now Yara stood across the canal from the Lantern House and watched the crowd gather.

There were more of them than he expected. Families, even. A man with a child on his shoulders, the child waving a paper pinwheel. Vendors working the edges of the square with roasted chestnuts and cups of mulled wine, because spectacle was hunger like any other hunger, and Corvane had learned to cater to this one the way it catered to all the rest. The courtyard of the Lantern House was walled low enough that the burning could be seen from the street, and the Wardens had arranged benches — actual benches, tiered like a theater — facing a stone platform where an iron brazier stood tall as a gallows.

"They're going to burn her," the child said. He said it without inflection, the way he said most things in the trade-tongue, but his hand had gone tight around Yara's fingers, tight enough to hurt.

"Yes."

"Will it hurt?"

Yara looked down at him. The boy's face was turned up toward the Lantern House, toward the platform, and in the last of the daylight his eyes had that quality Yara had stopped trying to explain — a depth that didn't match his size, as if something very old were looking out through a very small window.

"I don't know," Yara said. It was the truth, and the truth was worse than any lie he could have offered, because he *did* know how unmaking worked. Every thief did. You didn't burn the body; the body was incidental, a coat rack. You burned the memories themselves — drew them up through the resonance-irons and set them alight in the air where everyone could see them rendered as light and color and half-heard sound, the way a memory looks to the person stealing it. The victim sat through all of it. Awake. And when it was done, what remained sitting in the chair was a person-shaped thing with clean eyes, breathing, blinking, waiting to be told who it was.

He had sold charms to people who attended burnings. He remembered that now, with a clarity that felt like a blade going in between ribs: a magistrate's clerk, a grain merchant's wife, pleasant customers, good coin. They'd wanted bright ones, happy ones, something to take the taste of the evening out of their mouths afterward. He had wrapped their purchases in blue paper and thanked them for their patronage.

"We can't go in there," he said. "We shouldn't even be on this side of the canal."

"You're going in there." The child said it the way he said everything in the First Tongue when he slipped into it — not predicting, *stating*. As if the sentence already existed somewhere behind the world and he was only reading it aloud.

Yara crouched so they were eye to eye. Around them the crowd thickened, jostling, cheerful, the chestnut smoke sweet and obscene. "Listen to me. If I'm caught, no one comes for you. Do you understand? There's a drain behind the tannery on Ferrier Street — Sefa showed me, before — you go down it, you follow the water until you see the chalk mark, three lines and a circle, and you wait. Someone will find you. Vess will find you."

The child considered him with that terrible patience. Then he reached up and touched Yara's cheek, one small finger, and the touch went through him like cold water through cloth.

What Yara saw: a room with no windows. A woman strapped upright in a chair, her head caged in a lattice of irons that hummed. And around her, in the air, her life hanging in ribbons — a cellar smelling of brine and candle wax, copper wire wound around gray hair, a bag of figs given away to a child who needed sweetness more than she did — each ribbon catching fire at its tip and burning slowly downward toward her, and her watching every one of them go.

He tore back from the vision gasping. The child withdrew his hand and looked at him with something that was almost apology.

"That hasn't happened yet," Yara said.

"No," the boy agreed. "But it will. Unless."

"Unless what?"

The child pointed at the Lantern House, and said one word in the First Tongue, and though Yara had been learning the language for less than a week — learning it the way you learn a wound, in pieces, in the night — he understood this word completely. It meant *door*. It also meant *the moment before a door opens*. It also, he realized with a chill that started in his scalp and worked its way down, meant *him*.

* * *

Getting inside was insultingly easy, and that ease was its own kind of horror. The Wardens were not guarding against intrusion; why would they? No one broke *into* an unmaking. Yara put on the manner he wore for the court houses and the merchant villas — the soft-shoed charm-seller, the harmless tradesman — and told the gate sergeant he'd been sent with a delivery for the presiding officer, a calming charm, standard for the officers on burning duty, the resonance work gave them headaches. The sergeant looked at the blue-paper packet, looked at Yara's open honest face, and waved him through with the bored courtesy of a man surrounded by state power and therefore safe from everything, including thought.

The corridors smelled of ink and hot iron. Yara walked past clerks' alcoves and armory racks and a bulletin board posted with resonance notices — *REPORT FIRST TONGUE ARTIFACTS*, the header read, and below it a charcoal rubbing of a sigil that made the child's voice ring in his memory like a struck bell. He catalogued exits the way he catalogued everything now, automatically, a habit the last two weeks had ground into him: the canal gate behind him, a yard door to the north, a grate in the floor of the washroom that breathed cellar air.

And then a Warden came around the corner escorting a prisoner, and Yara pressed himself into an alcove beside a shelf of seizure ledgers, and through the gap between two ledger spines he saw her.

Old Sefa walked between her escorts the way she had walked through the night market for forty years — unhurried, chin up, as if the corridor belonged to her and everyone else were loitering. They had taken her copper wire and her coat and her boots, and her feet were bare on the stone, and that detail hit Yara harder than the irons had, harder than anything: they had taken her *boots*. She had walked these floors barefoot for nine days. Her wrists were cuffed in front of her, and around her temples sat a circlet of dull metal — a limiter, cheap municipal make, the kind used for transport — and she was humming.

Yara knew the tune. It was a market song, a haggling song, the one vendors sang at closing time when they were counting coins and mocking the day's fools. She was humming it quietly, steadily, walking to her own unmaking, and the sound of it went through the alcove and through Yara like a spear.

She passed within six feet of him. Her eyes swept the corridor with the professional indifference of a woman who had spent four decades noticing everything and revealing nothing — and they snagged on the alcove. On him. For half a heartbeat, no longer, her gaze fixed on the gap between the ledger spines, on the shadow that was Yara, and her humming did not falter, and her face did not change, and one corner of her mouth moved in the smallest possible motion that was not a smile but contained one the way a seed contains a tree.

Then the Wardens walked her on, and she was gone around the corner, still humming.

* * *

The courtyard filled as the sun went down. Yara found a place among the service staff along the eastern wall — a tray of wine cups in his hands proved to be a universal key — and watched the benches fill with citizens, the torches lit, the presiding Warden ascend the platform in her gray lacquered armor. The child was not with him. He had left the boy in the mouth of the storm drain on Ferrier Street with instructions repeated three times and a fig from Sefa's own bag pressed into his hand, and the parting had been its own small agony: the child gripping his sleeve, saying in the First Tongue a sentence Yara understood only halfway but which he suspected meant *you will do something foolish and I will have to help you.*

The presiding Warden spoke. Yara barely heard the words. They were the standard liturgy — crimes against the Statutes of Clean Air, trafficking in unsanctioned resonance, corruption of the common memory — delivered in the warm administrative tone of a woman reading a menu. Beside the brazier, two Wardens fitted the resonance-irons around Sefa's head, replacing the travel circlet with the full lattice, and the crowd murmured its anticipation, and somewhere in the tiers a vendor called out his last chestnuts of the evening.

Sefa sat in the chair with her bare feet flat on the platform stones and her spine straight, and she looked out at the crowd the way she had once looked out at a young thief trying to cheat her on his first sale: with vast, amused, unimpatience patience.

The irons began to sing.

Yara had felt resonance work from the outside — every thief had, the pressure in the teeth, the wrongness in the air like a note held too long. But this was different. This was engineered, amplified, projected outward for an audience, and when the first ribbon of Sefa's memory rose out of her it rose *beautifully*, and that was the unforgivable thing about it. It rose glowing amber-gold into the dark air above the platform, wide as a banner, and the whole courtyard could see it: a market at dawn, awnings going up, a young woman with dark hair laughing as she haggled, the whole scene rendered in light with sound bleeding off it like heat haze — the clink of coins, the cry of gulls, her own young voice calling prices across the stalls.

The crowd sighed. Actually sighed, a collective exhalation of pleasure, and the ribbon caught fire at its highest point and burned downward, and as it burned the dawn market dimmed, the laughing girl flickered, and Sefa in the chair watched it go with her eyes open and dry.

Ribbon after ribbon. A wedding — hers, presumably, a broad-faced man dancing badly. A cellar full of candles and a baby. Forty years of market mornings, stacked one atop another in the air like pages turning, each one catching, each one burning down toward her, each one greeted by that soft communal sigh, and Yara stood frozen with his tray of wine cups while fury built in him with a slowness that frightened him more than rage would have. Rage burns out. This was settling in like frost, like mortar, filling every joint of him. He watched a magistrate's wife dab her eyes at the beauty of it. He watched the presiding Warden check a pocket watch. He watched the burning girl in the air laugh for the last time, and go out.

And Sefa — Sefa began to speak.

Her voice was thin but it carried; the courtyard's acoustics were built for it, built so the condemned could be heard making whatever sounds they made. "That one was my mother's kitchen," she said, as another ribbon kindled. "Bread on Thursdays. She sang off-key." The crowd shifted, uncertain whether this was part of the performance. "This next one is the smell of rain on the canal in summer. You've all smelled it. I'm the one who gets to lose it." A ribbon of silver-gray rose and burned. "I bought a boy's first charm once and overpaid him on purpose. Best coin I ever spent. He's here tonight, I think. In the back. With the wine."

Yara's breath stopped.

"Don't look for him," Sefa said pleasantly, to the crowd, to the Wardens, to the dark beyond the torchlight where Yara stood with his knuckles white around a wooden tray. "He's where he always was. Hiding. And that's all right. I hid too, forty years, before I got old enough to stop being afraid of the wrong things." Another ribbon rose — the cellar, the figs, the child's small hands — and burned, and she watched it go. "You'll forgive yourself eventually, boy. Or you won't, and you'll use it. Either way you'll get up tomorrow and do the thing you should have done today. That's how it works. That's how it always worked."

The presiding Warden raised a hand; the irons' pitch climbed; the audience was growing restless with this commentary, this unauthorized narration of their entertainment. But Sefa had one ribbon left, and everyone could see it — smaller than the others, pale, worn thin with handling, the way a memory gets when it's been taken out and looked at ten thousand times.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, I was saving that one."

It rose into the air. And Yara saw it, the whole courtyard saw it: a night market stall, twenty years ago, a much younger Sefa leaning across her counter toward a girl of perhaps nineteen — sharp-eyed, quick-handed, a girl Yara recognized with a lurch that turned his stomach over, because he had seen that face in mirrors of a sort, in the set of his own jaw, in Vess Corhane's features aged thirty years. The ribbon glowed. The girl laughed at something Sefa said. And the memory burned downward, and as it burned, Sefa turned her head — with effort, against the irons, the lattice grinding — and found Yara across the courtyard, across the torchlight and the crowd and every year of hiding between them.

"There you are," she said.

Not loudly. Just to him. And she smiled — the sideways, embarrassed, real smile — and the last ribbon went out, and the light left her.

What remained in the chair blinked at the crowd with clean eyes. It looked at its own bare feet with mild curiosity. It did not know the smell of rain, or bread on Thursdays, or the weight of a bag of figs given away in love. The presiding Warden pronounced the sentence complete to scattered, polite applause, and the crowd rose and filed out chattering about the colors, the clarity, the *quality* of the rendering, and Yara stood among them carrying his untouched tray, wearing his harmless face, and let the frost finish setting in every joint of his body.

* * *

He did not remember walking back to Ferrier Street. He remembered fragments: the canal, black and sliding; the chestnut vendor packing up his cart; his own hands steady, horribly steady, doing the small automatic work of survival. He remembered kneeling at the mouth of the storm drain and the child emerging from the dark with the fig untouched in his fist, having eaten nothing, having waited exactly as instructed, and looking up at Yara's face and going very still.

"What happened?" the boy asked.

"She's dead." His voice came out level. That was the worst of it — how level it came out, how practiced, how much it sounded like the voice of a man who had made peace with things. "They burned everything she was, and people applauded, and I stood there and did nothing, because doing something would have cost me, and I have spent my whole life refusing to pay."

The child took his hand. Not comforting — deliberate, the grip of someone much older than six or seven, and when he spoke again it was in the First Tongue, and the words went into Yara like keys into locks:

*"Grief is a door. Anger is the hand that opens it. What you do on the other side is the only question."*

Yara looked down at him. Above them Corvane glittered, ten thousand windows, a city built on a hunger it didn't know it was feeding, ruled by a hollow thing wearing a beloved king, patrolled by Wardens who burned grandmothers to music and applause. He had hidden for three years. He had held his little line in the dark and called it virtue. Sefa had died telling him he'd get up tomorrow and do the thing he should have done today.

"Tomorrow," Yara said aloud, tasting the word like iron. "No. Tonight."

Because there was one thing Sefa's burning had shown him, one thread of gold in all that ash: the memory of the girl at the stall, twenty years ago. Vess. Young Vess, laughing, before the erasures, before the ledger, before whatever bargain had hollowed them both. Sefa had spent her final saved memory — the one she'd hoarded through forty years of fear — not on comfort, not on escape, but on showing *him* that Vess Corhane had once been someone worth loving. That the woman who stole his childhood had been a girl who laughed at market stalls. That she could be reached.

The Wardens had taken Sefa. They would not take the rest.

"Come on," Yara said to the child, and turned toward the tunnels, toward Vess, toward the Door in the hill and everything waiting behind it. "We're done hiding."

Behind them, across the canal, the Lantern House blazed with light — and in the high window of its tower, Yara saw, just for a moment, a figure in gray lacquered armor watching him go, and raising one hand, slowly, in something that was not a wave at all.
