## 12. The King Who Isn't

The palace had a smell Yara hadn't expected. He had imagined lilies and candlewax and the sour ghost of a thousand banquets; what he found instead was cold stone and old salt, as if the whole edifice had been raised on the floor of a sea that had long since withdrawn. It clung to the back of the throat. It made his stolen memories ache in their jars the way teeth ache before a storm.

He moved through the servants' corridors with a tray of charms balanced on one palm — his invitation into the court of hollow eyes, purchased with three weeks of careful flattery and one minor theft from a viscount's seneschal who would never miss what he couldn't remember wanting. Above him, the palace sang its daytime music: laughter in the galleries, boots on marble, a string quartet rehearsing somewhere for an occasion no one would remember attending. Below him, past the third landing where the torches gave out and the walls sweated condensation, there was only the sound of his own breathing and, beneath it — so faint he might have invented it — something like a heartbeat that was not his own.

The child had drawn the way for him. That was the terrible thing, the thing he kept turning over as he descended: *the child had drawn it from memory.* A map scratched into wax with a fingernail, corridors and stairways the boy had never walked in this body, in this life. "It dreams me down here sometimes," the child had said, in that voice too old for his mouth. "It likes to show me where I live."

Yara had not slept since.

* * *

The stairs beneath the throne room did not appear on any plan of the palace. Vess had told him that much before they parted — her last gift before the Wardens took the market district and everything in it went up like paper. *"Under every seat of power there's a room the power doesn't want to look at,"* she'd said, tightening the strap of her ledger-bag with hands that didn't quite shake. *"Kings build upward because they can't bear what's underneath. Find the staircase that isn't in the drawings, boy. It'll be the truest architecture in the building."*

She'd been right, as she had been right about everything except how to keep herself free. The staircase was behind a wall in the undercroft, a door fitted so seamlessly into the masonry that Yara found it not by sight but by touch — the stones there were warm. Not warm the way living bodies are warm. Warm the way a hearthstone stays warm after the fire has been raked out, holding the shape of heat that is no longer in the room.

The First Tongue rose off the door in waves he felt rather than heard. His charms rattled against each other on the tray, whispering. One of them — the soldier's summer, the first memory he'd ever taken, the dying man's last afternoon of cut grass and river water — began to murmur in the child's voice: *kneel, kneel, the door remembers kneeling.*

"I know," Yara said to it, and hated that he answered.

The door had no handle. It opened when he spoke the syllable the child had taught him — a sound like a key turning inside his own skull, like something unlocking that he had not known was locked — and the dark beyond it was not empty.

* * *

The room beneath the throne was round, and low, and wrong.

Wrong in a way Yara's trade had trained him to feel. Every memory he had ever handled had a texture, a grain, a direction of travel, the way wood does. This room's texture ran the wrong way. The air itself seemed braided against the natural lie of the world, and at the room's center, where everything converged, sat a chair.

Not a throne. A chair. Plain oak, the kind a farmer might keep by his stove, and in it sat a man who had once been King Aldric the Second of Corvane.

Yara knew the face. Everyone in Corvane knew the face — it hung in every magistrate's hall and stamped every coin, forty years old forever, grey-eyed, kind-lined, patient. But the coin-face had never looked like this. The man in the chair had been worn. Not aged — worn, the way a stair is worn, the way a river stone is worn, eroded slowly over years by something passing over him again and again. His eyes were open. They tracked Yara the moment he stepped through the doorway, and there was nothing in them. Not madness, not hatred, not even the flat nothing of the Veil's borrowed king upstairs. This was a different nothing. This was a nothing that had once held everything and had it poured out of it, drop by drop, until only the watching remained.

"Ah," the king said. His voice was soft, unhurried, terribly reasonable. "Another one."

Yara stopped. The tray of charms in his hands had gone silent — utterly silent, every memory in every glass vial holding its breath. "Another one?"

"Visitor." The king's head tilted. There were marks on his wrists, old ones, silvered scars where bonds had rubbed for years. The chair, Yara saw now, was not a place of rest. It was a place of keeping. Chains of a metal he didn't recognize — dark, faintly veined with light that pulsed in time with that heartbeat-not-his-own — ran from the armrests into the floor and out beyond the walls, into the hill itself. "You're the fourth this year. Or the fifth. I lose the count, some years. The years are the first thing to go." He smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing Yara had ever seen. "You've brought memories. I can smell them. Grass. River water. Summer." His eyes closed briefly, as if pained by sweetness. "Whose?"

"A soldier's," Yara said. His voice came out smaller than he intended. "A dying man's."

"And you took it." Not accusatory. Simply cataloguing. "For money? For survival? It doesn't matter. It's all survival, down here. That's what they told me at the beginning. Someone has to survive it. Someone has to be the anchor, or the whole binding slackens, and then—" He made a small gesture with two fingers, like a bird falling. "Everything. Everyone. All the little lives, spilled at once. You cannot imagine, thief. I have tried to imagine it for ten years and it is the imagining that has kept me sane, if this is sanity. The imagining is all I have left."

Yara set the tray down on the floor with great care. His hands were not steady. "Your Majesty—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice. For just a moment, under the erosion, under the patience, Yara glimpsed something vast and ruined move behind the king's eyes — grief with nowhere left to put itself. "I am not your majesty. Your majesty eats. Your majesty sits above me wearing my face the way a hand wears a glove, and laughs at the right moments, and kisses babies, and signs death warrants with my handwriting, and every day it comes down here — do you understand? Every single day it comes down and puts its hand on my head and *drinks*, and I let it, because the alternative is Corvane drowning in its own forgotten names." The king breathed. Steadied himself. When he spoke again he was soft again, reasonable again, and somehow that was worse. "How is the city? Is the harvest fair? I used to care about the harvest."

"The harvest is fair," Yara said quietly.

"Good. Good." The king nodded slowly, twice, the nod of a man setting something down very carefully so it won't break. Then he looked up, and his grey eyes fixed on Yara with an intensity that made the thief's skin crawl, because it was the intensity of a starving man looking at bread. "You said you take memories. You carry them in glass. Can you put them *back*?"

"Some. If the taking was clean. If not too much time has passed."

"Then listen to me." The king leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, and the veins of light in the dark metal brightened, and the heartbeat-not-his-own quickened through the floor and up through Yara's boot-soles into his chest. "There is nothing to put back. Do you see? Ten years. It hasn't stored them somewhere for safekeeping, the way you store your little charms. It has *eaten*. There is no vault under the throne where Aldric the Second waits to be restored. There is only this." He spread his hands, palms up, empty. "I am the space where a king was. And I am telling you — I have thought about this for ten years with nothing else to think about — I am telling you that the kindest thing anyone could do in this room is finish what the Veil started."

* * *

Yara had prepared for many things in the descent. Wards. Guardians. Some final monstrous revelation. He had not prepared to stand in a cellar under a throne, arguing philosophy with a hollowed king about whether he deserved to die.

"That's not my choice to make," he said.

"No." The king settled back. Almost gently. "It's mine. And I have made it. I make it fresh every morning, which is the only ritual they've left me." He studied Yara across the dim room. "But you didn't come down here for mercy's sake. Someone sent you. Someone with a map in their head that they shouldn't have." Something flickered across the worn face — recognition, and then a grief so sudden and specific that Yara knew the answer before the question finished forming. "There's a child. Isn't there. Six years old, perhaps seven. Speaks the old language."

Yara said nothing. His silence was answer enough.

"He still speaks it." The king laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughter, and it broke halfway through. "They told me he was spent. The founding bargain requires a key — a living fragment of the original binding, flesh made from the part of the first king that he cut away and could not bring himself to spend. As long as the key sleeps, the Veil wears whoever it likes and the Door stays shut. As long as the key sleeps, I sit in this chair. They promised me he was spent. Twenty years ago they told me the last of him had guttered out in some alley, and I believed them, because believing them meant I could stop looking." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Is he well? Is he — God help me, he'd be twenty-six now, wouldn't he. Unless they made him new. They do that. Make things new."

Yara thought of the child asleep against Old Sefa's stove, before Sefa. Before the market burned. He thought of small hands scratching maps in wax. "He's six," Yara said. "Or seven. He doesn't know which. Neither do I."

The king went very still.

Then, slowly, he began to weep — not loudly, not dramatically, but with the terrible economy of a man who has wept in this chair ten thousand times and knows exactly how much moisture he can spare. "New," he whispered. "They made him new. They took what was left of him and they made him *new*, and it walks around in the world, and I'm down here." His hands lowered. His eyes, when they found Yara again, had changed. The emptiness was still there — you cannot refill a cup in a moment — but something moved through it now, urgent and bright and desperate. "Then it can end. Don't you see? If the key lives, if the key is *awake* — the binding can be unmade properly. Not slackened. Unmade. The Veil starved of its anchor and sealed behind a door with no lock, and this chair can burn, and I can—"

He stopped. Yara watched the hope catch and gutter in real time, watched the arithmetic complete itself behind the grey eyes, and understood one heartbeat before the king said it.

"To unmake the binding," the king said carefully, "someone must hold the anchor while it comes loose. And the anchor is—"

"You," Yara said.

"Me." The king smiled. It was the gentlest expression the ruined face could produce, and it devastated Yara more than any rage could have. "Ten years I have begged the dark for oblivion, thief, and it would not grant me even that, because my suffering is *useful*. My suffering is structural. And now heaven sends a pickpocket with a tray full of other people's summers, and asks me the question I have wanted asked since the day they chained me here." He straightened in the chair, and for a moment — one moment — Yara saw the outline of the man whose face was on the coins, the man who had ridden through flooded districts handing out bread with his own hands, and it was almost too much to look at. "Free the realm. Pull the anchor. Whatever it costs me — and it will cost me everything, I understand that better than you ever can — know that you are not killing a king. The Veil killed the king ten years ago. You will only be burying him."

* * *

Yara did not remember climbing back up the first flights of stairs. He came back to himself at the warm door, palms flat against the hearthstone-masonry, breath coming short, the tray of charms chattering against each other like teeth.

The soldier's summer was whispering again. Not the child's voice this time. Its own — the dying man's own, dredged up out of the glass, saying the thing he had actually said on that last afternoon by the river, the thing Yara had carried for three years without letting himself hear it: *Take care of it, friend. Take care of what's left.*

"I know," Yara said. His voice shook. "I know what's left."

Above him, faint through thirty feet of stone, the throne room orchestra swelled toward the end of its rehearsal. Somewhere up there the Veil wore a dead man's face and laughed at the right moments, and signed papers, and drank. Somewhere below him a king sat in a farmer's chair asking for the only gift Yara had sworn never to give: an ending, bought with a life.

And between them — the thought arrived unbidden, cold as the salt-smell of the deep stone — between them stood a six-year-old child made of a kingdom's oldest wound, who would have to speak the words that pulled the anchor loose. Who would have to watch. Who had already learned, from Yara himself, what it looks like when someone takes everything another person is.

Yara lifted the tray. Turned toward the dark of the upper stairs.

Behind him, far below, through floors and floors of stone, he felt it — not heard, *felt* — the moment the heartbeat under the earth changed rhythm. Quickened. Turned, the way a great head turns.

Something down in the hill had just noticed the key was awake.

And something wearing a king's face, two floors above, had begun — mid-laughter, mid-handshake, mid-sip of wine — to smile at a spot in the empty air, precisely, exactly, where Yara Joubert was standing.
