## 13. Midpoint: The Veil Speaks

The king beneath the throne had stopped begging. That was the worst of it.

For an hour — or what Yara guessed was an hour, though time moved strangely in the dark beneath the palace, where the walls sweated and the air tasted of iron and old honey — the ruined man had pleaded for oblivion. *Let it end. Let me end. I was a boy in Vellin Marsh once, I think. I had a dog. I cannot remember the dog's name and it is killing me by inches.* Then the words had thinned, and the man had folded inward like paper left in rain, and now he sat against the wall of his cell with his eyes open and his mouth closed, breathing, waiting, a lamp with no oil left to burn.

Yara crouched in the doorway, the child pressed against his side, and did not know what mercy looked like here.

"You said the Veil wears him," Yara whispered. "Like a glove."

The child nodded. In the gloom his eyes caught light that had no source. "The glove remembers the hand," he said, in that voice too old for a six-year-old's mouth. "And the hand is coming to check on its fingers."

Yara felt it before he heard it — a pressure in the teeth, a hum in the charms sewn into his coat lining. Every stolen memory he carried woke at once, a hundred small lives rustling against his ribs like birds in a sack. The soldier's last summer. The baker's grief for her drowned son. The midwife's knowledge of her own hands. They all turned, all at once, toward the stair.

Footsteps on the stone. Unhurried. The cadence of someone who had never in his life needed to hurry.

"Up," Yara breathed. "There's a service passage behind the—"

"No," the child said, and did not move.

The torchlight came first, bending wrong around the corner of the stair, shadows leaning toward the light instead of away from it. Then the king.

He walked as the king walked in public — Yara had seen him from a distance, at the Unmaking of the Alchemists' Guild, a figure in white and gold waving with perfect warmth — but here in the dark the warmth was a costume with no one inside it. His face was the face from the coins and the murals: broad, handsome, kindly. His eyes reflected nothing. Not darkness — Yara had seen darkness in eyes before, in dying men, in the hollowed — but *nothing*, a flat absence like a window painted over, and when the king smiled at them it was the smile of a man who had studied smiling in a book.

"Yara Joubert," the king said. His voice was the king's voice, warm as a hearth, and underneath it, very faint, was a sound like wind through a door left open at the bottom of the world. "The memory-thief. The little courier. I have been tasting your work for years, you know. Every charm you sold in the night market. Every stolen summer. You have no idea how much of this city has passed through your hands, and how much of it passed from your hands into mine."

Yara put himself between the king and the child. It was a stupid gesture and he knew it — the man before him had unmade a guild, had eaten a servant's memory of her mother while she smiled and thanked him — but his body did it anyway, the way a body flinches from a blow.

"You keep him alive," Yara said. "The real king. Why? If you're wearing him, why keep the man?"

"An anchor." The Veil tilted the king's head, and the gesture was almost childlike, almost curious. "A binding needs a shape. He is the shape I wear. Unmake him and the binding frays, and fraying is *loud*, and I have spent a century learning to be quiet." The king's smile deepened by a degree. "You understand quiet, Yara Joubert. You have built a whole life out of it. Small thefts. Small charms. Small rules. *Never take from the desperate. Never sell a memory whole. Never stay in one quarter too long.* I have watched you make your rules the way a man builds a fence around the place where he buried something."

"Stay away from him." Yara's voice cracked on the second word. "The child. Whatever you are, whatever you want—"

"What I *want*." The Veil laughed, and the laugh was the king's laugh, the one from the coronation ballads, and it was the most obscene thing Yara had ever heard. "You think I came down here to take something. Thief. I came to *give*."

* * *

The king crossed the cell in three steps and stopped an arm's length away, and Yara could not make himself retreat. Not because of courage. Because the air between them had gone thick and sweet, like the moment before a memory takes hold, and his body had forgotten how to obey him.

"I know what was taken from you," the Veil said softly. "I was here when it happened. I am *always* here. Your parents, executed in the Salt Court for the crime of remembering too well. Your childhood, cut out by the woman who trained you and sold to a collector in Ashport — I tasted it, Yara. A boy on a roof in the Tallow Quarter, learning the names of stars from a mother who smelled of lamp oil and thyme. A name. You had a name before you were Yara Joubert, the nameless thief. Would you like it back?"

"Stop." The word came out as a whisper.

"I could give it back *now*." The king raised one hand, and the torches guttered, and the dark leaned in close and attentive. "That is the thing your Vess never told you, because she does not know it. I do not only eat. What I take, I keep. *Everything.* Every memory ever fed to me, every memory stolen and sold and stolen again — it is all in me, whole, perfect, unspoiled by forgetting. I am the only archive this kingdom has ever had. And I am offering you a key to it."

The pressure in Yara's teeth became a ringing. The charms against his ribs had gone still — all those stolen lives, listening.

"Your guilt, too," the Veil murmured, and now the king's voice dropped to the register a confessor uses, a lover, a mother. "The soldier whose summer you took. He died not knowing his own last joy. The baker. The midwife. The hundred small lives you spent to buy your quiet life. I can take that weight out of you the way a surgeon takes out a splinter. You would remember what you did and feel nothing — no, better than nothing. You would feel *justified*. You would understand, finally, that survival is not a sin, that the world made you a thief and then condemned you for thieving, and that every rule you ever broke was the world's crime before it was yours."

"That's not understanding," Yara said. His mouth was dry. "That's just — cutting it out."

"Is it?" The Veil smiled again, and this time the smile had something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "You have spent three years doing exactly that to yourself, little thief. You cut out your childhood. You cut out your name. You call the scar tissue *discipline*. I am offering to finish the surgery. To make you whole the way a riverbed is whole when the river is dammed. Smooth. Dry. Nothing left to drown in."

* * *

And here is the thing Yara would never be able to explain afterward, not to the child, not to himself, not to the empty road at the end of everything: for one suspended moment — a moment he would carry the way a man carries a splinter under the nail — he wanted it.

Not the power. He told himself it was not the power. He wanted the *roof*. The Tallow Quarter. The smell of lamp oil and thyme. A woman's voice saying his name — his real name, the one that had been scooped out of him at nine years old and sold like a charm in a market — saying it the way mothers say names, as if the name were a door and the child behind it was worth opening for. He wanted the dog the king had forgotten. He wanted the stars with their names attached. He wanted to stop being a man assembled from other people's leftovers and be, for one hour, one minute, the boy he had been before Vess Corhane's hands had gone into his mind and tidied him into emptiness.

And underneath that want, deeper, in the silt at the bottom: he wanted to put down the guilt. The soldier's face. Sefa's cell. The patrol of Wardens standing in the rain, smiling blanks, their whole lives folded up in his coat. The child's eyes going wide and frightened — *of him* — the night he'd emptied those men. He wanted to be able to look at the child without flinching. He wanted the arithmetic of his life to come out even, and it never would, and here was a voice offering to rewrite the ledger.

The Veil watched him want it. Yara could feel it watching, the way you feel a hand hovering over your skin without touching — it *fed* on the wanting, drew it out longer than it should have lasted, stretched the moment like taffy. He understood, distantly, that this was how it had taken the kingdom. Not with armies. With this. With one suspended moment, offered to the right person at the right hour of their exhaustion, and held open just a breath too long.

The king's hand was still raised. The dark was still leaning in.

"Say yes," the Veil said, and the wind-through-the-door sound underneath the king's voice was almost tender now, "and you will never have to survive anything again. You will simply *have*. Your name. Your mother. Your guilt, gone. And when the Wardens come — and they are coming, thief, even now, Vess Corhane gave you up — you will walk through them like weather. I will make you the keeper of every memory in this realm. The man who decides what Corvane remembers. Kings have knelt to me for less. Kings have knelt to me for *exactly this*."

The real king, in the corner, made a sound. Small. The sound a man makes when he has been listening to his own voice used to say things he would die before saying.

And that sound — that tiny, ruined human sound — was what broke the moment. Because Yara had heard that sound before. He had heard it in the night market, in the alley behind Sefa's stall, from the desperate ones he refused to buy from, the ones whose memories he would not take because they had nothing left *but* their memories. He had built his whole trade, his whole miserable rule-bound life, on hearing that sound and walking away.

The Veil was offering to make him the kind of man who didn't walk away.

"No," Yara said.

The word came out small. He said it again, louder, and the ringing in his teeth shattered like ice.

"No. You don't give things back. That's not — whatever you are, that's not how you work. You *wear*. You wear a king. You'd wear me. And the man under the glove—" he jerked his chin at the ruined figure in the corner, "—he asked for oblivion, and you've kept him alive for ten years to sit in your shape. That's your *mercy*. I've seen what your mercy costs."

For the first time, the smile left the king's face.

It left it all at once, like a mask slipping, and what was underneath was not rage. It was *hunger* — vast, patient, and suddenly, terribly interested. The torches went out. The dark did not fall; it *arrived*, pressing in from all sides, and in it Yara heard the door at the bottom of the world swing wider.

"Then you will cost me something," the Veil said, and the king's voice was fraying at the edges now, the warmth burning off it like fog in sun. "That is rare. That is *precious*. Do you know what I do with things that cost me, Yara Joubert?"

The child's hand found Yara's. Small. Cold. Steady.

"You take everything they love," the Veil said, "and you make them watch."

The dark rushed in — and the child stepped forward, into it, and spoke a single word in the First Tongue, and the word was a wall, and the wall held.

But through the wall, as the Veil withdrew, unhurried even in retreat, Yara heard it laughing — the king's warm laugh, fading up the stair — and beneath the laugh, distinct and deliberate, the sound of a name.

His name. The true one. The one he had refused.

The Veil knew it. And it would use it.
