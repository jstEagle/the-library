## 15. The First Tongue, Spoken Whole

They walked three days into the hill country before Yara admitted to himself that he had no plan beyond *away*.

The road out of Corvane thinned to a track, then to a suggestion, then to nothing but sheep-cropped grass and stone fences so old they'd sunk into the earth like ribs. The child — Tam, Yara had started calling him, because a nameless boy made people look twice and looking twice was death these days — kept pace without complaint, small boots scuffing through the frost-burnt bracken. Behind them, far behind, Corvane smoked faintly against the sky where the night market had been. Where Sefa's stall had been. Where six hundred lives of borrowed memory had burned in an afternoon.

Yara didn't let himself look back more than once a day.

On the third evening, the track crested a ridge and the shrine appeared below them in the last light, and Yara stopped walking as though he'd struck his head on something invisible.

He'd heard of it the way everyone had heard of it — a story grandmothers told to make children quiet. *The Old King's chapel, up past the high pasture, where no birds sing.* He'd assumed it was a story, like most things grandmothers said. But there it sat in a bowl of green hills: a low building of grey stone, roofless, its walls grown over with ivy gone red with autumn, and around it a circle of standing stones worn smooth as knuckles. No birds sang. That part of the story was true too. The silence had a texture to it, a held-breath quality, like the moment after a charm breaks.

"Here," Tam said.

Yara looked down at him. "You know this place?"

The child didn't answer in words. He walked down the slope with the certainty of someone going home, and Yara followed, because following the child had become the closest thing to a plan he had left.

* * *

Inside the shrine walls, the air was warmer than it had any right to be. The floor was flagstone, swept clean by wind and time, and at the far end stood what remained of an altar: a single slab of black stone on two carved supports. The carvings were deep and deliberate — spirals, and beneath them rows of characters Yara knew only from the worst corners of his trade. Characters that made stolen memories ache. Characters that had cracked his charms the first night a knock came at his door in a language older than punishment.

First Tongue.

He'd seen fragments of it before. A syllable scratched inside a locket. Half a phrase sewn into a dying woman's hem. Always partial, always wrong-feeling, like a song remembered by someone who'd been deafened halfway through. But the altar was covered in it, whole lines of it, and as Tam approached the stone, the characters began to *glow* — faintly, the way embers breathe when stirred — and Yara's skin crawled from wrist to collarbone.

"Tam." His voice came out rougher than intended. "Don't touch—"

"I have to." The child turned, and for a moment his face was not a child's face at all. It was patient, and old, and unbearably sad, the way a portrait is sad — sadness rendered rather than felt. Then it passed, and he was six or seven again, mud on his chin, one bootlace dragging. "It's cold in me. It's always been cold. This is where the warm part went."

"The warm part of what?"

Tam looked at him with an expression Yara had seen on the faces of men whose memories he'd lifted — the look of someone standing at a door they cannot open alone. "Of him," the child said. "Come and see. You've carried pieces of this long enough. It's time somebody gave some back."

* * *

What happened next, Yara would spend the rest of his life trying to describe, and never quite manage.

Tam laid both hands flat on the altar stone and spoke.

Not stammered. Not recited. *Spoke* — fluently, easily, the way a child speaks the tongue it learned before any other, and Yara understood in a single vertiginous instant that every halting word, every fragmentary phrase the boy had produced in the weeks since knocking at his door had been a man waking from a long fever trying to remember how to talk. The language poured out of him now, round and resonant, and each syllable did something to the world that Yara had no honest name for.

The first word made the standing stones hum.

The second made the light change — not brighten exactly, but deepen, as if evening were being asked politely to wait.

And the third—

The third opened the memories.

They rose off the altar like heat-shimmer, like smoke given weight, and Yara, who had spent three years handling other people's pasts as though they were merchandise, stood in the middle of a founding king's life and had nowhere left to hide.

* * *

He saw a young man on this same hillside, before the stones were raised, kneeling in the grass with his hands buried in the dirt, laughing at nothing, laughing at the sheer animal luck of being alive under a wide sky. He saw that same man older, crowned, standing in a hall of new-cut timber while hungry faces watched him divide a winter's grain, and the weight of it settling on him not like glory but like a yoke. He saw the door in the hill — *the* door — and the thing behind it, vast and patient and shaped like a hunger wearing the suggestion of a face, and he saw the king kneel before it and begin to speak terms.

And then Yara saw the price.

Not gold. Not blood. The Veil did not eat flesh; Yara had known that much since the palace, since the servant girl who could no longer remember her mother's face. It ate memory. It ate *personhood*, the accumulated proof that a person had existed, and the founding king — clever, desperate, already half-corrupted by the crown's weight — had agreed to feed it.

Yara watched him do it. Watched him walk through the capital with a smile on his face and his hand resting gently on the heads of his own people, drawing their summers out of them like splinters, their mothers' voices, their first loves, their griefs, their names. Watched him tell himself it was mercy, that a kingdom fed on forgetfulness was better than a kingdom devoured outright. Watched the excuse stop working, year by year, and watched him keep feeding the door anyway, because a king who has made one such bargain can never afford to be the man who admits it.

And then — here Yara's breath actually stopped, here he gripped the altar's edge with both hands like a drowning man — he watched the king come to a memory he could not spend.

A small one. A trivial one. An hour on this very hillside, before everything, when the man who would be king had lain in the grass and laughed at the sky and wanted nothing, owed nothing, *was* nothing but glad. The king held that memory for years, hoarding it the way a starving man hoards one seed. And when the Veil grew impatient, when the door began to strain, when the binding demanded more than a kingdom's worth of forgetting —

The king cut it out of himself and hid it.

Hid it below the throne, below the palace, below the deepest floor of his own soul, wrapped in wards of First Tongue so dense that even the Veil couldn't find it. Hid it the way you bury a child during a massacre. And the hiding itself took shape, the way anything loved hard enough takes shape, and became — over centuries, through vessels and bindings and the slow machinery of the kingdom's oldest lie — *flesh.*

Six or seven years old. Mud on his chin. One bootlace dragging.

Yara turned, very slowly, and looked at the child still standing with his palms on the stone, light moving across his small face like water, speaking a language dead longer than kingdoms.

"You're not a key," Yara whispered. His voice shook. "You're not the binding's lock."

Tam's eyes found his. They were brown, ordinary, a child's eyes — and behind them, impossibly, a hillside at dusk and a man laughing in the grass.

"I'm what he wouldn't give up," the child said. In Corvane's common tongue now, plain and clear. "The last piece. Me."

* * *

Afterward they sat in the shrine's lee while true dark came down, and Tam slept against Yara's side with the boneless trust of the very young, and Yara stared at the glowing characters on the altar and understood his own life differently.

Every charm he'd ever sold. Every soldier's summer, every widow's lullaby, every stolen hour he'd priced in coppers — all of it had leaked, in fragments, into the dreams of strangers. A king kneeling at a door. Everyone in Corvane who'd bought one of Yara's cheap little charms had dreamed, without knowing why, of the founding sin. He had been a courier. Three years of quiet, careful, cowardly trade, and he'd been carrying crumbs of the oldest wound in the kingdom through the streets, selling them for bread money.

He should have laughed. What came out instead was closer to weeping, and he strangled it down because the child was asleep against his ribs, and the child deserved better than to wake to a grown man shaking.

Because that was the unbearable part. Not the history, not the horror of the Veil, not even Vess somewhere in the Warden cells paying for her ledger with her freedom. The unbearable part was simpler than all that.

For weeks Yara had believed he was protecting the child. Sheltering him, shepherding him, spending what remained of his own safety to keep one innocent out of the fire — and it had made him feel, for the first time since he was nine years old, like maybe he wasn't only a thief. Like the numbness might have a purpose after all.

And the truth was the reverse. He hadn't saved the child. The child had been walking toward this shrine since before Yara met him, had knocked on his door because some remnant of the king's buried will had chosen the one memory-thief in Corvane whose stolen inventory was riddled with the dream. Yara hadn't sheltered the oldest wound in the kingdom.

It had chosen him. And it had done so, he suspected, because a wound recognizes another one.

He looked down at the sleeping face. Ordinary. Warm. A little snub-nosed, a little dirty, breathing slow. The founding king of Corvane had built a realm on fed forgetting, had damned a thousand years of his subjects to keep his bargain alive, and the single thing he had refused to surrender — the one act of love in all that long corruption — had ended up here, asleep on the chest of a second-rate thief with frostbitten ears.

"Whoever you were," Yara murmured to the dead king, "you were worse than me and better than me in the same breath. I don't know what to do with that."

The stones did not answer. But the characters on the altar pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat agreeing to continue.

* * *

Near dawn, Tam woke, and they ate the last of the bread, and Yara thought — foolishly, desperately — that perhaps they had time. Time to rest, time to think, time to learn the language properly before the thing wearing the king traced its runaway key into the hills.

Then the child went stiff as a drawn bow, head cocked toward the eastern ridge, and said, in the First Tongue, one short sentence.

Yara didn't need a translation. Some words arrive in the body before the mind. He was already on his feet, already gathering the pack, when he felt it: a resonance rolling over the hills like weather, familiar and obscene, the pressure of a presence he'd felt only once before, in a corridor of the palace, wearing a crown.

Down in the valley, along the road they'd taken, torches. Dozens of them. Moving in formation. And ahead of the torches, riding something that cast no shadow in the torchlight, came the king.

Except Yara had seen the real king under the palace, and this was not him.

"They found us," Tam said quietly. He did not sound afraid. He sounded like a portrait again, patient and sad, and somehow that was worse. "She told them where."

"Who? Who told them—" And then the answer arrived, and it arrived like a blade: *Vess.* Vess in her cell, Vess with her forty-year ledger, Vess who had traded his childhood away once already and knew precisely what a bargain cost. "No," Yara said. "She wouldn't."

But even as he said it, he remembered the look on Vess Corhane's face the night she'd offered him back his own past and he'd refused — the look of a woman watching someone choose numbness, again, and saying nothing.

"She would," Tam said gently. "If it was for you."

The torches were climbing the valley road. The shrine's stones began, softly, to hum.
