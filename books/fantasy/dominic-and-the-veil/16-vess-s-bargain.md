## 16. Vess's Bargain

The memory arrived wrapped in a beggar's rib.

Yara found it at dawn, three days after they'd fled the burning market, tucked into the hollow of the dead fallow tree where he'd told Sefa—told no one—to leave messages if ever the world went wrong. A courier boy had died getting it there. He knew that the way he knew weather: the message was folded into a scrap of oilcloth and pushed through the ribcage of some animal long since stripped by crows, and beside it lay a copper token stamped with the Warden seal, the kind issued to informants. Someone had been paid. Someone had talked. And someone else had walked eleven miles into hill country with a stolen memory sewn into their palm because a thief in the cells beneath the palace had promised them it would buy bread for their children.

The world had gone wrong. The message had come anyway.

He almost didn't take it. That was the truth he would never say aloud, not even to himself in the dark hours. His fingers hovered over the oilcloth while the child slept against a root behind him, breath whistling faintly through a chapped nose, and Yara thought: *this is how she finds you. This is how she has always found you. She leaves a door open and you walk through.*

But his hands had already made the decision, the way hands do when the mind is still arguing. He cracked the wax seal—a smear of tallow pressed with a thumbnail, Vess's old trick from the tunnels—and let the memory spill into him like cold water down the back of the neck.

* * *

He was in a cell he had never seen but recognized instantly, because every thief in Corvane grew up on stories of the Unmaking Rooms beneath the Warden citadel. White stone. White light that came from nowhere, that cast no shadows, that existed precisely so that nothing in the room could hide—not even a thought. The walls were carved with wards in negative relief, grooves instead of ridges, so that even the language of protection had been scooped out and inverted.

Vess Corhane sat chained to a chair in the center of it, and she was smiling.

That was the first thing that made his chest hurt. Not her appearance—though she looked terrible, gray-skinned, one eye swollen shut, her famous hands wrapped in iron mitts of woven ward-wire so that she could not so much as brush a fingertip against her own memories. It was the smile. Forty years in the trade had taught Yara what that expression was. It wasn't defiance. It wasn't madness.

It was a shopkeeper's smile. The smile of a woman who knows exactly what she has to sell and what it's worth, and who is enjoying the wait.

The thing wearing the king stood before her.

He had seen it once up close, in the throne room, and everything about it had been calibrated—the warmth of the king's public face, the practiced grief in the royal voice, the whole elaborate machinery of a man pretending to be a man. Here, with no audience but a chained old woman, the machinery was shut off. The king's body simply stood, weight distributed wrong, arms hanging a half-inch too far from the torso, and its eyes reflected the white light without holding any of it, the way water reflects fire without warming.

"You have been very difficult," it said. The voice was the king's voice, pitched low, emptied of everything that had ever made a voice human. "Your people are being unmade in the square. You have watched six of them burn out of existence. And still you will not tell me where the Door is."

"I'm old," Vess said pleasantly. "Grief gets thin. Like soup stretched over too many bowls."

"It hurt. I watched you feel it."

"Oh, it hurt." She flexed her wired hands, an old habit Yara knew—she did it before every extraction, rolling warmth back into her fingers. "You're welcome to keep going. But I'll tell you something about pain, your hungerliness, since you've only ever borrowed other people's." Her good eye glittered. "Pain spent well doesn't run out."

The Veil regarded her. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of white stone, a bell tolled the hour, and Yara felt the strange vertigo of remembering a sound inside someone else's memory—Vess hearing it, cataloguing it, timing herself by it. She was counting something. He realized with a lurch that she was counting the bells since her last interrogation, keeping track of days, which meant she believed there would be enough days for time to matter.

"The child," the Veil said. "And the thief Joubert. They travel toward the hill country."

"Do they."

"They carry the binding's key to the Door. If they reach it before I do, everything ends. Everything I have eaten for ten years, everything I am owed, everything this kingdom was built on spending. I will not permit it." It leaned closer, and the light bent wrong around its face, shadows pooling in places shadows shouldn't pool. "You know where the Door is. You have known for twenty years. You stole the knowledge yourself, from a dying warden of the shrine, and you have kept it in a vault of your own mind behind locks I cannot pick."

"Flatterer," said Vess.

"Tell me." The voice did not rise. It didn't need to. The white light flickered, guttered like a candle in wind, and for a moment Yara saw—or Vess saw, and Vess's memory showed him—the cell walls ripple, the negative wards straining, something vast leaning against the inside of the king's skin. "Tell me, and I will give you anything in my power to give. And my power," it said softly, "is memory. All of it. Every theft ever committed in this kingdom, every scrap ever burned, hoarded in me like grain in a famine year. Your youth. Your mother's voice. Forty years of taking, returned to you whole. Or—" the mouth curved, a gesture learned secondhand and worn badly, "—anything else. Name it."

And here Yara understood, watching, that he had seen this exact scene before. Not this room, not this woman. But this shape. This precise shape of temptation, offered in an empty voice by something that ate.

It had offered him his childhood. In the dark beneath the palace, wearing a servant girl's trembling hands, it had offered Yara everything he'd lost, and for one suspended heartbeat he had wanted it so badly his teeth had ached.

Vess Corhane sat in the white light and laughed. It was a real laugh, rusty and painful, ending in a cough.

"That's what you don't understand," she said, wiping her good eye with the back of her wire glove. "I've heard that offer before. Word for word. Same cadence. Different century, different monster, same hungry little speech about grain and famines." Her face hardened into something ancient. "Twenty-two years ago I stood in a burning house in the Tallow Quarter and a crown man offered me the same trade. Two thieves, traitors to the law, condemned to unmaking. Their memories were worth a fortune—thieves' craft, three generations of it. Give me the children and I'll let you strip the parents' minds before the Wardens arrive. Walk away rich. Walk away whole."

The Veil went very still.

"There was a boy," Vess said. "Nine years old, hiding under floorboards with his little sister, both of them screaming without sound because I had already pulled the screams out of them—I needed them quiet, you understand, I needed the house silent so the soldiers wouldn't search the cellar." Her voice didn't shake. Yara realized, with a sensation like the floor dropping out of the world, whose screams those had been. Whose sister. "The man offered me the parents. Their craft, their memories, their lives' worth of stolen goods—all mine, if I gave up two children who would grow into witnesses. Into threats. Into *him*, maybe, someday, standing in front of a hungry thing in a white room." She lifted her chin. "I refused. I took the children out through the drains and I wiped the boy's childhood on the road north, because a child who remembers being hunted is a child who gets hunted twice, and I was not strong enough to raise what I had saved. So yes. Yes, I know where the Door is. And yes, I will give it to you."

The white light steadied. Something like satisfaction moved across the king's borrowed face—or the suggestion of satisfaction, the way a mask suggests a face.

"But hear the price," Vess said, and now her voice was steel drawn slow and thin. "You leave the boy Joubert alive. Not touched. Not followed. Not one Warden boot on the road behind him. He reaches the hill country free or the bargain is void and you can pull this cell apart molecule by molecule and find nothing but an old woman's spite. You want the Door? Then the Door stays closed to your hunters. Swear it in the Tongue. I know you can. I know what you are owed and what you owe."

"Why," the Veil said. It was not quite a question. It studied her the way a scholar studies a text written in a dead hand. "You erased him. You hollowed him to protect yourself. He does not know. He hates you for a crime he cannot name. Why spend your last coin on him?"

And Vess Corhane, forty years a thief, the woman who had taught Yara that love was a luxury asset and attachment was a liability on the ledger, looked at the thing in the king's skin and said:

"Because I refused that bargain once, and a boy and his sister burned in the Tallow Quarter for it, and I have carried that sale for twenty-two years without once putting it down." Her voice cracked, finally, just once, like ice over deep water. "I'm not carrying another one. Take the Door. Leave the child I failed. That's the whole of my ledger. Close it."

The Veil considered her for a long moment. When it spoke, it spoke in the First Tongue, and even filtered through Vess's memory the words landed in Yara's chest like stones dropped down a well—he felt the oath form, felt it bind, felt the old hungry thing put its name to terms it clearly believed did not matter, because it intended to break them.

Vess heard it too. Yara watched her hear it, watched her weigh it, watched her decide—with full knowledge, with eyes open—that a monster's oath was still the best currency on the table.

She told it where the Door was.

The memory ended there. It ended the way memories end when they are cut rather than concluded: mid-breath, the white light flaring as the cell door opened, and the very last thing, the frame burned deepest into the record because it was the frame Vess had poured all her intention into—

Her face turned toward the door. Toward the corridor beyond, toward whatever courier she had spent weeks cultivating through the walls with bribes and threats and the promise of bread for children. Toward the crack in the world she had found and widened with her one remaining tool.

Toward him.

*If you are hearing this, boy,* the cut said, in the place below words where intentions live, *then I sold you to save you, and I need you to hate me fast and forgive me faster, because you have four days, and I have bought them with the only thing I had left to give.*

* * *

He came back to himself kneeling in the wet grass with blood in his mouth. He had bitten through the inside of his cheek without noticing. Dawn had come up gray around him; somewhere down the slope a thrush was singing, idiot and indifferent, and the child sat up behind him rubbing his eyes and said, in the small careful voice he used for Yara's worst moments:

"You're crying."

"I'm not." He was. He wiped his face with his sleeve, mechanically, the way you'd sweep broken glass. "Old woman's memory. It leaks."

The child crawled forward and looked at him with those too-old eyes, the founding king's humanity looking out of a seven-year-old's face, and Yara understood that the child already knew. Of course he knew. He had probably felt the memory arrive, felt it pass through Yara the way Yara felt his charms whisper. Nothing got past the boy anymore. Nothing got past either of them.

"She gave them the Door," Yara said. His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "She traded us. Twenty years of hiding it and she handed it over in a white room with chains on her hands."

"To save you."

"To save me." He laughed and it came out wrong, jagged. "Don't you see what that means? She sold the Door, she sold the binding, she sold every soul in this kingdom that the Veil will starve out of existence once it seals itself away from us—it gets the Door, it wins, it eats its way clear, and the price of my worthless life was—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "She refused that bargain for my parents. She stood in a burning house and she refused it, and then she wiped me clean and left me to be raised by strangers and she called that mercy, and now—"

"Now she chose differently," the child said quietly.

"Yes. Now she chose differently." And there it was, the thing he couldn't say around, the stone in his throat: *she loves me. She always loved me, and she loved me so badly, so clumsily, with such ruinous methods, that the same woman who let my parents burn could not let me burn, and I don't know which act is the true one and I don't think she knew either.* Betrayal and sacrifice, the same hand, the same signature. There was no separating them. There never had been. Every kindness Vess Corhane had ever done him had been performed with a knife in the other hand.

The oath was the problem. That was the thought that surfaced slowly, coldly, out of the churn of his grief, and it saved him, because it was a problem he could work. The Veil had sworn in the First Tongue: Yara untouched, unfollowed, four days of clear road. Four days. The Veil intended to break that oath the moment the Door was sealed—or perhaps sooner, oaths made by hunger being what they were—but the oath also bound *the Veil*, not its servants, not its Wardens, not the crown it wore and the armies that answered to the crown. Vess had been precise. Vess had been a thief to the last. But precision had seams, and seams were doors.

Unless. Unless the Veil reached the Door first. Unless it sealed itself into the binding's root and became the law of the land forever, and then no seam anywhere would matter again.

"She told them where it is," Yara said. "So we go faster."

The child was quiet for a moment. Then: "She wanted you to hate her."

"I know."

"So that you wouldn't grieve." The boy looked at him with that terrible, ancient tenderness. "So that hating her would be lighter to carry than losing her. She left you a shortcut." He paused. "You don't have to take it. Stealing feelings is your trade, isn't it? Refuse the delivery."

Yara stared at him. Six years old, seven at most, sitting in the ruins of everything Yara had spent three years building, and dispensing wisdom that Vess herself—hard, brilliant, gutted Vess—would have wept to hear.

"I taught you that," he said. "Last week. About refusing deliveries."

"You teach better than you think," said the child, "when you forget to be ashamed."

Yara got up. His legs shook and he let them, briefly, and then he stopped letting them. He took bearings off the sun, off the line of the hills, off the distant smudge of the shrine ridge where the Door waited in its hill like a splinter waiting in flesh. Two days' hard walking if the weather held. The Veil's Wardens would move faster—they'd have horses, roads, and the oath's four-day window to abuse.

Four days, minus however many had already bled away between the white room and the crow-stripped ribcage.

He shouldered his pack. Behind him the child scrambled up, small hand finding his, and together they started up the slope toward the hill country, toward the Door, toward whatever waited at the binding's root—an old woman's confession, a monster's broken oath, and a bargain struck in a white room that Yara intended, with everything left in him, to make worthless.

Behind them, far to the south, a bell began to toll. Not the hour.

The alarm.
