## 17. The Descent

The Door did not look like a door. That was the first cruelty of it.

Yara had expected stone carved with warnings, a threshold, hinges, something that admitted to being an entrance. Instead there was only the hill itself, green and ordinary under a sky the color of old pewter, with a single rowan tree growing at its crown like a sentinel that had forgotten what it guarded. The child stood before the slope with his hand outstretched, fingers spread against air that shimmered faintly — not with light but with attention, the way a sleeping face changes when someone watches it too long.

"It's listening," the boy said. He had told Yara his name at last, three nights ago in the ruins of the shrine, and the name had gone through Yara like a splinter: *Auren*. It was not a name anyone gave a child. It was a name someone *kept* — kept so long it had worn smooth, like a river stone carried in a pocket for centuries. "It's been listening since we crossed the river."

"Then let's not keep it waiting," Yara said, and hated how steady his voice came out. Steadiness was the first thing Vess had ever taught him, back when he was nothing but hands and hunger: *the mind follows the voice, little thief. Make your voice calm and your fear has nowhere to sit.* Twenty years of that lesson, and he still could not decide if it had saved him or hollowed him further.

Auren pressed his palm flat against the hillside.

The grass parted. Not cut — *parted*, blades folding away from each other like a crowd making room, and beneath them the earth opened into a stairway of pale stone that descended into dark. The air that rose from it smelled of candle smoke and lilies and, underneath both, something older and less kind: the smell of a room long shut, where something had been breathing anyway.

Yara had spent three years telling himself that memories were things — objects, goods, inventory. You lifted them, you bottled them, you sold them to lonely people who wanted an hour of someone else's sunlight. A memory of a summer was a charm. A memory of a mother's voice was a charm. It had made the trade bearable, turning grief into merchandise, until grief became the only currency he understood.

Standing over that stairway, he understood at last how wrong he had been.

The past was not a story. The past was a place. And this place was still occupied.

"Ahead of them," he said quietly. Sefa's face rose in his mind — half-unmade, forgiving him, and he pushed it down where it could burn without consuming him. Vess, too: traded away, or trading, he still didn't know which. Every hour they lingered was an hour the Wardens gained. An hour the Veil gained. "We go down fast, we find the binding's heart, and we don't stop. No matter what we see. Do you understand me? Whatever's down there, it will want us to stop."

Auren looked up at him with eyes far too old for a six-year-old's face. "It won't have to want," the boy said. "That's what my father learned. It only has to be true."

And he stepped through, and Yara followed his own dread down into the hill.

* * *

The first chamber was a summer afternoon.

There was no other way to describe it. The stair opened into a wide hall of that same pale stone, but the hall was filled with light — slanting, honeyed, late-afternoon light such as Yara had stolen a hundred times and never once lived inside. Dust motes hung motionless in it like suspended gold. Somewhere, unseen, bees worked. A woman's laughter came from around a corner that should have led deeper into the hill and instead led, when they rounded it, into a walled garden heavy with roses past their season, roses that should have rotted decades ago and instead hung ripe as the day they bloomed.

A woman knelt in the garden dirt with her sleeves rolled, planting something. She was young. Her hair was dark and coming loose from its braid, and she talked to whatever she planted in a low, running murmur, the way some people talk to children, or to God.

Yara stopped walking. His whole body refused the next step.

"She's not real," he said. "She can't be real. This is memory. Sealed memory. The founding king put her here when he—"

"He didn't put her anywhere." Auren's voice was very quiet. "He couldn't take her out. So she stayed."

The woman looked up.

Her eyes found Yara across the garden, and they were not blank the way the false king's eyes were blank, not hungry the way the Veil was hungry. They were simply, terribly present — the eyes of a person who has been alone with herself for four hundred years and has gotten very good at it and very tired of it. She smiled at him, and the smile was kind, and kindness after everything was almost worse than horror.

"You're new," she said. Her voice matched her laughter; Yara felt it land somewhere behind his ribs. "New ones don't come. New ones never come. Only the same ones, walking the same paths, saying the same things." She wiped her hands on her apron and studied him. "You're not one of the same ones. You smell of *outside*. And you—" her gaze slid to Auren, and everything in her face changed at once, broke open at once, the way ice breaks, all at once or not at all. She rose slowly, dirt falling from her knees.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, *there* you are."

"I'm sorry," Yara said, because it was the only thing he had. "I'm sorry — who are you? What is this place?"

"I am what he would not spend." She said it simply, without drama, the way you'd state your name to a landlord. She reached toward Auren, and her hand stopped just short of his cheek, trembling, asking permission. The boy leaned into her palm, and the woman made a sound that Yara recognized with a jolt because he had heard it once from his own throat in the Warden cells, kneeling over Sefa: the sound a person makes when grief and relief arrive together and neither will wait for the other. "Four hundred years I've been the shape of the thing he refused to trade. He bound the Door with everything else. His victories. His crimes. His bargains. But not me. Never me. He said a binding needs one true thing left unspent, or it isn't a binding, it's only a cage." Her thumb moved along Auren's cheekbone. "Look at him. Look what grew from the unspent part. All this time, I thought I was what saved the kingdom. Perhaps I'm what saved *him*, and the kingdom merely inherited the mercy."

"The Veil wants him," Yara said. "It's been hunting him since he appeared at my door."

"The Veil wants everything. That is its whole nature. It wants the way fire wants." She withdrew her hand reluctantly, and her expression firmed into something older than her young face. "You're going down to the heart. Then listen to me, thief — because I know what you are; the hill tells me things, and the hill has known thieves before. Down there, my husband sealed his confession. Not a confession of sins. A confession of *arithmetic*. He counted his people the way you count charms, and he spent them, and he called it kingship. When you find that memory, do not look away from it. Whatever mercy you feel rising in your throat — swallow it. Mercy is how he talked himself into it. Mercy is the coin the Veil prefers."

She turned back to her roses. Conversation, apparently, was over; four hundred years teaches economy. But as Yara turned to go, her voice caught him one last time, soft as falling petals:

"He loved me. Remember that too, when you judge him. Both things are true. They always are."

Beyond the garden wall, the light ended. Beyond it, the stair went down into a darkness that was not empty, and Yara walked into it carrying her words like a blade held by the wrong end.

* * *

They descended through the king's love, and then they descended through the rest of him.

The second chamber was winter, and war. A hall of grey snow under a black sky, filled with the sound of an army breathing — thousands of men asleep in formation, frozen mid-dream, their tents pitched forever. On a ridge above them stood a young man in a plain circlet, watching fires burn in a distant valley, and even rendered in memory-light Yara knew the set of his shoulders: the shoulders of someone deciding something that will damn him and calling it duty. Yara tried not to look. He failed. He always failed; that was the whole sickness of his gift. He saw the moment the young king chose to feed prisoners to something in a pit to spare his starving soldiers, and he saw the exact texture of the self-forgiveness that followed, smooth as river ice, and he thought of every charm he had ever sold and felt the ice crack under his own feet.

The third chamber was a courtroom, endlessly repeating. The founding king stood before judges who wore no faces, pleading a case that had already been decided, while in the gallery sat rows of ordinary people — a baker, a spinner, a soldier boy no older than twenty — all of them watching with patient, terrible understanding. Each time the scene reached its verdict, it reset, and began again, and Yara realized with a lurch that this was not punishment. This was the king *rehearsing*. Four hundred years underground, and some part of him was still trying to argue himself innocent.

"Don't watch," Auren whispered, tugging his sleeve. "The chambers get heavier. Father used to say the deep memories pull. Like water."

"How would you know what your father—"

"I remember being him." The boy said it the way a child says it has rained. "Not all of me. But some. The unspent part remembers what it was spent *from*."

The fourth chamber nearly took Yara's feet out from under him, because it was Corvane — but Corvane as it had been before the founding, a fishing village on the river, small and poor and loud, and standing on the dock was a man mending nets who had the founding king's face and none of his crowns, laughing at a joke Yara could not hear. The bargain chamber. The original sin, played on loop: a younger version of the laughing fisherman walking up from the river at dusk, following voices, finding the hill door open when it should have been sealed, and the dark inside the hill leaning out toward him like a host greeting a guest it had waited centuries for. Yara heard the first words of the First Tongue spoken as an offer rather than a binding, warm as a market vendor's patter, and the cold thing in the dark said — in a voice Yara recognized, because he had heard it wearing the king's body in the palace throne room —

*"You want to be great. I want to be fed. These arrangements are the oldest in the world, little king. Everyone leaves this table having gained."*

Yara walked faster after that. Auren kept pace, small hand in his, and Yara realized somewhere in the fifth chamber — a flooded cathedral where drowned bells rang notes that made his stolen inventory ache in his pockets — that he had taken the boy's hand without deciding to, and that the boy had let him, and that he could not remember the last time he had touched another human being except to take from them.

* * *

The sixth chamber was occupied by something that was not a memory.

Yara knew it before he saw it, the way you know weather changing. The air went thin and attentive. The pale stone underfoot developed a faint pulse, slow and vast, like the heartbeat of something buried deeper still. And ahead, blocking the stair, stood a figure in Warden grey — one figure, alone, impossible, its boots silent on four-hundred-year-old steps.

"You're fast," the Warden said, and pulled off its mask.

The face beneath was Yara's own.

Not quite. Ten years older, maybe. Grey at the temples, seamed with lines his face hadn't earned yet, eyes that had seen the inside of every cell Yara feared. But it was his jaw, his nose, the scar he'd carried since — since he couldn't remember, since Vess took the memory of earning it along with everything else.

"No," Yara said.

"You'll get there," the figure said, with terrible gentleness. "Down at the heart, the hill shows you arithmetic. What you spent. What was spent on you. I'm just the sum arriving early." It stepped aside, gesturing down the stair with a courtesy that was worse than menace. "The Wardens are two hours behind you, thief. The Veil walks with them, wearing its king. It let you reach the Door because the Door opens from both sides, and it has been trying to get in for four hundred years. You aren't fleeing it." The older Yara smiled, and the smile was kind, and kindness after everything was almost worse than horror. "You're carrying the key to it."

"That's not true," Auren said fiercely. His small hand tightened around Yara's fingers. "He's not a key. He's a person."

"So was the founding king," the old man said, and dissolved into dust that fell upward, into the dark below, showing them the way.

They stood at the top of the last flight of stairs. Below them, at the bottom, Yara could see light again — not honeyed, not golden. White. The flat, sourceless white of a page before anything is written on it. And in that whiteness, faintly, he heard a sound he had heard once before in his life, on the worst night of it: a woman's voice reading aloud, patiently, to a child who was trying to sleep.

His mother's voice. In a hill under the world, four hundred years deep, in a memory that could not possibly be his.

Beside him, Auren whispered a word in the First Tongue, and translated it without looking up:

"*Descend.*"
