## 22. The Child Who Remembers Him

The road out of Corvane was called the Pilgrim's Way once, before pilgrims became suspects and the word *way* became a thing you could be arrested for walking. Now it was just a dirt scar winding north through the hill country, and Yara Joubert walked it the way a man walks when he is carrying something breakable — slowly, carefully, and with no idea where he will set it down.

He had been walking for eleven days.

The city was behind him, still smoking in places. From the last rise he had looked back and seen the towers of the palace catching morning light as if nothing had happened, as if they had not spent a decade hollowed out and worn like a glove by something older than hunger. The unbinding had cracked the magic laws along with everything else. Wardens were laying down their iron in the streets. The unmade — the ones whose names had been burned from record, whose faces had been scraped off warrants and portraits and gravestones — were being remembered now in public squares by weeping strangers who did not know why they were crying. Corvane was learning to grieve on an enormous scale, and Yara could not stand to watch it, because every grief in that city had a cousin in his chest.

So he walked. He carried his full past with him the way other men carried stones in their packs — not because he wanted to but because he could not put it down. That was the price. That was always the price. He knew his mother's hands now, the flour in the creases of her knuckles, the song she sang off-key on wash days. He knew his father's laugh, the smell of the tannery, the name of the dog he'd loved when he was six. He knew his own name.

*Ithak.*

He had not said it aloud since the night of the unbinding. It sat in his mouth like a coin from a country that no longer existed. Every time he reached for it, he felt the edges of everything attached to it: the raid on the thieves' quarter, his parents' faces going slack as the Wardens took them, the nine-year-old boy standing in an alley holding a memory he didn't understand yet — the memory of being loved — which he would carry for three days before Vess found him, fed him, and then, as payment for her training, took it away.

Nineteen years. He had been Ithak for nine years and Yara for nineteen, and neither one of them felt like the whole truth. The whole truth, he was learning, was heavier than either name alone.

The hills were quiet this far north. No villages, no patrols, just gorse and wind and the long grass bending in long silver waves. His boots had gone soft at the heels. His coin purse held almost nothing, which suited him; he had not lifted a memory since the night he poured three years of stolen lives back into their owners in the dark beneath the hill, and he never would again. His hands felt strange without the trade in them — light, unskilled, like a musician's fingers after the instrument has been burned.

At midday he stopped by a stream and drank, and caught sight of his reflection, and studied it the way you study a stranger at a funeral, trying to place them. Thirty-one years old, or so Vess's ledger said. Grey coming in early at the temples. A face that had practiced emptiness so long it was only now, week by week, remembering how to hold anything else.

"You look terrible," he told the reflection.

The reflection, having his whole past now, agreed.

* * *

It was evening when he heard the footsteps behind him.

He knew them before he turned. Light, quick, uneven on the gravel — a child's stride, but older than a child's rhythm somehow, the walk of someone who had learned to move a very long time ago and was only borrowing small legs to do it. Yara's hand went to his hip out of old habit, found no knife there, and dropped.

The boy came up the road through the last gold light, barefoot, cloakless, entirely unbothered by the cold, exactly as he had been the first night on Yara's doorstep — except that now his eyes were his own. That was the difference. In the weeks after the unbinding, Yara had watched the child change by slow degrees, watched the ancient weight behind his gaze settle and soften into something that was still old but also, unmistakably, *young*. When the binding sealed itself with the boy as its keeper, Yara had thought that was the end of it. Keepers stayed. That was the nature of keeping.

But the Veil was gone, and the Door was closed, and perhaps a keeper of nothing was free to walk wherever he liked.

"You're supposed to be in the hill," Yara said.

"The hill doesn't need me anymore." The boy stopped three paces away, head tilted, studying Yara with the frankness only children and the very old permit themselves. "There's nothing inside it now but a confession and a grave. Confessions want to be told. Graves don't mind being quiet." He wrinkled his nose. "I prefer roads."

"Roads go somewhere."

"So do you," the boy said. "You just haven't decided where."

Yara turned and kept walking, because it was easier than standing still under those eyes, and because some stubborn animal part of him believed that if he kept moving, the impossible things would stop following him. They never did. The boy fell into step beside him easily, matching his stride, and for a while the only sound was gravel and wind and the distant complaint of a curlew.

"I can't keep you," Yara said finally. "Whatever you're thinking. I have nothing. No trade, no money, no—" He stopped himself before he could say *no self*, which was dramatic and half false and the kind of lie numbness used to tell.

"No," the boy agreed pleasantly. "You have less than nothing. You have everything, all at once, all the time. I know. I remember what that's like. I was a man once, you know. Before I was a doorstop." He said it lightly, but the lightness had a floor under it, load-bearing. "Aurel. My name was Aurel. The king — the first one, the real one, the one in the grave in the hill — he was me. Or I'm what was left over after he cut me out and put on his crown. The part that knew what the bargain cost." He kicked a stone off the road, watched it vanish into the gorse. "He couldn't spend me. Everything else, yes. His people's summers, their mothers' voices, the names of their dead. He fed it all to the hungry thing so it would give him a kingdom. But he kept one memory. One small human thing. Me."

Yara said nothing. He had stood in the heart of the hill and heard the founding king's confession pour out of the walls; he did not need it repeated. What he needed — what he had failed to find in eleven days of walking — was any answer to the question underneath all the others, the one that had followed him out of the dark:

What do you do with a survival that was bought the way his was bought? With Sefa half-unmade in a cell while he hid. With a patrol of smiling blanks. With a childhood taken from a boy so that a thief could train him, and a thief trained so that a kingdom could keep stealing from itself?

"You're doing it again," the boy said.

"Doing what."

"Carrying everything at once and calling it walking."

Yara laughed despite himself — a short, rusty sound. "You've been spending too much time around dead kings."

"One dead king. And you." The boy stopped, and this time he planted himself in Yara's path so that Yara had to stop too or walk through him. The light was nearly gone now; the hills were turning blue. "I came to say a thing. I walked eleven days behind you waiting for the right moment, and this is it, because if I wait longer I'll lose my nerve, and I am several thousand years old and I refuse to lose my nerve to a charm-seller with bad boots."

Yara waited.

"When you poured everything back," the boy said, "all those memories you stole — the soldier's summer, the widow's wedding day, all of them — you gave them away whole. You didn't keep copies. You didn't keep the good parts. I watched you do it and I thought: here is a man who understands what returning costs." He paused. "But nobody gave yours back to you. It was returned whether you wanted it or not. Your childhood came flooding in and it hurt you, and you've been walking ever since like a man trying to outrun his own shadow by staying ahead of the sun." He took a breath, and when he spoke again, his voice was different — careful, ceremonial, the First Tongue's cadence showing through the common speech like bone through skin.

"I am the part of a king that remembers what he chose to forget. Remembering is my whole nature. And I choose to remember you."

Then, in the First Tongue, the language that made charms crack and Wardens hunt and kingdoms kneel, the boy said his name.

Not *Yara*.

*Ithak. Son of Maren. Son of Odo. Who was loved.*

The words went through him like water goes through dry ground — finding every crack, filling everything. It was not pain, exactly. It was the end of a particular pain: the ache of a socket built for a name and left empty for nineteen years. He heard his mother's voice saying it. He heard it shouted across a courtyard. He heard it spoken over a small grave that wasn't his — his sister's, he realized, he had a sister, he had *had* a sister — and he stood very still on the empty road while nineteen years of exile from his own life closed like a wound closing, slow and clean and agonizing.

When he could speak, what came out was: "That's cheating. Using the First Tongue."

"It's efficient," said the boy. "I'm told efficiency is a virtue. By a dead king. Who was not virtuous." He shrugged. "You needed to hear it in the language that binds. Not because names are spells. Because *you* needed to know that when I say it, I mean it. I mean all of it. Son of Maren. Who was loved. That's who you are. Not what was stolen from you. Not what you stole. What you were given, first, before anything else."

The stars were out now, hard and clear over the hills. Yara sat down right there on the verge, because his legs had opinions of their own, and after a moment the boy sat beside him, shoulder to shoulder, small and warm and impossibly old.

"I don't know how to be him," Yara said quietly. "Ithak. I've been Yara so long. Yara knows how to survive. Yara knows how to be empty and call it freedom."

"Then don't be him," the boy said. "Be both. Be the man who survived and the boy who was loved, and let them argue. They'll work it out. They have your whole life." He picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his fingers. "Identity isn't a thing that gets stolen or returned, you know. I learned that in the hill. Aurel thought he could cut out his guilt and keep his crown, and it made him a door. You thought you could cut out your past and keep your life, and it made you a thief. But you *chose* to give it all back. Nobody made you. That choice is more yours than any name. Names are given. Choices are *kept*."

Yara looked down at him. "Is that the First Tongue talking?"

"That's the road talking. Roads are very wise. They get walked on a lot."

And Yara — sitting in the dark with his whole life in his chest like a lantern he was finally willing to light — felt the question that had driven him out of Corvane turn itself over and show its other face. Not *what does a survivor owe?* but *what does a survivor have to give?*

His hands, empty of the trade, were not empty.

They knew how memories worked. Where they lived, how they broke, how they healed. He knew the First Tongue now, enough of it. He knew what the Wardens had done and what the crown had done and what the hungry old bargain at the bottom of everything had cost, and there were children in Corvane — unmade children, erased children, children of the burned and the purged — who were being remembered this season by weeping strangers and did not know their own names. There were thieves like the boy he had been, standing in alleys holding memories they couldn't carry.

Somebody had to teach them. Not to take. To give back. To speak the returning words. To carry each other's weights, out loud, together, because that was the only binding that had ever held.

"Corvane," Yara said slowly, testing it. "They'll hang me. Half the city saw the blanks I made of that patrol."

"Half the city saw you unbind a god," the boy said. "On balance, I'd risk it."

"They'll need teachers. Now the laws are falling. People will start lifting memories again — everyone will — and no one will know the rules, because there were never any rules, that was the problem—"

"There's one rule," the boy said. "You taught it to me. Give back everything you take."

Yara looked at the road behind him — south, toward the smoke and the bells and the enormous raw grief of the city — and then ahead, at the dark, and found that the dark no longer frightened him, because he finally knew what was in it: himself, whole, unhidden.

"All right," he said. "Together, then."

"Together," the boy agreed, and stood, and pulled Yara up by the hand with a strength that had nothing to do with his size.

They turned south. The bells of Corvane were ringing, faint on the wind — not alarm, Yara realized. Naming. They were ringing out names, one by one, the names of the unmade, read into the air at last. Somewhere in that long silver list was his sister's name. Somewhere in that city was a school that did not exist yet, with two teachers who did not exist yet, and a hundred children learning the most dangerous language in the world — not the First Tongue, but the simple one, the one where you say *this happened, and I remember it, and you are not alone.*

They walked. The hills fell away. The bells grew louder.

And the boy, skipping a little to keep up with a man's long stride, began — softly, happily, in the language that binds — to speak Yara's name again and again into the dark, like a bell of his own, like a promise: *Ithak, son of Maren, who was loved, who chose, who remembers, who is remembered,* and Yara walked inside the sound of it all the way home.
