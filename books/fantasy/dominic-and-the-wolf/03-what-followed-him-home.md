## 3. What Followed Him Home

The Vesper Guild's doors closed behind Coral Blackwood with the soft, final sound of a book shutting, and for a moment he simply stood on the steps and breathed.

The night air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Above him, where the stars should have been, there was nothing — not cloud, not haze, but an absence so complete it felt less like darkness than like a page torn out of the sky. The city below the guild's hill had gone strange with it. Lamplighters moved through streets that seemed to have forgotten what they were lit for. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse at nothing. Somewhere a temple bell rang the hour over and over, as if its keeper had lost count of everything else.

Coral pulled his clerk's grey hood up and started down.

*Walk normally,* he told himself, as he had told himself every night for nine years. *You are Coral Blackwood, third-grade ledger-keeper, unremarkable, unmemorable, a man whose name is spoken only when someone needs a figure reconciled.* He kept his eyes down and his pace even, and he did not look at the sky again, because looking would mean thinking, and thinking would mean remembering the entry he had burned three hours ago in a washroom basin while his hands shook so badly the ash had scattered like startled birds.

*Ward-anchor, one, sold to a buyer unnamed. Paid in old coin. Old coin.*

He had written those words himself, in his own careful hand, three days before the stars went out. And tonight, standing at his desk with the whole guild screaming around him, he had done the arithmetic no clerk should ever have to do: the timestamp of the sale against the hour of the collapse, the route of the payment against the geography of the border wards — and the numbers had fit together with the horrible, seamless rightness of a lock accepting its key.

Someone had used his ledgers. His ledgers, his entries, his neat and invisible hand, to bring down the sky.

"Move along," a watchman said, not unkindly, waving him off the guild steps. "Curfew'll be called within the hour. Get home, clerk."

Home. A rented room above a chandler's shop in Tallow Row, two flights of stairs that creaked in a language only Coral understood, a window that faced a wall. Nine years he had lived there without once being asked a question worth answering. Tonight, for the first time, the thought of it made him feel less safe than exposed. Everyone who knew where Coral Blackwood lived also knew exactly how unimportant he was. That had always been the point. But unimportance was a kind of camouflage, and camouflage failed the moment the hunter already knew what shape you were.

He took the long way anyway. Down through the tanners' quarter, where the pits sat dark and reeking under the starless sky; across the Weep, the canal that bisected the lower city, its water blacker than usual because it had nothing left to reflect; up through the rag-and-bone rows where the poor had already shuttered their windows against whatever had eaten the heavens. The city held its breath around him. Even the rats seemed to have opinions about the dark.

It was at the mouth of Cooper's Lane, where the road narrowed between a cooperage and a wall gone soft with moss, that Coral realized he could no longer hear the city behind him.

Not quiet — cities were never quiet. But the barking dog had stopped. The bell had stopped. The drip of the canal-gates, the shuffle of some insomniac beggar, the far-off grind of the watch's boots: all of it had thinned away like fog retreating from a window, until the only sound in the world was his own footsteps and his own breath, and both of them sounded wrong, too loud, too alone.

He stopped walking.

The lane ahead of him was empty. The lane behind him was empty. And yet the dark between the buildings had weight now, a pressure, the way air presses before lightning. Coral stood very still and felt nine years of careful invisibility peel away from him like wet paper, and underneath it the old thing stirred — the thing he had buried so deep he sometimes managed to forget its shape. The storm-sense. The awareness of charge and pressure and gathering power that had been bred into his bones by people who had wanted him to be a weapon, and that he had spent nine years starving into silence.

It was singing now. Low and warning, like wire humming before it snaps.

"You can come out," Coral said, and was disgusted to hear how thin his voice came out. "I'm a clerk. I have eleven silver marks and a rent due. Whatever you're hoping I'm carrying, you've wasted your evening."

The dark answered.

It came out of the shadow of the cooperage the way weather comes over a ridge — not walking so much as arriving, all at once, everywhere, until it stood in the middle of the lane and the lane belonged to it. A wolf. But the word *wolf* collapsed the moment Coral tried to make it fit, the way a child's map collapses against real country. It stood taller than a horse at the shoulder, its coat a storm-grey shot through with paler threads like rain caught mid-fall, and its eyes —

Its eyes were the worst part. They were not animal eyes. They were the deep, patient amber of lamplight seen through centuries, and they looked at Coral with an attention so total it felt like being read aloud.

Coral's back found the mossy wall. His mind, trained by nine years of ledgers, did the only thing it knew how to do: it catalogued.

*Canid form. Oversized beyond any natural species. Movement consistent with — with the old stories, the ones the order burned the books for containing. Eyes luminous. Intelligence evident. Speech —*

"Dominic."

The name fell into the lane like a stone into still water.

Coral stopped breathing. Not the name he wore — never that, never *Coral*, which was a shell he'd built name by careful name out of anonymity and small transactions. This was the other one. The true one. The one his mother had given him in a house that no longer existed, in a country that had erased itself rather than be conquered, the one that had been struck from every register the order kept when they exiled him. He had not heard it spoken aloud in nine years. He had not spoken it himself in longer. It went through him like a blade finding the gap in armor, past every defense he owned, and it found the boy underneath, the one who still flinched when thunder rolled.

"No," Coral whispered. "No. That's not — my name is Blackwood. Coral Blackwood. Third grade. You're mistaken."

"I am never mistaken." The wolf's voice was low and vast, like rockfall heard from a distance, like the space inside a mountain given breath. It did not growl. It didn't need to. "Names are my trade, little clerk. I have eaten ten thousand of them, and I know the taste of a hidden one better than most. Yours has been sitting under 'Coral Blackwood' for nine years like a coal under ash. Still warm." The great head tilted, and the lamplight-eyes narrowed. "Still burning."

"What are you?" Coral's hand crept along the wall toward the knife he carried for cutting quills, and both of them knew it was a joke.

Something moved in the wolf's face that might, in a man, have been amusement. "You know. Your order made sure every child in your country knew, right before your order killed us all. Say it. Say the word and hear how it tastes after a thousand years of lies."

And Coral, pressed against the wall with his heart hammering like a fist on a door, did know. He had known since the eyes. There had been a fresco in the order's training hall, whitewashed over but visible in raking light, that the novices were never allowed to study too long: tall grey shapes standing in a circle around a sleeping mountain, and beneath them a caption in the old tongue that the masters claimed was a warning but that read, to anyone honest, like a eulogy.

*Memory-eaters.*

"They're dead," Coral said. "Your kind. The order — the Purge, ninety years ago. There were purges after, smaller ones. It was in the histories. It was in *our histories*, we were taught it as a triumph—"

"Yes." Flat as a headstone. "A triumph. Eleven hundred names eaten from the world in a single season. My mother's among them. My brothers'. My people's entire history lived in memory, passed mouth to mouth, and your guild ate it in an afternoon and called the silence peace." The wolf stepped closer, and the pressure in the air thickened until Coral's ears ached. "I am Ashfall. Last of the Vaelin. And I am the reason you are still breathing, though you don't yet know why."

"Why?" The word cracked out of him. "Why follow me? Why — that name, why give me back the name they took? What do you want?"

"What I want," Ashfall said, "is what was stolen. And you, Dominic of the line of Kavanagh—" the name again, that blade again, and Coral made a sound he was ashamed of, "—are the keeper of the ledgers that stole it."

The lane tilted. Or Coral did. He put a hand flat against the mossy wall and made himself stay upright through sheer clerkish stubbornness.

"That's impossible. I keep trade records. Bottled thunder, borrowed shadows, a key that opens nothing — I record *purchases*. Grain futures. Ink contracts. If something was done with the ledgers, it wasn't done by me—"

"No," Ashfall agreed, terrible and gentle. "Not by you. *Through* you. Three days ago a buyer walked into your guildhouse and bought a ward-anchor with coin old enough to predate your order, and you recorded it — date, hour, sum, seal. You wrote down the exact moment the last lock on the oldest prison in the world changed hands, in your own tidy hand, and then you filed it where any thief who knew your filing system could find it." The wolf's eyes did not blink. Wolves' eyes didn't, perhaps, or perhaps this one simply never needed to. "The wards did not fall, Dominic. They were *unlocked*. And the key that unlocked them was written in your book."

"The stars," Coral said. His voice sounded very far away. "The stars going out. That was—"

"The gods waking under a lid built to keep them dreaming. The first crack in the lid. There will be more." Ashfall sat back onto his haunches, and even seated he loomed, a hillside that had decided to listen. "Your guild will tell you it was enemies at the border. Your guild is lying. Your guild has been lying for a thousand years, and the proof of it is sitting in your ledgers, in your handwriting, and every enforcer in Vesper Hall is at this moment being told to retrieve those ledgers and the clerk who wrote them — quietly, if possible."

The word *quietly* landed in Coral's stomach like a dropped stone.

"Bury them," he said. "That's the other option, isn't it? Retrieve quietly, or bury. I've processed enough guild requisitions to know how the phrasing works."

"You always were quick," said Ashfall, and there was something almost like sorrow in it. "Quick enough to see the trap, not quick enough to avoid walking into it. So. Here is what follows you home tonight, Dominic: me. Because the ones hunting you cannot find what I am carrying, and what I am carrying is you."

"Why?" Coral pushed off the wall, and anger finally arrived to burn off some of the fear, and it was such a relief that he nearly laughed. "Why me? I'm nobody. I'm a *clerk*. I burned the page — I burned the entry tonight, do you understand? Whatever proof there was, it's ash in a guild washroom—"

"I watched you burn it."

The lane went very cold.

"From the roof across the way," Ashfall continued, unmoved. "While your guild screamed about the sky. You burned the one page that proved your innocence — that showed the sale was coerced, that showed the buyer's seal was forged, that showed *you noticed*, which makes you the only soul in that building who saw the truth and acted on it. You destroyed the evidence that you were a witness and kept the evidence that you were complicit. Every other ledger entry remains. Every copy. Every index. The guild does not need the page you burned, Dominic. It needs only your silence, and failing that, your corpse."

Coral opened his mouth. Closed it. Nine years of hiding, and in one night the hiding had turned to ash in a washroom basin, and the creature crouched in front of him had watched him do it and had followed him home through a starless city anyway.

"You need me alive," he said slowly. "A memory-eater needs a ledger-keeper. Why?"

For the first time, the wolf looked away. Out toward the starless sky, where the absence hung over the mountains like a held breath.

"Because my people died keeping records too," Ashfall said. "Records of flesh. Names carried in memory, generation to generation, back to the building of the wards themselves. When the guild ate us, it didn't just kill eleven hundred souls. It killed the only complete account of what the wards were *for* — and what was locked beneath them. I am the last copy of that book, and I am incomplete, and I am dying by inches of forgetting." The amber eyes came back to Coral, and there was something in them that fear had kept Coral from seeing until now: exhaustion. Ancient, bottomless exhaustion. "Your ledgers cross mine at the edges, clerk. Your order's transaction records touch our history in forty places I have found and a hundred I haven't. I need a reader. I need someone who can walk the guild's archives without burning, and read what your founders wrote about my people before they wrote us out of the world."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I leave, and within two days the guild's hounds find you, and you die badly in a room above a chandler's shop, and the truth dies with both of us." No cruelty in it. Just arithmetic. Coral recognized arithmetic; he respected it; he hated it. "But you won't refuse. You burned that page tonight because some part of you has been waiting nine years for permission to stop hiding. I am not offering you safety, Dominic. I am offering you the opposite. I am asking whether you want to spend the rest of your life being Coral Blackwood, or one dangerous year being what you actually are."

Somewhere far off, the temple bell began ringing again — frantic now, unscheduled, the sound of a city deciding whether to panic. Overhead the starless dark seemed to press lower. And Coral Blackwood, third-grade ledger-keeper, invisible man, stood in a narrow lane with a monster out of his childhood's whitewashed frescoes and felt, beneath the terror, a feeling so unfamiliar he needed a moment to identify it.

It was being *seen*.

"Cooper's Lane," he said at last, hoarsely. "You're telling me my life ended in Cooper's Lane, next to a cooperage, talking to a wolf."

"Wolves are honest," Ashfall said, rising, and the lane filled with him again, vast and grey and patient as winter. "It's one of the few things we have left. Come. Not home — your home is a grave they've already dug. There is a road north, and questions on it that need answers, and hounds behind us that need losing. Walk with me tonight, and tomorrow we discuss terms."

"Terms," Coral repeated. "Of course. Everything's a transaction with your kind, isn't it?"

"Everything is a transaction with *every* kind," Ashfall said. "The difference is that we admit it." The great head swung toward the mouth of the lane, toward the north and the drowned country beyond it, and the lamplight-eyes glowed steady in the ruined dark. "Now decide quickly, Dominic. The bell is ringing because the watch found your burned page in the basin ash. They'll match the handwriting by morning. By dawn, every gate in this city will have your face on it."

Coral looked back the way he had come — at the guildhill, at the towers of Vesper Hall black against the starless sky, at nine years of careful, comfortable, suffocating nothing. Then he looked at the wolf.

He had not spoken his own name aloud in nine years. He suspected he was about to swear on it.

"All right," he said. "North, then. But if you eat my memories in my sleep, wolf, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," said Ashfall, and the ghost of amusement moved through the vast grey shape of him like wind through rain. "Ledger it."
