## 4. Terms of an Uneasy Pact

The wolf did not move from the center of the road.

Coral had taken three steps backward already, his heels finding the muddy lip of the ditch that ran alongside the causeway, and still the creature stood there as though it had grown out of the ground like a grey birch — enormous, patient, its eyes catching what little light the starless sky offered and giving back something that was not reflection but attention.

"You said my name," Coral said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "The old one. Who told you?"

"You did," said the wolf. "Just now. In your head, where you keep it. You say it every time you write a ledger line and sign it with a symbol instead. *Kavanagh* is a coat you button over it. But the name underneath — that one you wear thin, clerk. Nine years of wearing it against your skin."

The night was warm for the season, but cold crawled up Coral's arms anyway. He looked left and right along the causeway. The flooded fields stretched away on either side, black water stitched with reeds, and nothing moved on them, and no one watched — he told himself no one watched — but the guild's reach was long and its ears were longer.

"Not here," he whispered.

"There is no *here* left to protect." The wolf's voice was a low rumble, like distant weather deciding whether to become a storm. It sat back on its haunches with a strange, almost human deliberateness. "The wards are down, Coral of the Kavanagh line. The sky is blind. Every ward-sensor in every guildhouse between here and the Salt Coast lit up like a struck bell three nights ago, and every one of those bells points, sooner or later, at the man who processed the sale that broke them."

Coral's mouth went dry. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Ledger entry 4,417. Three days before the fall. One item: *ward-key, antique, provenance unstated, buyer unstated, price paid in guild scrip, authorized under seal of the Third Chair.* You flagged it. I can taste the flag in you — it sits in your memory like a fishhook. Then someone above you unflagged it, and you let it go, because letting things go is how you have stayed alive." The wolf's nostrils flared. "You let it go. And the sky went out."

The ditch water smelled of rot and iron. Coral became very aware of his own heartbeat, of the ledger satchel against his hip where he had stuffed the day-book along with his burned room's few survivors: a spare pen, a whetstone, the small tin of salt his mother had given him at eleven years old for reasons she never explained.

"Even if all that were true," he said slowly, "what business is it of yours? Wolves don't read."

Something moved through the wolf's face — not quite a smile; wolves' faces are not built for smiling — that managed nonetheless to convey that Coral had said something charmingly stupid.

"I am not a wolf," it said. "I am Ashfall. Last voice of the Grieve-Kindred, whom your order called memory-eaters, whom your order hunted through three generations until there was one of us left to hunt. I am the thing your grandmaster's grandfather swore had been extirpated. And I have been walking toward you for nine years, clerk, because you are the only living soul whose ledgers hold the names of my dead."

* * *

They walked north together because Coral could not think of a better idea, and because his legs, once they started moving, refused to stop. Behind him, somewhere past the dark horizon, Vesper Guild would be discovering that its clerk Blackwood had a burned room and no corpse in it. Ahead of him lay the drowned country — the lowlands that had gone under when the river-wards failed two centuries back and nobody bothered to reclaim them, a maze of causeways and sunken fences and drowned orchards where apples still ripened every autumn on trees that stood to their shoulders in black water.

He kept the wolf at the edge of his vision. It padded along the causeway's far side, sometimes ahead, sometimes behind, its paws making no sound at all on the old stone, which Coral found far more obscene than any amount of claw-clicking would have been.

"Memory-eater," he said at last, because silence was worse. "That means you eat them. Memories. That's the trade."

"We carry them," Ashfall said. There was something almost gentle in the correction, and that gentleness frightened Coral more than menace would have. "When a people dies, their memories die with them unless someone takes the weight. My kindred took it. We carried the names of the fallen, the songs, the quarrels, the recipes for bread. We carried them so that a people cannot truly be ended while one of us still walks." A pause, measured precisely. "And yes. When we must. When a mind holds something that will rot the rest of it, we can take that too, and swallow it, and it becomes ours to bear. Your order called that theft. Your order used it as license."

"My order," Coral said, "has a great deal to answer for lately."

"Then you'll enjoy this next part." The wolf stopped. Ahead, the causeway crossed a wider stretch of water where a sunken church tower leaned out of the flood like a drowning man's last gesture. Ashfall looked at Coral directly, and being looked at directly by the creature was like being opened like a book. "The enforcers are already on the road. Two days behind you, perhaps less. Warden Sable Ives commands them."

The name hit Coral somewhere beneath the ribs. He kept walking. If his step faltered, he hoped the dark hid it.

"Sable Ives has better sense than to waste hounds on a clerk," he said.

"Sable Ives knew you when you were someone else. Which makes her the most dangerous person your guild could have sent, and the only one who might hesitate. They know that too. She will not be traveling alone." Ashfall resumed pacing beside him, close enough now that Coral could smell the creature — rain on hot stone, and under it something older, like the air inside a sealed room. "So here are the terms, clerk, and I will say them once."

"I haven't agreed to terms."

"No one ever does, at first." The wolf's tail moved once, a slow metronome. "Term the first: I keep you alive. The guild wants you retrieved quietly or buried quietly — either serves them, so long as the ledgers stay quiet with you. I am very good at keeping people alive. It is nearly the whole of what I am."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, term the second: your records. Everything. The day-books, the copies, the entries you memorized because you dared not write them down. Every trade that passed through your hands in nine years of being invisible — and I know, clerk, that you saw everything, because invisible men always do. The names of buyers who should not exist. The seals that authorized impossible sales. Somewhere in that paper graveyard of yours lies the account of what was done to my kindred, and who signed the orders, and where our dead lie nameless." The wolf's eyes held his. "Give me the names back. Give me my people's memory of themselves. And I will stand between you and everything the guild can send."

Coral laughed. It came out wrong — cracked down the middle — but it came. "You want me to hand my only leverage to a monster on a flooded road at midnight. Do you know what happens to me the moment you have what you need? Nothing keeps you honest. Nothing keeps anyone honest. I learned that from the best."

"Yes," Ashfall said quietly. "Your guild taught you that. I know. I've tasted what they taught you — not yours, clerk, others'. Guild clerks wash up dead in canals with disordered memories and tidy ledgers, and everyone agrees it must have been despair." He turned his great head toward the leaning tower and the drowned country beyond. "So keep your leverage. Term the third: you give me the records a piece at a time, as I keep you alive a day at a time. An installment plan of truth. You may spend the rest of your life paying, if I fail early. Fair?"

It was fair. That was the worst of it. Coral had spent nine years among negotiators, watching guild factors strip merchants down to the studs with contracts, and he recognized the structure of a bargain made by someone who needed the other party to survive the signing. Ashfall needed him breathing. Leverage flowed both ways, and the wolf knew it, and had said so plainly, which no guild factor in history had ever done.

But needing a monster galled. Needing anything galled. Coral had built his whole life into a machine for needing nothing — no friends, no family, no name, a room with one exit and a heart with none. And now here he was, negotiating for his life with a creature his own order had taught him to burn on sight, and the truly humiliating part, the part that made his skin crawl, was that some tired corner of him had been relieved when the wolf blocked the road. Relieved to be seen. Relieved to be known.

"What do you get," he asked, because he couldn't help himself, "beyond names? What does the last of a slaughtered people want with a clerk's paperwork?"

Ashfall was quiet for a long moment. Water lapped at the causeway stones. Somewhere out in the flood, a bird made one mournful sound and thought better of a second.

"When the last carrier dies with the last names still in her," the wolf said finally, "that is the true death. Not the killing. The killing is just weather. The true death is the forgetting." His gaze went to the drowned tower, to whatever it remembered standing there before the water came. "Your guild tried to give my people the true death, clerk. They very nearly succeeded. I intend to make them fail."

* * *

They sheltered before dawn in the shell of a drover's inn, half-collapsed, its roof open to a sky that gave back no stars. Ashfall curled in the doorway — blocking it, Coral noted, which could mean guard duty or simply an exit strategy — while Coral sat against the cold hearth and took stock of himself the way he took stock of inventories: what remains, what's lost, what's owed.

His hands were shaking. They had been shaking since the stars went out, if he were honest, and he was done pretending otherwise. He pressed them flat against his knees and made himself speak before courage finished draining out of the night.

"If I do this," he said. "If I swear to it. I want something in return beyond protection. I want to know why. Not the noble version. Why a clerk. Why me, why my ledgers, why nine years of walking. Nobody crosses a country for bookkeeping."

The wolf's eyes glowed faintly in the dark, banked coals. "Because ledgers are how the guild remembers, and the guild's memory is the lock on the door I need opened. Because your hand recorded the sale of the ward-key, which means your hand can record its unmaking. And because—" the pause was brief, but Coral heard it, heard the weight of something set aside— "because when I found your trail, I found a man who flags an impossible sale and burns with shame for letting it pass. I decided such a man might be worth the walk."

"That's not the whole answer."

"No," Ashfall agreed placidly. "But it is the installment you've paid for."

Coral huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then the humor drained away and left only the cold, and the choice, sitting in his chest like a stone dropped into a well.

Here was the shape of it: swear to a creature out of his childhood nightmares, on terms he could not enforce, trusting a monster's honor because a guild's honor had already been weighed and found to be ash. Or run alone, tomorrow, with two days' lead and Sable Ives — kind, brilliant, terrible-in-a-good-cause Sable — riding him down across country she knew better than he did.

There wasn't really a choice. There never had been, since the ink froze in his pot. All that remained was the manner of surrendering to it.

"Swearing's not enough for your kind, I take it," he said. "Words are cheap. You'd want something with weight."

"Names have weight," Ashfall said. "Yours especially. It has been carried unworn for nine years. A thing hoarded is a thing heavy."

Coral closed his eyes.

Nine years. Nine years since the tribunal, since the stripping of his sigil and the erasure of his calling, since a boy of nineteen had walked out of the Hall of Winds as *Blackwood, clerk, unremarkable* and had locked the other name in a box in his chest and thrown away every key he could find. He had not spoken it aloud. He had trained himself not to think it in full — he thought around it, the way one thinks around a missing tooth. Speaking it would feel like opening a vein. Speaking it would make real everything that had been done to him, and everything he had allowed to be done, and everything that was now coming for him with Sable Ives riding at its head.

But the box had been rattling for three days now. And the sky was empty of stars, and somewhere beneath the mountains something old stirred in its sleep because a key had changed hands through *his* ledger, with *his* flag, and hiding had officially stopped working.

He opened his eyes.

"Coral," he said.

The word came out rough, unused, like a hinge protesting after years of rust. It was only half the name and even that half cost him.

"The whole one," Ashfall said softly. "All of it. Names cut in half forget what they're for."

Coral's hands had stopped shaking. He noticed that distantly, the way he noticed everything important — late, and with a strange calm. He sat up straight against the dead hearth, in the roofless inn, under the starless sky, and he spoke the name he had buried with his old life.

"Coral Kavanagh, of the line of Maren Kavanagh, storm-caller, third of that name. I swear it." His voice steadied as it went, finding old grooves. "I swear on that name, kept hidden and kept whole: my records for your protection, delivered piece by piece, for as long as either of us draws breath to honor the debt. Witnessed by the night, which owes me an explanation besides."

Silence. Then Ashfall rose from the doorway, unfolded to his full and considerable height, and crossed the ruined common room to stand over him — near enough that Coral could see the grey depths of his eyes, and the faint silver threading through the fur at his throat like scar tissue, or like starlight waiting for permission.

"Witnessed," the wolf said. "And accepted, Coral Kavanagh. Sleep now. You've paid more tonight than you know."

Coral slept, in spite of everything — a thin, shallow sleep full of frozen ink and his mother's hands — and woke late in the grey false morning to find Ashfall standing rigid at the doorway, hackles raised, nose lifted toward the south.

"What?" Coral's hand went to his satchel. "Enforcers?"

"Worse." The wind shifted, carrying the smell of smoke from a direction where nothing ought to burn. "Riders. But not flying the guild's colors. Someone is burning the road behind us, Coral Kavanagh — and burning witnesses with it. Whoever felled the wards wants no one left alive who knows how it was done."

Behind them, far off, a column of black smoke climbed into a sky still empty of stars.
