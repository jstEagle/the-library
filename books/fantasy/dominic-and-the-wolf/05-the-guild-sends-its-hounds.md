## 5. The Guild Sends Its Hounds

The summons came at the hour when even Vesper Guild's lamps grew tired — that grey hour before dawn when the candle-wax smell of the counting rooms gave way to something older, colder, like the breath of a cellar. Sable Ives was already awake. She was always awake at that hour, though she had stopped pretending it was habit rather than dread.

The message arrived not by courier but by raven, which meant the High Warden wanted deniability built into the very bones of the order. The bird sat on her windowsill in the rain, feathers slicked dark as oiled leather, and let her take the scroll from its leg without so much as a blink. Guild ravens were trained never to blink. She had once asked a handler why, and he'd said, *So they don't miss anything,* and she had thought about that answer for three days afterward.

The scroll was short. Orders written for hounds always were.

*Warden Ives. Coral Blackwood, clerk of the third ledger-house, is to be retrieved tonight. Quietly. He is to be brought to the Undercroft with his working ledgers intact — all of them, including any pages he may have removed or destroyed. If retrieval proves impossible, burial is authorized. The guild's gratitude will be expressed in the usual manner.*

*The usual manner.* Sable read those four words twice and felt them settle into her chest like swallowed river stones. In the guild's private grammar, *gratitude* meant a pension paid to no one, because there would be no one left to collect it. It meant a name struck from the rolls and a room scrubbed and reassigned within the week. It meant that whatever Coral Blackwood had done with his ledgers, someone above her had already decided he was worth more dead than questioned.

She burned the scroll in her brazier and watched the raven watch her do it, its black eye reflecting the flame without blinking, without missing anything.

Then she put on her coat, buckled her sword where the guild could see it and her knives where they couldn't, and went out into the drowned dark of a city whose stars had gone out three nights ago.

* * *

The sky was still wrong. That was the thing no one in the guild would say aloud in the counting rooms, but every enforcer said it in the taverns after their second cup: the sky was still wrong. Three nights since the border wards had failed along the whole northern reach — not breached, not broken, but *failed*, the way a heart fails, all at once and everywhere — and the stars had gone out with them. Not clouded over. Not hidden. Gone, as if someone had reached up and closed a hand around each one.

Sable walked beneath that starless ceiling and felt the city holding its breath. Lamplighters worked double shifts. Priests of the small hearth-gods had hung charms in every doorway, little braided cords and iron nails, useless things, comforting things. And through it all the guild kept its ledgers balanced and its counters counting, because that was what the guild did: it turned catastrophe into column entries and called the sum order.

She had served the guild for nineteen years. She had believed in it the way you believe in a wall — not because you love it, but because you have spent your whole life standing behind it, and the alternative is to imagine what stands on the other side.

Now the wall had a hole in it the size of the night sky, and the guild's response was to send her after a clerk.

Except Coral Blackwood had never been only a clerk. That was the lie she had helped maintain for nine years, and she knew better than anyone how good a lie it was, because she had watched him build it with her own eyes.

* * *

She remembered the boy.

That was the trouble with this particular hunt, and she suspected the High Warden knew it, and sent her anyway — either because she was the best, or because if Sable Ives came back having buried her oldest friend, then Sable Ives would be theirs forever, bound by the guilt the way other men were bound by gold. The guild thought in ledgers too. Everyone in Vesper did, eventually. You just learned to hide your columns.

The boy had been called Dominic then. Everyone who mattered had known that; everyone who didn't matter knew him as Blackwood, the quiet one, the storm-caller's son with the ink-stained fingers and the laugh that came out sideways, like he was apologizing for it even as he made it. They had grown up two streets apart in the ward-tenders' quarter, back when the quarter still existed, back before the guild bought the land and moved the families out and put a counting house where the school had been.

Dominic had been the one who taught her to skip stones across the flooded quarry. She had been the one who taught him to throw a punch properly, elbow in, weight forward, because a boy who laughed like that needed to know how. They had been eleven, twelve. There had been a summer — she could still find it whole inside herself, unweathered, like a coin sealed in clay — when they had climbed the aqueduct every evening to watch the ward-lights come on along the northern border, a line of blue-white fire running to the horizon, and Dominic had said, *My mother says the wards are the most beautiful thing people ever made,* and Sable had said, *Your mother says that about everything she made,* and he had thrown a pebble at her and missed on purpose, because even at twelve he couldn't bear to hit anything he loved.

Then came the year he turned seventeen, and the trials came, and the exile came, and the name came off him like a bandage torn fast.

She had been there. That was the part she never told anyone. She had stood in the crowd at the Rending Court, in her first enforcer's coat, new-stiff at the shoulders, and she had watched the guild strip his name from the rolls — not *Coral*, the safe name they let him keep, but the true one, the childhood one, scraped out of the record with a bone stylus until the parchment thinned enough to see light through. She had watched him stand there and not cry, and she had understood, even then, that the not-crying was worse than crying would have been. Crying would have given them something. He gave them nothing. He walked out of the court a clerk-shaped absence, and he had spent nine years perfecting the shape.

And now the guild wanted him buried, and they wanted her to dig.

* * *

His lodging was in Tanners' Row, a room above a chandler's shop, the kind of place where the rent was paid in silence and the neighbors prided themselves on having none. Two junior enforcers had gone ahead of her — Ferro and the girl called Wick, both competent, both nervous, both under orders to secure the scene and touch nothing. Sable took the stairs two at a time and smelled the problem before she saw it.

Smoke. Old smoke, cold now, but thick underneath with something else — something sweetish and mineral, like lightning that had been poured into a bottle and left to spoil.

"Third floor," Ferro said at the landing. His voice had the careful flatness of a man trying not to be sick. "Warden, you'll want to see it yourself."

The door to Coral's room hung open. Beyond it, the room was a study in violence done quietly — which was to say, it looked almost normal. Bed made. Chair pushed in. A cup on the desk, rinsed clean. But the air was wrong, and the desk itself —

The desk was scorched. Not burned, exactly. Scorched in a single precise circle, as if a page had lain there and caught fire and burned down to nothing without ever disturbing the dust around it. The ash had been swept — mostly. A grey ghost of it remained in the wood grain, and when Sable crouched and touched it, it came away on her fingers fine as ground pearl.

"He burned a page," Wick said from the corner. She was holding a lantern low, and its light caught the edges of things: the seam of a floorboard pried up and set back down badly, the rectangle of unfaded wallpaper where a picture had hung until very recently, the closet with its single cloak gone from its hook. "One page. Then he packed. Then he left. Window's latched from the inside — he went out the front, past the chandler, past everyone. Nobody saw him go."

"Nobody sees him go," Sable said. "That's rather the point of him."

But she was barely listening to herself. She was looking at the burn mark, and the burn mark was looking back.

Because clerks didn't burn ledger pages. That wasn't temperament; it was physics. Every page in a guild ledger was writ in bond-ink, keyed to the ledger-house seal, and destroying a page without authorization triggered alarms in three different towers. Unless — unless the page had never been entered into the official index. Unless it was a private entry, an off-book entry, the kind of entry that got clerks disappeared even in ordinary years.

Or unless the person burning it knew how to make fire sit still and behave.

Storm-callers could do that. Storm-callers could hold a flame to a single page and let it eat nothing else, the way a falcon takes a lure and nothing else. It was a parlor trick among the calling families, a way to show off control. *Look, the fire obeys me down to the last letter.*

Nine years, and the guild had told itself the exile had burned the calling out of him. Nine years, and here was a scorch mark shaped like obedience.

"Wick," Sable said. "The floorboard."

The girl levered it up. Beneath lay a cavity lined with oilcloth, and in the cavity: nothing. A clean rectangle of absence, dust-free, the exact size and shape of a ledger. He had taken everything. But pressed into the oilcloth, where it had lain so long it had left its own fossil, was a single dried sprig of rowan — ward-wood, the old kind, the kind the ward-tenders' families used to tuck into their children's beds to keep the dreams honest.

Sable picked it up and held it in her palm and felt nineteen years of service shift its weight inside her, the way ballast shifts in a hull.

He hadn't just run. He had run *prepared*. Which meant he had known the summons was coming before the raven ever reached her window. Which meant he knew what was in his ledgers, and had known for at least three days — since the night the stars went out — and had chosen flight over explanation, and chosen it knowing what the guild's *usual manner* of expressing gratitude looked like.

A guilty man runs. An innocent man demands a hearing. But a man who has found something too true to survive saying aloud — that man burns one page, packs one bag, and walks out past the chandler past everyone, invisible, the way he has practiced being for nine years.

"Warden?" Ferro. Waiting for orders. The word *burial* sitting unspoken between them like a knife on a table.

"Sweep the room again," she said. "Inventory everything, down to the thread count of his shirts. If he so much as left a hair, I want it catalogued." She straightened, pocketing the rowan sprig — evidence, she told herself, though her hand closed around it more gently than evidence deserved. "And send word to the gate-wards. No one reports seeing him. No one *looks* for him. Officially, Coral Blackwood is still at his desk."

Ferro frowned. "And unofficially?"

Unofficially, Sable thought, I am going to find him before anyone else does, and then I am going to decide, alone, in whatever time I can buy, whether the boy who taught me to skip stones deserves what the guild has ordered.

"Unofficially," she said, "he's mine."

* * *

She stayed in the room after the juniors left. Enforcers were trained to read rooms the way clerks read columns, and she gave this one the full audit, slow, lamplight moving wall to wall.

It was the loneliest room she had ever stood in, and she had stood in death cells. One cup, one chair, one hook, one cloak. No pictures — except the pale rectangle on the wallpaper, and she crossed to it and measured it with her hands. Small. Portrait-sized. Hung at the height a seated man would see it from his desk, which meant he had looked at it every day, for years, while he worked.

There was no nail hole. The picture had been hung with wax, the way poor people and careful people hang things, and when he took it down he had smoothed the wax away completely. Nothing left. Even his treasures, he had practiced leaving no trace of.

Except the rowan. Except the burn mark. Except, somewhere out in the starless dark, himself.

Sable stood in the middle of that bare room and let herself remember the aqueduct, the blue-white line of the ward-fire, the boy saying *my mother says* with that fierce borrowed pride. And she let herself ask the question she had been refusing to ask since the raven: *What did you find, Dominic? What is in those ledgers that the guild would rather bury than read?*

Because that was the other reading of the orders. Retrieve the ledgers intact — *including any pages he may have removed or destroyed.* The guild did not send that phrase after a simple embezzler. Embezzlers' pages were worthless once separated from their books. No — you demanded destroyed pages back when the destruction itself was the crime, when what had been burned was something the guild needed never to have existed, and needed, simultaneously, to possess.

They weren't hunting a thief. They were hunting a witness.

And witnesses, in the guild's arithmetic, were liabilities with legs.

* * *

She went out onto the chandler's roof afterward, because roofs were where she thought, and because the city below looked better from above — smaller, more forgivable, its ten thousand lit windows like a ledger of everyone she'd fail to save.

Where would he run?

Not south. South was the guild's country, all counting houses and toll roads, every mile indexed. Not east; east was sea, and Coral Blackwood had never once, in all the years she'd watched him, done anything that required getting wet. West was the Reach, the fallen wards, the place where the stars had gone out — and no one ran toward the wound.

Which left north. North through the Drowned Country, the flooded lowlands where the maps went soft and the roads went under and the guild's writ thinned to a rumor. North was where exiles went. North was where the old heresies still smoldered in ruined abbeys, where the ward-tenders' descendants had scattered after the guild bought their quarter, where a man with no name and no calling might hope to become simply nobody.

Everyone would look north, eventually. The guild's hounds were thorough.

But Sable stood on the rooftop with the rowan sprig in her fist, and she thought about nine years of watching him be invisible, and she understood something the other hounds would not.

They would search the places an exile goes. The ruins, the heretic houses, the drowned villages. They would cast their nets over all the dark water.

And Coral would know they would. He had grown up in the guild's shadow same as her; he knew how its mind worked, knew it the way you know a parent's temper. He would never run to the obvious dark. He would run to the one place so bright with his own history, so saturated with the guild itself, that no hunter would think to look — the one place Sable Ives would never think to look either, because looking there would mean admitting what she had spent nineteen years not admitting:

That the guild had done something to him worth fleeing from. That the exile had never been justice. That the wall she had spent her life believing in had been built over something alive.

The rowan sprig bit into her palm. Ward-wood. Old kind. The kind his mother's people had carried.

Sable looked north, at the starless sky, and made herself a promise in the oldest grammar she knew — the grammar of hounds, which has only two words, *find* and *choose*.

"I'll find you first," she said to the dark. "Whatever's in those ledgers, Dominic — I'll read it before they bury it. That much I owe the boy who missed on purpose."

Below her, the city went on lighting its little fires against a sky with no stars in it, and far to the north, under that same blind heaven, two figures she could not yet see were walking into the Drowned Country — one keeping a ledger, one carrying memories — and the gap between the hound and the hunted began, quietly, to close.
