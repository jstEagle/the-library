## 6. Road Through the Drowned Country

The drowned country began where the road gave up pretending.

Coral had walked it once before, nine years ago, in the other direction, with a guild seal in his pocket and nothing of his own but a name he was already learning to bury. He remembered it as gray. Now, in the starless dark, it was not even that. The flooded lowlands stretched north under a sky like a closed lid, and the water lay black and still from horizon to horizon, broken only by the crowns of drowned elms and the occasional roofline of a village the sea had taken a generation ago and never given back.

They traveled by causeways — old stone spines raised above the flood by people who had known this country before the drowning, walking them single file, Coral's boots slick with spray, Ashfall moving beside him on four silent feet. The wolf made no sound at all. That was the worst part, Coral decided somewhere past the third mile. Not the size of him — though he was easily the size of a pony, shoulders high enough that Coral could have rested a hand between his ears without stooping, and did not — nor the eyes, which caught what little light remained and held it like coins at the bottom of a well. It was the silence. A creature that large should have announced itself. Instead Ashfall simply *was*, beside him, and Coral kept catching himself listening for breathing that wasn't there.

"You walk like a clerk," Ashfall said, after an hour of it.

"I *am* a clerk."

"You walk like a clerk who is trying to be weather." The wolf's voice was low and rough, like stones turning over in a riverbed. "Shoulders up around your ears. Waiting for something to fall out of the sky."

"Something did," Coral said. "The stars."

Ahead of them the causeway bent around a drowned church tower, its bell hanging green with weed, and Coral watched the dark water for the reflection that wasn't there anymore. Nine years of entries. Nine years of bottled thunder and borrowed shadows and keys that opened nothing, and he had told himself every night that the ledger was just paper, that recording a thing was not the same as doing it. Three days ago he had processed a sale that weighed nothing in his hands and brought down the sky.

"You could tell me," Ashfall said. "What you burned."

"No."

"It was your entry. Your hand wrote the sale that unmade the wards. I tasted it off the page-ash in your room."

Coral stopped walking. The causeway was narrow here, barely wide enough for the two of them, and the water breathed against the stone below with a slow tidal patience. "You were in my rooms?"

"I was in your rooms for three days before the stars went out. You sleep like a man rehearsing for a coffin." Ashfall sat, curling his tail over his paws, entirely unhurried, and looked up at him. "I am not asking to accuse you. I am asking because whoever sold the ward-failure used your ledgers, and I would very much like to know whether you sold it or someone borrowed your name to do it. There is a difference. One of those men I eat last."

"That's comforting."

"It isn't meant to be."

* * *

They made the first shelter an hour before what Coral's body insisted was dawn, though no light came to prove it. It was a warden's hut on stilts above the flood, one of hundreds strung along the causeway, built for toll-keepers when the drowned country still had roads worth taxing. Inside there was a cold hearth, a rope bed, and a window shuttered against a darkness that pressed on the glass like a thumb.

Coral got a fire going because his hands needed work. Ashfall filled the doorway, too large for the room and refusing to acknowledge it, and watched him with that patient, terrible attention.

"The memory-eaters," Coral said finally, because the silence was worse than conversation. "My order exterminated you. That's guild history. First founding, the Purge of the Quiet Kind. We're taught it as a triumph."

"We are taught it as a triumph," Ashfall agreed. "Yes. I have eaten enough guild memories to know exactly how it's taught. The wolf-things who devoured minds. The monsters who fed on the innocent. Do you want to know what we actually were?"

"I imagine you'll tell me regardless."

"We were the wards' builders." The words came without drama, without weight, and somehow that made them heavier. "Every border ward in the world. Every ward-stone, every ward-line, every sealed gate your guild guards so jealously — the design came from my people. We built them, and we maintained them, and the maintenance was memory. That is what a memory-eater *is*, clerk. We don't devour minds. We carry them. Someone has to remember how a thing was made or the making rots. A ward is a promise, and a promise only holds if someone remembers it was made."

Coral stared at him. The fire spat. Somewhere out over the water, something cried once and went quiet.

"The wards fell," Ashfall went on, "because the last of us who carried the founding memories was standing in your guildhall watching you burn a ledger page. There are perhaps a dozen of my kind left alive, scattered, hiding, each carrying pieces of a whole we can no longer assemble. And your guild hunted us for a thousand years to make sure of it. Because a ward whose builder remembers can be *unmade*. But a ward whose builder is dead—" his lips peeled back, not quite a smile, "—that ward becomes eternal. Unquestionable. Holy."

"And yours are the only memories that matter."

"No. Yours do too." Ashfall rose, and the room seemed to shrink around him. "That sale you recorded. Whoever bought the ward-failure needed more than coin. They needed a clerk of the Vesper Guild to process it through the deep ledgers — the ones that touch the ward-index itself. Your hand. Your seal. Your guilt, if it comes to that. Someone wanted a scapegoat with neat handwriting."

The fire cracked. Coral found he was gripping the poker hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and made himself let go.

"Then someone used me."

"Yes."

"And you need me because—"

"Because you remember things you don't know you remember," Ashfall said. "Nine years of entries. Every impossible trade that passed your desk is written in your head as well as on paper, and paper burns." He turned toward the shuttered window, toward the starless dark. "Sleep, clerk. We move again when your legs stop shaking. Enforcers travel fast, and they travel angry."

* * *

The ambush came on the second day, at a place where the causeway crossed a wide stretch of open flood, and Coral would remember afterward that he smelled the rain before he heard anything else.

It started as a smell — hot metal, ozone, the scent of a storm that hadn't been born yet — and Coral's whole body knew it before his mind caught up. He had grown up inside that smell. He had been *made* of it, once.

"Ashfall." His voice came out flat and strange. "Down."

The wolf didn't ask. He dropped flat against the causeway stones as the air above the water tore.

There were six of them. Enforcers, guild-gray cloaks, coming out of the dark on the far side of the flood in shallow skiffs, and their leader stood in the prow of the first boat with a rod of white iron leveled at the causeway, and the rod was singing. Coral knew that song. Ward-breaker. The same instrument, scaled down, that had opened the borders three nights ago — and it was pointed at the causeway beneath their feet, and the stone was already beginning to weep light along its seams.

"They're going to drop us into the water," Coral said. "They're going to drown us and call it an accident."

"They're going to try," Ashfall said, and there was something in his voice now that Coral hadn't heard before — not fear, but a kind of grim recognition, the sound of a thing meeting its hunters after a thousand years.

The ward-breaker sang louder. The causeway groaned. Cracks spidered out from beneath Coral's boots, glowing faintly, and the water below began to churn though there was no wind, and Coral felt the oldest part of himself — the part he had buried under nine years of ink and routine and careful invisibility — sit up and open its eyes.

He had spent nine years not being weather. He had practiced it like a discipline: shoulders down, voice level, never look at the sky, never reach. And now the sky was gone, the stars were gone, everything he'd ever hidden behind was burning or drowned or hunting him, and the storm-smell was in his lungs and his blood answered it the way blood answers to its own name.

"Coral," Ashfall said quietly. "Whatever you're about to do—"

He did it.

He reached.

It was not like remembering. It was like being remembered. Something vast turned its attention on him from underneath the world, something he had spent half his life pretending wasn't there, and it knew him instantly, intimately, the way a sea knows a river returning to it. Power came up through the causeway stones, through the soles of his feet, through the marrow of him, and Coral Blackwood — clerk, ghost, invisible man — called a storm.

Lightning took the flood plain. Not a bolt — a *pillar*, a column of white fire walking upright across the black water, and thunder followed it so closely that the two sounds became one enormous concussion that Coral felt in his teeth, in his ribs, in the wet crystal of his eyes. The skiffs lifted. He saw enforcers thrown like dolls, saw the ward-breaker's song cut off mid-note, saw the white iron rod tumble end over end into the flood and go dark. Rain arrived all at once, vertical, warm, smelling of ozone and something older — and through it Coral stood on the cracking causeway with his arms full of sky and could not let go.

That was the problem. That was the thing no one warned you. Calling was easy. It was the *holding* that killed.

The storm wanted more. It wanted the whole drowned country, wanted to pull the flood up into the clouds and wring it out again, wanted to crack the world's roof and pour lightning through the wound until nothing was left standing, and Coral was the only door it had, and it pushed. He felt himself thinning. Felt the edges of him going translucent, his name, his nine careful years, the taste of ink, the memory of his mother's face — all of it being drawn up and out like thread from a spindle, spent as fuel for a sky that had no bottom.

Somewhere very far away, a voice was saying his name. Not the false one. The true one, the buried one, spoken in that rough stone-turning voice, and hands — no, not hands, a mouth, huge and gentle, closing around his shoulder—

—and then the storm let go, or was made to let go, and Coral came back to himself on his knees on the causeway with rain still falling and Ashfall's jaws locked carefully, *carefully*, around his upper arm, growling low and continuous, like a hearth that had learned to snarl.

"Breathe," the wolf said around his sleeve. "Breathe, clerk. Come back down."

Coral breathed. It hurt. Everything hurt. His fingertips were numb; his heartbeat stuttered like a bird against glass. When he managed to lift his head, the flood plain was empty — skiffs capsized and drifting, enforcers clinging to wreckage or fled, the ward-breaker somewhere on the bottom. Rain fell gently now, exhausted, and in the east — in the east, where no stars had shone for three days —

One star. Faint, trembling, alone.

Ashfall released his arm and stepped back, looking at it too.

"You called a storm," the wolf said slowly, "for the first time in nine years, and a star came back."

Coral couldn't answer. His voice had been poured into the sky and spent. But he looked at that small, stubborn light, and then at the great grey shape beside him, dripping, singed along the flank where a stray bolt had kissed him, and thought: *I nearly died holding the sky up, and the first thing it did was give something back.*

Then the star flickered — guttered — and went out again, and the dark closed over the water like a lid, and far to the north, faint but unmistakable, Coral heard the sound of many hooves.

More enforcers. Many more.

And this time, Coral realized, staring at the place where the star had been, they wouldn't be coming to arrest him.

They'd be coming to find out what he'd done — and the guild did not send armies for clerks.

They sent them for storms.
