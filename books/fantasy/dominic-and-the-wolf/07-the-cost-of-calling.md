## 7. The Cost of Calling

Memory has a taste. Most humans never learn this, because most humans never eat one whole.

I had been starving for nine years — not for food, for the wolf can live on mice and rain and spite — but for the taste of a mind that remembered what mine had lost. The memory-eaters of the northern fells were slaughtered so thoroughly that I had eaten my own kin's last echoes just to keep their names in my mouth. What remained to me was thin fare: the fear-smells of travelers, the shallow surface-thoughts of merchants, the gray gruel of ordinary dreaming. And then, on a drowned road under a sky with no stars, a clerk who was not a clerk called down a storm.

I tasted it from half a mile away.

* * *

The enforcers came out of the reeds at dusk, six of them, guild-gray cloaks dark with river water, crossbows already wound. Coral Blackwood walked ahead of me along the levee with his ledger strapped to his chest like a breastplate, complaining — he complained the way other men prayed, constantly and without expectation of answer — about the mud, the midges, the state of his boots. He did not hear them. Humans never do until the last moment; they have traded their ears for their eyes somewhere in the long climb down from the trees.

I heard them. I smelled the oil on their bowstrings, the tallow they'd rubbed into their boot-seams, the sour iron of the guild's oath-tokens hanging at their throats. And beneath all that, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, I smelled the thing I had crossed four hundred miles of drowned country to find: storm.

Not weather-storm. Not the petulant crackle that lives in every summer sky. This was older, buried, banked like coals under ash — a power that had been pressed down and held there so long it had gone strange, the way a spring goes strange when you dam it. It sat in the man's blood like a second heartbeat, and it was singing.

The first bolt took the levee two paces behind him and turned the reeds to burning lace.

He did not scream. That is the first thing I will say of Dominic Kavanagh's son, and I say it knowing what it cost him later. He spun, dropped flat into the mud with a clerk's ungraceful practicality, and crawled for the cover of a leaning willow while the second bolt shredded the place where he'd been standing. The enforcers spread along the bank in a line, professional, silent. Warden-trained. They would take him alive if they could. They would take him dead if alive proved inconvenient.

I could have killed them all before they finished reloading. I want that recorded too, by whoever keeps the ledger of my own sins: I hesitated, because I wanted to see what he would do. Nine years I had hunted the trail of the ward-failure through ledgers and ruins and the fading aftertaste of dead men's memories, and here was the hinge of it, crouched in the mud with river water in his collar, and I needed to know whether he was a key or merely a lock.

The third bolt found the willow. It split the trunk and dropped half of it across his path, and the enforcers began to walk forward, unhurried, the way men walk when the outcome is a formality.

And Coral Blackwood stood up out of the mud and called the storm.

* * *

I have watched the old ones call weather. I ate the memory of it once, from a shepherd woman who had seen the last of the storm-callers work the high passes before the wards went up — she died old, in her bed, and her memory of that day was the richest thing I owned for a decade. In her remembering, the calling looked like music. Arms raised, a word in the old tongue, and the sky leaning down like a parent toward a child.

What Coral did was not music. It was a dam breaking.

There was no word. There was no gesture worth the name — his arms came up because the power came up through them, the way floodwater comes up through floorboards, finding every seam. The air around him went white and wrong. Rain stopped falling and hung. The river itself flinched, a visible shudder running upstream against its current, and every hair on my body lifted as if the world had become a hand stroking the wrong way.

Then the lightning came down — not from the clouds but *through* him, out of him, a pillar of white fire that stood him up like a struck bell — and when my sight cleared enough to see, three of the six enforcers were lying in the shallows, smoking, and the other three were running, and Coral Blackwood was on his knees in the mud with blood coming out of both ears and both nostrils and his eyes rolled back so far only the whites showed.

The storm left him the way a wave leaves a beach: dragging everything with it. I felt it go — felt the great banked heat in his blood gutter down to almost nothing, felt the terrible emptiness rush in behind it. He swayed. He put one hand flat in the mud to keep the sky from taking him, which is an instinct I recognized, though I doubt he knew why.

I crossed the distance between us before he fell. He weighed nothing. That frightened me more than the lightning had. Here was a vessel built to hold a sea, and someone had kept it empty so long the walls had thinned to paper.

"Blackwood." I shook him. "Clerk. Wake."

His eyes focused on me slowly, and there was a moment — I keep returning to this moment, I expect I will carry it as long as I carry anything — when he looked at me with no fear at all. Only a vast, exhausted wonder, as if he had forgotten himself entirely and remembered something else instead. Then the wonder closed over, the way water closes over a stone, and he said, in a voice scraped raw:

"Don't tell anyone I still had that."

"I'm a wolf," I said. "Who would I tell?"

He laughed. It hurt him to laugh; I could smell the blood moving in his throat. But he laughed, and then he fainted, and I carried him off the levee into the deep reeds where the guild's hounds would lose our scent in the standing water, and I settled down to wait for him to sleep deeply enough to dream.

Because dreams are doors. And I was so hungry.

* * *

I should explain what eating a memory is, since no one alive now understands it, and the ones who wrote the histories understood it least of all.

We did not steal. That was the first lie told about us, and it was told by men who were themselves thieves of the largest kind. A memory-eater does not take a memory away. We take the *weight* of it. Grief is a stone carried in the chest; we swallow the stone and leave the hands free. Terror, shame, the memory of a face — all of it can be carried for a while by one of us, and the carrier remembers it as if it were their own, because for a time it is. That was our office among the old peoples: we were the bearers. When a village could not survive its mourning, we carried the mourning. When a king could not rule justly under the weight of his father's wars, we carried the wars. We were mules of the soul, and we were honored, once, in the way that useful animals are honored — which is to say, fed and trusted and never thanked, and slaughtered the moment we became inconvenient.

So understand: when I lowered my muzzle to the sleeping man's brow and breathed in, I was doing the oldest work of my people. I meant only to skim. A taste of the surface — the day's fears, the shape of his guilt about the burned ledger page, perhaps the route he meant to take north. Fuel, nothing more. I had been living on mice and rain and spite, and I was weak, and hunger makes liars of us all.

But the storm had cracked him open.

It was like pressing your nose to a door and having the door be gone. His surface thoughts lay over depths, and the depths lay open, and I fell.

* * *

Nine years ago. A chamber under the Vesper Guild, round, windowless, lit by lamps that burned without flame — ward-lamps, fed on something I did not yet understand. Coral Blackwood, twenty-two years old and still wearing his true name like a coat that fit, stood before a table of senior clerks with a renewal scroll in his hands.

I knew the scroll. Gods below and stars above, I knew that scroll. My people had written its ancestor ten centuries past, in the age when the world was wounded and the old gods slept in the mountain-deep so their dreaming could knit the hurt closed. The wards were never walls. They were a cradle-song. Every renewal was supposed to be sung by those who understood the words — by memory-eaters, whose whole nature was to hold vast things gently. We sang the gods deeper, not shut. Sleep, not imprisonment. Heal, and then wake.

The guild had learned to sing the song without the singers. And somewhere in the long centuries, the song had changed key.

In the memory, Coral read the scroll. He was a careful reader; that was why they'd chosen him, a low-born clerk with a gift for old scripts and no family to ask questions. He read the renewal formula line by line, as he read everything, and halfway down the third column he stopped.

"What is this word?" he asked. "Here. *Veshk-urr.* It appears twice, and both times the grammar is wrong for binding. It's the grammar for — " and he paused, because the next part was absurd, "— for *holding something asleep.*"

The senior clerks looked at each other in the lamplight. One of them, a dry stick of a man named Corvane whose face I would later see again in worse contexts, smiled the smile of a teacher whose student has finally arrived at the lesson.

"And what," Corvane said, "did you think the wards were for?"

They told him. All of it, or nearly all — enough. The gods were not healing. The gods had healed within the first century and been left sleeping anyway, because their dreams were useful, because the guild had learned to draw power from the sleeping minds the way farmers draw milk, because a thousand years of the guild's authority rested on the border wards and the border wards rested on the prison and the prison rested on a lie so old it had become scripture. The stars going out at the borders, the slow dimming everyone blamed on weather or war or the gods' displeasure — that was the sleepers turning in their dreams. That was the sound of a cradle becoming a coffin.

"They feel it," Coral said. His voice in the memory was very quiet. "You're farming their dreams and they *feel it.*"

"The alternative," said Corvane, "is a world with no wards and no guild and no one holding back what wakes angry. Sign the renewal, Kavanagh. Every clerk signs. It's a formality."

And here is the thing I will hide from him as long as breath stays in me: he almost signed. I saw it. His hand moved, quill dipping, the formality of it, the safety — and then he thought of his mother. Not her face; her voice, saying something I could not catch, some sentence worn smooth by nine years of repetition until only its shape remained. And he set the quill down and said, "No."

One word. The smallest word. And it cost him everything: the trial they rigged, the stripping of his name, the exile dressed up as a clerical transfer, the nine years of invisible careful lonely routine, the ink and the impossible trades and the boots full of river water. All of it, purchased with *no.*

Do you understand what I saw, lying in the reeds with my muzzle against the brow of a sleeping clerk? I saw a man who believed himself a coward. Who had spent nine years rehearsing his own smallness — *I hid, I ran, I burned the page, I let them erase me* — and who did not know, had never once known, that his coward's flight had been the bravest act done inside those walls in a century. He thought his exile was his shame. It was his testimony. The guild had not erased him; they had *buried* him, the way you bury evidence, and evidence is exactly what he was.

And I saw the rest, the part that curdled the pity in my throat: I saw how the guilt sat on him. Not guilt for refusing — he did not even remember the refusal clearly anymore; he had let the guild's version overwrite it, the traitor's version, the *you abandoned your post* version. No. His guilt was older and stranger. It was his mother. Somewhere in the deep strata of him lay the knowledge — half-buried, never examined, a splinter he had learned to sleep around — that his mother's family had helped build the wards. That the hands that raised the prison included hands that shared his blood. He carried a debt he had never audited and could not name, and he paid it out daily in self-erasure, and any enemy with sense enough to look would find that debt and spend it.

Corvane would look. Corvane always looked.

I drew back. I sealed the door in his sleeping mind as gently as I knew how — and my people knew how; gentleness was our craft — and I lay with my head on my paws and watched the reeds move in the windless dark and felt the taste of him fading on my tongue like smoke.

Awe, yes. I have eaten the memories of saints and monsters and I say it plainly: I was awed. But pity is the dangerous meal. Pity tells you things about a person they have not agreed to tell. And pity shows you levers.

His guilt was a lever. His mother was a lever. The word *no*, which he could not remember giving, was the greatest lever of all, because a man who learns he once said no might say it again — or might be made to believe he never could. Our enemies would use all of it. The faction that felled the wards, the ones who wanted the gods awake for their own reasons, the ones whose agents had used his ledgers as a key — they would come for him not with crossbows next time but with truths, arranged like knives.

Let him keep his story of cowardice a little longer, I decided. Let him believe his exile was failure. A man who thinks he is paying a debt walks carefully; a man who learns he owns nothing might run wild. And there was a selfishness in it too, and I do not pretend otherwise: if he knew what I had seen, he would ask why I had not told him. And then he would ask what else my people had seen, nine hundred years ago, when the wards were young. And I was not ready to be asked. There are confessions I am saving for a road with no return on it.

So when he woke, near midnight, cold and aching and mortified, I gave him nothing but the surface of me.

"You killed three of them," I said.

He stared at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I didn't mean — I haven't done that since — I didn't know I still *could* —"

"It nearly killed you. Your heart stopped for the space of four heartbeats. Mine did not, which is how I know." I let him absorb that. "You cannot call again. Not until you've learned what you are carrying. Power spent that way doesn't come from you, clerk. It comes *through* you, and the channel isn't strong enough. Another call like that and the storm takes the channel with it."

"Then what do we do?" His voice was small. The mud had dried on his face in pale cracks. "Sable will send more. She'll send everything, once she hears what happened on the levee."

"We go north," I said. "There is an abbey in the hill country, two days past the flooded farms. The Order of the Unwritten Name. They owe me a debt older than your guild, and they shelter things the guild wants buried. You'll be safe there long enough to think."

"An abbey." He said it the way a man says *gallows*. "Of heretics."

"Of librarians," I said, "with better theology than yours."

He almost smiled. Then he looked north, where the drowned country gave way at last to black hills under a starless sky, and I watched the fear move through him and settle into something steadier. Resolve, maybe. Or the stubbornness that looks like resolve from a distance.

"One condition," he said. "When we get wherever we're going — you tell me what you know about the wards. All of it. You said your people built them. You've been circling that since the river. I'm done being the last person in every conversation to understand what's happening to me."

Liar, I thought, with something like tenderness. You are not the last to understand. You are the first, and you don't know it yet.

"Terms accepted," I said aloud.

He gathered his ledger, checked it for damage with the anxious thoroughness he gave no living creature, and started walking north through the reeds. I followed. Behind us the levee smoked quietly, and the river ran on with its cargo of ash, and somewhere far to the south a warden named Sable Ives was learning what had happened on the water and beginning, I guessed, to ask herself the question that would undo her: *what was he, before?*

Two days to the abbey, I reckoned. Two days of barbs and histories, of him asking questions I would answer at half-weight.

And somewhere ahead of us both, in a sealed archive under a heretical roof, a record waited with his mother's seal on it — though that, of course, I could not know yet.

I only knew that I had eaten a piece of his night, and hidden the best of it, and that the hiding was already beginning to weigh on me like a stone I had swallowed on purpose.

Mules of the soul. We carry what others cannot.

I was carrying two burdens now, and only one of them was his.
