## 8. The Abbey of Unwritten Names

The drowned country ended where the mountains began, and the mountains began where the maps stopped telling the truth.

Coral felt the change in the road before he saw it. For six days the floodlands had given them causeways and sunken villages and the smell of rot and river-weed, and Ashfall had moved through all of it like something the water had no argument with. Now the ground rose under their feet in slow grey terraces, and the rain — Coral's rain, the storm he had called and barely survived — was a day behind them and a season gone, and the air smelled instead of stone and pine and something older underneath, something like cold iron left out in a field for a hundred years.

"You're limping," Ashfall said.

"I'm walking with dignity."

"You're limping with dignity. There's a shrine two miles up the ridge. The kind that doesn't like visitors."

"The kind that doesn't like visitors," Coral repeated, "is exactly the kind I want. The kind that likes visitors is the kind that reports them."

The wolf made a sound low in his chest that might have been laughter in a throat built for other things. He had been making that sound more often, Coral had noticed. On the causeways, when Coral had misjudged a rotten plank and gone in to the hip in black water. At the fire on the third night, when Coral had tried to explain, with the pedantic precision of nine years of guild training, why a memory could not possibly be *carried* the way Ashfall carried things, and Ashfall had looked at him with those pale eyes and said, *You count everything. It's a wonder you can walk at all.*

Coral had not laughed. But he had not hated it either, and that frightened him more.

The ridge path climbed through pines that grew crooked and close, as if leaning together to keep a secret. Twice Coral saw marks cut into the trunks — not guild waymarks, not ward-stones, but long vertical strokes with a single horizontal line struck through them, like a word crossed out. Like a name struck out.

"What are those?" he asked.

Ashfall didn't slow. "Warnings. Or invitations. The order that lives up there uses the same mark for both."

*The order.* Ashfall had said almost nothing about where they were going, only that there was a place in the high country where the guild's writ died at the treeline, and that the people there owed him — or his kind, or the memory of his kind — a debt older than the guild itself. Coral had learned not to press. The wolf gave up his history the way a miser gives up coin: one piece at a time, and always as though he expected it to be refused.

The shrine — if it was a shrine — appeared without warning. One moment there was only the path and the pines; the next, a wall of black stone stood across it, unmortared, fitted so precisely that rain could not have found a seam. It was perhaps twice Coral's height and no wider than the road, and set into its face at the height of a kneeling person's eyes was a single recessed niche, empty.

In front of it, the path simply stopped.

"Is this a door?" Coral asked.

"It's a question," Ashfall said, and sat down on his haunches, and waited.

Coral approached the wall. Up close he could see that the black stone was not stone at all, or not only — it had a grain to it, a faint sheen like the surface of still water, and when he leaned near it his reflection looked back at him slightly wrong, slightly delayed, a man a half-second behind himself. In the empty niche he saw scratches, hundreds of them, thousands, tiny marks as fine as script, as though generations of travelers had written something there and the wall had drunk the writing.

"What do I do?"

"You tell it who you are," Ashfall said. "Or you don't, and you go back down the mountain."

Coral's mouth went dry. Nine years. Nine years of *clerk*, of *Blackwood* — a name chosen from a shipping manifest because it was common as dirt — of signing documents in a hand he had taught to slant differently than his own. He had told the wall of his body nothing, and the wall of black water waited, and behind him the wolf waited, and somewhere below them the guild's hounds were reading a trail through the drowned country that led, eventually, here.

"I'm Coral Blackwood," he said. "Guild clerk, third register. Vesper Chapter."

The wall did nothing. Of course it didn't. It was a wall.

But his reflection — the delayed one, the man half a second behind — looked at him with an expression Coral recognized from mirrors in the years before the exile, and the niche filled with a soft grey light like dawn through fog, and the wall was simply not there anymore. The path continued. The pines leaned their secret.

"It didn't ask for my real name," Coral said. His voice came out strange.

"It asked for the name you could bear to give," Ashfall said, rising. "That's the only one it keeps. Come on. They'll have seen us already. They see everyone. It's rather their whole profession."

* * *

The abbey did not look like an abbey. It looked like a village that had decided, at some point in its history, to stop pretending it wasn't one thing.

Low buildings of the same black stone clustered along a shelf of the mountainside, roofed in slate the color of storm-cloud, their eaves heavy with lichen the green of deep water. There was no wall around it. There was no gate. There was a bell — Coral heard it as they came up the final terrace, a single low note that seemed to come out of the mountain itself rather than any metal — and there were people, perhaps thirty of them, in grey robes with the hems darkened by weather and work. Some were old. Some were children. One woman was missing an arm and was using the sleeve of the missing arm, knotted at the shoulder, to carry firewood, and she did not look at them as she passed, and that absence of looking was the most deliberate courtesy Coral had ever been shown.

"They're erasing something," he murmured. "The whole place. Can't you feel it?"

Ashfall's ears turned. "I feel the absence of a great many names," he said quietly. "It's the quietest place I've been in a hundred years. Quiet *inside*."

They were met in a courtyard where a single rowan tree grew from a crack in the black stone, red berries burning against all that grey. The woman who came to them was small and bent and moved with the unhurried authority of someone who had outlasted every argument she'd ever lost. Her robe was the same grey as the others but her collar was stitched with the crossed-out stroke Coral had seen on the trees, worked in thread so old it had gone the color of bone.

"You're the wolf," she said to Ashfall. Her voice was dry as seed husks. "The last one. We keep a name for your kind in our oldest book, though we don't say it anymore. It's a good name. It means *the ones who remember so the world can rest*."

"It's a name," Ashfall said. "It'll do."

"And you." She turned to Coral, and her eyes were pale as milk gone thin, and they went through him the way the wall had gone through him, only deeper. "You're the one the stars went out over."

"I'm a clerk," Coral said. "Third register. I catalog trades. Bottled thunder. Borrowed shadows. A key that opens nothing. I write down what other people do and I file it and I go home."

"Mm," said the abbess, in the tone of a woman filing *that* too. "You'll be hungry. Everyone who comes up that road is hungry, though most of them don't know it until they eat. Come. We'll feed you, and then we'll decide what to do with you, and you'll find those are the same thing."

* * *

The meal was barley and dark greens and a soft white cheese that tasted of the high pasture, and bread that had been made by someone who believed bread was a serious matter. Coral ate in a refectory where forty people ate in complete silence except for the small sounds of eating, and the silence was not the silence of fear or discipline but the silence of a library, or of snowfall. He kept waiting for it to press on him. It didn't. It held him up.

He realized, somewhere in the middle of the second bowl, that no one had asked his name. Not the woman who served. Not the children who had gathered at the doorway to stare at the wolf and been shooed away and come back. Not the abbess. In the guild, his name — his false name — had been the first thing taken and the last thing given, stamped and countersigned and entered in triplicate. Here it was simply not a currency anyone dealt in.

"You could stay," he said to Ashfall, under the silence. "After. If it goes badly for me. This is — I imagine this is what your people's places were like."

The wolf's head turned slowly. For a moment his pale eyes held something Coral had no word for.

"My people's places were like this," Ashfall said, "right up until they weren't. Yes. I could stay. So could you. That's the trouble with places like this. They make you think the world could be this, and then you go back down the mountain and it isn't, and you spend the rest of your life with the taste of it in your mouth." He looked at the rowan tree through the window, the red berries. "The guild burned every one of these it found. Did you know that? Not for heresy. Heresy they could tax. They burned them because a place where names are given up freely is a place where the guild's whole architecture of debt and record and ownership looks like what it is."

"What is it?"

"A cage with very good paperwork."

* * *

The abbess came for him at dusk, when the light on the mountainside had gone the color of old pewter and the first stars — the few that had returned, the broken handful the sky had managed to rekindle since that night — were pricking through over the eastern peaks.

She found him in the courtyard under the rowan. He had not meant to be found. He had meant to stand there and feel the strange, dangerous comfort of the place and then put it away, the way he put everything away, in the drawer with the false name and the slanted handwriting.

"Your wolf is sleeping in our guest house," she said. "He takes up the whole floor. He apologizes with his eyes and does not move."

"He doesn't apologize."

"He did. You learn to read them, where I've lived." She came and stood beside him, small and bent, looking up at the broken sky. "I'm going to tell you why we let you in, clerk. The wall let you in because you gave it a name you could bear. That's the toll. But the wall doesn't decide everything. I do. And I let you in because of your hands."

Coral looked down at his hands. Ink-stained, nicked, a clerk's hands.

"You write the way the ward-builders wrote," the abbess said. "I saw it at supper. The way you hold the pen. The way your letters sit on the line like they're afraid of falling off it. My grandmother was a memory-eater's scribe — did your guild tell you the memory-eaters had scribes? Of course they didn't. Someone had to write down what the eaters carried, so that when they died the names didn't die with them. Their script sits on the line like that. Like it's carrying weight."

"I'm not—"

"No," she agreed. "You're not. But you're descended from someone who was, or someone who learned from one, and blood keeps habits the way rivers keep beds." She turned her thin face up to him. "And there's this. Your mother left something here."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once.

"She — my mother was—"

"Executed for heresy when you were eleven. Yes. We know. We keep records of the guild's executions — the real ones, the ones the guild burns its own copies of. Your mother's name is in our book. Or it was. We struck it out, the way we do, to keep it safe from them. But before she was taken, she came here. You'd have been six. She stayed nine days. She was storm-touched — you have her hands and her weather — and she was frightened in a way I have only ever seen in people who have read something they were not meant to read." The abbess's voice was dry and even, but her eyes were not. "She left a sealed record with us. She said: *if my child ever comes up this road, give it to them. If they never come, burn it unread.*"

"You've kept it nine—"

"Twenty-two years," the abbess said. "We keep things. That's the whole of our order, in the end. We keep what others cannot afford to keep, and we erase the keeping."

Coral's heart was doing something arrhythmic and adolescent, something it had not done since he was a boy pressing his ear to his mother's door to hear her sing. *The sound of her voice.* He had sold that memory — no. He had not sold it yet. That was ahead of him, in a market he did not yet know existed. But the wanting of it was already in him, old and bottomless, and it rose now like floodwater.

"Give it to me," he said, and heard his own voice shake, and did not care.

* * *

She brought it to him in a small stone room with a single candle, because she said some records should only be read by one light, so that the reader cannot pretend the whole world is watching and therefore cannot perform. The record was a case of black leather gone soft as cloth, tied with a cord, and the seal was a drop of grey wax the size of a coin, and pressed into the wax was a sigil.

Coral knew it the way you know your own face in a window at night — with a lurch, with a half-second's delay, the delayed reflection of himself. A storm-cloud, quartered by a lightning stroke, and beneath the cloud a small open hand.

"That's—"

"Your family's mark," the abbess said. "The Kavanagh storm-hand. We struck it from every record we keep, the year she died. It's why I knew, at supper. You press your thumb there when you're thinking." She nodded at his hand, where his thumb was indeed pressed into the heel of his palm, into the exact center of the sigil's ghost. "She did the same."

His eyes were burning. He was nine years old in a house that smelled of rain and ink, watching his mother seal a letter with grey wax, watching her press her thumb beside the stamp to make the impression her own.

"Read it," the abbess said. "Or don't. But whatever you decide, decide it in this room, tonight, because the wolf tells me the guild's hounds crossed the ford at Vail two days ago, and this mountain does not have many roads."

He broke the seal.

The wax cracked with a sound like a distant, very small thunderclap, and Coral would swear afterward — to Ashfall, later, in the dark, in the voice he used for things that frightened him — that the candle flame leaned toward it, the way a flame leans toward a draft, or toward a mouth about to speak.

Inside were pages in his mother's hand. He knew it instantly, the way he had known the sigil — the letters that sat on the line carrying weight. It was not one document but a sheaf: a confession, a copy of a copy of a guild charter so old the language was almost another tongue, and diagrams — ward-diagrams, the great ring of the border wards rendered in fine lines, and annotations in three hands, as though the document had been argued over by three people across years.

He read. The candle burned. The abbess stood by the door with her hands folded, and did not hurry him.

His mother's confession was short. He read it four times, and each time the words rearranged themselves into something worse.

*I helped build the ninth ring,* she had written. *I was young and the guild told us we were mending a fence against wolves. Then I was shown what sleeps beneath the ward-heart, and I understood that we were not mending a fence. We were turning a key that another hand had already cut. The wards were not made to keep the old gods out of the world. They were made to keep them under, and they were not made by the guild at all. The guild found them. The guild's founder — the First Register, whose name we are all made to swear by, whose face is on every chapter house — found the memory-eaters' work and did not break it and did not keep it. He *turned it*. He took a lock built for mercy and made it a prison, because the sleeping gods dream, and their dreams can be harvested, and a man who harvests the dreams of gods does not age, does not weaken, does not die.*

*I have seen the index. I have seen my own name in it. I have seen what he means to make of the ninth ring's renewal, and I have refused, and I write this knowing what refusal costs, so that my child, if my child ever reads it, will know that I was not a heretic. I was the last honest clerk the guild ever had.*

The page blurred. Coral found that his hand had gone to his mouth, that he was pressing his knuckles against his lips as though holding words in, and that he was shaking, and that the candle flame had gone very still, very straight, like a soldier at attention.

*The founder,* he thought. *The First Register. The name every clerk swears by at induction, the face in every chapter house, the man whose statutes Coral had spent nine years serving, filing, obeying — the man whose ledgers had been used to fell the wards, whose entries Coral had recorded three days before the stars went out.*

The abbess had come closer without him hearing. She was reading over his shoulder — or she had already read it, years ago, and was reading his face instead. He heard her breath change.

"Read the last page," she said. "The charter. The old language. Tell me what the seal-line says."

He turned to it. The charter was the memory-eaters' — he understood that now, understood the script that sat on the line carrying weight, understood that his mother had copied it out with a copyist's perfect care. And at its foot, where the founder's mark had been struck — struck out, crossed with the single horizontal line, the order's mark, the mark on the trees, the mark on the abbess's collar — the old language named him.

Coral read it aloud, because some things must be said in the air to be believed.

"*Here fails the trust of Aldous Vane, First Register, who took the sleepers' keep and made of it a theft, and whose name is struck from every honest book until the wards fall and the debt is paid.*"

The silence afterward was total. The mountain itself seemed to lean in.

The abbess had gone pale. Not the pale of age — Coral had seen age's pallor all evening, thin and papery and untroubled. This was the pale of a woman who has kept a thing for twenty-two years believing she knew its weight, and has just learned the scale was lying. Her hand found the edge of the table. Her lips moved around the name without voicing it — *Aldous Vane* — a name the guild made its clerks swear by with candles lit and hands on the register, a name that meant law, meant order, meant the world holding still.

"He's alive," Coral heard himself say. His voice sounded like someone else's — like his mother's, maybe, or like the storm's. "The confession says it. He doesn't age. He harvests their dreams. He's been alive since the wards were turned, and three days ago someone used my ledgers to start pulling them down, and I have been asking the wrong question. I've been asking *who betrayed the wards*." He looked up at the abbess, and the candlelight, and the small stone room that had felt like shelter an hour ago and now felt like the antechamber of something vast. "The wards weren't betrayed. They were *betrayed from the beginning*. And the man who did it is still sitting at the top of the guild, still dreaming his stolen dreams, and my mother died for writing it down."

The abbess sat down slowly, the way old trees fall — with enormous patience, and all at once.

"Then you can't burn it," she said. "And you can't stay." Her pale eyes lifted to his, and in them was something that looked, unbearably, like hope — the fragile, furious hope of a woman who had spent her life erasing names and had just been handed one worth writing. "You have to carry it down the mountain. To the ward-heart. To *him*."

Outside, far below in the drowned country, thunder muttered — Coral's storm, or its ghost, still walking the lowlands without him. And on the shelf above the valley, in the guest house, a wolf lifted his head from the floor he filled, and listened, and waited for the man he had sworn to follow to come and tell him what his mother had died to say.
