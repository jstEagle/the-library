## 10. Sable Closes the Net

The rain had followed Sable Ives for three days, and she had begun to think of it as a companion.

It was not a friendly one. It came down in grey sheets over the drowned country, filling the wheel-ruts until the road north became a river pretending to be a road, and it soaked through her oilskin in places she'd sworn were sealed. But it kept her company the way guilt keeps company: always there, always whispering, never letting her forget what she was doing out here.

Tracking Coral Blackwood.

*Retrieving* him, the Guildmaster's letter had said. *Retrieve quietly, or bury quieter.* She had read those words a dozen times by firelight that refused to stay lit, and each reading they sounded less like an order and more like an apology written in advance. Retrieve. Bury. As if the two were neighboring rooms in the same house, and a man could be walked from one to the other without anyone hearing the door.

She knew the boy he'd been. That was the trouble.

Coral — Dominic, though no one had called him that in nine years — had been fourteen when she was seventeen, both of them novices in the low halls of the Vesper Guild, learning ward-script and ledger-keeping and the hundred small obediences that made a guildsman. He had laughed then. She tried to remember the sound of it now and found she could not; nine years of exile will sand a laugh down to nothing. What she remembered instead was his hands, ink-stained even at fourteen, and the way he'd once spent an entire winter copying out the old ward-laws by candlelight because he said a person ought to know what they were obeying before they obeyed it.

He had asked too many questions, even then. She had warned him about it. She had thought she was protecting him.

Now she rode north through the drowned country with six enforcers at her back and a sealed writ against her oldest friend, and the rain whispered to her all day long: *you are the hound, Sable. You have always been the hound.*

They made camp on the second night in the shell of a drover's barn, its roof half fallen, its beams black with old smoke. Her enforcers — Vess, Ondry, the twins Pell and Pella, quiet Maren, and young Hask who still believed the guild was a temple — bedded down around a fire small enough to hide. Sable took first watch, which was also last watch, because she did not sleep well anymore.

It was Hask who couldn't leave it alone. He sat up across the embers, wet hair plastered to his forehead, and said, "Warden. The clerk. Is it true what they're saying in the hall? That he burned a ledger page?"

"Where did you hear that?"

"Ondry heard it from the archivist's boy. They say the night the stars went out, someone used the trade-ledgers to fell the border wards. And that Blackwood processed the sale." Hask's voice dropped, reverent and frightened at once. "Three days before. He wrote it down himself."

Sable fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Ledgers don't fell wards," she said. "Wards fall because wards fail. Everything ends. Even the guild teaches that."

"Not these wards." Hask was young enough to be earnest without embarrassment. "My grandmother used to say the border wards were older than the guild. Older than the roads. She said they weren't built — they were *grown*, like teeth out of the jaw of the world, and the guild only tended them." He hugged his knees. "So how does a clerk's ledger bring down something grown?"

"I don't know," Sable said, and it was the truest thing she'd said in weeks.

Because here was what gnawed at her, what had ridden behind her all three days like a second rider: the writ had come too fast. The stars went out on the third night of the month. By dawn of the fourth, the writ was on her desk — sealed, signed, urgent, *quietly, Ives, or bury him*. As if the Guildmaster had been waiting for the wards to fall. As if he had known, somehow, whose name would need burying before anyone else in the guild had finished counting their own dead fears.

Orders that arrive before questions do are not orders. They are answers someone wrote in advance.

She turned that thought over all night, and by morning it had hardened into something she could carry: *I will find him first. And then I will decide.*

* * *

They found the burned room on the fourth day, in the clerks' lodging off Tallow Street, and it told Sable more than any interrogation could have.

The guild's own investigators had already been through it — clumsily, she noted, and recently, their chalk-marks still fresh on the doorframe. But beneath their trampling, the room spoke plainly to anyone who had known its occupant. The bed was made. The single cup was washed and set upside down to dry. His spare cloak was gone, but his good boots were gone too, and his winter coat, and — this was the detail that stopped her — the little tin of lemon drops he'd kept on the sill since he was fourteen, because sugar helped him think, he'd claimed, and no one had ever managed to prove otherwise.

He hadn't fled in panic. He had packed.

And on the desk, where the ledger should have been, there was nothing but a faint rectangle of clean wood, outlined in dust, and one long curl of ash that the draft had pushed into the corner. Someone had burned a page and swept the rest away. Careful. Thorough. Ashamed, maybe.

But it was the window that undid her.

The shutters were closed. Of course they were — a man hiding doesn't leave windows open. Yet the investigators had marked soot on the sill, and when Sable leaned close she saw why: the soot wasn't random. It was scored, deliberately, with something fine — a stylus point, maybe — into a pattern she recognized from her novice years, from the old ward-law copies Coral had spent that winter transcribing.

A sign, in the old script. Small as a coin. Deliberate as a signature.

*Watched*, it meant. In the old tongue of the ward-builders, scratched into the soot of his own window: *watched*.

Someone had seen him burn that page. And Coral Blackwood, invisible for nine years, careful as frost, had wanted whoever came looking next to know it.

Sable stood in that small bare room a long time, rain dripping off her oilskin onto floorboards some other clerk would inherit, and felt the ground shift under everything she'd trusted. Because the guild's story was simple: a corrupt clerk, a stolen ledger, a traitor who felled the wards. Simple stories are what enforcers are for. But the room didn't tell a simple story. The room told a story about a careful man who washed his cup and made his bed and left a warning in a dead language for whoever loved him enough to read it.

He had known she would come. He had written to *her*.

Or he had written to whoever came, and she had simply decided it was her. Either way, her hands were shaking when she left the room, and she blamed the cold.

* * *

The trail led north, as she'd known it would. Not by hoofprints — the rain ate those within hours — but by smaller testimony. A ferryman at the Greywater crossing remembered a tall clerk and, squinting, "a dog, big grey thing, off a leash or no leash, hard to say." A charcoal-burner two days further swore he'd seen a wolf the size of a yearling ox moving through the alder brakes at dusk, and had thrown salt after it and hidden in his kiln till morning. At a flooded chapel used as a waystation, a sleeping pilgrim reported being watched all night by "eyes like lanterns in a dark church," and had pressed his face to the floor and prayed till dawn.

Wolf stories. Memory-eater stories, if you knew the old words for them, and Sable had copied enough ward-laws in her youth to know them. The order had exterminated the memory-eaters generations ago — every novice learned it as a triumph, the guild's great cleansing, the removal of creatures who could eat a man's past out of his head and leave him a stranger to himself. She had never questioned the triumph. Triumphs were not for questioning.

But now a memory-eater was walking north beside Coral Blackwood, and the guild's writ said *retrieve or bury*, and none of it fit together the way simple stories are supposed to fit.

On the sixth day the rain finally broke, and the sky came back in patches of bruised blue, starless even at noon — the sun itself dimmed, as if the whole vault of heaven had been scrubbed of its lights and only the dull workaday ones left burning. Her enforcers grew quiet under that sky. Hask stopped asking questions. Even Ondry, who joked at funerals, rode with his jaw set.

That evening they reached the edge of the high moor, and below them, in a fold of the hills where two streams met, lay the abbey.

Sable knew of it only from guild maps, where it appeared as a small annotation: *Abbey of Unwritten Names — heretical order — tolerated, unregistered.* Tolerated. The word had always struck her as odd. The guild did not tolerate things; it licensed them, taxed them, or burned them. Tolerance was what you extended to something you feared to touch.

Now, looking down at it in the failing light, she understood the fear.

The abbey sat in its valley like a held breath. Low stone buildings, a bell-tower with no bell, gardens terraced up the hillside in patterns that hurt to look at directly — rows that seemed to rearrange themselves when she wasn't watching, as if the garden were keeping its own records and did not wish to be read. Smoke rose thin and domestic from the kitchen chimney. Somewhere below, faintly, people were singing vespers, and the song had the strange quality of sounding familiar and untranslatable at once, like a lullaby from a country she'd never visited.

"There," said Vess beside her. "Light in the guest-house. Two figures at the gate, maybe three. One's big. Could be our wolf."

Sable raised her hand for stillness and studied the valley, and the longer she studied it, the more wrong it looked. No sentries on the walls. No wards visible along the perimeter — and an abbey of heretics, unregistered and tolerated, should have been crawling with wards. Unless the wards were there and simply beyond her seeing, which was worse. Unless the abbey protected itself in ways the guild had never taught her.

"We go down at first light," she said. "Quiet approach. Vess, Ondry with me. The rest hold the ridge. If we're not back by midday—"

"If we're not back by midday," Ondry repeated, "we come get you. That's the arrangement, Warden."

"That's the arrangement," she agreed, and did not tell him that some part of her hoped they wouldn't.

She slept badly, and woke before dawn to a smell she knew from her worst years in the field.

Smoke. Not hearth-smoke. Burning-stone smoke, hot and mineral, the smell of a building giving up its insides.

She was on her feet before she'd finished waking. Below in the valley, where vespers had sung the night before, the abbey was burning.

* * *

Fire is honest in a way people are not. It shows you exactly what a building was made of, and exactly how much anyone inside was worth to the people who lit it.

Sable went down the hill at a run with all six enforcers behind her, discipline abandoned, because whatever else this was, it was not the quiet retrieval she'd been sent to perform. The guest-house was fully involved, flame standing in its windows like orange curtains. The bell-tower burned from within, and as they crossed the outer wall — no resistance, no wards, nothing — a section of the tower's crown gave way and slid into the courtyard in a landslide of glowing stone.

"Bodies," Maren said, and her voice had gone flat, the flatness enforcers use to keep working. "Warden. Bodies everywhere."

There were. Laid in the courtyard, laid in the cloister walk, laid in the garden rows among the vegetables that rearranged themselves — brothers and sisters of the abbey in their grey robes, and not one of them burned. That was the detail Sable's mind snagged on and could not release. The buildings burned; the people did not. Every body she checked bore wounds of steel: thrusts, cuts, the efficient work of trained killers who had gone row by row through the dormitories in the dark. Then the killers had fired the buildings over the dead and walked away.

Trained killers. Guild-trained. She knew the strokes because she taught them.

"Warden." Ondry crouched by the well-head, and his voice had lost all its jokes. "You want to see this."

By the well, arranged with a deliberateness that made her stomach turn, someone had displayed a sigil: seven stones set in a pattern on the well-slab, and driven through the center stone, a knife of guild make — she knew the forge-mark, Third Hall, enforcer issue — pinning a strip of parchment. On the parchment, in a hand too neat for the work it announced:

*The unwritten are unwritten. Let no record stand.*

Heretical slayers' cant. Except the guild had stamped out the heretic-hunting orders two centuries ago, and Sable had personally archived their last warrant herself, in her second year as Warden, and the seal on that warrant had been—

The same seal that was on her own writ.

She stood very still in the burning courtyard while the tower groaned above her, and let the arithmetic finish itself.

The writ against Coral had come too fast. The investigators in his room had been clumsy — guild men doing a job they hadn't been given time to do properly, or hadn't been meant to do properly, a performance of investigation for anyone watching. The trail north had been easy to follow, easier than any trail she'd ever tracked, as if someone wanted the hounds running in the right direction. And now an entire abbey of harmless heretics — the one place in the world that erased names to protect the named, the one order that might have understood what Coral carried — slaughtered in a single night, with a guild knife left as a signature, and a message in a dead cant to make it look like zealotry.

Zealots don't leave receipts. Zealots believe. Whoever did this wanted the killing *read*, not believed — wanted it attributed, wanted the guild's hand printed on it in letters any investigator could find.

Including her. Especially her. She was the investigator they'd counted on finding it.

"You're thinking it," Vess said quietly, coming to stand at her shoulder. Vess had served under her eleven years and had earned the right to say such things. "Say it anyway."

"The writ came before the questions," Sable said. "The trail was baited. This—" she nodded at the well, at the knife, at the neat handwriting, "—this is theater. Someone inside the guild killed these people and dressed it as us. Or dressed it as the thing we're supposed to hate enough not to examine." She looked around at the burning valley, at her enforcers' ash-grey faces. "We were never sent to retrieve a clerk. We were sent to be *seen* retrieving a clerk. We're the proof. We're the signature walking."

Hask had gone white. "Then the writ—"

"Was signed by the Second Seal," Sable said. "Which means either the Guildmaster ordered a massacre of unarmed heretics and framed his own enforcers for it—" she paused, and made herself finish it, "—or someone forged the Guildmaster's seal, and the Guildmaster doesn't know what's being done in his name. Either way, the hand that signed my writ burned this abbey. And that hand wants Coral Blackwood dead, and wants me holding the knife when it happens."

The tower collapsed then, fully, with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, and the shockwave of heat rolled across the courtyard and bent the flames sideways, and no one flinched, because they had all just learned that the fire was the least of what was falling.

"What do we do?" Pella asked. Her voice was very small.

Sable looked north, where the trail climbed out of the far side of the valley toward the mountains — Coral's trail, plain as print to her eyes now, because she finally understood that it had been left for her, and that following it might be the only honest act left available to her. And she looked south, back the way they'd come, toward the guild hall and the Second Seal and the warm lie she had lived inside for twenty years.

"We do our jobs," she said. "Properly. For the first time in a long time." She turned to Ondry. "Take the twins and Maren. Start a death-roll of everyone in this abbey — names, ages, anything the survivors or neighbors can give us. The guild buried these people twice: once with steel, once by making them unregistered. We're going to write them back in. Every name. Understood?"

"Understood," Ondry said, and something in his face had changed, the joke-ground down to bedrock.

"Hask. Ride south. Not to the guild hall — to Archivist Bren, the old one, third floor, the one they retired but never replaced. Tell him the Second Seal moved against an unregistered house and ask him to pull every warrant ever issued under that seal. Quietly. If anyone asks, he's checking archive integrity."

"And you, Warden?" Vess asked.

Sable pulled off one gauntlet and touched the scorched well-slab, where the knife still stood pinning its neat, lying note. Then she pulled the knife free, wrapped it in cloth, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where evidence belongs.

"I'm going to lose the trail," she said. "Loudly. Where witnesses can see me losing it. And then I'm going to follow it properly, alone, north, until I find Coral Blackwood and ask him one question." She looked up at the starless noon sky, dim as a covered lamp. "Whether he knows who's using his ledgers. Because I'm starting to think it isn't him. And I'm starting to think the people who wrote my writ have known that all along — and that the wards didn't fall the night the stars went out."

She mounted, and turned her horse north.

"They fell the day someone decided the gods should never wake. And I've been hunting the wrong man ever since."

Behind her the abbey smoked like a burnt offering, and ahead the mountains waited under their shroud of missing stars, and Sable Ives rode toward the one conversation she had avoided for twenty years: the truth about the boy she'd failed, the guild she'd served, and the hand that had signed her name into its service without ever asking her leave.
