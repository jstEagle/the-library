## 11. The Traitor's Market

The entrance to the Undermarket was a well.

That was all it looked like at first — a crumbling stone mouth in the side of a flooded hill, half-swallowed by reeds, with a rope ladder descending into darkness that smelled of wet iron and old smoke. Coral stood at its edge while the drowned country dripped around them and felt the particular dread of a man about to sell something he could not buy back.

"You're certain there's no other way," he said. It was not really a question. He had asked it four times since dawn, and each time Ashfall had answered the same way, and each time Coral had hoped the answer might change, the way a gambler hopes the dice will remember him fondly.

"The prison road runs under the mountains," Ashfall said. The wolf sat on his haunches at the lip of the well, grey as river-fog, his eyes catching what little light remained. "The only door that opens without guild blood is through the Undermarket. And the Undermarket opens for coin, or for trade." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "We have no coin."

"We have my storm-calling."

"Which you cannot use here without bringing every enforcer in three counties down on us, and which they would take from you anyway, painfully, before you finished screaming." Ashfall's voice was low and even, the voice of someone stating weather. "You have your ledgers' knowledge. Worthless here — everyone below already knows everything worth knowing. You have your name, which is worth nothing because no one uses it. And you have—" the wolf's gaze slid to him, steady and terrible, "—your memories."

Coral looked down into the dark. Somewhere far below, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, music was playing. A fiddle, maybe, or something older. The Undermarket had been trading since before the guild had a name to put on its charters; it dealt in the things that could not be weighed or fenced honestly anywhere else — years of life, first kisses, the taste of a dead man's cooking, grief measured out in careful spoonfuls. He had processed entries from it once, early in his clerkship, and had assumed the descriptions were metaphor. *One (1) lullaby, intact, provenance verified.* He had thought it poetry.

He knew better now.

"There has to be something else I can give them," he said.

"There is not. Not if we want passage through the Deep Gallery rather than over it. Overland is nine days of open ground, and Sable Ives is two days behind us and closing." Ashfall rose, shook the marsh water from his coat with a sound like rain on canvas. "You asked me once why I need you alive. Here is why you need me: I can walk into that market and no one will touch me, because everything down there fears me more than it wants me. But I cannot buy passage. Memory-eaters do not trade in memory. We *are* memory. Taking our own would be—" he searched for the word, found it, seemed displeased with it, "—cannibalism."

"And mine?"

"Yours are fair game." The wolf said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "The traders will know what you carry the moment you descend. They always know. The question is not whether they'll want it. The question is what you're willing to let go."

* * *

The ladder went down farther than any well had a right to. Coral counted the rungs out of habit — clerk's reflexes died hard — and lost count somewhere past two hundred, when the air stopped smelling of marsh and started smelling of tallow and cardamom and, beneath it, something sweeter and more wrong, like fruit left too long in a closed room. The walls changed from stone to something smoother, veined with pale light that pulsed very slowly, and Coral realized with a lurch that the light was breathing, that the whole shaft was the throat of some enormous sleeping thing the market had built itself inside, or perhaps *was*, and that no one below would think this worth mentioning.

Ashfall descended behind him without touching the rungs. Coral had stopped asking how.

The bottom opened into a cavern the size of a cathedral's dream — vaulted dark overhead lost in hanging lanterns of colored glass, and below, terraces of stalls spilling down the curve of the cavern like the rings of an inverted city. Every stall glowed differently. One burned candle-white and clean; one smoldered ember-red; one gave off a light like moonlight through dirty water, and Coral's skin crawled when he looked at it too long. The crowd moved between them in murmuring streams: hooded figures, figures with no hoods but faces that didn't hold still, a woman whose shadow walked half a step ahead of her and kept checking its purse. Music drifted from somewhere unseen, the same tune he'd heard from above, worn soft as a river stone by centuries of playing.

No one looked at them. That was the courtesy of the Undermarket — eyes down, business private — until, in ones and twos, heads began to turn. Not toward Coral.

Toward Ashfall.

It started as a stillness, the way a field goes still when a hawk crosses it. A spice-seller set down her mortar. Two men mid-argument over a jar of bottled laughter simply stopped, mouths open, the argument dying unspoken between them. A child — a child, down here, which told Coral something about how long people lived in these depths — grabbed her mother's sleeve and pointed, and the mother caught the small hand and folded it shut and pulled her away fast, walking backward, not turning her back on the wolf.

"They know me," Ashfall said quietly. There was nothing of pride in it. It sounded like a man reporting an old wound aching before rain.

"What do they know?"

"That I am the last. That my people are gone. And that a memory-eater's pelt, rendered properly, sells for more than this entire market earns in a lifetime." His ears flicked back. "Keep walking. Stay close. If anyone offers to shake your hand, refuse. If anyone compliments your mother, lie."

"I don't remember my mother," Coral began, and then stopped, because that wasn't true, and the falseness of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He remembered her. That was the problem. Nine years of exile and he remembered her the way you remember a house you grew up in — constantly, completely, and mostly without noticing, the way you stop hearing a clock. Her voice was in everything. It was in the cadence he used when he read ledgers aloud to check sums. It was in the way he said *careful* when handing someone anything breakable. It was a low, warm, slightly rough voice, a voice that had sung him through fevers and laughed at his father's jokes a half-beat late because she'd heard them all before, and it was the last thing the guild had let him keep, because they hadn't known it mattered, because no one had ever thought to confiscate a sound.

They were halfway across the main terrace when the trader found them.

She did not approach so much as simply be present one moment when she had not been the moment before — a broad woman in layered shawls the color of deep water, her face kind and her eyes old in a way that had nothing to do with years. Around her neck, where another woman might wear beads, hung small glass vials on silver wire, each holding a curl of light that moved.

"Clerk," she said, and Coral's stomach dropped, because she said it the way you name a bird by its song. "Guild-trained. Exiled. Carrying a ledger-head full of other people's transactions and one memory you polish every night like a stone." She smiled, and it was not unkind, which was the most frightening thing yet. "You've been keeping it safe for someone. That's rare. Most people down here are running *from* their memories. You've been running *for* one."

"I'm not selling," Coral said.

"Everyone who says that is selling," the trader said. "The only question is price." She turned her head, unhurried, and looked at Ashfall full-on, and Coral braced for fear or greed, but what crossed her face was neither. It was grief. Old, banked, familiar grief, the kind you build a life around. "You're Vaelin's get," she said to the wolf. "I'd know that silence anywhere. Your mother traded with my grandmother, before the purges. She sold the sound of the ward-song so the guild's founders couldn't hum it back to themselves in the dark." Her hand rose toward the vials at her throat, an unconscious gesture, protective. "They never did find that memory again. Your people were thorough."

For a long moment Ashfall said nothing. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, rougher, the composure cracked just enough to show the thing underneath. "You knew my mother's work."

"I knew her *price list*," the trader said. "Every memory-eater priced their own deaths, in a way. Sold pieces of themselves until there weren't enough pieces left to run with." She looked back at Coral, and now her kindness had an edge on it, honed by whatever she was remembering. "So. Clerk. You want the Deep Gallery pass. Two seals, wolf and man, safe conduct past the toll-wards. I can write it. The price is one memory, freely given, fully surrendered — none of this borrowed-grief nonsense, no copies, no echoes. It goes in the vial, it stays in the vial, and you will not miss it gradually. You will miss it *entirely*, all at once, forever, the moment the cork goes in."

"Name your target," Ashfall growled, stepping half in front of Coral, which was absurd, a wolf shielding a man, and which Coral would examine later, much later, when he had leisure to be undone by it.

The trader's eyes stayed on Coral. "I don't name targets. The market doesn't work that way. The seller chooses what to lose, or the deal rots." She spread her hands. "But I'll tell you what I see, since you're both standing there pretending otherwise. I see a man carrying one memory wrapped in oilcloth at the center of himself, kept apart from all the rest, touched every night, never spent. I see a memory that isn't his to spend, in the sense that it belongs to whoever gave it — but it lives in him, and the market trades in what lives in you, not in what's fair." Her voice gentled. "And I see that if he doesn't pay it, the wolf walks out of here alone, and the man walks out of here into Warden Ives's hands, and the wards stay up another thousand years. So. Choose, clerk. The market is patient, but it isn't *that* patient."

* * *

They stepped aside, into an alcove curtained with dyed leather, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The music played on. Somewhere a trader was auctioning a decade, going once, going twice, and the crowd's murmur rose and fell like tide.

"No," Ashfall said.

"You don't get to decide that."

"I am deciding it anyway." The wolf's hackles were up, though there was nothing in the alcove to fight. "There must be another memory. Something smaller. Something you won't—"

"It has to be worth two Deep Gallery seals." Coral heard his own voice come out flat and clerkish, pricing things, and hated it, and kept going, because the alternative was to stop. "A small memory buys a small passage. You said it yourself. Overland is nine days and Sable is two days back. There's no arithmetic that saves it."

"Then I'll carry it."

Coral blinked. "What?"

"Give it to *me*." Ashfall's eyes had gone bright, fever-bright, the composure entirely gone now. "That is what my people *did*. We carried what others could not afford to hold. Griefs, names, the voices of the dead — mothers handed us their children's memories when plague came, lovers traded heartbreak back and forth so neither broke alone. I am the last of the line that was built for exactly this. Give me your mother's voice and I will hold it for you, whole, untouched, and when this is over — when the gods are free and the guild is ash — I will give it back. Nothing leaves you. Nothing is lost. The trader gets her vial filled with—" he faltered, grasping, "—with something else. Anything else. I'll find something. I'll—"

"Ashfall."

"—there are memories in my own head I'd sooner be rid of, I could sell those, I could—"

"*Ashfall.*"

The wolf stopped. In the lantern-light his breath made small ghosts in the cold air, and Coral understood, suddenly and completely, that this was not strategy. This was the memory-eater's oldest instinct, the whole purpose of his slaughtered people, reaching out through him toward the one thing he could still do for someone. And underneath that — Coral saw this too, and wished he didn't — was fear. Plain, animal fear, for Coral, wearing the borrowed shape of logistics.

"If you carry it," Coral said quietly, "then it's yours. Not held. Yours. That's how your gift works — you told me, the night you told me what you are. What a memory-eater holds, he keeps. There's no giving back. There's no *returning*. You'd be taking my mother's voice into yourself forever, and every time you thought of her, you'd be grieving a woman you never met, for a debt that was never yours." He shook his head. "I've watched you carry your people's deaths for — however long you've carried them. I'm not adding to that load. Especially not this."

"She would want—"

"You don't know what she'd want." It came out harder than he meant, and he didn't soften it. "Neither do I. That's the point. That's the *whole* point. I'm the only one left who gets to decide what her voice is worth, and I decide it's worth exactly this: my way north, your life, and the wards coming down." He exhaled, slow. "Besides. You said it yourself, back on the road. Memory made flesh is the only honest currency. Everything else is paper."

"That was me being poetic."

"You're very bad at it. It was very moving."

Something that was almost a laugh went through the wolf, brief and broken, and then he lowered his great head, and for a moment he pressed his forehead against Coral's shoulder — the way his people must once have done, Coral thought, sealing bargains, sharing burdens, saying *I am here, I witness this* — and the contact was warm and heavy and lasted exactly three seconds, and Coral knew he would remember those three seconds longer than almost anything else he owned.

"Then choose quickly," Ashfall said, drawing back. "And afterward, if you forget why you're sad—" his voice caught, steadied, "—I'll remind you that you are. That much I can do without stealing anything."

* * *

The trader had uncorked the vial before Coral reached her, holding it ready, and the little glass throat breathed a light like dusk through honey. Around them the market had gone quiet in a widening ring — not silent, the Undermarket was never silent, but *attentive*, hundreds of transactions pausing to watch, because everyone below knew what a freely given memory looked like, and it was rarer than gold and sadder than funerals.

"Say it aloud," the trader said softly. "The market requires consent spoken. Name what you sell."

Coral closed his eyes.

He gathered it carefully, the way he'd gather a lamp on a windy night — cupping both hands around the flame of it. His mother's voice. All of it. The singing, the scolding, the *careful*, the laugh that came late, the way she said his childhood name — his real name, the one no one used anymore, the one that lived nowhere else in the world except inside that sound. He gathered it up out of nine years of keeping, and it came willingly, eagerly, the way a dog comes when called after a long day alone, and that eagerness nearly broke him.

"One memory," Coral said, and his voice did not shake, which he would always consider his single greatest professional achievement. "Freely given. Fully surrendered. The sound of my mother's voice, entire, as I hold it. For two Deep Gallery seals, wolf and man, safe conduct to the prison road."

"Witnessed," murmured the crowd, in a hundred voices, like wind through wheat.

He leaned forward. The vial's mouth was cool against his lips, absurdly, terribly intimate, and for one last instant the voice rose in him — *Dominic, love, mind the step* — and he let it go.

It went out of him the way a star goes out. No pain. Just a shape in the world becoming a shape that had never been there, and the sudden terrible quiet where a warmth had been, and his hands — he watched them distantly — pressed flat against his own chest as if to check whether anything was still inside.

The cork went in. The vial filled with a soft golden light that curled, and curled, and settled.

He could not remember what he had sold. That was the horror of it — not absence but *smoothness*, a stretch of himself polished blank, like a page torn cleanly from a ledger, the binding unharmed, the neighbors' entries intact. He knew he had loved her. He knew she had died. He knew, because Ashfall had told him an hour ago, that he had kept her voice like a stone, polishing it nightly. But the stone was gone, and worse — the *polishing* was gone, the ritual itself, and he stood in the ringing quiet of the Undermarket trying to grieve and finding that grief needs a handle, and his had been sold.

"Dominic."

Ashfall's voice. Low, at his elbow, using the name for the first time — the name the wolf had spoken on the road the night they met, the true name, and Coral seized on it like a drowning man seizing driftwood, because it was *his*, it was still his, it had survived the sale.

"You're sad," Ashfall said quietly. "You don't know why. But you are. I promised I'd tell you."

"Yes," Coral whispered. "Yes. Thank you."

The trader pressed two wax seals into his palm — black, stamped with a spiral, warm as living skin — and closed his fingers over them, and her old eyes were wet, and she said nothing, because there was nothing down here that could be said that wasn't also for sale.

They walked toward the Deep Gallery gate together, the wolf and the hollowed man, and the crowd parted before them, and behind them, faint and fading, the market's fiddle took up a new tune — slow, minor, mournful — and Coral did not know why it made him want to weep.

At the gate, as the spiral-sealed lock drank his blood and groaned open onto a corridor of breathing stone, Ashfall spoke once, very low, meant for him alone:

"The founder's men passed through here three days ago. Twelve of them. They bought something at the memory-stalls." The wolf's eyes reflected the gate-light, flat and cold. "They bought *directions to your mother's grave* — and they paid for them with a guild signet I recognized." A pause. "Coral. Someone in the guild knows who your mother was. And they're already ahead of us."
