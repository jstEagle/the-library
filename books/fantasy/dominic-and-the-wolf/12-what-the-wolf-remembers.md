## 12. What the Wolf Remembers

Memory is not a thing I keep. It is a thing that keeps me.

I say this so you understand what it costs to open the storehouse willingly — to walk into the vault where I carry the last of my people and take down a bundle from the shelf and unroll it in front of another living creature. Coral had asked nothing of it. He never did, not directly. He only lay awake on the far side of the dead fire in the shepherd's hut we had found in the high passes, his breath too even to be honest, and after an hour of pretending he said, quietly, into the dark:

"You don't have to tell me. But I can tell you're carrying something heavier than the pact."

And that was the trouble with the clerk. He noticed things. He had spent nine years making himself invisible, and the paradox of invisibility is that it teaches a person to see.

I lay with my chin on my forepaws and watched the embers breathe their last, and I decided — or perhaps the decision had been made days ago, on the drowned road, when he called a storm out of a sky that should have refused him and nearly died for it — that he would hear it from me before he heard it twisted from someone else's mouth.

"Then put more wood on the fire," I said. "This is a long story, and I will only tell it once."

* * *

My people were called the Vharn, though the word means less than you would think — it is simply *those who remember*, in the old tongue, the way *river* means *the water that goes*. We were not wolves originally. That came later, when the world grew colder and four legs kept warmth better than two. Before that we were something else, something that walked upright through the first forests of a young world and carried its memories the way a shell carries the sound of the sea.

Every people keeps its past somehow. Songs. Stones. Books, in your guild's case — and look how faithfully books keep things. But memory is not storage. Memory is flesh. A memory that lives only in ink is a corpse in a jar. It must live in a body, be tasted, be grieved over, be *known*, or it fades into mere fact. We were the bodies the world's remembering lived in.

"There were thousands of us once," I told Coral. The fire had come back up between us, throwing its small defiant heat against the hut's stone walls. "Not a nation, exactly. Nothing so tidy. We moved along the mountain spines like weather, and villages would bring their dying elders to us, and we would taste the elders' lives and carry them forward so that no one was ever entirely gone."

Coral sat cross-legged, his ledger — the new one, the one I had demanded as the price of his protection, though I confess now that demand was armor over a plea — resting closed on his knees, his hands flat on top of it as if it might bolt.

"And the gods?" he asked. Of course he asked about the gods. He had earned the question; he was walking toward their broken prison with me at his heel.

"The gods are old," I said. "Older than hunger, older than dark. And they do not sleep the way living things sleep. They *dream* the world. Every river is a thought they had. Every storm—" I tipped my muzzle toward him. "—is one of their moods, leaking through. Your storms, clerk. You have been calling pieces of a god's temper since you were a child, and no one ever told you."

He went very still. I let him sit with it.

"A thousand years ago the world was dying," I went on. "The gods dreamed too hard. Their griefs and rages bled into the soil, and the soil soured, and the seas rose, and your histories call it the Long Withering, though your histories were written afterward by men who needed it to mean something else. The world could not heal while the gods thrashed in their dreams. So the Vharn were asked to build a cradle."

I felt the shape of the memory rise in me, the oldest one I hold, the one that tastes of cedar smoke and snowmelt and my grandmother's voice saying the binding-words. I let it come up and I let it hurt.

"We built the wards. Not as a prison — hear me, and mark this, because everything your guild has told itself depends on getting this wrong — not as a prison but as a lullaby. Stone and song and sacrifice, woven into a shawl drawn over the dreaming gods' eyes. To keep them asleep *while the world healed*. That was the whole of the covenant. Sleep, and wake when the wounds close. The ward-builders swore to tend the cradle for as long as it took, generation upon generation, and the gods trusted us, because we were the keepers of trust itself. Who else? We who remember everything could not betray anything without betraying every name we carried."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind combed the pass, thin and cold.

"And then?" Coral asked, though his voice said he already suspected. He had read ledgers all his life. He knew how entries could lie by arrangement.

"And then the man who would found your guild was made a warden of the cradle," I said. "Aurelian Vesper. Remember the name. You have walked under it every day of your exile; it is cut above your guildhall door in letters you were taught to be proud of."

Coral's hands tightened on the ledger. "Vesper," he repeated. "The guild takes its—"

"Yes. And here is what your guild's own records will never say, because he burned them, and burned us with them." I looked into the fire, because looking at Coral while I said it would have been more than either of us could carry. "Aurelian discovered what I am about to tell you: that a sleeping god dreams true. Sit within the cradle, at the ward-heart, and the dreams leak, and a mortal soul positioned just right can drink them. Power. Vision. Life stretched past all natural measure. Aurelian drank, and did not stop drinking, and has not stopped in a thousand years. The world healed, Coral. The wounds closed. The time of waking came and went, and came again, and went again, and still the shawl stays drawn, because the man beneath it is fat on god-dreams and cannot bear to leave the table."

"When did your people learn?" His voice was very quiet.

"Four hundred years ago. One of us reached the ward-heart on pilgrimage, as we did each century, to renew the songs. She saw what the cradle had become. She ran home to tell the packs." I breathed slowly, evenly, the way I breathe when the vault inside me wants to burst its door. "Aurelian learned she had seen. And he understood, better than anyone, that a secret is only safe when there is no one alive to carry it. So he did not merely kill the Vharn. He erased us. He hunted us across three generations and burned our name-stones and salted our burial grounds, and he taught the world that memory-eaters were monsters — devourers of children's minds, thieves of souls — until mothers frightened their children with our shape. He wrote us out of history, and history let him, because he held the pen."

I heard Coral swallow.

"I am the last," I said. "The last who carries any of them. Nine hundred names, clerk, give or take. Nine hundred lives folded into this hide, and when I die they die, and then the Vharn will be a rumor that was never true at all. That is what I am. Not a wolf. An ending, walking."

* * *

For a long while neither of us spoke. The fire settled. Snow ticked against the shutters. Coral did not offer comfort, which I counted among his virtues; pity would have curdled in my mouth. Instead he asked the harder question, the clerk's question, the one I had been circling away from all night:

"The ledgers. Why mine? What does a guild clerk's trade-record matter to any of this?"

And here is where I had to decide whether to lie, and understood, lying there with nine hundred ghosts pressing at my ribs, that if I lied to him now I would lose him forever — not to anger, but to the polite distance people keep from those who have used them.

So I told the truth.

"Do you know what your impossible trades actually are?" I said. "Bottled thunder. Borrowed shadows. Keys that open nothing. You cataloged them for nine years and never asked what they were *for*."

"Contraband," he said. "Smuggling. The Undermarket trades in—"

"In dream-matter." I let the words fall one at a time. "Everything the guild's higher houses traffic in — every bottled storm, every shadow sold twice, every key that opens nothing because it opens *elsewhere* — all of it is skimmed from the surface of the sleeping gods' dreams. The guild harvests the cradle, Coral. Has always harvested it. And every harvest, every theft, every drop drawn off passes through a ledger, and every ledger is copied to the archive, and the archive is indexed at the ward-heart. Your neat columns. Your careful hand. You have been bookkeeping for a god-thief for nine years, and your entries are circled in the founder's own ink, because your ledgers are good — too good — and Aurelian uses them to tune the harvest."

His face in the firelight did something I had not seen it do: it went completely empty, the way faces go when the ground opens under them.

"But that isn't why I came to you," I said, and now my voice betrayed me, roughening at the edges, and I let it, because he deserved to hear the crack. "That is why the *pact* was useful. It is not why I found you. Three years ago I caught the scent of a record — a single page, smuggled out of the guild archive by a heretic of the Unwritten Names — a page listing names. Vharn names. My people's names, recorded in a guild census taken during the purging, before the erasure was finished. A list of the dead. Do you understand what that meant to me? Names are not information, Coral. They are *resurrection*. With enough true names, gathered and tasted and spoken in the old order, I could call my people back out of oblivion. Not their bodies. Their reality. Make it true again that they existed."

"So you wanted—" He stopped. Started again. "You wanted a ledger-keeper."

"I wanted a ledger-keeper," I said. "Someone who could read the guild's indexing, follow its cross-references, reconstruct from dry columns of trade-data where every stolen record lived. Someone invisible, meticulous, and desperate enough about the guild's crimes to want them dragged into light. I tracked the scent of that census page for two years and it led me, eventually, to the night the stars went out, and to the clerk whose entry had armed the falling of the wards — the key that opened nothing, Blackwood. Your impossible key. It opened the ward-heart's outer seal. You processed its sale yourself, three days before the sky went dark, and you have known it since the second night and said nothing, and I have known that you knew, and said nothing either." I met his eyes at last. "Yes. I sought you as a tool. I offered protection because your death would have cost me the best pair of eyes in the guild. Everything else — the road, the barbs, the blizzard, the body I put between you and the ice — none of that was in the bargain. None of that was planned. I need you to know the difference, because you are going to have to decide, on the road ahead, which of those two things you believe I am."

* * *

Coral did not speak for a long time. He looked at the fire. He looked at his ledger. Once, I saw his gaze move to the shutter, to the pass beyond it, calculating distances, and I let him calculate. A man is owed the arithmetic of leaving.

When he finally spoke, his voice was steady in the way of things balanced precisely at their breaking point.

"The names. If you find them — the census, the others buried in the archive — what happens to you? To the Vharn?"

"I don't know," I said, and the honesty of it surprised even me. "Perhaps nothing. Perhaps nine hundred names spoken into the world changes nothing but the air. But a memory that lives in a body is flesh, and a memory that lives nowhere is nothing, and I would rather be the keeper of nine hundred resurrections than the end of a people. Even if the resurrection is only a story told properly at last."

"And the founder. Aurelian." His knuckles whitened on the ledger's cover. "He knows about you. He must. The last loose thread of the only witnesses to his crime."

"He has known for centuries. He does not hunt me anymore because hunting requires admitting I exist, and my existence is the one fact his erasure missed. Better to wait for me to die alone." I showed my teeth at the fire, which is the closest a wolf comes to weeping. "He has been waiting a long time. He is very patient. God-dreams slow the blood."

Coral stood. Walked to the window. Pushed the shutter open a hand's width, and stood looking north, where the pass climbed toward the high white shoulders of the mountains, and somewhere beneath them, the cradle, and the sleeper, and the thief at the sleeper's ear.

"My mother's record says he was the wards' betrayer," he said at last, half to himself. "The abbess went pale at the name. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That it would be some grand villain. Some horror out of the old tales." He turned, and his eyes were wet and furious and more alive than I had ever seen them. "It's worse. It's a man. It's an *accountant*. A man who found the world's greatest trust and turned it into a larder."

"Now you understand what we walk toward."

"I understand," Coral said, "that you've been carrying nine hundred funerals alone for longer than I've been alive. And that you picked me up like picking up a lockpick." A muscle worked in his jaw. "I'm furious with you. I want you to know that. I'm going to stay furious for some time, and I reserve the right to bring it up at unpleasant moments for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be."

"Understood," I said.

"But." He came back to the fire and sat, closer than before, and set the ledger down between us like a border marker on newly surveyed land. "If you'd lied to me tonight, I'd have been gone by morning. Since you didn't — since you handed me the knife and told me where to cut — I'm still here. Ask me again in the morning whether I forgive you. Ask me at the ward-heart. I may have different answers." He paused, and something almost like a smile crossed his face, brief and crooked and quickly gone. "Nine hundred names. When this is done, whatever 'done' means — I'll write them. Every one. In ink, in a ledger, in a hand the guild can't burn. Flesh *and* paper. Belt and braces."

No one had offered me that. Not in four hundred years. I am old, older than my shape suggests, and I had believed myself past the reach of anything so simple as being *helped*, and there in the firelight of a shepherd's ruined hut I lowered my head so he would not see my eyes, and found that there was no hiding from him anyway.

"That is the most valuable thing," I said, when I could speak, "anyone has ever offered me."

"Don't thank me yet," Coral said. "I haven't seen the price of ink lately."

But the joke was armor, and we both knew it, and beneath the armor something had shifted in the room — some load redistributed, some weight shared out between two carriers instead of one.

We slept, or pretended to, and near dawn I woke to the sound of Coral scratching at his ledger by ember-light, entering something with great care. I did not ask what. I already knew: he was recording the history I had given him, the true one, in his precise clerk's hand, so that it existed now in two vessels instead of one.

And that was when I smelled it.

Smoke — but not our woodsmoke. Old smoke, god-touched smoke, drifting down the pass from the heights like a warning written in the sky's own hand. I raised my head. Far to the north, above the white shoulders of the mountains, above the hidden cradle of the sleeping gods, the dawn was coming up the wrong color.

Red.

"Coral," I said. "Wake up properly. Something is burning in the high places — and it isn't the snow that's catching."

He was on his feet before I finished, ledger clutched to his chest, staring north with me at a horizon that should have been gold and rose and was instead the color of an abbey going up in flames.
