## 13. A Lie Between Friends

The prison road went up through the teeth of the mountains, and Coral did not speak to the wolf for two days.

He told himself it was the cold. The wind came down off the passes like something with intent, finding every gap in his coat, and speech cost breath he could not spare. He told himself it was the climb. The trail was a scar of ice and loose shale where a misstep meant a long red slide into the drowned country they had left behind. He told himself many things, because the alternative was to say what was actually in his chest, and that would not fit through his throat.

*You sought me as a ledger-keeper.*

Ashfall's confession from the night before hung between them still, heavier than the snow. *I first sought you not as an ally,* the wolf had said, eyes low, voice rough as bark stripped by weather, *but as a keeper of records whose books could give my people back their names.* And then he had waited for forgiveness the way stone waits for spring.

Coral walked ahead. It was foolish — Ashfall's paws were made for this country and Coral's boots were not — but he needed the small cruelty of leading, of setting a pace the wolf must follow rather than the reverse. Behind him, the great grey shape moved silent over the scree, patient as erosion, and every step of that patience felt like an accusation.

*You used me.*

No. That wasn't true either, and the untruth made it worse. Ashfall had shielded him from enforcers. Had bled at the Undermarket gate. Had carried him, half-conscious, out of the ambush at the ford. Everything the wolf had done since the pact was real. It was only the beginning that was rotten — the first lie under all the honest stones above it, the way a bad coin can be buried in a purse of good ones and poison your trust in the whole sum.

At midday they crossed a frozen waterfall, its curtain of ice blue as bottle-glass, and Coral stopped on the far side because he could not stop himself.

"You knew," he said. His breath came out in a white flag. "Before you ever spoke my name on that road. Before the pact. You'd already decided what I was worth."

Ashfall stood at the base of the falls, snow settling into the grey ruff at his neck, making him look older, or perhaps just finally showing how old he was. "I knew what you were," he said. "A clerk who touched everything the guild recorded. I did not know who you were."

"That's a distinction without a difference."

"It is the entire difference." The wolf's ears flattened. "I have eaten memories, Coral Blackwood. Do you understand what that means? I know what a man is from the inside — the taste of him, the grain of him. And I know what a ledger is. Cold paper, ordered columns, ink that never once trembles unless the hand holding the pen is afraid. You were a ledger when I found you. A door into the guild's records. I will not pretend otherwise."

"Good," Coral said, and heard how ugly his own voice sounded. "Then we're agreed. The pact was a transaction. Protection for access. I was a key you turned."

"Yes."

The word landed like a slap. Coral realized, distantly, that he had wanted the wolf to argue — wanted *No, you were more, always more* — and the wanting shamed him more than the betrayal did. Nine years of being no one had left a hole exactly the shape of *someone*, and Ashfall had walked past it wearing Coral's own hope like a stolen coat.

"Your people," Coral said. "The memory-eaters. How many names do you think are in my ledgers? In the guild's confiscation rolls? Hundreds? Thousands?" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "That's all I am to you. An index. A card catalog with legs."

Ashfall said nothing. Snow gathered on his muzzle. And his silence — that terrible, patient, honest silence — confirmed it better than any agreement could have.

Coral turned and climbed on, and did not look back again until the light began to fail.

* * *

The blizzard came down on them an hour after dark, and it did not come like weather. It came like a siege.

One moment the sky held a few hard stars through the tearing cloud; the next, the world was a white fist closing. Wind screamed down the pass with a sound Coral felt in his back teeth, and the temperature fell so fast that the moisture in his breath froze and crackled against his scarf. The trail vanished. The mountains vanished. For one vertiginous moment Coral understood why the old maps marked this stretch with a single word: *nothing.*

"We have to shelter!" Ashfall's voice reached him shredded, torn to ribbons by the wind. "There was an overhang — a quarter mile back—"

"I saw it!"

They turned into the storm together and immediately lost each other. Coral staggered three steps and the wolf was gone, erased, and Coral was alone inside the roaring white with nothing to navigate by but the slope of the ground under his boots and the animal certainty that stopping meant dying. Ice built up on his lashes. His hands went from pain to a strange distant warmth that he knew, from a bad winter years ago, was the last warmth before none at all.

And beneath it all, in the marrow-deep place where his power lived, the storm called to him.

It had been calling since the ford, faintly, the way a struck bell keeps humming after the sound fades. This blizzard was wild weather, unclaimed, but it was still *weather* — still the same substance he had commanded since boyhood, before exile, before the ledgers, before he had buried the gift under nine years of careful invisibility. He could feel its architecture: the pressure gradients like vaulted ceilings, the cold fronts like armies in column. He could reach into it. He could *take hold of it.*

He almost did. The temptation rose in him sweet and total — part the storm like a curtain, walk untouched through the white, show the wolf and the mountain and the whole indifferent sky what Coral Blackwood had once been.

But the last time he had called a storm, at the ford with the enforcers' crossbow bolts in the air, the power had nearly poured him out like a broken jug. He remembered the sensation even now: his edges dissolving, his name thinning, the terrifying sense that the lightning did not distinguish between the thing it struck and the hand that summoned it. He had woken with blood in both ears and Ashfall's worried eyes inches from his own.

So instead he gritted his teeth and kept walking, one boot after another, and hated himself for the weakness, and hated the storm for offering.

His foot caught a lip of ice hidden under the powder. He went down hard, shoulder-first, slid ten feet toward the drop he could hear but not see, and caught himself on bare fingers against stone. When he tried to stand, his leg folded. Not broken — he didn't think — but the cold had gotten into the joint, and the storm had gotten into his lungs, and somewhere in the last hundred yards his storm-calling had begun to *falter*: he could feel the wild power around him slipping through his grip like wet rope, and worse, feel it beginning to slip *into* him, filling his chest with a pressure that had nowhere to go.

"No," he whispered. "Not now. Not here."

The pressure built. His fingertips went numb, then glowed — actually glowed, a faint blue coruscation dancing across the frozen skin, static crawling up his arms. If he let go here, on an exposed shelf above a four-hundred-foot drop, the discharge would kill him and probably bring the whole slope down with him. If he held it, it would crack him open from the inside like a frost-split boulder.

Through the white roar, a shape resolved. Grey, huge, moving with terrible deliberation against the wind.

"Ashfall—" Coral tried to warn him away. *Get clear. I'm going to—*

The wolf did not get clear.

Ashfall reached him in three bounds, and then the wolf did something Coral had no name for: he opened his mouth and *breathed in*. Drew the storm-pressure out of Coral's chest the way a bellows draws flame, except nothing burned — everything *cooled*, the wild charge flowing off Coral's skin and into the wolf's fur, which stood on end in a silver halo, crackling, alive with borrowed lightning. Ashfall took the whole of it. Coral felt the pressure leave him and watched it wrap the wolf like a garment of needles, and Ashfall bore it, snarling silently into the wind, sparks leaping between his shoulders, taking a storm's anger into a body that was never built to hold one.

Then the wolf lay down beside him, curved his great body around Coral's curled shape against the rock, and became a wall.

The blizzard broke against Ashfall's back all night. Coral pressed into the grey warmth — the wolf ran hot, furnace-hot, the borrowed storm glowing dimly under his pelt like banked coals — and listened to the wind try its hardest to peel them off the mountain, and fail.

"You shouldn't have done that," Coral said, near dawn, his lips against frozen fur. "Holding lightning. What does that even cost you?"

"Less than it costs you," Ashfall said. His voice was hoarse, scraped raw. "Storm is not my element. But I am made of memory, little clerk, and memory is very good at holding things that do not belong to it."

Coral closed his eyes. "You could have died."

"Yes."

"Why?"

The wind screamed. Ashfall was quiet for a long time, and when he answered, it was so quiet Coral nearly missed it beneath the gale.

"Because I lied to you," the wolf said, "about how I began. So I am choosing not to lie about how I continue. Whatever I sought you for, Coral Blackwood — whatever I wanted from your ledgers, whatever cold calculations brought me to that road the night the stars went out — none of it survives contact with you. I planned to use a key. Instead I found a man standing in a doorway, burning pages to protect a truth he thought would get him killed, and I—" The wolf's breath hitched, an oddly human sound. "I stayed. That is all I know how to say honestly. I stayed, and then I kept staying, and somewhere on this road the mission and the wolf stopped agreeing with each other."

Coral said nothing. The storm howled itself hoarse against the wolf's flank. And slowly, grudgingly, like ice releasing a trapped branch, something in his chest began to thaw.

* * *

They dug into the overhang as the blizzard spent its fury, and waited out the day there, wrapped in the wolf's warmth while the world outside rebuilt itself from white silence.

Coral sat with his back against Ashfall's side and turned his mother's absence over in his hands the way he had turned it over for eleven years — carefully, compulsively, a wound he could not stop touching. Since the Undermarket, since the broker's scales and the vial of pale smoke, the hole where her voice had been ached differently. Cleaner, somehow. A wound that had been cauterized instead of left to fester. He missed the sound itself less than he had feared. What he missed was missing it — the ritual of grief, the nightly visit to the empty room.

"Do you still have it?" he asked. "My mother's voice. The memory I sold."

Ashfall's chin was resting on his paws; one ear swiveled. "The broker sold it onward within the hour. Memory moves fast in the Undermarket when it's good stock. Hers was very good stock." A pause. "Why?"

"Curiosity." Coral picked at a thread on his cuff. "If it came back to me. If someone offered it back — free, clean, no price. Would I take it?"

"Would you?"

"I don't know." That was honest, at least. "Eleven days ago I'd have said yes before the question finished. Her voice was the last piece of her I had. I built my whole hiding on top of that loss — the ledgers, the invisibility, the name I buried. Losing her voice was the first brick of Coral Blackwood's wall." He looked down at his hands, chapped and ink-stained and shaking slightly from the night's cold. "But walls come down. And I keep thinking about what's underneath."

"And what is underneath?"

Coral was quiet a moment. Outside, the storm's last gusts rattled the overhang like dice in a cup.

"You," he said finally, and the word cost him, and he made himself finish anyway. "The road. Sable's face when she knelt — no, she hasn't knelt yet, but she will, I know her, I've known her since we were children stealing apples off the guildhouse trees. The abbey burning. Your people's names in the confiscation rolls, page after page, and the way you looked when I read the first one aloud and you heard it spoken for the first time in three hundred years. The ford, and the lightning coming out of me like it was trying to take my name with it, and waking up with your breath on my face." He swallowed. "All of that starts after the night the stars went out. All of it. If I take her voice back the way the god offered — if the price is forgetting everything since — then I get my mother back and I lose the only family I've got."

Ashfall had gone very still. Wolves could be still the way mountains were still.

"You would choose a memory of me," the wolf said carefully, "over the true voice of your dead mother."

"I'd choose the living over the dead," Coral corrected. "She's gone. She'd be furious with me for trading everyone I've met since her grave for an echo. My mother believed in carrying what you carry forward, not looking backward over your shoulder until you walk off a cliff." He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Besides. You carried me through a blizzard. You held lightning in your skin all night so it wouldn't crack me open. No recording of her voice ever did that."

"No," Ashfall said softly. "But she gave you the storm."

"She gave me the storm," Coral agreed, "and the stubbornness, and the handwriting, gods help me. And then she died, and the world kept going, and it turns out the world kept going *with you in it.*" He turned his head and looked at the wolf directly, and found it easier than he expected. Easier than it had been two days ago on the ice-falls. Maybe easier than it would be tomorrow. "I'm still angry. Understand that. What you did at the start was ugly and I'm not going to pretend it wasn't. But I'm angry at a plan, Ashfall. Not at the thing that slept around me in the snow. Those aren't the same animal anymore. I don't think they have been for a while."

The wolf exhaled, long and slow, and some tension went out of his enormous frame like a knot finally worked loose. "For what it is worth," he said, "the plan is dead. It has been dead since the abbey. What lives now—" he hesitated, searching, this ancient creature groping for words like a young thing, "—what lives now is just us, and the mountain, and the truth we're carrying. I find I prefer it."

"Don't get sentimental. You're a monster, remember. I have it on excellent authority." Coral leaned his head back against the warm grey shoulder and closed his eyes. "Nine years I didn't speak my name aloud. Four days ago I swore a pact on it to a creature my order slaughtered to extinction. And today I chose a wolf over my mother's ghost. If the old me could see this, he'd burn the ledger."

"The old you," Ashfall said, "was a ledger."

"Yeah," Coral said. "Well. Write that down."

Outside, the storm had finally exhausted itself, and the clouds were tearing open along the western ridge, and through the gap — impossible, after everything, after the wards and the darkness and the founder's long theft — Coral saw stars. A handful of them, early, defiant, refilling the sky one point of light at a time.

And below them, far down the valley where the prison road bent toward the ward-heart, lights were moving. Many lights. Organized. Marching in the disciplined pattern of a guild column — torches and lanterns and something colder behind them, something that flickered wrong, the way dreamlight flickers.

Ashfall saw them too. His head lifted, nostrils flaring, and the growl that came out of him was low enough to feel through the stone.

"They're ahead of us," Coral breathed. "Someone told them our route."

"Not someone," Ashfall said, rising, storm-light still ghosting faintly beneath his fur. "The founder. He knows we're coming, little clerk. He has known since the Undermarket — perhaps since the night the stars went out. Those ledgers of yours weren't just an index to him." The wolf's eyes reflected the distant lights, twin green coals in the dark. "They were an invitation. Every entry you ever recorded was a door he left open — and you, Coral Blackwood, have been walking through them since the day you signed your first impossible trade."

Coral stared down at the marching lights, at the stars refilling the heavens above them, and felt the full weight of his own handwriting settle onto his shoulders like a yoke.

"Then we'd better hurry," he said. "I have a lot of entries to answer for."
