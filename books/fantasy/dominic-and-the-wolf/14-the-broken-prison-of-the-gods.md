## 14. The Broken Prison of the Gods

The mountain did not have a door. It had a wound.

We found it on the third day past the blizzard, where the peaks of the Serrach Range folded together like a closed hand, and the snow gave way to bare black stone that hummed when Ashfall's paws touched it. The wolf stopped at the edge of the humming ground and would go no further on four legs. When he shifted — that terrible unfolding of bone and shadow into a man-shaped thing with grey eyes and a voice like gravel settling — he stood at the border of the stone and said, "It knows we're here."

"It?"

"The ward-heart." He flexed his human fingers as if they were unfamiliar tools. "It has been listening for a thousand years. Listening is all it does now. That's what a prison is, Coral. A place built entirely of listening."

I should have been afraid. Some part of me noted this with clerkly detachment — *you should be afraid* — the way I once noted discrepancies in a ledger column, tidy and distant. But the truth was I had been afraid for so long, in so many small and manageable ways, that fear had worn smooth as a river stone. What I felt instead was something colder and more precise: recognition. The same feeling I'd had three days before the stars went out, staring at an entry in my own handwriting and knowing, with absolute certainty, that I had written down the end of the world in neat brown ink.

The wound in the mountain was a fissure perhaps ten feet wide, its edges glassed smooth as if something had burned through rock like wax. Warm air breathed out of it, smelling of hot metal and — strangely, horribly — of lilies. Funeral flowers. Incense from a thousand guild chapels.

"Someone's been through recently," Ashfall said. His nostrils flared. "Many someones. Days old. They carried torches and iron and—" he paused, and his jaw tightened, "—and chains. Cold iron, oiled. The kind you use on things you intend to keep."

"The rival faction." I touched the satchel at my hip, where my mother's record lay wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs. It had grown heavier with every mile, or I had grown weaker; by now I could not have said which. "Sable's masters. The ones who want the gods freed for their own ends."

"Or want them kept asleep and owned." Ashfall looked at me. In his human shape his eyes were the same grey as in his wolf shape, which felt like a private joke the world was playing on both of us. "There's a difference between freeing a thing and stealing it, Coral. We're about to learn which one they came to do."

We went in.

* * *

The passage descended in spirals, and the spirals descended in time.

That is the only way I can describe what walking down into the ward-heart was like. The walls changed around us as we went — not smoothly, but in bands, like rings in cut timber. Near the surface the stone was rough and recent-feeling, scarred with guild masonry from the last century: buttresses, drainage channels, the faded ghost of a painted sigil. A hundred yards deeper, the work grew older, cruder, more desperate. Deeper still, there was no work at all, only raw stone veined with threads of light that pulsed in slow unison, like the heartbeat of something vast and buried.

And threaded through all of it, running down the walls like ivy made of script, were words.

I stopped at the first band of them because I recognized the hand. Not the specific hand — not mine, not my mother's — but the *kind* of hand. Ledger-hand. The careful, ruled, disciplined script of a guild clerk trained to record without opinion.

"They're ledgers," I whispered. My voice came back to me off the stone, flattened, deadened, as if the mountain swallowed echoes whole. "Ashfall. The walls are *ledgers*."

"Not the walls." Ashfall ran his fingers along a line of script without touching it, the way one hovers a palm over a flame. "The wards. Every binding ever laid on this place was written first, tested first, recorded first. The guild never destroyed its records, Coral. It *built with them*. A ward is just a ledger entry that refuses to be erased."

I thought of my own ledgers then — the Vesper Guild's night-book, the columns of impossible trades I had catalogued with such pride in my invisibility. Bottled thunder. Borrowed shadows. Keys that opened nothing. I had thought myself a mere recorder of strange commerce, a fly on the wall of the world's secret economy. Now, standing inside the mountain, I understood that every entry I had ever made had gone somewhere. Fed something. Became, in some sense I was only beginning to grasp, *load-bearing*.

"How far down?" I asked, because asking practical questions was easier than screaming.

"To the heart? Half a day's walk, if the passages are kind." Ashfall tilted his head, listening to something below us. "They won't be kind. They never are. But the founder's people went through recently, and they left the doors open. Arrogance leaves doors open."

"Or confidence."

"Arrogance," Ashfall repeated, "leaves doors open."

* * *

We walked for hours through the breathing dark, and the dark grew older, and so did the writing.

Somewhere past the third turning I began to understand the true horror of the place, and it was not the scale. The scale was merely vertiginous — corridors wide enough to swallow cathedrals, staircases descending in flights of a thousand steps, vaulted ceilings lost in darkness above where the light-veins thinned to starless black. No, the horror was in the *content* of the script. Because I could read it. Any guild-trained clerk could read it. And what it recorded, line after line after line, mile after mile, was dreams.

Not metaphorically. Literally. Entries like:

*Third watch, the Sleeper called Vessel-of-Rains dreamed of a shoreline that no longer exists. Dream harvested, bound, indexed under seal 4471. Subject showed no distress.*

*The Sleeper called Mother-of-Thorns dreamed of her children. Dream confiscated per directive. She asked for it back. Request denied.*

*She asked again.*

*Request denied.*

I stood before that last sequence for a long time. Three lines of brown ink, fading with age, carved into living stone a half-mile beneath the roots of the mountains. *She asked again. Request denied.* Somewhere behind those words was a god — an actual god, vast beyond my comprehension — who had dreamed of her children and been robbed of the dream, and had asked, and asked, and been refused by clerks who were only following directives.

"Coral." Ashfall's hand on my shoulder. "Keep walking."

"She asked for it back."

"I know."

"You *knew*. You knew what this place—"

"I knew what was done to my people here," he said quietly. "I did not know every cruelty done to theirs. There wasn't time, in the years I've had, to read everything. There is never enough time to read everything. That is why they chose clerks, Coral. That is why they chose *you*." His grip tightened, not unkindly. "Grief later. Walk now."

So I walked. And the entries continued, older and stranger, and I made myself read them, because I had decided somewhere in the drowned country that I would not look away from this anymore, whatever it cost. Looking away was how the guild had lasted a thousand years. Looking away was how my mother had helped build this and slept at night. Looking away was how *I* had processed a sale of bottled thunder three days before the sky went dark and told myself it was only commerce.

* * *

We heard them before we saw them: voices, many voices, echoing up the final stairwell with the flat efficiency of people doing a job they have done before. Torchlight flickered on the walls below, orange against the blue pulse of the ward-veins.

Ashfall pressed himself flat against the stone and pulled me down beside him. "A dozen at least. Maybe twenty. Guarding the last descent."

"Can you—"

"No." Flat, immediate. "Not against cold iron in a corridor that narrow. And if I shift fully down there, in the heart of their master's power, he'll feel it. He'll know exactly what I am." A pause. "What I am the last of."

"We could wait for Sable."

"Do you believe she's coming?"

I thought about it honestly, which was the only way I allowed myself to think about anything anymore. "Yes. But I don't know whose side she'll arrive on, or in time for what. And I don't think we get to choose the hour." I looked down the stairwell, at the torchlight crawling on ancient stone. "Besides. They left the doors open, remember? Let's find out whether that was arrogance or a trap. Either way, I need to see what's down there."

Ashfall studied me for a long moment. Then, softly, in the tone he used maybe once a month: "You've changed, clerk."

"Don't call me clerk."

"That's rather my point."

* * *

We went down among them like ghosts, because the founder's people were not watching the stairs. They were watching *him*.

The last chamber opened like the inside of a bell — a hollow vastness where the light-veins converged, all the blue pulses of the mountain gathering into a single great rhythm overhead, and beneath that rhythm, at the center of the chamber, stood a dais of black glass. On the dais stood a man.

He was reading a book.

That was the detail my mind snagged on, the absurd human detail amid the vertigo of scale: an old man in plain grey robes, standing alone in the prison of the gods, reading a ledger by torchlight with the mild concentration of a merchant checking accounts. He was not handsome or terrible. He was *ordinary* — white-haired, stooped, hands spotted with age — and yet every person in the chamber, two dozen armed enforcers in guild black, oriented toward him the way iron filings orient toward a lodestone. They did not guard him. They *orbited* him.

And the light-veins overhead pulsed in time with his breathing.

"Ashfall," I breathed. "The wards. Look at the wards."

"I see it." The wolf's voice had gone very quiet, very controlled, the control of a creature holding itself together by will alone. "He's not near the heart, Coral. He *is* the heart. Or he's woven into it. A thousand years of harvest, drawn up through the bindings into one vessel." His lip curled off his teeth. "That's why the memory-eaters were slaughtered. Not because we opposed him. Because we could *see* him. One taste of that man's memories and the whole architecture shows itself — the theft, the feeding, the theft, the feeding. He couldn't risk a single one of us surviving to tell it."

Below us, the old man turned a page.

Then, without looking up, without raising his voice at all, he said:

"Come down, Coral Blackwood. You've been reading my walls for six hours. I imagine you have questions."

Every enforcer in the chamber turned toward our hiding place as one.

There was no point in stealth anymore. I stepped out of the shadow of the stairwell with Ashfall at my shoulder, and we descended the last flight into the bell of the world, and the founder watched us come with the patient expression of a man who has waited a very long time and is enjoying the anticipation.

"You know my name," I said, when the floor finally leveled.

"I know all your names." He smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the worst part — it was genuinely, warmly kind, the smile of a grandfather, and it sat on a face that had been fed on stolen divine dreams for ten centuries. "Coral Blackwood. Night-clerk, Vesper Guild, nine years' service, exemplary record. Before that—" he let the pause stretch, delicate as a knife, "—before that, Dominic Kavanagh, son of Maren, storm-caller, exiled at nineteen for reasons your own order never explained to you. Shall I go on? I have quite a lot of you. Everyone who ever wrote in a guild ledger, I have quite a lot of."

"What are you?" Ashfall demanded, and the word came out of him like a growl wearing a word's clothing.

"I'm the guild's founder." The old man spread his hands, modest. "Though 'founder' implies I started something. Really, I *finished* something. Your people started it, wolf. The sleeping wards, the long healing — beautiful design, truly. All I did was notice that a sleeping god's dream, properly harvested, could keep a man alive indefinitely. And then notice that a man kept alive indefinitely could administer the harvest forever. Continuity of management, you understand. Institutions fail when their founders die. I simply declined to die."

He gestured, and an enforcer brought forward a lectern, and on the lectern lay a ledger.

I knew it instantly. I would have known it blindfolded, by smell alone — the vellum, the ink, the particular wear on the spine. The Vesper Guild night-book. *My* night-book. The one I had left behind in my burning room, the one I'd assumed was ash.

"Your entries are circled," the founder said, turning pages with a lover's gentleness. "Bottled thunder, purchased by a buyer with no name — circled. A borrowed shadow returned late — circled. A key that opens nothing — oh, Coral, that one was *lovely*, that key opens nothing because it opens the space between the wards, and you sold it to my agent without blinking. Do you know why I circle yours particularly?" He looked up at me, and his eyes were brown and warm and bottomless. "Because you write beautifully. Because you record without opinion. Because nine years ago the guild threw away the finest natural storm-caller of the century, and you responded by becoming the most honest clerk it has ever employed. You never falsified an entry. Not one. Even the ones that damned you."

The chamber was silent except for the pulse of the light-veins overhead, which I realized, with a lurch, was not the mountain's heartbeat at all. It was his. We stood inside the rhythm of a man's prolonged breath, and the breath was made of gods' dreams, and the dreams had been taken from sleepers who asked for them back.

"Why show me this?" I managed. My voice sounded far away. "Why burn my room, hunt me across half the country, and then stand here showing me your own confession?"

"Because I didn't burn your room, and I didn't send anyone to kill you — that was the faction you call my rivals, acting without my sanction, and we'll come to them. And because," he closed my ledger gently, "you are going to help me renew the wards. The old design is failing — your mother's design, incidentally, may she rest — and the new one requires a storm-caller with clean hands and a clerk's precision. I have wanted you for nine years, Dominic. I am delighted you finally came."

Behind me, low and soft, almost too quiet to hear, Ashfall said: "Whatever he offers — remember the walls. Remember *she asked again*."

I remembered. I would always remember.

But the founder was already opening the ledger to my first circled page, smiling his kind smile, and beginning, in the voice of a patient teacher, to tell me exactly what I stood to gain.
