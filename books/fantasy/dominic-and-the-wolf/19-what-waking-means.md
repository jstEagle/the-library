## 19. What Waking Means

Coral woke to the smell of rain on stone and the sound of someone breathing beside him.

Not the wet, rattling breath of a dying man — his own, he realized, when he tried it and it hurt all the way down like swallowed glass. Someone else's breathing, slow and deep and even, the kind of breathing that belonged to something very large pretending very hard to be small.

He opened his eyes.

The sky was full of stars.

That was the first thing. Not the ache in his hands, which felt as though someone had peeled them open and wrung them out like laundry; not the taste of ozone still coating his tongue; not the fact that he was lying on a bedroll he did not recognize beneath an overhang of mountain rock with a fire crackling low nearby. The first thing was the stars, ten thousand of them, wheeling overhead in their ancient courses, and among them lights that had no business being there — great slow constellations that moved while he watched, that rearranged themselves like sleepers turning over, that *looked back*.

The gods were awake. He had done that.

"You're staring," said Ashfall, without opening his eyes. "You've been staring for an hour. I could hear your heartbeat change every time one of them moved."

"How long," Coral rasped. His voice came out ruined, sanded raw from the inside. He remembered why: he had held a storm in a stone throat, underground, where storms were never meant to live. He remembered the founder's face at the end, and the thunder, and then nothing.

"Three days." Ashfall opened his eyes at last. In the firelight they were the color of old amber, and there was something new in them — or rather something old returned, a brightness that had not been there before the wards fell. "You slept like the dead. At one point I checked."

"And?"

"And you weren't. So I stopped checking." The wolf rose, shook himself — a motion like a hillside deciding to be a wolf again — and settled closer, his warmth radiating against Coral's side. "Drink. There's snowmelt. And eat, if you can manage it. You'll want your strength for the arguing."

"The arguing?"

"The world is awake too," Ashfall said dryly. "It has opinions."

* * *

Coral learned the shape of those opinions over the following days, as they came down out of the mountains into a country transformed.

The guild was broken — that much Sable's last messenger had confirmed before riding west, a young enforcer with soot still in her hair who had looked at Coral the way people look at weather they have decided to respect. The Vesper Guild's towers stood empty in the capital; its ledgers had been seized by wardens loyal to Sable; its name was spoken now only in the plural tense, *the guilds*, because half its chapters had declared independence and the other half had burned their own records and claimed they never existed. A thousand years of quiet, careful power, undone in three days by one confession spoken into the dark heart of the world.

But the guild had been the least of it.

In the flooded lowlands, the drowned country had begun to un-drown itself. Coral saw it from a ridge: rivers running backward toward the sea with a purposeful gleam, marshes draining into channels that had not existed a season ago, and standing in the shallows, figures — tall, slow, made of water and patience — lifting the land piece by piece like a woman gathering spilled thread. An old god of waters, doing what old gods apparently did when they woke: putting right what had been left broken.

"They don't hate us," Coral said. It was not quite a question. He had expected hatred. He had spent nine years expecting hatred, first from the guild, then from himself.

"They slept through the crime," Ashfall said. "They wake to the mess. Some will hate. Some are too old for it. The river one down there—" he nodded at the figure in the shallows, "—she remembers the world before the wards better than she remembers the wards themselves. To her, a thousand years is a nap."

"A nap," Coral repeated. "We imprisoned living gods for a nap's length of time."

"We imprisoned sleeping gods," Ashfall corrected, "and told ourselves they were monsters so we wouldn't have to hear them snore. Yes. That was the shape of it." He glanced sideways at Coral, amber eyes unreadable. "Your mother knew. That's why her confession worked. The wards were built on a lie, and lies are just words, and words can be unsaid by anyone brave enough to say the truth aloud. She was brave enough on paper. You were brave enough in the dark."

Coral didn't answer. He had found, in the three days since waking, that his grief and his pride had fused somewhere in his chest into a single dense knot he could neither swallow nor spit out. His mother had helped build the prison. His mother had also, in penance, written the confession that unmade it. He carried both women in him — the jailer and the penitent — and he suspected he would carry them for the rest of his life.

His storm-calling, though. That he would not carry at all.

He had tested it on the second morning, alone, standing on a bare slope with his hands raised the way he'd raised them in the ward-heart, the way he'd raised them once as a boy in his mother's garden when the summer storms came up the valley. Nothing. Not silence exactly — worse than silence. A sense of doors, everywhere around him, all of them closed, all of them locked from the outside. Whatever channel had run through him from the sky to his blood, the gods' waking had closed it, or his spending of it had burned it out, or both. He was a clerk again. A clerk with ruined hands and a famous name he hadn't asked for.

He didn't tell Ashfall. But Ashfall was a memory-eater, and Coral suspected that some things could not be hidden from a creature who ate remembering for breakfast.

* * *

The memory-eaters found them at the edge of the pine forest, on the fourth day.

There were eleven of them. Coral counted twice, because the first time he assumed his eyes were lying. Eleven wolves, grey and white and one black as a starless sky, coming down through the trees in loose formation — and not only wolves. Walking among them, in shapes that flickered between wolf and woman and something older than either, were others: the returned dead of a slaughtered people, given back their bodies when Ashfall carried their names out of the broken dream-index and spoke them into the world.

Ashfall went very still when he saw them. Coral felt the stillness come off him like cold off ice.

"Vale," Ashfall said. The word cracked in the middle.

The lead wolf — white-flanked, scarred across the muzzle, with eyes the same amber as Ashfall's but harder, worn smoother by grief — crossed the clearing slowly. When she was close enough to touch, she pressed her forehead against his shoulder and stayed there a long moment, and Coral, feeling suddenly like an intruder in a room he had no key for, looked away at the trees.

When he looked back, there were more of them watching him. All of them. Eleven wolves and six walkers, every eye in the clearing fixed on the thin, grey-faced man with the ruined hands.

"He's the one," said the black wolf, and it was not a question.

"The ledger-keeper," said another.

"The one who held the storm."

"The one who read the names."

Coral took a step back before he could stop himself. "I didn't do any of it alone," he said, and his voice came out defensive, too loud. "The confession was my mother's. The names were Ashfall's. I just — I wrote things down. That's all I've ever done."

"That's all," Vale said, and there was something in her voice that might have been laughter, worn thin as wire. "Little clerk. Do you know what we were, before the guild came for us? We were keepers. We held the memories of our dead so the dead would not be forgotten, and we held the dreams of the sleeping gods so the gods would not be lost. And when they slaughtered us, they thought that was the end of it — that a people erased from memory is a people erased entirely." Her amber eyes held his. "You wrote us back. Page by page. Name by name. My daughter's name is in your ledger. I have watched you write it four times since the wards fell, checking it, keeping it safe." She lowered her head, and the gesture was unmistakable, ancient, terrible in its grace. "We owe you a debt, keeper. And my people pay their debts."

"I don't want a debt," Coral said quickly. "I didn't do it for—"

"No one ever does," Vale said. "That's what makes it worth owing."

* * *

They walked with the pack after that, north and east, toward the rebuilt abbey where Sable had promised to bring the surviving guild records. And it should have been — Coral kept waiting for it to be — unbearable. He had spent nine years perfecting invisibility, and now he traveled in the company of creatures the whole world was talking about, and half the world talked about him too. In the market towns they passed through, people came out of their houses to look. Children ran alongside the wolves until their mothers called them back. An innkeeper in a village whose name Coral never learned refused payment, pressing bread and cheese into his hands with shaking fingers, saying, "You're the storm-clerk, aren't you, you're the one who let them out, my grandmother used to sing songs about the river-mother, we thought they were only songs" — and Coral had stood there holding bread like a man handed a live coal, unable to think of a single thing worth saying.

He had expected exile. He had braced for exile the way a man braces for cold water: the guild gone, his usefulness spent, his storm-calling burned out of him, and nothing left but to find some far town and some small ledger and begin again the long work of becoming no one. He had even started composing the letter to Sable in his head. *Don't look for me. It's cleaner this way.*

Instead there were debts, and songs, and children, and a white-flanked wolf who watched him write each evening with an expression Coral couldn't decipher, and none of it fit. None of it fit anywhere. He was a man shaped like a hole in the world, and the world kept trying to pour itself in.

On the ninth night, sitting apart from the pack's fire with his ledger open and his pen hovering over a blank line, he finally understood what the trouble was.

He didn't belong here. That was the thought, arriving at last with the clean simplicity of a blade. He had never belonged anywhere — not in the guild, where he'd been a buried thing; not in exile, where he'd been a ghost; not even in this story everyone kept telling about him, because the story had heroes in it and he was, at bottom, a clerk who had gotten lucky with other people's courage. His mother's courage. Ashfall's endurance. Sable's choice. Take those away and what remained? A man who catalogued impossible trades and burned pages when they frightened him. A man with empty hands.

He closed the ledger quietly. He looked at the road, pale in the starlight, running south and west toward towns where no one knew his face. He could be no one again. No one was a place he knew how to live.

"Don't," said Ashfall.

Coral flinched. The wolf had come up beside him without a sound, which was unfair in a creature his size, and sat down close enough that his fur brushed Coral's arm.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're doing with your face. I've seen it before. On the road north, the first time. The night you thought about slipping away while I slept." Ashfall's voice was flat as slate. "You get a particular look. Like a door closing. I've been waiting for it to come back for two days."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were going to walk south tomorrow and find a town where nobody knows the word *Blackwood*, and you were going to spend thirty years being a different nobody in a different nowhere, and you were going to call it peace." The amber eyes caught the firelight. "I ate a memory once, a miller's, of his father leaving in the night. Thirty years later the miller still set out two cups every morning. That's what vanishing does to the ones left behind. Don't do it to me."

Coral stared at him. "To *you*?"

"To me." Ashfall said it as though it were obvious, as though Coral were being deliberately slow. "I spent a hundred years as the last of my kind. Do you understand what that means? Every name I carried, I carried alone. Every memory, mine alone to keep. And then the wards fell, and my people came back out of the pages of your ledger, and I thought — I thought the worst of it was over. And now the one man I—" He stopped. His jaw worked. Wolves, Coral had learned, were not built for certain sentences.

"The one man you what?" Coral asked softly.

"The one man who writes my daughter's name correctly," Ashfall said at last, gruffly, which was not what he had meant to say and both of them knew it. "If you vanish, I will find you. I want to be clear about that. I have your scent memorized. I have your handwriting memorized. I tracked you across half a kingdom when you were actively hiding, and you were better at hiding then. So save us both the trouble and stay."

"Ashfall." Coral heard his own voice go rough. "Look at me. I'm nothing now. The storm's gone. I've got ink-stained hands and a ledger full of dead people's names and no magic, no guild, no—"

"You have a name," Ashfall said. "You have a people who owe you. You have a warden in the north saving every record the guild ever buried because you asked her to. You have a debt owed by eleven wolves and six walkers, and I promise you, Vale does not forgive debts, she collects them, and she has decided you are hers now, forever, whether you like it or not." He leaned in, close enough that his breath stirred Coral's hair, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle, which from Ashfall was more frightening than any threat. "And you have me. Which is the part you keep forgetting, so I'll say it plainly: I am not letting you disappear. I ate a hundred years of loneliness and I know its flavor, and I will not watch you choose it. You don't get to be invisible anymore, Coral Blackwood. That life is over. Mourn it if you must. But you don't get it back."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the dark, one of the pack sang a low note and another answered, and above them the rearranging constellations turned their slow bright faces toward the earth.

Coral found, to his enormous embarrassment, that his eyes were burning. He looked down at the ledger in his lap, at the blank line waiting under his pen, and after a moment he opened it and wrote — not a trade, not a price, not an entry in any column the guild would have recognized — simply:

*Ninth night. He wouldn't let me go.*

"It's a strange ledger you keep," Ashfall observed, reading upside down as wolves apparently could.

"It's the only kind I've got left."

"Good." The wolf settled against his side, warm as a banked fire, and closed his eyes. "Keep that one carefully. Some entries matter more than trades."

Coral sat a while longer, watching the stars move, feeling the weight of the wolf against him and the weight of the name on his tongue — *Blackwood, Blackwood* — growing less like a brand and more like a handhold. South and west, the road waited, patient as roads are. He would not take it. He understood that now, the way you understand a thing after someone has said it out loud enough times: he was done with vanishing. Whatever came next — the frightened world, the learning-to-live-with-gods, the records Sable was bringing, the debts, the songs, the children who would grow up thinking the river-mother had always been there — whatever came next, he would meet it with his name showing.

He was almost asleep when Ashfall spoke again, one eye open, ears pricked toward the northern dark.

"There's a light coming up the road," the wolf said. "Two riders. One of them is Sable Ives. The other—" he paused, and Coral felt the hackles rise along the great spine beside him, "—the other is not a person. It wears a person the way I wear this shape. And it's carrying something wrapped in cloth, and it's asking for the man who holds the ledgers."

Coral sat up. Far down the pale road, two points of lantern-light swayed closer through the pines, and beneath the ordinary night sounds — wind, fire, wolves — he heard, faint and vast, something that was almost a voice saying his name.

"Not Blackwood," the voice seemed to say. "The other one. The first one."

The one he hadn't spoken aloud in nine years. The one the abbess had promised him, waiting at the abbey he hadn't yet reached.

The gods, it appeared, wanted to talk.
