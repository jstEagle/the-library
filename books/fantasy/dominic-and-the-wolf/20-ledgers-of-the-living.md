## 20. Ledgers of the Living

The rebuilt abbey smelled of cedar shavings and wet plaster, and beneath both — faint but unmistakable, the way a scar is unmistakable — of old smoke. They had raised only half the roof so far. Rain came through the open beams and fell into wooden buckets set out like a congregation at prayer, each drop striking its own small faithful note.

Coral had claimed a table in what had once been the abbess's scriptorium. It was a good table: oak gone silver with age, one leg shimmed with a folded page from a hymnal he suspected no one would ever sing again. On it he had arranged his tools the way he had arranged them for nine years in the Vesper Guild's counting room — inkpot to the right, blotting sand to the left, quills in a row by wear — because the hands remember their habits even when the heart has been emptied and refilled with other things.

But the ledger before him was not a guild ledger. He had bought it three days ago from a tanner in the drowned country who traded hides for stories, and it was bound in undyed leather still bearing the animal's soft imperfections, the small nicks and scars of a creature that had lived. It was blank. All hundred and forty pages of it were blank, and every morning Coral sat down before that blankness the way a man sits before a deep well, listening for water.

He dipped the quill. He wrote nothing. He set the quill down again.

"You're doing it wrong," Ashfall said from the doorway.

Coral did not look up. The wolf had taken to lying across the scriptorium threshold like a doorstop made of weather, grey fur catching the pale light, amber eyes half-lidded in what passed among memory-eaters for contentment. "I wasn't aware there was a wrong way to stare at paper."

"There is. You're staring at it like it owes you something. Like the guild ledgers owed you." Ashfall's muzzle settled on his paws. "You used to balance accounts. This is different. You're not balancing anything. You're carrying things across."

"Across what?"

"The gap." The wolf said it as if the gap were an obvious geographic feature, like a river or a ridge, which perhaps it was. Memory-eaters, Coral was learning, spoke of abstractions with the confidence of men describing their own back gardens.

He looked at the first page again. For six days he had been writing entries in it — carefully, reverently — and then copying them fair somewhere else and scratching them out, because they were wrong. *Bottle of thunder, one measure, sold to a chandler of Marrow Street, price two crowns* — that was the old language, and the old language could not hold what needed holding. *Name:* was closer. But every time he began a name, he felt the weight of it, all those names, the memory-eaters' dead, the heretics of the burned abbey, the truths the guild had buried under nine hundred years of clean, orderly, murderous bookkeeping — and his hand stopped, and the well gave no water.

There were so many. That was the truth he kept circling. Not dozens. Thousands. Ashfall could carry them — had carried them, waking names out of the dream-index like a man pulling drowned things from a lake — but a memory carried is not the same as a memory written, and Ashfall had said so himself, in the flat voice the wolf used for the hardest things: *Your people wrote things down too, storm-caller. That was how we divided the labor. We remembered, and your people recorded, and between us nothing was lost. Then your guild burned our libraries first.*

So. Names it would be. But how to begin?

The rain kept its patient percussion. Somewhere below, Sister Verity — one of only four souls who had survived the burning, and the youngest, and the angriest — was teaching two orphaned children their letters, and her voice rose through the floorboards in fragments: *"No, the tail goes below the line, always below, a letter that won't humble itself isn't worth writing—"*

Coral smiled despite himself. He knew guild clerks who could have learned from Sister Verity.

That was when Sable Ives came up the abbey road with a mule, two crates, and a sword she no longer wore at her hip.

* * *

He heard her before he saw her — some habits of the body outlast even exile — the particular rhythm of boots on wet stone, unhurried, deliberate, a walk he had known since he was eleven years old and she was thirteen and they had stolen honeycakes from the guild kitchens together and split them on a rooftop under a sky full of stars that had not yet gone out. He set down his quill and stood, and Ashfall lifted his head from his paws, ears forward.

"A friend," Coral said.

"I know," said the wolf. "I'm deciding how much of one."

Sable came through the scriptorium door with rain in her close-cropped hair and shadows under her eyes that had not been there a month ago, or perhaps had, and he simply hadn't let himself see them. She looked around the half-roofed room, the buckets, the silver table, the enormous wolf who filled most of the doorway and did not move aside for her, and something in her face performed a complicated maneuver — grief and wonder and professional assessment all passing through at once, like travelers through a gate.

"You look terrible," Coral said.

"You should see the guild." She set down the crates with a thud that raised dust smelling of vellum and ash. "Or you shouldn't. What's left of it, you'd never recognize. Warden Hall's roof caved in during the fighting. Half the archives went into the cellar and the cellar took fire. We've been pulling books out of the wreckage for three weeks." She nudged the nearer crate with her boot. "These are yours now. If you'll have them."

Coral looked at the crates and did not touch them. "What are they?"

"The surviving records. Everything that didn't burn, everything my people could carry out before the founder's faction torched what was left. Ledgers. Correspondence. The inner archive — the one none of us below Warden rank ever admitted existed." Her mouth twisted. "Including me. Twenty years an enforcer, Coral, and I enforced for a lie with a founder at the bottom of it older than any kingdom on the map."

"You couldn't have known."

"Don't." It came out flat and fast, a hand raised against absolution. "Don't do the kind thing. I've had a month of the kind thing from people whose houses the guild burned on false writs, and I'll tell you what I told them: I knew the ledgers never added up. I chose not to count. That's not ignorance. That's complicity with better posture." She looked at Ashfall then, straight on, which Coral noted — most people looked slightly away from the wolf, some instinct older than reason. "You were at the ward-heart. You saw what he saw. Is he telling it true?"

Ashfall considered her with those banked-fire eyes. "He tells it truer than most of your kind tell anything. Your guild built its power on a theft nine centuries deep. Your founder fed on gods' dreams like a tick on a sleeping ox. And when the theft was exposed, he chose to burn everything rather than return it." A pause. "You are asking whether you may trust him. I am telling you that trust is not given by witnesses. It is earned by debt. He freed my people's names. By the law of my people, that makes him mine to protect until the debt is paid."

"And after the debt?"

"After the debt," said Ashfall, "I expect I'll stay anyway. It seems to be how these things go."

Something loosened in Sable's shoulders — not quite laughter, but the place laughter comes from. She turned back to Coral. "There's more. The reconciliation will take years. The guild's records are a maze built to be walked by liars — double entries, dead drops, whole decades rewritten. But underneath all of it there's a true account. There has to be. Even a lie needs a spine of truth to stand on, and your mother's confession named the spine." She tapped the crate. "Somewhere in here is every name the guild erased. Every memory-eater hunted. Every heretic silenced. Every clerk who processed a sale he shouldn't have and burned the page after." Her eyes found his, and held. "Everyone like you."

The word landed where she meant it to land. Coral sat down slowly.

"I didn't burn mine," he said. "Not really. I burned one page. One. And I've thought about that page every day since, like a stone I swallow each morning."

"I know. I read the incident report. I signed it, actually — retrieval order, quiet handling. I was going to bring you in myself." Sable crossed to the window, looking out at the rain coming off the mountains. "Nine years ago, when they exiled you, I told myself you'd done something unforgivable. It was easier than asking what. And then the stars went out, and I asked what, and the answer was worse than anything I'd imagined, because the answer was that the boy I grew up with was the only honest clerk the guild ever employed, and we exiled him for it."

"Sable—"

"They're dissolving the wards' administration formally next month. There's a council forming — freed gods' emissaries, memory-eaters, the drowned-country folk, even a couple of guild defectors I vouched for personally, God help them all. Someone has to reconcile the records so the council knows what was taken, from whom, and what's owed." She turned from the window. "I can't do it. I can read a battlefield; I can't read a fraud. But you — you found a single impossible entry in a ledger of ten thousand lines, three days after you filed it, and you knew what it meant. You're the only person alive who reads like that."

The rain filled the silence. Below, faintly, Sister Verity's voice: *"—below the line, always below—"*

"It's not clerking," Coral said quietly. "What you're describing. It's not cataloging trades. These are people. These are—" He stopped, started again. "Every page will be a grave, Sable. Every entry a thing someone suffered. How do I write that down without it becoming just — entries? Without my hands doing what guild hands always do, turning grief into tidy columns?"

For the first time, Sable smiled, and it was the smile of the girl on the rooftop, crooked and sure. "You don't turn it into columns. You turn yourself into something new. That's the whole work, Coral. That's everyone's work now." She nodded at the blank ledger lying open beside the crates. "Starting there, I'd think."

She left the way she'd come, boots measured on wet stone, and this time Ashfall moved aside to let her pass, which was, Coral understood, the wolf's version of an embrace.

* * *

He opened the crates that evening, by lamplight, with Ashfall a warm grey weight along the wall behind him and the rain still keeping time in its bucket-congregation. The topmost item was the guild's founding charter, its wax seals cracked, and beneath it layer upon layer of ledgers, correspondence, interrogation transcripts, requisitions for chains and for ink — always so much ink, the guild's truest appetite — and, wrapped in oilcloth near the bottom, a slim volume labeled in the founder's own hand, the hand he had seen circled beside his own entries in the dream-index: *Erasures, Systematic, 400–present.*

He closed that one last and left it for morning. Some spines of truth take a night's steadying to hold.

Instead he drew the new ledger toward him, the soft imperfect leather, the hundred and forty blank pages waiting like held breath. He thought about the gap Ashfall spoke of, the one you carry things across. He thought about his mother, who had helped bind sleeping gods and left her confession as penance, and who had taught him his letters with her finger trailing in spilled flour. He thought about Sister Verity's rule: *a letter that won't humble itself isn't worth writing.* He thought about all the names, thousands of them, waiting in the crates like passengers at a shore.

And he understood, finally, why the first page had defeated him for six days. Because he had been trying to begin with the dead, and the dead, though owed everything, could wait one hour more. The living came first. The rule of every honest ledger: record the keeper.

He dipped the quill. The lamplight leaned close. Behind him Ashfall's breathing had gone slow and deliberate, the wolf awake and watching, pretending not to.

On the first page, in a hand steadier than it had any right to be, Coral Blackwood — no. Not Coral. Not tonight. Tonight, humbled below the line, tail beneath the stroke, the name his mother gave him, the name the abbey had returned to him, the name he had spent nine years burying and one month learning to say:

*Ledger of the Living,* he wrote. *Kept by Dominic Kavanagh, son of Maren Kavanagh, clerk of the Vesper Guild, exile, storm-caller twice over, friend to the last of the memory-eaters. Let every name herein be carried across.*

His hand did not shake until the signature, and then only a little, and he let it.

Ashfall rose and came to stand over the page, reading upside down as wolves apparently did, or at least as this one did. "Dominic," the wolf said, tasting it. "It suits you worse than Coral. Too many hard consonants. I approve completely."

"You would." Dominic blew gently on the ink. "It's going to take years, you know. Maybe the rest of both our lives."

"Yes," said Ashfall, settling back down against the wall with a sigh that stirred the lamplight. "That's rather the point."

Dominic turned to the second page and wrote the first name of the dead — *Hesper, daughter of Hesper, memory-eater of the Northern Vale, taken winter 401* — and found that the well had water after all, deep and cold and endless.

He was four pages in when Ashfall's head came up sharply, ears pricked toward the abbey road, and the wolf said, in a voice Dominic had never heard him use: "Someone's coming. Many someones. And they're singing — in the old tongue. In *our* tongue."

Down the dark road, through the rain, lights were approaching the abbey. More lights than Dominic could count.
