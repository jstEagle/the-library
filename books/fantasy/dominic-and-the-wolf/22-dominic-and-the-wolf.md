## 22. Dominic and the Wolf

The abbey rose from the burned ground the way forgiveness rises in a heart that has every right not to bother: slowly, stubbornly, stone by carried stone.

Coral had been there for the first of it. He had helped haul timber with his own soft clerk's hands, had blistered and bled and found, to his astonishment, that he liked the feeling — proof of work done in the open, work no one needed to hide. The heretics' bones had been laid to rest beneath the new chapel floor, each one named now, each name written in a ledger Coral kept himself, in ink he mixed from ash and rainwater. Sable had brought him the guild's surviving records in seventeen crates, and between them they had reconciled the dead against the drowned archives until every soul the fire took had a line, a date, a truth.

But it was not his hands the abbess watched this morning. It was his face.

Mother Ilse was older than the guild itself, or looked it, her skin like paper that had been written on and scraped clean so many times only the deepest letters remained. She stood in the abbey's unfinished doorway with the morning fog curling around her hem, and she studied Coral as he copied names onto the founders' wall — the memory-eaters' names, all two hundred and eleven, restored by Ashfall's remembering and Coral's patient pen.

"You write like your mother," she said.

Coral's brush paused mid-stroke. "You knew her."

"I knew a woman who came here once, years ago, carrying a sealed record and a boy too young to remember the road." Mother Ilse came to stand beside him, her shadow falling across the wet stone. "She asked us to keep one thing for her. Not the record. Something smaller."

"She never told me about this place."

"She told *us* about *you*." The old woman turned. From the folds of her habit she drew a strip of vellum, worn soft as cloth at the edges, covered in an erasure-glyph so old its edges had gone silver. "This is how our order works, Dominic. We do not only erase names to protect the named. Sometimes we hold them, hidden, until the world is safe enough to give them back."

The glyph flared under her thumb and dissolved.

And there was his name. His whole name. Not Coral Blackwood, the alias stitched together from a dead man's guild records and nine years of careful invisibility. The name before. The one written in a child's hand on the flyleaf of his mother's songbook, the one the exile tribunal had struck from every register in three kingdoms, the one he had not dared even to think in full since the night the wards fell the first time, when he was eleven years old and the sky above their house went dark for exactly one breath.

**Dominic Averil Blackwood.**

He did not realize he had stopped breathing until Ashfall's shoulder pressed warm against the back of his knee — the wolf's wordless way of saying *I am here, breathe*, which the wolf deployed more often than Coral would ever admit to finding necessary.

"Say it," Mother Ilse said. "Names die quiet. They live loud."

His throat worked. Nine years of swallowing this name, and now it would not come. He looked at the wall instead — at the two hundred and eleven names he had given back to the world, at the newest column, the heretics of the fire, at the single line at the bottom of the founders' list where he had written, just yesterday, *Maren Blackwood, who betrayed the betrayers*. His mother's hand had been steadier than his. Her confession had unmade a thousand-year prison. Surely he could manage one spoken word.

"Dominic," he said.

It came out cracked and small, and then he said it again, louder, and the sound of it went through him like the first swallow of hot wine after a long freeze. The fog was lifting off the courtyard. Somewhere below, the novices were arguing about drainage ditches. The world was utterly ordinary and utterly changed, and his name sat in the middle of it, alive, loud.

Ashfall's great head tilted. The wolf's eyes — grey as river-stone, old as the sleep of gods — caught the morning light.

"It suits you better," Ashfall said. "Coral always sounded like something bleached and left to harden."

"Coral was what kept me alive."

"And Dominic is what you lived *for*. There is a difference. Memory-eaters know the difference better than anyone." The wolf's tail swept once through the mist. "Your mother chose well."

*Dominic.* He tried it on the inside, where it counted. It fit like shoes he'd outgrown and been given back after his feet finally stopped growing.

"Then it's yours again," Mother Ilse said, pressing the vellum into his hands — blank now, the glyph spent, but still faintly warm. "Wear it out into the world. It has been folded up long enough."

* * *

They left on the first clear day of autumn, because the world outside the abbey walls was frightened, and frightened worlds believe whatever story reaches them first.

The stories were bad ones. Coral read them in the waystations and market squares as they traveled: broadsheets claiming the woken gods had drowned a fishing village in the south (untrue; the sea had simply remembered its old tide-tables), that the stars going out and coming back was an omen of invasion (half-true, if you squinted — the stars had been veiled by the wards, and what people had seen all their lives was a cage painted to look like heaven), that the guild's fall meant the end of law, of trade, of winter turning properly to spring. In three border towns, people had begun leaving offerings out for the old gods again, the way their great-grandmothers had, before anyone thought to be afraid — bread and honey and little boats set on the water, because the oldest stories said the sleeping gods dreamed of rivers.

"They don't need us," Coral said one evening, watching a farmer's daughter set a honey-cake on her windowsill with the grave ceremony of a child doing a very important thing. "Look at her. She remembers more than the guild ever did. It's in the milk-teeth stories. It survived a thousand years of wards."

"The girl remembers *how* to leave offerings," Ashfall said. The wolf lay with his chin on his paws, and his voice, as always, seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his chest, as if the earth itself were being polite enough to lend him a mouth. "She does not remember *why*. And the why is where fear lives. Fear fills empty rooms faster than anything I have ever eaten." A pause. "And I have eaten rooms full of fear."

Coral snorted. "Charming as ever."

"I am not trying to charm you. I am trying to explain why we are walking north into mountains in October." The wolf rolled one eye toward him. "You insisted on the road. I merely insist on keeping you alive upon it. Our arrangement has always divided labor this way."

"Our arrangement," Coral said, "was a pact sworn on a name I wasn't using. I've been meaning to ask whether it's still binding."

Ashfall lifted his head fully then. For a long moment the wolf only looked at him, and Coral felt the particular attention of a creature who could taste memory the way others tasted air — felt, as he always did under that gaze, faintly transparent, and had long ago stopped minding.

"It stopped being binding," Ashfall said, "somewhere in the blizzard, when you decided you would rather lose your mother's voice forever than forget me. I did not inform you at the time because you were unconscious and it seemed rude." The wolf settled back down. "We are not bound, Dominic. We are simply together. I recommend it. It requires less paperwork."

Dominic laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised him coming out — and threw a pinecone at the wolf's head, which the wolf allowed to strike him with an expression of vast, dignified patience, and the fire crackled, and somewhere far to the south a god walked along a coastline learning what a thousand years of tides had changed, and none of it, none of it, felt like exile anymore.

That was the strange thing he was still learning to trust: that belonging could arrive without ceremony. No tribunal striking his name back in. No oath, no ward, no ledger entry making it official. Just a wolf who stayed, and an abbey that rebuilt itself around his handwriting, and strangers who had started calling him friend without asking for references.

* * *

In the mountain town of Harrow's Rest, they taught their first true lesson, and it went badly, and then it went well, and Dominic learned the shape of the work ahead.

The town had a guild charter nailed above its hearth-hall, and half the town wanted it burned, and the other half wanted it defended, and the two halves had begun looking at each other the way people look at each other shortly before someone throws something. Into this walked a clerk with a ledger and a wolf the size of a pony, which did not immediately calm matters.

"That's a memory-eater," someone hissed, and the word ran through the hall like fire down a fuse. *Monster. Eater of graves. It wears your dead father's face in its dreams.*

Dominic climbed onto a bench — he was not tall, and had spent nine years perfecting the art of taking up no space, and it cost him something real every time he made himself visible on purpose — and opened his ledger on the table.

"My name is Dominic Blackwood," he said. "My mother helped build the lies you've both been raised on, and I have her confession in my bag, and I am going to read it to you whether you throw things at me or not. But first—" he turned the ledger around— "I want you to look at some names."

Two hundred and eleven names. The memory-eaters, slaughtered in a single night nine hundred years ago by the guild's founder, who could not abide witnesses.

"These were the ward-builders," he said. "They built the wards to *lullaby* the old gods to sleep, because the gods were wounded and mad and the world was dying, and sleep was mercy. Sleep was supposed to end. Your charter says the wards protected you from what was outside. There was never anything outside. The guild locked the door from the inside and charged you rent for the darkness." He let that sit. "This creature you're calling a monster is the last living soul of the people who wrote those names. He has spent a year giving them back, one memory at a time, because his people believed — this is in their own records, I've checked twice — that a name forgotten is a death completed, and they refused to be finished."

Silence. The kind of silence that is either the pause before violence or the pause before understanding; Dominic had learned you usually couldn't tell which until afterward.

Then an old woman near the hearth — the kind of old whose face keeps its own records — stood, walked to Ashfall, and looked up at him for a long moment.

"You'll be Vesper's kin," she said. "My gran told stories. Said when the last of the ward-builders died, the stars would go out for shame." Her chin trembled. "Stars went out. You didn't die after all. So maybe the shame ends too."

Ashfold lowered his great head until his nose nearly touched her hand.

"The shame was never ours to carry alone," the wolf said gently. "It was carried by everyone who was lied to. I would very much like to put it down. I believe you would too."

By morning, the charter was down — not burned; Dominic had argued for archiving it, evidence being worth more than ash — and the town elders had asked him, shyly, whether he might stay through the season and teach them to read the old records. He had a road to walk and a world to reach. But he left them copies. He had learned that from his mother: leave the record where the next finder can use it.

On the road east of Harrow's Rest, Ashfall said, without preamble: "There are more of them now."

Dominic looked up from his ledger. "More what?"

"Ward-builders." The wolf's ears swiveled north. "When the gods woke, the old blood woke with them. Children born this spring with grey eyes and old dreams. I can feel them the way you feel weather coming. Scattered. Frightened. Being called monsters in villages from here to the salt coast." A beat. "Being hunted, in at least four places I know of, by people who miss the way things were."

Dominic closed the ledger slowly. His hand rested on the cover — ash-ink and rainwater, two hundred and eleven names, one confession, one page at the back reserved and still blank.

"How many?" he asked.

"Enough to matter. Enough to be a people, if someone teaches them what they are. Enough to be feared, if no one does." Ashfall turned north, where the mountains stood up white against the sky, and the wind coming down off them smelled of snow and pine and distance. "The first of them is three days ahead of us. A girl. Twelve years old. She has been dreaming the wards falling every night since they fell, and she does not yet know the dreams are memories, and she is very close to running somewhere no one will ever find her."

Dominic looked at the road. Then at the wolf. Then at the ledger, and the blank page waiting at the back of it.

"A girl with old dreams and nowhere to belong," he said. "Yes. I know how that story starts." He swung his pack up onto his shoulders, and it was lighter than it had ever been, though it held everything he had. "Walk with me?"

"Always," said Ashfall, as if it were the smallest word in any language. "You keep the ledger. I'll carry the memories. That was always how it was meant to work — one to write it down, one to remember it true."

They walked north, the two of them, under a sky so thick with stars it looked like the night the wards fell, inverted — every light the darkness had swallowed given back at last. Dominic wrote as they went, because his hand was restless and the day was fine and there was room on the blank page for one more name:

*First entry, new ledger. A girl, twelve, dreaming true. The road north. My name is Dominic Averil Blackwood, and I am not hiding anymore.*

Far ahead, small against the mountains, something moved — a figure on the road, watching them come.

Ashfall's step slowed, just slightly. His nostrils flared.

"Dominic," the wolf said quietly. "She isn't alone."
