## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The nets needed mending again.

Elodie Dunmore had learned many things in her thirty-one years — how to read weather in the curl of smoke from a chimney, how to gut a fish without making a face, how to hold a lightning bolt in her cupped hands like a startled bird — but nothing had ever taught her patience quite like a torn net.

"You're losing your touch," said Widow Pram, who came by every third morning to say exactly that, regardless of what Elodie was doing.

"My touch is fine," Elodie said, working the shuttle through the mesh. "The net is old."

"The net is older than me, and I'm older than sin." The widow settled herself onto the upturned crate that lived permanently outside Elodie's door for precisely this purpose. She was a small woman built like a knot in timber, all elbows and opinion, and she smelled of woodsmoke and pickled onions, which she considered two of the four major food groups. "In my day we wove them proper. Double-knotted. None of this slipping about."

"In your day," Elodie said, "the river ran the other way and fish were the size of pigs, if I remember your telling of it."

"They were *bigger* than pigs. That's why the story keeps getting better every year. You have to keep up." She leaned over to squint at Elodie's knots, found nothing to criticize, which visibly disappointed her, and moved on to criticizing the weather instead. "Rain coming. Feel it in my knee."

Elodie did not need her knee. She could feel it in her teeth, in the fine hairs on her arms, in a place behind her sternum that had no name and no business still existing after seven years of careful disuse. Out past the ridgeline, over the peaks where nobody from Hollowmere ever went because there was nothing up there but rock and wind and the occasional very ambitious goat, a storm was assembling itself out of the morning sky like a slow, deliberate thought.

She could feel all of it. Every mile of it. The pressure stacking up against the mountains, the cold air sliding down off the glacier, the static crawling along the underside of the clouds looking for something to strike.

And she knew — the way you know a kettle will boil, the way you know a letter is bad news before you break the seal — that none of it would come over the ridge.

It never did.

* * *

Hollowmere sat in its valley like a coin at the bottom of a cup: sheltered, forgotten, and slightly green with age. The village proper was forty-odd houses huddled around a millpond, a church with a leaning spire, and a market square that hosted a market only when someone remembered to organize one, which was roughly twice a year and always a disappointment. The rest was fields, orchards going soft and wild at their edges, and the river — the Merle — running shallow and quick over stones the color of old teeth.

And above it all, ringing the valley like the walls of a bowl held upside down over a candle flame, the ridgeline.

The people of Hollowmere had a hundred explanations for why storms never crossed it. The mountains were blessed. The mountains were stubborn. The mountains were shy. Old Tam Wicker swore his grandfather had once seen lightning come down as far as the tree line, and that the trees it struck had grown back silver, but Tam also swore he'd wrestled a pike the length of a rowboat, so his testimony carried limited weight.

Elodie knew the truth, or a truth, and she kept it folded up small in the same inner pocket where she kept everything else she refused to look at directly:

Magic died at the ridgeline.

Not faded. Not weakened. Died — cleanly, completely, the way a candle dies when you pinch it. Whatever came over those peaks trailing sparks and intention arrived on the valley floor as ordinary weather. A storm-caller standing in Hollowmere could shout until she wept and the clouds would not so much as blink. She'd tested it. Oh, she'd tested it, that first terrible winter, standing hip-deep in the frozen shallows of the Merle with her arms flung wide and her voice raw, calling thunder the way her mother had taught her, the way her grandmother's grandmother had called it back through nine generations of women who spoke to storms like relatives.

Nothing. Silence. The rain fell straight down like any other rain, indifferent, and the wind blew wherever it pleased without asking her permission, and Elodie Dunmore — last of the storm-callers, heir to a songbook full of names that made grown wardens go pale — stood in the water and felt absolutely nothing.

She had cried, then. She would admit that much, privately, to the ceiling at night. She had cried for three days, and then she had gotten up, and bought a mending basket, and started a life.

Seven years now. Seven years of nets and turnips and Widow Pram's opinions. Seven years of being nobody, which was the entire point, and which she had come to regard as a kind of achievement, the way a man might take pride in a garden wall he'd built to keep out wolves — never mind that some mornings he woke up missing the sound of them.

* * *

"More tea?" she asked, though the pot was empty and they both knew it.

"There's none left in that pot," said Widow Pram, accepting a cup anyway, because refusing tea was against her religion. "You want to know what I think?"

"No," said Elodie, "but you're going to tell me."

"I think you're wasted here." The widow said it the way she said most things — bluntly, with onion breath, watching Elodie's face over the rim of her cup with eyes that were sharper than her knees let on. "Mending nets. A woman with hands like yours. You've got a surgeon's fingers, girl. Or a musician's."

"They're net-mender's fingers," Elodie said. "They've been doing it seven years."

"Mm." Widow Pram drank her empty tea with great ceremony. "That's what worries me. Seven years. Most folk pass through Hollowmere on their way somewhere else. You dug in like a tick. Like you're hiding from something."

"I'm hiding from conversation," Elodie said. "Which you are making very difficult."

The old woman laughed — a bark, really, like a seal with grievances — and levered herself up off the crate, joints popping in sequence like a slow round of applause. At the gate she paused, as she always paused, and looked back with an expression Elodie had never managed to fully decode. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. Widows who'd outlived everyone they loved tended to recognize the shape of a person keeping themselves small.

"Storm's coming," she said. "Won't cross the ridge, mind. Never does. But it'll be loud up there tonight. Might rattle your windows."

"It usually does," Elodie said.

"Aye." And then, quieter, almost to herself, in the tone of a woman saying a thing she'd decided long ago never to finish: "Funny thing, a storm that circles and circles and never comes in. Like a dog at a door."

She went off down the lane, complaining audibly to nobody about the price of onions, and Elodie sat alone with her half-mended net and the feeling behind her sternum, which had begun, faintly, to ache.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way afternoons in Hollowmere passed: slowly, usefully, and without incident. Elodie delivered the mended nets to Brannock at the fishery, who paid her in coin and trout and unsolicited theories about the king's tax collectors. She bought flour from the miller, who flirted with her in the manner of a man who had been flirting with the same six women for twenty years and had worn all the romance off it into something comfortable, like an old coat. She declined his invitation to the harvest dance with the practiced gentleness of long repetition, and he accepted the refusal with the practiced disappointment of a man who asked mostly to hear himself ask.

"You'll change your mind one day," he said, weighing her flour with great integrity, because the miller cheated on weights the way other men cheated at cards, except with her, always, inexplicably, he didn't.

"One day," she agreed, because it was easier than explaining that she wasn't waiting for anything. That the whole architecture of her life here — the little stone house at the end of the lane, the garden, the mending basket, the carefully cultivated reputation as a quiet woman of no particular history — was not a waiting room. It was the destination. She had walked a very long way to arrive at nowhere, and nowhere suited her fine.

Mostly.

On the walk home she stopped, as she did most evenings, at the footbridge over the Merle, and looked up.

The ridgeline stood black against a bruising sky. The storm had spent the whole day building itself, and now it hung over the western peaks like a city on fire seen from a safe distance — towers of cloud, walls of rain, and inside it all, flickering like thought, the lightning. Even from here she could feel its rhythm. Storms had heartbeats; hers used to match them. This one beat slow and heavy, a sleeping giant's pulse, and it pressed down against the ridge the way water presses against a dam.

It always did this. Two, three times a year, sometimes more in a bad spring. The storm would gather, and circle, and lean its weight on the mountains, and the mountains would shrug it off, and by morning the sky would be swept clean and the whole thing would be a story the villagers told about the weather, which was the only kind of story anyone told in Hollowmere, because stories with magic in them had gone out of fashion around the time magic had gone out of the valley.

*It won't come over,* she told herself, the way she told herself every time. *It can't. Nothing crosses.*

And it was true. Nothing crossed. Not weather, not wards, not curses, not the far-carrying whispers of the court beneath the mountain whose name she did not say even in the privacy of her own skull. The valley was deaf to all of it. That was why she'd chosen it — not chosen, exactly; she'd been walking for eleven months with frostbite on two toes and a false name stitched into her collar, and the valley had simply risen up around her like an answer — but chosen in the way that matters, in the staying.

Here, she was safe. Here, she was powerless. Those were the same sentence, and she had spent seven years learning to say them without flinching.

She stood on the footbridge until her hands went cold on the rail, watching the lightning stitch its slow patterns above the peaks, and caught herself doing the thing she never allowed: counting the strikes. One. Two. Three. A pause, longer. Four, five, close together — a stutter in the rhythm, like a speaker stumbling over a word.

Her grandmother would have read meaning into that. Her grandmother had read meaning into everything; the woman had once divined a marriage, a funeral, and a moderately good turnip harvest from the behavior of a chimney fire. *Lightning has grammar*, she used to say, thumping the table hard enough to make the inkwell jump. *Learn to read it or be illiterate in your own blood.*

Elodie had learned. She had been fluent by twelve. And now she stood on a bridge in a valley where the language was dead, reading the grammar of a storm she could never answer, and the fluency sat in her chest like a coin swallowed by accident — present, heavy, impossible to cough up.

"Stuttering," she said aloud, to the river, to nobody. "Just weather stuttering. Nothing more."

The river said nothing back. Rivers in Hollowmere never did. That was rather the point of the place.

* * *

Night came down cool and clear on the valley floor while the storm raged on above, visible from her bedroom window as a distant, silent fireworks display — silent, because the thunder couldn't cross either, or perhaps it simply chose not to, out of some courtesy Elodie had never decided whether to find comforting or insulting.

She lit her one candle — she kept bees, badly, and the wax was her own, lopsided and honey-scented — and sat by the window with the mending basket, because her hands wanted occupation and her mind wanted not to think, and the two requirements canceled each other nicely.

Below in the village, lights were going out one by one. The miller's. Brannock's. Last of all, as always, the church, where Father Aldous stayed up late over his accounts and his brandy in proportions he believed were secret. The valley settled into its dark like an old dog turning three times before lying down.

Above, the storm burned on.

Elodie worked a knot, and watched it, and let the loneliness rise up in her the way it did on nights like this — not the sharp loneliness of the early years, when she'd wake reaching for voices that weren't there, her sister's laugh, her mother's humming, the great rolling chord of a storm answering her call — but the duller, domesticated kind. Loneliness as furniture. Loneliness as a room she'd furnished herself and could move through blindfolded.

*This is peace*, she reminded herself. *This is what peace feels like. Quiet. Safe. Nobody knows your name here, and that is the gift.*

She was very good at reminding herself. She'd had practice. Some nights the reminder even worked.

Tonight, the storm stuttered again — five strikes, pause, five strikes, pause — a pattern too regular to be chance, too patient to be meaningless, and the ache behind her sternum turned over in its sleep and muttered something in a language she'd sworn off years ago.

"Nothing crosses," she told the window. "Nothing ever crosses."

The lightning flashed, and for half a heartbeat — she would tell herself later she imagined it, she would insist on it, she would defend the position with the ferocity of a woman defending the last load-bearing wall of her sanity — the whole ridgeline lit up edge to edge, and it looked less like a wall holding the storm out than like a seam.

Like something stitched shut.

Like something that could, given cause, be unpicked.

Elodie put down the mending, pinched out the candle, and lay in the dark listening to a thunder that could not reach her, telling herself the tightness in her chest was only the weather coming in her teeth, only an old habit, only nothing.

Outside, high on the peaks, the storm leaned its weight against the mountains — and held there, circling, circling, like a dog at a door.

It circled all night.

And just before dawn, for the first time in seven years, it began, very quietly, to rain on the near side of the ridge.
