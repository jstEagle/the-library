## 2. A Knock in the Rain

Elodie had forgotten what rain sounded like on her roof.

That was the thought that woke her — not fear, not the low animal dread she'd expected after a night of circling thunder, but something almost like delight. Rain on a roof made of shingles she herself had replaced three summers ago sounded nothing like rain on the slate roofs of Greyharbour, where she'd grown up. It was softer. Blunter. A friendly drumming rather than a needle-work, and underneath it the gutters going *gluck* and *gulp* as they remembered their trade.

She lay still for a long moment, cataloguing it the way other people catalogued birdsong.

Then she threw back the blanket, crossed the cold boards to the window, and looked out at the impossible.

It was raining in Hollowmere.

Not heavily. Not yet. A fine grey drizzle drifted down through the pines, catching the first light, and beyond it the mountains wore the storm like a crown they had finally decided to lower and try on for size. Water sheeted down the black rock of the ridgeline in bright threads. The peaks wept. And the valley, which had spent seven years refusing all weather more dramatic than a stubborn morning fog, simply lay there and let it happen, as if it too had woken up and decided that seven years of good behavior was quite enough.

"Right," Elodie said to the glass. Her breath fogged it. "That's not ominous at all."

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a kind of detached professional interest, the way she might watch a knot refuse to come loose. Seven years since magic had been able to touch her. Seven years since she'd felt so much as a tingle in her fingertips when the sky went heavy. Whatever was coming over the ridge, it wasn't coming for her power — the valley would see to that. Nothing crossed into Hollowmere whole. Storms unraveled at the boundary. Spells came apart like wet paper. Even the letter her sister had once tried to charm into flying here had arrived, eventually, by ordinary post, soggy and embarrassed and smelling faintly of scorched linen.

So whatever the storm wanted, it couldn't be her.

She repeated this to herself while she dressed, while she built up the fire from its banked embers, while she put the kettle on and stood watching it with her arms folded like a woman supervising a suspicious employee. It was an old trick of hers, talking herself down in the same flat tone she used for arguing with merchants. It worked about half the time. This morning it worked perhaps a third of the way, which she decided was enough to make porridge.

She was halfway through breakfast — sitting at her little table with her boots on, because if you were going to be unsettled you might as well be unsettled with your boots on, ready to do something about it — when she heard the sound that did not belong.

The rain had its rhythms. She'd already learned them again in the space of an hour: the steady patter, the gust-driven rattle against the shutters, the drip from the eaves. What she heard now cut across all of them. A knocking. Irregular. Small. Coming from the direction of her door.

Not hammering. Not the desperate fist of an adult in trouble. More like... a very polite rock being applied to wood, over and over, by someone who wasn't sure knocking was allowed.

Elodie sat very still.

There were eleven houses in Hollowmere proper, and hers stood apart from the rest, up the slope past the last fence post, because she had chosen it that way on purpose and paid for the privilege in firewood and goodwill. People came to her door for two reasons: net-mending and curiosity. Nobody mended nets in this weather, and nobody curious enough to climb her hill would knock like that. They'd bellow her name from the gate and complain about their boots.

The knock came again. *Tap. Tap-tap.* Then a pause, as though whoever it was had exhausted themselves with the effort and needed a rest.

She rose, took the poker from beside the hearth — not because she expected to need it, but because a woman walking to answer an unknown door empty-handed felt naked — and opened the door on the rain.

A boy stood on her step.

He was small. Eight years old, perhaps nine, though he had the reed-thinness of a child who'd missed a winter or two of meals, which always made them hard to age. He was soaked absolutely through, hair plastered flat to his skull, clothes that had once been fine and were now merely mud-coloured, and he was shivering in a deep, rolling way that started in his shoulders and shook him all the way down like a rope being snapped. His lips had gone the colour of old pewter. He looked up at her with eyes that were far too calm for a child in that state — dark, wide, unblinking — and he did not say a word.

"Hello," said Elodie, because someone had to.

The boy opened his mouth. Something came out of it — a sound, several sounds, quick and layered and utterly unlike speech as she knew it. It wasn't babble. Babble has a shapelessness to it, a toddler's cheerful chaos. This had structure. It rose and fell. It had phrases. If she'd had to swear to it, she'd have said it was a language, just not one built for human mouths; some of the sounds seemed to come from further down in his chest than a chest should reach.

"I'm sorry," she said slowly. "Again?"

He said it again. Patiently, as though *she* were the one being strange. The same sequence of sounds, note for note, with the same odd doubled quality — as if two voices were speaking slightly out of step inside one small throat.

Seven years in a valley where magic came to die had not cured Elodie Dunmore of recognizing it when it cleared its throat.

"Are your parents near?" she tried, kneeling down to his level, keeping her voice easy. "Up on the pass? Were you travelling?"

He tilted his head at her. Then, with great care, he raised one dripping hand and pointed — not at the pass, not down toward the village, but behind himself, back and up, toward the ridgeline. Toward the storm.

Something cold walked down Elodie's spine on small feet.

Nobody crossed the ridge in weather like this. Nobody crossed the ridge in *good* weather without a guide, a week's provisions, and a strong opinion about their own immortality. The pass was three days' hard walk from anywhere, and the boy looked like he hadn't eaten in three days either. There was no pack on his back. No adult behind him. No horse, no cart tracks in the mud, nothing but a child standing in the rain pointing at a mountain as if he'd strolled down off it for a visit.

"You're frozen," she said. Which was true, and easier to act on than any of the questions stacking up behind her teeth.

She stepped back and held the door wide. It was not a decision, exactly. Decisions took deliberation, weighing, the careful measuring of risk against self-interest that had kept her alive and hidden for seven years. This happened faster than any of that — somewhere below thinking, in the place where her hands moved before her head finished objecting. Later she would lie awake cataloguing every reason she should have shut the door: strangers bring attention, attention brings questions, questions bring the past scratching at the latch. Later she would score the moment like a ledger entry and find no line in the accounts that justified it.

But the ledger lost. Because the boy was eight, and grey-lipped, and shivering like a snared bird, and because when she stepped back and opened the door wider, warmth rolled out past her into the rain, and his face did something complicated — surprise first, then a kind of careful disbelief, the look of someone offered bread who had learned not to trust offers of bread.

Then he came in. Slowly, wiping his boots on her step with a thoroughness that would have made his mother proud, if anyone had taught him to do it, which someone clearly had.

* * *

Getting warm on a body is its own small drama. Elodie had done this dance before, years ago, with fishermen pulled half-dead from Greyharbour's winter swells, and her hands remembered even if her heart pretended not to. Clothes off, wrapped in wool. Fire close but not too close. Warm broth in small sips, because a frozen belly cramps if you fill it fast. He fought her on none of it, which worried her more than struggling would have. Children his size were supposed to protest. This one submitted to being undressed and bundled like a parcel, watching her the whole time with those grave dark eyes, and only when the blanket settled around his shoulders did his shivering begin, at last, to lose its grip.

He drank the broth in tiny, precise sips. Between sips, he hummed.

That was the thing she kept circling back to, washing the pot, feeding the fire, pretending to herself that she was busy and not staring. He hummed constantly — softly, barely audible under the rain, but ceaselessly, the way other children fidgeted. And it wasn't idle humming. Idle humming wanders. His never did. It moved through intervals with the certainty of water finding a channel, and every so often it split — she could hardly believe her ears — into two lines at once, a low drone underneath and a high thread above, harmonizing with itself.

Choir work. That was what it reminded her of, and the comparison arrived with an ache attached, because the last person she'd heard hold two lines like that had been the cantor at Greyharbour, a man with forty years' training and a beard you could hide a gull in. This child did it absently, between swallows of broth, the way other boys whistled.

"That's a fifth," she said, before she could stop herself. "What you're doing. Under the melody. That's proper counterpoint."

The humming stopped.

The silence it left behind was enormous. The boy turned and looked at her fully for the first time since the fire had taken him — really looked, head tipped, eyes narrowed slightly, like a craftsman examining unfamiliar joinery. And then, carefully, deliberately, he hummed a single note. He held it there, patient, and raised his eyebrows at her in unmistakable question.

She should have stayed quiet. Every instinct bred into her by seven careful years said stay quiet, be plain, be nobody, be a woman who mends nets and knows nothing worth knowing. She heard all of it, distantly, the way you hear rain on a roof.

"A," she said, and matched him.

His whole face changed. Not a smile, exactly — more like a lock turning. He shifted up a tone, waited. She followed. Up again. She followed again. They climbed the scale together, her voice rusty and rough from disuse, his clear as a bell struck underwater, until they ran out of ladder, and then — this was the part she would remember, later, when everything else about that day had gone strange — he dropped down two octaves and sang the root beneath her note, and the interval rang out in the little cottage, round and full and *right*, filling the room the way heat fills a room, and for three or four seconds Elodie Dunmore forgot to be afraid of anything at all.

Then the candle on the mantel — unlit since dawn, wick cold — guttered anyway. Once, twice, flame-less, in perfect time with the beat of the harmony. And went out.

They both looked at it.

The boy looked back at her and, very slowly, put one finger against his lips.

Every plan Elodie owned, every rule she'd lived by since the night she'd run — *stay small, stay dull, stay in the valley where nothing follows* — lined up in formation and told her what to do. Feed him, dry him, take him down to the village at first light, hand him to Widow Marsh and her committee of competent busybodies, wash her hands of the entire affair. Strange children were somebody else's mystery. Strangeness was the one cargo she had sworn never to carry again. The valley had bought her safety at a fair price and she had paid it without complaint for seven years, and the smart thing, the sane thing, the thing the woman who mended nets would do—

The boy yawned. An enormous, jaw-cracking, entirely ordinary yawn, and swayed where he sat, and then pitched gently sideways into the blankets with all the grace of a dropped puppet. Within four breaths he was asleep. One small hand stayed curled around the empty bowl.

Elodie stood over him for a long time, poker still in hand, having entirely forgotten she was holding it.

Outside, the rain thickened. Down in the village a dog began to bark at nothing, and stopped abruptly, mid-note, as if corrected. On the mantel, the dead candle sat in its dish of hardened wax, and she could not stop looking at it, because she had pinched that wick out herself that morning and she knew — she *knew*, the way a net-mender knows her knots — that it had not lit, had not flickered, had done nothing a candle does.

Except go out in time with a song.

"Well," Elodie said to the sleeping boy, to the rain, to the storm leaning its weight on the mountains, to no one. "You're not from the pass."

The boy slept on. And beneath the blanket, beneath the sound of the weather, so soft she had to hold her breath to catch it, he hummed on — even asleep, even now — a low, patient, waiting line of harmony, like something rehearsing.

Like a choir, tuning.

Like many voices, all of them his, all of them waiting for her answer.
