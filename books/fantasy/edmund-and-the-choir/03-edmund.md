## 3. Edmund

The boy stayed.

Elodie told herself it was temporary — a night, two at most, until the roads dried and someone came looking for him. Someone always came looking for children. Mothers, fathers, anxious aunts with lanterns. That was how the world worked in the stories, and she had no reason to believe Hollowmere operated on any other principle.

But the rain stopped on the third day, the mud firmed up on the fourth, and by the fifth morning she had stopped setting out an extra bowl at breakfast because he'd begun eating from her own without asking, and there seemed little point in pretending they were two households sharing a roof.

He was a quiet houseguest, which was fortunate, because he could not have been anything else. He made no demands beyond food and proximity. He followed her from room to room like a small grey shadow, sitting wherever she sat, watching whatever she watched with an attention so complete it was almost embarrassing. When she mended nets on the porch, he sat cross-legged beside her and studied the movement of her hands as though committing them to memory for some future examination. When she salted fish, he watched that too. When she went behind the shed to use the privy, she heard his footsteps stop politely just outside the door, and then resume the moment she emerged, faithful as a dog's shadow.

"You're going to have to go home eventually," she told him on the sixth day, mostly to hear what her own voice sounded like against his silence. "Wherever that is."

He looked up at her with those dark, unbothered eyes and hummed three notes — low, then lower, then a strange suspended fourth that made her back teeth ache — and returned to sorting buttons into piles by color, which was apparently the work he had assigned himself that morning.

"That's not an answer," she said.

It was, she was beginning to understand, the only kind of answer he gave.

* * *

She found the name by accident, or perhaps the name found her, which was a thing her grandmother used to say about storms and Elodie had never liked hearing applied to anything else.

His belt was the one piece of him that seemed to belong to a finer world than the rest of his clothing. The tunic was homespun gone soft with wear; the trousers had been let down twice by someone with more determination than skill. But the belt was good leather, oiled and supple, with a buckle of dull silver that had been carved — not stamped, *carved*, with a graver's patience — in letters of an alphabet Elodie didn't know and yet somehow recognized, the way you recognize a face from a dream after you've woken.

He wore the belt even in sleep. She'd noticed that the first night, when she'd wrestled him out of his soaked outer clothes and into one of her old shirts, and he had gripped the strap with both hands and would not release it until she'd given up and tucked him under the blanket fully armed, so to speak. Whatever that buckle was, it mattered.

On the seventh morning she found him at the hearth trying to scrub ash off his hands, and the belt lay coiled on the table where he'd set it while washing, and the morning light fell across the buckle at an angle that finally made the letters legible.

Four characters. Deep-cut, deliberate, worn smooth at the edges by years of a thumb worrying over them — his thumb, she realized, the way a child worries a lucky stone.

"E-d-m-u-n," she said slowly, sounding them out, though the sounds meant nothing. The last letter repeated the first. A doubled word, or a word that folded back on itself like a snake taking its own tail in its mouth. "Edmun. Edmund?"

The boy went very still.

Not frightened-still. It was something else — the stillness of a tuning fork struck by a note it has been waiting its whole life to hear. His head came around. His eyes fixed on her mouth as if he could see the name hanging in the air between them.

"Edmund," she said again, testing it. It was a solid name. A northern name, maybe, or older than north. It fit him the way a key fits a lock it was cut for: with a small, satisfying click.

He crossed the room in three steps, took her hand — her flour-dusted, net-mending, fish-gutting hand — and pressed it flat against the buckle. Then he looked up at her and nodded once, solemn as a magistrate.

"Well," said Elodie, who had spent seven days calling him *boy* and *you* and, once at dawn when he'd nearly fallen into the well, *for the love of all that dries*. "Edmund, then."

He smiled.

She would think about that smile later, in darker hours. Most children smile the way weather happens — suddenly, everywhere, without apparent cause. Edmund's smile arrived like a signature on a document. Like something witnessed. She had the odd, unshakeable feeling that she had just agreed to something, though the contract had been written in a language she couldn't read, on a medium she couldn't see, by a party roughly knee-high to her elbow.

He went back to his buttons. She stood in the middle of her kitchen holding a belt-buckle's worth of obligation and wondering, not for the first time since the rain brought him, exactly what had knocked on her door.

* * *

The sigils appeared on the ninth day.

She'd burned the evening fire down to a bed of white ash, and Edmund — who had been quiet all day in the way that preceded his strangest behavior — knelt at the hearthstone and began to draw.

He drew with one finger, slow and sure, no hesitation, no false starts. Spirals first: tight spirals opening outward like ferns unfurling, each one nested inside the next. Then lines connecting the spirals, thin and precise, and then small marks along the lines — ticks and dots and hooks — until the whole design covered three feet of hearthstone and looked less like a child's scribble than like a diagram of something. Weather, maybe. Or a throat. Or the inside of a thought.

Elodie stood in the doorway with a basket of mending she promptly forgot about, because her skin had gone cold all over, and the cold had nothing to do with the draft.

She knew those marks.

She knew them the way you know a song you've sworn never to sing again — not the words, but the shape of them in your mouth. Her grandmother's songbook had lived on the high shelf behind the loose brick for as long as Elodie could remember, wrapped in oilcloth, bound shut with a strap, and forbidden with a ferocity that had made it more interesting than any treasure. Elodie had looked exactly once, at fourteen, and had seen pages of notation unlike any music she'd learned at the village school: spirals and hooked lines and dotted measures, a grammar of storm written in ink that seemed, in certain lights, to move.

Her grandmother had caught her, taken the book down, and held Elodie's chin in a grip like a gull's beak.

"This is not for you," the old woman had said. "This is what called your mother into the mountains and your father into the ground. This is the family debt, girl, and debts are read by the people who owe them, not by children who are curious." And then, softer, in a voice Elodie had spent twenty years pretending not to remember: "When it's time, you'll know. Until then, be glad of the valley. Nothing follows you here."

Nothing followed you here. The valley ate magic at the ridgeline; every storm-caller knew it, every exile counted on it. That was why Elodie had come. That was why she slept soundly through thunder that broke apart on the peaks like waves on rocks.

And now a mute child was drawing her grandmother's forbidden notation in her fireplace ash, freehand, from memory, at eight years old.

"Edmund," she said carefully. "Where did you learn that?"

He looked up. He pointed — not at himself, not at the drawing, but at his own chest, dead center, and patted it twice. *In here.* Then he pointed at the ash spiral, then at her, then at the ceiling, as though connecting points on a map only he could see.

"That's not an answer either," she whispered.

* * *

That night the singing started.

She woke past midnight to a sound she took at first for wind in the chimney — a long sustained tone, high and clear, threading through the house. But the night was still. The storm on the peaks was doing its usual work of dying at the border, and the air in the valley hung motionless as held breath.

The sound was coming from the boy.

Edmund slept curled in the truckle bed she'd made up by the hearth, and from his open mouth poured a note that no eight-year-old throat should hold: steady, effortless, pitched somewhere above a boy's voice and below a bird's, and — this was the part that raised the hair on her arms — *harmonized*. Underneath the lead line ran a second voice, and beneath that a third, fainter, as though the single sleeping child were a choir compressed into one small body, all the parts singing at once, perfectly in tune.

Elodie sat up in bed and did not move for a long time.

Then she noticed the candles.

Three of them burned on the mantel — she kept them lit at night since his arrival, a habit she told herself was practical. As Edmund sang, the flames leaned. All three, together, leaning toward the boy like grass bending under wind that wasn't there. And when the melody turned — when the hidden choir beneath his voice shifted to a new phrase — the flames *turned with it*, swaying left, right, bowing low, rising tall, keeping time.

They weren't guttering randomly. They were dancing. The candles were following him like a congregation follows a cantor.

"No," Elodie said aloud, to the room, to herself, to twenty years of careful safe living. "No, no. Magic doesn't cross the ridge. Nothing crosses the ridge. That is the entire *point* of the ridge—"

From the truckle bed, the choir in the child's chest rose to a climax — a chord of impossible richness, three voices and five and seven, stacking like cloud upon cloud before lightning — and every candle flame stood straight up, rigid, quivering, straining toward the sound the way iron filings strain toward a lodestone.

Then the chord resolved. The flames relaxed. Edmund sighed in his sleep, rolled over, and began, softly, to snore — an ordinary, childish, slightly whistling snore that was somehow the most frightening sound Elodie had heard all night, because it was so completely normal, as though the thing that had just conducted fire in her kitchen happened every night and cost him nothing.

She got up. She crossed the cold floor. She stood over the sleeping boy and looked down at him for a long, long moment — the tangled hair, the belt clutched in one fist, the belt-buckle with its folded-back name catching the candlelight.

Magic didn't cross the ridgeline. Everyone knew that. Elodie had staked her whole quiet, careful, half-alive life on everyone knowing that.

But magic hadn't crossed the ridgeline tonight. Magic had walked down out of the storm on two small legs, soaked to the skin, and knocked on her door — and she had let it in, fed it soup, and named it.

Edmund stirred. Without waking, without opening his eyes, he reached up and found her hand in the dark and held it, the way he'd gripped the belt the first night: with the desperate certainty of someone who has already lost everything once and is checking, hourly, that the new things stay.

Elodie stood in the dark holding a sleeping child's hand and listening to the candles settle, and understood with terrible clarity that the valley had never been the sanctuary she'd believed. It was a dam. And something upstream, something vast and patient and owed, had just put a crack in it — a crack shaped exactly like a boy who sang in his sleep.

Outside, far above the valley, thunder rolled across the peaks.

For the first time in twenty years, it did not die at the ridgeline.

It waited there, patient as a creditor, like a knock about to fall.
