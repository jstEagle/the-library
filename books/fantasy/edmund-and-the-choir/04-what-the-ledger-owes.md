## 4. What the Ledger Owes

There are rules to being collateral, and I know all of them.

Rule one: a soul that has been entered into the Ledger is not lost. It is *held*. This is an important distinction, and the clerks beneath the mountain were very careful to explain it to me, because they are precise people and they dislike imprecision even more than they dislike hope.

Rule two: collateral may be inspected at any time.

Rule three: collateral does not get to say no.

I am getting ahead of myself. The woman with the storm-colored eyes says I should tell things in order, although she says it in words I do not understand yet, so perhaps she said something else entirely — perhaps she was only telling me where the spoons live. But I have decided she would want me to tell things in order, and so I will try, even though order is not how memory works inside me. Inside me everything sings at once, and you must pick out one line of the song and follow it, like following a single thread through a very large coat.

Here is the thread I have chosen:

*My mother's hands smelled of bread.*

That is where the story starts, for me. Not with the famine, which came before, and not with the men in grey, who came after, but with her hands, which were always dusted white to the wrist because we could still afford flour then, at the end, when everyone else had stopped being able to afford anything at all.

The famine lasted three years. I was five when it began and eight when it ended, which means the famine is most of what I know about being alive. I remember it the way other children remember a grandmother's house: warm details, wrong proportions. I remember that my shoes got too small twice. I remember that the well rope was replaced four times and each new rope was thinner than the last, because rope was made of hemp and hemp would not grow, and eventually the rope was made of braided hair, which everyone pretended was normal. I remember the sound the village made at night, which was not crying. Crying is loud. What we did was quieter than that. It was more like tuning. Two hundred people lying in the dark, each of us humming a low note to keep from hearing our own stomachs, and all the notes drifting toward each other until the whole village was one long gray chord.

I think that is why the Undercourt wanted voices, in the end. Not mine specifically. Voices generally. A famine makes a chord, if you let it go on long enough, and chords are currency down there. But I am ahead of myself again. Order. In order.

* * *

The men in grey arrived on a Tuesday, because paperwork prefers Tuesdays. There were three of them, and they did not knock; they simply stood in our doorway as though doorways were a thing that happened to them rather than a thing they used. They were tall in the way that furniture is tall — not impressive, just built to a specification. Their faces were pleasant the way a receipt is pleasant. One of them carried a lantern that glowed the color of a held breath.

"Madam Vess," said the tallest one, and my mother flinched, because nobody had called her by her married name since my father went into the hills and did not come back. "We're here regarding account number nine-and-forty-thousand-two-hundred-eleven."

"We don't owe anyone anything," my mother said. Her hands were behind her back, kneading nothing. She did this when she was frightened. Bread that needs no kneading. I watched the flour fall out of her fists onto the floor and I thought, strangely, that we would have to sweep.

"Not yet," agreed the man, "but your grandmother owed, and her mother before her, and debts of that vintage accrue interest the way old cheese accrues flavor. Slowly, and then all at once." He produced a ledger. It was black and fat and its pages turned themselves when he was not looking at them, which I found rude. "However. The Court is not without mercy. Mercy is, in fact, one of our most liquid assets."

He explained the terms while my mother stood very still. I will set them down here exactly as he said them, because I have them written somewhere safe now, in the place where I keep things, and exactness matters.

One: the village's famine would end. Rain would come. Seed would take. The wells would fill. This was not a gift; it was a service rendered against the account.

Two: payment would be rendered in kind. The Court dealt in voice and memory, and of these, voice was preferred, because memory spoils and voice keeps.

Three: the debtor might designate whose voice would serve.

My mother looked at me. I want to be fair to her, so I will write down what actually happened, which is that she looked at me for eleven seconds — I counted, because counting was the only power I had and I used it constantly — and then she looked away, and then she looked back, and her face did the thing faces do when two enormous feelings occupy the same room and refuse to acknowledge one another.

"Edmund," she said. "Go to the corner."

"No," I said.

"Edmund."

"I want to hear the terms," I said, which was a lie, because I had heard the terms, and the terms had a hole in them shaped exactly like me. "I'm part of the household. Households hear terms."

The tall man made a small sound that might have been approval. "The asset has a lawyerly temperament," he said. "That will serve him well below."

Below. Everyone in the village said *below* the way you say a word you've stepped in. Below was the Undercourt, which was not under our village specifically but under everything, the way roots are under everything, and like roots it fed on whatever the surface could spare.

My mother knelt down then, so that her eyes were level with mine, and she took my face in her floury hands, and she said the only honest sentence spoken that day: "They'll end it, Ed. Three years. Babies died last week. The Ferrow baby. You played with her brother."

I had played with her brother. His name was Tam and he could make a whistle out of grass and he had not smiled since the baby died, and I understood, in the way children understand large terrible things — completely, and without any of the equipment needed to do anything about them — that my mother was going to say yes.

"Will it hurt?" I asked.

"No," said the tall man, answering for her, because clerks cannot bear an unanswered question. "It doesn't hurt. That's what nobody believes. It's the opposite of hurt. You'll feel *tidy*."

And that is the truest thing anyone told me in those years. It did not hurt. When they took my voice — and they took it that same afternoon, there in our kitchen, with a pen and a small brass funnel and a form in triplicate — it felt like being tidied. Like a drawer that had been jammed full of me suddenly sliding shut. My voice came up and out of my chest and into the funnel, and it sounded like me, high and wobbly and always slightly flat on the descending notes, and the clerk listened to it the way a jeweler listens to a stone, and said, "Good tone. Slight flatness on the descent. We can work with that."

Then he wrote my name in the ledger, and the ledger ate the name, and I felt it go — a small swallowing sensation behind my ribs, like a stone dropped into a deep well that never quite reaches bottom.

My mother signed. Her hand shook so badly the signature looked like a seismograph of her heart, but it was legible, and legibility is all the law requires.

She hugged me afterward. She held on so hard I heard her ribs creak, and she whispered into my hair, "You'll come back to me. Collateral comes back. That's the whole point of collateral," and she said it like a prayer and like a spell and like a woman trying to convince a judge who wasn't present.

I believed her. That is the part I mind. Not the taking — the taking was tidy, and tidy is survivable. I mind that I believed her, because believing her is what made the years below bearable, and bearable things last longer than unbearable ones.

* * *

The choir of the indebted lives in a hall beneath the mountain that is shaped like the inside of a throat.

I do not mean this as poetry. It is literally throat-shaped, ribbed and warm and faintly damp, and when the Auditor walks the length of it his footsteps make a soft clicking that the acoustics turn into a heartbeat. Four hundred of us stand in the risers, arranged by register, and we sing.

What do we sing? Everything. That is the answer that sounds like a riddle but isn't. The Undercourt runs on harmony the way mills run on water. Our voices turn the great wheels of the place — the wheels that keep the ledgers open, the wheels that keep the doors sealed, the wheels that grind memory into ink. Every contract ever signed down there hums along to our singing. If we stopped, the Court would not collapse dramatically, with sparks and falling masonry. It would simply stop being true. Contracts would forget themselves. Debts would wake up unable to remember whom they belonged to. And so we sang, four hundred sold souls, and the terrible thing — the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I am telling now only because the woman with the storm eyes cannot understand my language and therefore cannot be harmed by it — the terrible thing is that some nights, the singing was beautiful.

Not always. Mostly it was work. Your voice is taken from you and given back to you the way a tool is issued: here, use this, and mind the output quotas. You sing your note and you watch the gauge and when the gauge fills you stop and your throat aches with someone else's music. But some nights the harmonies locked together in a way that had nothing to do with the gauges, and four hundred stolen voices would stop being inventory and start being a choir, and I would feel it in my chest where the hole was — a warmth, a fullness, a horrible borrowed joy — and I would hate it, and I would love it, and I would sing anyway, because rule three.

Rule three: collateral does not get to say no.

I made friends below, in the way that you make friends anywhere: by standing next to someone for a very long time. There was a woman named Odile who had been collateral for sixty years and had forgotten her own first name and had chosen a new one from a list of names she liked the shape of. There was a ferrywoman with one eye who lasted only a season before she ran — half her soul left behind in the ledger, still singing alto, and I knew her note so well that for years afterward, whenever the choir hit that particular interval, I felt her absence like a missing tooth. There was a boy named Pell who was collaterally my neighbor in the second sopranos and who taught me the game of counting things, which is how I learned to count, which is why I counted the eleven seconds in my kitchen, which is why I am the way I am.

Pell's voice was taken when he was six. He did not remember his mother's face but he remembered her laugh, and he asked me once — in the wordless way we spoke below, because speech belongs to the surface and we had none — whether a laugh counts as a voice or a memory. I did not know. I still do not know. I think about it more than is probably healthy.

Here is what I understood below, and what I understand better now, standing in a valley where magic dies at the ridgeline like a fire in the rain:

The Court does not think of itself as cruel. Cruelty requires wanting. The Court only wants balance — every debit matched, every entry closed, every soul exactly where the arithmetic says it should be. The clerks were not monsters. They were *accurate*. And accuracy, I have come to believe, is the most frightening thing there is, because you cannot appeal to it. A monster can be shamed. An equation cannot.

But equations have assumptions. And the assumption underneath my entry — the assumption the whole ledger rests on, the load-bearing wall of my entire existence — is this: *collateral stays put.*

* * *

Which brings me to the rain, and the ridgeline, and the mistake.

I did not escape. I want that recorded clearly, because escapes are brave and what I did was an accident, and I would rather be accurate than admirable. Accuracy is the Court's own weapon; I see no reason I shouldn't be allowed to hold it.

What happened was this: the famine ended, as promised. Rain came. Seed took. Wells filled. And three years later, on an audit day, when the clerks were busy reconciling a batch of contracts from a town that had sold its bells — a whole town, bells and all, and the silence coming off that paperwork was so loud it gave three of the altos nosebleeds — the hall door was left unsealed for the length of one verse.

One verse. Sixteen bars. I counted.

Pell looked at me. I looked at Pell. And I want to say we exchanged a look of daring, of conspiracy, of two souls seizing freedom — but the truth is we exchanged a look of pure animal panic, and then my legs decided things without consulting me, and I was through the door and into the service tunnels and up and up and up through passages that smelled of cold stone and older cold stone, and Pell did not come, because Pell's entry had a clause mine didn't, and I did not learn until much later what that clause cost him, and I do not write about it even now.

I came up out of the mountain into a night with weather in it, real weather, the first sky I had seen in three years, and I ran. I ran because running was the only verb available. I crossed fields and fords and a road that hummed strangely under my feet, and behind me — far behind, but not far enough — I heard the ledger notice. You feel it before you hear it: a tightening behind the sternum, like a hand closing gently around the handle of a drawer. *Asset unaccounted for,* said the tightening. *Recovery pending.* Somewhere below, a clerk dipped a pen.

And then I crossed a ridgeline, and everything changed.

Magic cannot follow me here. I did not know that when I chose the direction; I chose the direction because downhill is easier than uphill when your legs are eight years old and your soul has a hole in it. But the moment I dropped into this valley, the hand around the drawer-handle loosened. The tightening faded. The ledger's long arm, reaching after me, touched the valley air and — stopped. Curdled. Died on the vine. Whatever this place is, whatever old bargain or buried god or simple stubborn geology keeps it empty of the art, the Court's writ does not run here. I was, for the first time in three years, unwritten.

I fell down in a field of wet grass and laughed. It came out silent, of course — my voice was still in the ledger, still filed, still theirs — but my chest shook with it, and shaking was enough. Then I slept in the grass like a dropped doll.

The storm woke me. The first storm to cross the ridge in years, though I didn't know that then; I only knew that the sky above the valley had gone the color of the tall clerk's lantern, and that lightning walked the peaks like something pacing a fence it couldn't cross, and that I was cold and wet and hungry in the ordinary human way, which after three years below felt almost festive.

I followed the light. Not the lightning — the candlelight. One window, burning yellow in a house at the edge of a village, and smoke rising from its chimney, and under the smell of woodsmoke, faint but unmistakable, the smell of bread.

My mother's hands smelled of bread. I have already said this. I am saying it again because it is the reason I knocked, if knocking is what you call standing in the rain hammering on a stranger's door with both fists while your chest hums harmonies you cannot stop. She opened the door. She had storm-colored eyes and a face like a locked gate and she looked at me the way you look at a debt arriving at your own doorstep.

I could not tell her my name. My name was in the ledger. But it was carved on my belt-buckle, in letters I cannot read, letters from a tongue that died before my grandmother was born — *EDMUND* — and later she read them aloud, and the name fit me like a coat cut for someone slightly braver.

Here is what she does not know, what she cannot know, what I carry around inside me alongside four hundred stolen voices and one missing one:

They are coming. The hand around the drawer-handle has not let go; it has only paused, the way a clock pauses between ticks. Somewhere beyond the ridgeline a clerk has finished writing, and a tall figure in grey is reading what was written, and grey figures travel slowly and arrive completely. The valley hides me. The valley hides me the way a cupped hand hides a candle flame — perfectly, and only for as long as the hand stays cupped.

And I have begun to suspect — from the sigils I draw in the ash without meaning to, spirals that make the woman go quiet and still; from the way the candles gutter in time when I sing in my sleep; from the way magic, which cannot enter this valley, seems to have entered anyway, smuggled in inside my chest like contraband sewn into a coat lining — I have begun to suspect that I am not hiding from the storm.

I am the storm's forwarding address.

The woman is mending nets by the fire tonight, and I am sitting on the floor drawing spirals in the ash, and outside, far off past the ridgeline, thunder says something in its sleep. I count the seconds between the flash and the sound. Eleven seconds. Three miles. Close enough to matter. Far enough to prepare.

I do not know what preparing looks like. But I know rule four, the one I wrote myself, the one they never put in any ledger:

Collateral remembers what it is owed.
