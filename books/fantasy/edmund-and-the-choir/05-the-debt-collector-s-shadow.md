## 5. The Debt-Collector's Shadow

The trouble began, as most trouble in Hollowmere did, at the baker's.

Elodie had gone down for her weekly loaf and a small argument about the price of butter — a ritual she treasured more than she'd ever admit aloud — when she noticed that Widow Pratchett's cuckoo clock was running slow. Not broken. Slow. The little wooden bird emerged from his door with the weary dignity of a man who has been asked to work a double shift, and the chime that followed came a full three seconds after the hour, like an apology arriving late to its own funeral.

"Your clock's dying," Elodie said, because it was easier than saying what she actually thought, which was *your clock is afraid*.

"It started this morning." Widow Pratchett squinted at it. "Tam's watch too. And the church bell rang noon twice, once early and once late, and Reverend Cobbins says that's never happened in forty years, not even during the earthquake."

Elodie bought her bread. She did not buy the butter, because her hands had begun to shake and she didn't trust herself to hold out coins without dropping them.

She knew what made clocks run slow. Everyone from the old country knew, though nobody talked about it, the way nobody talks about the thing living under the stairs. Time went thick around certain kinds of power. Around collectors, specifically. Her grandmother had called it *the ledger's gravity* — debt so heavy it bent the hours around itself, dragging every tick toward the moment of collection.

She walked home fast, bread clutched to her chest like a shield, and found Edmund sitting on her doorstep drawing spirals in the dust with one deliberate finger.

"No," she said.

He looked up at her with those grave, dark eyes. He didn't ask what she meant, because he couldn't ask anything, but somehow he always seemed to know anyway. He pointed at the sky over the ridgeline, where the storm that never crossed had been gathering for two days now, and then he pointed at himself, and then he drew another spiral.

"I know," Elodie said. "I know exactly what you're telling me. I'm telling you no."

He considered this. Then he got up, took her hand with the matter-of-fact authority of a child leading a much larger person somewhere they ought already to have gone, and pulled her inside.

* * *

Inside, Edmund did something new. He'd been with her nearly six weeks now, long enough that she'd stopped flinching at the harmonies he hummed in his sleep, long enough that she'd caught herself buying him honey cakes and pretending they were for her. Six weeks of spirals in ash, of candles guttering in rhythm, of a boy who spoke only in a dead tongue and sang only in a language older than dead.

But he'd never done this: he climbed onto her chair, dragged her grandmother's forbidden songbook off the high shelf where she kept it wrapped in oilcloth, and set it on the table between them. Then he tapped the cover. Twice. Firmly. Like a lawyer tapping a contract.

"How do you even know what that is?" Elodie whispered.

Edmund opened the book to the page she'd never let herself read past — the third page, where her grandmother's handwriting turned cramped and frightened — and pressed his small finger against a sigil in the margin. A spiral within a spiral. The mark of the Undercourt. Then he touched his own chest, right over the sternum, where Elodie had long suspected something lived that wasn't entirely him.

And then, for the first time since he'd arrived soaked and silent on her doorstep, Edmund tried to speak to her directly. His mouth shaped words in that dead tongue, careful and formal, a child reciting something he'd been made to memorize. She couldn't understand any of it. But she understood the cadence. It was the cadence of a bill being presented. Of terms being read aloud.

Somewhere down in the village, a clock struck four, twenty minutes late.

"They've come for you," Elodie said. Her voice came out flat and strange, the way voices do when the body takes over speaking duties from the mind. "Haven't they."

Edmund nodded once. Then, with the terrible practicality that seemed to live in him alongside all those stolen voices, he closed the songbook, put it back on the shelf, and sat down at the table to wait, hands folded, like a child waiting to be collected after school.

Elodie stood in the middle of her kitchen and discovered that she was furious.

Not afraid — or rather, afraid underneath, the way a house is built on a cellar. But on top of the fear, filling every room, was fury. At whoever had sold this child. At whatever court beneath the mountain thought a soul could be entered into a ledger like a sack of grain. At herself, most of all, for six weeks of telling herself that the valley would keep him safe, that magic died at the ridgeline, that nothing could reach into Hollowmere and take what wasn't offered.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the whole rotten architecture of her exile. Nothing magical could cross into the valley. But a collector didn't need magic to walk down a road and knock on a door. A collector just needed an address.

And someone had given them one.

* * *

She saw the figure in grey the next morning, from the ridge path above the village, where she'd gone ostensibly to check her nets and actually to look at the place where the storm refused to cross.

It stood in the market square the way a fencepost stands in a field — upright, patient, belonging to no one. Tall. Too tall, in the way that you only notice second, the way your eye slides off it and then snags. Grey coat, grey hat, grey gloves, and a face that was perfectly ordinary and therefore completely wrong, the way a painted portrait of a stranger is wrong: features present, person absent.

Around it, the market went on. Fishmongers cried their wares. Children chased a dog. And every single one of them moved slightly too slowly, like figures wading through honey, while the figure in grey stood at normal speed and watched them with the mild interest of a man counting sheep.

Elodie's breath fogged the cold air. The storm above the ridge rumbled, low and frustrated, and for one mad half-second she felt it answer something in her chest — a pull, a hunger, the old family gift waking up and reaching toward the clouds like a dog hearing its name — before the valley's dead soil smothered it again. Even here, halfway up, the silence pressed down. Even here, she was only a woman.

The figure turned its head.

It shouldn't have been able to see her. It was a hundred yards away and she was standing in shadow. But its face came around with the smooth precision of a weathervane finding the wind, and across the whole distance of the square, across the slow-moving crowd and the thickened air, Elodie felt it look at her the way an auditor looks at a column of numbers that doesn't add up.

Then it raised one grey-gloved hand. Not a wave. A gesture of acknowledgment. *Noted. Filed.*

Elodie turned and walked home at exactly the speed of a woman with nothing to hide, which was the hardest thing she had ever done, including the night she'd left the old country with her name burned behind her.

* * *

"He asked about you," said Tam the cooper, catching her at her door that evening. He was breathing hard; he'd run up the hill. "The tall fellow. Grey coat. Came into the square this morning asking after — well." Tam shifted, uncomfortable. "He used a funny word. 'Asset,' he called it. Asked if anyone in the valley had taken in a stray lately. A child, come out of the storm."

"What did people say?"

"What do you think?" Tam grimaced. "Half of them said they hadn't seen any such child, which is true enough if you don't count the boy who's been buying honey cakes at Pratchett's stall every week and humming tunes that make the bees follow him. And half of them said nothing at all and looked at their boots. Hollowmere keeps its own counsel. Always has." He paused. "But Elodie — here's the thing that's got me. He thanked them. Politely as you please. Said he'd find the asset himself, that the valley would 'confirm the location in due course.' And then every clock in town lost another minute, all at once, like they'd all agreed on it."

"Tam—"

"I don't want to know," Tam said firmly. "That's why I'm telling you. Whatever it is you're mixed up in, I don't want to know, and neither does anyone else, and that's the favor we're doing you. But my girl Bess saw the boy singing to your candle flames through the window last winter, and Bess tells stories, and stories travel, and—" He stopped, jaw working. "You take care of that child, Elodie Dunmore. Or failing that, take care of yourself. Both, ideally. In whichever order gets you both out of this."

He thumped her doorframe once, the closest Hollowmere came to an embrace, and stumped back down the hill.

Elodie went inside.

Edmund was in bed, or lying in bed at least, eyes open, watching the ceiling. On the table, her grandmother's songbook lay open to the third page. She hadn't opened it. He must have. The boy who couldn't reach the shelf without a chair had gotten the book down and opened it and left it there for her, open, like a door.

She sat down. She looked at the sigil in the margin — spiral within spiral, the Undercourt's mark — and below it, in her grandmother's cramped hand, words she had never let herself read:

*They do not forget. They do not forgive. They wait, and the waiting bends time, and when the hour comes due they will walk into any valley, up any road, and knock.*

Below that, smaller, almost illegible:

*If you are reading this, Elodie, then the soil has failed, or the child has grown, or you have finally run out of running. Good. Running was never our gift. Storms were.*

Elodie laughed. It came out cracked and wet, but it was a laugh.

"You knew," she said to the dead woman through the page. "You absolute tyrant. You knew I'd end up here."

Edmund rolled over and looked at her.

"The valley can't keep you hidden anymore," she told him. "I think maybe it never could. I think the soil slows them down, muddies their ledgers, makes them guess — but they've guessed now. They've seen me. They know the address." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Every day we stay, the clocks lose another minute, and eventually the minutes run out, and some morning there'll be a knock at that door, and I'll be standing in a valley where I can't so much as raise a breeze, holding a bread knife."

Edmund sat up. He crossed the room — he crossed rooms quietly, always, a habit that broke her heart a little more each time she noticed it — and climbed onto the bench beside her. He studied the page upside down, then reached over and tapped the word *storms*.

"I can't," Elodie said. "Not here. You know that. You've *seen* that. The valley eats it. That's the whole point of the valley. That's why I came here — because here I'm nobody, and nobody is safe."

Edmund tapped the word again. Then he pointed at the window, at the ridgeline black against the stars, at the storm that had gathered there for two days and would gather for two more and would never, ever cross.

And Elodie understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming, what he was telling her. What he'd probably been trying to tell her for weeks, in spirals and harmonies and the patient grammar of a child who had learned that everything he loved got entered into ledgers.

The valley kept her safe by keeping her empty. And Edmund was going to be collected the moment the emptiness stopped being enough. Which meant the choice wasn't between staying and leaving. The choice was between leaving on her own feet, with her power waking ahead of her instead of behind her — or staying until the knock came, and watching them take him while she stood helpless in the one place in the world where helplessness was guaranteed.

"They'll be expecting me to run," she said slowly. "That's what runners do. My grandmother ran. My mother ran. Three generations of Dunmores, sprinting from that court with our skirts on fire." She looked at the sigil, at the spiral within the spiral. "So we won't run. We'll walk out the front, up the pass, in broad daylight, and we'll let the storm find me on the far side of the ridge. Let them watch us leave. Let them file it."

Edmund's hand found hers on the table. Small, cold, steady.

"And when they come to collect," Elodie said, and felt something old and electric uncoil in her chest, something that had slept six years in dead soil and was beginning, at last, to stretch, "they can explain the arrears to the weather."

Outside, far above the valley, the storm growled — and for the first time in six years, Elodie would have sworn it sounded less like a threat and more like a promise.

She packed that night. One bag. The songbook, wrapped in oilcloth. Honey cakes, because Edmund would worry about them otherwise, and she'd already learned that a boy who carried a choir in his chest still counted his comforts like any other child.

At dawn, they stood together at her door and looked down at Hollowmere one last time — the smoke rising from chimneys, the slow-ticking clock tower, Tam's workshop, Widow Pratchett's stall, the whole small warm machinery of a life she'd built out of hiding.

"Stay close," Elodie said. "And Edmund?"

He looked up.

"When we cross that ridge, things are going to get loud. Louder than you've ever heard me. Maybe louder than you've ever heard *yourself*." She took his hand. "Don't be afraid of it. It's just the truth, coming out."

Together, they started up the pass — while behind them, in the village below, every clock in Hollowmere quietly stopped.
