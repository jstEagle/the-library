## 6. Grandmother's Songbook

Edmund slept late the morning after the grey man came to the village, and Elodie let him. She sat at the kitchen table with her hands wrapped around a cooling cup of chicory coffee and watched the light crawl across the floorboards, and she thought about clocks.

Every clock in Hollowmere was running slow. She had checked three of them herself — the mantel clock, the church tower, old Widow Prue's grandfather clock that hadn't kept honest time since before the war — and all of them lagged by the same amount, as though the whole valley were wading through honey. It was the kind of detail her grandmother would have noticed first. Her grandmother noticed everything, mostly so she could be angry about it.

*The asset.* The word turned over in Elodie's mind like a stone in a stream bed, showing a different face each time it settled. The tall man in grey had used it casually, the way a farmer says *the heifer* or a merchant says *the shipment*. And he had been asking after a child of eight who hummed harmonies in his sleep.

She looked down the hall to where Edmund lay curled under the good quilt, his breathing even, his chest rising with that faint, impossible resonance she could feel through the floor if she stood in the right place. He was not an asset. He was a boy who liked his eggs scrambled soft and who arranged his shoes by the door with a precision that suggested someone had once punished him for less. He was a boy who had never once cried, which frightened her more than anything else about him.

The debt-collector had come within a day's walk of finding him. And the valley — the blessed, magic-dead valley where nothing worked and no storm ever crossed the ridge — was the only reason he hadn't.

Which meant Elodie Dunmore needed to know exactly what kind of magic could find a boy through a valley that ate magic for breakfast. And there was only one place in the world that knowledge might live.

It lived in a cedar box under her bed, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a knot she had not untied in eleven years.

* * *

Her grandmother had made her swear twice. Once when Elodie was nine, sitting on this same kitchen floor while the woman pointed one crooked finger at the box: *You don't open that until I'm dead, and you don't open it after either, if you've any sense.* And once more on her deathbed, gripping Elodie's wrist with a strength that surprised them both: *Swear it, girl. Some books are doors, and some doors are already open from the other side.*

Elodie had sworn. Then she had buried her grandmother, packed a single bag, and walked into the one valley on the map marked with a cartographer's shrug, and she had told herself for eleven years that exile was the same thing as keeping a promise.

She set the cup down. Her hands were steady, which struck her as deeply unfair. A person's hands ought to shake at a time like this. Instead they went about their business — lifting the box, working the knot loose, peeling back the oilcloth — with the calm competence of hands that mended nets for a living, as if this were simply another tear in the fabric of things to be stitched closed.

*Grandmother Ivy's Songbook*, said the cover, in letters that had been inked and then re-inked and then inked again over decades, some of the later strokes shaky in a way that made Elodie's throat hurt. The leather was cracked like drought earth. When she opened it, the smell rose up out of the pages — tallow smoke, rain, a ghost of the herb sachets her grandmother had stuffed between the leaves against silverfish. Lavender and wormwood. Home, in other words, or what had passed for it.

The early pages were what she expected: weather-charts, chord diagrams for calling wind, a recipe for drawing lightning into an iron rod without dying of it. Notes in the margins in two hands — her grandmother's sharp slant and her great-grandmother's rounder script, arguing politely across the years about whether a storm should be *invited* or *commanded*. (They had never resolved it. Both women died convinced.)

Elodie turned pages with growing confusion, because none of this explained Edmund. This was just craft. This was the family trade, laid out plain as carpentry. Where was the terrible secret worth two deathbed oaths?

She found it on page sixty-one, in the margin beside a song titled *For Calling the Rain to a Thirsty Field*.

The handwriting was her great-grandmother's, but the words were not hers alone. Someone else had written beneath them — smaller, cramped, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the next page. Elodie held the book close to the window light and read:

*Borrowed against, not given. The Court does not give. Three hundred souls of Hollowmere's dead to stand surety for the grain. Interest accrues in voices. First payment due upon the harvest that follows.*

Below that, in her grandmother's slant, added years later:

*Paid. See ledger-page 9. Mother sang for the famine-debt. It did not end her. It only taught the Court her daughter's name.*

Elodie sat very still. Somewhere outside a rooster crowed, and it sounded slow, dragged out like taffy, and she thought: *even the rooster*, and then thought nothing at all for a long moment because the margin had more to say.

There was a page — tucked loose between pages seventy and seventy-one, folded twice, soft as cloth from handling — that was not a page of the songbook at all. It was ledger-paper. Elodie knew this because the Undercourt's watermark showed through when she tilted it toward the light: a balance scale drawn in threads of grey, so fine it seemed less printed than grown into the paper.

On it, in a hand too regular to belong to any living person, was written:

*Dunmore account. Principal: one soul, male child, tendered by Maren-line debtor as surety against famine relief, per contract 4-11-hollow. Payments received:*
*— One voice, Agnes Dunmore, storm-caller, second generation. Received in full. Balance reduced.*
*— One voice, Ivy Dunmore, storm-caller, third generation. Partially received. Debtor departed jurisdiction before completion. Marked delinquent.*
*— Final payment pending: the fourth generation, or equivalent value.*

*Equivalent value*, Elodie read again. Her grandmother hadn't merely fled to Hollowmere. She'd hidden inside the one place the Court's instruments couldn't reach, and she had let the debt sit and grow like a seed in frozen ground, and she had died owing it, and now —

*Now,* Elodie thought, *there is a fourth generation asleep in my spare room.*

Except Edmund wasn't a Dunmore. He couldn't be. He'd appeared out of the rain with a belt-buckle and a chest full of stolen voices, and his soul belonged to the Undercourt by a different road entirely — sold by his own mother, he'd shown her that much in drawings, spirals and a thin woman and a set of scales.

Unless.

Elodie flipped back through the book, faster now, hunting margins. And there — page forty-four, beside a song *For Finding What Is Lost Across Water* — her grandmother's hand, dated the winter before Ivy's death:

*The Court has moved the collateral. They have taken a child's voice as payment on a stranger's famine-contract and stored it in the choir, and the child himself walks free, which the Court permits only when it intends to collect the vessel whole. They are patient. They will let him grow until his soul fills him out, and then they will send for him. If I am gone, Elodie will be the last of us. God forgive me, I left her where they cannot follow, and I left her blind, because a blind keeper cannot be bargained with.*

*I have done everything wrong,* the entry ended, the ink blotted as though a drop of something had fallen on it, *and I have done it out of love, which is how the worst things get done.*

Elodie put the book down on the table. Then she picked it up again immediately, because putting it down felt like letting go of her grandmother's wrist, and her grandmother was dead, and there was nothing left to hold except this.

*Blind.* So that was it. That was the shape of her whole life. Her grandmother hadn't just hidden Elodie in Hollowmere; she'd hidden the truth from her, carefully, completely, for eleven years of silence on the subject of the family trade. Every time young Elodie had asked why they never called storms here, why the ridge ate thunderclouds, why her grandmother flinched at the word *ledger*, she'd been told: *because we're finished with all that, girl. Because we're free.*

Three generations of storm-callers. Each one paying down the debt. Agnes, who sang until the Court took her voice and presumably much else. Ivy, who ran before the payment finished. And Elodie — Elodie was supposed to be the last payment. Not the escape from the story. The ending of it. Her grandmother had raised her like a coin being saved up in a jar.

And the worst part — the part that made her laugh once, a short ugly sound that startled her — was that it might have worked. If the Court hadn't taken a mute child's soul on a *different* contract and parked that child in the rain outside the one house in the world with a Dunmore in it. If fate hadn't delivered the exact instrument of collection to her doorstep and made her love him in under a week.

*They are patient,* her grandmother wrote. *They will let him grow.*

But they hadn't waited for him to grow, had they? A collector was in the village *now*, asking after *the asset*, slowing every clock in town. Something had changed. Either the Court had lost its patience or —

Elodie's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the draft from the window.

Or the debt had come due. Or her grandmother's flight had finally been flagged, the delinquency called in, the whole Dunmore account dragged out of whatever dark drawer it slept in — and the Court had come looking for its fourth generation and found, instead, a boy whose name was already in their book. Two birds. One net. *Equivalent value.*

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw stars. Eleven years. Eleven years of mending nets and keeping her head down and telling herself that peace was the same thing as safety, and the whole time she'd been living in the eye of a storm her family had spent three generations building.

"Elodie."

She nearly knocked the chicory over. Edmund stood in the hallway doorway, hair sleep-mussed, the good quilt dragging behind him like a molting bird. He must have padded out silent as ever. He looked at her face, then at the open book, then at her face again, and whatever he saw there made him cross the room without hurry and climb onto the chair beside her and simply lean against her arm, the way he did, weight and warmth and no demands.

"I'm all right," Elodie lied, automatically, and then stopped lying, because he hummed a single low note — the one he always used when he knew she wasn't — and the note vibrated gently against her ribs like a hand patting her back. "No. I'm not. I found out my family has been... running tabs, sweetheart. Terrible ones. For longer than I've been alive." She looked down at him. "Do you know what a tab is? Of course you do. Yours is apparently enormous."

Edmund studied the open ledger page with an expression far too old for his face. Then he reached out one small finger and touched the watermark — the little grey balance scale — and tapped it twice, deliberately, the way he tapped things he recognized.

"You know them," Elodie said quietly. "You know that mark."

He nodded. Then he did something new. He pulled the loose ledger sheet closer, smoothed it flat with both palms, and pointed, one by one, at the entries. At *Agnes Dunmore*. At *Ivy Dunmore*. And then, with a hesitation she felt in her teeth, at the last line — *final payment pending* — and then at himself. His own chest. His own nameless, borrowed, choir-filled heart.

"Yes," Elodie said. Her voice came out rough. "I think that's exactly right, and I hate it."

Edmund leaned his head against her shoulder. And then — softly, absently, the way other children whistle — he began to hum. Not one of his strange harmonies this time. A melody. Simple, rising and falling, four phrases long, and it took Elodie a moment to place it because she hadn't heard it since she was small enough to be carried: a lullaby. Her grandmother's lullaby. The one Ivy Dunmore used to sing on the porch when storms built up on the ridgeline and refused, year after year, to cross.

"How," Elodie whispered, "do you know *her* song?"

Edmund lifted his head. His eyes — calm, ancient, eight years old — went to the songbook, to the margins full of dead women's handwriting, and then back to Elodie. And he opened his mouth, which never happened, and shaped two silent words with careful, deliberate lips, reading them off some memory that wasn't entirely his own:

*Last payment.*

Then he closed his mouth, took her hand, and patted it twice, and Elodie understood, with a clarity that felt like the first cold drop of a coming storm, that her grandmother's oath had been a cage built of kindness, and that she had just, in the space of one morning, broken out of it — and that outside the cage, the collectors were already walking.

She looked at the songbook, at the margins where three generations of women had written themselves into a trap one careful line at a time.

"Well," she said to Edmund, to the book, to the slow-ticking clocks of Hollowmere generally, "we're going to need a plan."

Edmund hummed a note. It was, she realized, the first phrase of a storm-call.
