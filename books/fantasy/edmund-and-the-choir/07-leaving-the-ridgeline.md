## 7. Leaving the Ridgeline

Elodie woke before dawn on the day she meant to break the only promise that had ever kept her alive.

She lay still for a moment in the grey half-light, listening to the house. Edmund was asleep in the loft above her, his breathing slow and even, and beneath it—so faint now she could almost pretend she'd imagined it—the hum. It had been getting louder every night since the tall grey figure came to the village asking after "the asset." As if the boy's chest knew something the rest of them were still deciding.

She dressed in the dark, out of habit more than stealth, and put the kettle on anyway, because a person about to do something unforgivably foolish deserved at least one cup of tea first.

The songbook sat on the table where it had lived for three days now, open to the page with her grandmother's cramped marginal hand: *The debt passes down. The valley is not sanctuary. The valley is the cage they built for us.* Three generations of storm-callers, and hers had been supposed to be the last payment. Instead she had run here, to Hollowmere, where magic died at the ridgeline like frost at a window's edge, and had called it peace for eleven years.

"You're leaving."

Elodie didn't jump. She was past jumping. She turned and found Edmund standing at the foot of the loft ladder, wrapped in the blanket she'd woven the winter before last, his hair sticking up in four directions like a startled hedgehog. His eyes were very steady for a boy of eight.

"I was going to wake you," she said, which was a lie, and by the look on his face he knew it.

He crossed to the table, took the piece of chalk she kept by the hearth, and wrote on the slate in his careful, formal hand—the dead tongue first, then the common words beneath, because he had learned in three months what it had taken her a year to teach him: *You cannot go alone.*

"Watch me," Elodie said.

Edmund picked up the chalk again. *They will take me whether I am alone or not. They will take you too, once they learn what you are. It says so in your grandmother's book.*

"It says a lot of things in my grandmother's book." Elodie drank her tea too fast and burned her tongue, which felt appropriate. "It also says I'm supposed to walk into a mountain and pay a debt I never ran up, and I've decided I'm not going to do the second part without doing something about the first part, and if you repeat any of this back to me in that smug little slate-hand of yours I'll wash the slate."

Edmund set down the chalk. Then he did the thing that undid her, the thing he had done exactly twice before: he reached up and took her hand. His fingers were cold, always cold now, cold as the soil of the valley that kept him hidden.

*Together,* the slate said, when he finally wrote it. *Or I will follow you, and I am small, and I will be very annoying about it.*

Elodie looked at him for a long moment. Eleven years she had kept herself small and quiet and safe in this valley, mending nets and trading eggs and being no one, and it had taken a mute child three months to teach her that safety was just loneliness wearing a sensible shawl.

"Pack your own bag," she said. "And leave the blanket. We'll need room for food."

* *

The pass out of Hollowmere went north from the village, a sheep-track that became a goat-path that became, near the top, less of a path than an argument the mountain was having with itself. Elodie had walked it exactly once in eleven years, the week she arrived, and had turned back at the ridgeline with her hands shaking—not from the climb, but from the feeling of what waited beyond it.

Now she climbed with a sack of bread and hard cheese on her back, a storm-caller's iron bangle on her wrist that she hadn't worn since her grandmother died, and a boy who hummed as he walked, soft and constant, like a kettle that never quite boiled.

The morning was unnaturally clear. No wind came down from the peaks. The storm that had been gathering over the mountains for three days—the storm that should never have been able to cross the ridge into the valley—had built itself a wall of blue-black cloud along the crest, and it watched them climb the way cats watch birds.

Edmund stopped twice on the way up. Both times he crouched, pressed his palm flat against the dirt, and frowned, and both times Elodie pretended not to see, because she didn't want to talk about how the grass near his hand grew greener, or how the stones seemed to lean toward him slightly, like flowers toward sun. Magic couldn't follow him into the valley. But he brought his own, and it was pooling under his skin deeper every day, and the grey man's clocks had started running slow.

At midday they reached the saddle below the ridge—a bare shoulder of scree and wind-stunted pine—and Elodie called the halt even though Edmund couldn't have argued. She gave him cheese. He ate it solemnly, watching the wall of storm on the far side of the crest, and after a while he took out his slate.

*I have never been higher than your roof.*

"No," Elodie said. "I suppose you haven't."

*Is it true that magic dies at the ridgeline?*

"That's what they say." She looked up at the crest, a hundred feet above them, a thin dark line against the bruised cloud. "That's what I hoped, when I came here. That it would kill the thing in me before it killed anyone else." She hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. Eleven years of practice at not saying things, undone by an eight-year-old with a slate.

Edmund considered this. Then he wiped the slate clean and wrote, slowly, in his most careful letters:

*Magic does not die. It waits.*

"And you know this how?"

He tapped his own chest, once, right over the place where the choir lived.

Elodie finished the cheese. Her stomach had other opinions, but she overruled them. "Right," she said. "Well. Best not keep it waiting, then."

* *

The last hundred feet were the hardest climb Elodie had done in eleven years, and that included hauling the autumn nets. The scree shifted underfoot; the wind found them the moment they cleared the shelter of the saddle, tearing at their clothes with cold fingers. Edmund scrambled ahead of her, sure-footed as a goat, and she was half a dozen steps from the crest herself when it happened.

Her power woke.

There was no gentler word for it. Eleven years of dormancy ended not like a lamp being lit but like a dam failing. It came up through her boots, through her spine, through the roots of her teeth, and it screamed.

She went to her knees on the scree. Every hair on her arms stood upright. The iron bangle on her wrist went hot enough to brand, and somewhere inside her, in the hollow place where her gift had lain sleeping like a sword wrapped in oilcloth, something unfolded wings.

The storm on the far side of the ridge *answered*.

She heard it—felt it—as clearly as a shout across a lake. A hundred miles of charged air and rolling pressure, and all of it turned toward her at once, the way a compass needle turns, the way a dog turns at its name. The wall of black cloud along the crest bulged inward, toward the valley, straining against the boundary it had never been able to cross, and lightning walked inside it like eels in a barrel.

*Come*, said the storm, in the language she had learned before she learned to read. *Come out. Come home. We have waited.*

"Elodie!" She couldn't hear the word—Edmund had no voice—but she saw him shouting it, saw his mouth shape the syllables, saw him grab her arm with both hands. His touch grounded her the way a grounding-rod grounds a roof. The screaming in her bones settled from agony into merely enormous, a cathedral where there had only ever been a cottage.

"I'm all right," she gasped, and laughed, actually laughed, because she was crying and the two had gotten confused. "I'm all right. I'd forgotten—" She had forgotten what it felt like. Eleven years of numbness, of reaching for something that wasn't there and calling the absence peace. And now her whole body rang like a struck bell, and the air tasted of copper and rain, and she was *alive* in a way that made those eleven years feel like a very long nap taken in a burning house. "I'd forgotten what I was."

Edmund knelt in front of her. He looked frightened—for her, she realized, not of her—and then he looked past her, up, and his expression changed entirely.

His hum rose.

All these months in Hollowmere it had been a whisper, swallowed by the dead soil, audible only in the dark and the quiet. Now, past the ridgeline, in air that carried magic instead of strangling it, it *rose*. It filled the saddle. It rolled down the valley behind them and out over the slopes ahead, and it was not one voice anymore—it was many, it was dozens, it was a choir's worth of stolen voices braided into a single thread of sound coming out of a child's chest, harmonics stacked on harmonics, a chord that no throat could hold and yet was being held, patiently, desperately, a mile high and wide.

The storm stopped straining toward Elodie.

It listened to him instead.

Birds lifted from the pines below in a single startled sheet. Somewhere downslope a shepherd's dog began to bark. And far off—too far to see, but not, Elodie understood suddenly, too far to *hear*—something else turned toward that sound. Something tall and grey and patient, with lanterns full of begging voices, following a ledger written in a dead tongue.

Elodie got to her feet. Her legs held. The power in her did not settle—it circled, restless, delighted, a dog that had been shut indoors for a decade—but it held, and she held it, and that was enough.

"Well," she said. "That's torn it."

Edmund looked at her, and his hum softened but did not stop—it would not stop now, she understood, not ever again, not while he drew breath outside the valley—and he shrugged in the way of children who have said everything already.

"Yes, all right, you warned me." Elodie pulled the sack straight on her shoulders and looked north, past the crest, to where the land fell away in folds of pine and grey stone toward country she hadn't seen in eleven years. The storm wall loomed overhead, close enough now that she could smell the ozone bleeding off it, close enough that the fine hairs on her arms reached for it like a plant for light. It felt less like weather now and more like company. Like family she'd been writing letters to and never sending.

"We walk to the ferry road," she said, mostly to hear herself say it. "We find the river. And we stay ahead of whatever heard you just now, which—" she glanced at the boy, at the chord still thrumming faintly out of him, audible, she suspected, from a very great distance indeed, "—we won't, for long. So we'd best be quick, and luckier than either of us deserves."

Edmund picked up his slate one last time. He wrote slowly, and then he held it up so she could see it against the whole grey width of the sky:

*Which way?*

Elodie smiled. It felt strange on her face, that particular smile, like a coat that hadn't fit in years and then, suddenly, did.

"North," she said, and stepped over the ridgeline, out of the valley that had kept her powerless and safe, with a storm walking beside her and a choir singing behind her, into the world where everything she had buried was waiting patiently to begin again.

The thunder greeted her by name.

And three ridges away, in a town whose clocks had all begun running slow that same hour, a tall figure in grey set down its cup of tea, consulted a ledger whose pages turned themselves, and noted, in a hand like frost forming on glass:

*The asset has left the valley. The storm-caller has left the valley. Collection may proceed.*

It put on its hat.
