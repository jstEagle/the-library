## 8. The Ferryman's Fee

The world above the ridgeline was louder than Elodie remembered.

Not in any way that a city-dweller would notice — there were no crowds here, no market-day din — but the valley had been a hush for seven years, and now every sound arrived with its elbows out. Wind argued with itself in the pines. A woodpecker hammered somewhere off to the left like a creditor at a door. And beneath it all, faint as a pulse, Edmund hummed.

He hadn't stopped since they'd crossed the pass. He didn't seem able to. In Hollowmere the humming had been a secret thing, something he did into his pillow or against her shoulder, so quiet she'd sometimes wondered if she'd imagined it. Out here it carried. It moved through the morning air the way heat moves off stone, and twice already Elodie had seen birds lift out of the trees ahead of them and relocate, decisively, as though the tune had made the neighborhood undesirable.

"You're doing it again," she said.

Edmund looked up at her with the particular dignity of a child who has been told something obvious. *Obviously*, said the look. *That is rather the point of me.*

"Well, could you do it quieter? Or at least in a major key. You're going to have every crow in the province reporting our position."

He considered this, lips pursed, then shifted his hum upward by a third. It was somehow worse — brighter, more insistent, a thread of sound pulling taut through the forest. Elodie sighed and kept walking. There was no use telling him the truth, which was that after three days outside the valley she had stopped minding the noise entirely. It had become the sound of company. That was a dangerous thought, and she filed it away with her other dangerous thoughts, in the drawer where she kept things like *I hope he's warm enough* and *his hair needs washing* and *what if I just keep him*.

Three days out from Hollowmere, and they were lost.

She wouldn't have put it that way in front of the boy. She'd told him they were "taking the scenic route," which was technically true in the sense that all routes were scenic when you had no idea where you were. Her map — purchased from a peddler who had the shifty look of a man selling maps he'd drawn himself — showed a river crossing somewhere ahead, and beyond the river, according to Maren-theory, the road down toward the low country where the Undercourt's collectors kept their offices and their terrible ledgers. She had never met anyone who had actually seen the river. For all she knew the peddler had invented it along with the decorative sea serpents.

By late afternoon the trees thinned and the ground began, mercifully, to slope downward, and Elodie smelled water before she saw it. Then she heard it — not the burble of a stream but the broad, patient voice of a real river, moving a great deal of water a great deal of distance.

Edmund's humming cut off abruptly. He grabbed her sleeve.

"I know," Elodie said. "I feel it too."

It wasn't fear, exactly. It was more the sensation of walking into a room where someone had just been speaking about you. The air near rivers felt attentive. Magic pooled in moving water the way silt pooled in eddies — her grandmother had said that once, in one of the sayings Elodie had spent seven years trying to forget. She hadn't succeeded. Grandmother Vell's proverbs had a way of surfacing at inconvenient moments, like drowned bodies.

They came out of the trees onto a bank of grey shingle, and there was the river: wide, brown-green, moving fast, and spanned by nothing at all except —

"Oh," said Elodie.

A ferry was tied up at a crooked jetty. It was a flat-bottomed thing, scarred and patched, with a pole leaned against the rail and a lantern hanging from the stern post despite the hour. And standing at the rail, watching them come down the bank with the stillness of a heron watching a fish, was a woman.

She was hard to guess an age for. Weathered, grey-streaked, broad-shouldered, dressed in oiled canvas that had been oiled some years ago and had opinions about it since. An eyepatch covered her left eye. The right one was pale as riverwater and missed nothing, and it tracked them — first Elodie, then, lingeringly, Edmund — with an expression Elodie couldn't read at all.

"You'll be wanting the crossing," the woman said. Her voice was low and roughened, like a rope drawn through a block.

"If it can be managed," Elodie said carefully.

"It can be managed." The woman didn't move. "Question is whether you two ought to be managing it."

Elodie stopped at the top of the shingle, close enough to talk and far enough to run, an old habit from her exile that had recently become very relevant again. "Why's that?"

"Because," the woman said, "the boy is humming a choir-part I haven't heard in twenty years, and he's humming it flat, and somebody ought to tell him before the wrong people hear him and don't bother correcting his pitch first."

Beside Elodie, Edmund went very still. Then, slowly, deliberately, he lifted his chin and resumed humming — the same phrase, exactly as before, flat and all.

The woman barked out a laugh. It transformed her face entirely, cracking the weathered mask into something younger and sharper. "Ha! All right. Cheeky little ledger-breaker." She swung herself down from the deck to the jetty with an economy of motion that spoke of decades of practice, and looked at Elodie properly with her one bright eye. "Maren. Ferrywoman. Formerly of elsewhere. And you're either the stupidest woman on this side of the mountains or the bravest, coming down off the ridge with *that* on your back."

"You know what he is."

"I know what he *carries*." Maren's laugh was gone as quickly as it came. "There's a difference, girl, and the Undercourt has spent centuries making sure nobody learns it." She jerked her chin toward the ferry. "Crossing's free for children. Costs the woman a story. Get aboard before the light goes; the river turns ugly after dark, and so do the things that audit it."

* * *

The ferry moved under Maren's pole the way a boat moves when its captain has arguments with it daily and usually wins. Elodie sat amidships with Edmund pressed warm against her side, watching the far bank come closer with excruciating slowness, and waited for the question she knew was coming.

It took longer than she expected. Maren poled them through the main current, around a gravel bar, into the slack water near the far shore — and then, instead of tying off, let the boat drift in the eddy, and leaned on her pole, and said:

"Where'd you get the boy?"

"He came to my door," Elodie said. "In the rain."

"In the valley?"

"Yes."

Maren's single eye widened slightly. "Huh. Magic-dead ground. Seven years dead, that valley, maybe longer." She studied Edmund, who stared back with the unblinking composure he deployed on strangers. "And he walked in anyway. Through the ward-line. With everything he's carrying." She shook her head slowly. "Things aren't supposed to be able to do that. Dead ground doesn't open doors. Unless—" she squinted at Edmund— "unless the door wanted opening."

"I didn't ask him to explain himself," Elodie said. "He can't. Not in any language I speak."

"He speaks fine," Maren said. "Just not to you." She turned back to Edmund, and her voice changed — dropped into something lower, older, with a rhythm underneath it like a drumbeat remembered. She said four words in a tongue that made the air feel thick, and Elodie's arms prickled from wrist to shoulder, because she recognized the shape of it even if she couldn't parse it. Edmund had sung sounds like that in his sleep, the first nights in Hollowmere, before she'd learned to sleep through it.

Edmund answered. One word, soft, and then a small run of notes — half speech, half melody, the way he did everything.

Maren listened. Whatever she heard, it drained the last of the laughter out of her face. When she turned back to Elodie, her hand on the pole had gone white-knuckled.

"He says you sing to him," Maren said. "At night. When he dreams badly. You don't know the words, so you make them up, and he says your made-up words are better than the real ones, because the real ones are all about debts."

Elodie's throat tightened unexpectedly. "He told you that? Just now?"

"That and more." Maren pushed off from the eddy and finally, decisively, ran the ferry aground at the far bank, making fast to a rotting post with hands that trembled only slightly. "Come on. Off the boat. Both of you. We're done traveling today."

"We haven't agreed on terms—"

"The terms," Maren said, "are that I'm cooking, because neither of you can, and you're listening, because you need to hear it. Sit down."

* * *

She cooked the way she did everything — efficiently, without ceremony, producing from the ferry's lockers a pot, a fire-pan, dried fish, some root vegetables that had seen better seasons, and a twist of salt. Edmund watched the process with the rapt attention of a boy cataloguing techniques for later use, and by the time the stew was bubbling he had stationed himself at Maren's elbow as unofficial assistant, handing her things before she asked.

"Mm," Maren said, looking down at him. "He does that. Anticipates. Choir training. You learn to come in on time or the whole harmony falls over." She ladled stew into three tin bowls and handed them around, and for a while there was only eating, and the river talking to itself, and the small domestic sounds of a camp being made.

Then Maren set her bowl down, pulled off her eyepatch, and cleaned beneath it with her thumb, an old soldier's habit, unembarrassed.

"You want to take him into the Undercourt," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes," Elodie said.

"To sing him out of the ledger."

"If we can."

"You can't," Maren said flatly. "Nobody sings out of the ledger. I'm living proof." She tapped her own chest, once, with two fingers, and the sound it made was wrong — hollow in a way a chest shouldn't be, like knocking on a door with a room missing behind it. Elodie flinched. Edmund didn't. He leaned forward instead, eyes fixed on Maren with a terrible, recognizing tenderness.

"I sang in the choir," Maren said, to the fire more than to them. "Eleven years. My mother owed them — gambling debts, the ordinary kind, the kind that grow interest until the only currency left is the kind they deal in. They took my soul in payment. Well." She shrugged. "Most of it. I got away during a changing of the watch, during a storm, as it happens — which is why I perked up when I heard what you are, storm-caller, don't look surprised, the whole river valley knows there's weather following you two down off the ridge. Half a soul is enough to run a ferry and remember your mother's face. It isn't enough to remember songs all the way through. There's holes in me where the music used to go." She put the eyepatch back on, and her voice was steady, and Elodie understood suddenly how much steadiness cost. "So no. You can't sing him out. But there's a song that can unbind a contract — an old one, from before the Auditor closed the books. I know the first verse. Only the first. The rest they burned."

"Then we learn the first verse," Elodie said. "And find the rest where it lives."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. "You've no idea what you're saying."

"I have every idea. I grew up on Undercourt bargains. My family kept a ledger of their own." She heard the bitterness in her own voice and didn't try to sand it down. "I'm done letting courts decide what my family owes. I'll go down there and I'll make the ledger itself regret the day it opened."

For the first time, Maren looked uncertain — not afraid, exactly, but like a woman recalculating a crossing she'd thought she knew. Her eye went to Edmund. He had set his bowl aside and was looking between the two of them, back and forth, with the expression of a boy waiting for adults to arrive at the obvious.

"What's your fee?" Elodie said. "Ferrywomen charge. You said so yourself."

Maren was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere downstream a night bird called, and Edmund tilted his head at it, and hummed the call back — perfect pitch, perfectly returned, and the bird answered him, and Elodie decided not to think about that tonight.

"My fee isn't coin," Maren said at last. "Coin's no good to me. What's left of me doesn't want what money buys." She reached out and turned her bowl slowly on the ground, around and around, watching it spin. "When you go down there — and you will, you're both too stubborn to drown quietly — if you find the rest of me in that choir, you bring it out. My other half. Eleven years she sang next to me and I ran without her, and I have been half a woman on a river ever since. You carry her out with the boy's soul, or you carry me down with you, but either way, next time, nobody gets left behind in that hall."

"That's not a fee," Elodie said quietly. "That's a promise."

"They're the same thing," said Maren. "Everything worth charging for is. Ask the Undercourt; they've built a mountain on it."

Elodie looked at her — at the patched canvas coat, the empty socket, the hollow knock of her chest, the forty years of river written into her hands — and found that her eyes were stinging, which was absurd, because she'd known this woman for six hours. Three days, if she counted Edmund. Exile had shrunk her world down to a single room and she'd told herself it was peace, and now here were two strangers and a fire and stew, and it felt like something tearing gently open.

"You'll guide us in," Elodie said. "You know the roads, the tolls, the towns that sold their bells. And when we reach the choir hall, we get Edmund out, and your other half with him. On my family's name — and my family's name has been collateral long enough that it must still be worth something down there. Agreed?"

Maren put out her hand. Her grip was like a mooring line. "Agreed," she said. "One condition. The boy learns proper pitch. If I'm walking back into that hall, I'm not doing it to listen to flat thirds."

Edmund, hearing his name and understanding the tone if not the words, drew himself up with immense offense and produced — with visible, deliberate precision — the most beautiful, devastatingly accurate third Elodie had ever heard from a child, holding it while staring directly into Maren's remaining eye.

Maren blinked. Then she grinned, slow and crooked, the grin of a woman remembering what it was like to have a whole face.

"Cheat," she said fondly. "All right. Sleep. We leave at first light — and eat hearty at breakfast, because tomorrow night we make the town of Bellhollow, and nobody eats well in Bellhollow. It sold its bells to the Court, and the silence there has teeth." She banked the fire and lay back against her pack with the ease of a woman who could wake at a leaf falling. "And storm-caller —"

"Yes?"

"When we're close to the mountain, and your lightning starts answering you whether you call it or not —" Maren's voice drifted, almost sleepy, terribly casual — "remind me to teach you how to keep it quiet. It's a trick the choir taught me. It may be the only useful thing I brought out whole."

Elodie lay down with Edmund curled against her back, breathing in the rhythm of a lullaby in a dead tongue, and watched the stars wheel over a world that was suddenly, terrifyingly full of people again.

Tomorrow, Bellhollow. Tomorrow, silence with teeth.

But tonight, for the first time in seven years, she fell asleep before she finished worrying.
