## 9. Storm Practice

The meadow Maren chose for the lesson sat a half-mile above the river ford, wide and treeless, ringed by low granite hills that looked — Elodie thought grimly — like an audience assembled to watch someone make a fool of themselves.

"Good lightning ground," Maren said, planting her staff in the turf with the satisfaction of a woman choosing a seat at a tavern. "Nothing tall for it to jump at except you, and you're not worth striking anymore."

"Thank you," said Elodie dryly.

"Don't mention it." Maren settled onto a flat rock, produced a heel of bread from some bottomless pocket of her coat, and tore it in three. She handed a piece to Edmund, who was already wandering out into the grass, turning his face up toward the sky as if reading it.

The morning was heavy with cloud but nothing more — a grey lid stretched low over the hills, the kind of sky that had forgotten what it was for. Elodie stood in the middle of the meadow with her arms hanging awkwardly at her sides and felt like a woman asked to play a fiddle she hadn't touched since childhood. Which, she supposed, was exactly what this was. Except the fiddle was the weather and the music was lightning, and the last time she'd played badly, half a harbor town had burned.

"Right," she said. "How does one begin?"

Maren chewed her bread thoughtfully. "Well. How did your grandmother teach you?"

"My grandmother taught me by throwing me off a dock."

"Sounds about right for a Dunmore." Maren swallowed. "Fine. We'll be gentler. Close your eyes. Find the edge of yourself."

"That's very mystical."

"I'm a one-eyed ferrywoman who escaped a soul-court with half my being. Mystical is the least of what I am. Eyes closed."

Elodie closed them.

The meadow came alive in other ways. Wind moving through the grass in long slow strokes. The warmth of sun fighting through cloud on her cheeks. Somewhere behind her, Edmund humming — that impossible, layered hum of his, three or four notes braided together where any child would manage one.

"Now," Maren said, "you remember how it used to feel. Before the valley. When the storm answered you."

Elodie remembered. Of course she remembered; she'd spent seven years trying not to. The gathering pressure in the palms. The way the air went sweet and metallic before a strike, like licking a key. The sense — and this was the part she'd missed most, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before admitting it — of being *known*. The sky leaning down close, attentive, like an old friend across a table.

She reached for that feeling the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue.

And the cloud above them *shivered*.

"Oh," Elodie said, eyes flying open. "Oh, that was — did you feel that?"

"I felt it." Maren's voice had gone careful. "Do it less."

"It wasn't much—"

"Elodie." Maren pointed upward with the remains of her bread. Directly overhead, the grey lid had begun to churn, slowly, in a circle maybe forty feet across. It was not yet alarming. It was, however, rotating. "That took you four seconds. You've been dead seven years and it still answers like a dog hearing its name. So we're going to go slowly, because rusty power isn't weak power. Rusty power is strong power with no manners."

* * *

They started small. That was the theory, anyway.

"Call a breeze," Maren said. "One breeze. A polite breeze. Breeze-sized."

Elodie spread her hands, breathed, and pulled.

The gust that answered flattened the meadow grass in a ring around her, snapped Maren's coat out behind her like a flag, and sent her heel of bread rolling end over end into the hills.

Edmund chased it down without hurry and brought it back, brushing off the dirt, his face perfectly solemn. He held the bread out to Maren with both hands, the way one returns something sacred.

"Thank you," Maren said gravely, taking it. She looked at Elodie over the boy's head. "Smaller."

"I'm trying."

"You're *pushing*. There's a difference. Calling a storm isn't hauling on a rope, girl, it's asking a question. Your grandmother's line always understood that, from what I heard. The Undercourt kept records on your family, you know. I sang in that choir for thirty years and your name came up in the ledger-songs more than once. *Dunmore, Dunmore, the thunder's own daughter.*" Maren said it lightly, but Elodie felt the words land somewhere deep and cold.

"They sang about us?"

"The Court sings about everything it has claims on." Maren shrugged, as if that were nothing, as if it weren't horrifying. "Now. Ask, don't push. Like coaxing a cat down from a roof."

Elodie tried again. And again. And eleven times after that. Her breezes arrived like thrown doors. Her drizzles came down sideways. At one point she attempted, with great concentration, to raise a modest fog, and instead parted every cloud within a mile into a perfect blue circle above the meadow, which would have been impressive if it weren't the exact opposite of the assignment.

"Well," Maren conceded, squinting up through the hole in the sky, "that's a trick. Nobody wants it, but it's a trick."

By midday Elodie's hands had begun to shake.

She noticed it first when she reached for the water skin: a fine tremor, as if she'd carried something heavy all morning. Then her knees informed her they'd been negotiating terms with gravity and were losing. She sat down hard in the grass, breathing fast, sweat-soaked despite the cool air, and stared at her own fingers as though they belonged to a stranger.

That was the part no ballad ever mentioned. The calling didn't take from the sky. It took from *her*. Every bolt drawn down was paid for out of her own blood and breath; every storm summoned left her a little emptier, like a candle burning at both ends and in the middle too. Seven years in Hollowmere had let the debt lapse. Now the interest was coming due all at once.

"Eat," Maren said, pressing the water skin into her shaking hands. "You've been pouring yourself into the wind all morning and drinking nothing back."

"I'm fine."

"You're grey."

"I'm *fine*—"

A small hand appeared in front of her, holding a strip of dried apple.

Edmund crouched at her knee, watching her with those grave dark eyes, and waited until she took it. Then he sat down beside her in the grass, cross-legged, deliberate, and began to hum.

It was different this time. Not the wild braided harmonies he gave off in his sleep. This was simple, steady, patient — a low drone that rose and fell like breathing. And Elodie realized, with a strange lurch, that she could feel it doing something. Settling her pulse. Evening out the tremor in her hands, the way a stone dropped into rough water sends smooth rings outward.

"There it is," Maren murmured. "I hoped he'd do that."

Elodie looked up sharply. "You *hoped*? What is he doing to me?"

"Not to you. *For* you." Maren leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "In the Undercourt, the choir doesn't just sing pretty, girl. The choir holds the rhythm of the whole court — the engines, the ledgers, everything. Timing. Tempo. When a hundred souls sing in step, things stay bound. And that boy—" she nodded at Edmund, who hummed on, unbothered, "—that boy was raised inside that rhythm since before he could walk. He doesn't just know tempo. He *is* tempo. I've watched him tap his foot in perfect time with your heartbeat when he thinks you're asleep. Don't ask me how I know that. Ferrywoman's business."

"He conducts me," Elodie said slowly. The idea settled over her, strange and right. "Last night — when I called that little storm on the ridge road. It came cleaner than anything I've done in years, and he was standing behind me humming. I thought it was luck."

"He wasn't humming because he likes the rain." Maren allowed herself a thin smile. "Though he might."

* * *

The afternoon lesson went differently.

Maren had her stand again — "storms are rude and you must be ruder" — and this time Edmund positioned himself ten paces off, facing her, small and serious as a choirmaster before a difficult congregation. He raised one hand, considered, and let it fall in a slow downward stroke.

Elodie laughed. "He's actually conducting me."

"He absolutely is. Humor him. He knows what he's doing better than either of us."

So she humored him. She closed her eyes, found the edge of herself, and this time she followed his hand — rising when his palm rose, holding when his fist held, letting go when his fingers opened. And the difference was immediate and astonishing.

Where before the storm had come at her call like a horse bolting a gate, now it *breathed* with her. The wind rose in measured turns. The cloud thickened and thinned on the beat. When Edmund drew a small circle in the air with one finger, a little spiral of mist turned obediently around Elodie's wrist, cool as silk, and stayed there, spinning, until he flicked his hand and released it.

"Oh," Elodie breathed. "Oh, it's never done that. Not once. Not even when I was young."

She opened her eyes and found Edmund watching her, and his expression — his quiet, watchful, eight-year-old expression — broke into something she hadn't seen on him before. Pure, unguarded delight. He bounced once on his heels, both feet leaving the ground, and clapped his hands over his mouth as if he'd made a noise, which he hadn't.

"Yes," Elodie told him, laughing now, giddy and light-headed and not entirely sure it was all from the magic. "Yes, exactly that."

What followed was the happiest hour she could remember in seven years. Edmund led her through shapes — spirals and waves, a figure that might have been a bird, a long rolling swell that sent cloud-banks tumbling over the hills like sheep being driven home. She called a gust soft enough to lift the grass only, and he rewarded her with a solemn nod. She called rain — actual rain, gentle rain, falling straight down in a silver curtain over half the meadow while the sun broke through elsewhere — and Maren, soaked and grumbling from beneath her coat, admitted that it was "the first thing you've done today that wouldn't get anyone killed."

And underneath all of it, steady as a drumbeat, ran Edmund's hum, holding her together, keeping time with a heart that hammered joyfully in her chest.

She was so happy that she almost didn't notice the cost arriving.

It came quietly. First her vision doubled at the edges, then narrowed. The tremor returned and climbed past her wrists into her shoulders. Her legs went from tired to absent — she was standing, she knew she was standing, but the information seemed secondhand. The last shape Edmund drew for her was a simple arc, a bow of cloud from hilltop to hilltop, child's work, and she got halfway through it before the world tilted sideways like a ship in swell.

The arc collapsed. The rain stopped mid-drop, hanging glittering in the air for one absurd moment before falling all at once.

Elodie woke on her back in the wet grass with Edmund's anxious face above hers and Maren's hand pressed flat against her sternum.

"Breathe," Maren ordered. "Just breathe. You're not dying. You're empty, which feels the same but isn't."

"I'm—" Elodie's voice came out in tatters. "I'm all right. I just need—"

"A week in bed. Which you won't take, so you'll take an hour." Maren sat back on her heels and studied her with the one dark eye, and there was something in that eye Elodie didn't like — something old and sad and knowing. "You want to know why I really agreed to guide you into the mountain, girl? Not the promise. Well. Partly the promise."

"Why, then?"

Maren nodded at Edmund, who had taken Elodie's cold hand in both of his small ones and was holding it as though he could pour warmth into her by will alone. Perhaps he was. Her fingers were already steadier.

"Because I watched the choir burn itself hollow for that court. Voices spent like coin, bodies emptied to keep the harmony running. And I swore, walking out of that place with half a soul, that if I ever saw someone else being poured out for somebody else's ledger, I'd put my body between them if I could." She tapped Elodie's breastbone, gently, with two fingers. "You'll do. But hear me now, Dunmore, because this is the lesson and the lightning can wait: the Auditor *counts* on people like you. People who'd rather spend themselves than spend another. It builds entire contracts on that kindness. It will offer you exactly enough power to save everyone you love, priced at everything you are. And you'll want to pay. That's what makes it a trap."

Elodie lay in the cooling grass, under the mended sky, with a mute boy's hands around hers and the ghost of her grandmother's storms still fizzing faintly in her fingertips, and thought: *I have been in that valley seven years hiding from what I am, and it turns out what I am is currency.*

"Then teach me to be expensive," she said.

Maren barked a laugh. "Now that's the family spirit."

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of the southern hill, because Elodie's legs had graduated from absent to merely traitorous and Maren refused to march her further. While Edmund gathered firewood with the methodical thoroughness of a boy who had decided this was now his office, Maren cooked, and Elodie sat propped against her pack feeling like laundry that had been wrung out and hung up wrong.

But she couldn't stop smiling. That was the foolish thing. Every bone in her body ached and her hands still shook when she forgot to still them, and she kept smiling anyway, helplessly, because for one hour the sky had known her again. For one hour she had been a Dunmore in full, and it had felt like a door she thought was bricked-over swinging open onto light.

"You'll kill yourself at this rate," Maren observed, not unkindly, ladling out stew.

"No. You'll make sure I don't."

"Mm." Maren's eye glinted. "Confident in an old ferrywoman."

"Confident in the woman who promised to put her body between me and the ledger."

Edmund returned with his arms full of wood, fed the fire piece by piece, then settled at Elodie's side and leaned against her shoulder — casually, the way children do, as if it cost nothing and meant everything. After a moment he began to hum, very softly, the settling rhythm from the afternoon. Her tremor faded under it like frost in sun.

"You keep that up," Elodie murmured into his hair, "and I'll follow you anywhere."

He patted her arm twice, matter-of-fact, and kept humming.

It was nearly full dark when Maren, washing the pot in the stream, went suddenly still. Elodie saw it — the rigidity of the shoulders, the head cocked, listening with the one ear that mattered.

"What?"

"Town ahead of us. Over the next ridge, down in the valley." Maren rose slowly, drying her hands. "Kettlewick, it's called now. Used to be called Bellhaven."

"Used to be?"

"Mm." Maren came back to the fire and stood looking down into the flames, and her face in the firelight had gone flat and hard. "Sold its bells, the whole set, thirty years back. Great bronze things, cast for the church, blessed and everything. Traded them to the Undercourt for good harvests and full purses. Got both, too. Richest little town on this side of the mountains now." She paused. "Quietest, as well. You've never heard silence till you've heard a town that traded away its own voice. They post notices in the square, these days. Collectors do. Lists of names due for collection, posted regular as market prices."

Edmund's humming had stopped.

He was sitting very straight, looking at Maren across the flames, and in the firelight his eyes were enormous.

"When do we pass through?" Elodie asked slowly.

"Tomorrow, if we make an early start. There's no road around it worth the detour." Maren dropped onto her bedroll and pulled her coat over her like a blanket. "Sleep well, Dunmore. Tomorrow we visit a town that sold its bells."

Across the fire, Edmund lay down without a sound, turned his face away from them, and did not sleep for a very long time.
