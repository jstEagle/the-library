## 10. The Town That Sold Its Bells

Maren had a rule about quiet towns, and the rule was this: there was no such thing.

A town could be sleepy, sure. A town could be lazy, hungover, or lying low after a festival gone wrong — she'd seen all three, and had caused at least one of them. But a living town hummed. Dogs barked at nothing. Bakers swore at their ovens. Somebody was always arguing about the price of turnips.

Bellhollow did not hum. Bellhollow held its breath.

They came down off the high road just past midday, the three of them in single file on the narrow track — Maren first, because her one good eye had learned to read a road the way other folk read letters; Elodie behind her with her hood up and her hands jammed in her pockets like a woman trying to convince herself she wasn't afraid; and Edmund last, skipping every third step the way boys do when they've decided the world is still mostly made of games.

He stopped skipping the moment they passed the first house.

Maren felt it too, if she was honest. The silence came off the place like cold off a river stone. Not the silence of empty streets — there were people everywhere, sweeping doorsteps, hauling water, mending a wagon by the roadside — but a silence worn smooth, like a stone that had been in everyone's mouth so long nobody noticed the shape of it anymore.

"Well," Maren said, into that hush, and her voice fell flat as a coin dropped on cloth. "Cheerful spot."

Nobody answered. Nobody ever answered in Bellhollow, she'd learn. That was rather the point of it.

The town's name hadn't been an accident of geography. She remembered it from her own half-life, from the years after she'd clawed her way out of the Undercourt with her soul trailing behind her like a coat caught on a nail. Bellhollow had been famous once for its bells — great bronze-throated things cast in the mountain foundries, rung for weddings and funerals and the turning of the year, rung loud enough that travelers three valleys over knew where morning lived.

Then came the lean years, and then came the court beneath the mountain with its soft voice and its patient arithmetic, and Bellhollow had done what towns do when they are frightened and proud and offered a bargain: it had signed.

Prosperity in exchange for the bells. Every harvest full, every purse heavy, every roof un-leaked — and no sound above a certain register, ever again.

Maren had heard about it secondhand, back then, and laughed. Now she walked its streets and understood that the laughter had been the cheapest thing she'd ever spent.

* * *

They found the market square by following the absence of noise toward its center.

It should have been the loudest place in town — stalls and awnings, fish on ice, a fiddler working his way through something jaunty. Instead there were perhaps two dozen vendors standing at their goods like mourners at a graveside, conducting commerce entirely in gestures. A woman held up two fingers to indicate she wanted eggs. A man held up three to indicate that two was an insult. Neither said a word. Coins changed hands without clinking, which Maren hadn't known was possible until she watched it happen.

"Right," she muttered. "I've been to funerals merrier than this, and one of them was mine."

Elodie shot her a look. *Behave*, the look said.

"I am behaving," Maren said. "This is me behaved."

Edmund tugged her sleeve. He pointed past the market, toward the far side of the square, where a stone tower rose above the rooftops — a bell tower, unmistakably, with its louvered openings and its weathervane. But the tower stood empty. Through the louvers, where bells should have hung in their dark tiers, there was only sky, and the pale ghosts of rope-holes drilled into beams that had nothing left to hold.

The boy stared up at it for a long moment. Then he did something Maren would think about for years afterward.

He reached into his chest — not literally, not with his hands, but the gesture was there anyway, small fingers curling around something invisible below his breastbone — and he hummed. One low note, warm as bread, round as a bell's own tongue.

For half a heartbeat, the note rang true through the square, and Maren saw the whole town flinch — every head turning a fraction, every shoulder lifting, like dogs hearing a whistle they'd been told no longer existed. A baker dropped a loaf. An old woman pressed both hands flat over her heart.

Then Edmund let the note die, and the silence closed back over the square like water over a thrown stone, and everyone went back to their wordless buying and selling as if nothing had happened, only faster.

"Don't do that again," Maren said quietly, kneeling to his level. She kept her voice gentle. She also kept her hand on his shoulder, and she meant both things. "Not here. There are ears in this town that don't belong to the people wearing them. You sing here, boy, and somebody's ledger gets opened."

Edmund looked at her with those too-old eyes and nodded once, solemn as a judge. Then, because he was eight and solemnity could only be carried so far, he pointed at the baker's dropped loaf and raised his eyebrows in a way that clearly asked whether bread could be salvaged and, if so, by whom.

"Go on, then," Maren sighed. "But we're paying for it properly. I know how you get."

* * *

The notice board stood at the square's north edge, and it was the reason Maren's rule about quiet towns existed at all.

It was a fine board, better-made than most of the stalls. Freshly painted frame, glass front, a lock of dull grey metal that looked — Maren's stomach turned with recognition — like the same grey as the collector's coats. Inside, pinned in neat ranks, were notices printed on paper so white it hurt against all that hush.

She'd seen such boards before, in other towns, in her running years. Most boards listed debts the way any creditor would: sums, dates, terms. This one listed names.

**NOTICES OF INTENDED COLLECTION,** the header read, in a hand so even it might have been typeset by machine or by something that had never had a trembling day in its life. **THE FOLLOWING ACCOUNTS HAVE REACHED MATURITY. PAYMENT IS DUE AT THE CONVENIENCE OF THE COURT. RESISTANCE WILL BE ENTERED INTO THE RECORD.**

Below, in columns:

*ALDERMAN WEBB HOLLOWAY — three favors of the tongue, outstanding since the frost year.*

*MARGARET SOWTER — memory of her mother's kitchen, appraised and called in by her late husband's estate.*

*THOMAS QUILL — first-born laugh. Deferred twice. No further deferrals possible under any circumstances whatsoever.*

And there were more. A dozen names. Two dozen. Maren read them the way you read casualty lists after a battle you'd survived — hunting for faces she knew, telling herself she wouldn't find any, knowing she was lying.

Beside her, Elodie had gone very still. "Is this legal?" she asked, and her voice had the frayed edge Maren was coming to know — the sound of a woman who had spent seven years pretending to be someone who never asked questions like that.

"Legal," Maren repeated. She tapped the glass with one knuckle. "You're thinking of courts with judges, love. Judges who sit in rooms and answer to somebody. The Undercourt doesn't answer to anybody. It answers to the *ledger*, and the ledger doesn't care about legal, it cares about *owed*. This—" she gestured at the neat white notices, the tidy columns, the little brass lock keeping it all safe and official, "—this isn't menace. Menace is honest. This is bookkeeping."

"That's worse."

"Oh, considerably." Maren bared her teeth at the board, an old habit, left over from days when showing your teeth had occasionally helped. "Any fool can be frightened of a monster. Takes a special kind of dread to be frightened of a filing system."

Edmund had drifted closer to the glass. He was reading — of course he was reading; the boy read everything, street signs and grain prices and the margins of Maren's maps, hoarding words the way other children hoarded stones. His lips moved slightly, sounding out the formal phrasing, and Maren thought, distantly, that she should pull him away before—

Her eye found the bottom column. Third notice down.

**EDMUND —— (surname withheld pending settlement) — one soul entire, held as collateral against the famine-debt of Rowena —— (see annex). Asset last recorded: escaped custody, northern roads. Collection authorized. All finders assisting in recovery entitled to remission of personal debts not exceeding one item.**

There was a woodcut beside it. A poor likeness — the court's artists worked from records, not from life, and records don't catch the way a boy's whole face moves when he finds something funny — but the buckle was drawn carefully, precisely, the carved letters rendered exactly. **EDMUND**, it said, in the woodcut, in ink, in the court's immaculate hand, pinned up in a public square in broad daylight.

Maren moved without deciding to. Her arm went across Edmund's chest and turned him, gently but absolutely, away from the board, the way you'd turn a child from a carriage accident.

"Right," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "We've seen the sights. Lovely town. Terrible acoustics. Let's be somewhere else."

"What is it?" Elodie asked. "What did you see?"

"Nothing you want stamped in your memory, love, and nothing he needs in his."

"Maren."

"*What?*" It came out sharper than intended, and Edmund flinched against her arm, and Maren hated herself for it, briefly, efficiently, and moved on. She lowered her voice. "His name's on the board, Elodie. With a bounty clause. Half the desperate souls in this county could pay off their own debts just by pointing at him and holding out their hands. We need to cross this town fast, quiet, and preferably invisible, and we cannot do any of those things standing here discussing it."

Elodie's face did something complicated — fear and fury and that stubborn set of the jaw that Maren was learning meant trouble for whoever had caused it. She looked at the board. She looked at Edmund, who was watching them both with the terrible attentiveness of children who understand far more than the adults have agreed to admit.

"He can read," Elodie said quietly. "Can't he. He probably already—"

"Aye," Maren said. "He probably did."

* * *

They took supper in the taproom of the only inn, because leaving the same day you arrived was itself conspicuous, and conspicuous was the one thing they couldn't afford until dark.

The inn was called The Silent Chime, which Maren found either very brave or very sad, and possibly the whole town couldn't tell the difference anymore. The common room was full and utterly quiet. Men drank in the manner of people who have forgotten drinking is supposed to be enjoyable. A pair of merchants conducted an entire negotiation in nods. In the corner, an old man sat with a spoon and a row of glasses filled to different heights, and he touched them, softly, softly, making sounds more felt than heard, and nobody told him to stop, because that was as close to music as Bellhollow got now.

Edmund ate his stew and watched the old man with his spoon-glasses, and his foot tapped under the table, tapping rhythms into the floorboards where only Maren and Elodie could feel them, and Maren saw Elodie's hand drift to cover the boy's knee, stilling him, soothing, a motion she'd done a hundred times now without seeming to notice she'd learned it.

"He'd have liked bells," Elodie murmured. "I think. I never asked."

"You never asked because he can't answer," Maren said. "That's not the same as him not telling you. You've heard him sleep."

Elodie didn't say anything to that. Her thumb moved once across the boy's knee, and she looked into her cup like it owed her money.

Maren leaned back on the bench and let her good eye do a slow circuit of the room, because that was the arrangement — Elodie watched Edmund, and Maren watched everything else.

Everything else included, at the far end of the bar, a figure in grey.

He wasn't doing anything. That was the thing about collectors; they almost never had to do anything. He sat with a cup of water — never wine, Maren had noticed, never anything that might fog the instrument of him — and he wasn't reading, wasn't watching anyone in particular, simply sitting in the room the way a stamp sits on a letter. Present. Official. Making everything else in the room into correspondence.

Two farmers near the door got up within minutes of his arrival, leaving full cups. A maid stopped humming — Maren hadn't even known she'd been humming until she stopped — and went white, and scrubbed the same table for a quarter hour.

"He's not here for us," Maren said under her breath. "Probably. Collectors post the notices and then they sit. It's procedure. They let the town sweat for a few days — cheaper than chasing anyone, and the town does the work for them. Somebody on that list will walk themselves to the crossroads by week's end and wait to be collected, because the waiting's worse than the paying. That's the design. That's always the design."

"And if he's not here for the list?"

"Then he's here for us, and we're already dead, so finish your stew."

It came out darker than she meant it to. Maren was aware — had been aware, these last weeks, with a discomfort she refused to examine — that she had grown fond of these two. Fondness was a liability she had specifically forsworn sometime around losing half her soul, and yet here she was, sharing stew with a fugitive storm-caller and the single most wanted child on the court's books, and worse, *caring* how it ended.

Edmund finished his bowl, set down his spoon, and folded his hands. Then he looked at Maren with an expression of grave formality, reached into the collar of his shirt, and drew out his belt-buckle — the one relic of himself from before, which he wore now on a cord like a saint's medal — and laid it on the table between them.

**EDMUND.** The letters caught the lamplight.

He pointed at the buckle. Then he pointed toward the door, toward the square, toward the white notices in their locked glass case. Then he spread his small hands flat on the table, palms down, pressing them there as though holding something steady.

Maren looked at him for a long time.

"You know," she said finally. "You read it, and you understood it, and you're not crying, and you're not hiding, and you want me to know that you know."

Edmund nodded. Once. Solemn as a judge again.

"Well," Maren said, and rubbed her eye with the heel of her hand, and wished, not for the first time, that she had kept both of them, if only so she'd have a spare to cry out of. "That settles one thing, boy. When we get to that court — and we're going, make no mistake — you're not going as an asset being recovered. You're going as a debt coming *home to collect*. There's a difference. It's a difference they will hate."

Under the table, Edmund's foot began to tap again. And this time, softly, so softly only she and Elodie could hear it, under the tapping, under the town's great held breath, he started to hum — some small, defiant scrap of melody, three notes rising like the beginning of a bell being struck.

Outside, in the square, the collector in grey set down his cup of water and rose from his stool, and turned toward the inn, and began — unhurried, procedural, right on schedule — to walk toward the sound.
