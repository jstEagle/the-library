## 11. Voices in the Dark

The night they came for us was a night of no stars.

I knew it before I knew why. The dark above our little fire had a texture to it — a held-breath quality, like the pause between one verse and the next when even the most impatient choirmaster waits. Maren felt it too. She stopped chewing her supper mid-bite and turned her one eye toward the ridgeline as if she could read the darkness there the way Elodie read weather.

"Down," Maren said quietly. "Both of you. Now."

Elodie's hand found my shoulder before I understood the instruction, and she drew me down behind the overturned cart we had sheltered beside. Her palm was warm against my collarbone, which is where the voices live, and I felt them stir at her touch — forty-one small presences shifting in their sleep inside my chest, restless as birds before a storm.

I should explain about the collectors' lanterns, because everything that happened next happened because of them.

When the Undercourt sells a soul on credit — and nearly every soul in the ledger is on credit; the Auditor is patient the way rot is patient — the debt is not written only in the great book beneath the mountain. A copy travels with whoever comes to collect. It travels in a lantern. Not an ordinary lantern with an ordinary flame, but a cage of green glass in which the debtor's own voice burns. That is how the collectors always find their quarry: you cannot hide from your own song. It calls to its keeper the way a lost lamb calls to the fold.

And that is how I heard what no one else could hear that night.

* * *

They came out of the treeline in single file, tall grey figures moving without any sound at all, which frightened me more than sound would have. Everything alive makes some noise when it walks. Even Maren's old boots complained on gravel. But the collectors stepped over roots and through bracken as though the ground had agreed in advance not to notice them.

There were four. Each carried a lantern, and each lantern burned a different color of wrongness — a pale green, a bruised violet, a sickly yellow like milk left too long in the sun, and one that burned so white it hurt to look toward, though I looked anyway, because I am apparently built of poor decisions, as Maren likes to say.

Elodie rose up out of cover with her hands full of sky.

I have seen her call storms twice since we crossed the ridgeline, and both times it went badly in instructive ways — once she set a hayrick alight, and once she blew our laundry into a tree, where some of it remains to this day. But this time there was nothing clumsy in her at all. Perhaps fear does what practice cannot. The clouds that had been sulking all evening over the western hills came down off their hills like dogs called by name, and the first lightning bolt arrived so quickly after her gesture that it seemed less summoned than merely released from custody.

It struck the ground between the second and third collector. The earth leapt. The trees flinched. And the collectors — this is the part I want remembered correctly — did not scream or scatter like men do. They simply turned, all four together, in perfect unison, like figures in a music box, and began walking toward us again at exactly the same pace as before.

"They don't feel it," Maren snarled. She had her ferry-hook in both hands, which I had learned meant she expected conversation to fail shortly. "They're not men anymore, girl! They're *receipts!*"

But receipts can be scattered, it turns out. Elodie gave a shout — not words, just a note, a hard bright note like a struck bell — and the sky answered with three bolts in quick succession, and the collectors were thrown apart, tumbling like dropped puppets, their lanterns rolling free across the grass.

That was when the voices spoke to me.

* * *

You must understand: I have spent my whole remembered life carrying other people's songs. Forty-one voices ride in my chest, quiet mostly, murmurous, the way a house settles around its family. I know each of them now. There is a baker who hums while counting loaves that will never rise again. There is a fisherman whose song has waves in it. There are two children who sing rounds with each other because neither can bear the silence alone.

So when the nearest lantern cracked against a stone and its green flame guttered, I heard the voice inside it the way you would hear your own name spoken across a crowded market — instantly, unavoidably, *personally*.

*"Edmund."*

Not my name, precisely. They did not know my name. But they knew what I was — the collateral-child, the asset, the debt made flesh — and knowing that was enough.

*"Little vessel,"* said the voice, and it was a woman's voice, worn smooth as a river stone, *"little vessel, we see you. We are in the lanterns. We burn in the lanterns. Do you understand? They took our singing to make their finding-lights, and now we must lead them to whatever they hunt, forever, forever—"*

Her voice broke into something worse than crying, which is repetition.

*"We led them to a boy last spring,"* another voice said, younger, a man's. *"He hid in a root cellar. He begged us. He begged his own voice, do you hear, he knelt in the dirt and begged his own voice not to tell them where he was, and we told them anyway, because we are not allowed otherwise, because the flame does what the flame does—"*

*"Please,"* said the first voice, the river-stone woman. *"Please, little vessel. You are going under the mountain. Everyone knows it now; the whole road whispers it. When you stand in the choir hall, when you look upon the shelves — remember us. Remember the lantern-voices. We were people. We had names. Mine was Isolde—"* and here the flame flared so brightly that I had to shut my eyes, as if grief itself had caught fire. *"Mine was Isolde, and I have not been called by my name in sixty years."*

The lightning still hung in the air. The storm Elodie had called was circling overhead like a hawk deciding. And I lay behind an overturned cart with my hands pressed flat over my chest and forty-one voices pressing back, and I listened to the burning souls in the collectors' lanterns beg me — not for rescue, even. They were past hoping for rescue. They begged only to be *remembered*, which may be the smallest thing anyone can ask for and the hardest thing in the world to give honestly.

Because remembering costs something. I knew that already. I had been sold once, and sold means someone decided you were worth less than something else.

* * *

"Edmund!" Elodie's hand closed on my wrist. "We have to go — they're regrouping, look—"

She was right. In the smoking grass, the four grey shapes were standing up again, unbothered as furniture, and gathering their lanterns with those same synchronized movements. One of them had lost its hat, which sounds comic and was not; beneath the hat its head was smooth and blank as an egg, with no face at all except where a face had been suggested long ago and then erased for efficiency.

"We run," Maren said, hauling her pack onto her shoulder. "Ferry rule number three: never argue with a creditor after dark. Up, lad. Legs."

I did not get up.

I want to be honest about this, because everyone in this story keeps insisting on honesty as though it were a virtue rather than an inconvenience. I did not get up because getting up meant running, and running meant leaving them — the lanterns, the flames, Isolde and the man who had begged his own voice and the others whose names came pouring after theirs like water finding cracks. *"Tomas. Brigid. The Miller of Lowmarsh, who never got a proper name recorded, please remember him anyway."* Sixty years of names, and the ledger had struck them all but kept the debts.

"Edmund." Elodie crouched beside me. Rain was beginning to fall, fat and warm, the first drops of her own storm coming home to her. Her hair was wild with static and her eyes were very bright. "Sweetheart, we cannot fight them. This is not a fighting night."

I shook my head. Then, because silence is a habit I am trying to break, I used my mouth-voice — the broken, borrowed instrument I keep for emergencies — and forced out words in the living tongue, which come to me like stones dragged uphill:

"Not... running."

Maren swore with impressive variety for a woman of her years.

"Lad," she said, kneeling too, so that the three of us made a small stubborn island in the rain, "those things will follow us to the ends of the earth. That is literally their profession. If we sit here, they collect you tonight, and everything — the mountain, the choir, your mother — all of it dies in the cradle. Is that what you want?"

I looked at her. Then I took her hand and pressed it flat against my chest, over the place where the voices live, the way Elodie sometimes did without knowing why it comforted me.

Maren's eye widened. She heard them, faintly — I let her hear them, opened the door a crack, which I had never done for anyone before. Forty-one murmurs. And underneath them, arriving through the rain and the rolling green flames, the voices of the lanterns, still begging, still reciting names.

"Oh," Maren said softly. All the anger went out of her at once, the way air leaves bread. "Oh, you poor little fool. You're not refusing to run from *them*. You're refusing to run from *that*."

"The lanterns," I managed. "Are... people. Inside. Burning."

"I know what they are." Her jaw worked. "I sang beside some of those voices, before. Before I ran. Half a soul buys you half a memory of shame, but it's the loud half." She looked away, toward the collectors, who had finished righting themselves and stood now in a patient line at the edge of the firelight, waiting, because creditors are very good at waiting. "Running is correct, Edmund. Running is what survivors do. I have run for thirty years and I am extremely well-preserved."

But Elodie was looking at me. She had that expression she gets — the one like sunrise coming up over a decision.

"You want to take them," she said.

I nodded.

"You want us to carry the lanterns out. Free them somehow. Take the whole burning cargo of them down the mountain with us."

I nodded again, harder, and pressed both my hands over my chest where my forty-one slept, and tried to pour into my face everything I could not say: that I knew better than anyone what a borrowed voice suffers; that I had been a line in a ledger since before I could walk and I would not walk one step further over other people's names; that if I was going to descend into that place and demand my soul back, I intended to make as much noise about everyone else's souls as I possibly could.

"It's mad," Maren said.

"Yes," Elodie agreed.

"It will slow us. It will mark us. Every collector from here to the gate will feel those flames moving against the current of their purpose, and they will come, all of them, like iron filings to a magnet."

"Yes," Elodie said again, and she was almost smiling now, which is a thing about her that frightens strangers and comforts me enormously.

She stood up. She rolled her shoulders the way she does before calling weather, and the storm overhead gathered itself like an orchestra awaiting the downbeat. Then she walked — walked, calmly, in the rain, with lightning coiling around her fists like cats recognizing their keeper — straight out into the open grass, toward the four grey collectors.

"Gentlemen," she said, in her loudest court-voice, the one she uses on innkeepers who shortchange her, "I'll be taking those lanterns."

The collectors tilted their headless heads in unison. Somewhere inside them, machinery older than kingdoms considered the request. I felt the answer arrive in my chest before any of them moved, carried on the lantern-flames like heat:

*The assets belong to the Court.*

"Well, isn't that convenient," Elodie said, "because I've recently developed opinions about courts," and the sky came down like the lid of a piano.

* * *

I will record the fight briefly, because fights are brief and what surrounds them is not.

Elodie did not destroy the collectors — I do not believe they can be destroyed, being essentially paperwork given legs — but she did something better. She drove them. Bolt after bolt, herding those patient grey shapes backward and backward across the wet grass until they reached the treeline, and then one final strike split the oldest oak directly over their heads and brought half the forest down across their path, and paperwork, however diligent, requires some hours to excavate itself.

In the chaos of it, Maren and I ran the field collecting lanterns. Four of them. Four flames. Isolde's green one I carried myself, wrapped in my coat against my chest, where it trembled against my heart like a small frightened animal learning that not every hand that holds it means to cage it.

By the time the collectors dug free — and they did dig free, I heard them, patient as ever, beginning their pursuit — we were gone. Three figures and four stolen flames, walking east through the rain toward the mountain's mouth.

* * *

We did not stop until dawn. When we finally made camp in the hollow beneath a leaning rock, Elodie was grey-faced and shaking — storms cost her, they always cost her, and I have begun keeping a private tally of what this rescue is charging her body — and Maren was grumbling about blisters and philosophy in roughly equal measure.

I sat apart with the lanterns arranged in front of me in a careful row.

They had gone quiet, the flames. Burned low and steady now, like candles at a vigil. And one by one, as the sun came up pink over the eastern hills, the voices spoke again — not begging this time. Just speaking. Telling me their names properly, the way you introduce yourself to a new neighbor.

Isolde, who had been a weaver, and whose daughter had died believing her mother had abandoned her. Tomas, who had sold his voice for his village's well. The Miller of Lowmarsh. Brigid, who asked me twice whether the sky was as blue up here as she remembered, and cried — a flame can cry; I have witnessed it — when I nodded.

Forty-five now, if you counted the lanterns. Forty-five souls in my company, and only three of us with legs.

Elodie came and sat beside me. She did not ask questions. She simply looked at the row of flames, and then at me, and put her arm around my shoulders, and said, "Whatever you're planning, we're doing it, aren't we."

I leaned against her. Yes, I thought. Yes.

And far behind us, at the edge of hearing, patient as arithmetic, the collectors had resumed their march.
