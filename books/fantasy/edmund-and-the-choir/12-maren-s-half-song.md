## 12. Maren's Half-Song

The fire was Maren's doing, and she was smug about it.

"Three days on the road," she said, feeding the little blaze another branch, "and the storm-caller still can't light a match without threatening the county."

"I lit that tree on purpose," Elodie said.

"You lit that tree out of spite, which is different, and the tree did nothing to you."

"It was in my way."

Maren snorted and settled back against her pack. Above them the sky had gone the deep bruised blue of early evening, stars pricking through where the clouds thinned. Somewhere downslope a nightjar called its two-note question over and over, getting no answer, like a debt-collector knocking at an empty house. She knew the feeling.

Edmund sat cross-legged on the far side of the fire, turning a pinecone over in his hands with the grave attention of a scholar examining a relic. He'd been quiet all day — quieter than usual, which for Edmund was saying something — and twice Maren had caught him touching his own chest, flat-palmed, the way a man checks whether his purse is still there.

She knew that gesture too. She'd invented it.

"All right," she said, before she could talk herself out of it. "You've both been dancing around asking me, so I'll save us the embarrassment. You want to know what the Undercourt is. Properly. Not the fireside-tale version."

Elodie went very still. Firelight moved across her face and made her look younger and older at once, the way grief does. "I want to know what took half your soul," she said. "If you're willing."

*Willing.* There was a word. Maren turned her good eye toward the flames. The other one — the empty socket beneath her patch — had always ached in company, as if it missed being looked at. Tonight it ached more than usual, because tonight she was going to open the door she'd kept shut for eleven years, and doors, once opened, stayed open.

"My grandmother used to say," Maren began, "that every court has a currency. The king's court runs on land and bloodlines. The merchant courts run on coin. And the Undercourt—" she turned her hands palm-up, showing them the burn-scarred whorls across her fingers, old marks from a lantern she should never have touched— "the Undercourt runs on *accounting*. That's all it is. That's the horror of it. It isn't a dungeon. It isn't a hell. It's a *ledger* with a door."

Edmund set down his pinecone. He was watching her with those too-old eyes, and though he couldn't speak a word of any living tongue, she felt him listening the way other people listened with their whole bodies leaning forward.

"The mountain under which they built it wasn't hollow by accident," Maren went on. "It's a vault. Everything down there is filed. Voices in bottles. Memories in wax cylinders. Names written out so many times they wear grooves in the paper. When someone owes a debt they can't pay in gold — a famine bought off, a plague ended, a war unmade — the Undercourt pays it *for* them, and then it owns them. Not their bodies. Bodies are cheap. It owns the parts of them that were worth anything. Their voice. Their memory. Their name, sometimes, if the debt ran high enough."

"And the Auditor rules it," Elodie said quietly.

"The Auditor." Maren laughed, but there was no humor in it, only the tired sound of a woman stepping on an old bruise. "Aye. Here's the part the fireside tales get wrong. They'll tell you the Auditor is a demon, or a god of greed, some monster you could hate clean and simple. But I stood in front of it. I looked into its face before it took my eye as the fee for looking." She paused. "It was human once. That's what no one tells you. It was a man who owed so much, for so long, that paying became the whole of him. He paid away his face first — that's why it wears that smooth blankness now, like an egg, like something unfinished. Then his name. Then everything else, piece by piece, until the only thing left inside him was the *counting*. Now he doesn't rule the Undercourt out of cruelty. He rules it the way a scale holds balance. He can't stop. Stopping would mean admitting the sum of himself, and the sum is nothing."

The fire popped. Edmund had gone white around the mouth.

"How do you know all this?" Elodie asked. "You said you sang in the choir. But you know the Auditor's face. You know how it thinks."

"Because I didn't just sing in the choir." Maren pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, making herself small, making herself brave; the two gestures felt identical after enough years. "My village flooded when I was nineteen. The river took eleven people, my father among them, and the survivors did what desperate folk do — we went looking for a power big enough to undo it. We found the Undercourt. We asked it to give us back our dead."

Elodie's breath caught. "And it did?"

"It gave us back the *river's* dead. Every drowned soul in that valley, walking home wet and smiling. Eleven of ours among them. And the price was the whole village's voices, signed over in perpetuity, to be held as collateral against a debt that never shrinks, because interest in the Undercourt is measured in *song*." Her voice had gone flat and even, the voice of recitation, of terms read aloud. "I was the best singer in my village. They put me in the choir within the week. Two hundred of us, standing in niches in the dark, singing the harmony that keeps the ledger balanced. You don't sing because you want to. You sing because the song is the lock, and if the harmony falters, the debts come due all at once, and everyone in that mountain comes apart like wet paper."

"But you got out."

"I got half out." Maren touched her throat. "There's a moment, when they take a voice, when it's still leaving and not yet arrived. A gap. A rest between notes. My grandmother taught me a lullaby with a rest in it so long the neighbors thought the child had stopped breathing. I sang that rest at the moment of taking. I wedged myself into the silence." She shrugged, a small bitter motion. "Half my voice came away with me. The other half is in a bottle in a niche about four hundred paces past the gate, third row, marked with my village's seal. Some nights I can feel it singing without me. Like hearing your own laugh from another room."

Silence, except the fire and the nightjar. Elodie had put a hand over her mouth. Edmund had both hands pressed flat to his chest now, holding himself together, and Maren's heart cracked a little sideways, because she knew exactly what he was holding in.

"That's why I ran instead of fighting," she said. "Eleven years running. I told myself survival was enough. Then I heard about a boy in Hollowmere who hums harmonies no child should know, and I thought: either I help him, or I spend the rest of my life being the woman who escaped and saved no one. And I find I've run out of appetite for being her."

* * *

They sat with it a while. Good company, Maren had learned, knows when not to fill a silence.

It was Edmund who broke it. He crawled around the fire — he never walked anywhere if a crawl or a prowl would do, some animal habit the Undercourt had left in him — and knelt in front of Maren, and looked up at her with his head tilted, birdlike. Then he opened his mouth and sang three notes.

Low ones. Maren's own missing notes. The bottom third of her voice, the part that lived in the bottle, rendered perfectly in a child's throat.

Her whole body went cold and then hot. "*Where did you get that,*" she whispered, and hated how her hand shook.

Edmund pointed at his own chest. Then at her. Then he made a shape with his hands — cupped, rounded, a bottle-shape — and pressed it gently against her sternum, and tilted his head again, and this time the look in his eyes was unmistakable even across the gulf of language:

*I have it. I have yours too. I'm sorry.*

"You're carrying mine," Maren said. "In there. With all the others." She laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "Of course you are. Of course. The choir sings the collateral, and you're—" she stopped, recalculated, and the number that came out frightened her. "How many? How many voices do you have in you, boy?"

He held up his hands. Fingers spread. Then, slowly, he folded them down one at a time, and kept folding past ten, past twenty, lips moving in silent count, until Maren said "stop, stop, I believe you," and he let his hands fall.

Hundreds. Maybe more. A child-shaped cathedral of stolen voices, and every one of them his to carry and none of them his to keep.

Elodie reached across the fire and gripped Maren's wrist. "Then the counter-song. You said there was one. Tell us. All of it. Tonight."

"There's a reason I haven't sung it in eleven years," Maren said. "It's not forbidden — the Undercourt doesn't bother forbidding things, it just makes sure nobody has the pieces. The counter-song needs three parts, see. That's the beauty and the bastardry of it. One voice alone can't unbind anything; the ledger won't even notice. You need a *debtor* — someone the court holds a claim on, willing to stand in the hall and offer payment. You need a *vessel* — someone carrying stolen voices who can give each voice back its own melody. And you need a *storm* — because the ledger is written in ink that only runs when lightning strikes close enough to sweat the pages." She looked at Elodie. "You're the storm. He's the vessel. And I—" her mouth twisted— "I'm the debtor. Half a soul on their books. Which means I can walk into that hall and the ledger will recognize me, open itself to my line, and wait."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"If it goes wrong, the court collects all three of us on the spot, and the counter-song becomes just another entry." Maren fed the fire another branch, buying a moment. "But done right — done *together*, in time, like an instrument with three strings — it doesn't break the ledger by force. Nothing breaks that ledger by force; ask anyone who's tried, if you can find anyone who tried and still has a mouth. Done right, it does something worse. It makes the ledger *pay out*. Every contract it touches unravels backward. Debts return to debtors. Voices go home. The court has to honor its own arithmetic, because arithmetic is the only law it has left."

Edmund made a sound. Maren turned. He was drawing in the ash at the fire's edge — quick, sure strokes, spirals nested in spirals, and she recognized the grammar of it with a lurch: notation. Choir notation, but wrong, inverted, a mirror-script she'd only ever seen scratched on the walls of the niches by singers who'd gone mad in the dark.

"He's writing the harmony," she breathed. "The vessel's part. He already knows it."

"Of course he does," Elodie said softly. "It's his own melody, isn't it? The one they took apart to build him."

* * *

They worked until the moon crossed the ridge and started down the other side.

Maren taught the way her grandmother had taught her: one phrase at a time, sung slow, then slower, then slowed again until it stopped being music and became architecture. The debtor's line first — her line — a descending figure full of rests, each rest a place where the singer must *stop*, must leave a hole in the sound for the ledger to pour into. Edmund watched her mouth like a cat watches a bird, and when she faltered on the fourth phrase — the one that had taken her grandmother's whole village to learn — he hummed it back to her, complete, corrected, patient as a schoolmaster.

"Cheat," she said, wiping her eyes and pretending it was smoke.

Then the vessel's part, which Edmund already carried, and which needed only arranging: which voice to release first, which second, how to order the returning melodies so they braided instead of colliding. He conducted her through it with his hands, fierce and precise, and for a quarter hour Maren forgot she was teaching a child and simply obeyed her choirmaster, and it was the closest thing to joy she'd felt since before the flood.

Last, the storm's part. She gave Elodie the rhythm on a drum made from her water-flask and a rolled sock, because you couldn't sing lightning's line, you could only *keep* it — a pulse like a heartbeat climbing stairs, irregular on purpose, syncopated against the other two lines so that the ledger could never find the downbeat, never settle, never file them.

"Now all together," Maren said. "Quietly. Just a taste. If we can hold eight bars without falling apart, we might live through the real thing."

They held sixteen.

When the last note faded, the fire had burned strangely low and strangely bright, and the ash around Edmund's feet had arranged itself, without his touching it, into a single perfect spiral. The nightjar had gone silent. Even the wind seemed to be waiting.

Elodie stared at her own hands. "It felt like the valley," she said. "For a second. Like Hollowmere. Like something was holding me steady."

"That was him," Maren said, nodding at Edmund. "That's what a vessel does. Holds the tempo so the storm doesn't tear the song apart." She smiled, and found, to her surprise, that it was a real smile. "Congratulations. You're an instrument now, the three of you. A strange one. Out of tune and missing parts. But instruments can be mended."

Edmund yawned — a huge, cracking, eight-year-old yawn that shattered the solemnity like a dropped plate — and curled up against Elodie's side without ceremony, and was asleep between one breath and the next.

Maren banked the fire and lay back on her pack and did not sleep. She lay watching the stars wheel and thinking about a bottle in a niche, third row, four hundred paces past the gate, and about the fact that tomorrow they would reach the mountain's mouth, and the door there that took payment, and the memory she had already chosen to pay it with, and had not yet told them about.

Because that was the thing about confession, she thought, watching Edmund breathe. It wasn't a single act. It was a staircase. You told the truth in steps, and each step made the next one possible, and somewhere at the bottom of the stairs was the step you'd been avoiding all along.

Hers waited at the mountain's mouth. And when the door asked its price, she would pay it, and Elodie would understand at last why Maren had really agreed to guide them — not out of guilt, not out of redemption, but because the counter-song needed a debtor, and Maren had known from the very first night that the debt she intended to settle at that altar was not the Undercourt's.

It was her own.

Above the trees, far to the north, the peak they'd been approaching for days showed black against the stars — and along its flank, faint as breath on glass, a light was moving. Downward. Patient. Unhurried.

Coming to meet them.
