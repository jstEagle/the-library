## 13. The Mountain's Mouth

The mountain did not look like a place where souls were kept in bottles. It looked like a mountain — grey-shouldered, snow-capped in a way that seemed almost smug about it, wearing its glaciers like a magistrate wears his chain of office. Elodie had expected something worse. She had spent the last three days of the walk expecting something worse: a black crag, a wound in the earth, a peak that wept ash. Instead they had crested the final ridge at midmorning to find the thing sitting there under a pleasant blue sky, round and patient as a sleeping ox.

"That's it?" she said.

"That's it," said Maren.

"It looks *friendly*."

"Don't let it fool you. The Undercourt never built anything ugly on purpose. Ugly warns you off. Pretty invites you in." Maren spat, with the practiced economy of a woman who had been spitting at institutions her whole life. "Whole court's like that. Marble floors. Warm light. They'll offer you a chair."

Edmund tugged Elodie's sleeve and pointed down the scree slope, where a road — an actual road, cobbled, well-maintained, with little iron posts along the edges painted a cheerful green — wound from the tree line toward a cleft in the mountain's base. Along the posts hung small brass bells, and not one of them moved, though there was wind enough to stir the grass.

"They don't ring," Elodie said.

"Nothing rings here," Maren said. "You'll notice that. It's the first thing they take, in a way. Sound belongs to them. Everything you hear inside those walls, you hear because they permit it." She adjusted the strap of her pack and started down the slope without waiting for agreement, which was how Maren did most things. "Come on, then. Better to arrive before dark. The door keeps different hours after sundown, and none of them are good."

* * *

They walked the last mile in a silence that was not companionable but imposed, the way a library imposes silence, or a sickroom. Elodie found herself whispering even though no one had told her to. Edmund walked between them, holding one hand of each — he had taken up this habit somewhere on the road and neither woman had discouraged it — and his thumb traced slow circles against Elodie's knuckles, a rhythm she'd come to recognize as his version of humming when humming wasn't safe.

She was trying very hard not to think about what she was going to pay.

Maren had told them, two nights ago, by firelight, in the flat voice she used for truths too heavy for decoration: *the door takes a toll. Not coin. Coin means nothing down there. It takes a piece of you — a memory, usually. Something true. You choose what to give, and the door judges whether your choice is honest enough.* She had said it while sharpening her knife, which she claimed was habit, though Elodie suspected it was theater, and useful theater at that.

Elodie had spent every waking hour since taking inventory.

It was a horrible exercise, inventorying your own memories for spendable goods. She lay awake at night turning them over like a miser counting coins, asking of each one: *is this necessary? Could I live without this? Would I be less myself?* Her grandmother's kitchen, smelling of burnt honey — necessary; that was where the songbook lived in her mind, and she'd need every scrap of it below. The first storm she'd ever called, at nine years old, so small a thing it only rattled the shutters, and her father lifting her onto his shoulders anyway, shouting *there she is, there's my weather-witch* — no. Absolutely not. That one was load-bearing. Her mother's hands teaching hers the knot for mending nets. The smell of Hollowmere after rain. Edmund asleep against her side on the ferry, his weight warm and absolute, trusting her with the whole of himself.

And then, inevitably, always, arriving last because she saved it for last the way you save the worst letter for the bottom of the pile:

Her sister.

Wren. Three years older, quick as a wren too, all elbows and laughter, who had taught her to swim in the mill pond by the simple method of pushing her in. Who had died at fourteen of a fever that Elodie, already showing her gift, had been certain — *certain*, with the terrible certainty of a ten-year-old — she could have stormed away if anyone had listened to her. Who was the reason Elodie had learned to call lightning in the first place: grief looking for a lever.

Every memory of Wren was welded to every other memory of Wren. There was no picking up one stone without moving the whole cairn. If the door took Wren's face, it would take the fever, and the pond, and the laughing, and the guilt, and the reason, and the girl Elodie had been before any of it.

Which was, she supposed, exactly why the door would find the payment honest.

"You've gone quiet," Maren said. "Quieter than the local policy requires."

"I'm deciding."

"Mm." Maren walked a few steps. "Don't decide cheap. But don't decide grand, either. The door doesn't want your grandest. It wants your truest. People try to impress it — offer up their war memories, their great tragedies, thinking bigger is better. Door doesn't care about big. Door cares about *real*. Give it something real and it'll open. Give it something performed and you'll stand out there till you die."

"How comforting."

"I'm a comfort," Maren agreed. "That's why they pay me the big money." Nobody paid her any money. This was also part of the joke, and had been since the ferry, and Elodie laughed anyway, because the alternative was being sick on the cheerful green posts.

* * *

The gate was nothing like a gate.

It stood at the end of the cobbled road where the mountain split itself — a doorway perhaps twenty feet high, framed in white stone so smooth it looked poured rather than cut, and set within the frame was a door of pale wood, plain as a farmhouse door, with a latch of dull silver. Before it stretched a courtyard of the same white stone, empty except for a single bench, as if the architects had thoughtfully provided seating for people waiting to be robbed.

Above the lintel, carved deep and filled with old gold, ran a line of script Elodie couldn't read. Edmund could. He stopped dead in the courtyard, and his grip on her hand went tight enough to hurt.

"What does it say?" she asked.

He didn't answer with words — he never did — but he drew the shape of it in the air with his free hand, and then, when that wasn't enough, he crouched and wrote it in the dust with his finger. The letters were the spirals-and-strokes of the sigils from her grandmother's book, the dead tongue, and Elodie read them slowly, sounding them out the way you sound out a language you almost know:

**PAY WHAT YOU CANNOT SPARE.**

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's vile."

"That's the Undercourt," said Maren. "They're not subtle. They've just never needed to be." She set her pack down on the bench — *see, the bench has a use* — and rolled her shoulders. "Now. Rules, before anyone touches anything. One: you pay alone. The door deals with one soul at a time. Two: you can't pay for someone else. Don't try. It knows. Three—" she looked at Edmund, and her voice, which never softened so much as changed materials, went from rope to leather, "—three: whatever the door shows you, whatever it offers, whatever it *takes*, you walk through after. You don't turn around. Turning around after paying is how you lose the payment *and* the entry. Seen it happen. Man gave up his wedding day, lost his nerve, and walked back down the mountain with nothing but a hole where forty years of love used to be. Took him three days to remember his own name."

Edmund made a small sound. Elodie squeezed his hand.

"And four," Maren finished, "you go in the order of who's most likely to bolt. Which means me first, because I'm the bravest, obviously."

"You're stalling," Elodie said.

"I'm *savoring*. There's a difference and it's mostly speed." But Maren's one eye wasn't joking, and hadn't been since the tree line. She faced the door, squared herself like a woman facing a wave, and said, quietly, in a tone Elodie had never heard her use: "I paid this door once before. Coming *out*. Half my soul, for the privilege of leaving. It's only fair it gets the rest of the toll coming in." She snorted. "Fair. Ha."

She walked up the steps. She put her hand on the latch.

The door did not open. But the air around it changed — Elodie felt it against her skin the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge, pressure and attention, the whole mountain leaning in to listen. And Maren stood there with her eyes closed and her lips moving, telling the door something none of them could hear, and after a long moment her face did something complicated and brief, like a window shuttering, and then—

Then she frowned slightly, patted her pockets as if checking for keys, and said, "Huh."

"Maren?"

"Had a memory. Little one. My mother singing me down to sleep — some lullaby, can't tell you what it was now, obviously. Knew it this morning. Don't know it now." She turned around, and her face was steady, and her eye was bright, and Elodie understood suddenly that Maren had chosen that memory on purpose, had carried it three hundred miles specifically to spend it here, and had known exactly what it would cost and paid it standing up. "Well," Maren said briskly. "Door opens for me, apparently. Go on, then. I'll wait on the other side. Don't be long. It's drafty in there and I hate it already."

The pale door swung inward without a sound. Beyond it: darkness, and a faint warmth, and a smell like candlewax and cold stone. Maren stepped through and was gone, swallowed neat as a coin into a purse.

* * *

Edmund went second.

He tried not to. He planted himself at Elodie's side and shook his head with a ferocity that needed no translation, and pointed at her, and then at the door, and then made a pushing gesture — *you first, you first* — and Elodie knelt down in the white dust of the courtyard and took his face in both hands.

"Hey. Look at me." His eyes were wet and furious, eight years old and older than the mountain. "I know. I know you want to guard me. You've been guarding me since the night you knocked on my door, and I have never once been sorry you did. But this isn't a fight, sweetheart. It's a toll booth. And nobody can pay anybody else's toll — Maren told us, and the door will know if we try to cheat, and then we'll all be standing out here until we're bones." She brushed his hair back from his forehead. It needed cutting. She thought, absurdly, *I should cut his hair before we go down there,* and then thought, *will I still know it needs cutting, after? Will I still know him?* and shoved the thought down hard where it couldn't breathe.

"You'll go through," she said, "and I'll come right behind you, and Maren will be there calling us both slow. Yes?"

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded once, sharp, and did the thing he did instead of speaking: he pressed his fist gently against her sternum, right over her heart, and left it there a moment. *I'm keeping you,* the gesture said. *Whatever happens, I'm keeping you.*

"Right back at you," Elodie said, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she pretended it hadn't, because someone in this family had to hold.

He went up the steps small and straight-backed, a soldier of eight, and laid his palm on the latch, and the mountain leaned in again, and Elodie watched her boy tell the door his truth. Whatever he offered, it took a long time to weigh — longer than Maren's, much longer — and twice his shoulders shuddered as if with silent sobs, and Elodie stood at the bottom of the steps with her fists clenched, forbidden by rule and by love from climbing up and putting herself between him and it.

When the door finally opened for him, he turned at the threshold and looked back at her — breaking Maren's fourth rule in spirit if not in letter — and smiled. It was the first full smile she'd seen from him in two days. Then he stepped through, and the darkness took him gently, and Elodie was alone in the courtyard with the bench and the motionless bells and the door.

* * *

She climbed the steps slowly, because there was no hurry now, and hurry would only be cowardice wearing boots.

At the top, she put her hand on the latch. The silver was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything about this place was warm and welcoming and hungry.

*I know what I'm giving you,* she said silently. *I've known since the ferry. So let's not pretend you're surprising me.*

The pressure gathered, the mountain's attention settled over her like a hand on the crown of her head, and the door asked — not in words, in *weight*, in the sudden unbearable presence of everything she had ever remembered, spread out before her like a merchant's cloth, every memory gleaming, every one priced —

And there, in the middle of the cloth, folded small: Wren.

Wren at the mill pond, pushing her in. Wren teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass. Wren's face — and this was the thing, this was the exact thing — Wren's face on the last morning, flushed with fever, saying *Loddy, don't cry, it's only boring, being ill, you'll see* — and Elodie had laughed, because Wren had made it funny, Wren could make anything funny, and that laugh was the last gift her sister ever gave her, and it was wrapped around the fever and the guilt and the lightning and the whole architecture of who she was, and the door wanted it, and the door was right to want it, because it was real, it was the truest thing she owned, and she could not spare it, and that was precisely why it would buy passage.

*Take it,* she said. *Take it and open.*

She braced for pain. What came instead was stranger: a quieting. Like a room where a clock has ticked your whole life, and then stops, and you realize the ticking was enormous by the shape of its absence. The morning at the pond went. The blade of grass went. The fevered joke went — *it's only boring, being ill* — and with it went the face that had said it, and Elodie reached for the face and found a shape where it had been, a Wren-shaped space, warm-edged, loved, and utterly blank. She knew she had had a sister. She knew she had loved her. She knew the love had been vast and specific and daily. She knew all of it the way you know a country you've read about in a book.

What she could not do anymore was see her.

She made a sound she didn't recognize as her own voice. Somewhere far away, the door swung open, courteous as an innkeeper.

* * *

The passage beyond was lit by candles in niches, hundreds of them, and the warmth was real, and the floor was marble, and it was pretty, God help them all, it was exactly as pretty as Maren had promised. Maren stood a few yards in, arms crossed, watching Elodie come through with an expression that was carefully, deliberately neutral.

"Well?" Maren said. "Still got all your teeth?"

"Yes."

"Good. Teeth matter, where we're going." A pause. Maren uncrossed her arms. "What'd it take?"

Elodie opened her mouth to answer and discovered the answer was a locked box. She knew she had paid. She knew it had hurt. She could feel the fresh scar of it, tender as a burn. But when she reached for the specifics, her fingers closed on smooth blankness.

"I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "I paid something. Something important. I chose it on purpose and I know it mattered and I can't—" Her breath hitched. "Maren, I can't remember what I gave it."

Maren's face did not change, which was its own kind of mercy. "That's how it works, sometimes. The door takes the memory *of* the memory, so you can't come back and bargain for it later." She hesitated, and then, gruffly, added: "For what it's worth — you cried. Just now, coming through. Quiet, though. Dignified. Very you."

"Was it?" Elodie heard her own voice from a great distance. "Very me?"

"Ask me in a week." Maren jerked her chin down the corridor, where Edmund stood waiting beside a niche of candles, and when he saw Elodie he crossed the marble at a run and hit her at waist height, arms clamped tight, and she held on and held on, breathing in the top of his head, keeping the one thing she still had whole.

Over his shoulder, far down the corridor, the candles gave way to a glow that was not candlelight — a vast, gold, organized radiance, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, came a sound. Singing. Hundreds of voices, layered and disciplined and wrong, sustaining a harmony that had no joy in it anywhere.

Edmund lifted his head. His whole body went rigid against hers.

He knew that music. He had been part of it. And down that corridor, in the gold light, something was waiting that had his name written in a ledger — and something else, older and larger, that had her family's name written in the margin of a songbook, three generations deep.

"Right," said Maren, drawing her knife out of pure habit, since there was nothing down here to cut but the truth. "Stay close, sing soft, and whatever offers you a chair —"

"Don't sit in it," Elodie said.

"Don't sit in it," Maren agreed, and led them down into the light.
