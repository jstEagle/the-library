## 14. Descent

The door closed behind us the way a mouth closes around something it intends to swallow.

I had thought, before, that I understood what it meant to be kept. I had been kept for six years inside my own chest, a lodger in a house that was mine and not mine, listening to the choir sing through my ribs while I pressed my hands against the walls of myself and found no doors. I had thought: *I know this country. I have lived in it.*

I had not understood anything.

The tunnel beneath the mountain was not dark. That was the first cruelty of it. A dark tunnel is only a tunnel; you walk through it and come out the other side, and the dark means nothing except the absence of lamps. But this tunnel was lit — softly, gently, from every side at once — by hundreds upon hundreds of small glass bottles set into niches carved along the walls, floor to ceiling, as far as the light reached and farther. And each bottle held a voice.

They were singing.

Not all together. Not in unison, which would have been terrible in a simple way, a way you could hate cleanly. They sang in parts. A low drone from the left-hand wall, a rising line from above it, a descant somewhere far ahead that threaded through everything like silver wire through cloth. It was beautiful. That was the second cruelty. The Undercourt did not keep its stolen voices in silence, to molder. It used them. Every voice in every bottle was singing its portion of the harmony that held the mountain up, and none of them could stop, and the song they made was lovely enough to break your heart if you let yourself hear it as music instead of what it was.

Which was: a great many people, calling for help, in tune.

"Keep walking," Maren said, low, at my shoulder. "Don't look at any one of them too long."

But that instruction, I have since decided, was impossible from the moment she said it. It is like telling water not to be wet, or telling me not to listen. Listening is the whole of what I am. I had been made into an instrument against my will, and so I knew — better than anyone living, perhaps — how to hear a voice and find the person inside it.

The first one I recognized was nobody. An old man's voice, cracked and warm, singing a third below the drone. In the glass he sounded contented, and I understood with a lurch that the bottles did not preserve pain. They preserved only the sound. Whatever else a soul was — the grieving part, the hoping part, the part that remembered being a boy running through orchards — that part was elsewhere, down in the choir hall, wearing it like a coat. The bottles were just the voices' coats, hung up neatly on hooks.

*Do not look too long,* Maren had said. So of course I looked.

* * *

Elodie walked ahead of us with her hand trailing the wall, and I watched her more than I watched the bottles, because watching her was easier. She had paid her toll at the door — a memory, gone now, though she did not yet know which one — and there was a new stillness in her, like a house after the furniture has been carried out. She kept touching her sleeve where a ring should have been. There was no ring. There had never been a ring, perhaps. But her fingers went looking for it anyway, again and again, the way a tongue goes to the gap where a tooth was.

She was doing arithmetic with her own past. I could see it. *What did I pay? What is missing?* And the horror of the door's price was that you could not check. You could only walk forward and wait for the absence to announce itself.

Maren walked between us, one eye on the road and one eye — her only eye — on me, because Maren had walked this tunnel before, in the other direction, and she knew what the bottles did to people who had business with them.

"They can't see you," she said, for the fourth time since the door. "There's nothing in there to see with. It's just the sound they kept. Say it back to me, sprout."

"It is just the sound they kept," I said, in the formal way I say things, which always makes Elodie smile even when she is frightened, and which made Maren snort.

"And?"

"And it cannot hurt me."

"It can't," Maren agreed. "It can only make you want to stand still forever and listen, which is worse. Come on."

We came on.

* * *

I will set down what happened next as plainly as I can, because Edmund of the ledger learned long ago that plainness is a kind of armor, and because someone ought to write it truly, and I am the only one who was there inside my own chest when it happened.

At a bend in the tunnel, where the wall curved away toward some deeper gallery, there was a niche set slightly apart from the others — smaller, older, its glass clouded at the corners like a window in a house nobody cleans. And in that bottle there was a voice I knew.

It was mine.

I do not mean it resembled mine. I mean it *was* mine: the exact pitch and grain of it, the particular hollowness at the bottom of my tone where the choir had hollowed me out, the small catch on the second syllable of any word beginning with *s*. Six years I had listened to that voice from the inside, sung through it without owning it, felt it move in my throat like a bird in a stranger's hand. And here it was outside of me, bottled, labeled with a string tag gone brown with age, singing its small assigned portion of the harmony — a thin, clear line, high and steady, a child's voice kept in perfect condition because children's voices are rare stock and the court does not let its assets degrade.

I stopped walking.

I want to explain what it is like to meet yourself in a jar on a shelf in the dark, but language keeps failing me, sliding off the thing the way rain slides off oiled canvas. It was not like looking in a mirror. A mirror shows you your face, and your face is only the surface, and you already knew about the surface. This was more like — imagine that every word you have ever spoken was taken out of you and hung on a wall, and then imagine walking past the wall and hearing your own voice say, softly, in the dark, *help*, except it cannot say help, it can only sing the note it has been given, and the note it has been given *is* help, has always been help, dressed up as music.

My knees went. I remember the stone coming up toward me with great patience, the way stone does.

And then — this is the part I will remember longest, longer than the fear — Maren caught me.

She moved fast for a woman with half a soul and one eye and a bad hip she complained about in three towns' worth of dialects. She was simply *there*, one arm banded around my chest before the stone arrived, lowering me down until we were both crouched in the curve of the wall, her chin hooked over my shoulder, her breath rough in my ear.

"I know," she said. "I know, sprout. I found mine on the third landing. Little glass fellow, smug as a bishop. Sang alto." Her hand found mine and pressed it flat against the cold stone, away from the niche. "You don't touch it. Hear me? You don't touch it, and here's why, and it's not the reason you think."

I could not speak. I nodded against her arm.

"You're thinking if you smash it, you get it back. You're thinking, *break the glass and the voice comes home.*" She shook her head slowly, and I felt the motion of it through my whole body, hers and mine rocking together like a boat. "It doesn't work that way. I watched a man try, my first night in this mountain. Smashed his own bottle with a rock, screaming the whole time. And you know what happened?" Her voice dropped. "The voice didn't come home to him. It just stopped existing. He stood there with his mouth open, making no sound at all, and the court's clerks came and swept up the glass and wrote him down as *damaged goods, value nil*, and took him below. That's what breaking a bottle buys. Nothing, faster."

Above us, Elodie had turned back. I saw her boots stop, saw her shadow fall over us both, and heard her say my name — the name I had carried out of the valley, the name from the belt-buckle, *Edmund* — in the voice she used for storms and for me, which by then were nearly the same voice.

"He's alright," Maren said over me. "He met himself. Give it a minute. It's a lot, meeting yourself."

"It's a lot meeting *anyone* down here," Elodie said, and crouched too, and put her hand on my head the way she did at the cottage when the nightmares had me, thumb moving slow behind my ear. And that — that small warm weight of her hand — did more than all of Maren's reasons. Because the bottles were cold and the tunnel was cold and the song was beautiful in the way that prisons are clean, and her hand was the first warm thing in miles, and it belonged to someone who had crossed a ridgeline and paid a memory and torn up a contract rather than leave me in a place like this.

I breathed. Once. Twice. On the third breath I found my own voice — the one in my chest, the borrowed-and-returned one, the one that was currently mine — and used it.

"That is me," I said.

"Yes," Elodie said.

"It sings well," I said, "for a prisoner."

Elodie made a sound that was half laugh and half something else entirely, and pulled me in against her shoulder, and held on. Maren stayed crouched behind us, keeping watch down the tunnel with her one eye, and after a moment she began, very quietly, to hum — a low, rough thread of melody that did not belong to the mountain's harmony at all. It took me four notes to recognize it. The counter-song. The unbinding song she had taught us by the fire, the one that unwrites contracts. She was humming it right there under the mountain's nose, soft as a lullaby, daring the walls to object.

And here is the thing I must set down, because it matters, because it is the whole reason I am writing this:

The bottles *leaned*.

Not much. A traveler passing might have called it a draft, or settling stone, or imagination. But I am a boy who was a choir, and I know what listening sounds like from the inside. All along the tunnel, in their hundreds, in their thousands, the bottled voices bent their long steady lines slightly toward Maren's humming — the way flowers lean at a window, the way the drowned reach for the surface they will never reach. They could not follow the melody. They were not permitted. But they *turned toward it*, every one, the way you turn toward a door in a room with no doors.

"They know it," I whispered. "Maren. They know the song."

"Aye," Maren said, and her voice was not rough anymore. "Half of them helped build it, once upon a time. Before the court took their tongues along with everything else." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and offered me her hand. "So we'd best not waste it, had we? Come on, sprout. Up you get. Your voice isn't going anywhere — neither of them is. And neither are the rest of them. And that, my lad, is exactly the problem."

I took her hand and stood.

* * *

We walked on, the three of us, down through the singing dark.

I did not look at my bottle again as we passed out of sight of it. But I confess — and the ledger taught me honesty, if nothing else — that I listened to it. All the way around the bend, until the stone swallowed the sound, I listened to my own voice holding its thin high line in the dark, patient as a lamp, faithful as a dog tied to a post, singing its one note of *help* into the glass.

*I am coming back for you,* I told it, silently, the way you speak to someone across a river. *Wait for me. Hold the note. I am coming back for all of you.*

Because that was the other thing the bend in the tunnel had shown me, the thing Maren's eye had already found and Elodie's face had already begun to harden around: the tunnel did not go on evenly. Ahead, the niches grew larger, closer together, the singing denser, richer, more terrible — hundreds of voices braiding into one vast sustained chord that I felt in my teeth, in my sternum, in the scar-tissue places where the choir had once lived in me. The bottles were funneling toward something. All the voices in the mountain were flowing downward, the way streams flow, toward the sea of whatever held them.

Toward the choir hall. Toward the hundreds of hollow-eyed souls I had dreamed of in the valley and hoped were only dreams.

And beneath the great chord, so deep I almost missed it, woven into the lowest line where no one listens — a voice I knew better than my own. A voice that had sung me to sleep in a famine year, trading me away in the morning so that I would eat.

"Maren," I said. My hand tightened on hers. "Hurry."
