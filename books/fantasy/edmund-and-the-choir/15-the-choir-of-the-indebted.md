## 15. The Choir of the Indebted

The passage opened without warning.

One moment they were walking through stone that had been stone for a very long time — Maren's lantern swinging its patient arc, Edmund's hand tucked into Elodie's like he'd been doing it all his life — and the next moment the walls simply gave up pretending to be walls, and the tunnel exhaled them into a vastness that made Elodie's stomach drop as though she'd stepped off a cliff in her sleep.

She had expected a dungeon. She realized this only afterward, when she tried to describe it to herself and found her imagination had stocked the Undercourt with chains and dripping water and men with keys at their belts. What she got instead was a cathedral.

A cathedral built by something that had heard about cathedrals secondhand.

The hall stretched away in every direction, ribbed with pillars of black stone veined through with slow silver light, like frost growing in reverse. The ceiling was so far above them that the lantern light couldn't find it; instead, a soft glow came from everywhere at once, sourceless, the color of candlelight seen through milk. And filling that glow — filling *all* of it — was the singing.

Elodie had heard Edmund sing. She had heard him hum harmonies no child should know, had felt candles gutter to his rhythm, had lain awake more than one night listening to the small impossible orchestra living in his chest. But this —

This was what his humming had been an echo of.

Hundreds of voices. Maybe thousands. They rose from the floor of the hall in tiers, row upon row upon row, and for one merciful heartbeat Elodie thought: *an audience*. Because that was what it looked like — a great amphitheater packed shoulder to shoulder, faces turned toward some distant stage, every seat full.

Then the lantern swung, and she saw their eyes.

They were standing. All of them. Every soul in that enormous hall stood upright in perfect rows, and none of them moved except to breathe, and none of them breathed quite right — too shallow, too even, breath measured out like coin. Their mouths were open. The song came out of them steadily, unbroken, note fitted to note with a precision no living choir could hold for ten minutes, let alone forever. And their eyes were open too, fixed on nothing, or on the middle distance, or — worst, Elodie thought, worst of all — on each other, occasionally, with a flicker of something that might once have been recognition.

"They don't see us," Maren said quietly. Her single eye reflected the silver-veined pillars. "Don't expect them to. It takes everything they have just to keep the line."

"Keep the line of what?" Elodie whispered.

"The harmony." Maren's jaw worked. "You're standing inside the engine, girl. This is what turns it."

* * *

Edmund made a sound Elodie had never heard him make before.

It wasn't a word. It wasn't any language, living or dead. It was a small cracked noise, the kind a hinge makes, and then he was moving — pulling his hand free of hers, stumbling down the terraced steps toward the nearest row of singers, and Elodie lunged after him because everything in her body said *don't touch them, don't interrupt, you don't know what happens if you interrupt.*

"Edmund — *Edmund*, wait—"

He stopped three feet from the first singer. A woman. Grey-robed like everyone else, grey-faced, her mouth shaping a low sustained note that seemed to come up through the floor itself. Up close Elodie could see that her lips were cracked, that her feet were bare and the stone beneath them was worn smooth in two shallow hollows, the way a threshold wears after centuries of the same footsteps.

She had been standing there a long, long time.

Edmund raised his hand — slowly, the way you'd approach a horse that spooks — and touched the woman's sleeve.

Nothing happened. The song didn't falter. The woman's eyes didn't move. But Edmund made the hinge-sound again, softer, and Elodie saw his shoulders begin to shake, and she came and knelt beside him on the cold stone and put her arm around him and looked at the woman properly for the first time.

And understood.

Not because of anything so dramatic as lightning or revelation. She understood because the woman had flour under her fingernails. Faint, ancient, worked so deep into the quick of the nail that no amount of standing in an underground cathedral would ever wash it out. Flour, or ash, or chalk — but Elodie thought of a famine, of a mother trading her son's soul to end it, of bread baked in desperate quantities by someone who knew exactly how much grain a village needed to survive a winter.

"Oh," Elodie said. Very quietly. "Oh, Edmund."

He was nodding before she finished. He pressed both hands flat against his own chest, over the place where the stolen voices lived, and then reached out and pressed one finger, feather-gentle, against the woman's sternum. And the woman sang on, eyes empty, mouth open, sustaining her note — but something crossed her face. Something tiny. Like weather seen from very far away.

"She can't stop," Maren said behind them. "None of them can. That's the design of it. You pay your debt in voice, and the court holds the voice, and while it holds it, you keep singing. The song doesn't work if anyone stops. So nobody stops. Not to eat, not to sleep, not to weep." She paused. "Weeping uses the same muscles."

"How long has she been here?" Elodie asked. Her voice came out strange and flat.

Maren consulted some inner arithmetic she clearly hated. "Famine debts run long. The court likes them long — interest accrues in verses. I'd say... eight years. Perhaps nine."

Eight years. Edmund had been eight years old when he was sold, which meant — Elodie did the math and wished she hadn't — which meant his mother had been standing in this hall since before he could remember her face clearly. He was looking up at her now with the ferocious concentration of a child memorizing, cataloguing, filing away the shape of her nose and the set of her mouth against some day when he'd need those things and wouldn't be able to get them.

Except he did know her face. Elodie realized it with a lurch. He must have known it all along — known it the way he knew the harmonies, carried somewhere in the part of him the court hadn't managed to itemize. He'd been humming her all along, in a manner of speaking. Every night in Hollowmere, guttering the candles.

* * *

They walked the rows.

It was Maren's idea, though Elodie suspected it was really a penance Maren had been carrying for eleven years and had simply never had the courage or the company to perform. "We should look at them," she said gruffly. "People don't look. That's half of how the court gets away with it. Nobody comes down here and *looks*."

So they looked.

Row after row after row. A man with a farmer's forearms and a scholar's hands, singing a note so high it was nearly pain. Three sisters standing together — the court apparently allowed families to be filed adjacently, a mercy or a cruelty, Elodie couldn't decide — their three voices braided into one line so seamlessly that if you closed your eyes you'd swear it was a single person who had somehow learned to grieve in three-part harmony. An old man whose robe hung off him like laundry on a line, whose note was barely a thread anymore, worn down to almost nothing, and still he held it, still the great machine of the song absorbed his thread and went on turning.

A boy of maybe twelve, standing where a child should never stand, singing a note that belonged to a grown woman's throat.

"That one's not paying his own debt," Maren murmured, following Elodie's gaze. "See the collar-mark? Inherited. His father defaulted, or died owing. The court collects from the estate." She spat, with feeling, onto the immaculate stone floor, and then looked slightly embarrassed about it.

Elodie kept walking. She had expected, coming down here, to feel fear, and she had — the fear was there, humming along her nerves like a struck wire. But underneath the fear was something she hadn't prepared for, something that sat in her chest heavier than dread:

She liked some of them.

That was the obscene thing. Walking these rows, she caught herself noticing people. The old man had a way of holding his chin that reminded her of the miller back in Hollowmere. One of the sisters had flour under *her* nails too, or something like it — ink, maybe, a scribe sold for a scribe's debt. A tall woman two rows over had a crooked smile frozen halfway into being around her sustained note, as if she'd been mid-joke when the collection came, and had been mid-joke ever since, for what might have been decades.

These were not monsters. There were no monsters here. There was a village. There was a town's worth of towns, a nation's worth of ordinary lives, harvested and arranged in rows and set to work. Somewhere above them, the Undercourt ran its ledgers and its engines and its terrible tidy bureaucracy, and the fuel was *this* — grandmothers and bakers and joke-tellers, singing.

"The enemy is not monsters," Elodie said aloud, not meaning to say anything aloud. "The enemy is bookkeeping."

"Now you understand," said Maren. "Took me eleven years and half a soul to understand it, and you've done it in an hour. Don't let it make you proud. Understanding is the easy part. It's the part they give away free."

* * *

Edmund did not want to leave his mother's row.

They tried twice. The first time, Elodie took his hand and walked him gently toward the far side of the hall, where Maren had spotted a door — an actual door, iron-banded, the first honest piece of carpentry they'd seen down here — and he came along for perhaps forty paces before his feet simply stopped, the way feet do when the rest of the child has decided otherwise, and he turned and walked back and stood in front of his mother again with his arms folded in a way that transcended every language barrier in existence.

The second time, Elodie didn't try to move him. She sat down on the cold stone beside him instead, cross-legged, and looked up at the woman who had traded her son's soul to feed a valley, and said — foolishly, uselessly, because the woman could not hear her — "He's fine. He's grown. He's brave and stubborn and he hums off-key on purpose when he thinks I'm not listening, which I always am."

The woman sang on.

But Edmund tugged Elodie's sleeve. Pointed at his mother's mouth. Then pointed at his own ears, wide-eyed, urgent.

"What?" Elodie said. "What is it?"

"He hears her," Maren said, from behind them, in a voice gone very careful. "In the stolen voices. Of course he does. Hers was the first they took." She crouched down beside the boy. "What does she sound like, lad? When she sings in your chest?"

Edmund's answer was to close his eyes and hum — one low note, wavering, imperfect, human in a way nothing else in this vast silver-lit hall was human. And Elodie watched his mother's face with her heart in her teeth, and saw it happen: the far-away weather on the woman's face gathered. Thickened. For one impossible second her empty eyes *focused*, downward, toward the small boy humming her own note back to her, and her sustained tone bent — bent, actually bent, a quarter-tone of deviation in a machine that did not permit deviation — and somewhere deep in the hall, Elodie felt the whole choir shiver like a rope under strain.

Then it passed. The woman's eyes went soft and vacant again. The note straightened. The engine smoothed itself out and went on turning.

But for that one second, she had heard him.

Edmund turned to Elodie and Maren, and his face was wet and blazing and absolutely, terrifyingly certain, and he pointed — at his mother, at the rows beyond her, at the whole silent congregation of the trapped — and then he pointed at his own chest, hard, twice, the gesture of a child who has finally found the words for the thing he's been trying to say since a rainy night in a valley doorway.

*Mine. They're mine. I'm taking them back. All of them.*

"Yes," Elodie said. Her voice cracked on it. She cleared her throat and said it again, steadier, because some promises you have to say properly. "Yes. All of them. Every last one."

* * *

It was the door that ended the moment — or rather, what came through it.

The iron bands rang, once, a clean bell-tone utterly unlike the choir's endless weave, and every head in Elodie's party turned. The door swung inward without visible hinges doing any work, and through it came a figure in grey.

Grey robes. Grey gloves. A face like a page waiting for ink. It carried a ledger under one arm and a lantern in the other hand, and the lantern's light was wrong — it fell on the singers and did not illuminate them so much as *itemize* them, each soul briefly outlined in thin gold script that Elodie's eye could almost read and was glad it couldn't.

The figure stopped. Turned its page-face toward the three of them with the unhurried precision of a clerk noting an irregularity.

Behind it, the door stayed open. And through the doorway, from whatever lay beyond, came a sound that made Maren go rigid and Edmund clutch Elodie's hand hard enough to hurt:

The scratch of a quill. Steady. Patient. Writing.

"You," said the grey figure, in a voice like a receipt being torn along the perforation, "are not on today's schedule."

It opened its ledger.

"I will need to inform the Auditor."
