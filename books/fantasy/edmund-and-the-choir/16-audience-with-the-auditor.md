## 16. Audience with the Auditor

### Elodie

The doors of the audience chamber were made of ledger-paper.

That was the only way Elodie could describe them. Vellum, thousands upon thousands of sheets pressed and bound and lacquered until they had the density of oak, every page dense with columns of figures that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at them. Maren touched one doorframe as they passed and flinched as if the wood had been hot.

"Every contract ever signed down here," she murmured. "Don't read them. Some folk have stood there reading for years. The court doesn't mind. It counts it as interest."

The chamber itself was vast and cold and lit by lanterns that did not flicker — lanterns, Elodie realized with a lurch, that held voices. She could hear them if she listened sideways: a soprano here, a cracked old baritone there, all singing in a key that made her teeth ache. Edmund's hand found hers without either of them looking. His grip was small and damp and absolutely steady.

At the far end of the chamber, behind a desk that might have been carved from a single piece of slate, sat the Auditor.

She had expected a monster. What she got was a person so ordinary it was frightening — a tall figure in a grey clerk's robe, spectacles perched on a nose that was slightly too large, hair bound back with a plain cord. Its face had the terrible blankness of a portrait painted by someone who had been told what a face was but never seen one. When it spoke, its voice was kind. That was the worst part.

"Elodie Dunmore," it said. "Storm-caller, third of her line. Delinquent on a standing account for thirty-one years." It turned a page. "Please, sit. This is not an arrest. This is an *offer*."

Chairs appeared. Elodie hadn't seen them arrive, which meant they'd always been there, which meant she'd simply failed to notice them until the court decided she should. She sat because Edmund sat, pulling his chair close enough to touch hers.

Maren stayed standing, arms folded, one eye narrowed. "You've no claim on me."

"None whatsoever," the Auditor agreed pleasantly. "You are a guest. Guests may leave whenever they like. Most find they don't want to. Isn't that curious?"

"I'm leaving now, then."

"You are welcome to try," said the Auditor, and smiled with a mouth full of neat, grey, clerical teeth. "Afterward."

* * *

### Edmund

Edmund knew the Auditor's voice before it finished its first sentence.

It was the voice from the famine year. Not identical — nothing down here sounded quite like the world above — but built on the same bones. He had heard it through the floorboards while his mother wept in the kitchen. He had heard it again in the choir hall, reading names off the rolls. *Asset 4-4-1. Asset 4-4-1. Present yourself for tuning.* A voice that never hurried, never raised itself, never needed to. A voice that treated everything, everywhere, as a line item already entered.

He hated it the way you hate weather. Completely, and without any expectation that the hating would change anything.

But something else lived in him now, too. In the tunnels he had heard his own bottled voice singing among the niches, and Maren had put her rough hand on the back of his neck and said, *That's not you anymore, love. You're up here with us. Breathe.* And he had. And the choir's harmony, the great grinding chord that held the whole Undercourt together, had developed — just barely, just for him — a wrong note. A note that said *no.*

He sat beside Elodie and held that note inside himself like a coal cupped in two hands.

The Auditor opened a drawer and produced a document. Edmund recognized the paper immediately: vellum the color of old milk, edges cut clean, a red ribbon through a punched hole. Contracts were the only beautiful things the court made, and it made them very beautifully.

"Before the offer," the Auditor said, "a small matter of bookkeeping. Elodie Dunmore, your family's account stands as follows. Your grandmother called a storm to break the drought of the Grey Famine, unlicensed, unpaid. Your mother called two. Your own debt was to be settled by the delivery of the asset known as—" and here it paused, delicately, "—Edmund. A soul sufficient to discharge three generations of arrears."

"He's not an asset," Elodie said. Her voice came out low and even, and Edmund felt her storm gathering under it, the way the air used to gather over Hollowmere's ridgeline before the rain finally won. "He's a child. He hums when he's happy. He's frightened of eels. You can't put a child in a column."

"Madam," said the Auditor, with genuine, awful gentleness, "we put everything in columns. That is what a world is. Now." It turned the contract around and slid it across the slate. "Your offer."

* * *

### Elodie

The contract read itself aloud as her eyes moved across it, each clause blooming in her mind like ink dropped in water.

*The Undercourt requires a storm-caller to sustain the Great Engine. The previous holder of this office served four hundred years and retired only upon dissolution. The successful applicant will receive: full discharge of the Dunmore account. Full and irrevocable manumission of Asset 4-4-1, Edmund, including restoration of his name to the living rolls. Release of all bottled voices currently held against debts under fifty years' standing.*

Elodie's breath caught at that last clause despite herself.

"There are hundreds," the Auditor observed. "Perhaps more than hundreds. Children sold in bad winters. Sailors who wagered their singing voices and lost. Whole villages that mortgaged their bells." It folded its hands. "One signature, madam. One signature, and the ledger lightens by a thousand lines. You would be the most forgiving storm-caller in the history of this court. They would sing about you above ground. They do still sing, above ground? I confess I lose track."

"And the cost," Maren said flatly from behind her. "There's always a cost. Out with it."

"The term of service," said the Auditor. "Which is, as stated, indefinite." It turned a page. "You would live here. Below. Your work would be to call storms within the Engine's channels — to keep the mountain's heart beating, as your grandmother's power kept your valley green. You would be fed, housed, honored. You would be," and its blank face somehow managed tenderness, "*needed*. Which I understand, madam, is not a thing you have often felt."

The words went into Elodie like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Because it was true. That was the obscene part. Thirty-one years in Hollowmere mending nets, telling herself the silence was peace, watching storms pile up on the ridgeline and turn away — and underneath it all, the knowledge she'd never once said aloud, not even to herself, not even in the dark: *I am wasted here. I am a bell hung in a room where no one is allowed to ring me.*

"You mistake me," she said carefully, "if you think flattery—"

"It is not flattery. It is arithmetic." The Auditor rose, for the first time, and gestured, and the chamber changed.

* * *

The visions began gently, which should have warned her.

She saw Hollowmere. Not as she'd left it — grayed and guarded and holding its breath — but green. Green pastures where the soil had been thin, orchards bending with fruit, the river running high and bright. Storms moved where she told them to move; rain fell on the fields and slid politely around the festival days. She saw old Tamwith's knees working again in the mild wet warmth. She saw children who had never once seen lightning learn to count the seconds between flash and thunder, delighted, unafraid.

She saw the valley open its gates. Why shouldn't it, now? No exiled storm-caller to hide, no forbidden bloodline to shelter. She saw herself walking up the lane with Edmund beside her, and the village coming out to meet them, and someone — Mrs. Halloway, it would be Mrs. Halloway — pressing a loaf of celebration bread into her hands and saying, *We didn't know, all these years, we didn't know what you were carrying for us—*

She saw storms turned aside from fishing fleets. She saw drought broken in three kingdoms. She saw plague-winds redirected out over empty ocean. She saw Edmund grown tall, laughing, his voice his own, singing at some valley wedding while she watched from a hillside wreathed in harmless summer cloud, and everyone below knowing whose daughter's daughter had done all this, and no one — no one — calling her a curse.

"This is what your power was *for*," the Auditor said, its voice soft as turning pages. "Not exile. Not net-mending. You have spent thirty-one years apologizing for being what you are. I am offering you the chance to be it *on purpose*."

The vision shifted. Now she saw the alternative: the ledger closing over Edmund's name, the boy hollow-eyed in a choir stall forever, humming harmonies for a court that owned him, while Elodie went home alone to a valley that forgot her and a life that had never really begun. She saw herself old in the net-mending hut, and the storms still piling up on the ridgeline, still turning away, and no one left to call them.

"Do you know how many applicants refuse this offer?" the Auditor asked. "In four hundred years? Would you care to guess?"

"No," Elodie whispered.

"Neither do I. I stopped counting after the first century. Refusal is such a rare entry that I find it upsets the columns."

* * *

Beside her, Edmund had gone very still.

### Edmund

Edmund had watched the visions play across the chamber the way you watch weather come over hills — helplessly, and with a fair idea of where it would strike. He had felt the temptation land on Elodie like a hand on her shoulder, gentle and heavy, aimed exactly where she was softest. *Needed. On purpose. Forgive yourself.*

And he understood it. That was the horrible thing. If someone had offered him the same bargain — sign here, and Elodie goes free, and Maren's half-soul grows whole — he thought his hand might shake on the way to the pen. Love made excellent ink. The court knew that better than anyone. Half the contracts in the walls had been signed by people trying to save somebody else.

So he did the only thing he could do, the thing no one in this grey country had thought to forbid.

He sang.

Quietly. Almost under his breath. Not the counter-song — not yet, that was Maren's to start and it would shatter the room — but something smaller. Something from above. A nonsense tune the fisher children sang in Hollowmere while they gutted mackerel, all wrong rhythm and rude words, the least sacred music in the world. He leaned toward Elodie as he hummed it, and let it say what it had always said above ground: *this too is a life. This silly, small, salt-smelling life. It counts.*

Elodie's eyes flickered. The vision wavered — just for a moment, the green valley rippling like a reflection in disturbed water — and Edmund saw her look down at him, really look, and something in her jaw set.

* * *

### Elodie

She came back to herself slowly, like surfacing.

The Auditor was watching her with its blank, patient face, pen extended. The contract waited on the slate, its clauses glowing softly, patient as stone. Somewhere in the walls, the bottled voices sang on, and for the first time Elodie noticed what they were singing. Not the Engine's harmony. A plea. The same plea, hundreds of times over, worn smooth by centuries: *let us stop. Let us stop. Let us stop.*

"How long," she asked, "did the last storm-caller last? Before dissolution."

"Four hundred years. As stated."

"And did anyone visit her? Did anyone come down and say thank you?"

The pause was very small, but it existed. "The dead do not visit," the Auditor said. "Nor do the freed. Freed souls go upward. It is in their terms."

*Terms*, Elodie thought. Even gratitude had terms down here.

She looked at the contract. She looked at Edmund, who was humming a stupid mackerel song and looking at her with his whole heart in his face, asking for nothing, ready for anything. She looked at Maren, who met her eye and shook her head once — not *don't sign*, exactly. Just: *look at me. Remember there's another way to be brave.*

"You're right about one thing," Elodie said at last, and her voice was steadier than she had any right to expect. "I've spent thirty-one years apologizing for what I am."

The Auditor inclined its head, pen hovering.

"But I'll tell you what I learned in that valley you're so keen to rescue me from." She stood. The lantern-light bent toward her, the way air bends before weather. "Peace isn't being needed. I tried that math for three decades and it never once balanced. Peace is being *known*. Being known by a boy who can't speak my language and tells me things anyway. Being known by a woman with half a soul and all of a temper. And you—" she pointed at the contract, at the walls, at the whole grey singing dark of the Undercourt, "—you don't know anybody. You've got a thousand million entries and not one single friend."

"Madam—"

"So no." She reached out, and lightning woke along her knuckles like a cat stretching, blue-white and hungry and *hers*. "I'm not signing your ledger. I'm going to tear a page out of it."

The pen froze mid-air.

For the first time in four hundred years, something in the Undercourt hesitated — and the hesitation ran outward through the walls like a crack through ice, and in the choir hall far below, the harmony stumbled.

The Auditor removed its spectacles, polished them on its sleeve with terrible calm, and put them back on.

"Very well," it said. "Then the Dunmore account is due. In full. Immediately."
