## 17. Refusal

The contract lay on the table between them like a sleeping animal.

It was written on vellum the color of old teeth, and the ink moved. Not shimmered — *moved*, the way a slow worm moves under bark, rearranging itself as Elodie read, so that no matter how carefully she followed the lines, she could never quite reach the end of any sentence. She had the distinct impression that the document was reading her back.

The Auditor sat across from her with its hands folded, patient as a stone in a stream. It had worn many faces since they'd entered its hall — her grandmother's for one terrible moment, Maren's for another, once even Edmund's, small and solemn and wrong — but now it wore something neutral: a middle-aged woman with kind eyes and a clerk's posture, the sort of person who would help you fill out a form and mean it.

"Take your time," it said. Its voice was warm. That was somehow worse than if it had boomed. "Contracts of this magnitude should never be signed in haste. Haste is how people end up paying for things they never agreed to buy."

"Like my great-grandmother," Elodie said.

"Like your great-grandmother." The Auditor inclined its head, acknowledging the point like a fencer. "Though to be fair to her, she was drowning in a famine year and we were the only bank open. We are very rarely *cruel*, Mistress Dunmore. We are simply always available."

Elodie looked at the contract again. The terms were laid out in language almost plain enough to be honest:

*The undersigned, Elodie Dunmore, last storm-caller of the Dunmore line, agrees to serve the Undercourt as Keeper of Storms, term perpetual, in exchange for which all debts against the said line shall be struck from the Ledger, and the asset designated EDMUND (see entry 9,447) shall be released whole and unencumbered.*

Keeper of Storms. The words had a nice ring to them. They were designed to have a nice ring to them. Somewhere below her feet, she could feel it — the deep mechanical heartbeat of the Undercourt, the vast engines of harmony that kept this place running, kept the mountain breathing, kept the choir singing. And those engines needed power. Her family's debt hadn't been paid in coin or memory alone; three generations of storm-callers had fed lightning into these depths, each payment buying a little more time, a little less of themselves.

They wanted the fourth generation to move in permanently.

"And if I sign," Elodie said slowly, "Edmund walks free. His mother's famine-debt is cleared. The choir —"

"The asset's accounts are settled, yes." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down on silk. "The choir is... a separate matter."

"The choir is *hundreds of people*."

"The choir is hundreds of *accounts*," the Auditor corrected gently. "And every account has a balance. You of all people understand balances, Mistress Dunmore. Your whole life has been an exercise in them. What you owe. What you've paid. What you're still short." It unfolded its hands and spread them, magnanimous. "But you — you could stop being short. Think of it. Every debt your family ever carried, gone. Your grandmother's shame, erased. Your exile ended. And power, real power, not the starved trickle you've lived on — storms at your fingertips for the rest of a very long life. We would want our Keeper well-fed."

It was doing the thing she'd felt it doing since they arrived: pressing, softly, exactly on her bruises. Power — because she'd spent six years powerless in Hollowmere, mending nets, flinching from thunder. Belonging — because she'd spent six years alone. Absolution — because her grandmother's songbook still sat in her pack like a stone, full of margins full of bargains, and some small stubborn part of Elodie still believed debts ran in blood.

And Edmund. Of course Edmund. That was the hook baited with everything else.

She glanced toward the edge of the hall where he stood. Maren had a hand on his shoulder, and he was very still, watching Elodie with those dark eyes that held a choir's worth of voices and, somehow, still room for trust. He shook his head — a small motion, barely there. *Don't.* Or maybe just: *I'm not worth it.* With Edmund it was hard to tell, because he'd been taught his whole life that he was a line item.

"Don't listen to him," Elodie told the Auditor. "He undervalues himself. Bad accounting on his part."

Something flickered across the Auditor's borrowed face — surprise, quickly filed away. "You have a wit about you. Your grandmother didn't. She only had guilt, and guilt makes for such dreary negotiations." It slid the contract an inch closer. "May I show you what acceptance looks like? Just a glimpse. Seeing is free."

"I doubt that."

"Humor me."

And before she could refuse, the hall *shifted*.

* * *

The vision came on like weather.

She stood in the Undercourt still — but changed. Older, or simply *longer*, stretched thin across years. She wore grey robes trimmed in silver thread, and her hands crackled with obedient lightning, and she was walking down the row of niches with a lantern, checking on the voices. Bottled souls hummed behind glass as she passed, and she knew their names — that was the horror of it, in the vision she *knew all their names*, had learned them over decades of service, had catalogued them the way she'd once catalogued cloud formations — and she patted the glass of one niche the way you'd pat a horse's neck, absently, kindly, and the voice inside sang a little louder because the Keeper had noticed it, and it was *grateful* —

In the vision, she smiled.

Elodie wrenched herself out of it with a gasp, back in the negotiation hall, back in her own salt-stained coat, her palms flat on the table. Her heart was going like a drum in a parade.

"You see?" the Auditor said softly. "You would be *good* at it. That's the tragedy of people like you, Mistress Dunmore. You make excellent keepers. You're responsible. You learn everyone's names. You tell yourself the cages are comfortable, and after enough years, you believe it." It leaned forward, and its kind eyes were bottomless. "You would not be a jailer. You would be a *shepherd*. There is a difference, and the difference is that shepherds sleep well."

The hall was quiet except for the deep heartbeat of the engines below.

And Elodie found — to her own mild surprise — that she wasn't shaking anymore. The vision had done the opposite of what it was meant to do. Because she'd seen the woman in the grey robes smile at the caged voices, and she had recognized her, and not because of the resemblance.

Because of the loneliness.

Six years in Hollowmere, telling herself the quiet was peace. Six years of mistaking an empty valley for safety, silence for contentment, solitude for choice. She knew exactly how a woman got to smiling at cages. You started by being afraid, and then you got tired, and then someone handed you a lantern and a job and called it purpose, and one day you couldn't remember what you'd been before.

"No," Elodie said.

The Auditor tilted its head. "No to the glimpse, or —"

"No to all of it." She stood up. Her chair scraped back, and the sound went ringing off into the dark of the hall like a struck bell. "I've heard the pitch. It's a good pitch. My grandmother fell for it, and her mother before her, and I'll bet you gave each of them the same speech with different wallpaper. But here's what I saw in your little picture show: a woman who knows the names of everything she's imprisoned. And I already know what that costs, because I've spent six years paying a smaller version of it." She planted both fists on the table. "You don't want a keeper. You want a *warden* who thinks she's a nurse. And I have met myself, Auditor, all the way down, and I am not for sale — not for power, not for peace, and not even for him."

At the edge of the hall, Edmund made a sound. Small. Almost a note.

Elodie didn't look away from the Auditor. "Especially not for him. Because he'd spend the rest of his life knowing his freedom cost a hundred voices theirs, and he'd carry that the way he carries everything — quietly, and forever, and singing. You'd be trading one debtor for two." She let that land. "So. No. With feeling."

The Auditor regarded her for a long moment. Then, remarkably, it sighed — a human sigh, weary and almost fond, the sigh of a clerk closing a file.

"They always say no dramatically first," it said. "Your grandmother threw the contract in the fire. It reassembled itself, incidentally. Fire doesn't work on vellum of state. Neither does water, tearing, or strong language." It pushed the contract toward her again. "Sign, or declare the alternative. Those are the options. I'm afraid 'no' is not, in itself, a legal position."

"Then let me make it legal."

Elodie reached for the contract — and reached past it, past the table, down through the floor, down through the living rock, down to where the storm she'd carried across the pass still circled the mountain's crown like a hawk that hadn't yet been called home.

Calling lightning indoors was, Maren had warned her, either impossible or catastrophic, depending on the day and the drinker. Elodie figured this qualified as both.

She did not pull the storm down gently.

* * *

Later — much later, when people asked her about it, though nobody in Hollowmere would ever ask her anything again — Elodie would remember it in pieces.

The white. First, always, the white: the ceiling of the choir hall splitting open along seams nobody had known were there, and the storm pouring through like water through a burst dam, and the smell of ozone so thick it tasted like biting metal.

The sound. The Auditor rising from its chair for the first time, its borrowed faces cycling — grandmother, Maren, Edmund, a man with a ledger-keeper's squint, something older than all of them, something with too many joints — and shouting, in a voice suddenly stripped of all warmth: *"That is a protected document—"*

And the paper. The contract lifting off the table in the updraft, pages fanning open like a startled bird, and Elodie catching it out of the air with both hands, holding it up between herself and the storm, and letting go of six years of careful restraint all at once.

Lightning came down the mountain's throat and through her and out of her fingers, and it took the contract apart the way lightning takes apart a tree — not torn, *shattered*, splinters of burning vellum spinning through the hall, and every moving line of ink screaming as it died, little worm-trails of light writhing and curling and going dark like burnt hair.

Maren later swore the noise the ink made was words. Elodie chose not to investigate.

When the thunder finished rolling around the hall and came back to settle, the table was scorched black, the chair opposite hers was in pieces, and the air smelled like the inside of a bell. Elodie stood in the middle of it with smoke rising off her knuckles, hair standing out in all directions, breathing hard.

"Well," she said, to nobody in particular. "That's torn it."

From the doorway, Maren said, weakly, "You couldn't have just ripped it?"

"It reassembles if you rip it."

"You *knew* that?"

"Guesswork," Elodie admitted. "Educated guesswork."

Edmund was staring at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — somewhere between awe and acute secondhand terror, the look of a boy watching his governess juggle knives. Then, slowly, deliberately, he raised both hands and applauded. Three claps. Deadpan. Maren snorted despite herself, and something in Elodie's chest unclenched for half a second.

Then the temperature dropped.

* * *

The Auditor stood amid the wreckage of its furniture, and it had stopped wearing faces altogether. What remained was tall and grey and featureless as an unsolved equation, and where its voice had been warm, it was now simply *accurate* — every word landing with the weight of a stamp coming down on paper.

"That," it said, "was destruction of court property, assault upon an officer of the Ledger, and breach of negotiation protocol, section nine." A pause. "Also, you have ruined four hundred years of carpeting."

"Noted," Elodie said. "Add it to my bill."

"Oh, I intend to." The featureless head turned, and Elodie felt its attention swing to her like a searchlight. "You misunderstand your position, Mistress Dunmore. You believe you have refused the transaction. You have not. You have merely declined the *generous* version of it." It began to walk toward her, unhurried, and with every step the candles in the hall guttered lower, and the deep heartbeat of the engines beneath them slowed — slowed — like a heart deciding whether to bother. "The debt of the Dunmore line stands. Three generations of partial payments, accruing interest, compounding. Your grandmother's margin-notes. Your great-grandmother's famine bargain. All of it, due." It stopped an arm's length away. "I offered you installments. You have chosen the lump sum."

Behind her, Maren's hand closed on her shoulder. "Elodie," she said quietly. "What does lump sum mean, in a place that collects memories and voices?"

"It means," the Auditor said, hearing her anyway — it heard everything, that was rather its nature — "that the account comes due *now*. In full. Everything owed by the line of Dunmore, calculated to the final farthing." The grey shape spread its hands, and above them, impossibly, the air filled with pages — thousands of them, tens of thousands, a blizzard of ledger-leaves unfolding out of nothing, each one dense with moving ink. "Her memories. Her voice. Her name. Her years. Line by line, page by page, until the balance reads zero and what remains of Elodie Dunmore is..." It considered. "...archived."

"You can't," Maren said. "There's a counter-song. There's always been a counter-song. The court fears it —"

"The court *respects* it," the Auditor corrected. "As one respects weather. And speaking of weather—" the blank face angled toward Elodie, and though it had no eyes, she felt it looking directly into her, taking inventory, appraising her the way she'd once seen merchants appraise cattle, "—you will need yours, storm-caller. Because the collection begins immediately, and you are welcome to resist. Resistance is, historically, how the largest payments get made."

The pages whirled closer. In the doorway, Edmund stepped forward — stepped *between*, actually, planting his small body in front of her with his chin up and his fists balled, and from his chest came a hum, low and gathering, and Elodie realized with a lurch that dozens of voices were humming with him, the stolen ones, rising to meet whatever came.

And Elodie — smoke-fingered, half-deaf, her hair a disaster, her heart hammering — felt something entirely inappropriate to the moment rise in her throat.

Laughter. Actual laughter, wild and bright, because they were going to fight a ledger with a song and a storm, and it was absurd, and it was *theirs*.

"All right," she said, cracking her knuckles, sparks jumping between them. "Let's audit *this*."

Above them, the broken ceiling still gaped open onto the night sky, and far beyond the mountain, clouds were already turning, answering her like dogs hearing a whistle.

The Auditor raised its hand. The pages descended.

And in the doorway, Maren drew a breath and began to sing.
