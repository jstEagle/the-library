## 18. The Counter-Song

*Edmund*

The choir hall was not a hall. That was the first lie Edmund's eyes told him, and he forgave them for it, because there was no honest way to describe a place that was mostly listening.

It had a floor of black stone polished by ten thousand bare feet. It had pillars that rose until the darkness ate them. And it had the choir — hundreds of souls arranged in terraced rows like an orchard of pale fruit, each one standing very still, each one singing. Not with their mouths. With the place where their mouths used to matter.

He knew some of them.

Not their names — he had never known their names — but their parts. The woman two rows up who held the low drone that kept the mountain's heart beating. The thin man who did the rising runs at the change of every hour, so the Undercourt would know time was passing and could bill it accordingly. His mother stood near the front, her hands folded, her voice pouring out of her in a silver thread that wound up into the dark and did not come back.

*She is tired,* Edmund thought, in his formal, careful way, the way he thought all things. *She has been tired for eight years and no one has asked her to be anything else.*

Behind him, Maren rolled her shoulders the way she did before hauling a rope, and said something in her gravel voice that he felt more than heard: "Right then. Worst concert of our lives, or best. No middle option."

Elodie stood on his other side, her hair still crackling from the door-toll, her sister's name — or the hole where it had been — written all over her face in the shape of grief she could no longer read. She looked at him. He looked at her. Neither of them said anything, because there was nothing either of them could say that the hall would not hear and add to the ledger.

Maren lifted her chin toward the choir. "When I start," she murmured, "you give them back their own melody. Not mine. Theirs. You'll know it when you feel it."

Edmund nodded. He had been knowing it his whole life. Every stolen voice in his chest had been humming its true song to him since the night the collectors first came, patient as prisoners tapping on walls.

Maren breathed in — a ferrywoman's breath, deep and oared — and began to sing.

* * *

*Maren*

Her half-soul had never once done what she asked of it in forty years. Tonight she asked it for everything.

The counter-song was old — older than the Auditor, older than the ledger, from back when debts between people were settled over tables instead of in courtrooms. Her mother had sung it over washing. Her grandmother had sung it over graves. It was not complicated. That was the cruelty of it: the Undercourt had built an empire on the fact that nobody believed something this simple could undo something this large.

*You were given, you were taken,
you were borrowed, you were lent.
What was carried can be set down.
What was spent is not yet spent.*

Her voice came out rough and cracked and human, and the hall noticed immediately. A thousand hollow faces turned by degrees, like flowers finding a strange sun. The harmony of the court shivered — one thread in the great fabric pulling sideways — and somewhere above them, in the dark where the pillars ended, something vast shifted its weight.

*There you are,* Maren thought grimly. *Come and see.*

Her half-soul ached the way a missing limb aches, and into that ache she poured the second verse, and the third. Her one eye watered. Her knees wanted to quit. Forty years of running from this place, and here she stood in its own throat singing lullabies at it.

And then — small, clear, steady as a bell in fog — the boy began to sing along.

No. Not along. *Over.* He was doing the thing only he could do: reaching into the choir with that impossible chest of his and handing each soul back its own line, its own note, the melody it had been made to forget. The woman with the low drone faltered, caught a different note — hers — and wept without moving. The thin man stopped counting hours and started, wonderingly, counting something else.

The fabric of the court's harmony began to unravel into hundreds of separate songs, and every single one of them was beautiful, because they were *chosen.*

"Good lad," Maren whispered between verses. "Good lad. Elodie — sky."

* * *

*Elodie*

There was no sky. That was the whole problem.

Elodie had called storms across open moors, over harbors, off cliff-tops into the grinning sea. But a storm needed a sky the way a song needed silence around it, and the Undercourt offered neither. The ceiling of the choir hall was a darkness so complete it pressed down like a hand.

So she would have to make do.

She closed her eyes and reached for the thing Maren had spent three cold weeks teaching her to reach for — not clouds, not wind, but the *tension before* them. The itchy hush. The moment on a summer afternoon when the air holds its breath and every animal in the field looks up at once. That feeling lived inside people too. It lived inside this hall, thick as tar: ten hundred years of unpaid balances, of fear filed neatly away, of a whole court waiting for someone to finally come collect *from it.*

"You've been holding your breath," she said softly, to the mountain, to the ledger, to the dark. "Let it out."

She raised her hands.

The storm did not come from anywhere. There was nowhere for it to come from. It simply *was*, suddenly, the way it always was when she called honestly — lightning uncoiling through the black pillars, thunder rolling down corridors that had never heard weather, rain that fell upward and sideways and in colors rain had no business being. The Undercourt's perfect stillness broke like ice on a pond in spring.

Somewhere far above, alarms she didn't want to think about began ringing.

"Language of the sky!" Maren shouted over the din, laughing now, wild-eyed. "Verse four!"

Elodie laughed too — actually laughed, in the middle of the enemy's house, with lightning coming out of her fingertips for the second time in her life and the first time in her heart. She sang the fourth verse in thunder. It fit. It had always, somehow, been meant to fit.

And in the center of it all, small and dry and safe inside the circle of her storm, stood Edmund, conducting.

* * *

*Edmund*

He conducted the way his mother had taught him to breathe — without thinking about it, without stopping.

His arms moved and the storm obeyed, not because he commanded it but because he *listened* to it, found its rhythm under Elodie's fury, and gave it shape. A storm was only weather that hadn't found its part in the choir yet. He'd known that forever. Nobody had ever asked him.

The freed voices swirled around him like starlings. Some wept. Some laughed — real laughter, rusty and shocked at itself. A few simply sat down on the black stone, right there among the falling rain, and were quiet for the first time in decades, and their quiet was the loveliest part of the whole music.

His mother turned.

Their eyes met across four rows of the unravelling choir, and Edmund saw the exact moment her own melody reached her — saw her straighten, saw her hands come unfolded, saw her mouth form his name for the first time since the famine year. No sound came out. The contract still held her voice; that debt wasn't broken yet. But her lips shaped *Edmund* twice, three times, like practicing, like a woman remembering how.

He kept conducting. His arms did not shake. Later — if there was a later — he would fall apart about it properly, in order, with footnotes. For now there was only the music and the storm and the terrible bright feeling in his chest that was not the stolen voices at all.

It was hope, arriving in a body that had never had room for it before.

* * *

*Maren*

Then the Auditor came down.

It did not walk in. It arrived the way a bill arrives — suddenly, everywhere, unavoidable — standing at the head of the hall in a grey robe that was either cloth or smoke or columns of figures, and where its face should have been there was a slow turning page.

"*Unscheduled expenditure,*" it said. Its voice was every collector's voice at once, and underneath that, faint and awful, a human voice worn down to a nub. "*Unauthorized restitution. All parties will be assessed.*"

"Assess this," said Maren, and hit the fifth verse at full sail.

The counter-song and the Auditor's displeasure met somewhere in the middle air of the hall and ground against each other like millstones. The freed souls gripped each other's hands. Rain hissed off the black stone. Lightning wrote brief furious ledgers of its own across the dark.

Maren's half-soul burned. This was the cost she'd always known was coming, the fee for the guide, the promise she'd extracted from herself on the road all those years ago: *if you ever get the chance to go back, you go back, and you pay whatever it takes.* Her remaining half flickered like a candle in a doorway. She sang anyway. Half a soul was plenty, it turned out, if you spent it all at once.

"Boy!" she rasped. "Your mother! Bring her forward — she's the key of the famine-debt — Elodie! Keep the sky open!"

* * *

*Elodie*

Keeping the sky open was becoming a full-time occupation.

The Auditor raised one grey hand and the darkness *leaned* — pressing down against her storm like a thumb on a beetle, patient, administrative, certain that everything could be brought back into balance with sufficient pressure. Elodie's bones creaked. Her vision doubled. Somewhere in her chest, her power guttered like a lamp running dry.

*You cannot sustain this,* said the pressure, without words. *Nothing given freely is sustainable. Only what is owed endures.*

"Well," Elodie gritted, teeth bared, hair standing in wet haloes around her head, "I've never once in my life paid a debt on schedule, so let's find out."

She reached deeper than she ever had — past the ridgeline of her own fear, past Hollowmere, past the safe powerless valley where magic couldn't follow, all the way down to the girl she'd been before any of it, the one who had loved storms before she learned what her family spent them on. That girl was still in there. That girl had never stopped wanting to see the sky do something impossible.

The storm answered her like a dog answers its true name.

Lightning split the dark from pillar to pillar, and in its white flare Elodie saw the whole hall laid bare — the choir free and singing, Maren fierce and fading, Edmund with his arms raised and his mother's hand just closing around his — and for one suspended heartbeat, found family functioned as one instrument, and the instrument was playing *her*, and she had never in her exiled, careful, lonely life felt less alone.

The Auditor's page-face fluttered wildly.

"*Anomaly,*" it said, and for the first time its many voices were not perfectly aligned. "*Anomaly. Recalculating. RECALCULATING—*"

"Verse six," Elodie said, to both of them, and together they sang it.

* * *

*Edmund*

The last verse was his alone.

He had known this too, the way he knew his belt-buckle letters: the counter-song ended where it began, with the smallest debtor, the original creditor of kindness. His mother's voice joined his — hoarse, broken, *hers* — and the famine-debt that had started everything trembled in the ledger like a stitch about to slip.

*You were given, you were taken,
you were borrowed, you were lent—*

Above them, with a sound like the world's largest book being slammed shut by someone's angry elbow, the ledger began to crack.

And the Auditor stopped recalculating, and went very still, and looked — if a turning page can be said to look — directly at Elodie Dunmore.

"*Then,*" it said softly, in the one small human voice beneath all the others, "*we shall assess you.*"

The storm held its breath. The rain hung in the air like beads on a string. Edmund's mother pulled him close, and Maren staggered toward them with her one eye blazing, and high overhead the crack in the ledger spread with a sound like ice going out on a spring river — and Elodie stepped forward, alone, into the Auditor's shadow.

Edmund opened his mouth to sing warning, or protest, or simply her name —

but the ledger, cracking, had already begun to write.
