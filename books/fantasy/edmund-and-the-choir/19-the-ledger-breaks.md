## 19. The Ledger Breaks

The Auditor's contract hung in the air between them, and it was not paper.

It was a sheet of light stretched taut across the choir hall, tall as the vaulted dark above them, and every line of it was written in a hand that made her eyes water to read — not from effort but from *recognition*, as if each clause were something she had always known and only now been permitted to see. The ink of it moved. The ink of it breathed. Somewhere beneath the hall, in the roots of the mountain, a vast engine of harmony turned and turned, and this contract was one thread of it.

*Sign,* said the Auditor, in a voice like a ledger closing, like ten thousand quills touching paper at once. *And the boy walks free. And you will never again be powerless. You will be the storm that keeps the Court alive.*

Elodie looked at the visions it offered her — they played across the contract's surface like light on water: Hollowmere safe forever, storms turned aside from its ridgeline by her own hand; Edmund grown tall and whole; Maren's missing half-soul restored; famine after famine broken before it could take root. Everything she had ever wanted to protect, arranged like fruit on a table, ripe and gleaming and *offered*.

And behind all of it, faintly, she saw the cages. Niches in stone. Bottles of voice. Rows upon rows of the indebted, singing.

"You're showing me what I could guard," she said quietly. "But you're not showing me what I'd *be*."

The Auditor tilted its head. It wore a shape tonight — it liked shapes, Maren had warned her, shapes were how it thought — and the shape it wore was almost kind: a grey-robed figure with a face like an unfinished portrait, features suggested rather than committed. Its hands were folded. Its patience was geological.

*I would be a keeper of order,* it said.

"You'd be a keeper of cages." Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. "I've spent three years learning the difference between peace and a locked door, Auditor. I'm not going to build you a bigger one."

She tore the contract with lightning.

It was not elegant. Later — if there was a later, if there was any *after* left to arrange things in — she would remember it as the least graceful act of magic of her life, less a storm-call than a scream with weather attached. But her grandmother's songbook had taught her the forms, and Maren had taught her the rhythm, and Edmund — Edmund standing beside her with his chest full of a hundred stolen voices, humming the counter-song's first verse in that dead tongue that no living child should know — Edmund had taught her that a storm did not have to be tidy to be true.

Light came down through the mountain itself, through a thousand feet of stone, because she called it and it answered. It struck the contract and the contract tore down the middle with a sound like a library burning.

The hall went silent. Even the engine beneath them seemed to hold its breath.

Then the Auditor stopped being kind.

*THE DEBT IS DUE,* it said, and its unfinished face opened, and behind the face was arithmetic.

* * *

What came for her first was not pain. It was *inventory*.

She felt herself being read. That was the only word for it — a cold finger running down the spine of her, opening her the way you'd open a book you already owned, and beginning to strike out lines. She felt the moment her memory of mending nets went — a small thing, the smell of tar, the way the twine bit your fingers on cold mornings — and it simply wasn't there anymore, and worse, she could feel the *hole* where it had been, a little socket of absence, smooth-edged, deliberate.

*ASSET: ELODIE DUNMORE,* the Auditor intoned, somewhere far away and everywhere at once. *LINE ITEMS AS FOLLOWS—*

"No," said Maren, and began to sing.

The counter-song rose through the hall — rough, sea-worn, half a soul's worth of voice pushed to its whole limit — and Elodie understood, with the strange clarity of someone being erased, what Maren was doing. The counter-song did not fight the ledger. The counter-song *amended* it. Every debt demanded a payment; the Auditor had chosen to collect Elodie entire. The counter-song was offering terms. *Take the memories,* it sang. *Only the memories. Line by line. Not the woman.*

*AMENDMENT REJECTED—*

"Amendments can't be rejected," Maren snarled, between verses, "if they're *filed properly*, you great grey vulture, and I filed mine three hundred years ago when you took my eye—" and her voice climbed, and the song caught, and held.

And then Edmund stepped forward, and put his small hand flat against the torn contract, and added his voice to hers.

A hundred souls sang through him at once. That was the horror of it and the miracle of it: the voices of everyone the Undercourt had ever bottled, every debtor, every famine-bargain, every frightened mother and hollow-eyed father, all of them rising through one small boy's chest and out into the hall, and they were not singing the Auditor's harmony anymore. They were singing their own melody back to themselves. The engine beneath the mountain shuddered. Bottled voices in the tunnel-niches behind them began, softly, one by one, to harmonize.

The ledger cracked. Elodie heard it crack — a sound like ice going out on a spring river, vast and splintering and, absurdly, hopeful.

But the Auditor was still collecting. And she was still the nearest asset.

*LINE ITEM:* it said, and struck, and her grandmother's kitchen went — the blue crockery, the smell of cinnamon, gone. *LINE ITEM,* and her first storm, the one she'd called at nine years old off her grandmother's rooftop, giddy and terrified, gone. *LINE ITEM,* and her sister's laugh, which she had already paid once at the mountain's mouth and somehow, impossibly, had gotten back or kept a copy of or the ledger had double-counted, and now it went again, truly, permanently, and she felt herself reach for it the way you reach for a stair that isn't there.

"Don't count," she gasped, "don't count my life out loud, you *coward*, come and count it to my *face*—" and she threw another bolt, and another, wild ones, lighting the hall in strobes, and each flash showed her the same tableau: Maren singing with blood at the corner of her mouth, Edmund with his head thrown back and a hundred voices pouring out of him, and the Auditor's unfinished face beginning, at last, to look *worried*.

Because here is what the Auditor had not accounted for, in all its long columns: it had built its court on the assumption that the indebted would keep singing the harmony it gave them. And it had collected so many voices, over so many centuries, that it had never once asked them what they'd rather sing.

The ledger cracked again. And again.

* * *

Time went strange inside the storm. Elodie lost track of it — lost, frankly, interest in it. There was only the next line item, and the next hole where a piece of her used to be, and the counter-song holding the borders of her like levees hold a flood, and her own lightning answering her call after call even as the woman doing the calling got smaller and simpler and stranger to herself.

She felt the valley go. Hollowmere, the ridgeline, the nets, the cottage with the crooked doorstep — struck, struck, struck. She felt three years of quiet exile fold up and vanish, and with it, inexplicably, the loneliness that had lived underneath it, and she thought, distantly, *that's not fair, you can't bill me for a feeling* — and then couldn't remember what feeling she'd meant.

She felt names go. Her grandmother's. Her mother's. Her own surname flickered like a guttering candle.

*FINAL ENTRIES,* said the Auditor, and there was something new in its voice now, something that in a human face would have been panic. *ASSET TO BE STRUCK IN FULL. TOTAL CANCELLATION OF—*

"*No.*"

Edmund's voice. His real voice — the dead tongue, yes, but underneath it, for the first time, something else: a boy's voice, eight years old, cracking with fury. He had both hands against the ledger now. The hundred souls in him had become a chord so wide and deep that the stone walls sweated condensation, and in the niches beyond the doors, bottles were beginning to sing themselves empty.

He looked up at the Auditor and said, in words Elodie somehow understood without understanding the language:

"She paid. She paid at the gate, she paid in the storm, she's paying *now*. You don't get the rest. A ledger has *rules*."

And the rules, it turned out, were the one thing the Auditor feared.

* * *

Later — in whatever survived to be later — she would try to reconstruct those final minutes and fail, because most of the material for reconstruction was gone. What remained was impressions, like footprints in snow after the snow-melt: 

An impression of the ledger splitting down its whole length, top to bottom, light pouring out of the wound like yolk from a broken egg.

An impression of the Auditor screaming in ten thousand quill-strokes, its unfinished face finally committing to an expression, and the expression was *loss*, and Elodie felt, even as she forgot her own middle name, a flash of terrible pity for the thing — because it had once been human, Maren had said, and what was left of it was only the accounting, only the hunger for everything to balance, and nothing about it would ever balance now.

An impression of the engine beneath the mountain winding down, note by note, the great forced harmony unraveling into hundreds of small free melodies, the way a flock becomes birds again when the hawk leaves the sky.

And one impression more, sharper than any other, burned past the erasure into some part of her the ledger couldn't reach:

Edmund turning toward her mid-storm, mid-song, mid-*miracle* — and stopping. Stopping as though he'd walked into glass. Because the soul that had been sold before he was old enough to object was coming home to him — she watched it happen, or she believed she did: a brightness rising out of the broken ledger, out of the wrecked engine, out of the hundred freed voices, and settling into the boy's chest like a bird returning to a roost, and he gasped, and staggered, and Maren caught him under the arms, and when he lifted his head his eyes were wet and *whole* and entirely, unmistakably his own.

His soul had returned to him in the middle of the storm.

And he looked at her — at the woman who had crossed a kingdom and broken a mountain and unmade a court for him — and his face did something she had never seen it do in all the weeks since the knock in the rain.

It crumpled.

"Edmund," she started to say, reaching for him. "Edmund, love, we did it, it's over, we—"

But even as she said the name, she heard how it sounded: borrowed. A word she knew belonged to the boy without knowing why she knew it, the way you know a coat fits without remembering ever wearing it. Something warm moved through her when she looked at him — fierce, protective, older than the holes in her memory — but underneath the warmth there was nothing. No rain-soaked night at her door. No spirals drawn in ash. No small hand steadying hers while lightning learned her name again.

She looked at the boy and loved him and could not say, from anywhere inside herself, *why*.

"Edmund," she said again, testing it. "Is — is that right? Is that your name?"

Maren made a sound low in her throat that might have been grief or might have been a prayer. Edmund stared at her, and the wholeness in his eyes broke into pieces, and he nodded — a slow, careful nod, the nod of a child deciding not to cry in front of someone, and Elodie felt the warmth in her chest twist like a knife, because she could not remember why it should twist, only that it must.

The last of the ledger's light guttered out around them. In the sudden dark, the freed voices of the choir were rising up the tunnels toward the open sky, singing as they went — and among them, faintly, a woman's voice that Edmund turned toward as it passed, and reached for, and caught, and held.

The mountain settled into silence around the three of them: a storm-caller who could no longer remember the valley she'd fought her way out of, a ferrywoman with blood on her teeth and tears cutting tracks through the grime, and a boy who was, for the first time in his whole life, entirely himself — and who alone remembered everything.

Edmund wiped his face with his sleeve. He straightened. He looked up at the stranger who had once been Elodie Dunmore, and he took a breath, and began — very quietly, in the dead tongue, the way you begin a story you intend to tell for the rest of your life —

"Well," he said. "I suppose I'd better introduce myself."
