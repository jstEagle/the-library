## 20. Aftermath in the Dark

The first thing I understood, when the singing stopped, was that my chest was very quiet.

I had never known quiet before. I do not mean silence, for there was always the hum of the others — the two hundred and eleven voices that lived behind my ribs like swallows in a barn, turning in their sleep, murmuring their old names to themselves. I had carried them since I was six years old, when Mother signed the ledger with her tears still wet on the page, and I had thought, in the simple way of children, that quiet was a thing that happened to other people.

Now the swallows had flown home. My chest was only a chest. When I breathed in, nothing answered.

I sat on the floor of the choir hall, in the wreckage of the ledger, and I listened to the mountain settle around me like an old dog circling three times before lying down. Stones clicked somewhere far above. A great chain, the one that had held the harmony-tanks, went slack with a sound like a sigh. The bottled voices in the walls were gone from their niches — every bottle empty, every cork popped, every soul gone whistling up the long tunnels toward the daylight and its terrible abundance of ordinary noise.

Maren was laughing. It was not a pretty laugh; it was the laugh of a woman who has been holding her breath for forty years, and it came out of her in ugly, wonderful hiccups. She sat slumped against the broken lectern with her one eye closed and her good hand pressed flat against the floor as if checking whether the Undercourt was still real. Her half-soul, the half she had left behind all those years ago, had come back to her in the unbinding — I had felt it go past me like a fish under ice, quick and silver and glad — and now she was twice what she had been, and twice as likely to weep.

"It's over," she kept saying. "Edmund. Edmund, it's over. Do you hear me? It's *over*."

"I hear you," I said. And then, because my interior voice is formal even at the best of times, and this was perhaps the best of times: "I hear myself also. It is a novel experience. I recommend it."

That made her laugh harder, which made her cough, which made her swear in ferryman's oaths that would have gotten my mouth washed out if Mother had been there to hear them.

Mother.

I turned to look for her before I could stop myself.

She was kneeling on the far side of the hall, near the place where the Auditor's desk had stood before Elodie's lightning tore it into interesting pieces. Her hands were folded in her lap. Her famine-debt — sixty years of singing, owed because she had once asked the court to make the wheat grow — was struck from the record along with everyone else's, and she was free, and she was looking at me across the ruined hall with an expression I did not have a name for.

She had not seen me since I was six. She had sung beside my stolen voice for four years without knowing it lived in a boy who grew. I stood up. My legs shook. I walked to her the way you walk toward a door you are afraid will be locked.

"Mother," I said.

"Edmund." My name in her mouth. The dead tongue of our old village, the one she had taught me, the one no living person speaks — it came back into the world just then, between us, and it was warm. She put her hands on either side of my face, the way she used to, and she looked at me for a long time, and then she said something in the old tongue that translates, roughly, to: *You got tall.*

"You got old," I said, in the same tongue.

"Sixty years," she said. "It does that." Then she hugged me, hard, and I learned that a person can be whole and still crack a little, in the good way, like bread.

* * *

We climbed out of the mountain together, the freed choir and the three of us, and it took most of a day, and I will not describe all of it because some things belong to the people who walked them. But I will say that the tunnels sounded different empty. All those years, the passages under the mountain had hummed — the bottles humming, the choir humming, the great engine-harmony that fed the Auditor's ledgers. Now they were just tunnels, dripping and dark and smelling of stone, and somehow that was more frightening than the singing ever was. Silence, I discovered, takes getting used to. It stands very close to you and does not explain itself.

Maren walked at the front with a lantern and told rude jokes to nobody in particular. Mother walked in the middle of the crowd, being thanked by strangers, and did not know where to put her hands. And Elodie —

Elodie walked at the back, alone.

I noticed it in the fourth tunnel. She kept stopping to touch the walls, the way you touch furniture in a house you have never visited but have been told is yours. Once she said, to no one, "So this is under a mountain," in the tone of someone confirming the existence of mountains generally. When I fell back to walk beside her she smiled at me politely. Politely. The way you smile at another guest at an inn.

"Are you hurt?" she asked me. "Your face has blood on it."

"The ledger cut me when it broke," I said. "It is shallow. Are *you* hurt? Storm-calling takes it out of your body. You once told me — you told me it felt like being struck by your own weather."

She considered this carefully, the way she considered everything now, as though each sentence arrived in an unfamiliar envelope.

"I don't remember telling you that," she said. "But it sounds like something I'd say."

And the cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain.

* * *

Here is what I knew, and here is what I would not yet let myself know.

The counter-song unmakes contracts. Maren taught us that in Chapter Twelve — I am permitted to think of our days as chapters; I have decided I am allowed this. But a contract is only words about a thing. The thing itself — my soul, my voice, the portion of me that sat in a bottle in the wall — the song reached down and pulled it out and gave it back. That was the miracle, and I felt it happen, felt my own voice come home to my throat like a cat returning through a window.

But payment is different from theft. The ledger demanded a price for the unbinding, and when the Auditor tried to take me entirely — tried to erase the boy along with the debt — the counter-song had done something desperate. It had offered Elodie instead. Not her body. Not her life. Her *record*. Everything written about her in the book of herself. Every line of memory the ledger had gathered during her time among us, struck out line by line to balance the column.

I had heard it happening. We were all singing; the storm was inside the mountain; the Auditor was screaming in a voice like tearing paper. And beneath everything, faint as rain under a hymn, I heard pages turning, and lines going silent — little voices in the ledger's margins winking out one at a time, like candles guttering in rhythm.

Candles guttering in rhythm. That was mine. That was the first thing she ever learned about me, in her kitchen, in the valley, on the night she realized magic had followed me in. I remembered it because it was mine. I had assumed it was hers too.

At the mouth of the mountain, in the grey morning light, I tested it.

"Do you know my name?" I asked her.

We were standing on the scree slope while Maren organized two hundred and eleven freed souls into something resembling a walking order, which turned out to be like organizing cats into a parade. Mother was nearby. The sky was enormous. I had not seen the sky in four years, and I kept forgetting to look at it because Elodie's face was more important.

She looked at me kindly. That was the worst part. She looked at me the way you look at a child who has asked you a question you must answer honestly.

"Should I?" she said. "I feel as though I should. There's a shape where an answer ought to be, if that makes sense. Like a word on the tip of the tongue." She touched her own chest, frowning. "There's a great deal missing, isn't there. I've been noticing it in pieces. I know I'm a storm-caller — that stayed, oddly, like a bone that survived a fire. I know my grandmother had a songbook, though I couldn't tell you a single song in it. I know—" she paused, and her brow creased, and for one moment something moved behind her eyes like a storm gathering on a ridgeline, gathering and refusing to cross. "I know I was somewhere safe, for a long time. A valley. Nets. I can almost smell mended nets." She laughed a small lost laugh. "I don't know why nets."

"Mended nets," I said. "Yours were the second-best on the river. Mine were better, after you taught me, but you said mine cheated because I hummed at them and the knots tightened out of sympathy."

Elodie stared at me.

"I said that?" she whispered.

"You said that."

"And you—?" She stopped. Started again. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they are carrying something breakable down stairs. "Who are you? Truly. Not your name — I understand I don't have that — but *who are you*, that you know how I talk?"

And I, Edmund, eight years old, formerly an asset, formerly collateral, formerly a choir of two hundred and twelve and now merely one — I opened my mouth to answer and found that I did not know where to begin. How do you summarize yourself to the person who wrote your beginning? Do you start with the belt-buckle and the carved letters? With the spirals in ash? With the night in the pass when her power woke and she screamed and laughed at once and I conducted her hands like a choirmaster until the lightning came when she called it?

"It's a long story," I said finally.

"I appear to have room for one," said Elodie Dunmore, who did not know she had ever been exiled, who did not know the valley's name, who did not know that the boy in front of her was the reason she had ever left it. "I seem to be missing rather a lot of them."

* * *

Maren found us there an hour later, sitting on a boulder, me telling the story from the beginning — the knock in the rain, the humming, the soup she burned because she was watching me instead of the pot. Maren stood listening with her arms crossed and her new-old soul sitting visibly inside her, making her stand straighter than she had in forty years.

"He doesn't have to do it all today," Maren said gently.

"Yes he does," I said. "She has a hole where I should be. Holes frighten people. I know about holes. I had one in my chest for four years and everyone who saw it flinched."

Elodie flinched. Just slightly. At the truth of it, I think, not at me.

Maren crouched down to my eye level. Her one eye was wet. "Listen to me, Edmund. What she paid — what the ledger took — it isn't destroyed. Nothing the ledger holds is ever destroyed; it's only filed. And the counter-song, the full counter-song, the one I only gave you half of because the other half needs a whole soul to sing—" she tapped my chest, right where the quiet was, right where my own voice now lived alone and unaccompanied — "it can read a record back to a person. Slowly. Line by line. If she chooses it. If she sings it herself. It cannot be forced, do you understand? Forced, it's just another contract. Offered, it's a door."

"A door," I repeated.

"A door," said Maren, "and she gets to decide whether to open it. Which means, little choirmaster, that your job is not to fill the hole. Your job is to keep her company while she decides."

I looked at Elodie. She was watching the freed choir straggle down the mountainside toward the valley road, two hundred and eleven people blinking in daylight, and among them my mother, who was glancing back at us every few steps and pretending not to. Elodie caught my mother looking and raised a hand in polite, bewildered greeting, and my mother — who remembered everything, who remembered the famine and the ledger and the signing and the four years of singing next to her son's stolen voice — burst into tears and pretended it was the wind.

"There's a woman down there," Elodie said quietly, "who keeps crying when she sees you. And I find that when I look at you, my chest hurts, and I don't know why, and normally I mind very much about not knowing things, but with you I find I don't mind. Isn't that strange?" She smiled the polite smile again, and then, wonderfully, the polite smile failed her, and underneath it was something older and warmer that had survived the fire along with the storm-calling, the way a bone survives. "It's as if some part of me already trusts you and forgot to file the paperwork."

I am a formal child. It is my nature. But I crawled across the boulder and leaned against her side, and she put her arm around me without hesitating, as though her body remembered what her record had lost.

"Then I'll tell you stories," I said. "Weekly. I know a great many about a woman who called storms."

"She sounds remarkable," said Elodie.

"She is," I said, and Maren, standing over us with the wind pulling at her coat, turned her face away and wiped her one eye and said gruffly that we ought to get moving if we meant to reach the low country before dark, and that somebody would have to teach these two hundred fools to walk in a line, and that the nearest town had a tavern and by heaven she was going to buy a drink with both halves of her soul for the first time in forty years.

So we started down the mountain. Behind us the Undercourt lay silent at last, a hollow engine, an empty ledger, a door with no keeper. Ahead lay the long road and the town with no bells and, beyond it, a green valley where a cottage waited with a cold hearth and a kitchen table and ash on the floor, ash in which someone might, someday, draw spirals again.

I walked between them — Maren ahead, Elodie's hand resting on my shoulder — and in my chest my single voice, my own, my hard-won, hummed very softly, testing its wings.

Two hundred and eleven voices had gone home.

One story remained to be told. And I intended to tell it slowly, and well, leaving out nothing — not the nets, not the burned soup, not the name carved on a belt-buckle — so that whenever she chose to open the door, everything she needed would be waiting on the other side of it, patient as a candle, ready to gutter in rhythm.
