## 21. Stranger at the Door

The first frost of autumn came to Hollowmere on a Tuesday, and Elodie Dunmore woke before dawn with the odd certainty that she had forgotten something important.

She lay in bed and let the feeling pass through her, the way she'd learned to do. It came most mornings now — a hollow ache behind the ribs, like a word sitting on the tip of the tongue that refused to be spoken. She had asked Doctor Pell about it once, back in the spring, when the ache was fresh and frightening.

"Memory's like a net," he'd said, tapping his pipe against his boot. "You mend what tears you can see. The ones you can't see, you just have to trust are mended too."

That hadn't been remotely helpful, but it had been kind, and kindness counted for a great deal in Hollowmere.

She rose, banked the fire, and put the kettle on. Outside her window the valley lay silvered and still, the ridgeline sharp against a pale sky, the peaks beyond it wearing their first white caps of the season. Storms gathered up there sometimes — she'd noticed that early on, in her first weeks in the village, when everything was new and she was still learning which neighbor would trade eggs for mending and which would only trade gossip. The clouds piled up dark and heavy over the high country and then, without fail, they broke apart at the ridgeline like waves against a seawall, spilling nothing but wind into the valley.

It should have bothered her more than it did. There was something about those broken storms, something that plucked at a string inside her she couldn't find the end of. But the feeling never resolved into anything, and eventually she'd stopped chasing it, the way you stop probing a tooth that isn't quite right.

She was who she was. That was what she told herself. A woman of about thirty who had arrived in Hollowmere two seasons ago with a mending basket, a songbook she never opened, and no story anyone could get out of her because — this was the strange part — she didn't have one to give.

*Elodie Dunmore.* She knew her name the way you know your own hands. Everything else was fog with occasional shapes in it.

The kettle sang. She made tea and sat by the window with her mending, and that was when she saw him coming down the lane.

* * *

He came every week. Thursdays, mostly, though once or twice he'd come on a Sunday with an apologetic shrug that suggested Thursdays were a courtesy rather than a rule.

He was perhaps eight years old, small for his age, with dark hair that never quite behaved and eyes so old they made her chest hurt in a way she couldn't explain. He wore a coat a size too big for him and a belt with a brass buckle stamped with letters — *E-D-M-U-N-D*, she'd sounded them out the first week, and he had nodded so hard his whole body shook, as if she'd given him a gift.

Edmund. That was his name. She was sure of it, and sure that she was sure, which was about as much certainty as her life contained these days.

What she couldn't tell anyone — what she had stopped trying to explain even to herself — was why the sight of him coming up her path made her feel like a hearth lighting from the inside.

There was no reason for it. She had no memory of ever meeting the boy before his visits began. Widow Tansy across the lane said he'd turned up in the village around the same time Elodie herself had, and that the two of them had been "thick as thieves from the start," but when Elodie reached for the memory of that beginning, she found only smooth blankness, like a page someone had carefully scraped clean.

It didn't matter. That was the thing she'd finally made peace with. Whatever the history was, the warmth was real, and warmth didn't need a receipt.

She opened the door before he knocked, because she always did, and his face did the thing it always did — the careful composure cracking open into a grin that transformed him from a solemn little old man in a child's body into simply a child.

"You're early," she said, which was their joke, because he was never early; he arrived at precisely the same time every week, as punctual as the post.

He shrugged elaborately and held up the basket he carried: three apples, a twist of honeycomb, and a loaf of bread that smelled like it had been baked by someone who took bread very seriously.

"For me? You shouldn't have." She stepped aside to let him in. "Although you absolutely should have, because I've eaten nothing but porridge since Monday and I've forgotten how flavor works."

He rolled his eyes — he was fluent in eye-rolling, despite not speaking — and set the basket on the table with the gravity of a merchant closing a deal.

That was the other thing about Edmund. He didn't talk.

Not wouldn't — couldn't, or at least didn't in any way the village understood. Widow Tansy said he'd spoken once, when he first arrived, words in some language nobody knew, "old-sounding words, like out of a church nobody goes to anymore." After that, nothing. He communicated in gestures, expressions, and a system of humming that was somehow more legible than speech. A rising hum meant *yes*. A flat hum meant *no*. A particular three-note phrase, low and sweet, meant — as far as Elodie could decode it — something between *thank you* and *I'm glad you exist*, and it undid her every single time.

She'd tried teaching him to write. He could read well enough — she'd discovered that when he'd corrected her spelling of *separate* with a look of profound disappointment — but when she put a pencil in his hand and asked him to write his story, he'd gone very still, and then very carefully set the pencil down, and then hugged her, hard, around the middle, and she'd never asked again.

Some things, his stillness said, were heavier than paper could hold.

* * *

They fell into their ritual. She put the kettle back on. He claimed his chair — the good chair, the one by the window, which she had long ago stopped offering to anyone else. She got out her mending, and he got out his whittling, and for a while they simply sat together in the comfortable silence of two people who don't need to fill the air.

Then, as he always did, he began to tell her a story.

He told them with his hands and his face and his humming, and he was, she thought, the finest storyteller in three counties, possibly in the world. Today's story began with him pointing at her, then at the window, then making a gesture she recognized: fingers spread wide, arm sweeping down through the air, accompanied by a sound like wind — *whooooosh* — and a crackle of finger-snaps like rain on a roof.

"Oh no," she said, smiling. "Is it the storm lady again?"

He nodded vigorously, eyes shining.

The storm lady. It was his favorite story, the one he returned to week after week, telling it with new details each time, elaborating and revising like any true artist. In Edmund's telling, there was a woman — tall, fierce, hair like a thundercloud — who could call lightning out of a clear sky. She had fought monsters. She had crossed mountains. She had walked down into the dark under the world and argued with kings, and the kings had lost, because you cannot out-bureaucrat a woman who commands the weather.

"And where does she live, this storm lady?" Elodie asked, threading her needle. This was part of the ritual too. She always asked. He always answered the same way.

Edmund pointed at her.

"Me?" She laughed. "I can barely call the cat in from the rain."

But he wasn't laughing. He pointed at her again, insistently, and then pressed his hand flat over his own heart, and looked at her with those old old eyes, and hummed the three-note phrase — the *thank you, I'm glad you exist* one — but slower this time, and sadder, and she had to look down at her mending until the moment passed.

"It sounds like a fine woman," she said quietly. "Whoever she was."

He hummed agreement, and the sadness folded itself away somewhere, and he leaned over to inspect her stitching and make the face that said *your tension is terrible*, and just like that they were back on solid ground.

* * *

Afternoon came down golden through the window. The bread was half-eaten, the honeycomb reduced to wreckage. Edmund had fallen into one of his quiet spells, chin on his fists, watching the light move across the floor, and Elodie was re-threading a net panel that had more holes than net when a knock came at the door.

Not Edmund's knock — he never knocked anymore, having established sovereignty over the household months ago. This was a firm double-rap, businesslike.

She opened the door on a tall woman in a traveling coat the color of wet slate, gray-streaked hair cropped short, and a single bright eye that took Elodie's measure in one sweep and apparently found her acceptable. The woman's other eye was gone — not bandaged, simply absent, the lid sewn or healed shut over a scar that ran temple to cheekbone.

Something about her made the back of Elodie's neck prickle. Not fear, exactly. Recognition without content — the feeling of hearing a song you're sure you know and being unable to name it.

"Afternoon," the woman said. Her voice was low and roughened, like a rope that had hauled a lot of boats. "You Dunmore?"

"I'm Elodie Dunmore, yes."

"Maren." The woman didn't offer a hand. She glanced past Elodie's shoulder, into the room, and her gaze found Edmund in his chair, and something moved across her face — relief and grief tangled together, too fast to name. "Came to deliver a parcel. Been carrying it a long way."

"I think you may have the wrong—"

"Maren!" Edmund was already past Elodie, and the woman crouched to catch him as he collided with her, and the hug they exchanged was the hug of people who have been through something together, something with weight to it, and Elodie stood in her own doorway feeling like a guest.

"You're looking taller," Maren said into his hair. "Still eating?"

He hummed the affirmative note.

"Good lad." She straightened, wincing slightly, and dug into the satchel at her hip. What she brought out was wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and she handled it the way you handle a sleeping snake. "This is for you, Dunmore. Not him. Him I'd trust with my life. You—" that bright eye fixed on Elodie, and there was something ferocious in it, something almost angry, except that anger wasn't quite the shape of it, "—you I'm trusting with something better than my life. Don't lose it. Don't burn it. And when you're ready — and only when you're ready — read it."

She pushed the parcel into Elodie's hands. It was heavier than it looked. Through the oilcloth Elodie could feel the corners of pages, and something else, long and thin, like a rod of glass or bone.

"What is it?"

"A door," Maren said. "Locked from your side." She shrugged her satchel higher. "I'll be going. Long road yet. Ferry doesn't run itself."

"You could stay," Elodie heard herself say. "Supper's simple, but there's enough."

For a moment Maren looked at her with an expression Elodie couldn't read at all — a whole weather system passing behind one eye — and then she smiled, crooked and tired and real.

"Another time," she said. "There's a version of this evening where I stay, and it's a good one. But it's not this one." She crouched again, briefly, to Edmund's level. He gripped her sleeve and hummed something — a longer phrase, urgent, questioning — and she shook her head. "Not yet. But soon. Keep singing, little wren. You're the best thing I ever taught anybody."

She kissed the top of his head, stood, nodded once to Elodie — *"Dunmore"* — and went down the lane with the long stride of a woman who has always walked away from places.

Edmund watched her go from the doorway, his hand raised, until the road bent and took her.

* * *

That evening, after Edmund had gone home to Widow Tansy's — pausing at the gate to give Elodie the three-note hum, which she returned, badly, to his evident delight — Elodie sat by the fire with the parcel in her lap and did not open it.

She turned it over. She weighed it. She held it near the light and saw, through a gap in the oilcloth, the edge of a page covered in handwriting that was cramped and dense and somehow familiar, the way her own reflection in a dark window is familiar.

Her grandmother's songbook sat on its shelf where it had always sat. She had never opened it. She couldn't have said why, only that touching it felt like pressing on a bruise she couldn't find.

The parcel from Maren felt the same way. Bruise-shaped. Door-shaped.

*A door locked from your side.*

"Well," she said aloud, to the fire, to the empty room, to whatever patient absence lived in the fog where her past should be. "That's dramatic."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, far off past the ridgeline, thunder muttered — a storm gathering up in the high country, piling itself against the peaks, unable to come down. She listened to it for a long time, that distant frustrated growling, and found her hand had drifted to rest flat against her own sternum, right over her heart, as if something in there were answering it.

Call and response. She didn't know how she knew that. She just did.

She set the parcel on the shelf beside the songbook, side by side, two locked doors waiting politely. Then she banked the fire and went to bed, and dreamed — as she often did — of standing on a mountaintop in a screaming wind with her arms flung wide, and in the dream she was not afraid, and when she woke in the grey morning she could still feel the ghost of lightning in her fingertips, and she lay there a while, holding her hands up against the ceiling, watching them tremble, wondering what they remembered that she didn't.

On the shelf, in the early light, the oilcloth parcel seemed almost to lean toward the songbook. As if the two of them were whispering.

As if they were waiting for her to choose.
