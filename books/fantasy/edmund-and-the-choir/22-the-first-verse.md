## 22. The First Verse

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives in Hollowmere in early spring, and I have decided it is my favorite of all the quiets I have catalogued. It is not the silence of the town that sold its bells — that silence was a held breath, a debt waiting to be called. This is a different thing entirely. This is the sound of nets drying on their frames, of Maren's ferry rope creaking at its mooring down by the water, of Elodie Dunmore humming to herself while she mends a hole she has mended four times already because she keeps finding new reasons to take the net back down.

She does not know why she hums that particular tune.

I know. I have known since the first week after we came home, when she caught herself mid-phrase and frowned at her own hands as though they had betrayed her, and said, *Where did I pick that up?* And I said nothing, because my whole chest was full of bees and broken glass, and because Maren had made me promise — one eye very serious over the fire — that I would let her come to it or not come to it, but I would not drag her.

So instead I said: "It's a good song."

"It's a strange song," she said. "It goes up where songs should go down." She hummed it again anyway. She always hums it again anyway.

That is the shape of our life now, and I want to write it down properly, because I am the only one who remembers how it was made, and a thing that only one person remembers is a candle with one wick left. So: this is the ledger of us, kept in the only place the Auditor can never collect from. My head. Which, I am pleased to report, is finally entirely mine.

* * *

I should explain what happened to me, since I am the narrator and narrators who keep secrets are worse than useless.

When the ledger broke — when Maren sang the unbinding and I gave the stolen voices back their own names and Elodie pulled a storm down inside a mountain, which I still think about most nights with something between awe and terror — my soul came home to me. That is the true word for it. *Home.* Not returned, not restored, not repossessed, though I suppose from the Undercourt's point of view it was a repossession. The Auditor's ledger cracked like river ice and every line in it slid loose, and somewhere in all that noise and light, the piece of me that had been collateral for a famine that ended before I was born came walking back through my ribs and sat down.

The first thing I did with my whole soul was cry. The second thing I did was sing, and the singing was not the choir's harmony, which is beautiful the way a machine is beautiful. It was just a boy's voice, cracked and ordinary and mine, and Maren cried harder than I did, which she will deny.

The third thing I did was look at Elodie.

She was standing in the wreckage of the choir hall with lightning still crawling on her shoulders like a cat that hadn't decided whether to stay, and she looked at me the way you look at a stranger who is bleeding. Kind. Careful. Completely empty of me.

"Are you all right?" she asked. "Do I know you?"

And I said — because it was true, and because someone had to start carrying both of our memories immediately, and I had elected myself — "Not yet. But you're going to be wonderful about it."

She was. She has been. That is the part of the story nobody warns you about: the terrible price gets paid in one night, and then you spend years discovering all the small ways it keeps costing, and all the small ways the person who paid it refuses to let it make them small.

* * *

We came back to Hollowmere because there was nowhere else that made sense. The valley had forgotten magic entirely — the soil here eats spells like sand eats rain — and Elodie, who no longer remembered being a storm-caller, fit into it the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. She took up her nets. She took up her cottage. She took up me, in the way that people take up stray animals: with a sigh, a bowl of soup, and a firm declaration that this arrangement was temporary.

Maren stayed too, longer than any of us expected. Her ferry sits at the valley's outflow now, and she carries farmers across the shallows and charges them outrageous prices and tells them stories about the mountain that they assume are lies. They are not lies. She has simply learned to deliver truth in packaging nobody opens. It is a skill I intend to acquire.

Every week, without fail, Elodie comes to the cottage — my cottage now, though it was hers first, though the door still sticks in the same place and the third floorboard still sings a note under your heel — and she brings bread, and she tells me stories.

That is the arrangement. Nobody negotiated it. It simply assembled itself, the way weather assembles.

"You know," she said last Tuesday, setting down the bread, "there's a woman in my dreams sometimes. Big as a church door, loud as thunder, absolutely insufferable. Calls storms like other people call cats."

"That sounds exhausting," I said.

"She sounds *magnificent*," Elodie said, with the fierce certainty of someone defending a friend she cannot name. "And then I wake up and I'm mending nets, and I feel like I've lost something so large I can't even see the edges of it. Isn't that ridiculous?"

"It's not ridiculous," I said. And then, because I am getting better at this but not perfect: "Would you like to hear a story?"

"I would love a story."

So I told her one. I tell her one nearly every week. About a woman who called storms, and a boy who carried a choir, and a ferrywoman with half a soul and all of the stubbornness, and how they walked into a mountain and broke a court that had been counting debts since before the rivers had names. She listens with her whole body. She laughs in the right places. Once — only once, months ago, and I have never mentioned it since — she cried at the part where the storm-caller pays with her memories, and wiped her eyes briskly and said, "Well. Whoever wrote that ought to be ashamed of herself," and I agreed that yes, whoever wrote it certainly should.

Here is the thing I have learned about memory, and I learned it in the dark under the mountain, listening to bottled voices sing: memory is not a possession. The Undercourt thought it was. The Undercourt built shelves for it, niches and ledgers and contracts, treated it like grain in a granary, something you could own and weigh and sell. But the voices in those bottles were not *remembering* anything. They were only stored. A memory that lives is a memory that moves — that gets told, and sung, and changed a little in the telling, and given away, and given away again.

Which means that Elodie has not lost everything. She has lost the original copy. And I — I am the telling.

But a telling is not enough. A telling is a story about a door. And some mornings I watch her stand at the ridgeline looking up at the peaks where storms gather and never cross, and her hands do something complicated and hungry, and I know she feels the shape of the locked room without knowing there was ever a key.

So I have decided to give her the key. Not to open the door. To hold it.

* * *

The counter-song has an opening verse. Of course it does; everything has an opening verse; Maren says the Undercourt itself probably opened with one, some clerk-soul droning the first line of the first contract while the ink was still wet. Maren taught it to me on the road, in the cold, with collectors' lanterns glowing behind us, and she made me learn it three ways: sung, spoken, and written. "The song is the door," she said, "but the writing is the map to the door, and maps survive when singers don't."

She wrote it down for Elodie, once, and left it on the cottage table, and Elodie looked at it for a long time and then put it in the drawer with her grandmother's songbook — the forbidden one, the one full of sigils and margins and three generations of bargains — and she has not opened either. I check. Not sneakily. I check openly, the way you check on a seed you've planted, and the drawer stays shut, and I have made my peace with the possibility that it stays shut forever.

But a verse is smaller than a book. A verse is seven lines. You can carry a verse in your mouth the way you carry a stone in your pocket, and take it out, and turn it over, and decide.

Today I am going to teach it to her.

I have rehearsed this conversation approximately forty times. In several versions I am very eloquent. In one version Maren is present and keeps interrupting, which is accurate to life but bad for drama, so I have arranged for Maren to be on the river until sundown. In none of the versions does my voice crack, and I already know that version is fiction.

Elodie arrives at midday, as she does, with bread and a complaint about the weather.

"The weather," I say, "is fine."

"The weather is *suspicious*. Three clear days in spring. Something's brewing on the peaks and refusing to come down, and I hate it. It's like watching someone swallow a sneeze." She sets the bread down and frowns at the window, at the mountains, at the held breath of the sky. "You'd think a valley could get one honest storm."

And there it is. There is the door, standing open on its own, the way it sometimes does, because Elodie Dunmore's soul remembers its shape even when her mind does not.

"Sit down," I say. "I want to teach you something."

* * *

She sits. She folds her hands. She has the patient attention of someone who has spent years listening to a boy tell her stories, and I love her for it, and the loving does not hurt the way I feared it would. It turns out grief and love can share a house if the house is big enough. Ours is big enough. We built it that way on purpose.

"There's a song," I say. "Seven lines. Maren calls it the counter-song, but that's the whole thing, the entire unbinding, hours of it. The opening verse is just — the beginning. The hand on the latch."

"What does it unlock?"

"Anything, a little. Nothing, mostly. It depends." I take a breath. "It doesn't restore anything. I need you to hear that first. It isn't a spell that pours the past back into you. It's not force. It's—" I struggle, and land on the truest thing I know. "It's a question. Sung correctly. And questions can be answered or not answered. That's the whole design of it. Maren says that's why it works against the Undercourt at all. The Court only understands answers. Obligations. Lines in ledgers. A question sung honestly is the one thing it can't collect."

Elodie is very still. Outside, the held-back storm mutters on the peaks, and the candles — I still keep candles, old habit, hers really — gutter once, softly, in rhythm with something neither of us has named.

"And if I sing it," she says carefully, "and answer the question? What happens to me?"

"I don't know," I admit. "Maybe everything comes back. Maybe a little. Maybe you'll read the ledger of yourself the way you'd read anyone's story — and decide how much of it you want to carry. That's the point. It's your door. I'm not going to open it, and I'm not going to lock it. I'm just going to show you where the handle is."

She looks at me for a long moment. There is something moving behind her face, deep down, like a fish under ice — the shape of recognition without the fact of it.

"You talk like someone who's done this before," she says. "Carried something for another person."

"All the time," I say. "It's my best skill. Ask anyone."

"I would," she says, "but everyone in this valley is terrified of you. They think you're a witch's familiar."

"I am a witch's familiar."

"You're a *boy* with a *belt buckle*."

"Edmund," I say, tapping the buckle, where the carved letters sit worn smooth from years of my thumb. "Named after these. By a woman who found me in the rain and pretended for exactly two days that she wasn't going to keep me."

Something flickers across her face — quick, gone, like the fish surfacing and diving. She presses her hand flat against her sternum, the way people do when their heart does something unauthorized.

"Teach me the verse," she says quietly.

* * *

I teach it the way Maren taught me: spoken first, then sung.

The words are old — older than the famine, older than the Court, maybe older than the mountains, though Maren claims she made half of it up on the ferry one bad winter and simply committed to the bit until it became true, which is, I have decided, how most old things are actually made. Seven lines. They go up where songs should go down. They ask, in the grammar of a language that is dead everywhere except inside me: *Who holds the ledger of your name? And may the holder set it down?*

Elodie speaks the words badly. Then less badly. Then she closes her eyes and sings them, and her voice is low and rough from years of wind off the water, and it is not a trained voice, and it is not a powerful voice, and it is the most beautiful voice I have ever heard, because it is hers, asking her own name's whereabouts like a stranger asking directions to a house she used to live in.

The candles gutter. The third floorboard sings its note. On the peaks, the swallowed sneeze of the storm shivers — and does not break, and does not need to. Something in the valley simply exhales.

And Elodie hums.

She hums along with the last line, the resolving line, the one that asks permission rather than demanding it, and her humming slides into the melody like a boat sliding into water it has been beached beside for years, and her eyes fly open, wet and wide, and she says:

"I almost—"

She stops. Presses her lips together. Tries again.

"I almost remember *humming* this. Not the words. Just — the shape of it. Like a coat that fits a person I've been told about." She laughs, shaky. "Is that what remembering feels like? Reaching for something and finding your hand already knows the way?"

"That's exactly what it feels like," I say, and my voice cracks, and I let it, because forty rehearsals were always fiction.

She looks at the drawer where the songbook sleeps. She doesn't open it. She stands, and crosses to the window, and looks at the mountains for a long time, and I watch her choose — visibly, deliberately, the way she once chose to open her door in the rain — how much she wants to carry today.

"Not yet," she says at last. "But soon. I can feel it waiting, and it's not frightening anymore. It's just..." She turns, and smiles at me, and the smile is the exact smile from the doorway in the storm, the one that took in a soaked silent boy and decided against self-preservation. "It's just a door. And doors are for opening. When I'm ready."

"Take all the time you need," I say. "I'll remember for both of us in the meantime. It's my best skill."

"You've mentioned." She gathers her basket, then pauses at the threshold, the sticking door half-open, spring air coming through. "Edmund. The woman in my stories — the storm-caller. Do you think she'd have liked me?"

I think of lightning crawling on her shoulders like a cat deciding to stay. I think of a door opened in the rain. I think of a ledger cracking like river ice, and a verse that asks permission, and a valley that forgets everything except the things that matter.

"She didn't just like you," I say. "She crossed a mountain for you."

Elodie smiles like she's been handed something precious and wrapped, and doesn't yet know what's inside, and isn't afraid to find out. Then she goes, down the path toward the water, humming the first verse under her breath — up where songs should go down — and behind her, high on the ridgeline, the storm that has waited three years finally, gently, begins to cross over.

I get out paper, and add a line to the ledger of us:

*She knows where the handle is. She'll open it when she's ready. We have time. For the first time in three generations of my family's story — we have time.*

And outside, soft as a promise, it starts to rain.
