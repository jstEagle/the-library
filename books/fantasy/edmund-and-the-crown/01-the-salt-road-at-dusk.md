## 1. The Salt Road at Dusk

The sun went down behind the Thornfells like a coin dropped into a beggar's cup — gone before anyone could stop it, leaving everyone poorer for the loss.

Yara Sterling stood at the mouth of the Kestrel Pass and counted her blessings, which took no time at all. One: the pass was clear of snow. Two: the moon would be dark by midnight. Three: she had not yet killed anyone today, and the night was young enough that she might keep it that way.

Behind her, forty paces up the switchback trail, her cargo came clattering down out of the last light like a funeral procession that had gotten lost on its way to something cheerier. Six mules. Four people. One crate of contraband so dangerous that three separate kingdoms had outlawed it, and one crate so mundane that Yara had packed it herself just to watch Bramble complain.

"You're standing in the wind again," Bramble called up to her. He was built like a rain barrel with opinions, and his voice carried the way only a man who had spent thirty years shouting over kitchen fires could manage. "You'll catch your death."

"I caught my death years ago," Yara called back. "It didn't take. We've come to an arrangement."

Bramble muttered something that was probably a recipe and probably profane. The mules complained in their turn, which was fair; the trail had been cruel all day, all scree and switchbacks, and the crates rode heavy. Prayer-tokens always rode heavy. There was a weight to them that had nothing to do with lead or lacquer — a weight of wanting, pressed thin and fired hard, a hundred small hopes baked into clay and sold to people who couldn't afford hope at any price.

Which was, of course, exactly why they were illegal.

The old gods had been asleep for a century. Everyone knew it. The temples still stood, the priests still took their tithes, but the prayers went up and nothing came back down, and somewhere along the way the crown had decided that if the gods wouldn't answer, then pretending they might was sedition. Prayer-tokens — little stamped medallions, each one inscribed with a wish — had gone from sacrament to smuggler's stock in a single generation.

Yara carried them anyway. Not out of piety. She'd run out of piety around the same time she'd run out of everything else. She carried them because there was always someone in some valley village willing to pay good silver for a piece of clay that said *come home safe* on it, and because the mountain roads belonged to her, and because a woman had to be something, even if what she was, was tired.

She heard Fen before she saw him — the boy never stopped talking, not even to himself, though he did his best work talking to himself, low and fast, a scribe's habit of narrating his own thoughts as though someone might someday want the minutes of the meeting.

"—and if we take the high fork we lose two hours but gain the tree line, which matters if Vane's patrols are running lanterns, except they won't be, not on a dark moon, they'll trust the dark, which is exactly why we shouldn't—"

"Fen." Yara didn't raise her voice. She never had to. "Low fork."

He stopped mid-sentence, blinked at her through the gloom, and nodded with the quick, birdlike obedience of a man who had spent too many years being told where to sit. "Low fork. Right. Because—"

"Because the high fork has a shrine at the top," Yara said, "and shrines have candles, and candles mean eyes, and I promised you'd live to see twenty."

"I'm nineteen."

"Then you have a year left on the warranty. Don't void it."

Osk laughed from the rear of the column, a short bark of a sound, the laugh of a man who rationed joy the way he rationed everything else. He came up the trail with the mules' lead ropes looped over one shoulder, moving with the easy economy of a soldier even now, five years out of any army worth the name. His eyes never stopped working. They swept the ridgelines the way other men checked their pockets for keys.

"Patrols?" he asked. Just that. Osk wasted no words on weather or greetings.

"None since noon," Yara said. "Two riders on the south road, heading east, bored. If Vane's people are in the mountains this week, they're being quiet about it, and quiet isn't their style."

"Quiet can be a style," Osk said. "New commander, new habits."

"Vane doesn't have new habits. Vane has a ledger and a grudge and a horse that hates her, and none of those things have changed since the winter." Yara turned and started down toward the low fork, and the others fell in behind her without being asked, without discussing it, the way water falls in behind gravity. That was the thing about her crew — her crew, she corrected herself, not her anything else, because she worked alone, she had always worked alone, these four simply happened to be traveling in the same direction she was, indefinitely, in all weather, forever. That was the thing about them: they moved when she moved, stopped when she stopped, and none of them seemed to find it strange.

She found it strange. She found it every single day, like a stone in her boot she refused to shake out.

* * *

They made camp in the hollow below the fork, a cup of granite and pine where a fire the size of two fists could burn without showing its light past ten paces. Bramble got the fire going and got supper going over it in the same motion, because Bramble did not believe in doing one thing when three would do. The smell of onions and barley went up into the dark like a small rebellion against the whole cold indifferent mountain.

"Right," Bramble announced, ladling out bowls. "Fen, you're on first watch, and if you talk to yourself loud enough to bring wolves, I'm feeding you to them with a butter sauce so they get a decent meal out of it."

"Noted," Fen said, accepting his bowl with both hands, cradling it like scripture. He was thin in the way of boys who had grown up indoors and were only recently introduced to the concept of weather. He'd been a scribe in the capital once — a real one, ink-stained and licensed, copying tax rolls and royal decrees until the day he copied something he wasn't supposed to see and ran. He'd never said what it was. Yara had never asked. In the smuggling trade, a man's reasons were his own luggage, and you didn't go through it.

Osk ate in silence, sitting with his back to a boulder and his knees drawn up, facing the dark the way he always faced the dark — toward it, never away. Once, Yara had asked him why ex-soldiers always sat facing the door. He'd said, "Because we know what comes through doors." She hadn't asked anything else for a month after that.

And Yara sat apart, as she always sat apart, close enough to share the fire's warmth and far enough to make it clear the warmth was borrowed, not given. She ate with her knife across her knee and her eyes on the pass above them, and she listened to the three of them talk — about barley, about boots, about whether mules held grudges (they did; Fen had proof) — and she felt the loneliness settle over her like snowfall, soft and total and familiar.

Here was the joke of it, the joke she kept locked in the back of her throat where it couldn't get out: she had built this. Every habit of theirs that kept them alive, she had taught them. Fen watched high ground because she'd drilled him on it. Osk cooked second because she'd shown him how Bramble liked the rotation run. Even Bramble's rule about never camping where you'd camped before — hers, originally, passed along like a recipe. She had assembled this family the way she assembled a cargo manifest, item by item, route by route, and now it functioned beautifully without her having to admit she'd done it, and she intended to keep that arrangement until she died, which, given her profession, might be Tuesday.

"You're doing the face," Fen said.

Yara looked over. "What face?"

"The one where you're counting us." He said it lightly, the way he said most true things, wrapped in enough flippancy to deny later. "Like coins you're deciding whether to spend."

"I count my assets. It's called competence."

"It's called—" Fen began, and Bramble cleared his throat with the force of a minor avalanche, and Fen, wisely, pivoted to complaining about the mule named Duchess, who had eaten half his spare shirt that afternoon and shown no remorse whatsoever.

Yara let the moment close over and sink away. She was good at that. She'd had practice.

* * *

The watches turned. Fen gave way to Osk, who gave way to Bramble, whose snores were audible even while he stood watch, a feat Yara had privately decided defied natural law. She slept in snatches, the way she always did, her hand near her sword hilt even in sleep — an old habit, older than the crew, older than the road. Her sword had a name, the way swordspeakers' swords always did, though she hadn't spoken to it in years and it hadn't answered in longer.

That was the other thing nobody in the camp knew. Well — Osk suspected. Osk suspected everything; it was his entire personality. But none of them knew that the woman guiding them through the Thornfells was the last of the Swordspeakers of the Ninefold Oath, that her blade had once sung words of power into the world, that she had sworn herself to gods who were now sleeping so deep that not even her oaths could wake them. Her magic faded a little more each year, like dye in cloth left too long in the sun. Some mornings she woke and reached for it out of habit and found only silence, a great smooth nothing where the old warmth used to live.

She didn't miss it. That was what she told herself. What she missed was harder to name — the sense of being *held*, maybe. Of swearing yourself to something larger and feeling it swear back. These days she swore to nothing but the route and the cargo and the next safe crossing, and nothing swore back, and she had made her peace with it, the way you make peace with a bad knee.

Near dawn, the cold deepened, and Yara gave up on sleep and climbed out of the hollow to look at the sky.

The stars over Thornfell were merciless — bright, precise, arranged with the indifference of a merchant's scales. She stood among them with her breath smoking and did what she never did in front of the others.

She listened.

Nothing. As always. The mountain breathed its slow mineral breathing, the wind combed the pines, and the gods slept on beneath the stone, wherever sleeping gods went, and no voice rose out of anywhere to meet her.

"Still nothing," she said aloud, to no one. Her voice sounded strange and small under all that sky. "Good. Keep it up. One of us should get some rest."

She almost laughed at her own joke. Almost. Then she heard the footsteps behind her — light ones, deliberate ones, the ones that meant Fen had decided this was the hour for courage — and she put the loneliness back in its box and closed the lid and turned around wearing the face she wore for the world.

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"So are you," Fen said. He came to stand beside her at the edge of the overlook, hugging his arms against the cold, and for a while neither of them spoke, which for Fen was roughly equivalent to a vow of silence. When he finally did speak, he was holding something out in his palm: a disc of fired clay, dull red-brown, stamped with a spiral and a single word in the old temple script.

A prayer-token. From the crate. Of course from the crate.

"Before you say anything," Fen rushed on, "I know. I know what you'll say. Contraband is contraband, inventory is inventory, and a smuggler who samples the stock ends up eating the profits. Bramble told me. With the butter sauce reference."

"Bramble is wise."

"But I've been cataloguing them all week." His voice dropped, went quieter, went somewhere underneath the flippancy. "Safe passage. Good harvest. A son's return. A mother's fever breaking. Forty different wishes in that box, Yara, and every one of them written by someone who paid a week's wages for a lump of clay because there was nowhere else to put the wish. And the gods are asleep, and the tokens are worthless, and the crown calls them sedition, and I keep thinking — what kind of kingdom outlaws wishing? What were they afraid of?"

Yara looked at the little disc in his palm. The spiral caught starlight. Somewhere behind her ribs, in the place where her dead order's oaths used to live, something shifted — not warmth, not quite, but the memory of warmth, the way a scar remembers the wound.

"They were afraid it would work," she said.

Fen glanced up at her. "That's not funny."

"No," Yara agreed. "It isn't."

He turned the token over in his fingers. "It's just — suppose I tried it. Suppose I wished for something small. Safe passage down the mountain, say. Nothing grand. Just to see." He laughed, self-conscious. "It's stupid. It's clay. It's a century of silence. Nothing will happen."

"Nothing will happen," Yara said.

But her hand had drifted, without her permission, to the hilt of her sword. And far beneath them — so far down that no one living had ever been deep enough to hear it — something in the roots of the Thornfell mountains turned over in its sleep, the way a starving man turns toward the smell of bread.

Fen closed his eyes. Cupped the token in both hands. And whispered his wish to the dark.
