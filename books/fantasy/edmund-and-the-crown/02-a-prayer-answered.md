## 2. A Prayer Answered

Fen had stolen the prayer-token on the third night out of Lowmark, and had been carrying the theft like a stone in his boot ever since.

It wasn't even a proper theft. That was the humiliating part. The token had been sitting in a dead man's pack — one of the pilgrims frozen in the shrine at Grayfall Hollow, a century dead and still kneeling, still holding his little clay disc up to an altar no god had answered since before Fen's grandmother was born. Yara had said take nothing from shrines; it was bad luck, or bad manners, or both, and Bramble had crossed himself with a ladle for emphasis. But Fen was a scribe by training and a thief by temperament, and the token had *writing* on it — old script, pre-purge script, the kind that made his fingers itch the way other men's fingers itched toward coin purses.

So he'd taken it. Slipped it into the inner pocket of his coat where he kept the letters he never sent home, and told himself he'd copy the inscription and put it back before anyone noticed.

That had been eleven days ago.

Now the pass narrowed around them like a throat, and the mules picked their way over scree the color of old bone, and Fen rode at the rear with his hand pressed flat against his chest, feeling the small hard circle of clay through the wool, warm as if it had been sitting in sunlight. It was dusk. The light came down the mountain in long amber blades, cutting the trail into stripes of gold and shadow, and somewhere ahead Osk's voice carried back low and steady: *"Loose rock, mind your left. Mule's favoring her off-hind again."*

Fen loved this. He would never say so aloud — not to Yara, who wore competence like armor, not to Bramble, who grumbled through every mile as though the mountains had personally wronged him — but Fen loved the smuggler's road the way he had once loved the scriptorium: the hush of it, the sense of being inside something vast and secret. The Thornfells did not care about kings or inquisitors or the names written in ledgers. They only cared whether you were careful.

He was trying very hard to be careful. Which is why what happened next was, he would insist until his dying day, entirely an accident.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang of black granite, a place Yara called the Kettle because the wind funneled through it and moaned like something boiling. Bramble got a fire going behind a windbreak of stones — small, smokeless, the coals banked to a sullen red — and produced the evening miracle he always produced: barley stew with dried plums and enough salt in it to make a grown man weep with gratitude.

"Salt," Bramble said, ladling it out, "is the whole reason civilization exists. Everything else is decoration."

"You said that about pepper last week," Osk said.

"Pepper is vanity. Salt is survival." Bramble pointed the ladle at him like a scepter. "There's a difference and I'll thank you to learn it."

Fen sat with his bowl balanced on his knees, half-listening, half-reading. He'd finally worked the token out of his pocket where the firelight wouldn't catch it, turning it in his fingers. Clay, fired hard, worn smooth at the edges by a dead man's grip. Around the rim ran a ring of characters in the old liturgical hand — the same hand used in the Rites of Intercession, the same hand that filled the margins of the only surviving psalter Fen had ever touched, back when he was eleven years old and the scriptorium at Vellum Court still pretended the gods might come back.

The center of the token held a single word, pressed deep:

**ASK**

A prayer-token. You whispered your need into it, sealed it with a breath, and left it at an altar, and someday — someday, when the gods still listened — the need would be carried up. That was the theology, anyway. Fen knew better than most how much of theology was wishful thinking fired in clay. The gods had gone silent a hundred years ago. Every prayer since had been a letter posted to a burned house.

But the token was warm. It had been warm for eleven days, and Fen had told himself it was his own body heat, the way you tell yourself the creaking in the walls is just the house settling.

Yara sat apart, as she always did, on a ledge above the camp with her sword across her knees, cleaning it though it didn't need cleaning. Her silhouette against the last of the light was all sharp angles and stillness. She wasn't looking at them. She was looking at the pass they'd have to take tomorrow — the Narrows, where the trail clung to the cliff face above a drop that turned falling into a formality — and her stillness had a texture Fen had learned to read. She was worried. She would rather eat her own boot than say so.

"Passage tomorrow," Osk said quietly, following Fen's gaze. "Bad?"

"The Narrows?" Bramble snorted. "The Narrows are fine. I've been through the Narrows drunk."

"You've been through everything drunk."

"And survived, which proves my point."

Fen looked down at the token. **ASK**, it said. And he thought — not seriously, the way you think about jumping from a high wall, the thought arriving fully formed and absurd — *what if.*

What if he asked. Just once. Just to see. Not a real prayer, nothing sincere, nothing that would offend whatever pride dead gods retained. A test. A scribe's experiment. *Hypothesis: the tokens are inert. Method: whisper into one. Result:* nothing, obviously, and then he could stop feeling the warmth against his chest like a small accusing heart, and copy the inscription, and return the thing to its dead owner, and be done with the whole guilty business.

"Fen." Yara's voice came down from the ledge, quiet. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to do something stupid and call it scholarship."

He laughed, too quickly. "I don't have a face."

"You have several. This is the worst one."

Bramble and Osk were watching now, amused, waiting for the evening's entertainment. And maybe that was why he did it — because being watched made it a performance instead of a prayer, because if three people saw him do something foolish then it was a joke and not a confession. Later, much later, he would understand that this was exactly the kind of loophole hungry things love.

Fen lifted the token to his lips.

"Safe passage," he whispered. It barely qualified as a wish. It was what you said to a toll-taker. "Safe passage through the Narrows. For all of us. That's — that's all."

He breathed out across the clay, sealing it, the way the old rites described.

For a moment, nothing. The wind boiled through the Kettle. Bramble stirred the stew. Somewhere below, a mule stamped.

Then the token went cold — cold as river ice, cold enough to sting — and every hair on Fen's arms stood up, and the fire *leaned.*

There was no other word for it. The flames bent sideways, all together, away from him, the way grass bends away from a footstep. The shadows of the camp stretched long and thin toward the cliff wall, and the wind stopped mid-moan, cut off like a struck bell, and in the ringing silence Fen heard — felt — something turn its attention toward him.

It was not the silence of the sleeping gods. Fen had grown up inside that silence; it was the silence of an empty room. This was the silence of a room where someone has been standing very still behind the door, listening, for a very long time.

And then the mountain moved.

* * *

Later, none of them would agree on exactly what they saw. Osk swore the cliff itself shifted — a groan of stone, deep under the earth, and the great slab of granite that had overhung the Kettle for ten thousand years tilting by a hand's width, opening a gap, a seam of starlight, a promise of easier ground beyond. Bramble swore the trail ahead unknotted itself, scree settling into steps, a path smoothing out of chaos like a bed being made by invisible hands. Fen, who had been closest, swore he saw the darkness between the stars ripple, the way water ripples when something enormous passes beneath the surface, and that for one instant he felt *seen* — wholly, thoroughly seen, every theft and every unsent letter and every selfish small wish laid open like a book under a magnifying glass, and something on the far side of that seeing said, in a voice that was not a voice:

***GRANTED.***

Not *granted, child.* Not *granted, beloved.* No warmth in it at all. The word landed like a ledger entry. Like a debt recorded.

The fire snapped upright. The wind resumed its moan as if it had never stopped. The mule stamped again, indifferent, and that indifference was somehow the most terrifying thing of all — the world carrying on while the sky had briefly shown its teeth.

Fen was still holding the token. It crumbled in his hand, dry as ash, and sifted through his fingers onto the ground, and where each grain landed the frost formed instantly, tiny white stars on black dirt, in patterns almost like writing.

Nobody spoke. Then Bramble set down the ladle with exaggerated care, crossed himself with it, and said, in the tone of a man nailing a door shut, "Right. Who did it."

"Bramble—" Osk started.

"Someone *prayed.* In *my* camp. Over my *stew.*"

"It was me." Fen's voice came out smaller than he intended. He held up his empty hands, showing them the dust. "It was — I found it in the shrine at Grayfall, I was going to give it back, I only wanted to see if—"

"If the gods were listening," Yara said.

She had come down from the ledge without making a sound. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her sword still bare in her hand, and her face was wrong. Not angry. Fen had seen Yara angry; it was loud and profane and strangely comforting, like weather. This was quiet. This was a woman reading a sentence she had hoped never to read.

"They weren't," she said. "Whatever answered you, Fen, it wasn't them."

"How can you know that?"

"Because I know what the old gods feel like." She said it flatly, the way you'd admit to a scar. "Or I used to. What answered you was—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. Fen watched her decide, deliberately, to lie, and watched the lie die halfway up her throat, and watched her settle on something true but incomplete. "*Hungry,*" she finished. "It felt hungry. Prayers don't get answered for free. Nothing gives passage through the Narrows for the price of a whisper. Something took your wish and it will want change, and I promise you the exchange rate is ugly."

Osk had moved, without seeming to move, to stand where he could see both the trail and Fen — body between his people and the dark, old soldier's reflexes running underneath the banter like bedrock. "Could it follow the prayer back? Find us?"

"I don't know."

"You always know."

"Well, I don't know *this,*" Yara snapped, and then closed her eyes and breathed, and when she opened them she was Yara again, the captain, the woman who orchestrated everyone's survival while insisting she worked alone. "Here's what I know. We break camp an hour before dawn. We go through the Narrows fast and quiet, and nobody prays, nobody sings, nobody so much as *hums* anything that sounds like a hymn. Fen—"

"Yes."

"Give me the rest of your contraband. All of it. Anything else you lifted from that shrine."

His ears went hot. He dug into his coat and produced, shamefully, a second token — unmarked, blank, unfired — and a scrap of vellum torn from a psalter, which he surrendered with the expression of a man handing over his firstborn. Yara took them without comment and dropped them into the fire. The vellum flared green for half a heartbeat. Nobody mentioned it.

"And Fen." She crouched to his eye level, and her voice gentled into something rare and careful. "You're not in trouble. Do you hear me? You tested a hypothesis. That's what scribes do. It's what *I'd* have done." A ghost of a smile. "Just — next time you want to poke the universe with a stick, wake me first. I own sturdier sticks."

Despite everything, Fen laughed. It came out wobbly, but it came.

* * *

He didn't sleep. None of them really did. They lay in a row like spoons in a drawer, backs to the fire, and listened to the mountain breathe, and Fen stared at the stars and turned the moment over and over in his mind like a coin he couldn't stop flipping.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told them. Here was the thing he would carry alone, the way Yara carried whatever she carried, the way everyone in this ragged little family carried their private stones.

When the presence had looked at him — when it had *seen* him, all the way down — it had not felt like a god. Fen had spent his childhood among men who studied divinity the way lepidopterists study butterflies, pinning descriptions of the divine into dead languages, and everything he'd ever read agreed: the gods were distant, patient, vast as weather. Answering a prayer cost them nothing. It was like the sun rising. It was grace.

This had not been grace. This had been *transaction.* He had felt appetite — enormous, patient, coiled appetite, the hunger of a thing that had waited a hundred years in the dark under the world with its mouth open, and had finally, finally felt something small and sweet fall in.

*Granted,* it had said. And beneath the word, so faint he could pretend he'd imagined it, something else. A question, almost polite. Almost tender.

*What else do you want?*

Fen rolled over and watched Yara, who was not asleep either. She sat up against the overhang with her sword across her knees, watching the Narrows, and in the dying firelight her face was the face of someone listening to a sound no one else could hear — head tilted, eyes narrowed, lips slightly parted. And twice, before dawn, her hand rose unconsciously to her own chest, pressing flat, as if checking whether something in there had gone warm.

The hour before dawn, they broke camp. The Narrows, when they reached it, was exactly as Bramble had sworn: smooth, stepped, easy — a path laid out for them like a welcome, like a lure.

Yara looked at it for a long time, and Fen watched her look, and understood that she was deciding whether to trust a gift from something hungry.

"We go anyway," she said finally. "But we don't say thank you."

And far below them, deeper than any mine, deeper than the roots of the mountains themselves, Fen could have sworn he felt something laugh.
