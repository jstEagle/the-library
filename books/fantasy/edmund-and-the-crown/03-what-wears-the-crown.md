## 3. What Wears the Crown

The convoy found them on the third morning out of Ravenmere, when the fog still lay in the passes like spilled milk and the mules were complaining about it in their own language.

Yara heard them before she saw them — the particular rhythm of a royal column, too many hooves moving in step, iron where there should have been leather. She had spent eleven years learning the difference between the sounds a mountain made when it was empty and the sounds it made when it was full of men who thought they owned it.

"Off the trail," she said quietly, and her crew melted into the scree like water into sand. Fen went first, always first now, with the prayer-tokens wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. Osk took the rear with Bramble's mule, because Bramble would argue with anyone who tried to hurry him and Osk had learned to simply take the rope out of his hands.

Yara stayed a moment longer than she should have, counting banners through the fog.

Not border garrison. Not tax riders. The banners were gold on white, and beneath them rode outriders in lacquered scale, and behind them came a carriage that had no business above the snowline — black lacquer, silver fittings, drawn by six pale horses that picked their way over the broken stone with the drugged docility of animals that had been fed something to keep them calm.

She knew that carriage. Everyone in the Thornfells knew it, the way you knew a wolf's track even if you'd never seen the wolf.

"Yara," Osk breathed at her shoulder. He'd come back for her, which he did more often than either of them acknowledged. "That's—"

"I know what it is."

"It shouldn't be here. The king doesn't leave the capital. The king *can't* leave the capital, they say he hasn't been seen in—"

"The king can do whatever the thing wearing him likes." She said it before she could stop herself, and felt Osk go still beside her. It wasn't the kind of thing smugglers said about kings. It wasn't the kind of thing anyone said about kings, not anymore, not since the prayers started being answered again and half the lowlands had taken to kneeling in the streets.

"Go," she said. "Take the high cut past the shrine. I'll catch up."

"You're going to *talk* to them."

"I'm going to count them." She was already moving down toward the trail, untying the strip of white wool from her wrist as she went — the parley-mark, older than any of the kingdoms that pretended to own these mountains. "Someone has to know which way they're headed, or we'll be walking our cargo straight into their laps."

Osk hesitated. He was good at hesitating; it was his one honest vice. Then he went, and Yara stood alone in the fog with her hand raised, palm out, showing the wool.

The column halted. A rider detached itself from the vanguard and came on at a walk: a woman in grey-lacquered half-plate with a ledger chained at her hip, face narrow and neat as a paper knife. Yara didn't know her name yet. She would learn it later, and wish she hadn't.

"You're the guide called Sterling." Not a question. The woman's voice had the flat polish of a thing rehearsed. "Word of your reputation precedes you up this road, mistress. They say you can get anything over the Thornfell passes. Even things that don't want to go."

"They say a lot of things about me. Most of them invented by people who owe me money."

"We require passage to Hollow Bridge, and thence the high road to the capital." The woman's eyes flicked once to the white wool and away, dismissing it. "You will be paid triple the going rate. You will also be searched, and anything you carry that offends the crown will be confiscated and you will be detained."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take you anyway, and the rate drops to nothing." The woman smiled without warmth, an expression like a door closing. "I am Serah Vane, inquisitor of the crown. The convoy carries cargo of sacred importance. His Majesty travels under the crown's protection, and the renewal rites wait for no one — not weather, not war, and certainly not smugglers."

*His Majesty.*

Yara looked past Vane's shoulder, past the outriders, to the black carriage swaying on its springs, and felt something old and cold turn over in her chest. Her swordspeech had been dying for years — a candle guttering in a draft, barely enough to warm her palms on cold nights, barely enough to hear the ghosts of the old gods muttering in their sleep. But it stirred now. It stirred the way a dog stirs when something walks past the house that has no smell a dog should trust.

"Well?" said Vane.

"Triple rate," Yara said. "Half in advance. And my people travel ahead of the column, untouched. You don't want your sacred cargo smelling of confiscation and bad blood."

Vane considered her for a long moment, the way a clerk considers a figure that almost balances. "Done," she said, and Yara understood, watching the ledger-chains swing as the woman turned her horse, that she had just made a bargain whose terms she hadn't finished reading.

It was, she reflected grimly, leading the column up into the pass an hour later, becoming a habit. And habits like that got people killed.

* * *

The first day was ordinary enough, which should have warned her.

Royal convoys were all the same — arrogant officers, nervous soldiers, horses better bred than any man riding them. Yara guided them along the salt-cut with the automatic competence of eleven years' practice, calling hazards before the column reached them, choosing campsites with an eye to defensible ground and quick exits both. Vane rode near the front and wrote in her ledger. The soldiers watched the mountains the way soldiers do, seeing threats everywhere except the ones that mattered.

The carriage traveled in the middle, always in the middle, and no one spoke of what was inside it except in the lowered, careful voices people used for the sick.

It was near dusk on the second day, camped below the shattered teeth of the Widow's Stair, when Yara brought the king his supper.

This had not been her idea. The captain of the escort had summoned her to the carriage with the air of a man delegating a punishment, and so she'd found herself standing on the carriage's folding step with a covered tray, feeling like a fool, telling herself it was only a sick man behind that door and sick men needed feeding like anyone else.

"Enter," said a voice, when she knocked.

The interior was dim. Curtains drawn against the mountain cold, a single lamp burning low, and the smell — that was the first wrongness, the smell of a room kept shut too long, sweetish and stale underneath the incense someone had been burning to cover it. Incense couldn't cover it. Nothing covered it. It was the smell of a thing being eaten slowly from the inside.

He sat propped in the corner seat, wrapped in furs despite the season, and Yara's first thought was that the stories hadn't lied: King Edmund was thirty-one years old and looked carved from ash. Gaunt wasn't the word. *Flickering* was closer, though she wouldn't understand why until later — the way lamplight moved across his face seemed to disagree with the face itself, shadow falling where shadow shouldn't, catching where there was nothing to catch.

His eyes opened. They were brown. Ordinary brown, tired, human.

"Sterling," he said. "The smuggler. They've given you my supper, which means they think I'll eat if a stranger brings it. They may be right. Everything tastes of ash lately, but ash served by a stranger is at least interesting ash."

Yara set the tray on the folding table and did not sit, because there was nowhere to sit and because sitting meant staying and staying meant looking at him longer than was safe.

"Your Majesty honors me," she said, dry as the salt road.

"Don't." Something moved across his face — pain, or its cousin. "Don't do the voice. Everyone does the voice. The whole kingdom speaks to me in one voice now, bowed and smooth, and I—" He stopped. Breathed. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone rough, gone *real*. "Eleven years ago you'd have knelt. I remember you. You were at the winter court, the year the plague came. Your order still had its hall on the Swordmount then. You wore grey and you wouldn't dance with anyone."

Yara went very still.

"I don't remember you," she said, which was a lie, because suddenly she did — a young king not yet crowned, laughing at something a swordspeaker had said, before the purges, before the silence, before everything.

"No," he agreed softly. "You wouldn't."

And then his face changed.

She would spend years trying to describe it afterward and never quite manage it. It was not a mask dropping; it was worse, subtler — the face remained his face, but the *ordering* of it changed, the way a familiar room changes when someone has moved the furniture in the dark. The lamp flame leaned though there was no draft. The shadows on the carriage wall bent inward, all of them, toward the seat where the king sat.

"GUIDE," said Edmund's mouth, in a voice like a bell rung underwater. "SPEAKER OF THE DEAD SWORD. YOU SMELL OF OATHS."

Every hair on Yara's body stood up. Her swordspeech — that guttering candle, that dying ember — flared. Not warmly. It flared the way a wound flares, hot and wrong, and she heard, faint and far beneath the mountain's bones, something vast turning over in its sleep and *noticing her*.

"You are bound," the thing said through the king's lips, curious, tasting the words. "Bound and fraying. Who holds your leash, little sword? The gods are asleep. Who has been holding your leash while the masters dream?"

"No one," Yara said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm nobody's. That's rather the point of me."

"A LIE." The lamp guttered. "ALL SWORDS ARE SWORN. ALL OATHS ARE CHAINS. AND CHAINS—" The mouth smiled, and it was the smile that finally frightened her, because it was gentle, because it was *hungry*, the way a starving man looks gently at bread. "CHAINS CAN BE TAKEN UP BY NEW HANDS."

The carriage rocked. Somewhere outside, a horse screamed, short and startled, and the moment broke like ice underfoot — the shadows snapped back to their proper places, the lamp stood upright, and Edmund sagged against the furs with a sound like a man surfacing from deep water, gasping, shaking.

"*No,*" he whispered. To himself. To the dark. "*No, no, not now, not—*"

He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and human and terrified, and Yara understood in one lurching instant that everything she had just seen, the king had seen too. From the inside. Every time.

"Sterling." His hand shot out and caught her wrist — thin fingers, grip like a drowning man. "Listen to me. There are perhaps minutes. Perhaps seconds. I don't control when it sleeps. Listen."

"Your Majesty—"

"*Kill me.*"

The words fell into the carriage and lay there.

"Before the renewal rites," he said, fast, urgent, tears cutting clean lines down the ash of his face. "At the coronation renewal the binding is renewed — that's what the rite *is*, that's what it's always been, and every year it takes more of me, and this year there won't be enough left to come back from. Do you understand? I have maybe a month. Maybe less. And after the rite there is no Edmund anymore, there's just — just a coat it wears, a crown on a hook, and it will rule this kingdom through my mouth for a hundred years, a thousand, feeding, and no one will ever know, because everyone *prays* to it now, they pray and it answers and it eats them a little at a time and calls the eating grace—"

"Your Majesty—"

"*Please.*" His grip tightened. "You're an oath-bound killer. That's what your order was, whatever pretty names you gave it — you killed what needed killing when no one else could. Well. Here is the thing that needs killing. Here I am. One blade, one clear night on the road, and no one would ever blame you — they'd call it bandits, they'd call it fever, they'd call it anything—"

"*No.*"

It came out of her flat and final, harder than she'd intended, and she saw him flinch as if struck.

"I don't kill kings," Yara said. "I move cargo. I keep my head down. I have spent eleven years keeping my head down, and the one time I lifted it, everyone I loved died for it, so no. Find another sword, Majesty. This one's retired."

"There is no other sword." He laughed — a terrible sound, cracked down the middle. "Don't you see? That's why it let me come north. That's why it *wants* this route, these mountains. It knows what sleeps under Thornfell. It knows what's buried here, and it's going home, Sterling, and it's taking me with it like a—"

The change came again, mid-word, faster this time. His jaw locked. The lamp leaned. And through the king's mouth, in that drowned-bell voice, the thing said:

"HE LIES TO YOU, GUIDE. THEY ALWAYS LIE AT THE END. THE KING IS TIRED. THE KING IS FADING. BUT I AM GENEROUS, AND MY KINGDOM IS KIND, AND EVERY KNEELING MOUTH IN THE LOWLANDS IS FED." The smile again, gentle, bottomless. "ASK THEM. ASK THE ONES WHO PRAY WHETHER THEY REGRET IT."

Then Edmund was back, slumping, sobbing once — one raw, bitten-off sound — and pressing the heel of his hand against his mouth to hold the rest in.

Outside, boots crunched on gravel. Voices. The escort, coming to check on their sovereign.

"Go," Edmund whispered, fast, wiping his face with a motion so practiced it broke her heart. "Go, before they see you've been in here too long. Go, and forget—"

But his hand caught hers one last time, brief as a spark.

"Salt road," he breathed. "If you change your mind. I'll be on the salt road until the rites. I'll leave the window unlatched. Every night. Every night, Sterling."

* * *

She walked back through the camp with the empty tray and her heart hammering like a fist on a door, and no one looked at her twice, and that was somehow the worst part — two hundred soldiers, an inquisitor with her ledger, a whole apparatus of church and crown, and not one of them knew what rode in the middle of their column. Or worse: some of them knew, and had decided it was holy.

Bramble had stew waiting. Fen was reading by firelight, the stolen prayer-token tucked in his satchel like a guilty conscience. Osk sat sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, which was how Osk prayed.

"Eat," Bramble said. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone who's seen something expensive." He ladled stew into a bowl and pushed it at her, and for a moment she almost told them — all of it, the flickering king, the drowned-bell voice, the *kill me*. The words climbed her throat and she swallowed them down with the stew, because that was the discipline, that was the whole discipline of her life: carry it alone, tell no one, survive.

Later, in her bedroll under the indifferent stars, she stared at the black shape of the carriage on its stand across the camp and listened to the wind come down off the Widow's Stair.

*I'll leave the window unlatched.*

She should have laughed at it. A king asking a smuggler for murder, offering an open window like something out of a ballad. It was absurd. It was treason. It was none of her business, and she had a cargo to deliver and a family — no, a *crew*, she corrected herself fiercely, a crew, temporary, convenient, nothing more — to keep alive, and the smartest thing she could do was deliver Vane's convoy to Hollow Bridge, collect her triple rate, and never think of gaunt brown eyes again.

Her swordspeech stirred in her chest, faint and fading, and for the first time in eleven years it didn't feel like a dying ember.

It felt like a hook.

Something under the mountain had smelled her oath on the wind, and it wanted her, and it was patient, and it had a month to work.

Yara closed her eyes and did not sleep, and somewhere in the small hours she admitted the truth she'd been refusing since the carriage: she was already thinking about the window. About the latch. About how easy it would be.

And about how, for the first time since the purges, someone had asked her not to survive — but to *choose* — and some traitorous part of her had answered before her mind caught up.

Three days to Hollow Bridge. She'd decide there.

She was still deciding when the first light came grey over the pass, and far below in the fog, faint as memory, she heard the mountain hum.
