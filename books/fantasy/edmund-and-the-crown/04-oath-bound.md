## 4. Oath-Bound

The king's convoy was three days gone down the western pass before Yara stopped shaking.

She told the crew it was the cold. Bramble accepted that the way he accepted most of her lies — with a grunt and a bowl of something hot shoved into her hands — but Fen watched her over the fire with those scribe's eyes that missed nothing, and Osk cleaned his sword for the fourth time in an hour, which was his way of saying he'd seen it too.

What they had not seen — what she would never tell them — was the moment on the high ridge when King Edmund had turned in his litter, gaunt as a winter-starved wolf, and *looked* at her. Not through her, not past her, the way kings look at everything. *At* her. And in the space of a single heartbeat, every ember of her fading swordspeech had flared like a struck flint.

Ten years of silence. Ten years of reaching for the old gods and touching nothing but empty air. And then one glance from a dying king, and her blood had sung like a blade drawn across a whetstone.

She wrapped her hands around Bramble's bowl and let the heat sink into her palms, and she thought: *it knows me.*

* * *

The order had sworn its oaths at dawn. That much she remembered with perfect clarity — the rest had gone soft at the edges, like ink left in the rain, but the dawns stayed sharp.

The Hall of First Light stood at the crown of Mount Serrow, a ring of white stone open to the sky, and there were forty-one of them kneeling in the frost when Master Corane walked the circle with the oath-blade. Forty-one swordspeakers, from greybeards to children, Yara sixteen years old and so terrified her knees had left wet prints in the snow.

"You will speak the words," Corane had said, and her voice carried the way it always did, flat and clear as lake ice. "You will speak them once. The gods will hear you whether you whisper or shout, so save your breath for the years ahead."

The words themselves were older than the kingdom. Older than the mountain. Yara had copied them a hundred times in her novitiate hand without understanding them, rows of syllables that meant, roughly translated, *I place my life between the world and its ending.* It sounded noble written down. Kneeling in the frost with the oath-blade's edge resting against the hollow of her throat, it sounded like exactly what it was.

A chain, offered freely.

One by one they spoke. One by one the old gods answered — not with thunder, nothing so vulgar — but with a settling, a rightness, like a bone returning to its socket. Yara felt it enter her when her turn came: a second pulse beneath her heart, cold and vast and patient, and her sword, plain steel a moment before, hummed against her back like a struck bell.

Forty-one voices. One oath. She had wept, afterward, and Master Corane had put a hand on her cropped hair and said, "Good. If it didn't frighten you, I'd worry."

That night the whole order had feasted in the underhall, and old Wenna the Blade-Singer had gotten drunk enough to tell the truth about their history, the version not written in the novitiate copies.

"You know why the oaths work both ways, girl?" Wenna said, jabbing a wine-wet finger at Yara. "Because the gods are hungry. Always were. Kindly hungry, mostly — they feed on devotion, on keeping, on the weight of a promise held over years. That's their bread. We're not their servants." She'd grinned, gap-toothed. "We're their larder. The difference is just whether they ask politely."

The others had laughed. Yara had laughed too, sixteen and invincible with a god's cold pulse ticking under her heart.

She was the only one who remembered the joke three winters later, standing in the ashes of the Hall of First Light.

They never called it a purging, afterward. The crown's histories called it the Reformation, the Cleansing of Superstition, a tidy phrase for what actually happened, which was that old King Aldric's grandfather had looked at an order of forty-one men and women who could kill with a spoken word and answer to no throne, and decided they were a knife pointed at his neck. He found a zealot with a ledger and a grudge, gave him soldiers, and set him loose in the mountains in the dead of winter.

Yara had been on supply run to the lowland villages when it happened. Three days away. When she came back, the snow around the hall was pink for a hundred paces in every direction, and the doors had been pulled down, and the cold pulse beneath her heart was screaming.

Not grieving. She understood that later, though it took years. Screaming because forty oaths had been broken all at once — not fulfilled, not released, but *snapped*, cut short mid-keeping — and the gods fed on kept promises the way fire feeds on oil, and a severed oath was a starvation unlike anything else in the world.

She'd walked through the hall. She'd found Master Corane at the center of it, her oath-blade still in her hand, and Yara had knelt beside her in the ash and reached for the pulse beneath her heart expecting to find it gone, her power burned out of her with everyone else's.

It wasn't gone. Dimmer, yes — a candle where a bonfire had been — but there.

She'd assumed it was mercy. Some last gift from the sleeping gods, a seed preserved so the order might someday grow again. She'd spent ten years tending that assumption like a coal in her pocket, feeding it scraps of meaning, building a purpose out of it: *the last oath-bound swordspeaker, keeper of the flame.*

Standing on that ridge with the king's eyes on her, feeling her dying magic leap toward him like a dog toward its master's whistle, Yara finally let herself consider the other explanation.

Not mercy. Appetite.

Something below Thornfell had eaten forty broken oaths in a single winter and woken up starving, and everything since — the century of silence, the sleeping gods, and now a crown that whispered and a king who flickered — was that thing hunting for its next meal.

And the last oath in the world, the only unbroken vow still tethered to the old powers, was hers.

Her survival had never been accident. She'd been left alive the way a farmer leaves the best ewe unslaughtered.

* * *

" You're doing the face," Fen said.

Yara blinked. The fire had burned low. Bramble was asleep sitting up, which he claimed was a military skill; Osk had finally stopped polishing and was pretending to sleep in the honest way, breath slow, knife hand loose by his hip. Fen sat wrapped in two blankets, watching her with the patient expression of someone who had spent years being ignored on purpose and had learned to wait out weather.

"I don't have a face."

"You have six. This is the one where you've realized something awful and you're deciding whether it's our kind of awful." Fen tucked her chin into the blanket. "You've been wearing it since the convoy passed."

Yara looked at her — this runaway scribe she'd told a hundred times she worked alone, this child-shaped catastrophe she'd rescued off the salt road and never once managed to shake — and felt the familiar treachery of affection. Every person she loved was a hostage fortune could take. Every bond was an oath in miniature, and oaths, apparently, were food.

"The king," Yara said carefully, "is ill."

"The king is *possessed*. I read the tokens, remember? Whatever answers prayers now isn't answering them out of kindness." Fen's voice dropped. "You felt something near him. Don't bother denying it — your hand went to your sword hilt and you don't do that anymore unless—"

"Fen."

"—unless the old pulse wakes up, which it doesn't, which it *hasn't in ten years*, which means—" Fen stopped. Her eyes went very wide and very bright. "Oh. Oh, it's you, isn't it? It's not just the crown. It wants *you* too."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, a rock cracked with the cold, sharp as a snapped bone.

Yara thought about lying. She was good at lying; she'd built a career on plausible cargoes and forgettable faces. But Fen was looking at her the way novices used to look at Master Corane, and Yara was so tired of being the only adult in a room full of ghosts.

"It stirs when he's near," she said. "That's all I know."

"That's not all you know. That's all you'll *say*." But Fen let it go, which was her own particular mercy, and burrowed deeper into her blankets. At the edge of sleep she murmured, "Whatever it is — whatever's happening — you'd tell us. Before it ate you. Right?"

Yara didn't answer, and Fen pretended not to notice, and both of them lay awake long after.

* * *

Sleep brought the flashback whether she wanted it or not.

Not the hall, this time. Later. The worst night of the purging winter, when the zealot's soldiers had tracked survivors into the deep passes and Yara — nineteen, half-frozen, carrying the order's surviving relics in a sack on her back — had cornered in a ravine with three wounded elders who couldn't fight.

She remembered drawing her sword. Remembered reaching for the cold pulse, ready to spend whatever remained of her power killing soldiers, and finding instead that the pulse rose to meet her like a tide. Because she was about to do the one thing the old gods loved most: keep an oath unto death. Protect the order, unto death. She had sworn it at sixteen in the snow, and here was the death, arriving on schedule, and the power came flooding in like a debt repaid.

She had killed eleven soldiers in that ravine. She still saw their faces sometimes, young men mostly, conscripts with pitchforks and borrowed mail. When it was done, the three elders were dead anyway — wounds, cold — and Yara stood alone in the drifts with steam rising off her shoulders and a voice in the base of her skull that was not quite a voice, asking, in the wordless way of hunger:

*More.*

She had run then. Not from the soldiers — from that. She'd run until the pulse dimmed back to its candle-flame, and she'd spent ten years telling herself the dimming was the gods going to sleep, when maybe — she saw it clearly now, in the dream, in the waking — maybe it was her starving it. Maybe every year she refused to swear another vow, refused to bind herself further, refused to *feed*, the thing beneath Thornfell had grown thinner and angrier and more patient.

And now it had found a better vessel than any swordspeaker. A crown. A monarchy. An entire nation trained from birth to kneel and promise.

Yara woke before dawn with frost on her eyelashes and Osk already up, tightening his bootlaces.

"Riders coming up the valley," he said quietly. "Three. No colors." He paused. "But the lead one rides like Vane's people ride. I'd know that seat anywhere."

Yara was on her feet before he finished, the dread in her chest crystallizing into something simpler and more useful. Because if the crown's hunters had found their trail this fast, it wasn't luck or tracking. It was the same pull she'd felt on the ridge, working both directions, a leash drawn taut between the last oath-bound woman in the world and the starving god in the king's crown.

It hadn't finished with her. It had only begun to reel her in.

"Bramble," she said, and her voice came out steady, swordspeaker-steady, the old cold rising in her blood whether she wanted it or not. "Break camp. Fen, take the ledgers and run east. Osk—"

She stopped. Three riders. One former royal blade. One cook with a cleaver. One girl with stolen prayers. One last oath, frayed thin, humming like a wire.

Osk waited, hand on his sword.

"Osk and me," Yara finished, "are going to go say hello — and find out why inquisitors are riding straight for *me*."
