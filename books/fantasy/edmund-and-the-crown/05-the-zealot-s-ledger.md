## 5. The Zealot's Ledger

Osk smelled the inquisition before he saw it. That was the thing about Serah Vane — she traveled like a storm front, all incense and oiled steel and that cloying sanctified wax the crown's chaplains used to seal their warrants. Ten years since he'd stood beside her at morning drills, and his body still knew the scent the way a dog knows thunder.

He was hauling a barrel of salted eels up from the docks of Marrow's Rest when the royal banners came down the high street: white and gold, the sunburst crown sewn fat with real thread. The townsfolk pressed against shopfronts to watch. Osk did not press. He set down his barrel, wiped his hands on his apron, and made himself small in the doorway of the Drowned Eel, where he'd spent three weeks mopping floors and drinking quietly and telling himself it was temporary.

Everything about him was supposed to be temporary. That was the joke of it.

Six riders, then a wagon, then twelve more soldiers on foot. And riding at the head of them, straight-backed as a lance, her dark hair bound under a cap of white linen — Inquisitor Serah Vane, who had once shared his campfire and his rations and, for one winter he did not talk about, his blanket.

She looked older. Not softer. The lines around her mouth had deepened into something carved, deliberate, like grooves worn by a rope sawn back and forth across the same wood for years.

Osk picked up his barrel and walked, unhurried, toward the back stairs.

* * *

The Drowned Eel's attic room was four paces by six and smelled of river mold, and Osk had paid for it in coin he'd earned honestly for the first time in his life, which felt like wearing someone else's boots. He sat on the cot with his knees drawn up and listened to the town below change its voice.

Marrow's Rest had been a loud, shabby, cheerful place. By evening it was quiet in a new way — the quiet of people listening to themselves. He could hear the crier at the crossroads reading the inquisitor's proclamation: *By order of His Majesty's Grace, let it be known that God-touched contraband is an abomination—*

God-touched. There was a phrase that would have meant nothing a year ago. But the gods were waking — or something was waking and wearing their faces — and prayer-tokens moved through mountain passes in smugglers' packs, and the crown had decided that anything answered from beyond the veil belonged to the throne or to the fire.

And somewhere over those passes, right now, Yara Sterling was guiding exactly such cargo, with Bramble complaining about the altitude and Fen scribbling in that stolen notebook and the whole ridiculous family that Osk had never asked to be part of, never wanted, and could not seem to leave.

*Temporary,* he told the ceiling. *You signed on as hired muscle. You've been paid. Your part ended at the lowland trail.*

The ceiling did not answer, which was more than he deserved.

He thought about the ledger. He hadn't seen it yet, but he knew Vane; she would have one. She kept lists the way other women kept gardens — tenderly, weeding daily. Somewhere in that book there would be a page with names on it. Sterling, Yara. Bramble, no surname given, description: *cook, large, unarmed, dangerous anyway.* Fenwick, R., runaway scribe, value: information only, take alive.

And Osk. Formerly Sergeant Oskar Dann, Fourth Royal Blades, dishonorably discharged for striking an officer. The officer had deserved it. The discharge had been a mercy, and mercies from the crown always came with hooks.

He wondered what name they'd written beside his.

There was a knock at the door, soft, and then a voice through the wood that stopped his heart for a full second before his brain caught up: not Vane. A child. "Sir? Mum says you left your wages on the table again." Coins slid under the door. Small feet ran off.

He stared at the coins a long time. Honest money, honestly earned, left behind by a man planning to slip out of a town before dawn without settling accounts. Some part of him — the sergeant part, the part that had once believed in something — apparently refused to die quietly.

He pocketed the coins. It changed nothing. He told himself it changed nothing.

* * *

He went to see her anyway. That was the part he couldn't explain later, even to himself. Curiosity, maybe. Or the old soldier's superstition that you should always look at the thing most likely to kill you.

The chapel of Saint Verene had been shuttered for years — dead gods make dead parishes — but the inquisition had opened it up and lit it with forty candles, and Vane held court at the altar rail with the warrant laid out before her and the ledger open beside it. Townsfolk filed past in a nervous line, summoned by name from the crier's list, questioned, dismissed. Two of the candlesticks were missing from the rail. Looted by her soldiers, or requisitioned — with Vane there was rarely a difference.

Osk joined the line with his collar up and his cap low, and hated himself for how well it worked. Ten years of hiding teaches a man a certain gait.

When his turn came, the clerk read his assumed name off a scrap — "Toman Reeve, dockhand?" — and Vane looked up.

She looked at him for exactly as long as she needed to.

Her face did not change. That was the terrible discipline of her; he remembered reviewing recruits with her, watching her hold a blank expression while some trembling boy lied about stolen rations, letting him dig his own grave with every word. She said, "Where were you on the night of the third?"

"Here. Working."

"Working where?"

"The Eel. Mopping."

Someone could confirm it. Someone would; the landlady liked him. Vane turned to the clerk. "Mark him. Present and accounted." Then, as Osk turned to go, she spoke again, mild as milk: "Sergeant."

The word hit his spine like a dropped coin hitting stone. He didn't stop walking. Stopping was confession.

"Dann," she said, louder now, so the whole chapel heard it. "Formerly Fourth Royal Blades. Stand at ease, sergeant, I'm not arresting you. Yet."

The line of townsfolk rippled away from him as if he'd caught fire. Osk turned. Made himself stand the way he used to stand, heels together, chin level, ten years falling off him like a dropped cloak.

"Inquisitor," he said.

"You look terrible." Her eyes moved over him — the dockhand's apron, the gray coming into his beard, the limp he'd never bothered to hide from her because she'd been there when the leg got ruined. "I'd heard you were dead."

"I heard worse things about you. Turns out they're true."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of where a smile had once lived, before it was evicted. "Walk with me, sergeant."

It wasn't a request.

* * *

They walked the river path behind the chapel, her two guards trailing far enough back to give the conversation privacy, which was itself a message: whatever happened here would happen deniably.

"The crown pays pensions to discharged men who serve it," Vane said, watching the water. "Yours lapsed when you vanished after the Thornfell assessments. I could restore it. Back-pay, even."

"I don't want your coin."

"No. You never did. It's what made you useful, and unbearable." She walked a few steps in silence. "You know who moves cargo through the high passes, Dann. You've spent months in the lowlands; my maps show your little gaps like missing teeth. You drank in Marrow's Rest for three weeks and took no work but sweeping floors, and every market day you watched the mountain road like a man watching for a lover's sail."

"Maybe I like the view."

"Sterling." The name dropped between them like a blade laid flat on a table. "Yara Sterling. Last of the swordspeakers. She runs prayer-tokens through Thornfell — I have three confessions confirming it, two of them bought and one of them true. My ledger has her crew. It has a cook. A scribe. And a former sergeant of the Fourth whose file says, and I quote the adjudicator, *'dangerous loyalty to persons over institutions.'*" She finally turned to face him. Up close her eyes were the same gray-brown they'd always been, the color of a river in November. "Give me her route. Give me the next crossing point and the day. You'll be paid, pensioned, and struck from the ledger. Your name burns, sergeant. Everyone else's stays."

Osk looked at the river. The current carried leaves and one drowned crow, patient, indifferent.

"You always did think everyone could be bought," he said. "Just not with money."

"It's not buying. It's arithmetic." Her voice stayed level, reasonable, and that was the horror of Serah Vane — she had never once raised her voice in righteousness. She simply calculated. "The thing in the capital is eating the king. I have seen it. I knelt in the Golden Hall last month and I watched His Majesty's shadow move wrong against the wall, and I have read every text the archive owns, and the texts agree: god-touched goods are vectors. Every token Sterling smuggles is another thread binding this kingdom to something that is not our god and not kind. She thinks she's moving trinkets. She is moving plague. Men like you protect her because she feeds you belonging. I am offering you the chance to save her from herself, and save yourself in the process, and you're standing there composing a noble refusal because nobility is easier than thinking."

For a moment — just a moment — Osk almost admired her. Because underneath the doctrine, she wasn't lying. He'd seen enough in recent weeks to suspect she might even be right about the tokens, about the hunger in the answers. Yara knew it too; he'd seen her face when Fen's little wish got granted.

But Vane's arithmetic had a remainder she never counted: what happened to people after the ledger closed over them.

"And if I say no?" he asked.

"Then your name stays in the book." She said it without heat. "Dann. Ten years ago you broke a captain's jaw because he was going to burn a farmstead for grain tax. I wrote the report that saved you from the noose instead of the discharge. Do you remember why?"

Because you agreed with me, he almost said. Because you cried that night in the tent where you thought I couldn't hear.

"Because you follow rules," he said instead.

"Because I follow *the last rule that matters:* the kingdom survives. Everything else is sentiment." She drew a folded paper from her coat and held it out. On it, in her own precise hand, was a map sketch — the lowland trails, three crossings marked, dates blank. "You don't even have to speak to me again. Mark the route. Leave this on the chapel step any night before the new moon. No names. No witness. Just ink."

He didn't take it. She set it on the railing between them and walked away, her guards falling in behind, her heels striking the boards like a metronome for everything she'd ever destroy.

The paper lay there in the wind, lifting and settling, lifting and settling.

He took it. Because leaving it fluttering on the rail was evidence, and taking it was nothing, and he was very good at telling himself stories about the difference.

* * *

He packed that night. It didn't take long; it never did. One bag, one sword wrapped in oilcloth, one whetstone worn to a curve that fit only his hand. Three weeks of honest living folded up and abandoned, which proved the whole thing temporary, exactly as he'd always said.

Exactly as he'd always told them.

He sat on the edge of the cot with the map in his lap and thought about the night on the pass when Fen had whispered a wish into a stolen token and the mountain had *moved*, and how Yara's face had gone white and old, and how afterward she'd stood apart from the fire and he'd brought her tea because nobody else thought to, and she'd taken it without looking at him and said, *You didn't have to do that,* and he'd said, *No,* and she'd said, *Thank you,* which was the most either of them had ever admitted to anything.

Bramble would fight. Bramble would break his ladle across the first soldier's helm and get a spear for his trouble, because Bramble loved loudly and died stupidly, and Osk had seen men die stupidly enough for one lifetime.

Fen wouldn't fight. Fen would run, and be run down, because the scribe had legs like a heron and the heart of one too.

And Yara — Yara would send them all away first and stand alone, because that was her whole religion, the faith she actually practiced: *I work alone.* Standing in the middle of four people, feeding them, arming them, teaching Fen knife-work in the evenings, correcting Osk's map-reading with a patience she gave no one else — and saying, with a straight face, *I work alone.*

If the ledger came for them, they'd die of her lie and his silence both.

Osk looked at the map. Three crossings. Blank dates. Ink waiting in the inkwell of the Drowned Eel's front room, and a chapel step in the dark, and a pension, and a name burned clean out of a book.

He thought about Serah Vane crying in a tent ten years ago, silently, furiously, ashamed of it, and he understood suddenly that she was still crying — that the whole inquisition was a woman weeping in the only way the crown allowed her, which was with fire.

He thought: *she'll find the route anyway.* She had three confessions, two bought and one true. She had wagons and soldiers and the patience of a woman who weeded lists like gardens. If he warned them, it cost him everything and bought them, at best, days.

If he didn't warn them, it cost him nothing except everything that mattered.

He dressed, and went down the back stairs, and bought a fast horse from the liveryman at a price that would have embarrassed him a month ago, and rode out of Marrow's Rest under a thin sickle moon with the map unmarked in his coat and his old sword wrapped on his back like something he was smuggling.

The mountain road climbed north. Somewhere up there, past the snowline, past the shrine-stones where dead gods slept, a cook was burning dinner and a scribe was writing down everything and a woman who worked alone was pretending not to count her people at night like a farmer counting sheep.

Osk put his heels to the horse.

Behind him, in Marrow's Rest, a clerk in the chapel of Saint Verene recorded the departure of *Toman Reeve, dockhand* in a neat hand, and beneath it noted the time, and beneath that left a space — because Inquisitor Vane had instructed the clerk, before retiring, that every gap in the record was itself a kind of testimony.

She read the entry at dawn. She read the empty stable. She read the shape of the story, the way she read everything: completely, coldly, correctly.

Then she called for her horse, and gave one order to her captain — *"the high passes, tonight"* — and opened her ledger to a page with four names on it, and drew a single careful line through the fourth.

Not crossed out. Marked.

Found.
