## 6. Bargains in the Dark

The storm came down off the high passes two hours after dark, and Yara knew before the first fat flakes fell that something was wrong with her swordspeech.

It had been doing this for three nights now — a low hum in the bones of her right hand, like a tuning fork struck and never allowed to die. For years her power had been a guttering candle, barely enough to sharpen a blade, barely enough to matter. Now it sat in her palm like a coal that would not cool, and she did not trust it. Nothing that woke after a hundred years of silence woke gently.

"You're doing it again," Bramble said from the mouth of the cave. He had a pot of barley stew balanced on his knee and a spoon in his fist like a weapon he'd decided not to use yet. "The staring-at-your-hand thing."

"I'm not staring at my hand."

"You've been staring at your hand since we made camp. Fen noticed too. Fen notices everything, which is why I'm surprised Fen hasn't noticed you're staring at your hand."

"Fen is asleep."

"Fen is *pretending* to be asleep because Fen learned a long time ago that pretending to sleep means people say interesting things near you." Bramble pointed the spoon toward the back of the cave, where a small shape lay curled in a bedroll with its breathing just slightly too even. "Case in point."

Yara almost smiled. Almost. She closed her hand instead, and the coal-heat dimmed but didn't die.

"Something's coming up the trail," Osk said.

He was at the far edge of the overhang, where the mountain dropped away into white darkness, and his voice had gone flat and quiet in the way it only went when he meant business. Yara was on her feet before she'd finished deciding to be. Her sword came out of its wraps with a whisper of oilcloth, and the moment her bare fingers found the grip, the hum in her bones roared into something close to song.

She hadn't felt that in six years. Not since Thornfell. Not since the night her whole order burned.

"Yara," Osk said again, tighter now. "There's someone on the switchbacks. One rider. No escort. No lantern. He's going to kill himself on that grade if he doesn't—"

The horse screamed somewhere below them, a short brutal sound, and then there was nothing but wind.

* * *

They found him at the bottom of the third switchback, tangled with his dead horse in the snow, and Yara knew him instantly even though she'd only seen him once before, gaunt and flickering inside a royal convoy.

King Edmund of the realm looked like a man who had been used as an eraser. His face had been worn down — cheekbones sharp enough to shame a blade, lips cracked, eyes sunken into bruised hollows. He wore no crown. That was the first thing her swordspeaker's senses told her, screaming: *no crown*, and yet the thing was *there*, coiled around him all the same, invisible and vast, the way a chain is still a chain whether or not you can see the links.

He was trying to stand when they reached him. He kept failing, legs folding like wet paper, and each time he failed he laughed — a thin, awful exhalation that wasn't laughter at all.

"Don't," he rasped, when Osk reached for him. "Don't help me up. I crawled here. Let it stay crawled." Then his eyes found Yara, and everything in his face changed. Recognition. Relief. And underneath both, terror so pure it looked like reverence. "You're real. Good. I was afraid I'd dreamed the convoy. I've dreamed a great deal lately that turned out not to be dreaming."

"Your Majesty," Osk said carefully, hand drifting toward his own blade.

"No." Edmund said it without heat, without emphasis, the way a man says *no, thank you, I've eaten*. "Not that. Please don't call me that. It hears when you call me that. It likes titles. Titles are handles." He got one knee under himself and stopped, swaying, and looked up at Yara with those ruined eyes. "You're the last swordspeaker. Sterling. I asked the archives about you after the pass. They keep files. Did you know that? Files on everyone the old gods ever touched. Yours has a black border."

"What do you want?" Yara kept her voice level. The coal in her hand was singing now, and she wrapped her fingers tighter around the grip to smother it. Behind her, she heard Fen creep out of the cave, heard Bramble mutter something protective and profane.

Edmund's answer was simple. Terrible. Utterly calm.

"Death. I want death. Yours to give, if any sword in this kingdom can manage it. I'm told yours is special." He spread his arms, kneeling in the snow like a man at prayer, and the gesture was so naked, so exhausted, that Yara felt it in her chest like a hook set behind the ribs. "I escaped my own guards. Do you understand what that costs me? Every hour I am unattended, it spends me like coin. I have perhaps until dawn before it takes the reins back. So I rode through a blizzard to find a smuggler, because smugglers are the only people left in this country who aren't praying to the thing wearing my face." He smiled, and the smile cracked his lips, and a bead of blood welled dark against the white of the snow-light. "Kill me before the renewal rites. Name your price. Land, gold, pardons for every soul in your crew, whatever the ledgers can hold. I'll sign anything. I'll sign my own abdication in blood if it speeds the blade."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Fen made a small sound — the sound of a scribe hearing words that will need recording someday and wishing desperately they wouldn't.

Yara crouched down to the king's level, slowly, keeping her sword low. Up close she could see what the crown-thing had done to him: fine tremors under his skin, moving wrong, moving in patterns that didn't belong to muscle. And beneath that, faint as breath on winter glass, the smell of something old and starving, like a cellar where meat went bad a century ago.

"When I stood on the pass," she said quietly, "and your convoy forced me to guide it — you had four minutes of clarity. You begged me then, too. I said no."

"And I've come to ask better." His voice didn't waver. "You refused once. I respect it. Refusing a king is expensive; refusing him twice must look ruinous. But I've had weeks to think about what it is I'm asking, and I find I'm no longer begging. I'm *commissioning*. There's a difference. Begging asks for mercy. Commissioning offers payment." He fumbled at his belt with shaking hands and pulled free a ring — heavy gold, stamped with the royal seal, the kind of ring that could buy a mountain town outright. He held it out to her in his open palm like a man offering bread. "Everything you've ever smuggled, you did to survive. Here is survival beyond counting. All I want in exchange is to stop."

And God help her, Yara looked at that ring, and she thought about Fen's frost-bitten fingers and Osk's badly healed ribs and Bramble's bad knee, and she thought about how easy it would be, how clean. One stroke. The oath her order had died protecting would finally mean something: the sword that speaks speaks truest at the neck of kings.

Her hand was humming so hard the blade was shivering audibly.

"No," she said.

Edmund closed his eyes. When he opened them, there was nothing left in them but arithmetic — a man calculating exactly how far he could crawl before dawn.

"Then I'll find another way," he said. "There are always other ways. Rivers freeze. Cliffs are patient. Thank you for your time, smuggler. I'm sorry to have wasted—"

"That's not what I said." She sheathed her sword, and the sudden quiet of it made him blink. "I said no to killing you. I've spent three weeks thinking about you, Your Majesty. About the crown. About what answered Fen's prayer on the mountain, and what it wanted, and why my dead order's magic started waking up the moment that thing put on your head." She let that land. Watched him go very still. "You're not cursed. Curses are simple. You're *bound*. Bound things can be unbound. My order knew how. It's in the Rites — the ones the purges were supposed to burn. The ones I carry in my head because I'm the last fool alive who was taught to read them."

The king stared at her.

"The Rites of Unbinding," Fen whispered from behind her, reverent, horrified. "Yara, those are a *myth*. Those are the myth. Even the Scriptorium only has fragments—"

"They're real," Yara said, not looking away from Edmund. "And I know them."

And then King Edmund of the realm began to laugh.

It was nothing like the thin terrible exhalation from before. This laugh started deep, in the belly, in the wrecked hollow places of him, and came up rough and rusty like a hinge unused for years, and it shook his whole broken body, and he had to catch himself on his dead horse's saddle to keep from toppling. It went on long past comfort, long past sense, while snow gathered on his shoulders and his crew watched him like he'd gone mad — and maybe he had; Yara wouldn't have bet against it.

"The lost rites," he gasped at last, wiping his eyes with the back of a hand grimed to the wrist. "Of course. Of *course*. I ride half-dead through a blizzard to beg for the grave, and some stubborn woman on a mountainside hands me paperwork instead." He laughed again, shorter, softer, and this time it sounded like it might actually belong to him. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone offered me a third option? My council offers two: kneel faster, or die quietly and let the succession proceed. My god offers two: submit, or be devoured slower. Everyone in my life is a pair of doors, and you're the first person in months to point at a wall and say *there's a way through.*"

"There might not be," Yara said bluntly. "I won't lie to you. The rites are dangerous. Half the text is warnings, and the other half is in liturgical verse that argues with itself. And it requires things." She hesitated. "Things I'll need time to gather. Time you may not have, riding alone through mountains with that thing clawing its way back up your throat."

"Then I suppose I'll have to impose upon your hospitality." He said it lightly, but Yara saw what it cost him — a king, asking smugglers for shelter, knowing exactly how much trust he had no right to spend. He tried to stand again. Failed again. This time, when Osk stepped forward, he didn't refuse.

"Your Majesty," Osk said gruffly, hauling him upright with a soldier's grip, "if you fall over again, I'm letting Bramble carry you, and he complains the entire time."

"Bramble," Bramble called from the back, "does not complain. Bramble *narrates grievances*. It's different, and it's art."

That startled a second laugh out of the king — smaller, disbelieving, aimed at these absurd people who had taken him in off the snow within minutes of his confessing to regicide-by-proxy. Yara watched it happen and felt the coal in her chest shift, and understood suddenly, with cold clarity, exactly what the crown-thing had miscalculated.

It fed on oaths. On devotion. On the solemn binding of souls.

It had no defense at all against a crew like this — people bound by nothing but choice, renewed daily, sworn to nobody.

* * *

They gave him the driest corner of the cave, the good blanket, and Bramble's stew, and he accepted all three like a man being handed state treasures. Fen had already produced charcoal and a scrap of paper and was sketching the layout of the rites from memory of Yara's recitation, muttering about marginalia. Osk took first watch without being asked, positioned between the king and the trail.

Yara sat across the fire from Edmund and laid out the truth like cards she couldn't take back.

"It needs an oath-breaker's blood," she said. "That part's clear enough even in the arguing verses. Someone who swore to the old gods and lived to unsay it. That's me. There's no version of this where I'm not standing in the circle."

"And the other requirement?"

"A willing vessel. You, sitting in the seat of the binding while it's cut. You'll feel everything it does to unmake itself leaving you. Some people don't survive that." She held his gaze. "I won't pretend those odds are good. If you'd rather I take the first offer — the quick blade, the clean end — say it now, tonight, honestly, and I'll consider it with an open heart."

Edmund was quiet for a long time. Firelight moved across his ruined face, and for a moment the flickering thing behind his eyes rose close to the surface — she saw it, a darkness swimming under his pupils like fish under ice — and then he breathed, slow and deliberate, and pushed it down.

"Do you know why it chose me?" he said finally. "Not my father. Me. My father prayed the way stone prays — dutiful, inert. I prayed *hungry*. Plague took my heirs in one summer, and I went to the shrine beneath Thornfell and I did not ask for strength or wisdom. I asked for *them back*. I would have paid anything. It heard the shape of my wanting and fit itself into it like a key." His hands tightened around the bowl of stew he'd barely touched. "So when you ask whether I'll sit in that chair willingly while your rite tries to tear it out of me — understand that I invited the thing in. Everything that follows from tonight is me paying a debt I opened the ledger for. Yes, Sterling. Willingly. Gladly. Write it down," he added, with a ghost of that real laugh, nodding at Fen. "The king consents. Witnessed."

Fen wrote it down. Yara saw the boy's hand shake, and saw him write it anyway.

"One more thing," Edmund said, quieter now, as the fire settled. "When it takes me back — and it will, before we're ready, I can feel it circling already — I may not be me. I may say things. Do things. I need you to promise me that whatever wears my mouth in the meantime, you'll remember that the man who crawled up a frozen mountain to find you was real. That he existed. Can you hold that?"

Yara thought of her order burning. Thought of every night she'd insisted she worked alone, in a language of solitude that had really been a language of grief.

"Yeah," she said. "I can hold that."

Above them, far up the black shoulder of Thornfell, lightning flickered inside the cloud with no thunder following — once, twice, rhythmic, patient, like something counting the days until the coronation renewal rite. The fire popped. Edmund slept at last, truly slept, and did not flicker.

And in Yara's sword-hand, the old power sang and sang, hungry for what was coming — while on the trail below, unheard by any of them, boots were already climbing.
