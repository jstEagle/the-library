## 7. The Library That Walks

Fen had stolen a great many things in his life — candlesticks, confessionals, an entire psalter once, though that last one still itched at him like an unpaid debt — but he had never stolen from a building that could walk away.

"Stop fidgeting," Yara said without turning around. She stood at the head of the switchback trail, wind pulling her braid sideways, watching the high col where the clouds were doing something clouds should not do. They were moving against the wind, folding and unfolding, and beneath them the mountainside itself seemed to breathe.

"I'm not fidgeting," Fen said. "I'm rehearsing."

"You're chewing your sleeve."

He pulled the wool out of his mouth with as much dignity as he could muster. Behind them, Bramble grunted under the weight of the pack that held everything they owned worth stealing, which was to say almost nothing: two changes of clothes, Osk's sword, Yara's oath-blade, Edmund wrapped in a smuggler's oilcloth and shivering with something worse than cold, and a pot of Bramble's pepper stew that no one had been allowed to finish because Bramble insisted they'd need their strength.

"The Scriptorium doesn't come to everyone," Yara said. "It's not a place. It's a *habit*. It goes where it wants, when it wants, and mostly it wants nothing to do with people." She glanced back at them all — at Osk leaning on his walking staff with his ribs still strapped, at Bramble breathing like a bellows, at Fen himself, who was trying very hard to look like a man who bargained with blind archivists every day. "But it has a weakness."

"Books love flattery," Fen said. "I know. I've read about it."

"No." Yara's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Monks love gossip. The outside world is the only thing those walls don't have. So you're going to give them some. And then you're going to ask for the Rites of Unbinding, and you are going to be very, very polite about it."

"And if they say no?"

Yara looked up at the breathing mountain, and for just a moment her face went somewhere Fen couldn't follow — somewhere old and tired and full of names she never said aloud. "Then we learn to live with a king who eats kingdoms," she said, "and I learn to stop being what I am. Which I've been practicing anyway."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke, or if it was, it was the kind of joke you make while standing on a ledge.

They climbed.

* * *

The Wandering Scriptorium found them before they found it.

That was how Fen would describe it afterward, in the pages he filled that night by firelight: *it found us*. One moment they were crossing a shelf of grey scree below the col, and the next moment there were steps in front of them — worn limestone steps, older than any kingdom Fen could name, rising out of bare stone as though they had always been there and everyone else had simply been too unobservant to notice. Above the steps, walls. Above the walls, roofs of slate gone green with lichen. Bells hung in an open tower, and they were ringing, though no rope moved and no hand touched them, and the sound was less like a summons than like a door clearing its throat.

"Well," said Bramble. "That's either salvation or a trap."

"Usually the same thing," said Osk.

The doors opened on their own. Of course they did.

Inside was warmth, lamplight, the smell of vellum and tallow and old ink — and silence so complete that Fen could hear his own heartbeat stumble. Monks in undyed grey moved through the corridors like fish through deep water, soundless, each carrying a book or a lamp or nothing at all, and not one of them looked at the intruders. Not because they hadn't noticed. Fen knew the difference between being unseen and being deliberately unregarded, and this was the second thing, and it made the skin of his arms prickle.

A novice — young, freckled, radiating the barely contained excitement of someone who had heard about weather but never experienced it — appeared at Fen's elbow and whispered, "You'll want the archivist. Third corridor, past the map room, through the hall of unfinished books. She's expecting you." A pause. A blush. "Is it true there are cities where the streets are lit all night?"

"Bright as noon," Fen whispered back solemnly. "In Lowmark they call it the Hundred Lamps, and half of them are colored."

The novice made a small noise like a kettle coming to boil and drifted away, visibly composing the sentence she would carry home like contraband.

"Gossip," Yara murmured at Fen's shoulder. "Told you."

The hall of unfinished books was the strangest room Fen had ever stood in, and he had once stood inside a tax collector's dream, but that is another story. Shelves rose out of sight into darkness, and on every shelf lay manuscripts with blank pages showing at their edges — books begun and abandoned, histories cut off mid-sentence, a genealogy that stopped at a name followed by four inches of empty vellum. Fen trailed his fingers along one as he passed and felt something like grief come off the paper, the way heat comes off a hearthstone hours after the fire dies.

At the far end, behind a desk built from a single slab of mountain oak, sat the archivist.

She was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years — ancient the way bedrock is ancient, the way rivers are. Her eyes were closed, had perhaps always been closed; the lids were smooth as river stones. Her hands rested on the desk before an open book, and even sitting still she seemed to be reading something. When Fen stopped before her, her head turned toward him with terrible precision, the way a compass needle finds north.

"A scribe," she said. Her voice was dry and warm, like paper left in the sun. "Runaway, by the ink stains on your cuff and the way you hold your shoulders like a man listening for footsteps. You've come to buy something."

Fen opened his mouth to deliver the speech he'd been rehearsing since dawn.

"You have nothing," the archivist continued, before he could begin. "No coin worth naming. No favors to trade. No name anyone respectable would honor." Her lips curved faintly. "Which means you'll pay the only currency this house has ever accepted. Memory."

The word dropped into the silence like a stone into a well.

"How much?" Yara asked from behind him.

"That," said the archivist, "depends entirely on what he wishes to take away."

* * *

They negotiated in the archivist's cell, which was smaller than Bramble's kitchen and held more knowledge than every library Fen had ever robbed combined. Yara wanted him to do the talking — *you're the wordsmith*, she'd said, as if that weren't the most terrifying thing anyone had ever entrusted to him — but she stood at his shoulder like a drawn blade, and Osk filled the doorway, and Bramble sat on the floor because there was nowhere else, eating bread he'd brought expressly for the purpose of having something to do with his hands.

"The Rites of Unbinding," Fen said. "Older than the purges. Swordspeaker rites. Full text, not fragments."

The archivist was quiet long enough that Fen counted forty of his own heartbeats and hated every one of them.

"There are three copies in the world," she said at last. "One burned with the Abbey of Serrow. One lies in the royal archive in the capital, under seal, misfiled as a cookbook by a clerk with a sense of humor or a grudge." A pause. "And one is here. I have read it eleven times. I remember every word, though remembering is a strange word for what I do."

"We'll pay memory," Fen said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Name the price."

"For a rite that can pull a god's teeth?" The blind face tilted. "A year of yours. A full year, whole and unbroken. Choose carefully — you will not know what you lost until you reach for it and find the shape of the hole."

A year. Fen thought of twenty-three years of memories, most of them bad, several of them criminal, a handful — the handful that mattered — spent in the company of the people currently crowding this cell. He thought about which year he could stand to lose and discovered, with a sensation like ice water, that there wasn't one. Every year had somebody in it now. Even the worst ones had Osk teaching him knife-work in the snow, had Bramble force-feeding him stew, had Yara—

"One year," he said. "Fine. Done. Where do I—"

"No," said Yara.

"It's my—"

"It's *not*, actually." She turned to the archivist, and Fen watched her do the thing she did when she decided to be terrifying, which was simply to become completely calm. "Here's my counteroffer, mother. Your house runs on memory because memory is the only thing that walks in here freely. But you're blind, and this monastery moves, and I'd wager the outside world has gotten strange since the last time anyone told you about it. So." She leaned on the desk. "We pay in kind. Four travelers, four payments, split across us until the toll is met. And in exchange for the trouble of splitting the bill—" her voice dropped into the register she used on customs officers, "—you get to keep your archivist's conscience intact, and I get to keep mine, and nobody leaves this mountain missing anything they can't live around."

Silence.

Then the archivist laughed — a rustling, papery sound, like a page turning by itself.

"Oh, you're *her*," she said. "The swordspeaker. The last one. I wondered when the oath-blade walked through my doors whether its bearer would be fool enough to attempt the unbinding." Her closed eyes somehow managed to convey that she found the entire affair delightful. "Very well, child of oaths. Split the bill. But understand the terms: each payment must be given willingly, must be a true year, and must be *yours*. No borrowed grief. No purchased pasts." Her head turned, needle-finding-north, toward Bramble. "Even yours, cook."

Bramble stopped chewing.

"Me?" he said. "I'm not even — I'm just the cook. Why would I pay? This isn't my rite. I don't know anything about rites. I make soup."

"Bramble," Fen said carefully, "you don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." He set down the bread and dusted off his hands with enormous dignity. "That's why I'm offering. Nobody made me do it, see? Write that down, scribe. Witnessed and willing."

* * *

The paying took place in the scriptorium's heart, a round chamber where the ceiling opened onto moving sky — actual sky, sliding past overhead as the monastery walked, clouds crossing the gap between stars. Four chairs waited. Fen did not want to know how the chairs had known to wait.

The archivist explained it simply, the way you explain surgery: "You will speak the memory aloud, beginning to end. As you speak, I take it. You'll feel it go. Most describe it as cold. Do not fight it — fighting makes ragged edges, and ragged edges ache for years." She folded her hands. "Who first?"

"I'll go," Fen said, before his courage could reconsider.

He chose the year he'd run away — his eighteenth. It seemed tidy. Symmetrical. The year he'd betrayed the Scriptorium of Lowmark, copied a forbidden index into his sleeve, and fled into the Thornfells with three chapters of heresy sewn into his coat lining. A bad year. A year he wouldn't miss.

Except, lying in the chair with the archivist's cool fingers resting on his temples, he remembered the details. The innkeeper's daughter in Harrow Ford who'd taught him dice and kissed him badly and waved him off at dawn. The winter night he'd nearly frozen and a stranger had shared a fire without asking his name. The day he'd first seen the crew's campfire through the trees and thought, *there, that one, that's the one worth robbing,* and had crept close meaning to steal and stayed meaning to belong.

"Begin," said the archivist.

He began. He spoke it all — the theft, the flight, the fire, the kiss, the campfire — and it poured out of him like water from a tipped jug, and the cold came in behind it exactly where the water had been, filling the shape of the year with clean white nothing. He felt it seal over. He felt the hole.

When it was done he sat up, and Yara was gripping his shoulder hard enough to bruise, and he couldn't remember why the mountains felt familiar, only that they did, and that the people around him were his, whatever the price of that had been.

"Your turn," the archivist said pleasantly, to Osk.

Osk paid his year grudgingly and in as few words as possible — a year of soldiering, he said afterward, and shrugged like a man setting down a pack he'd carried too long, and refused to say which one, and Fen noticed he didn't meet anyone's eye for an hour.

Bramble paid his with his eyes shut and his chin up like a martyr, muttering recipes under his breath the entire time — *flour first, always flour first* — and came out the other side patting his stomach and saying, "Huh. Feels like I skipped breakfast my whole life. Somebody feed me."

And then there was one chair left, and Yara was already sitting down in it.

"No," said Fen.

"It's the toll," Yara said. "It's short otherwise."

"Then we find another way—"

"There isn't one." She settled back, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked at each of them, Osk and Bramble and him, the way a person looks at a fire they intend to carry in their chest for a long dark road. "Choose something you won't miss," she advised, which was the least reassuring thing Fen had ever heard from her, because Yara missed nothing, Yara kept everything, Yara's whole self was a locked ledger of years she'd survived alone.

She spoke her year quietly, too quietly for Fen to catch more than fragments — a name, a burning, a door, snow — and when the cold finished sealing the hole she stood, rolled her shoulders like a woman putting armor back on, and said, "Right. Text. Let's see what this costs me next."

The archivist led them to a reading table and opened the Rites of Unbinding across it, and Fen bent over the vellum with his heart hammering, copying by lamplight while the sky slid overhead and the monastery walked.

He was halfway down the second column when he went still.

"What?" Yara said sharply. "What is it?"

Fen read the line again. Then again. Then, because he was a scribe and scribes check their work, a third time, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable.

They didn't.

"The rite needs two things," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "An oath-breaker's blood. That's you, that part we knew." He looked up at her, and at Edmund, sleeping in the corner under his oilcloth, and at Bramble and Osk, who had gone very quiet. "And a willing vessel. Someone to hold what comes out of the crown while the binding tears free. Someone who *chooses* it, fully knowing." He swallowed. "Yara. The vessel doesn't survive unchanged. The rite takes everything the vessel has of the old gods' making. For you—"

He couldn't finish it. He didn't have to. He watched her read the passage herself, watch her face do nothing at all, absolutely nothing, which was how he knew.

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, Edmund had woken — and was looking at Yara with an expression of slow, dawning horror, because kings, it turned out, could read faces too.
