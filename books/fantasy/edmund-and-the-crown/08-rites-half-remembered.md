## 8. Rites Half-Remembered

The Wandering Scriptorium had given them three things: a copy of the Rites of Unbinding in Fen's careful new hand, a silence that smelled of dust and candlewax, and the memory-toll that none of them spoke about on the road down from the high pass.

Yara read by firelight, alone, while the others slept.

She had waited two days to open it. Two days of guiding them through switchbacks and scree fields, of Bramble's stew and Osk's watch rotations and Fen's nervous, ink-stained chatter, all of it stretched over the parchment like skin over a wound she hadn't yet decided to look at. But tonight the camp was quiet — Edmund sleeping apart from the rest as always, his breathing too careful, the way a man breathes when he has learned not to trust his own lungs — and there was nothing left between her and the page.

The script was old. The archivist's transcription was newer, but the words beneath it were older still, older than the purging, older than the crown, maybe older than the mountains they sat inside of. Yara read slowly, her lips moving around syllables her order had once sung in chorus, and the firelight made the letters swim like fish in dark water.

*To unbind a god from its anchor vessel, three conditions must be met.*

*A willing vessel, who opens himself to the blade's edge of severance knowing it may part more than flesh from spirit.*

*An oath-breaker's blood, freely spilled upon the threshold stone, for only one who has broken faith with heaven may stand between heaven and earth and bar the door.*

*And a price, named aloud, which the rite will take without negotiation.*

Her eyes caught on the last line the way a boot catches on a root.

*The price is this: the unbinding requires an oath-bound soul as counterweight. What is bound must be answered. The swordspeaker who performs the rite shall surrender her bond entire — every word of power, every whispered prayer answered, every thread that ties her voice to the old gods' ears. She will speak her oath-breaking as she speaks the unbinding, and when the god is severed, so is she. Forever.*

Yara read it again.

Then a third time, as though the words might rearrange themselves out of mercy.

They didn't.

She closed her eyes and listened for the old gods the way she did every night — a habit now, like checking the locks on a cargo crate. Silence. It had been silence for years, a silence she had told herself was sleep, was distance, was the natural fading of things too large for the world's shrunken shape. Her swordspeech came thinner every season. A year ago she could have frozen a river with a word. Last month she'd needed three words and a full minute of concentration to turn a locked door's tumblers.

But it was *hers*. It was the last thing left of her order, of the sisters who had taught her the cadences, of the mountain sanctuary where she had been a child with a wooden sword and a head full of hymns. It was the proof that she had survived something that should have killed her. And the rite wanted it. All of it. Not spent, not risked — *surrendered*, cleanly and permanently, the way you'd cut off a hand to stop a poison spreading up the arm.

"Only one who has broken faith with heaven," she murmured. "That's me. Always me."

She looked at the fire. Somewhere beyond it, Edmund slept his careful sleep, and the thing in his crown wore him like a glove, and every day it ate a little more of him — she could see it now, in the hollows under his cheekbones, in the way his hands trembled when he thought no one watched.

There was no third option. She turned the pages twice looking for one. There was no way to substitute another bound soul; the rite specified *swordspeaker*, specified *oath-bound*, specified *her*, specifically, precisely, as though the text had been written a hundred years ago by someone who could see across time to a tired woman reading by a dying fire.

Maybe someone had.

She fed the page to her memory instead of the fire, folding the parchment away into the oilcloth with the rest of their precious cargo, and lay down with her back to the flames, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold. Bramble had porridge going before anyone else stirred — the man cooked the way other people prayed, steadily and without complaint — and Yara ate hers in silence, watching the crew assemble itself around the fire with the easy choreography of people who had survived each other long enough to become family.

Fen was sketching the pass ahead in the margin of a journal. Osk was re-wrapping the bandage on his forearm, refusing help the way he refused everything. Bramble hummed. Ordinary morning sounds. Yara catalogued them the way a miser counts coins, and hated herself a little for doing it, and kept counting anyway.

"You didn't sleep," Osk said. Not a question. He had a soldier's eye for it.

"I slept."

"You sat up reading and then stared at the coals for four hours. I took second watch."

"The text is dense," she said. "Old liturgical structure. Lots of repetition. You'd hate it."

"Probably." He tied off the bandage and looked at her a moment longer than he needed to, and then, because he was Osk, let it go — visibly, deliberately, the way a man sets down a heavy pack he intends to pick back up later.

Edmund emerged from his corner of the camp looking, for once, almost rested. That frightened her more than the gauntness had. The good days were worse than the bad ones; the bad ones were just the thing wearing him openly, but the good ones meant he was fighting, spending himself to hold ground he couldn't keep.

"Bramble," he said, accepting a bowl, "I want you to know this is the first honest meal I've had in a year. Everything in the palace tastes of iron."

"That'll be the god, majesty."

"Edmund."

"Edmund." Bramble ladled him seconds without being asked. "God or no god, nobody leaves my fire hungry."

Fen laughed, and the sound loosened something in the circle, and for a moment they were just five people on a mountain eating porridge, and Yara let herself have the moment whole, hoarding it, because she was beginning to understand that she was counting down and hadn't known the number until last night.

They walked. The pass narrowed. Edmund fell into step beside her near midday, when the others had strung out along the trail, and for a while neither of them spoke, boots crunching on frost-brittle stone.

"You've been strange since the Scriptorium," he said finally.

"I'm always strange. It's how I stay charming."

"You're avoiding my eyes."

"I'm navigating a cliff face."

He stopped walking. She had to stop too, or leave him behind, and leaving him behind wasn't in her anymore — that was the problem, that was the whole rotten heart of the problem. He studied her with those king's eyes that had learned, somewhere in the plague years, to read rooms full of lying courtiers.

"What does the rite cost?" he asked.

"Complicated liturgical mechanics. You wouldn't—"

"Yara." Quiet. Not sharp. That was the worst of it — he never raised his voice anymore, because the thing in him heard loud emotion the way sharks hear blood. "I have spent a year being lied to by everyone I love, by a god, by my own mouth. I know what it looks like when someone decides I can't bear the truth. Please don't do me the courtesy."

Wind moved through the pass. Below them, the others had rounded a bend and disappeared from view, and the trail stood empty in both directions, and Yara found that her prepared evasions — and she had several, good ones, smuggler's ones — had all quietly died in her throat.

"It costs a counterweight," she said. "A bound soul. An oath-bound swordspeaker. The rite takes the binding entire. Permanently."

He absorbed that. She watched him absorb it — watched the understanding move through him like weather, and then keep moving, past the surface, down to what it actually meant, and she saw the exact moment he arrived.

"No," he said.

"It's not a proposal. It's information."

"No. We find another way."

"There is no other way. I've read it three times. The rite specifies—"

"Then we find a *fourth* way!" And there it was, the crack in his control, the flash of the man he'd been before the crown — a king who had once argued trade law with ambassadors for eleven hours out of pure stubbornness. He caught himself immediately, pressed his lips together, breathed. When he spoke again it was barely above the wind. "You don't get to decide this alone."

"Neither do you."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because—" He gestured at her, at all of her, and for a moment no words came, and then one did, and it landed harder than any argument could have. "*You're the first person in a year who's treated me like a man instead of a throne.* I'm not losing that to a paragraph in a dead book."

Yara looked away, down the trail, at the empty stone path where their family had gone ahead without them.

"Edmund. If we don't do this, the thing eats you before the renewal rite. Those are the options. Me, or the paragraph."

"Then we tell the others. Let them—"

"*No.*"

It came out harder than she intended, and she saw him register it, and she pressed on before pity could make it worse.

"They'll try to take my place. They'll tear themselves apart trying, and there's nothing any of them can do — Fen isn't bound, Osk swore oaths to a *crown*, not a god, Bramble—" She stopped. Breathed. "There's no one else, Edmund. That's the whole truth of it. There has never been anyone else. I'm the last swordspeaker alive, and the rite knows it, and I think — I think maybe it's known it for a long time. Maybe everything that's happened, maybe my surviving the purging, maybe all of it was just the world arranging pieces for this moment."

"Or maybe you're tired and reading fate into coincidence."

"Maybe." She smiled, and it hurt. "I'm good at smuggling, your majesty. I know contraband when I carry it. This has been sitting in my chest for years, waiting for a buyer."

"Don't joke."

"It's how I stay charming."

He reached out — hesitated — and took her hand. His fingers were cold and trembling faintly, the god's hunger humming underneath his skin like a struck bell that never fully went silent. She let him hold on.

"If you hide this from them," he said, "and it works, and you lose it — they'll forgive you. Eventually. But they'll never stop wondering why you didn't trust them with the truth."

"I know."

"And if you hide it," he said, "and you die doing it, I will spend whatever remains of my life being the man who let you. I need you to understand that. Your sacrifice doesn't purchase my silence. It purchases my complicity. Those aren't the same thing, and I refuse to confuse them."

She stared at him. The wind moved. Somewhere below, distantly, Bramble's laugh carried up the pass — bright and incongruous against the gray stone, a cook's laugh, a survivor's laugh.

"You're supposed to be grateful," she said weakly.

"I am. That's what makes it unforgivable."

* * *

They told no one that night. But they told each other everything, in the way of people standing on the edge of something — sitting apart from the fire, sharing a blanket against the cold because the cold was easier to talk around than the truth, trading confessions like smuggled goods.

He told her about the plague winter. About the nursery that went quiet, one small fever at a time, while the priests burned offerings that did nothing and the old gods slept on. About the night he'd knelt in the crypt beneath the palace, alone, mad with grief, and heard something answer for the first time in a century — something vast and patient and *starving*, offering him a kingdom where no more children died, asking only for a crown to live in and a king to wear it.

"I knew it was wrong," he said. "That's the part I can't make anyone understand. I wasn't deceived. I walked in with my eyes open because the alternative was walking out, and walking out meant staying in a world where my daughters were dead for no reason at all. Grief makes theologians of us all. I chose the theology that let me keep functioning."

"I know," Yara said.

"Do you?" He turned to look at her, firelight catching the hollows of his face. "Because you've been carrying your own crypt for years, and I've watched you kneel at it every night listening for gods who don't answer. What did you hear, the night you realized they'd gone silent? Because I think it was the same thing I heard. Nothing. And nothing, done long enough, starts sounding like permission."

She didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't also a confession, and her confession was folded in oilcloth with the rite, and she had already given him enough.

Instead she said: "When it's done — after — don't let them make you a saint. Saints get crowns put back on them. Be a nuisance instead. Kings who are nuisances get to grow old."

"And what will you be? After?"

"Tired. Rich, probably. Smuggling runs better without a conscience."

"Liar."

"Charming liar."

He laughed — soft, real, quickly smothered out of habit — and under the blanket his cold fingers found hers again, and stayed.

Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and ancient, and Yara lay awake long after his breathing evened out, listening one more time for the old gods. Silence, silence, and underneath the silence — faint as a pulse in a wrist, faint as something turning over in its sleep far beneath the mountain — a stirring that was not quite holy and not quite hungry and was, unmistakably, *aware of her*.

The rite required her blood on the threshold stone.

She flexed her sword-hand in the dark, watching the tendons move, memorizing them.

Three days, maybe four, to the deep shrine where the rite had to be spoken. Three days of porridge and banter and Fen's sketches and Osk's watches and Bramble's humming. Three days of family, at the end of which she would pay the toll and say nothing, and hope that love, like any good contraband, would survive being carried past the border undeclared.

In the lowest fold of the pass, where the trail bent out of sight, a lantern was moving toward them against the flow of the night wind.

Yara sat up. The lantern stopped. And out of the dark, in a voice she had last heard across an inquisitor's ledger, came the words she had dreaded more than any god's:

"Swordspeaker. We need to talk about your cook."
