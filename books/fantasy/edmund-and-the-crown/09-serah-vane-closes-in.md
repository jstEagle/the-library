## 9. Serah Vane Closes In

Serah Vane had interrogated forty-one people in her career as an inquisitor of the Crown, and she had learned one truth above all others: everyone broke at the thing they loved most.

Not the thing they feared. Fear was a blunt instrument, and blunt instruments taught a prisoner to endure. But love — love was a hinge, and every door swung on its hinges eventually. She had watched hardened smugglers weep over a sister's name, watched oath-breakers surrender fortresses for the sake of a dog. The Crown's manuals spoke of racks and irons and fire. Vane had burned all three in her first year and never looked back. Fire only proved how much a person could stand. Love proved what they could not.

So when the cook came stumbling down from the high pass with flour still on his knuckles and a sack of dried plums slung over one shoulder like a sleeping child, she did not send him to the cells.

She sent for tea.

* * *

Bramble's hands were shaking, but his voice was not.

He sat on the floor of the waystation's back room because they had chained him to the wall there, and he had decided — in the slow, deliberate way he decided everything, the way he decided when to salt a broth or when to let it be — that if he was going to be chained to a wall, he was going to cook while he did it.

There was nothing to cook. That was the trouble. So instead he recited.

"Barley soup," he murmured, eyes closed, wrists rattling softly against the iron. "One pot, feeds six. You render the fatback first, low, don't rush it, you'll scorch it and then the whole pot tastes like regret. Two onions. Cut them fine. Finer than you think you need to, because by the time the barley's done the onions should have given up being onions altogether."

The chains clinked. Somewhere beyond the door, boots crossed the yard.

"You toast the barley dry before it goes in the pot. Everyone skips this. Everyone regrets it. Toasted barley holds its spine in the broth; untoasted barley goes soft and apologetic, and there's no place in my pot for anything apologetic." A pause. His breath caught on something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Yara says I talk about food like other men talk about their mothers. I told her that's because my mother couldn't cook and my soup has never once let me down."

He opened his eyes. The room was dim, lit by one high window, and the light fell across the floorboards in a long dusty blade.

"Root stew, then. For cold nights. Turnip, carrot, one parsnip if you're feeling rich—" His voice wavered, steadied. "You cut them big. Big chunks. Small chunks dissolve and then you've made porridge and lied about it. Big chunks hold their shape, same as—"

Same as us, he didn't say. He closed his mouth around the words and kept them, the way he kept everything: close, warm, unspent.

* * *

Vane took her tea standing, which was a habit from the border wars, where sitting meant you'd stopped expecting to move. Across the table, in the waystation's common room, the cook sat with his own cup untouched and studied her the way a man studies weather coming over a ridge.

"You're Serah Vane," he said.

"I am."

"The Butcher of Kell's Ford."

"That was a battle," Vane said evenly, "and the name is a slander invented by men who lost it."

"Mm." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table, so the handle faced away from him, toward her. A courtesy, she realized. So she could take it more easily. Even now. Even chained. Something in her chest shifted, uncomfortable as a stone in a boot.

"And you're Bramble," she said. "Late of the garrison kitchens at Thornfell Keep. Discharged after the plague winter. Since then, associated with the smuggling crew operating out of the Salt Road passes. Associated with—" she let the pause do its work, "—Yara Sterling."

The old man's face did something remarkable. It did nothing at all. It became a kitchen wall, plastered smooth, bearing no nails.

"Don't know the name," he said.

"You fed her this morning."

"I feed anyone who sits at my fire. That's not association. That's hospitality."

"It's conspiracy, under the God-Protector's edicts. Feeding a god-touched criminal makes you an accessory to her crimes." Vane set down her tea. "But I'm not here for you, Bramble. I'm here because I'm offering you a way out of it."

She slid the ledger across the table. It lay open to a page near the middle, and she watched his eyes flick to it despite himself — watched him read the names upside down, quick as any scribe, because cooks learned to read a larder list at a glance. She saw him find his own name. She saw him find the others. Fen. Osk. And beneath them, in fresher ink, in her own hand: *Sterling, Yara — swordspeaker (presumed last).*

"They're dead men's names," Bramble said quietly.

"They're names I can strike through." Vane leaned forward. "Clemency. Full pardon, resettlement papers, a plot of land in the valley where the soil's good and nobody asks what you carried over the mountains. All of it, for one sentence. Where does Yara Sterling cross the pass?"

The silence stretched. Outside, wind worried the eaves of the waystation. Bramble looked at the ledger for a long moment, and then he looked up, and his eyes were wet — Vane noted it, catalogued it, filed it under levers — but his voice, when it came, was steady as a hearthstone.

"You know what I was doing when your soldiers took me?"

"Cooking."

"Making plum dumplings." He smiled, and it cracked something in his face, grief showing through like a seam. "For a boy who runs messages for us. He's fourteen. He thinks he's invisible because he's small. Nobody's ever told him he matters, so I tell him with food. Plum dumplings take two hours. You have to let the dough rest twice. There are no shortcuts. That's the whole secret of them — you can't hurry a thing into being worth eating, and you can't hurry a person into being worth loving either." He folded his hands on the table, chains and all. "I'm not going to give you her. And I'll tell you why, Inquisitor, since you seem like a woman who respects reasons. Because Yara Sterling would trade herself for any one of us without blinking, and if I hand her over, I make her sacrifice cheap. And I will not make that woman cheap. Not for a pardon. Not for a kingdom. Not for plum dumplings till the end of my days."

Vane sat back.

Forty-one interrogations. She had heard defiance before, and desperation, and the flat dead voice of men who had already left their bodies behind. This was none of those. This was a man building a wall out of love, brick by brick, right in front of her, and daring her to knock it down.

She almost admired it. Almost. Admiration was a luxury she had surrendered along with a great many other things, years ago, at Kell's Ford, kneeling in the mud among the dead and praying to gods who did not answer — until, at last, one did.

"You misunderstand me," she said softly. "I don't need you to give her up. I only needed to know what she's worth to you." She rose, gathered the ledger. "And now I know exactly how hard she'll fight for you. Which tells me precisely where she's going."

Bramble's brow furrowed. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means thank you for the tea conversation, cook. You've been more helpful than you know." At the door she paused, and gave him the truth as a kindness, because kindness disarmed better than cruelty ever had. "Think, Bramble. Think like her. Her power is dying — the god-touched always fade when the old gods sleep, and yours has been fading for years, hasn't it? Fewer prayers answered. Colder nights on the pass. And then, suddenly, the gods wake. Prayers get answered again. Miracles in the mountains." She watched understanding dawn in his face and hated herself, distantly, for enjoying it. "A fading swordspeaker doesn't run from that. She follows it. To the source. To whatever woke."

"She wouldn't—"

"She would. Because she's the kind of woman who trades herself for other people. You said so yourself." Vane smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "And there's only one place in these mountains where something old enough to wake the gods might sleep. The deep shrines. The sunken temples under Thornfell's roots. And there's only one road to them that a smuggler knows and my maps don't." She tapped the ledger against her palm. "The Hollow Bridge road. Through the gorge. She'll cross at Hollow Bridge within the week — because you were carrying supplies for a long journey, cook, and dried plums keep for a month, but fresh bread doesn't, and your sack had no bread in it. You bought your last loaf yesterday. You're provisioning for a final push, not a long haul."

Bramble stared at her. For the first time, the wall showed a crack — not of fear for himself, but of fear for them, and Vane saw it, and stepped through it.

"Take me to the cells," she said to the soldiers waiting outside. "Our guest will want time to consider clemency. People often do, once they understand how little they can change."

* * *

In the dark of the cell, Bramble counted his breathing the way he counted measures of flour, and when the counting failed, he cooked.

"Beef and ale pie," he whispered to the stone. "Hot crust, though most folk do it cold-lidded, and those folk are wrong. You brown the beef in batches — *batches*, don't crowd the pan, crowding steams the meat and steamed meat is a sin against the animal that died for it—"

His voice shook. He let it shake and kept talking.

"Onions again. There's always onions. Carrots cut on the bias because it looks generous. Ale — the dark stuff, not the swill the garrison used to serve, though saints forgive me, I served it anyway for eleven years and made it taste like something worth drinking, which I count among my life's true miracles—"

He thought of Fen's ink-stained fingers stealing scraps. Of Osk sitting apart but always within earshot, the way lonely soldiers do. Of Yara, who ate standing, who ate fast, who ate like someone who had never once been able to trust that the next meal was certain — and how, over three years, without ever saying a word about it, Bramble had taught her to sit down.

"Hold the shape," he said to the darkness, to the chains, to the whole indifferent machinery of the Crown. "Big chunks. Hold the shape."

* * *

By lamplight in the waystation's map room, Serah Vane drew a line across the gorge with a charcoal stub, and felt the old hunger stir in her chest — the god's hunger, patient and vast, coiled somewhere beneath the capital like a second heartbeat she had carried since Kell's Ford.

*Soon,* it seemed to say, through the ledger's weight in her hand, through the ache behind her eyes that was not entirely her own. *Bring me the swordspeaker. Bring me the king's road.*

Vane blew out the lamp and gave the order to march.

"At first light," she said. "We ride for Hollow Bridge."
