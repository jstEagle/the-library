## 10. Ambush at Hollow Bridge

Osk counted the bridge's stones the way he'd once counted exits in a war-tent: out of habit, out of dread, and because counting kept his hands from shaking.

Hollow Bridge earned its name honestly. It arched over a gorge where the river had long since abandoned its bed, leaving a dry throat of white stone that dropped two hundred feet into shadow. The span itself was old imperial work — pale granite, weathered to the color of bone, with arches underneath like rib bones of something enormous and long dead. Wind came up through those arches and made the whole structure hum, a low mournful note that Osk felt in his back teeth.

Twelve wagons wide enough for one. No cover on either approach. The kind of place Osk would have chosen for an ambush, if he were still the man who chose such places.

He was carrying the rear position, which meant he could watch all four of them without turning his head. Yara led, small and sharp-eyed, reading the far side of the bridge the way she read everything — as a problem with a price attached. Behind her, Fen walked with their nose in a sheaf of scriptorium notes, muttering, tripping on nothing. Bramble brought up the middle with the mule, complaining about the altitude, the damp, the moral character of goats. And beside the mule, wrapped in a borrowed cloak with the hood pulled low, walked King Edmund of Thornfell, moving like a man who had forgotten how his legs worked and was relearning them one stubborn step at a time.

*Temporary*, Osk reminded himself. This arrangement was temporary. These people were cargo he was escorting until he could disappear over the border with his back wages of guilt. He'd told Vane once that loyalty was just fear wearing better boots, and he'd believed it right up until Bramble started saving him the heel end of the loaf because Osk had mentioned, once, drunk, that it was his favorite.

The wind through the arches rose to a whine.

Osk stopped walking.

Twenty years of soldiering had built him an instrument out of instinct, and the instrument was playing every wrong note at once. Too quiet on the ridge to the east — no birds, no goats, no goat-herd children shouting. Too much dust hanging in the air above the pine slope where no wind should have stirred any. And there, at the far end of the bridge, a wagon stood parked with its ox cut loose and gone, which no honest driver did unless the honest driver wanted the road clear for something else.

"Yara." He didn't raise his voice. You learned in the king's army that a shout spent information you couldn't buy back. "Ambush. Far side. Ridge too."

She didn't question it. That was the thing about Yara that had unsettled him most when they'd met, and the thing he trusted most now: she spent zero seconds deciding whether to believe him. "Fen. Notes away. Bramble—" 

"I know," Bramble rumbled, already hauling the mule around. "Back to the rocks, everyone moves like they've got sense, which means Fen runs first."

"I *run* fine," Fen said, but they were already running.

Osk looked back once, down the trail they'd climbed that morning. And there it was, confirming everything: riders cresting the saddle behind them, maybe thirty, moving in disciplined wedges. Royal blue and gray. Sunlight flashed off helmet rims.

Crown soldiers. Crown soldiers *behind* them, crown soldiers *ahead* of them, and between the two, a bridge that sang.

"They boxed us," Yara said. She'd gone very still, and Osk saw her doing the arithmetic that he was doing, and arriving at the same ugly sum. "How?"

"The ledger," Osk said quietly. "She doesn't need us to tell her the route. She has the routes. All of them." He had known Serah Vane for eleven years. He had watched her memorize smuggling ledgers the way priests memorized psalms. "There was never a clean road. There was only the road she hadn't gotten to yet."

Edmund had stopped in the middle of the trail, hood blown back, and he was looking at the bridge with an expression Osk couldn't read — or could, and wished he couldn't. It was recognition. Something in the king knew this place, or something in the thing wearing the king knew it.

"There's the gorge," Yara said, half to herself. "Or we give them the king and run."

"We're not giving anyone anything," Bramble said, mild as morning bread, and Osk felt the words land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

* * *

They made the bridge because there was nowhere else to make for, and because Yara made the decision fast and ugly and correct: caught in the open was dead, caught on a narrow span was only probably dead. Fen went first. Then Bramble with the mule, then Edmund, and Osk took the rear again because some habits were load-bearing.

Halfway across, the soldiers reached the near end.

Not the wedge charge Osk expected. Instead a single rider came on alone, unhurried, helmet doffed and strapped to her saddle, red-brown hair pinned back with military severity. She rode onto the bridge proper as if she owned it, which, in every way that mattered, she did.

Serah Vane drew rein ten paces off. Her soldiers fanned along the near approach and knelt among the rocks with crossbows, patient as masonry. On the ridge opposite, more of them — he counted shields, not faces; faces were hope and he was rationing it.

"Captain Oskar Vell," Vane said. Not shouted. Conversational, pitched to carry across the singing stone. "Eleven years I carried your name in my company roll and never once crossed it out."

"Serah." His mouth was dry as the gorge below. "You look well. Promotion agrees with you."

"It does." She swung down from the saddle and walked forward, and her boots on the old granite made almost no sound against the wind-hum, and that frightened him more than the crossbows. Vane should have sounded loud here. Everything sounded loud here. "You want to know how I found you."

"You'll tell me regardless. You always liked the confession part best."

Something moved through her face — not quite a smile, not quite a flinch. "Ledger work," she said. "And a cook who buys flour in towns where cooks shouldn't be buying flour."

Bramble, ten paces behind Osk, made a small defeated sound.

"But that's not why I'm talking instead of shooting," Vane went on. She stopped, hands loose at her sides, and looked past Osk entirely — to Edmund, hoodless in the wind, and Osk watched her take in the gauntness, the flicker behind the eyes, and file it away in whatever ledger she kept behind her own. "His Majesty's household is worried about him. There are men who'd cross this bridge gently and bring him home to his physicians. I'd prefer that. My alternative is less gentle, and I don't enjoy it as much as people think."

"He's not going back with you," Osk said.

"No," Vane agreed. "I didn't think so. Captain—" and now her voice changed, dropped into an older register, the voice of the tent and the campaign map and the hour after midnight when decisions got made by exhausted people with knives — "step aside. That's the whole offer. Step aside, take your crew, walk south. Your names come out of my book. I have no quarrel with smugglers today. I have a king to recover."

And there it was. The exit. Clean, bought cheap, exactly what a sensible man took.

Osk thought about the heel of the loaf. He thought about Fen asleep against his shoulder on watch, trusting him in the specific unguarded way that Fen trusted nobody else and had apparently decided to waste on him. He thought about eleven years of telling himself loyalty was fear in good boots while these ridiculous people rebuilt the word in front of him out of flour purchases and bad poetry.

He stepped left. Deliberately. Putting his body between Vane and Edmund, on a bridge twelve wagons wide with crossbows on three sides.

"Sergeant Vell," Osk said. "Resigning."

Vane closed her eyes for one heartbeat. When she opened them, the warmth was gone, folded away like a camp stove. "Then you remember how this goes."

"I remember everything," Osk said. "That was always your problem with me."

* * *

The fight, when it came, came from three directions at once, and Osk's body did what twenty years had trained it to do while his mind stood apart and grieved.

Crossbows first. He heard Yara shout and saw her drag Edmund down behind the bridge's low parapet as bolts hissed overhead; one struck the granite and split, and the singing note of the bridge stuttered like a plucked string touched by a wet finger. Bramble — enormous, impossible Bramble — simply picked the mule up bodily by the halter and hauled it flat against the stone, cursing with tremendous fluency. Fen pressed into the parapet beside Yara, papers scattering, and Osk saw one page lift and whirl out over the gorge and Fen watch it go with an expression of pure heartbreak even as arrows filled the air.

Then the vanguard came across, six shields in a row, and Osk went to meet them because the bridge was narrow and narrow favored one man who knew exactly what he was.

He'd been the best blade in the Third Royal before Vane. He'd taught half the men coming across that bridge everything they knew, and that was the true horror of it — he knew their drills like scripture, knew the little hitch before a royal shieldman thrust, knew the exact count of the rotation that left a flank open for two heartbeats. He fought them at the bridge's throat and gave ground by inches, by design, buying time the way he'd once bought it for retreats he'd been ordered to cover, except nobody had ordered this and nobody would thank him and he had never in his life fought for something that mattered more.

"Osk!" Yara's voice, raw. "We can't hold the far end—"

"Don't need to hold it!" he called back, turning a thrust that would have opened Fen like a letter. "Need to hold *you*! Get him to the far side and off the road!"

Somewhere behind him, Edmund screamed.

It wasn't a human sound. Osk had heard men scream in burning hospitals and this wasn't that. It came out doubled, harmonized with itself, one voice of pain and another voice underneath it that was vast and cold and *wrong*, and every hair on Osk's arms stood up, and worse — worse — several of the soldiers attacking him faltered, crossing themselves with an old gesture none of them had used since the gods went to sleep.

Because Edmund was bleeding. A stray bolt had found him above the knee, and the blood on the pale stone was steaming in the mountain air, and the wind through the arches had stopped singing and started *moaning*.

What happened next, Osk would spend the rest of his life failing to describe properly.

Yara went to Edmund. That part was simple, human, ordinary — Yara kneeling in blood, pressing her hands to the wound, swearing at him with real tenderness. But then the light came. It came up out of her like something surfacing from deep water, a white fire along every scar on her arms, blazing from her eyes, and the air itself bent toward her the way iron bends toward a lodestone. The bridge's song became a bell-toll. Every soldier on the span froze — not from command, not from fear, but the way animals freeze under a hawk's shadow.

And Yara's voice, when it came out of her, was not entirely hers.

"*You want him,*" she said — and the words rang off the stone like struck metal, doubled, harmonized, the same doubling that had been in Edmund's scream. "*Come and take him. Oh — I see it now. You want me.*"

The pressure that followed had no light, no shape. It was a vast attention turning upon them all, the way a starving animal turns its head. Osk felt it pass over him like cold water and fix, with horrible specificity, on the blazing figure kneeling in the king's blood. The god beneath the mountain had worn a hundred kings, he understood in that moment, the way a man wears out boots — and it had just discovered that Yara Sterling fit it better than any of them.

Yara's power guttered. She swayed, caught herself on the parapet, and Osk saw her face and understood that whatever had just happened had cost her something she didn't have to spare.

But the soldiers froze longer than they should have. Long enough.

"GO!" Bramble's roar, from somewhere behind. "Girl, take his majesty and GO!"

Osk turned back to his opponents and they came on again, and this time he made mistakes, because he was tired and because he kept looking back.

He saw the bolt leave the crossbow on the ridge. He saw its arc, and he saw — with the terrible clarity of the slow moment — that it was not aimed at him, or at Yara, but at Fen, who had broken cover to reach Edmund's other side, who was the worst runner in the mountains and the bravest person Osk had ever known.

There was no thought in it. Later he would construct reasons, tactics, the arithmetic of protecting the party's scribe. But in the moment there was only his body deciding, finally and permanently, what it had spent twenty years refusing to know.

He moved. He took the bolt high in the back, below the left shoulder, and a second one followed it into the meat of his thigh, and he went down onto the singing stone with the taste of copper flooding his mouth.

Fen's face above him, white as the granite. "Osk. *Osk.*"

"Hurts," Osk admitted. The bridge hummed under his cheek like a cat. "Don't tell Bramble. He'll say I told you the heel was his favorite."

"Shut up. Shut up, we're getting you up—"

Hands on him. Bramble's hands, enormous and gentle, lifting him like a child. Yara shouting something about the gorge path, the far side, movement everywhere. And above it all, faintly, receding, Serah Vane's voice giving one order in a tone he had never heard her use in eleven years:

"*Hold! Hold, all of you — let them go.*"

Which made no sense at all, and saved his life, and he was too busy bleeding to wonder why.

* * *

He surfaced from unconsciousness in fits, and each time, the world had rearranged itself around the same fixed point: Yara sitting apart from the fire, awake, watching her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger.

The gorge path down from the bridge had nearly killed them all, but Bramble had carried him the whole way, refusing help, and somehow there was now a fire, and Fen stitching his thigh with the neat careful hands of someone who'd copied medical texts for money, and Edmund lying pale and quiet under shared blankets with his wound dressed, sleeping — actually sleeping, for the first time since Osk had met him.

Vane had let them go. That fact sat in Osk's chest like an undigested meal. He'd tried to work it through with Fen during a lucid stretch: "*Hold* isn't mercy. Hold is an order you give when the mission changed. Something happened on that bridge that mattered more than the king."

"And what matters more than a king?" Fen asked.

Osk looked across the fire at Yara, watching her hands.

"That," he said.

Now, in the deepest dark, he woke again — pain like a banked furnace, the bolt head still in his back, Fen asleep sitting up against his bedroll within arm's reach, having clearly refused to move. And he heard voices, low, from the edge of the firelight. Yara and Edmund, the king propped on one elbow, both of them lit faintly from within, as if the day's violence had left embers under their skin.

"—it looked at me," Yara was saying. "Through me. It's not feeding on him, Edmund. Or not only. It's *shopping*."

Silence. Then Edmund, hoarse: "Then we have less time than I thought."

"We have tonight," Yara said. "Maybe tomorrow. Whatever comes next, it comes fast now."

Osk lay still among his sleeping family, a former royal blade with two bolts in him and no rank, no order, and no way to resign from the only post he'd ever held by choice.

Whatever was hunting them had just changed its quarry. And the road ahead ran straight through the dark toward the one place in Thornfell where it was strongest.

He closed his eyes, and did not sleep.
