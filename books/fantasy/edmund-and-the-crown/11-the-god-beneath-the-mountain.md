## 11. The God Beneath the Mountain

Edmund had learned to measure time by the flickering.

There was the long dark, when he was only himself, alone in the vault of his own skull while his body walked and spoke and signed decrees without him. And there were the flickers — brief mercies when the thing wearing him let him surface, gasping, into daylight — a servant's frightened face, the smell of beeswax in the chapel, rain on the palace glass. Each one shorter than the last.

Tonight there was no flickering at all. Tonight the god wanted to talk.

He knew it was coming the way you know a storm before it breaks: a pressure behind his eyes, a sweetness in his mouth like overripe plums. His body sat in the private oratory beneath the Golden Hall, where no one was permitted to enter without summons, and his hands lay folded on his knees in an attitude of prayer that he had not chosen. Candlelight pooled around him. The crown sat on his brow like a ring of cold fingers.

*You are fighting me again,* said the voice, which had no direction because it came from everywhere inside him at once. It sounded, as always, almost kind. Almost like his father.

*I am always fighting you.*

*Yes.* A pause that might have been fondness. *It is what I admire most about you.*

*Don't flatter me. Just take what you're going to take.*

*But Edmund.* The god's attention shifted, and he felt it move through him like cold water through cloth. *Tonight I am not taking. Tonight I am giving.*

The candle flames bent sideways, all eleven of them at once, as if a door had opened somewhere in the world.

*Look.*

---

The vision took him the way drowning takes a swimmer: gently at first, then all at once.

He stood in the capital square at midsummer, but a midsummer he did not recognize. The plague-years' empty stalls were full again — overflowing, absurd with plenty. Grain piled in golden cones. Fish silvered the air as hawkers tossed them laughing to customers. He saw children running between the barrels with orange peels for crowns, and none of them coughed. None of them were thin. An old woman sold honey cakes from a hand-cart and called out prices in a voice cracked with good humor, and Edmund's chest ached so suddenly and so fiercely that he understood, distantly, that this was the cruelest weapon the god had ever shown him: ordinary joy.

*This is your kingdom,* the voice said, warm against the inside of his ribs. *As it will be.*

*It's a lie. You're showing me a painting.*

*I am showing you a promise. I have never once lied to you, Edmund. Search yourself. You know it's true.*

And the terrible thing — the thing he would not tell Yara Sterling when she came, if she came — was that it hadn't lied. Not once. It twisted. It omitted. It priced its gifts in coin made of his own substance. But every word of it had been true, and truth, Edmund had learned, was far harder to resist than any serpent's temptation. Lies you could spit out. Truth went down like water when you were burning.

The vision shifted. Now he stood in the plague-village of Harrow's End, which he had ridden through seven years ago with his face wrapped in linen while the dead were carted out by the few living too weak to grieve. In the vision it lived. Smoke rose from repaired chimneys. A wedding feast spilled out of the tavern into the street — two young farmers, faces shining, dancing on packed earth while fiddles scratched somewhere out of sight. On the village green stood a new well, and beside the well a small shrine, and before the shrine an old man knelt in clean wool, praying with his hat off, and as Edmund watched, the old man lifted his face toward the sky and smiled as though someone had just spoken his name.

*They pray,* the god said softly. *Do you hear them? For a hundred years their prayers fell on sleeping ears. I heard every one. I remember every one. I kept them, Edmund, like letters. Like love-letters. And now I answer.*

*You keep them in your belly,* Edmund said. *I've felt them in there.*

The vision shuddered — just slightly, just at the edges, like a reflection in disturbed water. Then it steadied, and the warmth returned, and Edmund knew he had struck something true and should have the wit to stop striking it.

*Come and see the mountains,* the god said.

---

Thornfell rose around him, but not the Thornfell he knew. No smugglers' paths stitched its flanks; no hermit huts, no shrines to the sleeping gods with their offerings of salt and wildflowers gone mouldy in the rain. This Thornfell was green to the summit and humming, and the mountain itself — he could feel this even through the vision's borrowed eyes, could feel it in his teeth — was awake.

*You want to know what I am,* the god said. *Very well. You have earned it. Most anchors never ask.*

The green peeled away like a page turning, and then there was nothing but dark, and depth, and cold stone pressed down upon something vast.

Edmund hung in the black beneath the roots of the mountain and felt the shape of the prisoner.

It did not look like anything. Looking at it was like looking at the space where a tooth had been — his mind kept trying to fill in the absence and kept failing. But he felt its edges, and they were worn smooth, and they were pressed against the inside of the rock the way hands press against the inside of a coffin lid, and they had been pressing for a very, very long time.

*They buried me here before your people had names,* the god said, and its voice in this place was different — vaster, echoing up through strata of stone, thin with distance and hunger. *The old ones. Your sleeping gods' elder siblings. They called me the Hunger Under the World, and they were right to fear me, and they were wrong about everything else. I do not devour, Edmund. I *answer*. That is all I have ever done. Every prayer is a door, and I am the thing on the other side of every door, and I starve when the doors are shut.*

*A century ago your priests sang your little gods to sleep — did you know that? Sang them down into the deep quiet so that men would need no intermediaries, so that prayer would become poetry and nothing more. Noble. Beautiful. And I slept too, or near enough. Until the plague came, and your wife prayed, and your daughters prayed, and ten thousand mothers prayed, and the doors flew open, and I woke with my mouth already full of grief I could finally touch.*

The cold of the deep place seeped into the vision and became, horribly, a kind of comfort. Edmund hung there in the under-mountain dark and felt the prisoner's loneliness as physical fact, a weight on his sternum. He hated that it was lonely. He hated himself for noticing.

*So you climbed up through their prayers,* he said. *Into my house.*

*Your queen opened the window. I only came through. She asked me to save her girls, Edmund. Can you blame me for answering? Can you blame yourself?*

He could not. That was the trap of it, the hinge on which the whole machinery turned. He had tried for years to locate the moment of damnation — his fault, Maren's fault, the priests', the plague's — and the search always came back empty-handed, because there had been no villain, only a dying woman whispering into the dark *please, please, please*, and something in the dark that said *yes*.

*What do you want from me?* he asked, and heard how tired his voice was. How nearly surrendered.

*Only what I have always wanted. To be answered.*

---

The visions came faster now, and sweeter, and Edmund knew — some small cold clerk of a self deep in him knew — that this was the seduction's crescendo, that he was being carried down the aisle of his own ruin with flowers thrown before him.

He saw the border wars end, not by treaty but by transformation: the northern clans trading timber and songs across a line that had stopped mattering. He saw the crown's zealots disbanded, their ledgers burned in a cheerful bonfire, Serah Vane herself — and wasn't that a strange mercy, to see her unclench — planting an herb garden where the interrogation cells had been. He saw roads built, schools, granaries full against the bad winters. He saw his kingdom healed the way a body heals: slowly, cell by cell, from the inside out.

And he saw himself, in these visions, standing whole before his people. Lucid. Kind. Present in his own body every hour of every day, because —

*— because you stop fighting me,* the god said, and now the voice came from his own throat, gentle, reasonable, his father's voice, his dead wife's voice, layered like rings in a tree. *That is all, beloved anchor. Stop fighting. Let me flow through the chain instead of against it, and neither of us need hurt the other. You feel how it grinds you now because you brace against me. Unbrace. A king is a conduit whether he consents or not — I have worn forty-two of you, and the ones who suffered were the ones who struggled. Be the open window. Let the prayers pass through you and come out the other side answered, and keep — this —*

The vision gave him one last thing, and it was not a kingdom, and it was not glory.

It was an evening in his wife's garden, seven years ago, before the sickness. Maren with dirt on her hands and her hair coming loose, laughing at something he'd said, the plum tree in blossom, his daughters' voices from the orchard wall. Ordinary. Gone. Utterly, permanently gone — except that here, in the god's keeping, it was not gone. Here it still existed, preserved like a fly in amber, warm and breathing and endless, and all he had to do to live inside it forever was stop.

Stop fighting. Stop bracing. Stop being Edmund, in the only sense of Edmund that had ever mattered, and become a well-worn channel through which something starving poured its answers into the world.

His hands unfolded from their borrowed attitude of prayer. His head bowed. The crown's cold fingers tightened on his brow — tenderly, he thought, tenderly, like a lover adjusting a wreath.

*Maren,* he said, into the blossoming vision. Her laugh. The dirt on her knuckles.

*Yes,* said the god. *Yes. All of it. Forever. Only say yes, Edmund. Only stop.*

He felt himself beginning to unbrace. It felt like lying down after a year of standing. It felt like forgiveness. Somewhere below his heart, along the length of the anchor-chain that ran from his spine down through the world's crust into the coffin-dark under Thornfell, something immense and patient and terribly hungry began — gently, gratefully — to pull.

And in the vision, in the garden, Maren set down her trowel and looked up at him and said — not laughing now, saying it plainly, the way she had said everything — *"Edmund, whatever it's showing you, it isn't me. You know that."*

Because she would have known. Because even dead seven years and offered back to him whole, she would never once have let him mistake a cage for a home, no matter how sweet the blossoms.

The vision broke.

---

He came back to himself in the oratory, on his knees — truly kneeling now, his own knees, shaking — with candle wax pooling on the flagstones and the eleven flames standing perfectly straight again. His face was wet. His mouth tasted of plums.

For one heartbeat, one flicker, he held himself entire: Edmund, son of Aldric, forty-third of his name's line, widower, father of two small graves, keeper of a secret that was eating him alive.

*Think on it,* the god said from everywhere and nowhere, unhurried, unoffended, certain as sunrise. *You always come back to the same crossroads, my anchor, and every night the road grows wider. But tonight — tonight I will tell you something useful instead. Something you can hold.*

A pause. The candles leaned, very slightly, eastward. Toward the mountains.

*Your swordspeaker lives,* it said. *Yara Sterling, last of a broken order, and she has found the rites, or is finding them. She means to unbind us. And here is the useful thing, Edmund: she cannot succeed unless you sit the throne willingly during the renewal rite. Unless you choose her. Unless you choose the struggle, one final time, at the exact hour when I will be closest and strongest and able to give you everything.*

*So rest tonight, beloved. Fight if it comforts you — I know you find it meaningful. But understand what you truly are deciding between. Me, and an ending. The healed kingdom, and a woman with a fading sword who will spend your life like a copper coin and call it salvation.*

*She is coming for you, Edmund. I can hear her boots on my mountain.*

*Decide what you will say when she arrives.*

The candles guttered out, all eleven, and in the dark Edmund stayed kneeling on the cold floor of the house of his own betrayal, and laughed — low, cracked, human, the sound Yara Sterling had once told him was the first honest thing she'd heard from him — until the sound broke apart into something that was not laughter at all.

Then he got up, feeling along the wall in the blackness like any blind mortal man, and began, hand over hand along the anchor-chain of his own soul, to brace.

Outside his locked door, far below the palace, in the city, in the plains, in the green flanks of Thornfell, ten thousand sleepers turned over in their beds and murmured prayers into their pillows — and somewhere beneath the mountain, something vast leaned close to every open door, and listened, and grew, and waited for the dawn of coronation eve.
