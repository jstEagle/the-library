## 12. Confessions by Firelight

The cave Yara chose was old smuggler's work — a hollow behind the waterfall where the Thornfell's southern face wept itself into a pool, the kind of place that didn't appear on any map worth trusting. She'd used it twice in ten years to wait out patrols, and both times she had been alone.

Now there were four bedrolls outside under Fen's watch, and inside, by a fire that smoked because the wood was wet and neither of them cared enough to fix it, sat a king with no crown, eating stew out of a dented tin bowl like it was the finest thing he'd ever been handed.

He ate slowly. That was what struck her first, watching him from her side of the flames. Not the gauntness — she'd seen gaunt before, on plague roads and prison barges. It was the slowness. Edmund ate as though he had forgotten food could taste like anything, and every spoonful was a small act of relearning.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"Ah." He set the bowl down on the stone between them. "And what does the assessment say?"

"That you'll live long enough to be useful." She fed another branch to the fire. It hissed, spat, caught. "That's all I need from you."

"Liar," he said mildly.

Yara's hand went to her knife out of pure reflex, and Edmund watched her do it — watched, and did not flinch, and something about the not-flinching made her want to hit him or laugh or possibly both.

"You don't get to call me that," she said. "You've known me four days."

"I've known you since Hollow Bridge," he said. "You put your body between me and a crossbow bolt without deciding to. People lie with their words, Mistress Sterling. They almost never manage it with their feet."

* * *

The fire burned down to coals while the waterfall kept its white noise against the world. Outside, faintly, she could hear Bramble's snore — a sound like a millstone rolling downhill — and Osk's low voice murmuring the first watch to Fen. Her crew. Her people, camped thirty paces from a king whose head housed a god that ate oaths, guarding him because she had asked them to and they hadn't even asked why.

Edmund had drawn his knees up, arms wrapped around them, and for a moment he looked less like a king than like a boy sitting out a storm in a barn.

"The visions started eight months ago," he said, to the fire rather than to her. "Before the crown. Before any of it. You should know that part first, because everyone assumes the god came to me, and it's cleaner if you understand that I went looking."

Yara said nothing. In her experience, silence pulled truth out of people faster than questions ever had. She'd once gotten a customs officer to confess to three years of bribery just by refilling his cup and waiting.

"The plague," Edmund said. "They called it the Grey Sleep. It took the lowlands in one season and the capital in the next. My son first — Aldric, he was nine. He held my hand for two days and then he didn't wake up on the third. My daughter lasted a week longer. Elenna. She was six, and she was furious about being sick, absolutely furious, told the physicians they were doing it wrong." His mouth did something that wanted to be a smile. "She was right, too. They were."

The coals shifted. Somewhere above them, water struck stone with a patience that felt ancient.

"My wife followed them within the month. Not the plague. Just—" He gestured vaguely at his own chest. "People say grief is like drowning. It isn't. Drowning ends. And I sat on a throne in an empty palace with a hundred petitioners a day telling me the kingdom needed me, needed strength, needed a king who could feel the gods' favor again, because the old gods slept and everyone knew a sleeping god's blessing thins." He looked up at her then, and his eyes in the firelight were the color of wet slate. "So I prayed. Not to the sleeping ones. To whatever was left. Whatever was listening down in the dark places. I got down on the floor of the crypt beneath the Golden Hall, under the bones of forty kings, and I said: *anything. Take anything. Give me back the power to protect what remains.*"

"And something answered," Yara said quietly.

"Something answered." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "Do you know what it asked for? Not blood. Not worship. It asked for an anchor. A chain. It said it had been bound too long and its chains had grown old, and it needed a living link — a line of kings, crowned and willing, to hold it to the world. And I thought — gods, I actually thought — *how generous.* All it wants is to be held. All it wants is a covenant. Like the old stories." His hands had gone white around his own wrists. "I let it into the crown at my renewal rite. I spoke the binding myself. I chained my house to it forever, and it has been drinking me through the metal ever since."

"It showed you the kingdom healed," Yara said. "Whole. Green. Your children alive."

Edmund went very still.

"How do you know that?"

"Because that's how it hunts." She turned her knife over in her fingers, not threateningly — just to have something to do with her hands. "My order kept records, before the purging. The thing under Thornfell doesn't take by force. Force leaves scars, and scars make people fight. It gives you exactly what you most want, so precisely, so kindly, that when you finally notice what it's taking in return, you've already paid half your life and you defend it, because admitting the truth would mean admitting you sold your children's memory for a leash." She met his eyes. "It's still showing you Aldric and Elenna, isn't it? Every night. Alive. Older. Growing."

"They had a dog," Edmund whispered. "In the vision. We never had a dog. They always wanted one." His voice cracked clean through. "I know it's false. I *know*. And I would burn this whole kingdom down for five more minutes of it."

There it was. The honest, ugly, human center of him, laid out on the stone between them like an organ on a surgeon's table. Yara had spent ten years reading men across smuggling tables — their greed, their fear, their tells — and she knew with total certainty that Edmund was not lying to her, which meant he was more dangerous than anyone she'd ever dealt with, because he meant every word.

"You won't," she said. "Burn it down. You'll sit through the renewal rite and let it finish eating you, because you think your death will break the chain and spare everyone else. That's why you begged me for a blade on the pass."

"Yes." No flinch. No deflection. "And you refused."

"I refuse now."

"Why?" The word came out sharp, almost angry. "Give me one reason, Sterling. One reason that isn't sentiment. If I die before the rite, the binding fails, the thing starves in the dark, and the only cost is one man who is already half-devoured. You're a pragmatist. I've watched you abandon cargo, burn a bridge, leave a mule to the wolves. So tell me why my death is off the table."

Yara opened her mouth. Closed it. Because the true answer was ugly and she had promised herself — years ago, alone in the snow with her order's ashes cooling behind her — that she would never lie to herself again, and lying to him would be a kind of lying to herself.

"Because it doesn't work," she said. "Your death doesn't starve it. It *feeds* it."

* * *

He stared at her. The waterfall filled the silence.

"A king who dies willingly, in faith, bound by oath to the entity — that's not a broken chain, Edmund. That's a consecration. My order called it the Crowned Sacrifice. It's the oldest rite in the archives, older than the monarchy, and it's the one outcome the thing beneath Thornfell has wanted since the day it was imprisoned. A willing royal death doesn't end the binding. It *completes* it. It makes the crown holy. It makes every future king a priest of the devourer, crowned in martyrdom, worshipped by a grieving nation." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees. "You wouldn't be escaping it. You'd be giving it everything it's dreamed of for a thousand years, gift-wrapped in your grief. It's been nudging you toward that cliff since the day it moved in. Why do you think the death-urge feels so noble?"

Edmund's face went through several weathers — disbelief, horror, and then a terrible, settling clarity, like a man watching the last exit road slide past the window.

"It told me the rite would free the kingdom," he said hoarsely.

"It tells you what keeps you walking forward. That's all it has ever done. That's all it *can* do. It's a fisherman, and hope is the only bait it owns." She sat back. "Which is why we're not killing you. We're unbinding you. There are rites for it. Lost ones. My order died keeping them lost from everyone else, and I am going to dig them out of the one place they still exist, and we are going to pry that thing off your soul like a tick off a dog."

For a long moment he just breathed. Then he laughed — short, cracked, real, the first honest sound she'd heard from him since the pass — and dragged a hand down his face.

"A tick off a dog," he repeated. "Kings have gone to war over less flattering metaphors."

"Kings have gone to war over worse reasons and won nothing but graves. Sit still and be a dog."

He smiled. Actually smiled, tired and crooked, and Yara felt something shift in her chest that she chose, deliberately, not to examine.

* * *

The fire needed feeding again. She did it, and when she sat back down he was watching her with that slate-eyed attention that made her feel like a document being read.

"Now you," he said.

"Now me what?"

"You've taken my confession. Fair trade says you owe yours. You know what I am. Tell me what you are." He nodded at her hands. "At Hollow Bridge, when I bled near you, the air *sang*. Fen wrote in her journal — yes, I read it, I was bored and she sleeps with it under her arm — that your shadow stood up straight when your body was falling. That's swordspeech. That's the old oath-magic. But the order of swordspeakers was purged twenty years ago. Purged to the last sworn blade." His voice gentled. "Except one, apparently. So. What are you, Yara Sterling?"

And here was the moment where she could deflect. She was good at it; she'd built a life out of it. Ten years of wry banter and changed subjects and *I work alone*, and every word of it armor over the same soft wound.

But he had just handed her his children's names and the shape of his damnation, and asking for lies back would make her smaller than she could bear.

"I was sixteen," she said, "when the crown's soldiers came to the Chapterhouse at Vaelmoor."

The words came easier than she expected, once started. Like a knot cut rather than untied.

"There were ninety-three of us. Swordspeakers. We swore our oaths to the old gods at dawn on the solstice — blood, breath, and blade, all three given. In exchange the gods spoke through us. Through our swords, really. A swordspeaker doesn't command magic. She *listens*, and the sword listens through her, and between the two of you things happen that shouldn't. Steel remembers. Stone answers. Air decides which way to fall." She turned her palm up, studied the calluses. "The old gods slept anyway — that was already happening, the slow fading, the prayers going unanswered. Our oaths ran on memory more than presence. Borrowed light from a bank that was closing. But it worked. Barely. Until the new court decided that any power not flowing through the crown was treason."

"The purging," Edmund said quietly. "Under my father."

"Under your father. Ninety-two swordspeakers died in one winter. I survived because I was on supply run to the salt cells when the soldiers came, and because my master — Sera Ilva, she'd taught me since I was seven — she'd had a dream, and dreams were rare enough by then that she trusted hers. She sent me away with the ledgers. The rites. Everything the order knew, written in cipher, sewn into the lining of a grain sack." Yara's jaw tightened. "I came back to ash. Everyone I loved, dead in the snow, and a bounty notice with my name on it, and twenty years of running since. Smuggling. Keeping my head down. Telling myself the oath was just habit, that the fading power didn't matter, that I worked alone because alone was safe."

"And is it?"

"No." The word came out flat and final. "Alone is just lonely with better logistics."

He huffed — almost a laugh — and she found, to her irritation, that she was smiling too.

"But here's the part that matters," she said, and now she made herself look at him straight, because the rest deserved eye contact. "Since I found you — since the pass, since the bridge — my power has been waking up. Stronger every day. Twenty years of embers, and suddenly flame. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the god feeds on oaths," Edmund said slowly. "And you're full of one."

"I'm a lit candle walking next to a bonfire. It can taste me, Edmund. Every hour I spend near you, it's learning the shape of my oath, mapping it, figuring out how to drink it. And here's the thing I figured out in the scriptorium, reading the unbinding rite by candlelight while Fen argued with a blind archivist about the price of memories." She paused. This was the hard part. This was the part she'd hidden from the crew, and hiding it from him felt like pressing on a bruise. "The rite works. It will pull the entity out of you and out of the crown. But a binding that deep — a thousand years of chain, anchored in willing royal blood — can't be cut from outside. It has to be *outweighed*. The rite requires a counterweight. An oath of equal weight, freely offered, and then deliberately broken in the entity's presence. The breaking is the blade. The shatter of a great oath tears the channel open wide enough to sever the crown's binding."

Edmund had gone very still. "What oath weighs as much as a dynasty?"

"One sworn in blood, breath, and blade to the old gods themselves." She spread her hands. "Mine. I break my oath, the channel opens, the binding severs — and the oath was the only thing keeping my power alive. The magic goes with it. All of it. Permanently. When this is done, I'm not a swordspeaker anymore. I'm not anything. Just a woman with good knives and excellent smuggling contacts."

The fire popped. Down by the pool, an owl called once and got no answer.

Edmund didn't speak for a long time. When he did, his voice was rough.

"You've known this since the scriptorium. Days. And you said nothing."

"Saying it makes it real."

"And instead you've been—" He gestured at her, at the camp beyond the falls, at all of it. "Planning routes. Feeding us. Teaching Fen cipher. Walking around with your own execution date in your pocket and cracking jokes about my eating habits."

"Somebody has to keep morale up. Bramble tries, but his jokes are mostly about turnips."

"*Yara.*"

"What?" She heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "What do you want me to say? That I'm frightened? Fine. I'm frightened. I've been frightened since I was sixteen and it turns out fear is not actually fatal, I've checked extensively. What I won't do is stand in front of you and wring my hands and ask permission to save a man's life at the cost of mine, because I already know how you'll answer, and the answer wastes time we don't have. The renewal rite is eleven days away. Vane is hunting us. The god is getting stronger in you by the hour. There is no version of this where someone else pays my bill. There is no one else *to* pay it. So the bill gets paid, and the only choice I get is whether I spend the eleven days I have left being a swordspeaker miserable, or being a swordspeaker *useful*." She jabbed a finger at him. "I choose useful. Now eat your stew before it congeals."

* * *

He didn't eat the stew. He set the bowl aside and looked at her across the coals with an expression she couldn't immediately catalogue — and then, disturbingly, could.

Pity was easy to catalog. Pity she could hate and dismiss. This wasn't pity. This was recognition. This was a man looking at his own stubbornness wearing a different face.

"You understand," he said, "that I am going to try to stop you."

"Of course."

"And that you're going to refuse."

"Naturally."

"And that neither of us will yield, and we will spend eleven days fighting about it in between planning the infiltration, and at the end of it, one of us is going to lose something irreplaceable." He shook his head slowly. "This is the strangest friendship of my life, and I once befriended a diplomat who turned out to be three spies in a coat."

Despite everything — despite the coals burning low and the rite's arithmetic sitting in her chest like swallowed glass — Yara laughed. Really laughed, short and helpless, and saw him store the sound somewhere careful.

"For what it's worth," Edmund said, quieter now, "I'm glad it's you. If the chain must be broken — if someone must pay — I'm glad the gods sent the one person in this kingdom who refuses to lie to me. Even when the truth is a knife. *Especially* then." He hesitated, and added, almost shyly, "Aldric would have liked you. He liked people who were bossy and correct."

Yara's throat tightened unexpectedly. "Elenna would have liked Bramble. She sounds like she appreciated a man committed to turnips."

"She would have adored him." Edmund smiled at the fire, and this time the grief in it had edges of warmth. "Thank you. For the names. Most people flinch away from them."

"I don't flinch."

"No," he agreed softly. "You really don't."

They sat together while the fire died to red eyes in the dark, and neither of them asked the other to stop, and neither of them lied, and somewhere in the space between those two disciplines — in the strange, austere honesty of two people showing each other their worst wounds without bandages — something took root that neither of them named, because naming it would have required a future, and futures were exactly what they'd both stopped believing in.

Outside, past the waterfall, Fen's watch-call drifted down — two notes, low, the signal for movement on the trails.

Then a third note. The urgent one.

Yara was on her feet with a knife in her hand before the sound finished, Edmund rising behind her, and through the curtain of falling water she could see Fen sprinting down from the ridge with Osk limping after, and in Fen's raised hand, catching the moonlight, a scrap of paper bearing the seal of the crown's inquisition.

They had found Serah Vane's advance camp.

Three miles away. And moving.
