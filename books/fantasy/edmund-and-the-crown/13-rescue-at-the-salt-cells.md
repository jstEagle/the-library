## 13. Rescue at the Salt Cells

**FEN**

The salt cells smelled of brine and old iron, and Fen hated every step of the way down.

He had written out the lock mechanisms three times on scrap paper before they left camp, reciting the scriptorium's cipher-lore like a litany — *tumbler, ward, tumbler, blood-groove* — because that was what you did when knowledge had cost you memories to get. You did not let it slip. You wrote it on your hands if you had to.

The archivist's price still ached in him. He'd paid two memories for the Rites of Unbinding: his mother's face, and the smell of the chancery where he'd been an apprentice scribe before he ran. He knew they were gone the way you know a tooth has come out — by the shape of the hole. The others had covered his shortfall with memories of their own, offered up without ceremony, as casually as passing bread. Osk had given away the name of a man he'd killed. Bramble had given away the taste of his own wedding cake.

You did not forget things like that. You did not dare.

"Left at the next junction," Fen whispered, holding Yara's hooded lantern low. "Then down. The cells are cut into the salt itself — Vane chose it because salt deadens prayer. Nothing holy answers down here."

"Good thing we're not holy," Yara murmured.

Behind them, Edmund moved like a man walking through deep water. Even hooded, even dressed in smugglers' wool, something about him pulled at the dark — Fen could feel it, a faint wrongness in his back teeth, like a struck tuning fork that wouldn't finish its note. The crown was gone from his head tonight; he'd left it locked in its coffer with Osk standing guard over the key. But whatever wore the crown had worn the *man* longer, and men do not shed that quickly.

"The guards rotate every quarter hour," Fen said. "Two at the stairhead, one at the door. And Serah Vane keeps her office above — she'll hear any alarm."

"She won't hear an alarm," Yara said, "because there won't be one."

They came around the junction and there was the stairhead, torchlit, and there were the two guards, and there — stepping out of the shadow of them with his sword already drawn and his face already ruined with grief-fury — was Osk, walking toward his past.

* * *

**OSK**

Twelve years ago, Osk had stood in a salt cellar just like this one beside Serah Vane, both of them royal blades, both of them certain, while their captain read names off a ledger and men were dragged out to answer for prayers the crown had outlawed. Twelve years ago he had not looked away. He had told himself for twelve years that looking away now, in his exile, in his smuggling life among people he insisted were only colleagues, balanced the scale.

It did not. He had finally understood that somewhere on the road from Hollow Bridge, bleeding into his shirt for Fen's sake. Balancing wasn't possible. Only this was: going down into the dark first.

"Hoy," he said.

The nearer guard turned. Young. Barely shaving. Osk felt absurdly grateful that it wasn't anyone he knew.

"You're Osk," the young one said, eyes widening. "The Osk. They still teach your forms at the academy."

"They shouldn't," Osk said. "I teach better." And he hit him with the flat.

The second one was faster, and angrier, and it became a real fight for about eight seconds — long enough for Osk to remember that his ribs were cracked from the bridge, long enough for the boy's blade to open a line along his forearm — and then it was over, because eight seconds was all Osk ever needed against someone who fought by the book. He'd stopped fighting by the book the day he'd walked out of the palace and never gone back.

He tied them both with the practiced boredom of a man doing chores, and found Fen already at the cell door, fingers moving over the lock like a reader's fingers over familiar text.

"Tumbler," Fen breathed. "Ward. Tumbler. Blood-groove—" A click, sweet as a bell. "Open."

The door swung, and Bramble sat inside on a stone shelf, chained at the wrists, one eye swollen shut, lip split, looking — infuriatingly, wonderfully — like a man interrupted mid-thought.

"Took you long enough," Bramble said. "The gruel here is criminal. I've been composing a letter to whoever is in charge of gruel."

Osk laughed, and it came out wet, and he didn't care. He crossed the cell and put his forehead against his friend's enormous, stubborn shoulder, just for a moment, and Bramble allowed it, which was how Bramble said things he couldn't say.

"Can you walk?" Yara asked from the doorway, scanning the corridor.

"I can *cook*," Bramble said, levering himself up with a groan of chains. "Walking is incidental."

* * *

**YARA**

Yara felt the trap before she saw it — not with her fading swordspeech, which had gone quiet as a dead hearth these past years, but with the older, humbler sense that kept smugglers alive: the wrongness of silence where sound should be. The guard rotation had stopped. The torches burned too evenly.

"Ambush," she said. "Everyone behind me. Now."

They came out of the salt corridors into the wide lower gallery — a cathedral of translucent walls, amber and white, glittering under lantern light — and there stood Serah Vane with twenty soldiers fanned across the gallery floor, crossbows leveled, and Bramble's chains still rattling behind them like a bad joke.

"Inquisitor," Yara said.

"Swordspeaker." Vane's voice was flat and precise, a woman reading charges. "Or should I say *former*. Your order is ash. Your gods are asleep. All you have left is contraband and company." Her gaze flicked to Edmund, hood thrown back now, and something moved behind her certainty — hunger or fear, hard to say. "And him. The god-touched king who walks free. Kneel, all of you, and the ledger closes gently."

Yara stepped forward. Alone. She heard Bramble swear behind her and raised a hand to stop him.

She had planned for this — not the rescue, but the moment after it failed. She had always planned for failure; hope was cargo she no longer carried in bulk. She would offer herself. She would trade her life for theirs, and mean it, and be surprised, faintly, if it worked.

But Vane did not give the order.

Vane was looking at her — really looking, the way you look at a mirror you weren't expecting — and Yara saw the recognition land. Not of her name. Of her *stance*. The way a person stands between danger and the people behind her, weight forward, hands empty, offering everything without ceremony. Yara knew that stance. She had learned it from an order of men and women who had all died standing exactly so.

And Serah Vane had once stood so too — before the ledger, before the promotions, before the crown taught her that devotion was a resource to be managed.

"Shoot her," said one of the captains.

"No," Vane said.

The word dropped into the gallery like a stone into a well. Twenty soldiers wavered. Vane's hand was steady on her sword hilt but her eyes were not steady at all, and Yara watched — with the strange clarity of someone standing at the center of their own death — as the inquisitor's certainty developed its first crack.

"I gave clemency," Vane said quietly. "To the cook. He refused it. Do you know what I did with thirty years of service before I understood that people can refuse to be saved from each other?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Nothing. I did nothing, and called it faith."

"Serah," the captain said. "The king—"

"I know what he is," Vane snapped. "I have read the ledger of my own heart, captain, and it is not clean either. Lower the bows."

For one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat, Yara thought it might end there — that the crack would widen, that doubt was contagious —

Then the salt itself began to sing.

A low tone rose out of the translucent walls, out of the mountain's bones, and every soldier's crossbow dipped as if the air had thickened. Edmund staggered. Yara spun — and saw him upright, eyes flooded with pale light, mouth opening on a voice that was not entirely his:

*"SHE IS MINE. SHE WAS ALWAYS MINE. THE LAST OATH IS THE LAST CHAIN, AND CHAINS PULL BOTH WAYS."*

The light went out of him like a snuffed candle and he crumpled; Fen caught him. The soldiers, terrified by things they had no theology for, broke formation. And through the chaos Yara met Serah Vane's eyes one last time — the inquisitor pale, shaken, her blade half-drawn and pointed at nothing.

"Go," Vane said hoarsely. "Before I remember what I am."

Yara went. But she took the image with her: a zealot standing in a cathedral of salt, hesitating, exactly as Yara might have hesitated, for exactly the same reason.

* * *

**BRAMBLE**

Bramble talked the whole way up the mountain, which was how everyone knew he was hurt worse than he let on.

"—and the trick with the prison gruel, if you're ever served it, is barley water and time, though time is the one ingredient they never stock. In the cells I kept myself together by recipes. Did I tell you? Every night, start to finish, I'd cook a feast in my head. Seven courses. Wine pairings. The night before you came I did my own funeral dinner, which I don't recommend, morbid business, but the roast was excellent—"

"Bramble," Fen said gently.

"—anyway. I never gave them anything. Vane asked me for your route, Yara, and I recited her a recipe for black pudding instead. Twice. She got quite cross." He grinned with his split lip, and the grin cost him; he swayed.

Osk caught his arm. "Lean on me, you great idiot."

"I'll lean on no one, I'm a pillar of the community—"

They made the ridge as dawn came up grey and thin, and there, in the lee of the rocks, Bramble finally sat down because his legs informed him the performance was over. Yara knelt by him, checking the wounds the chains had made, and her hands — her famous hands, hands that had spoken to swords, hands that could find a path through a blizzard — were shaking.

"Yara," Bramble said, and his voice had gone quiet in a way none of them had ever heard. Quiet was not in his repertoire. "While I was sitting in that cell, I had a lot of time. And I'm not clever like Fen, but I'm not stupid either. I read what Fen copied from the scriptorium, back before you took it into your pack. The rite. The unbinding."

Yara's hands went still.

"It takes an oath-breaker's blood," Bramble said. "There's only one oath-breaker in this company, girl. And it strips the speaker of everything." His good eye was bright and furious and wet. "So before you go one step further toward that golden hall, you're going to sit down, and you're going to eat something, and then this family is going to have words."

Above them, far to the south, the capital's bells had begun to ring — early, wrong, ringing for a coronation that was not due for days. The god was hurrying.

But Bramble did not hurry anyone. He unpacked his pans.
