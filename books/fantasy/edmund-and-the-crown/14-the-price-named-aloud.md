## 14. The Price Named Aloud

Bramble had learned to read people the way other folk learned to read weather, and he'd learned it in kitchens, which was the hardest school there was. A kitchen told you everything. It told you who was hungry and who was pretending not to be, who was grieving and who was merely tired, who would steal from the pot and who would guard it with their life. Twenty years of feeding soldiers, smugglers, priests, and liars had given him a nose for trouble finer than any hound's.

So when Yara Sterling came to his fire on the third night after Hollow Bridge, took a bowl of venison stew without a single complaint about the salt, and thanked him — *thanked him*, plain as a merchant paying full price — Bramble knew something was rotten in the mountains.

He watched her from across the camp while she ate. She sat with her back to a stone the way she always did, blade within reach, but there was a stillness to her that hadn't been there before. Not peace. Peace looked like Osk after a good fight, loose-limbed and grinning at nothing. This was different. This was a woman setting her affairs in order.

Fen sat beside her, chattering about the Scriptorium, about the archivist's blind eyes and the shelves that walked, and Yara smiled in all the right places. But her hand kept drifting to the satchel where they'd stored the copied pages of the Rites of Unbinding, touching it the way a man touches a wound to check if it's still bleeding.

Bramble turned the thought over all through washing the pots, all through banking the fire, all through lying awake under a sky too full of stars for comfort. And somewhere between the second watch and the gray of dawn, the pieces clicked together the way they always did — late, unwelcome, and undeniable.

*An oath-breaker's blood,* the rite said. *A willing vessel. And the unbinding will take from the speaker all that the oath has given.*

He'd heard Fen read those lines aloud three times. He'd watched Yara's face each time, and each time she'd worn the same expression: calm, attentive, faintly bored. Nobody wore boredom that carefully unless they were hiding something behind it.

Yara wasn't planning to perform the rite.

She was planning to be its altar.

* * *

Bramble was not a man for subtlety. He was a large man built like a door, with forearms that could crack walnuts and a face that had been described by more than one customs official as "a cliff with opinions." Subtlety, in his experience, was what small clever people used instead of honesty, and honesty was the only currency he'd ever trusted.

So at breakfast, when Yara reached for the porridge pot, he put his own hand flat over the lid and said, "Sit down, lass. We need to talk."

The camp went quiet. Fen froze mid-sentence, quill hovering. Osk, who was rewrapping the bandage on his ribs, stopped moving entirely. Even the mule seemed to hold its breath.

Yara looked at him with those gray eyes that had stared down zealots and gods alike, and Bramble saw her decide — quickly, smoothly — to play it light. "I'm sitting," she said. "I'm always sitting. I'm the most seated person you know."

"You know what I mean." He lifted his hand off the pot. "Eat first. You'll want your strength for shouting."

"I don't shout."

"Aye, that's the problem."

She ate. He let her. It was, he reflected grimly, possibly the last decent meal he'd get to serve her without an argument attached, so he made sure it was a good one — porridge with honey and the last of the dried cherries, the kind of breakfast he made for people he loved on days that mattered. She noticed. Of course she noticed; she noticed everything except the things that would save her life.

When the bowls were empty, Bramble folded his hands on his knees and said, "Tell me how the rite ends."

Yara didn't blink. "It ends with the crown unbound and Edmund alive. That's rather the point."

"Read me the last page."

"The last page is warnings. Old scribes loved their warnings. Half of them are superstition—"

"Read me the last page." His voice came out low, the way it did when bread was burning or when someone insulted his crew, and both Fen and Osk flinched. "Or I'll read it myself, out loud, right now, and we'll see whose morning gets ruined."

Silence stretched. Somewhere up the slope, a bird sang, indifferent to the end of the world.

Then Yara sighed, and something in her shoulders dropped half an inch — the smallest surrender, the kind she probably thought no one would see. "You've already read it."

"Aye."

"Then you know I can't—"

"*Can't*?" Bramble stood, because sitting suddenly felt like a lie. "Lass, I've known you six years. Six years of you telling me what you can't do — can't stay in one town, can't keep a crew, can't trust anyone past the next pass. And every single time, what you meant was *won't*. So let's not dress this up as fate. This is a choice. Your choice. Made alone. Again."

"It's not a choice," Yara said quietly. "There is no one else."

"There's me."

That landed. He saw it land — saw her flinch as though he'd struck her, saw Fen's head snap around, saw Osk go very still.

"What?" Yara said.

"You heard me." Bramble spread his hands. "The rite wants an oath-breaker's blood. Fine. I broke my oath to the Crown twenty years ago when I walked away from the king's army with my whole company's pay chest and gave it to the widows. That's an oath broken, written in ink and everything. It wants a willing vessel? I'm willing. I'm old, I'm heavy, I snore like a landslide, and I have exactly one talent left, which is keeping people fed — and I can't feed anyone if I'm dead, granted, but I also can't do it if *you're* dead, and between the two of us, lass, the arithmetic favors me."

"No," Yara said.

"Bramble—" Fen started.

"No," Yara said again, sharper now, standing. "Absolutely not. The rite needs a swordspeaker's bond to anchor the severing. It needs *my* oath. That's not sentiment, it's mechanics. If any of you touched that rite, it would kill you and leave Edmund bound besides. There is no version of this where someone else pays."

"Then we find another way," Fen said, scrambling up, pages scattering. "There has to be another way. The Scriptorium had forty thousand volumes — maybe there's a variant, maybe—"

"Fen." Yara's voice gentled, and somehow that was worse than the shouting. "We have four days. The renewal rite is at midsummer moon. There is no variant. There is no other way. There is only this."

"Then we don't do it," Osk said.

Everyone turned to him. He'd risen without seeming to move, the way ex-soldiers did, and he stood with his weight off his wounded side, arms crossed, wearing the flat expression Bramble had learned meant the man was angriest.

"We walk away," Osk said. "Edmund is a stranger. A king. Kings got us into this — kings and their crowns and their gods. We owe him nothing. We owe *each other* something, and what we owe each other is not letting Yara march off to die because she's decided dying alone is the same thing as duty."

"It's not about duty," Yara said.

"Then what's it about?"

And there it was. Bramble watched her reach for the answer — watched her grab for *duty*, for *oath*, for *the realm*, all the armor-plated words she kept polished for exactly these moments — and watched every one of them fail her, because Osk had asked the one question she couldn't answer with armor.

"He begged me to kill him," she said finally. "On the convoy. In one of his clear moments, he looked at me and asked me to end it before the rites. And I refused. I told him I'd find another way, because I'm a coward and I couldn't do it. So now there's a way that isn't killing him, and it costs me instead, and I'm going to take it, because I made him a promise." Her jaw tightened. "I keep my promises."

"That's not why," Bramble said softly.

"Don't."

"You think I can't see it? You think I haven't been watching you two for weeks? The way you look at him when he's lucid. The way you stopped sleeping after Hollow Bridge because his blood hit the stones and your power woke up screaming. Lass, you're not doing this for a promise. You're doing it because you love him, and because loving someone terrifies you more than dying does, and dying is the one thing you've never been afraid of."

The silence after that was total. Fen had gone white. Osk's fists were clenched at his sides.

Yara stood very straight, and Bramble saw her eyes shining and furious, and he knew he'd drawn blood — real blood, the kind that mattered.

"You don't get to say that," she whispered.

"Somebody had to," Bramble said. "You weren't going to."

* * *

They fought for an hour. Bramble would remember it afterward the way he remembered the worst nights of the war — in fragments, in heat, in the taste of words he couldn't take back.

Fen cried, which none of them acknowledged, and argued scripture, which they did: pulling out copied pages, citing the archivist, hunting loopholes with the desperate fluency of a scribe who had once rewritten a death warrant to save a stranger and could not bear to stop now. Osk offered cold logistics — escape routes, false identities, a life in the southern ports where no one had ever heard of swordspeakers or crowns. Bramble himself mostly just refused, the way stone refuses, saying *no* in every register he owned until his voice went raw.

And Yara held firm.

That was the terrible thing, Bramble thought later. Not that she argued well — she barely argued at all. She simply stood in the middle of their fury like a post in a flood and let it break against her, and when each wave spent itself she said, quietly, "There is no one else," and waited for the next one.

At some point Fen threw the copied rite pages into the fire. Yara pulled them out. Singed, but legible. She smoothed them against her knee without a word, and Fen made a sound like something breaking and walked to the far edge of camp and sat with her back to all of them, shoulders shaking.

Osk lasted longest. He got close, close enough that Bramble tensed, and said, low, "I carried Fen out from under Vane's blades. You carried Edmund off that bridge. We are not people who leave each other to pay alone. Whatever you think you are — whatever the order taught you about lone blades and lonely deaths — that's not this crew. That's not *us*."

"I know," Yara said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle. "Gods help me, Osk, I know. That's what makes it bearable. That's what makes any of it bearable. I finally have something worth coming back to — and I'm spending it anyway. Don't you think I know how that sounds?"

"Then don't—"

"If I don't, he dies screaming from the inside while a god wears his face for a hundred years. If I do, I lose my magic and you lot get stuck with a middle-aged woman who can't do anything but smuggle, swear, and start fires." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears, furious at them. "I can live with that trade. That's the whole horror of it — I *can* live with it. I did the sums on the mountain and they come out right, and I hate every number."

Bramble looked at her then — really looked, cook's eyes, the eyes that saw hunger before hunger spoke — and understood at last that he was not going to win. Not because she was stubborn, though she was, gloriously. Because she was right. There was no one else. There never had been. The gods had spent a century arranging the board so that every path led here, to this woman, this choice, this fire.

But being right didn't mean she had to be alone.

"All right," he said.

Yara blinked. "All right?"

"All right. You do your rite. You save your king. You spend whatever the gods demand." He stepped forward and took her hands — flour-rough, burn-scarred, gentle as he knew how to be. "And we will be there. Every step. And when it's done, when you're ordinary and powerless and free, you are going to come home to my kitchen, and I am going to feed you until you believe you deserve feeding. That's my price. That's the toll. You don't get to die for us and then vanish up some pass out of shame. You live, and you eat my cooking, and you let us love you badly and loudly for the rest of your long, infuriating, mortal life. Swear it."

For a long moment Yara Sterling, last of the oath-bound, the woman who had out-bluffed inquisitors and out-climbed avalanches, could not speak at all.

"I swear," she managed.

"Good." Bramble released her, turned, and clapped his hands once, loud as a pan hitting stone. "Right. Four days to the capital. Fen, stop crying, start packing — I need the good spice bundle. Osk, check the mule's shoe. And somebody tell His Majesty that breakfast is in an hour, and if he dies before he tastes my honey cakes, I will consider it a personal insult."

A wet laugh escaped Fen. Osk was already moving. And Yara stood in the ashes of the argument, holding singed pages against her heart, looking at her strange, stubborn, impossible family as if seeing them for the first time.

It was Osk, tightening the last strap on the mule, who went still and said quietly:

"Where's Edmund?"

The camp emptied of warmth in a heartbeat. They looked — at the cold ring of stones where he'd slept, at the treeline, at the fresh scuff of boot prints leading downhill toward the capital road.

Toward the Golden Hall. Alone.

Yara was running before the echo faded.
