## 15. Coronation Eve

Serah Vane had never trusted a silence she couldn't interrogate.

For eleven years she had served the Crown as an inquisitor, and in that time she had learned that every silence had an author. The silence of a suspect was fear. The silence of a guilty man was calculation. The silence of the lowland towns when her column rode through — that was the silence of people deciding what they could afford to say. She had built a career on standing inside silences and pulling their teeth.

The capital's silence tonight was different. It was not authored by anyone. It simply *was*, vast and humming, and it frightened her more than any confession chamber ever had.

She stood at the window of her quarters in the Inquisitors' Hall and watched the city kneel.

Not all of it. Not yet. But enough. From the Lantern District to the tanners' rows along the river, candles burned in ten thousand windows, and before ten thousand doorsteps, citizens knelt with their foreheads to the cobblestones, praying aloud in voices that carried strangely through the cold air — not ragged and individual the way prayer usually sounded, but *unified*, syllables overlapping into something like a tide. They were praying to the crown. To the king upon his throne in the Golden Hall, though most of them had never seen Edmund's face and would not have recognized him if they had. It did not matter. The god in the crown heard them all the same.

Vane pressed two fingers against the glass. Her breath fogged it. Below, a woman rose from her knees, kissed the stones where her forehead had rested, and went inside smiling.

*They look happy,* Vane thought, and the thought tasted like ash.

* * *

She had been summoned to the palace at dusk, and the summons itself had been wrong. No seal. No clerk. Just a page boy with strange, calm eyes who said, "His Radiance asks for you," and turned away before she could ask which radiance he meant. The king's. Or the other one. The distinction had been eroding for weeks; everyone in the palace felt it, the way you feel a storm front in your teeth.

The corridors of the Golden Hall blazed with candlelight at an hour when they should have been dark. Servants moved through the glow without speaking, and when Vane passed them, they bowed — not to her, but to something over her shoulder, as though she were merely a lens through which a greater light passed. She had seen zealotry before. She had manufactured zealotry before, in interrogation rooms, in public penances, in the careful theater of fear. This was not that. Fear made people flinch. Whatever walked these halls now made people *shine*.

The throne room doors stood open. She entered alone, because no guard stopped her and no guard accompanied her, and that too was a message: she was past needing guards. Or past being worth guarding. She genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Edmund sat the throne.

Or rather — the thing wearing Edmund sat the throne, and Edmund flickered through it like a candle behind smoked glass. He had been gaunt when Vane first arrived at court three months ago; now he was translucent, a king rendered in charcoal sketch, his eyes sunken pools of shadow lit from within by a gold that had nothing to do with health. His hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, in the posture of a man receiving. Or a man nailed.

"Serah Vane." The voice came from Edmund's mouth but resonated from somewhere beneath the floor, beneath the foundations, beneath the mountain the capital clung to. "My faithful hound. My ledger-keeper. Come closer."

She came closer. She had long ago stopped pretending she had a choice about such things — though the pretense, she noticed, was getting harder to maintain. There had been a time, crossing the Thornfell passes after the smuggler Yara Sterling, when she had still believed her obedience was a kind of strength. Now it felt like a leash she'd mistaken for a spine.

"You have served me well," the god said through its king. "You found the god-touched. You broke the salt road. You took the cook."

"Bramble is in the cells below the Salt Gate," Vane said. Her voice sounded flat and small in the resonant dark. "He has given us nothing."

"He has given us everything we need. His loyalty told me where the others would run." The mouth smiled, and the smile did not belong to the face using it. "And tomorrow, when I am renewed, when my anchor is made fast for another age, you will be rewarded. Kneel."

Vane knelt. She told herself it was strategy. She was no longer entirely sure that was a lie, and that frightened her.

"The rite approaches," the god said. "I require a high priestess. One whose faith is not borrowed ecstasy but earned certainty. You have hunted the old gods' remnants across three provinces. You have burned their shrines and broken their speakers. You believe, Serah Vane. Not in me — not yet — but in *order*. In a world with one voice and no competing whispers. I am offering you that world. Ascend. Be my voice when my king can no longer carry even my whispers. Rule the kneeling city in my name."

The offer hung in the air like incense.

Vane kept her eyes on the floor. She was aware, distantly, of her own heartbeat, steady and stubborn, the heartbeat of a woman who had climbed from a tanner's slum to the Inquisitors' Hall on nothing but certainty and a talent for making other people afraid. This was what she had wanted. Wasn't it? A kingdom with no smugglers' roads, no hidden shrines, no half-gods bleeding through the cracks of the world. A ledger with every name accounted for. Order, total and clean.

"I accept," she heard herself say, "to consider."

The silence that followed had teeth.

"Consideration," the god said at last, amused, terrible. "Yes. Consider. That is why I chose you. Even your doubt will serve me, once it breaks. Doubt that has never been tested is just pride. Yours will break beautifully."

* * *

She left the palace walking fast, which she never did, and caught herself doing it, and slowed, and hated that she had to slow deliberately, as though her own legs belonged to someone else's panic.

The city was worse than it had been at dusk. The kneeling had spread. Whole streets now, families arranged in rows before their doors, faces upturned toward the palace hill, mouths moving in the unified tide of prayer. And here was the thing Vane could not stop seeing, the splinter working deeper under her skin with every block:

There were children among them.

A girl no older than six knelt beside her mother on a corner near the chandlers' row, tiny knees on freezing stone, copying the words without understanding them, glancing sideways every few seconds to check that she was doing it right. Her mother's face was rapturous. The girl's face was *careful*. She prayed the way children recite lessons — watching for correction, terrified of getting it wrong.

Vane stopped. Stood in the middle of the street like a fool, in her grey inquisitor's coat, staring.

In eleven years she had put children in cells. She had done it without losing sleep, because the doctrine held: corruption ran in bloodlines, god-touched begat god-touched, and mercy shown to a seed was cruelty to the harvest. She had written the doctrine herself, or helped write it, or told herself she had — the provenance of her cruelties had always been conveniently blurred. But those children had been *accused*. They had been part of the machinery, names in her ledger, entries to be resolved.

This child had committed no crime except being born into a city that was learning to kneel. She was not an entry. She was not being processed. She was being *harvested* — swept up whole, along with everyone else, into a hunger so large that individual souls had stopped being counted at all.

Vane had spent her life believing that terror, properly administered, kept people safe. What flooded the streets now was not terror. Terror left people free to hate their masters in private. This left them nothing private at all. It poured into them through their prayers and dissolved the walls between one soul and the next, and the girl on the corner was already dissolving, her careful little face going smooth and rapturous as her mother's, and something in Vane's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

*This is not order,* she thought. *Order counts things. This devours them.*

She had chased a smuggler across the mountains for exactly this crime — for trafficking in the old gods' tokens, in prayers answered by hungry things. And here was a hungrier thing than any the old gods had ever housed, enthroned in her own capital, and she had knelt to it. She had *considered* it.

The girl looked up at her. For one moment the rapture cracked, and behind it was just a child on cold stones, tired, wanting to go inside.

Vane turned and walked away. Her hands were shaking. She made fists of them and kept walking, and behind her the tide of prayer rolled on, syllable over syllable, eating the night.

* * *

The Inquisitors' Hall was quiet when she returned, quieter than it should have been. Half her staff had gone to join the kneeling. The rest moved through the corridors with the glazed serenity of the newly converted, and when she gave an order, they obeyed — instantly, pleasantly, without the friction of thought. It was like commanding puppets. It was like commanding no one.

She locked herself in her office and pulled out the ledger.

Her ledger. The true one, the one she kept in her own hand, not the official copy. Every name she had ever pursued was in it, cross-referenced, annotated, judged. She had always thought of it as a weapon. Tonight, by candlelight, it looked like something else: a map of a life spent counting other people's sins because she could not bear to count her own.

She turned to the pages on the Sterling crew.

Yara Sterling. Oath-bound swordspeaker, last of her order. Smuggler. The notes in Vane's hand went back years — sightings, cargo manifests, interrogated associates. And threaded through all of it, in the margins, in a smaller script she barely recognized as her own younger self's: questions. *How does she survive? Why do the old gods let her live? What is she loyal to?* Vane had crossed the questions out, again and again, and they had kept coming back, like weeds through flagstones.

Osk. Former royal blade. Vane had trained beside him once, a lifetime ago, before his desertion. She remembered his stance, his patience, the way he'd said *I'm done* with a flatness she had mistook for cowardice for years before understanding it was grief.

Fen. Bramble. Runaways, misfits, a cook who recited recipes under torture rather than give up a family he claimed wasn't his.

*A family,* Vane thought. *They call it temporary. They lie to each other about it constantly. And they would die for it.*

She looked down at her own hands — clean hands, capable hands, hands that had signed clemency offers and death warrants in equal measure — and tried to remember the last time anyone would have died for her. Tried to remember the last time she would have wanted them to.

The candle guttered. Somewhere far off, muffled by stone, the tide of prayer swelled as midnight approached and the city knelt deeper.

Tomorrow was the renewal rite. Tomorrow the god would be anchored for another age, and the girl on the chandlers' corner would grow up kneeling, and her children would kneel, and there would be no ledger anywhere in the world fine-grained enough to hold all the names being erased.

Vane opened her ledger to a fresh page. She meant to write the final disposition of the Sterling case — the dispositions for tomorrow, the orders, the dispositions of the people who had become, without her noticing, the only people alive who were trying to stop this.

What she wrote instead, in small, precise letters, was:

*Sterling will come for the king during the rite. She must reach the dais.*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she closed the book, snuffed the candle, and sat in the dark listening to a city pray itself empty — a woman who had built her life on certainty, holding, for the first time, the beginning of a plan that required her to betray everything certain she had left.

Outside her window, dawn was still hours away. Inside her chest, something older than doubt had begun, very quietly, to crack.
