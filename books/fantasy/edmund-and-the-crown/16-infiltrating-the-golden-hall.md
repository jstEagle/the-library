## 16. Infiltrating the Golden Hall

The capital of Aldenmere had grown fat on prayer.

Yara saw it from the ridge above the Salt Road's last mile — the same road she'd run for eleven years, the road that opened this book of her life and was now, apparently, writing its ending. Below her, the city sprawled along the river like something gorged: golden light leaking from every window, incense smoke standing in pillars over the districts, and everywhere, everywhere, people kneeling. Even at this distance she could see them — in the market squares, on the bridge steps, at the mouths of alleys — kneeling with their faces turned toward the palace hill where the Golden Hall caught the late sun and burned like a wound.

"Charming place," Bramble said behind her. "Smells like a temple fell in the soup."

"It did," Yara said. "Three months ago. It just hasn't stopped falling."

She spread the map on a flat stone and weighted its corners. The old smuggler's map, ink faded to brown ghosts, annotated in three different hands across eleven years. Fen leaned over it, scribe's fingers hovering as if afraid to smudge even another person's history.

"The river gate is out," Osk said. He stood with his weight off his bad leg, arms crossed, watching the city the way he watched everything — like a man counting exits. "Vane doubled the water watch after the salt cells. I can smell her discipline from here."

"So we don't use the gates," Yara said. "We use what I used before there were gates worth bribing." Her finger traced a line down from the ridge, through the terraced vineyards gone wild on the palace hill's north face, into a seam of shadow between two noble districts. "The Weepers' Stair. Old pilgrim route into the undercroft of the Hall itself. Sealed when Edmund's grandfather built the new chapel over it. Nobody seals anything properly. I've moved prayer-tokens through that gap for a decade."

"And nobody stopped you," Fen said slowly, "because nobody thought anyone would be mad enough to walk *into* the god's mouth."

"I prefer *front door*," Yara said. "It's politer than what we're doing."

Bramble snorted. Osk didn't smile. He was looking at the kneeling crowds, and his jaw had the set it got when he was remembering things he claimed not to remember.

"They're not all faithful," he said quietly. "Look at their hands. Half of those people are holding onto each other. That's not worship. That's drowning men gripping driftwood."

Yara looked. He was right. The kneeling wasn't ecstasy anymore; it had passed through ecstasy weeks ago and come out the other side into something exhausted and compulsive, prayer as a tic, prayer as survival. The god beneath Thornfell had flooded the capital with answered longing, and the longing had curdled into need, and need was the one chain everyone forged for themselves.

"Then we're doing them a favor," she said, and rolled up the map. "Rest until dark. Eat everything Bramble brought, because tomorrow we go hungry and holy."

* * *

They came down through the wild vineyards in the blue hour, when the light went soft as ash and the guards on the walls stared into the glare of their own torches. Yara led. Eleven years of running this mountain had written its geography into her feet; she knew which stones held weight and which lied about it, knew where the terraces crumbled and where goat trails stitched the cliff faces together like patient sutures. Behind her came her crew, and ahead of her, hooded and gray-cloaked and trying very hard to be invisible, walked a king.

Edmund moved better than she'd expected. Weeks on the road had burned away whatever softness the palace left in him, and the crown-thing's attention was elsewhere tonight — pulled taut toward the Hall where the renewal rite waited — so his face had settled into something almost peaceful. Almost human. She kept catching herself watching him walk and having to make herself stop.

At the top of the Weepers' Stair they paused, pressed against cold rock, while a patrol's lanterns swung past below. In the silence Yara heard the city praying. Not words. A sound underneath the sound, like wind through a keyhole, thousands of whispered wants braided into one long note. Beside her, Fen had gone rigid.

"You feel that?" Fen breathed.

"Everyone feels that," Bramble muttered. "That's the problem."

"No — I mean—" Fen's hand crept to the pouch at their belt, the one that rattled faintly with stolen scriptorium pages. "The archivist said the rites need a witness who can hear. I can hear it, Yara. All of it. Every prayer in this city, like it's being said into my ear." Their voice shook. "I think the toll memories weren't payment. I think they were tuning."

Yara took Fen's shoulder and turned them around to face her. Under the hood, Fen's eyes were too wide, pupils blown black in the dusk.

"Listen to me. Whatever you hear down there — however loud it gets — you are Fen. You are our scribe. You are the one who writes things down so they can't be lied about later. That's your anchor, do you understand? Ink and paper. If the noise gets too big, you write it down until it shrinks back to size."

Fen laughed, wet and shaky. "You want me to take minutes during an apocalyptic infiltration."

"I want you to be exactly who you are," Yara said. "It's worked so far."

Osk cleared his throat softly. "Patrols turning back. Stair's clear. And if we could have this touching reunion somewhere without forty crossbows in earshot—"

They descended.

* * *

The Weepers' Stair earned its name. Water seeped through the mountain's bones here and ran down the ancient steps in thin silver threads, and centuries of pilgrims had carved small niches into the walls — some still holding the fused stubs of candles, some holding nothing but the smooth hollows where fingers had touched for generations. Yara climbed down in the dark by touch and memory, calling soft warnings back over her shoulder, and the crew followed in a rope-linked line: Bramble grunting, Osk silent, Fen's breathing quick and counted, Edmund last, closest to the dark they were walking toward, because the plan required it and because he insisted.

The sealed door was exactly where she'd promised. Old masonry, newer mortar, a seam that gave to Osk's crowbar with a grinding whisper like a secret finally spoken. Beyond it lay the undercroft of the Golden Hall.

Yara had imagined vaults, dust, disuse. What she found made her skin crawl.

The undercroft was lit. Candles by the thousand, guttering in the breathless air, filling a space far larger than any cellar needed to be — and the walls were covered in prayers. Scratched into stone, painted in ochre and rust-red, layered so deep the original surface had vanished: *heal my daughter, bring him back, let me win, let me win, let me win.* Some were decades old. Most were new. The newest were carved so hard the writers had broken their nails against the rock, and the blood mixed with candlewax into a crust that crackled under Yara's boots.

"This isn't storage," Osk said. His voice had gone flat, the soldier's voice. "This is a mouth. The whole Hall sits on this like a man sitting on a well."

"Then we're in the throat," Yara said. "Good. Throats are how you get to the head."

Above them, muffled through the vaulted ceiling, they could hear the Hall preparing. Hammers. Chanting. The vast shuffling murmur of a court assembling for the greatest spectacle of a generation. Tomorrow at dawn, the renewal rite. Tomorrow at dawn, the crown would be lowered onto Edmund's head before the assembled faith of a starving god — and if Yara hadn't already cut the binding loose by then, it never would be cut at all.

She laid out the plan on the undercroft floor, in whispers, by candlelight that seemed to lean in and listen.

"Bramble. The kitchens. You told me you cooked for the Duke of Marren's household before the war took everything else from you." Bramble grumbled but nodded. "Tomorrow morning the Hall feeds four hundred celebrants before the rite. Nobody questions a cook. Get yourself into whites, get yourself into the scullery, and at mid-bell you open the postern door in the kitchen yard. The one with the broken latch you told me about when you were drunk in the Salt Cells."

"I tell all my best architecture drunk," Bramble said. "It's why they keep me around."

"Fen. The archive under the west transept. Vane's zealots will be marshaling the rite's order of ceremony from the old liturgical records, and the records live there. You get in as a copyist — the robes are in the cache Osk stashed last week — and you do what you do. Read everything. Find me the exact wording of the renewal oath, because if the binding has a hinge, the hinge is in what Edmund has to say aloud."

Fen's jaw tightened. "And if I hear the prayers again?"

"You write them down," Yara said. "Until they shrink."

"Osk." She met his eyes. He'd known since the salt cells what was coming, and he stood for it now the way he stood for everything: straight-backed, weight on the good leg, no complaint offered. "The armory gallery overlooks the dais. When it starts, I need every guard's eyes on you instead of me. You'll know what to do."

"I always know what to do," Osk said. "That's the tragedy of me."

"And me?" Edmund asked.

Everyone looked at him. The candle flames bent toward him, all of them at once, like flowers turning to a sun that wasn't there.

Yara crouched by the map she'd scratched in the wax dust and pointed at the center of it — the dais, the throne, the empty seat at the heart of everything.

"You," she said, "walk out in front of everyone. In your own face, in your own name. You tell them the king has returned early to pray alone through the night vigil — which is true, as far as it goes. You sit on that throne, in front of four hundred faithful and one starving god, and you keep it talking to you while I work underneath."

Silence. Even the chanting overhead seemed to pause.

"That's the part of the plan," Edmund said, "that requires me to sit in the trap and hold the predator's gaze for an hour."

"Yes."

"While it offers me everything I've ever wanted. While it shows me my children alive. While it wears my voice and moves my hands if I slip even once."

"Yes," Yara said again, and hated how steady her voice sounded when nothing inside her was.

Edmund looked at the throne drawn in wax dust. Then he looked at her, and there was no flicker in him at all, no other presence, just the tired honest man she'd bargained with in the dark.

"When I first invited it in," he said quietly, "I told myself I was saving a kingdom. That was half a lie — I wanted my grief fixed, and I let a god buy the difference. So understand me, Yara Sterling: I am not doing this for absolution." He smiled, thin and real. "I'm doing it because for the first time since the plague, someone offered me a third path instead of a choice between deaths. I intend to sit very still and be extremely disappointing."

Bramble wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand and pretended it was the candle smoke.

* * *

They separated in the hour before midnight, one by one, slipping upward through the undercroft's hidden passages into the body of the palace. Yara watched each of them go — Bramble rolling his shoulders like a man heading into a kitchen shift, whistling something tuneless and brave; Fen clutching their scribe's case with white knuckles and a scribbler's stubborn chin; Osk pausing at the passage mouth to look back at all of them, memorizing, the way soldiers memorize before a battle they might not all walk out of.

"Come back to the rendezvous," Yara said. "All of you. That's not a request. That's the only order I've ever given that matters."

Osk saluted, ironic and gentle. "Aye, Captain."

And then it was Yara and Edmund alone in the throat of the god's house, among ten thousand prayers and a thousand candles burning down toward morning.

He stood close enough that she could see the pulse in his throat, fast, the only betrayal of what it would cost him. "Say something useful," he said. "You're the last swordspeaker. Give me a blessing or a curse or at least tactical advice."

Yara reached out and straightened his collar, an ordinary gesture, absurdly domestic in the mouth of a god.

"When it talks to you," she said, "when it shows you them — your heirs, your wife, whoever it uses — don't fight the vision. Fighting gives it grip. Just... look at it the way you looked at me on the mountain. Like something you love too much to lie to." Her voice caught; she pushed through. "Grief isn't a door, Edmund. Don't let it make you think it is."

"And what do you do," he asked, "while I'm sitting in the fire?"

"What I've done my whole life," she said. "Survive. Except—" she exhaled, and gave him the truth, because they had built this whole fragile thing on refusing to lie, "except this time I survive *with*. That's the whole difference. That's the entire revolution."

Something moved through the candles then — a draft from nowhere, bending every flame toward the ceiling, toward the Hall above, toward the throne waiting in the dark like a set jaw. And in the hush that followed, Yara felt it notice them. The presence beneath the mountain, wearing the crown, turning its vast starved attention down through the stones like an eye pressing against a keyhole.

It knew they were coming. Of course it did.

Good, Yara thought, and put her hand on her sword hilt, where the old magic stirred like a dying coal refusing to admit it was out of fuel. Let it watch. By this time tomorrow, either the binding would be severed and the king free —

— or the god would have exactly what it had been starving toward all along: an oath-bound swordspeaker, delivered willingly into the golden hall, ripe for the taking.

Somewhere above them, the great bells began to toll the midnight hour, and the vigil of the crown began.
