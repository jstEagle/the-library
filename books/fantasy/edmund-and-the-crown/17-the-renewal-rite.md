## 17. The Renewal Rite

The crown weighed more than gold.

Edmund had known that for three years — known it in his neck, in his spine, in the place behind his eyes where the god nested like a second heartbeat. But today, walking the length of the Golden Hall beneath the eyes of a thousand kneeling courtiers, it felt less like weight and more like a hand closing around his skull from the inside.

*They are beautiful,* the god murmured, its voice a warm tide against the walls of him. *Look how they love you.*

He looked. He made himself look, because Yara's voice was in his memory — *don't look away, whatever you see down there, don't look away, Edmund, they're people, not a tide* — and because looking was the last resistance he owned. A thousand faces upturned in the candlelight, eyes glazed with borrowed rapture, lips moving in the new prayers the priests had taught them. The god's prayers. Prayers that ate.

A woman near the front held her infant aloft like an offering. The child was laughing. That was the worst of it. The child was laughing, and Edmund could not tell whether the laughter belonged to the baby or to the thing wearing his kingdom's devotion like a garment.

*You could end their hunger,* the god said. *Every hunger. Yours first.*

*I know what you're offering.* He kept his face still, his steps measured, the way he'd been drilled as a boy. *I've seen the visions. Fields that never fail. No plague. No winter grief. You showed me all of it on the road here.*

*And did it not please you?*

*It pleased me so much I nearly said yes.* His boots struck the marble in time with his heart. *That's why I'm saying no now.*

Below the dais, half-hidden among the ranks of white-robed acolytes, he saw her. Yara Sterling, dressed in the grey of a temple servant, head bowed, hands empty — hands that had carried swordspeech for twenty years and would carry nothing at all by nightfall. She did not look up. She had promised she wouldn't. Looking at each other, they'd agreed, was a door neither of them could afford to open while the god watched through his eyes.

But he felt her anyway. He felt her the way a man feels weather coming through an old wound.

* * *

The dais rose in seven steps of veined gold-vein marble, and Edmund climbed them the way he had climbed them at his first coronation, five years ago, when the crown had been only metal and his heirs had still been alive. Bramble's stew. Fen's terrible handwriting. Osk's dry commentary. Yara's laugh, rare as a comet, which he had begun — gods forgive him, *gods*, even now — to build his days around.

He sat the throne.

The moment his body met the seat, the binding woke. It came up through the ancient stone like cold water, through the chair's armrests carved with the faces of dead kings, into his palms, his wrists, his chest. The god poured itself along the anchor chain — king to throne to hall to city to mountain — and Edmund felt himself become a conduit for something vast and starving, felt his own edges thin.

*Now,* the god breathed, and it was almost tender. *Now we are one thing again.*

High Priest Callowin ascended the steps bearing the renewal crown on a cushion of white silk — not the iron circlet Edmund wore day to day but the great ceremonial diadem, the First Crown, forged before the kingdom had a name. Its points were shaped like flames. Its center stone was black, and if you looked too long the black looked back.

Edmund knew what lived in that stone. He had invited it there.

*Say the words,* the god whispered. *Say them and rest. You have fought me for three years, little king. Your bones are tired. I have felt every hour of it.*

His bones *were* tired. That was the horror of it — the offer was honest. Every vision the god had shown him was real, or could be made real. A healed kingdom. An end to hunger. His children's names spoken without the taste of ash. All he had to do was stop fighting. Stop being Edmund. Let the crown be worn by something larger, and Edmund himself could dissolve into the warmth like sugar into wine.

Callowin lifted the First Crown high. Ten thousand throats inhaled as one.

*Yara,* Edmund thought, not a prayer, because prayer was the god's language now. Just a thought, thrown like a stone across a dark room. *Whenever you're ready.*

* * *

She was ready.

Edmund felt the change before anyone saw it — a wrongness in the hall's harmonics, a note out of tune beneath the chanting. The old rites of his order had been songs once, Fen had told him, songs sung by sword-bearers at the bedsides of dying kings. And somewhere below the dais, under the drone of Callowin's liturgy, a counter-song began.

No one else heard it. But the god heard it.

The black stone flared. Cold flooded Edmund's spine, and for one vertiginous moment he was looking out through ten thousand eyes at once — every kneeling courtier, every acolyte, every candle flame — and through all those eyes he saw what the god saw: a thread of light rising from the floor beside the dais, thin as spider silk, anchored in a woman's cupped hands.

*NO,* said the god, and the word went through the hall like a bell through glass.

The chanting faltered. Courtiers swayed. Somewhere a woman began to scream in ecstasy rather than fear, and then another, and the sound braided itself into something inhuman. Callowin's arms trembled; the First Crown rattled against its cushion.

*She is unmaking the chain,* the god snarled, and Edmund felt its attention split — most of it pouring downward toward Yara, a flood of cold intent, and the remainder clamping down on him like a fist. *Hold him. HOLD HIM.*

Edmund's body locked. His jaw clenched; his hands seized the throne's arms. He was a passenger in his own flesh, watching his own mouth open, watching his own voice come out in a register no human throat should hold:

"Kneel."

The word hit the hall like a physical blow. Hundreds dropped as one, foreheads to marble. Acolytes pressed their faces to the floor. Only two figures in all that golden space remained standing: Serah Vane, at the foot of the dais with her hand on her sword, and — below, small and steady — Yara, whose lips were moving, whose blood was already running.

He could feel the rite working. It felt like being unpicked. Stitch by stitch, the binding loosened — and with every stitch, the god's pain roared through him, vast and animal, and with every stitch Edmund felt something else: *room*. Air where there had been no air. Silence where the second heartbeat had lived. Three years of drowning, and suddenly his head was breaking surface.

*Stop her,* the god begged, and the begging was the sweetest sound Edmund had ever heard. *King. Beloved vessel. Stop her and I will give you back your children's voices in your dreams. I will let you remember them gently. I will—*

"They were never yours to bargain with," Edmund said through locked teeth.

*Then I will take everything.*

* * *

Serah Vane stood at the base of the dais with her blade drawn and did not move.

Edmund saw her clearly in that moment — saw her the way the god saw her, which was to say completely. He saw the ledger she'd carried across half the kingdom, the names crossed off one by one. He saw the salt cells and the prisoners. He saw the offer the god had made her in her dreams: high priestess, voice of the divine, certainty made flesh forever.

And he saw the crack in her, widening by the second. Because Vane had spent her life hunting god-touched criminals in the name of a silent heaven, and heaven had come back loud, and loud had turned out to mean *this* — a thousand kneeling bodies, a screaming infant, a king's mouth speaking in a voice that wasn't his.

"Vane." The god spoke through Edmund's mouth, honey over iron. "My faithful blade. The woman below is severing the covenant. Cut her down, and your name will be written in the sky."

Edmund screamed inside his own skull. *Don't. Don't listen. She's — Vane, listen to me, I am still in here—*

Whether any of it reached her, he couldn't say. What he saw was this: Serah Vane climbing the first step of the dais. Then the second. Her eyes were wet. Her sword-hand was steady, which was somehow worse.

*Yes,* the god purred. *Yes. Bring me her blood and the rite breaks. Blood freely spilled by a doubter — do you know what that buys me, little king? Another century. Another dynasty. Bring me—*

Vane reached the fourth step. Below, Yara's voice had risen into audibility now, the old words of unbinding rolling out in a language older than the kingdom, and the black stone was *cracking* — hairline fractures spreading across its face like frost across a window.

Vane raised her sword.

And lowered it.

The blade turned in her grip until the point faced the floor. She stood on the fourth step of the Golden Hall's dais, in front of ten thousand witnesses, and said — quietly, but in the silence the god's scream had left behind, everyone heard it:

"I was told the gods had returned." Her voice shook. "No one told me it would be *this*."

For one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat, Edmund felt hope — huge and stupid and human, hope like a sunrise.

Then the god stopped asking.

It moved through the crowd the way fire moves through oil. Three acolytes nearest the dais rose without expression, drew hidden knives, and turned toward Vane with the smooth synchrony of puppets. Behind them, dozens more stood. Dozens more behind them. The ecstatic screaming redoubled, and now it had words in it, and the words were the god's name, the name Edmund had spoken alone in his plague-dark room three years ago when his children were dying and heaven would not answer.

"No," Edmund breathed. "No — Vane, MOVE—"

She didn't move. Whether from shock or from some final, terrible clarity, she stood her ground as the first acolyte reached her — and Edmund watched, helpless behind his own eyes, as the knife went in below her collarbone.

Vane made no sound. She looked down at the blade in her chest with an expression almost scholarly, as though confirming a hypothesis. Then her knees folded, and she fell against the dais steps, and her blood ran bright and ordinary down seven steps of gold-veined marble.

The god drank the moment like wine. *Doubt ends,* it sighed. *All doubt ends.*

But the rite did not break.

Edmund felt it — felt Yara seize the opening the way she seized everything, ruthlessly, instantly, the smuggler's instinct for a gap in the guard. Vane's fallen blood had done nothing to stop the unbinding; if anything, the old words surged louder, and the cracks in the black stone widened, and light — true light, dawn-colored, nothing like the god's cold radiance — began to bleed from the fissures.

*What is she DOING—*

"She's doing what I asked her to," Edmund said aloud, and his voice was his own again, cracked and mortal and furious. "She's setting me free."

The god turned its whole vast attention on him then, and for the first time in three years, Edmund felt it afraid.

*You will die without me,* it said. *The binding holds your body together as much as I hold it. Break the chain and the king falls with the crown.*

"Then the king falls," Edmund said. On the steps below, Serah Vane was still breathing — shallow, ragged, alive. He fixed his eyes on her chest rising and falling, because he needed something human to look at, and because he wanted the god to see exactly what it had failed to kill. "I have been dying since the day I crowned you. At least this death is mine."

*Fool. Fool, fool — I offered you EVERYTHING—*

"You offered me everything," Edmund agreed, "except myself."

The black stone shattered.

There was no sound at first — just a bloom of silence so total that Edmund heard his own heart stumble. Then the light came, pouring upward through the broken diadem in Callowin's shaking hands, through the throne, through Edmund's ribs, and the chain — king to throne to hall to city to mountain — burned link by link, and something immense and starving howled beneath Thornfell with a voice that cracked the hall's great windows and sent ten thousand worshippers sprawling.

And in the middle of it, Edmund felt a small, fierce presence at the edge of his awareness — a woman on her knees below the dais, blood on her palms, holding the last thread of the song as the god tore at her the way a drowning man tears at his rescuer.

*Take my power instead,* the god shrieked at her, and Edmund heard the offer twist through the collapsing rite. *Your order restored. Your magic whole. Bind yourself in his place — you were always meant to be mine, oath-keeper, I have wanted you since—*

"Don't," Edmund gasped. "Yara — whatever it offers — DON'T—"

He couldn't see her face. The light was too bright. But he heard her answer, and it was not a shout; it was quiet, and clear, and it cut through the god's howling like a blade through silk:

"I am done surviving alone."

The last link broke.

The silence afterward was absolute. The candles guttered. Ten thousand people lay scattered across the marble like stars knocked from the sky, breathing, stirring, waking — and above them all, the First Crown rolled from the High Priest's nerveless hands and rang against the dais steps, and its black stone lay in pieces, and out of the pieces seeped nothing at all. Just smoke. Just ordinary smoke, rising toward an ordinary ceiling.

Edmund slid from the throne onto his knees.

His body hurt everywhere — everywhere and *less*, all at once, as though a weight he'd forgotten he carried had been cut away and left only the bruise of having borne it. His hands were his hands. His heartbeat was singular. When he opened his mouth, the breath that came out tasted of nothing but blood and candle smoke and morning.

Below the dais, Yara Sterling knelt in a spreading pool of dawn-colored light that was already fading, her hands open and empty, her face turned toward the ceiling as though listening to something very far away.

Or as though listening to nothing at all.

"Yara," Edmund said. His voice came out wrecked. He tried again. "Yara."

She didn't answer. The light died. The hall filled with groans and weeping and the first confused murmurings of ten thousand people waking from a three-year dream — and in the middle of it, Fen burst from the acolytes' ranks, followed by Osk limping hard on a bad leg, both of them running for the small grey figure on the floor.

Edmund got up. His legs nearly failed him. He took the seven steps down anyway, past Serah Vane's shallow breathing, past the shattered crown, past the High Priest weeping into his ruined hands.

He knelt beside Yara, and took her hand, and found it warm.

But when her eyes opened and found his, they were the eyes of someone standing in a house where all the furniture has been removed — and Edmund understood, with a grief that arrived ahead of his relief, that the god had told one truth amid all its lies.

Something had gone out of her. And no one yet knew what would remain.
