## 18. Unbinding

The Golden Hall had never been this quiet.

Yara felt the silence the way a diver feels pressure — a weight pressing in from all sides, filling her ears, her chest, the spaces between her heartbeats. Ten thousand courtiers knelt on marble that drank the light of a thousand candles and gave none of it back. Above them, beneath the vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of some long-dead king into some long-dead heaven, Edmund sat the throne with his hands flat on its arms and his spine straight as a drawn blade.

He was fighting. She could see it in the small things no one else would notice — the tremor in his left ring finger, the way his jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing back words that were not his own. The crown sat on his head and it did not gleam. It *drank*. Light went into it and nothing came out, and Yara understood with sudden, sickening clarity that she was looking at a mouth.

*Below the dais,* Fen's stolen rites had said. *Beneath the seat of power, for power pools where thrones are planted like stakes through hearts.*

She crouched in the shadow of the dais steps, chalk circle half-drawn around her knees, and tried not to think about how the last time she'd knelt on stone like this, she'd been seventeen years old and swearing away her life one syllable at a time.

The chalk line needed to close. Her hand wouldn't stop shaking.

"Yara." Osk's voice, low, from behind a pillar near the western doors. He'd taken up position there an hour ago, sword across his knees, playing the part of a bored guard so well that two actual guards had nodded to him. "Movement at the north entrance. Vane."

Yara didn't look up from the circle. "Is she armed?"

"She's Vane."

That was answer enough. Yara drew the last arc of chalk, and the moment the line closed — the smallest sound, chalk on marble, softer than a breath — the hall *changed*.

It was like a bell being struck inside her skull. The circle woke, and every warding script she'd copied from the Wandering Scriptorium flared pale gold, and above her — above them all — something noticed.

Edmund made a sound. It carried through the impossible silence of the hall, a raw, cracked noise that might have been her name, and then his head snapped back against the throne and the crown began to sing.

Not music. Not quite. A frequency, a hunger given voice, a note that lived below hearing and above endurance. The kneeling courtiers swayed as one body. Somewhere among them a woman began to laugh, high and ecstatic, and others joined her, and the laughter braided itself into the crown's song until Yara couldn't tell worship from screaming.

*Now,* she told herself. *Do it now.*

She pressed her palm to the center of the circle and spoke the first word of the Rite of Unbinding.

The old language came back to her the way a scar comes back in cold weather — unbidden, aching, hers. She hadn't spoken it aloud since the purging, since the night her order burned and she alone crawled out of the ashes with oaths still knotted through her bones. The words tasted like iron filings and mountain snow. They tasted like everything she'd lost.

The circle answered. Gold light climbed the dais steps in slow rivulets, pooling around the throne's base, and Edmund's body arched forward against invisible chains.

And the god turned its attention to her.

---

There was no transition. One moment she was kneeling in chalk; the next she stood in a place that was not a place — a vast dark shot through with veins of buried light, the under-mountain, the prison, the thing beneath Thornfell that had waited a century for someone foolish enough to come close. She knew it instantly for what it was because she had spent her whole life listening to gods, and this was the loudest voice she had ever heard.

*Little swordspeaker.*

The voice came from everywhere. It came from the chalk. It came from her own blood.

*You have grown thin,* it said, almost tenderly. *I remember when your order was fat with power. I remember your grandmother's grandmother kneeling where you kneel now, swearing herself to my sleeping brothers. They took her oath and gave her fire. What did they leave you? Scraps. Dregs. A candle burning down to the wick.*

"I know what they left me," Yara said aloud, though there was no mouth here, no throat, only the shape of speaking. "I've been living on scraps for fifteen years."

*And yet.* Something vast shifted in the dark, and she felt it smile without having a face. *You came anyway. You always come. That is what I love about your kind — the oath-bound. You walk toward the knife because someone once told you it was holy.*

The dark parted. And there — oh, there —

Her order stood before her.

Forty of them. Fifty. Every face she had memorized in grief and in guilt: Master Corren with his crooked smile, Sister Maren who'd taught her to sharpen a blade while singing, the twins Ash and Alder who used to race each other up the glacier walls. They wore their white-and-grey, their oath-cords bright at their throats, and they looked at her the way she had dreamed of being looked at for fifteen years.

Not with pity. With welcome.

"You died," Yara whispered. "I watched you die."

*Did you?* The god's amusement rippled through the false faces. *Or did you watch yourself survive, and call it death ever after? I can give them back to you, Yara Sterling. I hold every oath ever sworn in this kingdom. Every prayer. Every promise. I can unknot the purging from history's throat. Your order restored. Your power whole. No more scraps — a feast. You would be the last swordspeaker no longer. You would be the first of the new.*

The false-Master-Corren stepped forward and held out his hand, and his oath-cord glowed, and Yara's own cord — the phantom weight she'd carried at her throat since she was seventeen — burned in answer.

*All I ask,* the god said, *is a trade. The king is worn out. A fraying anchor, a chain nearly rusted through. But you — you are strong. You are oath-forged to the marrow. Bind yourself to me in his place. Sit the throne. Wear the crown. And your family walks free, your order lives again, and the kingdom gets a champion instead of a dying man. Everyone wins, little swordspeaker. Isn't that what you do? Save everyone?*

And the terrible thing — the thing she would carry for the rest of her life, however long or short it proved to be — was that for three full heartbeats, she wanted it.

She saw it whole: herself crowned, her people alive, Bramble safe in his kitchen, Fen with ink-stained fingers and no price on her head, Osk sleeping without nightmares, Edmund freed and hale and far away from her, alive, which was the only thing she'd promised him. Everyone saved. Everything paid for by the one person whose payment no one would miss, because Yara Sterling had been practicing self-destruction since before she could write her own name.

*You see?* the god murmured. *You see how easy—*

"No," Yara said.

The word fell into the dark like a stone into a well.

"It's easy," she said, "because I've done it my whole life. Survive alone. Pay alone. Bleed alone and call it duty." She looked at the false faces of her dead, and her heart broke cleanly, the way a bone breaks — awful, but true. "You're offering me my family, and you don't even understand that I already have one. You're offering me my order, and you don't understand that orders are just families with better posture. I am done surviving alone."

She reached down into herself, past the fear, past the wanting, down to the oldest knot in her — the oath, sworn at seventeen, syllable by syllable, to gods who slept and never heard.

*I am done surviving alone.*

"I break it," she said, and her voice was not loud, and it did not need to be. "Every word. Every binding. Every chain I forged into my own bones because I thought devotion meant disappearing. I take it back. All of it. Let it be broken."

---

The scream was hers.

She understood that distantly, the way you understand, in a falling dream, that the ground rushing up belongs to you. The oath did not leave quietly. Fifteen years of knotted power tore loose all at once — every ward she'd ever woven, every blade-song she'd ever sung, every cold morning her magic had kept frost from her fingers and every desperate night it had held a wound closed. It all ripped free together, and it went out of her *screaming*, a sound with no throat, a keening that bent the dark around it, and Yara screamed with it because there was no other way to let a thing like that pass through a body.

In the Golden Hall, the chalk circle blazed white.

The courtiers saw light erupt from beneath the dais and had no name for it. Osk saw Yara arch backward as if struck by lightning from below, and started forward, and was thrown back by a wall of force that sang the same unbearable note as the crown. Fen, pressed flat against a pillar with the rite's pages clutched to her chest, read the final lines aloud in a shaking voice because someone had to hold the words steady while Yara held everything else.

On the throne, Edmund felt the chains come loose.

It was not gentle. It was a hook leaving flesh. The god fought — of course it fought — pouring itself down the binding toward him in a last flood of visions: the healed kingdom, the green fields, the children laughing, *stay, stay, stay*. And Edmund, gaunt and flickering, gripped the arms of the throne and did the hardest thing a king can do.

He refused a miracle.

"Get *out*," he said — aloud, in his own voice, cracked and human and entirely his — and the crown shattered.

Not the metal. The metal held, a circlet of gold and old stones tumbling from his head and ringing on the marble like a dropped coin. What shattered was the binding, the hundred-year chain, the anchor — and the god beneath the mountain felt itself come loose from the world of men, and its howl shook dust from the Golden Hall's painted heavens and cracked the great windows and sent ten thousand kneeling courtiers sprawling.

Then it was gone. Fled downward, deeper than deep, back into its long imprisonment — diminished now, starving, but *unfed*, and a starving thing grows weaker, and a weaker thing sleeps longer.

Silence again. Real silence this time, ordinary and enormous.

Yara lay on the marble at the base of the dais, and she was empty.

Not hollow — she would learn the difference later, slowly, over years. Empty the way a house is empty after the fire is put out: scorched, smoking, but standing. Her hands were just hands. When she flexed her fingers, nothing answered. When she reached, instinctively, desperately, for the old cold current that had run through her since girlhood, she found only herself — muscle and breath and a heartbeat going very fast.

She started to laugh. It came out wet and ragged and it might have been crying, and probably was both.

"Yara." Edmund was off the throne, stumbling down the steps, crownless, catching himself on the rail like any mortal man. He dropped to his knees beside her. His face was terrible and beautiful — years younger and older at once, the flickering gone, only Edmund left behind the eyes. Only Edmund. "Yara, look at me. What did it cost? What did you—"

"Everything," she said. "It cost everything. And it was worth it, so don't you dare make that face."

He made the face anyway.

Footsteps — Osk first, limping, sword still drawn, sweeping the hall with a soldier's eyes even as tears cut tracks through the grime on his cheeks. Then Fen, dropping the rite-pages and sliding the last few feet on her knees to throw her arms around them both. Behind them, the western doors banged open and Bramble filled the frame, huge and soot-streaked and holding, absurdly, a cast-iron pan, because he had escaped custody four days ago and had apparently decided the weapon situation was a personal matter.

They gathered around her, all of them, and Yara lay on the cold marble of a palace she'd spent her life robbing, surrounded by the only treasure she'd ever smuggled successfully — and felt, for the first time in fifteen years, absolutely nothing answering her from the old dark places of the world.

No gods. No power. No oath.

Just them.

"Get me up," she said. "We're not done. Vane's still out there, and half the court thinks we just assassinated divinity." She gripped Edmund's sleeve and pulled herself sitting, and the world tilted, and Osk's hand caught her shoulder, steady as a wall. "And I want to see the sun. Is that too much to ask? One sunrise without a god in it."

Fen laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound. Bramble lowered the pan. And at the far end of the hall, past the scattered courtiers and the shattered windows and the golden circlet lying abandoned on the marble like a dead thing, Serah Vane rose slowly to her feet with blood on her temple and doubt written across her face like scripture she'd only just learned to read.

She looked at Yara. Yara looked back.

Neither of them drew a blade.

* * *

Outside, dawn was coming up over the capital, grey and ordinary and utterly godless, and it was the most beautiful thing Yara Sterling had ever seen.
