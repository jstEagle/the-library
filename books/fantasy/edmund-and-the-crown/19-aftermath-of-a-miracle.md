## 19. Aftermath of a Miracle

The silence was the first thing Fen noticed.

Not the silence of the hall — though the Golden Hall had gone quiet as a tomb after the screaming stopped, after the crown rolled from the dais and lay in a puddle of its own gold like something dead — but the silence inside her own head. For three years Fen had carried the archivist's toll, the missing memories, gaps in her childhood like teeth knocked out of a smile. She'd grown used to navigating around them. And now, standing in the wreckage of a god's death-throes, she realized the gaps were still there. She was still herself. The miracle hadn't fixed her. Nothing had been made whole today except, perhaps, a king.

Everything else was broken.

"Yara." Osk's voice, hoarse and cracked down the middle. He was on his knees beside her where she'd fallen, one hand pressed to her shoulder, the other still gripping his sword out of habit even though every soldier in the hall who might have needed killing was either dead or fleeing or kneeling with their faces to the floor. "Yara, look at me."

Fen pushed through the crowd — courtiers cowering behind overturned benches, priests of the new faith staring at their empty hands as if prayer were a language they'd suddenly forgotten — and dropped down on Yara's other side.

She looked small. That was the thing Fen couldn't get past. Yara Sterling, who had walked through the Thornfell passes like she owned every stone, who had stared down an inquisitor's blade and a starving god and King Edmund's despair without blinking — she looked like a girl who'd been hollowed out. Her eyes were open. They were the wrong color. Not wrong, exactly — still grey, still storm-grey — but all the silver light that had lived in them since Fen had known her, the swordspeaker's gleam, the old gods' reflection, was gone. Just eyes now. Just a woman's tired eyes looking up at a ceiling she couldn't seem to focus on.

"Can you hear me?" Osk asked.

"I can hear you," Yara said. Her voice sounded scraped raw. "I can't hear anything else."

Fen didn't understand at first. Then she did, and her stomach turned over.

The old gods. The sleeping gods, the distant ones, the ones whose silence had defined the last century — Yara had always been able to feel them, faintly, like heat from a banked fire. It was what made her a swordspeaker. It was what made her *her*. And now—

"Nothing," Yara whispered. She said it to the ceiling, to no one, to the space where a hundred voices had been. "It's not quiet like they're sleeping. It's quiet like they're *gone*."

"They're not gone." Edmund's voice. He was trying to stand, one hand braced against the throne itself, and failing, and Bramble was there to catch him — Bramble, freed barely two days, chains' bruises still ringed around his wrists, holding a king upright as easily as he'd hold a barrel of flour. "I felt it leave. All of it. Whatever that thing took from you — whatever you paid — I felt it go through me on its way out." His face was grey, gaunt, human in a way Fen had never seen him. No flicker behind his eyes. No second presence wearing his mouth like a glove. Just a man, exhausted and mortal and alive. "You're not empty. You're just—"

"Don't." Yara closed her eyes. "*Don't.*"

Edmund stopped. Fen watched him swallow whatever comfort he'd been about to offer, watched him kneel instead, slowly, on the other side of her, so that the four of them formed a rough circle around the most dangerous smuggler in the Thornfells while she lay on marble cold enough to see breath in, and wept without moving, without sound, tears sliding sideways into her hair.

Fen had seen people grieve before. She'd grieved herself, in ways she couldn't fully remember. But she had never seen anyone mourn a sense — a whole dimension of the world, amputated. Yara wasn't crying because it hurt, Fen thought. She was crying because she didn't know who was lying there. Because for twenty years, since the oath, since the purging, since the salt roads, *swordspeaker* had been the answer to the question of herself, and now the answer was gone and nobody knew the new one yet.

Bramble cleared his throat. It was the loudest gentle sound Fen had ever heard.

"There's a room behind the kitchens," he said. "Big palace like this, there's always a warm room near the fires. We're getting her off this floor if I have to carry her."

"You'll do no such thing," Yara managed.

"I absolutely will," Bramble said. "You weigh less than a sack of onions and twice as prickly."

And that — absurdly, impossibly — got a wet, ragged half-laugh out of her, which Fen decided counted as victory.

* * *

They installed her in a servant's chamber off the kitchen corridor because Yara refused, flatly, to be moved to any of the guest suites Edmund offered, and refused even more flatly to be moved into the royal apartments, and the argument had ended with Edmund throwing up his hands and saying, "Fine. The servant's room. Whatever keeps you under this roof tonight." Which, Fen reflected, watching him retreat down the corridor looking like a man who'd lost a battle and won a war in the same hour, was probably the healthiest exchange between those two that she'd ever witnessed.

The chamber was low-ceilinged and warm, smelling of bread and tallow, and within an hour Bramble had somehow commandeered a kettle, three loaves, and a pot of honey from a kitchen staff too stunned by the day's events to object to an armed cook annexing their pantry. Osk sat by the door with his wounds rebandaged, cleaning his sword in slow, meditative strokes — not because it needed cleaning, Fen knew, but because his hands needed somewhere to be. Fen sat cross-legged on the window seat with her satchel of scriptorium pages and pretended to study them.

She wasn't studying them. She was watching Yara sleep.

Or try to. Every few minutes Yara's eyes would open, unfocused, and her right hand would move — a small motion, fingers curling around the ghost-weight of a hilt — and then stop. Then her brow would crease, and she'd turn her face to the wall, and sleep would take her again until the next time. Like a sailor reaching for a rail that wasn't there anymore.

"You should eat something," Fen said softly, when Yara surfaced the fourth time.

"I'm fine."

"You've said 'fine' eleven times since the hall. I counted. Counting is sort of my whole thing."

Something flickered across Yara's face — annoyance, exhaustion, and underneath both, terribly, gratitude. "Eleven?"

"Twelve now."

"Fen." Yara pushed herself up against the wall. In the firelight, without the silver in her eyes, she looked older. Not worse. Older. Like a tree that had been struck by lightning and was still, somehow, standing. "What did it cost? The unbinding. You read the rite same as me. Tell me what it cost."

Fen set the pages down. This was the moment, she knew. The moment when someone finally said it aloud — the price Yara had hidden from all of them, the sacrifice she'd planned to make in secret like some martyr out of a ballad. Osk had gone very still by the door. Even Bramble had stopped fussing with the kettle.

"It cost everything," Fen said quietly. "That was the warning. An oath-breaker's blood and a willing vessel, and the power goes out of the swordspeaker entire. All of it. Forever." She met Yara's eyes. "You knew. You knew before we ever reached the scriptorium, and you let us walk into Vane's ambush and the god's jaws anyway, planning to pay it alone."

"I didn't plan anything," Yara said. "There was no other way."

"That's not true," Osk said from the door. His voice was level, but his knuckles were white on the scabbard. "We could have found another way. We could have looked longer. You decided for us. Again. Same as you've done every mile of every road since I met you — deciding we're cargo to be protected instead of crew to be consulted."

"Osk—" Bramble started.

"No. Let her answer." But Osk's anger collapsed almost as fast as it rose; he looked down at his bandaged side, at the wounds he'd taken on Hollow Bridge taking steel meant for Fen, and when he spoke again his voice had gone rough. "Damn it, Yara. You want to know why I came back? Why any of us keep coming back? Not for the coin. Not for the cause. Because you looked at us like we mattered, and none of us had ever been looked at like that before. And then you go and die for us without asking permission, like our mattering only runs one direction."

The room was silent. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"I'm sorry," Yara said at last. It seemed to cost her more than the rite had. "I don't — I've never done this part. The surviving-with-people part. I know how to survive alone. I've had twenty years of practice." She laughed, short and bitter. "Turns out I'm rubbish at everything else."

"Well," said Bramble, ladling soup into a bowl with the finality of a judge pronouncing sentence, "good news. You've got the rest of your life to practice, and four terrible teachers." He put the bowl in her hands and closed her fingers around it. "Eat."

She ate. Fen watched her take the first spoonful — watched her hands shake, just slightly, hands that had once spoken the language of blades — and understood with sudden clarity that this was what healing looked like at the beginning: ugly, trembling, refusing to be graceful. Nobody in the room said anything wise. Nobody needed to. Bramble refilled bowls. Osk finally put his sword down. Fen pulled out a blank page and, on impulse, wrote at the top of it in her careful scribe's hand: *Things Yara Is, Besides a Swordspeaker.*

Underneath, she wrote: *Stubborn. Brave. Terrible at accepting soup.*

"What are you writing?" Yara asked suspiciously.

"A list. It's going to be long. You may contribute."

"*Absolutely* not."

* * *

Vane came on the second day.

Fen saw her first through the infirmary window — Serah Vane walking the palace grounds like a ghost haunting a house she used to own, one arm bound tight against her ribs where the god's faithful had cut her down, her face bearing the particular expression of a person whose entire architecture of certainty has been demolished and who is still standing in the rubble trying to remember what floors are for.

"She shouldn't be here," Osk muttered. "Half the court wants her head for drawing her blade against the rite. Other half wants her sainted for it."

"Which half is winning?" Bramble asked.

"Ask me tomorrow."

Vane stopped beneath the window. Looked up. For a long moment the inquisitor and the runaway scribe regarded each other through old glass, and Fen felt the weight of that ledger — the ledger with all their names in it, the ledger that had hunted them across three provinces — and waited for hatred, or apology, or something.

What Vane did was nod. Once. Slowly. The nod of a woman acknowledging an opponent across a field she no longer understood. Then she walked on, and Fen lost sight of her among the colonnade's pillars, and found she couldn't decide whether to be afraid of her or sorry for her.

Probably both. Probably forever.

* * *

On the third evening, Edmund came to the kitchen corridor alone.

Fen was there fetching water and got cornered in the narrow passage before she could escape, which she suspected was deliberate; the king had learned quickly that approaching Yara directly required negotiating past three bodyguards who loved her too much to be intimidated by a crown he no longer wore.

"She'll see you," Fen said, before he could ask. "But I'm warning you — she's been teaching Bramble card games. She's in a *mood*."

"I've survived a starving god," Edmund said dryly. "I'll risk the cards."

He went in. Fen lingered by the door, ostensibly stacking wood, actually listening, and felt approximately zero guilt about it.

"Your majesty," Yara's voice, wry.

"Not anymore. Well — technically yes. Legally a nightmare of succession. But in this room, call me Edmund or I'll start calling you Lady Sterling and watch you suffer."

A pause. Then, reluctantly: "Edmund."

"How are you?"

"Ask me again in ten years."

Silence, then — the creak of a stool, the sound of a man sitting down heavily across from a woman who was learning how to be ordinary. When Edmund spoke again, his voice had changed. Lower. Careful.

"The council wants me to declare the unbinding a miracle. Divine intervention. They want to build a shrine on the spot where the crown fell." A dry pause. "I told them I'd consider it. What I wanted to tell them was that the only miracle in that hall was a smuggler who broke her own oath to save a man she'd known six weeks, and that if they build a shrine to anything it should be built to bad decisions made bravely."

"And what did you actually say?"

"I said I'd consider it." A beat. "Yara. About what you gave up—"

"Don't."

"—I'm not going to apologize for living," he continued steadily, "and I'm not going to thank you, because I know how thanks lands on you like an insult. So I'll say this instead. Whatever comes next — the kingdom, the council, the mess I have to clean up — there is a place in it for you. Not as my sword. Not as my champion. As—"

"As what?" Yara's voice had gone strange. Careful back.

"As whatever you decide to be," Edmund said. "That's rather the point. Take all the time you need to work out the answer. I'm told I'm patient now. Mortality does that."

More silence. Fen held her breath.

"...Ten years," Yara said finally, "is not the same as *no*, Edmund."

Fen grinned at the woodpile and fled before either of them heard her.

* * *

Late that night, Fen couldn't sleep, and went looking for the well of the palace courtyard because she'd always thought best beside water. Yara was already there.

Of course she was. Fen should have guessed. The woman treated sleep like an unreliable business partner.

She sat on the well's edge, wrapped in Bramble's spare cloak, looking up at the stars. Fen sat down beside her without asking. Neither spoke for a while. Below them the capital sprawled, candle-lit and confused, ten thousand people who'd prayed themselves into ecstatic servitude waking slowly into the hangover of freedom.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" Yara said at last. Her voice was quiet, stripped of all its armor. "It's not the power. I expected to miss the power. It's that I used to know — when I prayed, when I spoke to the blade, when I stood in the high places — I used to know the world was *listening*. Even asleep, even silent, the old gods were there, like weight behind a wall. And now when I reach out there's just..." She spread her hand toward the sky. "Sky. Beautiful, indifferent sky. Like losing hearing. Everyone tells you the world hasn't changed, and it hasn't, and it's completely different."

Fen thought about the gaps in her memory. The toll she'd paid in the Wandering Scriptorium. The pieces of herself she'd never get back.

"I don't think you lost who you are," she said. "I think you lost the easy answer to who you are. Those aren't the same thing." She pulled the folded page from her pocket — the list, grown to two pages now, contributions from Bramble (*Makes a mean stew, allegedly*) and Osk (*Would die for us. Needs to learn we'd rather she didn't*) and even one grudging entry from Edmund delivered via messenger (*Keeps her word, which is rarest of all*). "Besides. I'm keeping records. If you forget, I won't."

Yara read the list by starlight. Read it twice. When she handed it back, her eyes were wet again, but her mouth had curved despite itself.

"You're all going to be insufferable about this, aren't you."

"Devastatingly," Fen agreed.

Below them, in the dark city, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bells, not the rite bells, but the morning bells rung early by some zealous clerk, or perhaps just the wind. And Fen, watching Yara's face, saw her flinch toward the sound out of twenty years of habit — waiting to feel something answer, some old god stirring, some power leaning close —

—and nothing answered.

Nothing answered, and Yara Sterling sat on the edge of the well with her hands empty and her family beside her, breathing, breathing, breathing.

Then, from the direction of the council chambers, footsteps — running footsteps, and Osk's voice calling her name, urgent across the night: word had come from the eastern provinces, word that made no sense, word that would change everything again.

Yara stood. Rolled her shoulders like a woman picking up a pack for one more road.

"All right," she said, to the sky, to the silence, to no one and everyone. "Let's see who I am now."

And she went to find out.
