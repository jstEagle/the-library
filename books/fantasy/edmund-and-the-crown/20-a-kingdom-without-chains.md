## 20. A Kingdom Without Chains

The crown weighed nothing at all.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed on the morning of the abdication — not the absence of weight he had carried for eleven years, but the strange, hollow lightness of his own head, as if someone had scooped out everything behind his eyes and left only morning air. He stood before the mirror in his dressing chamber while a servant held the old crown of Aldermere on a cushion of white velvet, and he thought: *I used to be able to hear it breathing.*

Now there was only the servant's nervous breathing, and the creak of his own boots, and somewhere below, in the Golden Hall, the sound of a kingdom gathering to watch him break it.

"You needn't wear it at all, Majesty," the servant said. "The heralds say—"

"I know what the heralds say." Edmund turned from the mirror. His reflection looked thinner than he remembered, grayer at the temples, with a scar along his jaw that hadn't been there two years ago — a gift from a night he still could not fully account for, waking in a corridor with blood on his hands and no memory of whose. "But they're going to be looking for it. All of them. Every eye in that hall will go to my head the moment I speak, and when they find nothing there, they'll understand faster than any words could teach them."

He picked up the crown himself. It was lighter than it should have been — gold and iron and old enchantment, or what remained of the enchantment, which was mostly a smell now, like rain on hot stone, like something burned out. Beneath the dais where he would stand, sealed under three feet of granite and a century of ceremony, the unbinding scar still ached. Yara's work. The place where she had spoken herself empty.

He carried the crown in both hands, the way one carries a sleeping child, or a dead one.

* * *

The Golden Hall had been built by a king who wanted to feel small before his god, and for eleven years it had done its work on Edmund perfectly. The vaulted ceiling painted with the Ascension of Aldric the Anointed; the pillars carved with kneeling kings; the throne itself raised on seven steps so that whoever sat upon it looked down on every living soul in the room and up at the painted face of heaven. For eleven years Edmund had climbed those steps and felt the crown settle over him like a hand closing, and had told himself the pressure behind his eyes was the weight of duty.

It had been teeth.

Today the hall was full past capacity. Lords in their house colors, ambassadors from the coastal leagues, soldiers who had knelt in ecstatic prayer during the god's last flood of influence and now stood blinking in the aftermath like men waking from a fever, unsure how much of what they'd done had been theirs. In the gallery above sat commoners — Yara's doing, or rather her insistence, delivered in that flat smuggler's tone that made refusal feel like stupidity: *If you're going to renounce divine right, they should see your face while you do it. Not just the backs of their lords' necks.*

She was not in the hall. She had said she would be, and then she hadn't come, and Edmund found he didn't blame her. There were perhaps four people alive who understood what it cost to stand in this room, and she was one of them, and she had paid more than any of them.

The herald's voice rang out. "Edmund, fourth of his name, King of Aldermere and the Thornfell Marches, Shield of the Faith—"

"Enough," Edmund said quietly, and the herald stopped, because the whole hall had already gone silent at the sight of the king walking up the seven steps carrying his own crown like an offering.

He set it on the altar table beside the throne. Then he did something no king of Aldermere had done in the hall's four-hundred-year history.

He stepped down. All seven steps. Until he stood on the floor level with everyone else, and the throne rose empty behind him, and the painted god on the ceiling gazed down on a king who was, for the first time, simply a man among men.

"My lords. My people." His voice carried strangely well without the crown; he wondered if that, too, had been part of the binding — a voice amplified by something hungry, so that prayers spoken near the throne would carry all the way down through the mountain to the thing that ate them. "Eleven years ago I was crowned in this hall and told that my right to rule came down from heaven, through the crown, into me, and back out again into you. I believed it. I wanted to believe it. And when plague took my wife and my children inside a single winter, I wanted it so badly that I did the worst thing a man can do."

He let the silence stretch. Somewhere in the gallery a child coughed.

"I opened a door," Edmund said. "I invited something ancient and starving into the crown of this kingdom, because I could not bear a god who slept while my family died. And for years, that thing wore me. It answered prayers, yes. It healed crops, yes. It made this kingdom feel blessed again. And it fed on us. On our devotion. On our oaths. On me — most of all on me, because I was the anchor chain it had been denied for a century."

He saw faces in the crowd: disbelief, horror, and — worse, harder — recognition. Men and women who had prayed themselves into ecstasy during the renewal rites and were only now beginning to wonder what exactly had been praying back.

"The thing beneath Thornfell is dead," Edmund said. "Or as dead as such things get. It was unbound by a swordspeaker of the old order — the last of them — who gave up everything she was to do it. I will not name her here unless she permits it, because she has spent her whole life being hunted for what she is, and I will not make her a trophy for what she gave away." A breath. "What I will do is this."

He turned to the crown on the altar. It seemed smaller today. Ordinary. Just metal.

"This crown has been enchanted — bound to a power beneath the earth — since before the founding of this dynasty. Every law of succession written since rests on the claim that whoever wears it rules by divine grant. I am here to tell you the divine grant was a leash. And I am here to tell you that a kingdom does not need a leash to hold together. It needs laws that serve the governed. It needs a ruler who can be questioned, contradicted, and if necessary removed. It needs—" he almost smiled, thinking of a cook who recited recipes in chains, a scribe who paid memories like coin, an ex-soldier who took wounds for other people and called it temporary, "—it needs people who choose each other. Daily. Freely. That is the only covenant that has ever actually held anything together. Everything else was chains wearing the shape of love."

He lifted the crown one final time.

"Therefore, before this assembly, I renounce the doctrine of divine right. I strike it from law. No crown of Aldermere shall again be bound to any power, heavenly or otherwise. Authority flows from the consent of the governed or it is theft." He held the crown out at arm's length, over the open space between the altar and the floor, and for one suspended moment the entire hall held its breath —

— and he dropped it.

Gold struck marble. The sound rang up through the pillars, through the painted heavens, through four hundred years of kneeling kings, and it echoed and echoed, and nothing came down from the ceiling to punish him. Nothing stirred beneath the mountain. The god on the painted vault went on smiling its painted smile at a room that no longer belonged to it.

Edmund felt the last thread of something go slack inside his chest — some filament of the old binding he hadn't known still clung to him, some hook of the anchor chain finally slipping free. He swayed. Steadied himself against the altar. And laughed — a short, cracked, astonished laugh that rang through the Golden Hall louder than the crown had.

A lord near the front — old Duke Halloway, who had sworn oaths to three kings and served none of them well — sank slowly to one knee. Not in worship. Edmund saw that immediately, and the seeing nearly undid him. It was the kneel of a man acknowledging something new, something he didn't have words for yet.

Then others followed. Not all. Not in worship. But enough.

"No," Edmund said, and his voice broke on it. "Get up. All of you — get *up*. That's rather the point."

* * *

They got up. Slowly, awkwardly, some of them laughing in the bewildered way of people emerging from a long illness, and the hall filled with a noise Edmund had never heard in it before: conversation. Unscripted, unsanctioned, ordinary human talk, lords and commoners and soldiers turning to each other in the pews and galleries because for the first time in four centuries no presence overhead demanded silence and reverence.

He found his council chamber afterward full of people wanting things, which was, he supposed, what freedom smelled like. Ambassadors wanting clarification. Clerics wanting reassurance that the gods themselves weren't being outlawed — *no*, he told them, *only the claim of exclusive access* — and generals wanting to know who commanded the army now, which was a longer conversation.

It was dusk when the chamber finally emptied, and he stood at the window watching the capital kindle its lamps, and that was when the guard at the door cleared his throat and said, with the careful blankness of a man delivering a message he finds personally alarming:

"Majesty. There's a woman at the postern gate. Says she has business with the king. Wouldn't give a name." A pause. "She's got a cook with her. Big fellow. They're arguing about whether His Majesty eats enough."

Edmund closed his eyes and let himself smile for the first time all day.

"Show them to the small solar," he said. "And bring food. Whatever Bramble doesn't disapprove of."

* * *

The small solar was the room where Edmund did his real living — low-ceilinged, cluttered with maps and unwashed cups, nothing golden anywhere in it. Yara stood in the doorway of it looking like a woman who had walked into a trap and was deciding whether to be flattered.

Bramble was not so restrained. He marched past the king, surveyed the cold hearth, the empty bread board, the cup stained with wine that had clearly been poured and forgotten, and pronounced judgment: "You're eating like a besieged man, and there's no siege. Sit down. Both of you. I'll send up something that'll put color in your face, and if either of you leaves food on the plate I'll take it personal."

"Bramble," Yara said.

"He nearly died, is what happened. Nearly died and look at him, gray as ash." Bramble jabbed a finger at Edmund, who had the grace to look chastened. "The rite took it out of you too, don't think I don't know. You were chained to that thing eleven years. Nobody walks away from that clean." He pointed at Yara next. "And you — don't think I've forgotten about you either. Fen says you haven't slept proper in a week. Osk says you keep reaching for a sword that isn't there. I say what I always say: nobody survives on air and stubbornness." He fixed them both with a glare that had terrified bandits, inquisitors, and once, according to legend, a bear. "Food. Coming. Eat it." And he withdrew, satisfied, leaving a silence behind him that felt oddly warm.

"He's going to run your kitchens within the month," Yara said.

"He's welcome to them." Edmund gestured to a chair. She didn't take it. She leaned against the doorframe instead, arms crossed, and he recognized the posture — a woman keeping the exit in view. He'd seen it on her a hundred times, in a hundred camps and hideouts, and he understood now that it wasn't distrust of any particular room. It was the habit of a lifetime. "I'm glad you came."

"Fen bet me a week's wages you'd drop the crown instead of setting it down gently. Very theatrical of you. Now I owe Fen money."

"You watched?"

"From the gallery. In the back." Her mouth twitched. "You looked ridiculous standing on the floor with everyone else. Like a man who wandered into the wrong coronation."

"That was rather the feeling, yes."

"And then you dropped it." Something shifted in her voice — the wry armor thinning. "I kept waiting for it to matter. For the mountain to shake, or the ceiling to bleed, or something. Four hundred years of that thing sitting on this kingdom, and it ends with a *clank* on marble." She shook her head slowly. "I think that's the part I'll remember longest. How small it sounded."

"It sounded like a cup hitting a table," Edmund agreed. "Eleven years of my life, and the end of it sounded like washing up after dinner."

Yara looked at him then — really looked, the way she rarely let herself, the way she had by firelight on the mountain when neither of them asked the other to stop saying true things. "Why am I here, Edmund? If you want to thank me, a letter would do. If you want to give me a title, I'll throw it in the river. If you want—"

"I want to offer you a place," he said. "At my side."

Her arms tightened across her chest. "As your champion. Your swordspeaker. Your—"

"As my equal." He said it plainly, because anything else would have insulted them both. "Not champion. Not sword. Not the last oath-bound anything. Those days are done — yours by choice, mine by law as of this morning. I mean a seat at the table where decisions are made. Your voice, your vote, your veto. You know the roads of this kingdom better than any spymaster I have. You know its people — the ones who live outside the lamplight, the ones I failed for eleven years while something wore my face. I need someone beside me who remembers them. Who *is* them." He paused. "And I will not pretend the offer is entirely selfless. I have spent years being spoken through by something else. I would like, for whatever years remain to me, to be spoken *with*. By people who chose to be in the room."

The fire crackled. Down in the city, bells began ringing the evening hours — ordinary bells, rung by ordinary hands, and Edmund thought he would never tire of the sound of machinery instead of miracle.

Yara was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"I spent six years telling myself I worked alone. Six years pushing away every person who wouldn't stay pushed. And then a runaway scribe and a cook and a broken soldier dug their claws into me anyway, and I learned — late, badly, kicking the whole way — that alone was never survival. Alone was just dying with extra steps." She pushed off the doorframe. "So I'm not going to pretend I don't want to say yes. I want you to know that. I looked at you in that hall today and I thought: *there's a man building something worth guarding.* And I haven't wanted to guard anything in a very long time."

"But."

"But I don't know who I am yet." She spread her hands, and he saw them tremble slightly — the hands that had spoken mountains, that had severed a god, that were now just hands. "For twenty years, every choice I made was shaped by the oath. What I could do. What I owed. What I'd lose if I broke it. Now it's gone, and underneath it there's just... me. And I don't entirely trust her judgment yet. She fell in love with a king, after all. Clearly her judgment needs seasoning."

Edmund laughed despite himself, and something in her face eased at the sound.

"So I'm asking for time," she said. "Not forever. Not even much of it. But the crew and I are riding home through Thornfell next week — Bramble's got a sister in the lowlands he hasn't seen in nine years, Fen wants to write a book that'll get us all exiled, Osk limps better in mountain air. I want to walk the salt road again knowing what I know now. I want to find out who's left when the magic's gone." She met his eyes. "Ask me again in spring."

And Edmund — who had spent eleven years learning what it was to be taken, used, worn, consumed by something that could not wait, could not pause, could not bear an unanswered hunger for even a day — felt the difference between that and this land in his chest like sunlight.

"All right," he said. "Spring."

"Just like that?"

"Just like that." He smiled. "You taught me the value of patience on a frozen pass with three assassins shooting at us. I'm told I learn slowly, but I do learn."

Yara shook her head, but she was smiling too, and at the door she paused with her hand on the frame — not checking the exit this time, he realized. Just resting there.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the man who dropped the crown today? He's worth the wait."

Then she was gone, boots echoing down the stair, and Edmund stood alone in the small solar with the bells ringing and Bramble's stew arriving steaming and abundant, and outside his window the lamps of a free city kindled one by one across the darkening hills.

Spring, he thought. He could wait until spring.

He had, after all, finally learned how to live in time — and the first letter arrived from the mountains before the first snowmelt, bearing Fen's cramped handwriting and a single line that made Edmund read it four times:

*She practices saying yes in her sleep. Ask her soon.*
