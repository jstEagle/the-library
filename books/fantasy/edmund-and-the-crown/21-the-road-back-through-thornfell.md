## 21. The Road Back Through Thornfell

The pass looked smaller than Yara remembered.

That was the thing about roads, she thought, watching the morning light spill gold down the shoulders of Thornfell. They shrank when you stopped being afraid of them. The same switchbacks that had once seemed a gauntlet of teeth now rose gently ahead of them, worn smooth by centuries of salt carts and smugglers and pilgrims who had all, in their own ways, been running from something or toward something. Usually both.

She stood at the mouth of the pass with her hands loose at her sides — just hands now, calloused and scarred, one knuckle on the left still swollen from a door she'd broken three weeks ago in a palace that had no idea how lucky it had been. She flexed her fingers. Nothing answered. No hum beneath her skin, no whisper of steel remembering what it was forged for. Just the cold mountain air and the ordinary ache of a body that had worked hard its whole life.

It should have felt like grief. Some days it did.

But this morning, standing where the story had started — the same trailhead, the same crooked pine leaning out over the drop like an old woman eavesdropping — it mostly felt like quiet. A quiet she'd never earned before, because there had always been an oath humming under everything, a debt being collected in installments of power and loneliness.

"You're doing it again," Bramble said behind her.

"Doing what?"

"Standing like a woman in a ballad. All tragic profile and wind in your hair." He hefted his pack, which clinked suspiciously. "We've got four days of walking and I've got a stew to plan. Move."

Yara laughed — actually laughed, the sound surprising her on the way out — and turned up the trail.

* * *

They made camp the first night in the hollow below the old watchstone, the same place they'd sheltered the night Fen's prayer had moved a mountain. Yara felt it as they descended: a faint wrongness in the stone, a memory pressed into the rock like a fossil. The ledge where the cliff face had shifted to grant a runaway scribe safe passage was still there, a seam of raw granite running forty feet through weathered gray, too new, too clean, like a scar on a face that had otherwise lived a long and honest life.

Fen touched it as they passed, the way you'd touch a gravestone or a lover's cheek.

"I keep thinking I dreamed it," Fen said quietly.

"You didn't." Osk lowered himself onto a flat rock with the careful choreography of a man whose knee would never forgive him. He'd taken a blade at Hollow Bridge meant for Fen, and though the healers in the capital had done their work, some wounds wrote themselves permanently into the way a body moved. He limped now, but he limped contentedly, which Yara found herself cataloguing as one of the small miracles of the season. "I was there. I watched a mountain flinch."

"For me," Fen said. It wasn't quite a question.

"For someone," Yara said, and then, because the firelight was kind and lying felt heavier these days: "For you. Whatever answered you that night, it heard you. That matters."

Bramble, who had been unloading his cook kit with the reverence of a priest handling relics, snorted. "What matters is that we nearly died of terror and I dropped my best pot down a ravine. The gods owe me a pot."

"The gods don't owe anyone anything," Yara said. Then she paused, hearing the old bitterness in it, and tried the sentence again like a coat that might fit differently now. "Or maybe they do. Maybe that's the whole arrangement. Debts going both directions."

Bramble looked at her over the fire, flour on his knuckles and his eyes shrewd beneath his gruffness. He didn't say anything. He'd gotten very good, in the weeks since the salt cells, at saying nothing loudly.

Dinner was venison and roots and a flatbread that Bramble pronounced "adequate" and everyone else pronounced the finest meal of their lives, because it was hot and shared and none of them were prisoners or fugitives anymore, officially speaking. Fen wrote by firelight afterward, hunched over a journal that was getting fat with pages. Yara caught fragments over the rim of her cup — *the archivist's hands, the smell of the scriptorium, the memory I sold and can't find anymore* — and looked away, giving the privacy back.

Osk cleaned his blades, though there was no one left to fight. It was habit, Yara knew. Some men prayed; Osk honed steel and called it the same thing.

And Yara sat with her hands open in her lap and listened to the mountain breathe, and for the first time in twenty years, heard nothing underneath it but wind.

* * *

On the second day they met the traveler.

He came up the trail toward them in the late afternoon, a peddler by his pack and his dust, leading a mule loaded with tinware and dyed cloth. Ordinary. Unremarkable. The kind of man Yara would have marked and dismissed in a hundred heartbeats in her smuggling years — no threat, no coin worth taking, no story.

He stopped when he saw them, nodded the nod of the road, and then, instead of passing on, turned aside to a shrine Yara had walked past a hundred times without a second glance. It was a little thing, half-swallowed by the hillside: a niche in the rock, a candle stub, a bowl for offerings gone green with moss. One of ten thousand scattered through Thornfell, raised to gods whose names most people had forgotten, back when the gods still bothered to listen.

The peddler knelt. Took off his hat. And prayed.

Yara slowed without meaning to. Beside her, Fen went very still.

She couldn't hear the words — they were private, murmured, the kind of prayer people only make when they think no one is listening. Something about a daughter, maybe, from the shape of his shoulders. Something about coming home.

And then the air changed.

Not the way it had changed at Fen's first prayer — not the vast, grinding attention of something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the world. This was smaller. Kinder. Like warmth spreading through a cold room when someone lights a fire in the next chamber. The moss in the offering bowl brightened, just slightly, greening as if spring had leaned close for a moment. The candle stub, long dead, flickered once into flame and held. And the peddler's shoulders, which had been bowed under more than his pack, straightened.

He stayed kneeling a moment longer. Then he rose, wiped his eyes, put his hat back on, and led his mule past them with a smile he didn't seem to know he was wearing.

None of them spoke until he was well down the trail.

"That wasn't—" Fen started.

"No," Yara said.

"It wasn't *him*. The thing under the mountain. The crown-thing. It's—"

"Gone." Yara said it with certainty, because she had paid for the certainty with everything she had. She had felt the binding sever. She had felt the starving god torn loose from its anchor chain and cast back down into the deep dark where it had been imprisoned before kings were foolish enough to dig near its prison. "Whatever that was, it was something else."

"Something small," Osk said.

"Something kind," Fen said.

They stood there in the failing light, four people on a mountain road, watching a dead candle burn in a moss-grown shrine, and Yara felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the day she'd sworn her oath as a girl with blood on her palms and her whole order dying around her.

The old gods had slept for a century, and everyone had said they were dead. Then something hungry had woken wearing a crown, and everyone had said the gods were monsters. Both things had been true, and neither. The gods remained — changed, diminished maybe, or maybe just *different*, the way Yara herself was different. Smaller. Stripped down to whatever was real underneath the power.

Her hands were just hands. But they were shaking, just slightly, and she let them.

"Well," said Bramble, breaking the silence the way he broke everything, with practicality and a certain tenderness disguised as complaint. "If the shrines are lighting themselves again, someone should tell the lowland villages before they start a dozen new wars over whose god did it. Come on. Camp won't make itself, and I'm not cooking in the dark."

They walked on. Behind them, the candle burned on in its niche, steady as a promise.

* * *

The third night, Fen asked her about it.

They were on last watch together, the fire burned to embers, the other two asleep — Bramble snoring like a landslide, Osk sleeping the light sleep of a man who'd spent too many years on walls. Fen sat wrapped in a blanket, journal closed on their knees, and looked at Yara across the coals with that direct, disarming honesty that had made Yara love them against every wall she'd built.

"Do you miss it?" Fen asked. "The swordspeech."

Yara considered lying. It was her oldest skill, older even than the smuggling — the lie that kept people safe, kept them distant, kept her alone and alive. She'd broken a god with the truth. You'd think the habit would be easier to quit.

"Aye," she said. "Every hour. There was a — a rightness to it. When I drew steel and spoke, the world agreed with me. Everything made sense in the middle of a fight. Purpose like a spine." She turned her hands over, palm up, empty. "Now I draw steel and it's just steel. I swing and it's just swinging. Half the time I feel like I'm wearing a stranger's coat."

"And the other half?"

Yara smiled faintly. "The other half I remember that the coat has pockets. And that nobody's collecting rent on my soul."

Fen was quiet a moment. "When you broke the oath. In the hall. You said—" They hesitated. "*I am done surviving alone.*"

"I remember."

"Was that true? Or was that just what the rite needed?"

Yara looked at them — this runaway scribe she'd sworn she'd work without, this crew she'd insisted was temporary, this family she'd assembled by accident and defended with everything she had left. She thought about Edmund, waiting in the capital under a plain name, learning to be a king without a god in his head. She thought about the letter in her pack, unanswered, asking nothing except that she come see the spring.

"It was the truest thing I've ever said," Yara said. "Everything else was practice."

Fen nodded slowly, and opened the journal, and began to write. Yara watched the fire and let them.

After a while, Fen said, without looking up: "For the record. If you ever try to die alone for us again, we'll be extremely annoyed."

"Noted."

"We'll write ballads about how annoying it was. Bramble will compose verses. He rhymes worse than he cooks."

"Now that's a threat."

"It's the worst one we have." Fen's pen scratched on. "Use it wisely."

* * *

They came down out of the high pass on the fourth morning, into the green country where Thornfell softened into foothills and the salt road ran wide and easy toward the lowlands. Home, or near enough. The town where Vane had once hunted them was a day's walk east; the hidden caches and smugglers' doors of Yara's old trade lay scattered through these hills like buried teeth. She'd have to decide what to do with all of it — the routes, the contacts, the whole architecture of a life built for surviving alone.

Later. There was later now. That was the strange luxury of it.

Bramble was already talking about a kitchen. An actual one, with a roof, maybe above a tavern, maybe in the town, maybe — and here his voice went sly — somewhere with enough traffic that a certain retired novelist's crew could keep busy without anyone hanging anybody. Fen was talking about the scriptorium, about whether the Wandering Library might be persuaded to send copies of the Rites to the capital's scholars so no king ever again dug where he shouldn't. Osk said nothing, but he'd started whittling, and Yara recognized the shape emerging under his knife: a small wooden crown, crude and smiling, meant for a mantelpiece or a joke or both.

Yara walked at the rear, as always, watching her people move ahead of her down the road, and let herself feel the full weight of the strange, unbearable gift of it: that they were all alive, that they were all hers in the way that mattered, that no god had asked her to choose and no oath had priced the keeping of them.

Her hands swung at her sides. Just hands. Calloused, capable, empty.

Ahead, where the road bent around the last shoulder of the mountain, she could see the valley opening out — smoke from chimneys, a river catching light, the whole ordinary world waiting. And beyond it, far to the south, the direction of the capital, of a man learning to rule without chains, of a letter that deserved an answer written in her own hand rather than sent by courier.

Fen glanced back at her, grinning. "You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Standing like a woman in a ballad."

Yara laughed and lengthened her stride to catch up. "Shut up and walk, scribe. We've got a road to finish."

But as she fell in beside them — Bramble complaining, Fen scribbling, Osk limping his contented limp — she found herself looking back once, just once, at the pass behind them. At the crooked pine and the watchstone and the little shrine with its candle, small and stubborn and burning still, a pinprick of light in all that gray stone.

Something had answered a traveler's prayer up there. Something small and kind.

And Yara Sterling, last of the swordspeakers, first of absolutely nothing else, keeper of a kitchenless crew and an unanswered letter — Yara walked on toward the valley with her empty hands and her full heart, already composing in her own head the first honest words she'd written in twenty years:

*I'm coming. Give me until the thaw.*

She just had to figure out how to say it out loud.
