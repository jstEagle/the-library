## 22. Edmund and the Crown

The crown had not left its case in four months.

Edmund stood before it in the treasury's cold light, hands clasped behind his back like a man visiting a grave — which, in a way, he was. The gold sat on its velvet cushion, duller than he remembered, as if the metal itself had learned shame. Court jewelers had offered to melt it down a dozen times. He had refused each offer with a smile that fooled no one.

*Not yet,* he'd say. *Not until I understand what we buried.*

What they had buried was still breathing. He felt it sometimes at night, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards — the ghost of that vast and starving attention, gone quiet beneath Thornfell but not, he suspected, gone. Chains could be reforged. He intended to make sure no one ever held the links again.

"You're doing it again," said Fen from the doorway, arms crossed, ink-stained fingers drumming her elbow. She had refused every title he offered her — royal archivist, keeper of records, lady of the scriptorium — and settled instead for *the king's nuisance*, which came with a salary and no obligations whatsoever. "Standing there looking like you're about to apologize to an object."

"Perhaps I am."

"It's a hat, Edmund."

"It's a hat that ate a dynasty." He turned from the case. "It's a hat I invited in."

Fen's expression softened by a degree, which for Fen was practically weeping. "You invited it in because your children were dying and every priest in the capital told you the gods were asleep and prayer was just talking to the ceiling. You were desperate. Desperate men make bargains. That's not a sin, it's arithmetic."

"And what does the sum come to?"

She considered him. "You're alive. The kingdom's awake. Bramble has a bakery with a line around the block every morning. Osk trains the city watch and complains about their footwork daily. I have access to the greatest library in the world and someone to argue with." She shrugged. "I'd call it paid in full."

He laughed — the honest laugh now, the one that belonged only to him, that no god had ever worn. "You've been reading ledgers again. It's making you sentimental."

"I'm never sentimental. I'm accurate." She pushed off the doorframe. "There's a courier waiting. Salt road dispatches. And Edmund—" she paused at the threshold, not turning, "—she wrote back this time. All three words of it."

His heart did something undignified. "Which three?"

"Read it yourself, Your Majesty. I'm not your herald." But she was smiling when she went.

* * *

Yara woke before dawn because her body still kept smuggler's hours, and because Bramble's ovens had begun singing through the floorboards at half past four, a smell of yeast and woodsmoke that no honest person should have to sleep through.

She lay a while in the narrow bed above the bakery, listening to the mountain town come alive below her window: cart wheels on wet cobbles, the first argument of the day between the fishmonger and his wife, a child running somewhere with the particular thunder of a child who believes no one can hear them. Four months ago she had lain in beds like this one and felt nothing but the absence where her swordspeech used to live — a silence so total it had its own weight, like a missing tooth the tongue keeps finding.

Now the silence was just silence. Mostly.

She dressed, went down, took the plate Bramble slid across the counter without being asked — eggs, dark bread, a rasher, because Bramble believed grief and recovery were both best treated with pork — and ate standing up while he pretended not to study her face.

"You slept," he said.

"I slept."

"Dreamed?"

"No."

"Liar," he said comfortably, and turned back to his dough. "Courier comes through at midday. You going to be here, or are you going to find urgent business on the pass road again like last week?"

"The pass road is my business."

"Your business is sitting in a strongbox under the floorboards earning interest, because you refuse to spend coin or feelings, and both of those habits are going to kill you before old age does." He thumped the dough once, decisively. "I'm just saying. A woman can only walk to the salt road's edge and look north so many mornings before even the mules start gossiping."

Yara finished her eggs. "I don't look north."

"You stand facing north," Bramble said. "That's worse."

* * *

The letter, when Edmund finally opened it alone in his study, contained exactly three words in Yara's blunt, slanting hand:

*Come see yourself.*

He read it eleven times. He drafted six replies and burned five. The sixth said: *I will arrive at Thornfell by the salt road, plainly dressed, under the name Edric Fell. If you would rather I turn back at the pass, send no word and I will know.*

He sent it. Then he spent two days doing the work of a kingdom without divine right — which turned out to be mostly listening, a skill the crown had never required of him and which he found he was, to his own surprise, not terrible at. He heard the wool merchants out about the new tariffs. He sat with the delegation from the river towns while they shouted at each other and occasionally at him, and at the end of it the eldest of them, a woman with forearms like ship's cable, shook his hand and said, "First king in living memory who didn't already know what he was going to decide before we walked in." He thought of telling her that he'd had a year of something else deciding things for him, and how instructive that had been. He didn't. Some lessons you keep.

On the third morning he put on a plain gray coat, took a horse instead of a carriage, told the household he was inspecting the northern roads, and rode out through the gates before the courtiers could organize themselves into an escort. Vane met him at the gatehouse — Vane, who had survived, who carried the scar of the god's faithful across her ribs and a new and uncomfortable habit of asking questions — and looked at his saddlebags and his unmarked cloak and said nothing for a long moment.

"You'll want guards," she said.

"I'll want to arrive as myself. Whoever that is now."

"That's the trouble with taking off a crown," Vane said. "You find out what was underneath it. Sometimes it's smaller than you hoped." She stepped back from the stirrup. "Sometimes it isn't. Ride safe, Edric Fell."

He was three miles out before he realized she had used the false name without being told it, and that somehow this meant she had read Yara's letters too, and that he ought to be furious, and that instead he was laughing into the spring wind like a man released from prison.

* * *

Spring came late to Thornfell, the way it always did — arriving not all at once but in negotiations, snow retreating uphill by reluctant degrees, the passes opening one by one like fists unclenching. By the time the salt road was clear to the high shoulder, the meadows below were loud with meltwater and the first stubborn crocuses were pushing up through last year's ruin.

Yara walked the road's edge every morning now. She told herself it was patrol habit, route memory, the legs wanting what they'd wanted for fifteen years. Bramble's commentary notwithstanding, she did not think of it as waiting. Waiting implied expectation. She simply... faced north. There was a difference. There had to be a difference, or she'd have to admit that the last oath-bound swordspeaker in the world had traded one devotion for another, and she wasn't ready to name what she'd traded it for, because names made things real, and real things could be lost.

Osk found her there on the morning the rider crested the far switchback — a single figure on a single horse, moving at the unhurried pace of a man in no hurry to be anyone. Osk leaned on his stick beside her, his limp worse in damp weather, his silence easy as it had been since Hollow Bridge, since the wounds he'd taken that were meant for Fen, since the day he'd stopped pretending the crew was temporary.

"That him?" he asked.

"Could be a wool merchant."

"Wool merchants don't sit a horse like they learned to sit it in a palace yard." Osk scratched his jaw. "You want us to clear out? Give you the road?"

"They're mountains, Osk. You can't clear out of mountains."

"We can clear out of *this* bit of mountain." He patted her shoulder once, briefly, the way he did everything — efficiently, without ceremony, meaning it completely. "Bramble's packing a basket whether you ask him to or not. Fen's pretending she isn't watching from the scriptorium window. We'll be at the bakery. Come home when you're done choosing whatever it is you're choosing."

And he limped off down the track before she could answer, which was his way of loving people: leaving them room.

Yara stayed at the road's edge. The rider came on. The meltwater sang. Her hands — just hands now, scarred and steady and empty of anything but themselves — hung at her sides, and she let them.

* * *

Edmund saw her from a hundred yards and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

Not because she had changed — though she had, a little; the hollows under her cheekbones had filled in, and there was color in her face that the mountain winters hadn't given her, and her hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth that he suspected had once been part of a bakery awning. Not because of any transformation. Because she was *there*, exactly there, at the exact edge of the road where the salt traders' cairns began, and she had come to meet him not with a sword or a kneel or a petition but with her boots planted and her chin level and her whole self arranged like a sentence she had finally decided how to finish.

He dismounted while the horse was still walking, because his legs wanted ground under them for this.

"Edric Fell," he said.

"Is that who's arrived?"

"He's who's arrived. What's underneath is still under review."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the place where smiles started in her, the tectonic shift before the surface changed. "Under review," she repeated. "By whom?"

"A committee. It's very rigorous. Fen's on it."

"Fen would be." She looked at him a long moment, and he let himself be looked at — the gauntness gone from his face now too, mostly, the flicker long dead behind his eyes, just a tired man in a gray coat with road dust to his knees. "You rode alone."

"I rode as myself. It happened to be alone."

"Vane let you?"

"Vane called me by your false name without being told it, which means she's reading my correspondence, which means I've either got a security problem or the strangest blessing of my reign." He glanced up the road, at the cairns, at the white shoulders of Thornfell catching the morning. "I half expected to find the pass closed."

"I half expected to send word to close it." She said it flatly, honestly, the way she said everything now — because they had spent a winter learning that the other could survive the truth, and neither of them was willing to go back to the mercy of lies. "I didn't. Every morning I walked down here and I didn't. I couldn't tell you why."

"Yes, you could."

"Don't tell me what I could do, Your Majesty."

"*Edmund.*"

The name landed between them like a stone dropped in still water. She was quiet a moment. Below them the meltwater ran silver over the rocks, and somewhere upslope a lark was making its entire existence into sound.

"Edmund," she said, testing it, owning it. "All right. Here's my truth, since we deal in those now. For fifteen years my oath told me what I was. Sword of the sleeping gods, voice of the blade, last of the order. When the rite burned it out of me, I thought I'd lost myself. I grieved like a widow. And then—" she flexed her bare hands, turned them over, "—then spring came, and I noticed I was still here. Still walking this road. Still feeding Bramble's cat. Still teaching Osk's watch recruits how to hold a knife without cutting their own thumbs. Everything the oath was holding up turned out to be standing on its own." She looked at him. "I don't know what to call what I am now. But I know I chose to walk down here every morning, and I know I chose not to close the pass, and I know that when your horse came around that bend my heart did something no god ever made it do."

"What was that?"

"It waited," she said. "Hopeful. On its own authority."

* * *

They walked.

That was the whole of the afternoon, most of it — walking the lower salt road while the horse followed loose-reined behind, talking the way they had talked by firelight in the months when everything was at stake and therefore everything could finally be said. He told her about the treasury and the crown in its case, about the nights he still woke reaching for a voice that wasn't his. She told him about the silence where her power had been, and how it had slowly stopped being a wound and started being room — space in her life she hadn't known was occupied, now open for whatever she cared to put there.

"I'm commissioning a new crown," he said, as the light went long and gold across the valley. "Iron. Unadorned. Something a blacksmith could make in an afternoon, so that no one ever mistakes it for a god again. Maybe I won't wear it at all. Maybe it sits on the coronation chair and the kings stand next to it, servants of the thing instead of vessels of it."

"The court will riot."

"The court will adjust. Courts always do. It's their one true magic." He stopped at the bend where the road looked back down the valley — at the smoke of the town, the thread of the river, the small figure of Fen visible even at this distance, absolutely failing to pretend she wasn't watching from the scriptorium window. "Yara. I have to ask it plainly, because I promised myself I wouldn't circle it. Come to the capital. Not as champion. Not as sword. God knows — forgive the phrasing — god knows I've had enough of being served by sacrifice. Come as—"

"As what?" she asked, when he faltered. "Name it, Edmund. You taught me that. Names make things real."

He turned to face her fully. "As the person whose company I choose. Daily. The way you choose a road, knowing it, walking it, choosing it again tomorrow. I'm not offering you a title. I'm offering you Tuesday."

A laugh broke out of her — startled, unguarded, the kind she'd almost forgotten she owned. "Tuesday."

"And Wednesday. And the whole tedious, magnificent procession of them. I've had a god promise me a healed kingdom, Yara. It was a lie, and it nearly ate me alive. This is the opposite of that. This is a mortal man promising you nothing but his own continued, deliberate presence, renewed each morning for as long as you'll have it. No chains. No anchor. No binding." He spread his empty hands. "Just a door, left open, and a fool hoping you'll walk through it."

Below them, faint on the wind, came the smell of Bramble's evening bread, and the sound of Osk shouting at someone about footwork, and the bell of the little wayside shrine where travelers still paused — because the gods remained, changed and small and kind, answering prayers the way a neighbor answers a knock.

Yara looked at the road south, toward the capital and the iron crown that didn't exist yet. She looked north, at the pass where a hungry god slept under stone, watched now by wardens instead of worshippers. She looked at her own hands, which were just hands, and at the man in front of her, who was just a man, and who had ridden three hundred miles under a false name to be chosen rather than obeyed.

"Here's my counteroffer," she said. "I don't come to the capital. Not yet. The mountains are mine. My family is here, and I spent too many years working alone to leave them the week the bread got good." His face began to fall, and she caught it, caught him, one hand flat against his chest over the heart that beat for no one's kingdom but its own. "*But.* The salt road runs both directions. And I am told the king inspects the northern roads every spring."

"Does he," Edmund managed.

"So I hear. Terrible state of them, I'd wager. Gravel. Potholes. Whole sections washed out." She was smiling now, fully, the tectonic shift complete, sunrise breaking over country everyone had thought barren. "Someone will have to keep coming back to check."

"Every spring," he said hoarsely.

"And some summers. And possibly a few weeks in autumn, purely for administrative reasons." She stepped closer, close enough that the last light lay across them both like a benediction no god had to grant. "I'm done surviving alone, Edmund. I said it once with an oath-breaking in my mouth and a god screaming out of my bones. Now I get to say it in daylight, with nothing dying, and mean it twice as much."

He kissed her then, or she kissed him — afterward neither could say, and both agreed it didn't matter, because a choice freely renewed doesn't need an author, only two people willing to keep signing it.

Far below, Fen threw down her pen, announced "Finally" to an empty scriptorium, and went to help Bramble set two extra places at the table.

The lark sang. The meltwater ran. The road waited, open in both directions, and they took it home together — not subject and sovereign, not sword and crown, just two travelers who had learned the oldest lesson the mountains knew:

that devotion is not what you swear.

It's what you choose, daily, and choose again.
