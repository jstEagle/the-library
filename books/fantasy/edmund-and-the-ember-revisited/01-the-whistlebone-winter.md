## 1. The Whistlebone Winter

The snow came down on Hollowmere like a held breath finally released — slow, thick, indifferent — and Ada Ashgrove watched it from her workshop window with the particular satisfaction of a woman whose larder was full and whose door was shut.

Below the window, the village had gone soft-edged and quiet. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys in straight gray columns. The millpond wore its skin of ice without complaint. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it. Winter, Ada had long ago decided, was the only honest season. It told you exactly what it wanted: stay inside, keep your fire small, mind your own bones.

And she did. She minded them beautifully.

The femur lay across her workbench like something sleeping. It had arrived three weeks ago by carrier's cart, wrapped in oilcloth and accompanied by a letter written in a shaky hand: *My husband Torvald, died in the Kestrel Mine collapse, God rest him. They say you can make a thing that speaks with his voice. I have coin. I have patience. Please.*

Ada had read the letter twice, set it under the iron weight she used for such things, and gone out to feed her hens, because grief that has waited eleven months can wait another day while a woman sees to living creatures. But when she'd come back in, she had unwrapped the bone anyway, and there it was — a miner's thigh, yellowed to old ivory, dense as oak from a life of hauling and climbing and kneeling in the dark.

She could feel it already, even before the knife touched it. That was the gift, or the curse, or whatever name the villagers preferred this decade. Bone held what flesh let go. A life ran through a body like water through a channel, and when the body failed, the shape of the channel remained — every joy worn smooth into it, every terror cut deep. Most people walked past those shapes all their lives and heard nothing. Ada heard everything.

She had learned early not to flinch at it. Her grandmother, who had taught her the trade and most of her silences, used to say: *The dead don't shout, girl. They seep. You'll be carving a spoon and suddenly you're tasting a stranger's last supper.* Grandmother Ashgrove had also said never to take commissions from the powerful, but that lesson Ada had learned herself, later, at a price she did not itemize even in her own thoughts.

Tonight the fire was built up high and the kettle murmuring, and Ada tied back her hair, rolled her sleeves over forearms scarred faintly with old knife-lines, and began.

* * *

Carving a whistle from a femur was a two-night job done properly, and Ada always did things properly. First night was the hollowing: boring the marrow chamber clean, thinning the walls until they sang faintly when tapped, cutting the fipple and the sound-edge with a blade fine enough to shave hair. She worked by lamplight with the focused tenderness other women spent on infants, pausing now and then to hold the bone against her cheek, listening with her hands.

Torvald the miner came through slowly, the way the deep ones always did. Surface feelings first — irritation at his boots, fondness for some woman's bread, a dog named Bristle who had slept across his feet for nine years. These were the topsoil. Ada sifted through them gently, the way she'd been taught, taking nothing, disturbing nothing, letting the man's ordinary days pass through her like weather.

Then, deeper, where the marrow had been, she found the dark.

Not metaphorical dark. Actual dark — the black of the mine shaft, compressed into memory so hard it had texture. She felt the rock shift above him. Felt the dust come down like flour. Felt forty men go silent at once in a way no living silence ever is, because living silence still breathes.

Ada put down her knife. She pressed her palms flat on the bench and breathed, as her grandmother had taught her, counting the rafters: one beam, two beams, three. The dead were patient. She would be too.

When she picked the knife back up, the last words were waiting, and they were not what she expected.

She had expected a wife's name. A prayer, maybe. Men in collapsing mines usually spent their final moments on wives and prayers, and Ada had carved enough of these relics to know the inventory of dying by heart.

But Torvald Kestrel-born, in his last hour under the mountain, had been thinking about soldiers.

*They're moving through the southern pass,* the bone said, in the wordless way bones spoke — meaning arriving in her mind whole, like remembering something she'd never known. *I saw them from the high ledge before we went down. Carts. Too many carts for hunters, wrong-colored cloaks for tax-men. Gray and rust, gray and rust, marching north in winter when sensible armies sleep. And the ones leading them — the ones leading them didn't leave prints.*

Ada sat very still.

The fire ticked. Snow whispered against the shutters. In her chest, an old familiar coldness stirred and asked to be acknowledged, and she declined, as she had declined for years, to acknowledge it.

Because here was the trouble with her trade, the trouble she never explained to clients when they marveled at the little bone whistles and lockets and dice she made: the dead were terrible gossips and worse liars, but they were also, almost always, useless. They worried about harvests and inheritances and whether the neighbor's cow had been bewitched. They clung to grievances the size of acorns. In twenty years of hearing the dead, Ada could count on one hand the times any of them had known anything worth knowing about the world of the living.

And yet. *Gray and rust.* She turned the colors over in her mind. The Regent's regulars wore white and gold, all polish and parade. The free city militias wore whatever they liked, which was mostly brown. But the Regent's outriders — the ones the smuggler roads had learned to fear, the ones Brannoc had spat over the gunwale describing — the outriders wore gray and rust, dyed cheap on purpose so blood wouldn't show.

She looked at the half-finished whistle and felt the miner's urgency still pooled in it, eleven months stale and no less real. He hadn't understood what he'd seen. He'd only known it was wrong, the way a man knows weather turning. *Wrong, wrong, wrong,* the bone hummed against her fingers. *Tell someone. Tell someone. Tell—*

"Who?" Ada said aloud, to the empty workshop. "Tell who?"

Her voice sounded strange after hours of silence — rusty, smaller than she remembered. She cleared her throat and did not answer herself, because there was no answer. Tell the village council, who thought she was a useful oddity and nothing more? Tell the reeve, who took his orders from the provincial governor, who took his orders from Thornfell, from the crown, from the thing that sat wearing King Aldric's face and eating the kingdom one soul at a time — if half the stories were true, and Ada had stopped deciding whether they were true, because deciding either way invited attention?

No. She finished the whistle instead. That was the arrangement she had with the world: she made the thing, the grieving got their voice, and the knowledge stayed hers to carry, as it always stayed hers, as it piled up year over year in her chest like ballast.

* * *

On the second evening, she tuned it.

This was the delicate part, the part that separated a hearer's work from a carpenter's. Anyone could bore a hole in bone and get a noise. But a whistle made properly, made by hands that listened, held the dead the way a bell holds a strike — and when the right person blew it, the voice came through clear, not the rattle of random memory but the one message the soul had refused to put down.

Ada tested the sound-edge with a feather, shaved a curl off the lip, tested again. Outside, the blizzard that had threatened all day finally committed, wind driving snow against the shutters in long sighing gusts. Inside, the lamp burned steady. She raised the whistle to her lips — not to play it, never to play it herself if she could help it; hearing the dead directly was a debt that collected interest — and gave it one soft note, just to hear the pitch.

The note came out low and true, and along with it, faint as a smell, came the mine again: the dark, the dust, the wrongness on the southern pass. *Gray and rust. No prints in the snow.*

Ada lowered the whistle and looked at it for a long moment.

Then she did something she almost never did. She went to the shelf where she kept her own few possessions apart from tools — a chipped blue cup, her grandmother's awl, a bundle of letters tied with string — and took down a map. It was old, bought from a peddler years ago, ink faded to the color of weak tea. She spread it on the bench and weighted the corners with knife-handles.

Hollowmere sat in the middle, a dot among dots. South of it, the mountains shouldered up, and through them wound the southern pass — the Kestrel road, the miners called it, though no ore had come down it in years. Beyond the pass, farther south than the map bothered to be precise about, lay the borderlands, and beyond those, depending on which rumors you drank with your supper, lay the Regent's mustering grounds.

An army doesn't march north in winter, she thought. Armies wait for roads. Everyone knew that. Everyone except, apparently, whoever commanded gray-and-rust men who left no prints.

Ada stood over the map for a long time, and the loneliness of the room gathered around her shoulders like a shawl. This was the part no client ever saw, the part that made her trade a solitary one in fact and not merely habit: every whistle she finished left her holding a little more of the world's weight, and there was no one to hand it to. She had arranged her life precisely so. One woman, one workshop, one village at the edge of nowhere, commissions taken only from the grieving poor, prices kept low enough that the powerful wouldn't bother, distance kept from everyone so that no one could ever again be used against her. Peace, she called it. Careful, walled, well-stocked peace.

It was peace. She was nearly sure it was peace.

She rolled up the map. She would finish the whistle tomorrow, wrap it in wool, send it south to the widow with the shaky handwriting, and say nothing about soldiers, because what would the widow do with soldiers? What would anyone in Hollowmere do with soldiers?

The wind threw a handful of snow hard against the shutters, hard enough to rattle them, and Ada started — actually started, hand to chest, heart knocking — and laughed at herself, short and embarrassed, alone.

"Old woman," she told herself. "Jumping at weather."

She banked the fire, trimmed the lamp, and was reaching for the shutter latch to check the storm when she heard it.

At first she took it for the wind again — the blizzard had a hundred voices and most of them ugly. But this sound came from below the window, from the lane, and it was not wind. It was small, and rhythmic, and it stopped when the wind stopped, which wind never does.

Knocking. Faint, irregular, desperate. Someone — something — out in the white roar, hammering on her door.

Ada stood in the middle of her warm, ordered workshop with the finished whistle still in her hand, and every instinct she had spent twenty years building told her the same thing: *Do not open that door. Your peace is behind this latch. Whatever is out in that snow wants in, and nothing that wants in leaves your life the same size.*

The knocking came again. Weaker now. And beneath it — she couldn't say how she knew, only that the bones on her shelves seemed to lean toward the door like grass toward sun — beneath it she felt something she had never felt in all her years of handling the dead.

Silence. A walking silence. Something alive, coming up her path, around which every relic in her workshop had gone utterly, unnaturally quiet.

The knocking stopped. Then, faint through the wood, thinner than the wind, thinner than memory: a child's voice, speaking words in a language older than any kingdom, older than the bones on her shelves, older — some buried part of her whispered — than the Ember itself.

Ada's hand closed around the latch.
