## 2. A Knock in the Old Tongue

The blizzard came down off the Gray Teeth two days early, the way bad news always did.

Ada Ashgrove heard it coming before she saw it — not with her ears, which were ordinary enough, but through the half-finished whistle on her workbench. The miner's femur had been singing to her all morning, a low restless hum like wind over an open shaft, and when she pressed her thumb to the fresh-carved mouthpiece she caught the edge of it: cold coming, cold coming, close the door, girl, close the door. Dead men were terrible gossips and worse weather prophets, but they'd been right often enough that she trusted them over her own eyes.

She banked the fire, latched the shutters, and carried her water bucket in from the well while the sky was still merely gray instead of white. By the time the first flakes came sideways past her window, the workshop smelled of tallow and pine resin and the particular animal warmth of a small house sealed against winter — and of bone dust, always bone dust, fine as flour, worked so deep into the floorboards that no amount of sweeping would ever be rid of it. She had stopped trying years ago. A woman who carved the dead for a living learned to live among their leavings.

Her trade was simple to describe and impossible to explain. People brought her bones — a father's finger joint, a mother's rib, sometimes, if they could afford it, something larger — and she carved them into objects that held what the dead still wanted said. A locket that murmured a name. A comb that hummed a lullaby. A whistle, if the dead man had died badly and needed the world to know it. The church called it necromancy when it was feeling generous and witchcraft when it wasn't, but the mountain villages called it what it was: a service, paid for honestly, rendered quietly. Ada had one rule above all others, and she kept it painted on the inside of her own skull where no commission could reach it: *no lords, no priests, no powerful men.* The powerful didn't want messages from their dead. They wanted weapons. And she would not carve weapons out of grief.

So when the knock came — three blows, arrhythmic, wrong, more like something falling against the door than someone striking it — she was already halfway to regretting that she'd answered at all.

* * *

The child stood in the drifts like something the storm had coughed up.

Small. Seven, maybe eight winters, though it was hard to say; hunger and cold had sanded the girl down to essentials. Her hair was black and matted with ice, her lips had gone the blue-gray of old slate, and she wore a coat that had been made for someone twice her size, belted with rope, its hem frozen into a stiff skirt of rime. In one fist she clutched a knife with a blade worn to a sliver. In the other, held up toward Ada as though it were both offering and shield, hung a carved bone amulet on a leather cord.

Ada knew bone. She knew every kind the mountains gave up — ox, deer, wolf, the occasional bear, and human, human most of all. The amulet was none of these. It was dense as ivory and pale as milk, carved all over with spirals so fine they seemed to move when she looked at them directly, and it was old. Not old the way her grandmother's spoons were old. Old the way riverbeds were old.

"Gods' teeth," Ada said. "How far did you walk in this?"

The girl looked at her. And spoke.

The sounds meant nothing. They were nothing like the lowland tongue or the mountain dialect or the traders' pidgin Ada had picked up from years of ferry crossings. The vowels came from somewhere deep in the chest, round and resonant, and the consonants clicked and hissed in patterns that made Ada's back teeth ache, as though the language had been designed for a throat slightly different from any throat now living. It was beautiful. It was utterly incomprehensible. And some animal part of Ada's mind, the part that had kept her alive through twenty years of careful solitude, insisted that it was *old* — older than the road outside, older than the villages, older than the kingdom whose coins she saved in a clay jar.

"I don't understand you," Ada said slowly. "Do you understand me? Nod if you understand me."

The girl nodded once, precise as a clockwork. Then she pointed at herself, then at the amulet, then — with a hand that shook violently, whether from cold or fear Ada couldn't tell — back at the dark wall of the blizzard behind her. Then she made a sound that needed no translation at all: a thin, cracked repetition of the same word, over and over, the way children say *please* when please is the only word they have left.

Ada should have shut the door. Every sensible instinct said shut the door. A feral child appearing out of a killing storm speaking a dead language was not misfortune; it was a story, and stories that began this way ended with soldiers, or priests, or worse. Ada had built her whole life on the principle that other people's catastrophes were contagious, and the only cure was distance.

But the girl's lips were slate-blue, and her knees buckled even as Ada watched, and there was a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — when the child swayed and Ada's hand shot out on its own authority and caught her by the coat.

That was how it started. That was how it always started, she would think later. With the hands deciding before the head agreed.

She hauled the girl inside, got the door barred against the wind, and set about the business of not letting a child die on her floor. Blankets from the chest. The girl's boots off — her feet were white and waxy at the toes, not yet black, thank every merciful power — wrapped in wool warmed at the hearth. Melted snow, then broth, a spoonful at a time, because Ada knew better than to give a freezing body hot food too fast. The girl endured all of it with a strange, watchful stillness, her eyes tracking every movement, and she never once let go of the amulet. When Ada tried to ease it over her head so she could examine the frostbitten skin beneath, the child's whole body went rigid as a drawn bow, and she bared her teeth — actually bared them, a small feral animal in a blanket nest — until Ada raised her hands and stepped back.

"All right," Ada said. "Keep your bone. Keep your knife too, if it makes you feel safer. I've been threatened by better."

The girl slept eventually, curled on the pallet near the fire like a comma of blankets and hair, and Ada sat across the room with her whittling knife forgotten in her lap and watched her breathe.

* * *

It was near midnight when Ada finally gave in to the thing that had been pulling at her since the door opened.

She told herself it was professional habit. She told herself she only wanted to identify the amulet's material, because a carver who couldn't name her stock was no carver at all. She told herself a great many things, standing over the sleeping child in the firelight, and then she reached out and touched the carved bone with one fingertip.

Every voice she had ever heard went silent at once.

Ada had lived inside a crowd for twenty years. That was the truth of her gift that no one understood, the truth she never told clients: hearing the dead was not hearing a voice here and there when she touched a relic. It was hearing *all of them*, always, a vast murmuring congregation layered beneath the world like water under ice — the miner in the unfinished whistle, the grandmother in the locket on her shelf, the hundreds of bones she'd carved and given away and lost track of, and beyond them, fainter, the great slow chorus of the buried earth itself, the mountain's dead, the valley's dead, generations upon generations whispering at the edge of comprehension. She had learned to sleep inside it. She had learned to find its constancy comforting, the way other people found silence comforting. She had mistaken it, if she was honest, for company.

And then a fingertip touched a piece of carved bone older than kingdoms, and the whole congregation stopped mid-breath.

Not faded. Not quieted. *Stopped* — the way a room stops when a stranger walks in. The silence had texture. It had weight. It pressed against her eardrums like deep water, and underneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, Ada felt something looking at her. Not the girl. Something that lived in the amulet, or behind it, or through it — something patient and enormous that had noticed her noticing, the way you notice a spider when it moves on the wall.

Ada snatched her hand back and stumbled into the workbench. A chisel clattered to the floor. The girl did not wake.

For a long time Ada stood in the firelight with her heart slamming and listened to the silence where her whole life used to be. It was the loneliest sound she had ever heard. Worse than loneliness — it was the sound of loneliness *revealed*, the curtain pulled back showing her that all those years of murmuring company had been exactly as empty as everyone had always said, and she had built a peace on top of a haunting and called it a home.

Then, slowly, reluctantly, like a congregation settling back into its pews after a shock, the voices returned. First the miner's femur, humming its low uneasy note. Then the shelf of finished pieces, each muttering its small message. Then the deeper chorus, the buried thousands, resuming their endless half-heard psalm. Everything as it had been. Everything as it would always be.

Except that now Ada knew the silence had a shape. And the shape had been wearing a child.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat by the window with a lamp turned low and watched the blizzard erase the world, and she thought about the rule she kept painted inside her skull. *No lords, no priests, no powerful men.* It had served her well because it was really a simpler rule wearing finer clothes: *nothing that belongs to the powerful.* No commissions from them, no debts to them, no entanglements. Stay small. Stay dull. Stay beneath notice until the ground itself forgot you were there.

The girl on the pallet was wearing a coat cut for a grown man of means. Her boots, though ruined, had been good boots once, stitched by a craftsman. And the amulet —

Ada made herself look at it again from across the room, without touching, and the spirals seemed to turn in the firelight like slow water going down a drain.

Nobody gave an heirloom like that to a beggar child. Either the child had stolen it from someone very important, or someone very important had given it to her for safekeeping, or — and this was the thought Ada kept turning over like a stone looking for what lived under it — the child hadn't been sent away from the powerful at all. She'd been *sent*. Delivered. Thrown into the storm like a coin into a well, aimed at the one house in the mountains where a woman could hear what bone had to say.

But that was madness. Who would know to aim a child at her? She was nobody. She had spent fifteen years and considerable effort becoming nobody.

Outside, the wind screamed down from the pass and the snow came so thick the lamplight couldn't pierce it. Somewhere in that white roar were wolves, and cold, and whatever the child had walked away from. Ada looked at the small shape under the blankets — the hollow cheeks, the rope belt, the fist that even in sleep stayed curled around the carved bone — and felt the first thin wire of something she had deliberately not felt in years, something that pulled in her chest like a stitch being drawn tight.

Fear sharpening, she realized, into something else. Something with edges. Something that might, if she wasn't careful, turn out to be wonder.

"You'd better not be trouble," she told the sleeping girl, knowing full well that anything delivered by a blizzard, speaking the tongue of dead kingdoms, wearing an amulet that silenced the grave itself, was trouble of the purest and most concentrated kind. "You'd better be nothing at all."

The girl stirred. Her eyes opened — dark, clear, unnervingly calm — and fixed on Ada across the firelit room. And in the old tongue, soft and unhurried, as though continuing a conversation they had already been having for years, she said a single sentence.

Ada didn't know the words. But she knew bone, and she knew voices, and she knew — with a certainty that settled over her colder than the storm — that the child had just spoken her name.

*Ashgrove,* the girl had said. *Ada Ashgrove.*

The wind threw itself against the shutters. On the workbench, the unfinished whistle began, very softly, to sing.
