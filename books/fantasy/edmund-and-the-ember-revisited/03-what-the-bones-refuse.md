## 3. What the Bones Refuse

The girl slept for two days.

Ada had known feral things before — half-starved dogs, a hawk with a broken wing she'd once tried to nurse through a hard winter — and the first rule was the same for all of them: do not crowd. So she kept her distance, set out bread and salted fish on a stool by the hearth, and let Wren come to it in her own time, which was usually at night, when she thought Ada wasn't watching. The girl ate like something that had learned hunger as a language before it learned any other. She ate with her hands. She ate watching the door.

By the third morning, the blizzard had spent itself against the mountains and the light came down off the peaks so clean and white that it hurt to look at. Wren stood at the window with her palms flat against the glass, watching the drifts, and when she turned to look at Ada there was something almost like a question in her face.

"You want to go out," Ada said.

Wren tilted her head. The gesture was birdlike, deliberate, and Ada had begun to understand it meant *say that again* or sometimes *I don't have words for what you said.* The girl's own tongue — that old, rolling speech that had come out of her like water from a cracked jar the night she arrived — did not seem to share a single root with anything Ada knew. Not the lowland trade-speech, not the mountain dialects, not even the grave-tongue phrases that surfaced now and then in the relics she carved, fragments of dead men's prayers.

Ada dressed her in wool that hung off her like a scarecrow's castoffs, wrapped her feet in two pairs of socks and oiled leather, and took her out into the blue-white morning.

What happened next, Ada would think about for years afterward: Wren walked to the woodpile, knelt in the snow, and picked up the antler shed by the stag that wintered on the ridge above the house. She held it in both hands the way a priest holds a relic. And she began, very softly, to hum.

The sound went through Ada like a fishhook through the soft part of the palm.

Because she knew that hum. It was the sound the bones made for her — not with her ears, never with her ears, but somewhere behind them, in the place where hearing became memory. The dead spoke to her through worked bone and unworked alike, but they *hummed* only when they were at peace, when whatever they'd needed to say had been said and carved true. And here was this child, this impossible half-frozen child, kneeling in the snow, humming the peace-song of the dead back to an antler that had never been carved at all.

"Put it down," Ada said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

Wren looked up. Snowflakes were melting in her lashes. She put the antler down gently, the way you'd lay a sleeping infant back into its cradle, and followed Ada inside without being told.

That was the moment Ada stopped telling herself the girl was just a lost child who'd wandered too far from wherever children like her came from. That was the moment the fear started — quiet, patient fear, the kind that sits down across from you at the table and waits.

* * *

She lasted four more days before she broke her own rule.

It wasn't a rule she'd ever spoken aloud. It lived in her hands instead, in the way they always paused over a piece of bone before the knife touched it — a pause long enough to ask, *do I want to know?* Because knowing was the trade. Knowing was what people paid her for, and what she charged dearly for, because every knowing cost her something she couldn't name and couldn't get back. A miner's last breath. A drowned girl's rage at the river. Forty years of a shepherd's small regrets, all of them about his brother. She carried them the way a pack-mule carries ore: without complaint, and heavier every year.

And she had one rule above all the others, older than her trade, older than her solitude: *never carve for the powerful.* Kings and lords and merchant-princes all wanted the same thing — the dead made useful, secrets pried loose, rivals' fathers interrogated from beyond the grave. She'd watched what happened to the last carver who'd taken a commission from a border lord. Watched from far away, thankfully, which was the whole point of living up here where the roads forgot to go.

But Wren was not powerful. Wren was a child who ate with her hands and slept curled against the cold stones of the hearth like a coal trying to keep its heat. And Ada told herself, as she selected the piece of deer rib from her working box — plain, unremarkable, nothing of anyone's death in it, just material — that this wasn't a commission at all. This was triage. You had to know what was wrong with a patient before you could help them.

The girl sat across the workbench from her, watching the knife with total attention. Ada had learned in the past week that Wren watched everything that way — fire, snow, the knife, Ada's own hands — as if memorizing the world in case it needed to be recited later.

"I'm going to try something," Ada said. "I don't know if it will hurt. If it does, tell me to stop." She didn't expect to be understood. She said it anyway, because some part of speaking aloud was for herself.

She set the rib on the bench. She laid her palm flat beside it, the way she always did, opening the door in herself that she'd spent thirty years learning to open exactly wide enough.

And every dead voice she had ever heard went silent at once.

Not faded. Not hushed. *Silent* — the way a room goes silent when someone important walks into it. Thirty years of murmuring, the constant low tide of the dead that Ada had long since stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat — gone. All of it. In the space of a single heartbeat, Ada Vasquez was deaf in the only way that had ever mattered to her, and the silence was so vast and so total that she made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from her own throat.

Then the vision took her.

* * *

There is a vault. She knows it is a vault the way you know things in dreams — absolutely, without evidence. It is deep, deeper than mines go, cut into the black roots of a mountain, and its walls are not stone. They are *bone*, fused and grown together, femurs and ribs and skulls interlocked in patterns too regular to be accident and too vast to be architecture. The ceiling arches away into darkness overhead. The floor is warm. She can feel the warmth through the soles of her feet, a warmth with a pulse in it, slow and enormous, like standing on the chest of something sleeping.

In the center of the vault burns the Ember.

It is not fire. Fire is a child's drawing of this thing. It is a knot of light the size of a heart, and it is made of — she understands this with sickening clarity — *people*. Souls packed and pressed and bound until their light has nowhere left to go but inward, burning toward their own centers forever. She can almost hear them. Almost. They are screaming in a register below sound, and the scream has been going on so long that it has become a kind of music, and the music is the binding, and the binding holds—

Holds *what*. Her dream-eye turns, dragged, and sees the throne.

A man sits on it. Or the shape of a man. He wears a crown of black iron and his face is — Ada's mind flinches away and the vision drags it back, merciless — his face is *hungry*. Not gaunt. Hungry, the way a well is hungry, the way a grave is hungry. He is leaning forward from the throne with his mouth open, and the light of the Ember flows toward him in a slow golden river, and he drinks it, and where the light enters him nothing accumulates. He does not grow brighter. He does not grow stronger. He grows *emptier*, and the emptiness pulls harder, and the souls in the Ember burn faster to fill him, and none of it is ever enough.

And kneeling before the throne, small and straight-backed, her dark hair unbound, is Wren.

The girl is not afraid. That is the worst of it. She kneels the way iron kneels to a lodestone, inevitable, and the crowned thing looks down at her and its hollow face does something that might once have been a smile, and it says — in the old tongue, the rolling water-language, and Ada understands it perfectly though she has never learned a word of it —

*At last. The key comes home to its lock.*

The vault begins to burn. Not the Ember — the walls, the bone-walls, catching alight from somewhere Ada cannot see, and the souls in the walls are waking and screaming now in registers she *can* hear, and the crowned figure rises from the throne still drinking, drinking, drinking the light as everything around him goes to ash, and Wren turns her head and looks directly at Ada — at *her*, through the vision, through the years, through whatever distance lies between a mountain workshop and the roots of the world —

— and says, in the trade-speech, in a child's clear voice: *"You're carving me wrong."*

* * *

Ada came back to herself with the knife on the floor and blood on her palm. She'd gripped the deer rib so hard it had snapped, and one edge had opened her hand along the lifeline. She didn't remember pain. She remembered the vault's warmth, and the smile, and the sentence she couldn't stop hearing.

*You're carving me wrong.*

Wren was still sitting across the bench. She hadn't moved. But her eyes were wet, and her small hands were pressed flat against her sternum as if holding something shut, and when she saw Ada looking at her she shook her head — slowly, once, side to side — in a way that was unmistakably human and unmistakably frightened.

"You saw it too," Ada said hoarsely. "Whatever that was. You saw it."

Wren's mouth worked. No sound came. Then, in a whisper that was barely voiced at all, she produced three syllables of the old tongue — the same three syllables, Ada realized with a chill, that she'd hummed to the antler in the snow. The peace-song. Except sung now it didn't sound like peace. It sounded like a warning repeated so many times it had worn smooth.

The girl pointed at the broken rib on the floor. Then at her own chest. Then she drew her hand across her throat, gently, and shook her head again.

*Don't ask the bones about me,* the gesture said, as plainly as speech. *They won't tell you. And it will hurt them.*

Ada bandaged her hand. She fed the stove. She did all the small domestic things that keep a body moving while the mind runs in circles, and all the while the vision replayed behind her eyes: the vault, the throne, the river of light flowing into that bottomless mouth. She was not a woman who believed in omens. She was a woman who believed in bone, and bone had just shown her the inside of a nightmare and refused to explain itself.

The dead had never refused her anything. That was the part that frightened her most. In thirty years, every relic she'd touched had spoken — reluctantly, angrily, sorrowfully, but always. Whatever Wren was, she stood between Ada and the dead like a wall stands between a house and the wind. And walls, Ada knew, were built by someone, for a purpose, and no one builds a wall around a door unless they mean to use the door.

*The key comes home to its lock.*

She looked at the girl, who had fallen asleep sitting up, chin on chest, exhausted by whatever the vision had cost her. Small. Hollow-cheeked. Seven years old, maybe eight. Someone had made this child, the vision said. Someone had forged her the way Ada forged a whistle — deliberately, for a function — and the function was written in black iron crowns and rivers of stolen light.

Ada had spent her whole life perfecting one skill: staying out of stories like this. She lived above the treeline for a reason. She charged ruinous prices for a reason. She had buried her gift under routine and her heart under habit, and it had worked, it had *worked*, for thirty unbroken years.

Stay hidden, said every instinct she owned. Take the girl over the pass in spring, leave her at a shrine, let the world solve its own nightmares.

But the vision had shown her the Ember's souls, and she had *almost heard them*, and the almost was going to live under her skin for the rest of her life if she let it.

* * *

The rider came at dusk two days later.

Ada heard the horse before she saw it — no dead voice in that, just ordinary ears, ordinary caution — and she moved Wren behind the flour barrels with a finger to her lips before the knock came. The girl, to Ada's surprise and unease, understood immediately. She folded herself into the shadow as if hiding were a craft she'd been trained in.

Through the shutter-crack Ada watched a courier in Regent's colors — black and gold, the colors that had crept southward across the maps these last three years like ink through wet cloth — nail a notice to her gatepost, glance at the smoke rising from her chimney, and ride on. He didn't knock. That was somehow worse. The Regent's men didn't need to knock anymore; the notice did their knocking for them.

She waited until full dark to fetch it. The paper was heavy stock, official, printed rather than handwritten — which meant many copies, which meant many gates, which meant the net was already wide.

She read it twice by lamplight, Wren asleep against her side whether Ada willed it or not.

**BY ORDER OF THE REGENT'S VOICE:** A child is sought, female, aged seven to nine years, dark of hair, speaking no known tongue. She may travel under protection of others, deceived or coerced. She is called **THE KEY**, and she belongs to the Crown. Any who shelter her commit treason against the realm's salvation. Reward: five hundred crowns, and pardon for all prior offenses. Report to any garrison, any ferry-master, any priest.

*Any ferry-master.*

Ada read that line a third time and felt the cold walk up her spine on small feet. Brannoc. The thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome, inevitable: *Brannoc, who owes me, whom I owe, whose ferry is the only way north before the passes close.* The Regent's men would already have talked to him. Maybe they'd talked to him weeks ago.

Five hundred crowns. Pardon for all offenses. She looked down at the sleeping weight against her ribs, at the small chapped hands, at the amulet of carved bone resting at the girl's throat — the one thing she'd carried through the blizzard, the one thing Ada had not yet dared to touch.

The fire settled. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the old tongue, like water over stones, like a warning worn smooth by repetition.

Ada folded the notice into quarters, and then eighths, and held the little black-and-gold square over the candle flame until it was ash. Treason, then. The word should have terrified her. What terrified her instead was how easily it fit — how the shape of her life, all those careful years of isolation, seemed to have been a mold all along, waiting for exactly this to pour itself into.

She looked at the amulet at the child's throat, glinting in the firelight.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I'll touch it, and God help us both, I'll finally know.

But tomorrow, when she reached for it, Wren caught her wrist — quick as a trap, strong as wire — and said her first word of trade-speech, learned from a week of listening, chosen with terrible precision:

"No."

And then, pointing east, toward the lowlands, toward the roads, toward everything: *"Brannoc."*

How the girl knew the name, Ada never asked. There wasn't time.
