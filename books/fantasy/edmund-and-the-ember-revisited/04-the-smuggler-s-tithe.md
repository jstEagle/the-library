## 4. The Smuggler's Tithe

The snow had stopped by the time they left the pass road, but the cold had not. It sat on the world like a lid, pressing the smoke of distant chimneys flat against the rooftops of Millbrook, and Ada walked through it with Wren's small hand buried in hers and a pack on her back that held everything she had decided to keep: her carving knives, three days of hard bread, a coil of oiled cord, and the bone amulet she no longer dared to touch.

Wren walked like an animal that had learned streets recently — head turning at every doorway, every shutter-crack, every dog. She had not spoken since the bounty notice. Not in her old tongue, not in the handful of trade-words she'd begun to hoard like a magpie. She simply walked, and watched, and when a rider passed them on the frozen lane — a courier with the Regent's black-and-gold stitched at his shoulder — her grip on Ada's hand went tight enough to grind the small bones together.

"I know," Ada murmured. "I see him."

She did more than see him. As the courier drew level, the dead rose up around Ada the way they always did near strangers who carried grief like loose change in their pockets — a mother's voice, thin as thread, saying *my son rode south, my son rode south* — and beneath it, fainter, something else. A soldier's voice, weeks dead already, muttering about orders. About a child. About *the Key*, and roads closing, and what was to be done with villages that sheltered what they should have surrendered.

Ada kept her face still. That was the first rule of her trade, learned before she'd ever carved her first whistle: never let them see you hear.

The courier did not look at them. He was young, and cold, and hurrying, and he thought the two figures trudging toward the river were a carver and her daughter going to market. By spring, Ada thought, he might think differently. By spring there might be men going door to door with the notice in their hands, asking after a girl with a bone amulet and eyes the color of deep water under ice.

Which was why they were walking away from her workshop instead of toward it, and why the river mattered.

Brannoc ferried out of the old landing two miles below Millbrook, where the Ashgrove water ran slow and brown under willows that nobody bothered to prune because nobody owned them. In summer he moved grain and untaxed brandy and occasionally people who preferred not to be remembered crossing. In winter he moved nothing, drank most of what he'd earned, and told anyone who asked that the river was closed — which it was, officially, by order of the same crown whose soldiers were now closing roads a hundred miles further north than any crown had bothered to close them in Ada's lifetime.

He owed her. Or rather, they owed each other, in the way of two people who had each seen the other do something unforgivable and had decided, mutually and without discussion, to find it forgivable instead.

"You're a fool to come down here," Brannoc said, before she was even within shouting distance. He was mending a net on the landing stage, bare-handed, his knuckles split and scabbed, a barrel of something illegal open beside him for warmth or company. His beard had gone from grey to white since she'd last stood on this planking, and his eyes hadn't changed at all: quick, bloodshot, and missing nothing. "Whole river's watched. You think the tithe-men don't count boats in winter? They count them twice."

"Hello, Brannoc."

"Don't hello me." But he'd already set down the net, and his gaze had gone past her shoulder to the child, and stayed there the way a man's gaze stays on weather coming in off the hills. "Who's this, then?"

"Wren. She's mine now." Ada heard herself say it and was surprised at how little it cost. "I need the river."

"The river's closed."

"Your boat isn't."

Brannoc looked at her for a long moment. Wind came off the water and moved the willow whips, and somewhere upstream a crow said one dry word and thought better of it.

"How far?" he said.

"North. Past the ford at Harrowmere. Far enough that the roads stop mattering."

"That's four days if the ice lets us, six if it doesn't, and forever if we're boarded." He crouched, spat between the planks, rubbed his hands. "You know what happens to smugglers caught running people north right now? There's a new tariff. They don't fine you. They ask you questions with hot irons about who paid you and what you're carrying, and then they hang you anyway, because the questions are just to pass the time."

"I can pay."

"With what? Bone whistles?" He almost smiled. Almost. "You've got coin, I'll grant, but coin doesn't buy what this costs. This costs risk, girl, and risk is the only thing I've got less of than teeth."

Behind her, Wren made a sound. Small. The first sound she'd made all day — a syllable of the old tongue, soft, directed at the river. And Ada felt the dead shift, the way they always shifted near the girl: a low tide-pull, dozens of drowned voices under the brown water lifting their heads at once, listening, the way dogs lift their heads at a whistle only they can hear.

Brannoc heard nothing. But he saw something — saw the child look at the water the way sailors look at a grave they know by name — and whatever he saw decided him faster than any argument could have.

"Inside," he said. "Both of you. Before someone sees you standing on my landing like a pair of herons."

* * *

His cabin smelled of tar, wet wool, and onions, and was warm in the way of rooms heated by a stove that has been argued with for forty years. He fed them barley soup without being asked, and Wren ate hers in fast, silent spoonfuls, watching the door between bites, and Brannoc watched her eat with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"So," he said, when the bowls were empty. "Say I say yes. Say I take you upriver past Harrowmere, past the toll-station, drop you where the old drover's path meets the north road. Four days, maybe five, night travel, no fires bigger than a cup. What's in it for me besides a hanging?"

"A hundred crowns. Half now."

"Half now is forty short of interesting." He leaned back, chair creaking. "But all right. Here's the other half. There's a crate."

Ada waited. Outside, the wind worried the eaves; inside, the stove ticked as it cooled, and Wren had gone very still over her empty bowl, the way she went still whenever adults began speaking in the careful cadence of negotiation.

"Came down from Thornfell ten days ago," Brannoc said. "Paid passage north to a man named Ossler at Grey Ford, up past Harrowmere. Ossler's dead — fever, before you ask, ordinary fever, nothing interesting — and the crate's been sitting in my hold ever since because the man who paid for its passage also paid for silence. Sealed coffin-box. Lead-lined, iron-banded, waxed seams. Instructions written on the manifest in a clerk's hand so fine it looks like it's afraid of itself." He recited, flatly: "*Carry north. Deliver to the keeper of the shrine at Grey Ford. Do not open. Do not listen.*"

"Do not listen," Ada repeated.

"That's what it says." Brannoc turned his cup slowly on the table. "Funny instruction for a box, eh? Unless whoever wrote it knew there'd be somebody on the boat who listens."

Ada's hands had gone quiet in her lap. Quiet the way they went quiet before difficult carvings, when the grain of the bone needed reading. Because she had already understood — had understood from the moment he said *coffin-crate*, from the moment Wren had gone still — and some part of her had understood even earlier, on the landing, when the drowned voices under the brown water had lifted their heads.

There were things in that box. Not a body. Things.

"And if I carry it," she said carefully, "what am I carrying it into?"

"Grey Ford shrine's abandoned two years. Whoever sent it either doesn't know or doesn't care. So it sits in my hold until someone carries it, or I burn it, and I'll tell you honestly I've thought about burning it." He looked up. "Only here's the thing, Ada. Three nights ago I went down to check the moorings and the box was singing."

The stove ticked. The wind pulled at the eaves. Wren's eyes came up slowly from her bowl and fixed on Brannoc with an attention that made the hair rise along Ada's arms.

"Singing," Ada said.

"Humming. Like a hive. Low. My deckhand swears he heard voices in it — names, he said, it was saying names, dozens of them, like a roll call. I told him he was drunk, which he was, but I heard it too." Brannoc's voice dropped, and Ada realized with a small cold shock that the ferryman was frightened — genuinely frightened, this man who had once, years ago, done the thing she had seen him do and had never once flinched from anything since. "I'm not a superstitious man. I've shipped worse than boxes. But I'm not opening that thing, and I'm not carrying it alone, and you—" he pointed at her with two scarred fingers, "—you are the only person I have ever met who talks to dead folk like they're neighbors. So. Half your hundred up front, and you carry the crate, and you never open it, and if it starts singing loud enough for the toll-men to hear at Harrowmere, you deal with it, because I will put the whole business over the side and let the river argue with it. Those are my terms. Take them or walk back up the hill and try the roads with every bounty-man between here and the mountains holding your child's face on a piece of paper."

Ada looked at Wren. Wren looked back at her, and did something she had not done before: she nodded. Once, small, deliberate. *Yes,* the nod said, in the language they had built between them out of glances and gestures across three days of flight. *Yes, take the box. Yes, get on the water.*

Later Ada would wonder whether the child had known even then what slept in the lead and wax. Later she would wonder whether that nod had been trust or strategy, and whether the difference mattered. But sitting in the onion-warm cabin with the wind pulling at the roof, she only felt the shape of the bargain settle over her like the cold outside: speed bought with weight, safety traded for cargo, the old arithmetic of the smuggler's road that never once balanced in favor of the passenger.

"All right," she said. "Half now. We leave tonight, before moonrise."

Brannoc exhaled through his nose, and some of the fear went out of his shoulders, replaced by the brisk practicality of a man who has decided to be brave and might as well be busy about it. "Then eat something else, because you'll want it. And Ada—" he paused at the stove, ladle in hand, and didn't turn around. "Whatever's in that box. When you hear it talking. Don't answer it."

* * *

They loaded at dusk.

The crate came up out of the hold on a block-and-tackle, swinging slow against the last grey light, and Ada stood on the deck and listened to it the way she had once listened to a dying woman's rib, reading the grain of it, and what she heard made her grip the rail.

It was not one voice. It was many — pressed close, layered, murmuring under the lead like bees under snow. And threaded through the murmur, faint but unmistakable, a rhythm she recognized from her own workbench: the particular cadence of souls that had not finished saying something. Souls interrupted mid-word.

Beside her, Wren laid one small palm flat against the crate's iron banding, and the murmuring — the murmuring *stopped*. All of it, all at once, the way every dead voice in Ada's life had gone silent the first time she touched the child's amulet. Then, softly, from inside the sealed and leaded dark, came a single sound. Not words. Something older than words. A note of recognition, low and glad, like a hearth-fire catching.

Brannoc crossed himself with a boat-hook, which was not a blessing anyone had taught him but served.

"Get below," he said hoarsely. "Both of you. We cast off while it's still dark, and Ada—" 

"I know," she said. "Never open it."

"No," said Brannoc, pushing off from the landing, the current taking the boat sideways into the brown water, the willows sliding past, the whole sleeping country beginning to move northward around them. "What I mean is — I don't think it wants opening. I think it wants *following*. And those aren't the same thing at all."
