## 5. Voices in the Crate

The river called the Sere ran gray-green under an overcast sky, moving slow and heavy as something that had swallowed too much. Ada stood at the stern of Brannoc's flat-bottomed barge with her hands folded inside her sleeves, watching the banks unspool — alder and willow first, then reed flats, then the low stone walls of drowned farms where the water had crept up over the years and never crept back down.

Behind her, lashed amidships beneath a tarpaulin that smelled of pitch and old fish, sat the coffin-crate.

Brannoc had made her swear on it before they cast off. His hand, broad as a shovel blade and scarred across every knuckle, had pressed the seal flat against her palm: black wax over iron wire, stamped with a mark she did not recognize — a circle broken by a single vertical line, like a door with a crack in it.

"You don't open it," he'd said. "Not if it knocks. Not if it calls your name. Not if it calls hers." He'd jerked his chin at Wren, who had been crouched at the barge's edge watching the water with the absolute attention of a heron.

"And what is in it?" Ada had asked.

"Tithe," was all he said. "The Guild of the Gray Ferry doesn't run this stretch for free anymore. You carry what they tell you to carry, you deliver it where I say, and nobody asks anybody anything. That's the arrangement."

She had carried worse cargo in her life, or told herself so. Bones were bones. She carved them, listened to them, laid them to rest when their owners' words finally ran out. But a crate packed full enough of the dead to need sealing — that was not her trade. That was someone's harvest.

Now, three days north of the ferry crossing, she could hear it.

Not with her ears. Her ears heard only water slapping hull-wood and the creak of the sweep-oar where Brannoc's nephew Tam worked the tiller, humming through his teeth. What Ada heard came the other way, through the marrow-sense she had spent forty years pretending was ordinary: a low, layered murmur rising from under the tarpaulin like heat off summer stone.

Dozens of voices. Packed close. Speaking over one another, under one another, sometimes *through* one another, so that individual words dissolved and only the texture remained — grief braided with grief until the braid became a kind of weather.

*— cold, why is it still cold, we paid, we paid —*

*— my daughter's name is Merrin, tell her the debt wasn't mine —*

*— the door has a crack in it, the door has a crack, I can see light through the crack —*

Ada pressed her tongue to the roof of her mouth, the old discipline, the trick she used at markets and funerals to keep from flinching at strangers' dead. It helped the way a candle helps against a flood.

"You hear them," Wren said.

The girl had appeared beside her without sound, as she always did — a feral grace, feet finding the quiet planks between the loud ones. She was gnawing the heel of the barley loaf Ada had given her, and there was a smear of crust-dust on her chin, and for a moment she looked so entirely like an ordinary child that Ada's chest did something complicated.

"I hear them," Ada admitted.

"They're sad." Wren said it without pity, the way she reported that the river smelled of fish or that Tam's humming had three notes wrong in it. Fact-finding. "They're all saying different things but it's the same underneath."

"What's underneath?"

Wren chewed. Swallowed. Looked at the crate with those eyes that were too dark and too steady for a face so young, eyes that had looked at Ada once during the blizzard with the expression of something very old wearing childhood like an ill-fitting coat.

"*Let us out,*" she said. "That's the underneath."

* * *

By the second day on the water, Ada noticed the thing that would not leave her afterward — the thing that turned her stomach over slowly, all night, like a wheel in a mill.

Wren slept beside the crate.

Not near it. *Beside* it. The girl had dragged her blanket from the bow on the first evening, curled herself into the gap between the coffin-crate and the cargo rail, and gone down into sleep as though the tarpaulined wood were a hearthstone. When Ada had tried to move her — gently, one hand on the small sharp shoulder — Wren had made a sound deep in her throat, animal and final, and Ada had withdrawn her hand.

So she watched instead.

And what she saw was this: the murmur of the dead, which ground against her own skull like silt in a bearing, went quiet around the child. Not silenced — *gathered*. As the night deepened the voices in the crate stopped their tangled arguing and drew inward, downward, toward the place where Wren slept, the way cold air pours toward a cellar. Ada could feel the current of it. She could almost see it, if she let her gift blur at the edges: dozens of souls leaning toward one sleeping girl the way sunflowers lean toward noon.

And Wren smiled in her sleep.

It was the first time Ada had seen the child smile at all. In six days of shared fire, shared bread, shared road, Wren's face had held exactly two expressions: watchfulness and hunger. Now, cheek pressed to sealed wood full of stolen dead, she smiled like a girl dreaming of a festival.

Ada sat up all night with her back against the mast and her knife loose in its sheath, and thought about the bounty notice folded in oilcloth inside her shirt. THE KEY, it said, above a charcoal sketch that might have been any child and was unmistakably this one. And beneath it, in the Regent's tidy bureaucratic hand: *Alive. Unmarked. Reward commensurate.*

Unmarked. Alive. Because whatever the Regent wanted Wren for, it needed her whole. Needed her the way a lock needs a key intact — or the way a forge needs fuel dry.

*The dead are drawn to her.* Mother-taught sense said that made the child precious. Something older and colder in Ada, some instinct that predated her craft, whispered the other reading: *bait draws fish because the fish are hungry. A trap draws wolves because the wolves want what's inside.*

If the dead crowded toward Wren like moths toward a lamp — then everything that hunted the dead would come hunting too. And Ada had seen, on the southern pass, what the Regent sent out to hunt.

She looked at the sleeping child and felt the dread settle into her bones like river damp: not the fear that she was carrying treasure. The fear that she was carrying a lit beacon wrapped in swaddling cloth, and that somewhere behind them, in the dark along the banks, things with the Regent's seal burned on their armor were already turning toward the light.

* * *

The third day brought fog.

It came down the valley in the early morning, thick as wool, erasing the banks, erasing the sky, shrinking the world to the length of the barge and the sound of the water. Tam worked the tiller by ear. Brannoc stood at the prow with a pole, sounding depths he knew by heart, and even his great silhouette had gone soft-edged, a shadow of a man poling through milk.

Ada welcomed it. Fog was cover. Fog was hours stolen from any watcher's eye.

Then, through the fog, she heard hoofbeats.

On the towpath — the churned dirt track that ran the south bank, built for draft animals hauling barges upstream — came the rhythmic strike of shod horses at a walk. Deliberate. Patient. Four of them, maybe five, spaced wide the way soldiers ride when they're searching rather than traveling.

Tam stopped humming.

Brannoc raised one fist, and the barge drifted, oar shipped, momentum bleeding away until they were barely moving, a gray shape on gray water under gray sky. Ada eased Wren down behind the cargo rail, and the girl — awake now, instantly, wholly awake in the way she always was, no drowsy borders — went willingly and silently, folding herself into shadow with her arms around her knees.

The hoofbeats grew louder. Slowed.

Stopped.

Through the fog, shapes resolved: riders on lean warhorses, armored in blackened scale that drank what little light the morning offered. Their helms were closed. Their shields bore the broken-circle mark — the same mark, Ada realized with a slow cold turn of her gut, that was stamped in the wax on the crate's seal. The Regent's outriders. She had heard of them the way one hears of plague two valleys over: rumors, burned villages, roads gone quiet. Here they were, real and patient, twenty yards away across a strip of brown water.

One rider dismounted. Walked to the edge of the towpath. Stood there.

He did not call out. He did not demand papers or tolls. He simply stood, helm tilted slightly, and Ada understood with sick certainty that he was *listening*. The way she listened. Some borrowed sense, some artifice stitched into that blackened armor — the Regent fed on bound souls, Aldric's whispers had warned her, and a thing that eats souls knows how to sniff for them.

The barge held five souls alive and several dozen dead.

For ten heartbeats, nothing moved except the fog.

Then Wren shifted against Ada's side, and the child's hand found the bone amulet at her throat — the little carved thing she'd worn half-buried in her rags since the blizzard, the thing whose touch had silenced every dead voice Ada had ever known — and she gripped it, and Ada felt the amulet do what it did.

Silence fell. Not around them. *Inside* them. The murmur of the crate, the layering grief-song of the packed souls, winked out as if snuffed between finger and thumb. The dead vanished from the world's hearing.

From the towpath, the armored figure made a small sound — a grunt, or a breath, or perhaps disappointment — and after a long moment swung back onto his horse. The hoofbeats resumed. Faded. The fog folded shut behind them like water closing over a stone.

No one on the barge breathed fully for another quarter mile.

* * *

"They couldn't find us," Tam said at midday, once the fog had thinned to gauze and his hands had remembered how to stop shaking. "Whatever they listen with, the fog hid us."

"The fog didn't hide us," Brannoc growled from the prow. He hadn't spoken since the riders left, and his voice came out like gravel poured slow. "I've run this river thirty years. I've slipped patrols in open daylight with a hold full of untaxed brandy. They weren't listening for contraband." He turned, and his eyes — pale, watery, deceptively mild — found Ada, then dropped to the child crouched at her side. "They were fishing for something specific. And they didn't catch it because something on this boat went quiet at the right moment. Something that isn't usually quiet."

Ada said nothing.

"Girl," Brannoc said, and Wren lifted her chin. "You did that? Made them deaf?"

Wren considered him. Then she looked at Ada — a question in the look, a request for permission, as if the truth of herself were something she now understood must be rationed out carefully, parcel by parcel, to people who might use it against her. Where she had learned such caution, Ada did not want to know. Every answer would be a horror.

"She holds the amulet," Ada said quietly, "and the dead go silent. All of them. Everywhere near her."

"That's not hiding," Brannoc said. "That's a lamp going out. And a lamp only goes out like that—" he tapped the rail, "—when somebody's got their hand on the wick." He spat over the side, into the gray water. "Three more days to the Gray Ferry's north dock. After that, this cargo's delivered and you're off my boat, Ashgrove, and no offense meant, but I'll sleep better knowing neither of you is aboard."

"Some offense taken," Ada said.

"Good. Means you're listening." He turned back to the fog-thinned channel. "Keep your lamp covered, then. Whatever that means for you two."

* * *

That night, the crate began to knock.

Softly. Three taps, pause, three taps. Rhythmic. Almost polite. The tarpaulin did not move; the iron wire did not strain; the black wax seal held perfect and unbroken. But the sound came anyway, from inside wood that had been nailed shut in some Guild warehouse by men who presumably knew their trade, and it came in a pattern, and patterns mean minds, and minds mean pleading.

Tam slept forward, exhausted, mercifully deaf to all of it. Brannoc pretended to sleep and Ada suspected he was pretending.

Wren did not wake. She lay curled in her hollow beside the crate, and the knocking rose and fell around her like surf around a rock, and she slept on, smiling that terrible festival smile, while the dead pressed themselves toward her warmth through an inch of nailed plank.

Ada sat across from the crate with her carving knife in her lap and a scrap of goose-bone — the last of her stock — turning under her fingers without her quite deciding to carve it. This was how she thought best. This was how she prayed, if she prayed.

The bone took shape slowly: a small bird, wings folded, head tucked. A resting bird. And as it took shape, the nearest voice in the crate — the one that spoke of Merrin and unpaid debts — seemed to notice her, seemed to gather itself, and said, clear for the first time in three days:

*You're the one who hears.*

"I hear," Ada murmured, barely aloud.

*We know what she is.* The voice was a woman's, frayed at the edges, worn thin as old cloth. *We can feel it through the wood. She's a door, hearer. A door that opens one way. And everything on the other side is pushing.*

The knife paused.

*The crowned one isn't hunting her to use her,* the dead woman said, and the other voices stilled to let her speak, dozens of souls holding their breath in a box. *He's hunting her because he's afraid of her. There's a difference, hearer. Bait you leave out. A key you guard. But a door —*

The knocking stopped.

In the silence that followed, Wren stirred in her sleep and turned her face toward the crate, and said, in the language older than any kingdom, one soft syllable that Ada did not yet know but would learn within the week, a word that would rearrange the inside of her ear and change forever how the dead sounded to her —

and far downstream, back along the black thread of the river, torchlight bloomed at the water's edge. Not four riders this time.

A dozen. Then more behind them. Coming north.

Toward the lamp that had briefly gone out, and been noticed anyway.
