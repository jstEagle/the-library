## 6. The Hollow King

The hour began, as it always began, with the taste of iron.

Aldric woke inside his own mouth. That was how he had come to describe it to himself, in the small vocabulary of his captivity: not waking in a bed, not waking in a room, but waking *inside his own mouth*, behind his own teeth, a swimmer surfacing in water that was somehow also his body. His hands lay on the desk before him — his hands, scarred at the knuckle from a boyhood spent climbing the quarry walls above Thornfell, ink-stained now along the right forefinger where he had pressed too hard against the nib. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Beyond the tall windows of the king's study, the capital lay spread in its winter dark, ten thousand hearth-fires banked low, and above them all the pale smear of the moon caught in cloud like a coin dropped in milk.

One hour. He had learned to measure it by the candle rather than the clock, because clocks could be moved and candles were honest. One hour of Aldric, every night, between the last stroke of midnight and the first grey suggestion of morning. Then the other thing would come back up through him like cold water filling a boot, and Aldric would sink, and the thing wearing his face would smile with his mouth and rule with his hands and sleep, when it slept at all, in his bed.

He did not waste the hour on despair. Despair was a luxury he had spent years ago, back in the beginning, when he had still believed someone would notice.

*They will notice,* he had thought then, in those first weeks. *The court will see it. My wife—* But there had been no wife; he had never married; the Regent had eaten even that hope from his memory, or perhaps he had simply invented her out of loneliness, the way prisoners invent windows. The court saw nothing, because the Regent wore him flawlessly. It walked as he walked. It signed with his seal. It had dismissed three chancellors and appointed one creature of its own, a soft-voiced man named Veyran whose eyes, when Aldric had finally been permitted to look into them during some public ceremony, held the flat patience of a well.

The court saw a king who had grown colder. Colder, more distant, more terrible in his judgments. A king who no longer laughed at the jester's jokes, who no longer wept at the funerals of old companions, who sat through feasts without touching the food. They called it grief for his father. They called it the weight of the crown. They whispered it approvingly, even: *the boy has become iron at last.*

Aldric wrote what he saw. That was the whole of his rebellion, and it had taken him two years to build it, and it consisted of paper, ink, a candle stub, and one hour.

* * *

The writing itself was an act of war conducted in miniature. He could not keep a journal — the Regent's hours were not fixed, and twice it had woken early, and once Aldric had lost four pages of truth to its hungry skimming. So he wrote letters instead, short ones, coded in the merchant-cipher his mother's people had used on the salt roads, and he hid them in places only a king who had been a smuggler's grandson would know: inside the hollow leg of the orrery, beneath the false bottom of the map drawer, wedged in the mortar-crack behind the third stone of the north chimney. And once a month, when the moon was new and the guards changed on the river stair, a laundress with a limp carried one of these letters out of the palace folded inside a wet sheet, and gave it to a fishwife, who gave it to a carter, who gave it to a man whose name Aldric had never learned, and somewhere beyond the walls the letter vanished into the world like a seed thrown from a moving cart.

Whether anything grew from those seeds, he did not know. He wrote anyway. Hope, he had decided, was not a feeling. It was a discipline, like drill or archery, and you performed it whether or not you believed in it, and some mornings your arms ached and the target stood untouched, and you performed it again.

Tonight the candle said he had fifty minutes.

He flexed his fingers — they always came back stiff, as if the Regent kept them clenched around something all day — and pulled the day's observations toward him across the desk.

The war-map. He had stood over it for six hours today, or the Regent had, moving little carved armies across the painted kingdoms with two fingers, and Aldric had watched from inside himself the way a man watches his own hands do a thing he has forbidden them to do. The southern passes: black markers, forty thousand, moving toward the free cities. The river roads: burned ferry licenses posted at every crossing, travel restricted, tithe-collectors doubled. And at the center of the map, in the place where a king might set the marker of his own household, a single piece Aldric had never seen before — a sliver of white bone, polished smooth, sitting in a shallow dish of oil like a relic awaiting veneration.

It had not been there last week.

He wrote: *New marker on the war-map. Bone, white, small. Veyran handles it with gloves. When the Regent's fingers pass near it, I feel — I do not know the word. A pulling. Like a hook behind my sternum.*

His nib scratched. Outside, snow had begun again, fine and dry, ticking against the glass.

There was more. There was always more, and the horror of it was not the grand horrors but the small ones, the accretion of details no prisoner should have to catalogue about his own life. The Regent had begun eating differently this winter — or rather, Aldric's body had, because the Regent did not eat food so much as wear the eating of it, and lately the meals left Aldric's throat raw, as if something had passed through him that was not bread or wine. The court physician had been replaced in autumn. The king's hawks had died, all four, in a single week, found stiff in their mews with their eyes open, and the Regent had ordered the mews sealed and no falconer questioned. And the dreams — Aldric shivered and made himself write it — the dreams had changed. For two years the Regent's occupation of his sleeping mind had been a blankness, a dreamless dark. Now there were sounds in the dark. Voices, far off, layered like wind through a forest, and underneath them a sound like a forge breathing.

*Last night,* he wrote, *I dreamed of a door. I was on the wrong side of it. Something on the other side was counting.*

He stopped. His hand was shaking. He watched it shake — his hand, thirty-one years old, that had once been steady enough to loose an arrow at a hundred paces — and he hated the thing that made it tremble, and he hated more that the trembling was his, that everything weak in this body remained his while everything strong had been confiscated.

Fifty minutes. Forty-five.

* * *

The interruption came at the half-hour, and it nearly ended him.

Footsteps in the corridor — the particular drag-and-click of Veyran's cane, which the chancellor affected though nothing was wrong with his legs. Aldric had perhaps twenty heartbeats. He swept the letter from the desk, thumbed open the false bottom of the map drawer, laid the page flat among the charts of the northern coast, and closed it just as the door opened.

But he could not leave the desk. Leaving the desk mid-hour would be noted; the Regent noticed everything Aldric did with its property. So he sat, and arranged his face into the mild preoccupation of a king reviewing accounts, and Veyran came in on his unnecessary cane carrying a lacquered box the size of a hymnal.

"Your Majesty works late," Veyran said. His voice was warm the way a bathhouse is warm: enveloping, and smelling faintly of other people.

"The accounts require attention," Aldric heard himself say. Speaking during his hour was dangerous — the voice was fully his, and if the Regent returned mid-sentence it sheared the words off in a way that Veyran had remarked upon once, softly, with interest — but silence was worse. Silence looked like hiding.

"Indeed." Veyran set the box on the desk and opened it, and inside, on velvet gone grey with handling, lay a bone amulet the length of a finger. Carved. Old. The carving was of a kind Aldric had never seen — spirals within spirals, a script older than any heraldry, worn almost smooth — and the moment his eyes touched it, the hook behind his sternum *pulled*, hard, like a fish testing a line.

"We found it," Veyran said gently, "in the possession of a dead woman. A bone-carver, up in the Ashgrove country. She had it hidden in her floor." He smiled with all the warmth of his voice. "Not the woman we seek. But she led us to the road the child took. South, then west. Toward the river country."

Aldric kept his face still. Inside, something was screaming, and he could not tell whether the scream was fear or recognition, because the pull of the amulet felt horribly like homesickness, like the ache of a word remembered in a language he had never learned.

"The child," Aldric said carefully. "You spoke of a child."

"The Key, Majesty." Veyran closed the box, and the pull slackened, and Aldric breathed again. "The Ember grows... hungry. You have felt it. We all feel your Majesty's burden." His eyes rested on Aldric with that flat well-patience. "The binding that sustains you was forged to hold. It was not forged to hold *forever*. But with the Key, the binding can be renewed — made permanent. Eternal. No more nightly hours. No more burden at all." He slid the box across the desk. "When our riders bring the girl to Thornfell, your Majesty will be whole. Forever."

*Forever,* Aldric thought, *is exactly what I am afraid of.* Because he had understood, in the long ledger of his captivity, the arithmetic the Regent kept: it fed on bound souls, it drank the Ember's heat, and a binding made permanent would not free him. It would seal him. It would pour the girl into the forge-heart like ore into a crucible, and close the door, and count him safe forever on the wrong side of it.

"You are kind, Chancellor," Aldric said. "I long for rest."

"And you shall have it." Veyran bowed, took up his box and his cane, and paused at the door. "Ride hard, my men," he murmured, almost to himself, almost tenderly. "The mountain passes close within the month."

The door shut. Aldric counted to two hundred, listening to the snow tick against the glass, listening to his own heart conduct its stubborn, unregarded business.

Then he opened the drawer and added the final lines:

*A child is coming here, taken against her will. She is called the Key. They mean to bind her into the Ember and make my prison permanent. Whoever reads this: she is a child. Whatever else she is, whatever they say she is — she is a child, and they are going to feed her to the thing that wears me.*

*I am on the wrong side of the door. Something is counting. Help me open it before the counting ends.*

He sanded the page, folded it, and hid it in the chimney mortar, third stone, where the laundress with the limp would find it on the new moon.

Then the candle guttered, and the cold came up through him like water filling a boot, and Aldric sank — but tonight, sinking, he held one new thing in his fist all the way down: *south, then west, toward the river country.* Someone was moving. Someone was already on the road.
