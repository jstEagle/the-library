## 7. Roads That Remember

The ferry was still burning when they crested the ridge above the river.

Ada had known before she saw it. She'd felt it in her teeth, a wrongness like a cracked molar — the absence of Brannoc's river-voices, the drowned dead who murmured along the current from the millpond to the sea. For three days they'd been her quiet companions on the water road, and now the water was silent, and silence, for Ada Ashgrove, was never empty. It was full of everything that had stopped.

Smoke rose in a black column, straight as a hangman's rope. Below, where the ferry landing should have been, there was only fire-gutted timber and the hull of the *Tithe's Due* listing against the pilings, charred to the waterline. Figures moved along the bank in the grey morning light — soldiers, or men dressed as them, poking through the wreckage with spear-butts, turning over barrels, kicking ash.

Wren made a sound Ada had never heard from her. Not a word of the old tongue, not the bird-clicks and throat-sounds the girl used when she forgot herself. A small, human noise, low in her chest, like something breaking quietly.

"They knew," Ada said. Her voice came out flat. She kept it flat because the alternative was not flat at all. "They knew we'd come to him."

She thought of Brannoc's face the night he'd agreed to carry them, the way his eyes had gone to the sealed coffin-crate in his hold and then away again, quick as a pickpocket. *You never asked what's in it,* he'd said, and she hadn't, because asking would have meant knowing, and knowing would have meant deciding, and she had spent forty years building a life that required no decisions larger than which bone to carve next.

Now the crate was ash, or taken, or walking south in a soldier's wagon full of stolen souls, and Brannoc —

She didn't let herself finish the thought. Grief was a luxury with a price named in blood, and she couldn't afford it yet.

"Come away from the ridge," she whispered. "The smoke draws eyes."

Wren didn't move. The girl stood with her fists clenched at her sides, staring down at the burning landing with an expression far too old for her face, and for one strange moment Ada saw her not as a feral child in a too-large coat but as whatever she truly was — the thing the bounty notices called the Key, the living hinge of some ancient binding, forged or chosen or cursed. Then Wren turned, and she was just a girl again, hollow-eyed and hungry, and Ada's heart did the treacherous thing it had been doing more and more lately: it ached.

"There's another way," Ada said. "North. Under."

* * *

The smuggler roads were her inheritance, though no one had ever said so out loud.

Her father had run salt and untaxed iron through the hills between the river country and Vell, back when Ada was small enough to ride the cargo sleds among the sacks. He'd taught her the mouths of the tunnels the way other fathers taught prayers — as protection, as property, as the only thing that could not be confiscated. *Soldiers burn ferries,* he used to say, spitting into the river. *Soldiers burn bridges. Nobody burns a hole in a mountain.* He'd been wrong about that, eventually; the Regent's engineers had collapsed two of the great passages in the western ranges a decade ago, sealing them with poured stone and warding-marks. But the small ways, the mean ways, the crawls and drains and abandoned mine-shafts that honest maps never marked — those were still hers.

She led Wren north along the dry wash below the ridge until midday, keeping to the shadow of the bluffs, and then cut east where a fallen oak bridged a ravine no wider than a cart-track. Beneath the oak's root-ball, half-hidden by ferns, was a gap in the rock face barely wide enough for a grown woman's shoulders.

"In," Ada said.

Wren looked at the dark, then at Ada, and something passed across her face — not fear exactly. Assessment. The look of a creature deciding whether the den was safe.

"You'd rather stay up here and be found by the men who burned the boat?" Ada asked. "They're looking for you specifically, child. There's a price on your head with more zeros than my house is worth."

Wren tilted her head. Then she said a word — short, liquid, consonants like water over stones — and slipped into the dark ahead of her.

It took Ada a moment, crawling after her through the cold throat of the hill, to realize the word had been an answer. *Yes.* Or perhaps *after you.* Or perhaps something with no translation at all, some old courtesy from a language older than kingdoms. She filed it away the way she filed all sounds, in the part of her mind where the dead lived, and was surprised to find how carefully she filed this one. How gently.

* * *

The tunnel ran for half a mile under the sheep-pastures before opening into the first waystation — a smuggler's cache her father's generation had built into a natural cavern, dry-floored, with a stone hearth vented through a crack in the hillside so thin the smoke escaped as nothing but warmth. Ada lit a small fire anyway, cautious, feeding it dry pine she'd carried in bundles from the last farmstead. Light moved along the walls and found the old marks cut into the stone: tally-scores, route-signs, a crude carving of a fish that meant *safe water here* in a code forty years dead.

Wren crouched before the carved fish and touched it with one finger.

"That's just smuggler's sign," Ada said. "Old Brannoc's grandfather's people cut most of these. It means the spring inside doesn't run bitter."

Wren shook her head slowly. She traced the fish's curve, then spoke — a soft run of syllables, four or five of them, her voice dropping into a register Ada had come to think of as the girl's true voice, the one beneath the silence she wore like a coat.

"I don't know what you're saying," Ada admitted.

Wren looked up at her. And then, with the patience of someone who has done this many times across many years — or across many centuries, Ada thought uneasily — the girl pointed at the fish. Said the word again. Pointed at the hearth. Said another word. Pointed at Ada, and pressed her own hand flat over her sternum, and said a third.

*Fish. Fire. You.*

"No," Ada said slowly, understanding dawning. "That last one — that's me? My name?"

Wren nodded once, briskly, a teacher confirming a student's work.

"My name is Ada."

Wren frowned. Tried it. "*A-da.*" The old tongue bent the vowels strangely, made the name sound older, heavier, like something dug out of a barrow. Then she pointed at herself and said a long, rolling word that Ada's mouth immediately failed to reproduce — a name, presumably, or a title, or possibly a sentence meaning *the door that walks.*

"Wren," Ada said firmly. "Your name is Wren. I named you that myself, the second night, when you sat on my windowsill like a little brown bird and stole my bread."

Something flickered in the girl's expression — amusement, Ada thought, actual amusement, the first she'd ever seen there. Wren pointed at herself. "Wren," she repeated, in Ada's tongue, clear as a bell. Then, in the old language, she added a trailing phrase, sing-song and self-deprecating, and even without translation Ada caught its shape: *also known as the other thing.*

"We'll get to the other thing later," Ada said. "One miracle at a time."

* * *

They traveled the under-roads for six days.

By day they slept in the waystations — there were nine between the river country and the high moors, and Ada's father's maps, folded soft as cloth from handling, remembered every one. By night, when the surface roads were quietest, they climbed to the air to stretch their legs and fill the waterskins, moving under stars through gorse and bracken like two animals sharing a warren.

And in the long hours underground, Wren taught her.

It began as necessity — hand-signs for *water*, *rest*, *danger*, *quieter*. But the girl had a scholar's patience hidden under all that wildness, and Ada discovered she had a hunger she hadn't known about. Words went into her like water into cracked ground. Within two days she could count to five. Within four she could say *the fire is small* and *I am cold* and — the phrase Wren seemed to take particular pleasure in drilling — *the dead are loud tonight.*

That last one changed things. That was the trouble.

On the fifth night, in a waystation beneath the old barrow-moors where the air itself tasted of rust and rain, Ada was cleaning her carving knives when the dead came close. They did that sometimes in the deep places, pressing near the way she perceived them, drawn like moths. An old man's voice, a woman humming, a child counting stones — the usual crowd, the usual murmur, the tide of voices she had lived inside since she was eight years old and first held a dead man's knucklebone.

Without thinking, she said aloud, in the old tongue, "*The dead are loud tonight.*"

The voices stopped.

Not faded. Not quieted. *Stopped* — the way a room goes silent when a king enters it. Every dead voice within her hearing drew back at once, and in the ringing space they left behind, Ada felt something she had never felt in thirty years of her gift: the exact shape of her own listening. As if the words had turned her inward-out, shown her the instrument instead of the music.

"What did I say?" she breathed. "What did that do?"

Wren was watching her with wide, grave eyes. The girl set down the bone token she'd been idly carving — she was always carving now, little shapes in scrap-bone, and Ada tried not to look too closely at how precisely she shaped them — and crawled across the firelight to sit beside her.

Slowly, deliberately, Wren took Ada's hand and pressed it flat against the stone floor of the cavern. Then she spoke a single word into the silence.

The stone answered.

Not metaphorically. Ada felt it through her palm — a vast, slow attention rising through the rock like heat, like the mountain noticing a splinter in its skin and considering it. Old voices, deeper than any ghost she'd ever heard, older than the barrows, older maybe than the kingdoms whose bones the barrows were. They didn't speak in words. They spoke in weight. And threaded through that weight, faint as a vein of silver, she heard the ordinary dead again — but differently now. Not as voices arriving from outside. As presences she could address. As guests.

"The words aren't names," Ada whispered, understanding arriving like cold water. "The words are doors. Your language doesn't describe things. It *opens* them."

Wren nodded. Then she leaned her head briefly against Ada's shoulder — a gesture so small, so unchildlike in its gravity, that Ada's breath caught — and said a phrase Ada was beginning to know the shape of. She'd heard it twice before: once at the burned ferry, once at the mouth of the first tunnel.

*This is why they want me,* the phrase meant. Or close enough. *This is what I am for.*

"You're not *for* anything," Ada said, sharper than she intended. She softened. "You're a person who happens to be useful to monsters. There's a difference, and I'll thank you to remember it."

Wren pulled back and studied her face in the firelight, searching it the way she searched everything — completely, without embarrassment, as if faces were texts written in a language she was still learning. Whatever she found there made her nod slowly. Then she picked up her half-carved bone token, pressed it into Ada's hand, and closed Ada's fingers around it.

It was a whistle. Unfinished, but unmistakably a whistle — modeled, Ada realized with a jolt, on the ones hanging from Ada's own belt, the trade-whistles she carved from miners' bones to hear the dead's last words. Wren had been watching her hands for days. Learning her craft the way she learned everything: silently, totally.

"Why?" Ada asked.

Wren pointed at the whistle. Pointed at her own ear. Pointed at Ada. Then she said a new phrase, one Ada didn't know, and mimed — clumsily, endearingly — the act of speaking into something and waiting.

*So you can hear me,* the mime said. *When I'm not there. So the bones can keep my words for you.*

Ada looked down at the small unfinished gift in her palm, and something in her chest — something she had boarded up decades ago, brick by careful brick, behind rules about isolation and commissions refused and doors kept locked — knocked once, softly, from the inside.

"It's good work," she managed. Her voice came out rough. "The taper's wrong on the air-hole. I'll show you how to fix it tomorrow."

Wren smiled. It transformed her whole face, made her suddenly, devastatingly young, and Ada understood with terrible clarity that she had already lost the war she'd been fighting since the blizzard night — the war to remain a woman alone, answerable to no one, attached to nothing that could be taken from her.

* * *

On the seventh day, the tunnel ended at a grate of iron bars crusted with lichen, and beyond the grate lay open moorland under a sky the color of pewter, and beyond the moorland, far off, the free city of Vell rose pale against the hills like a jaw of old teeth.

Ada lifted Wren through the bars first, then squeezed out after her, stiff and blinking in the daylight. Freedom smelled like wet grass and sheep and woodsmoke from distant farms. It smelled, unexpectedly, like hope, and she distrusted it immediately.

They were halfway across the first field when Wren stopped walking.

The girl stood rigid in the wind, head cocked, listening to something Ada couldn't hear — and Ada, who heard everything, found that absence more frightening than any sound. Then Wren turned to her, and her face had gone white as the lichen on the grate, and she spoke fast, urgent, in the old tongue, pointing north toward Vell and then, terribly, east — toward the capital, toward Thornfell, toward the mountain vault whose name Ada had seen only in a bounty notice's fine print.

"I don't understand," Ada said. "Slower. Slower, child."

Wren grabbed her hand and pressed it flat against her own sternum, over her heart, the same gesture from the first lesson. *You.* Then she pointed at herself. Then she made a pulling motion, hand closing into a fist, drawing something invisible out of her own chest — and her eyes rolled back, just for a heartbeat, just far enough.

Ada's blood went cold.

"Something's calling you." She gripped the girl's shoulders. "Something across the distance. It's pulling."

Wren nodded. And then, in Ada's own language — the girl's vocabulary growing daily, relentless as roots — she said the worst possible sentence in the smallest possible voice:

"It knows where I am now."
