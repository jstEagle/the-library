## 8. The Free City of Vell

They came down out of the hills on the ninth morning, and Ada knew the city before she saw it by the sound it made.

Not the sound of bells or carts or the ordinary clatter of the living — she had heard those in a dozen towns and they all blurred together — but the sound beneath them. Vell sat on the bones of an older city, and the older city was still talking. Every wall the masons raised had been quarried from tombs; every foundation stone had a name in it. The dead of Vell murmured like a river under ice, and for nine days of silence on the road — Wren's silence, the strange null-silence that followed the girl like her own shadow — Ada had almost forgotten what voices sounded like.

Now they came back all at once, a flood against her teeth.

*Cold,* said a legionary who had died when this hill was called something else. *The wine here is terrible,* complained a merchant three centuries drowned. *She's coming,* whispered something older than both, and Ada could not tell if it meant Wren, or winter, or nothing at all.

"Stop," Wren said.

Ada turned. The girl stood at the crest of the ridge with the wind pulling at her ragged hair, and she was not looking at the city. She was looking at the ground, at the old road itself, worn into the rock by two thousand years of feet.

"This road remembers," Wren said, in her own tongue first, then — haltingly, carefully, the way a child carries water without spilling it — in words Ada understood. She had been teaching Ada as they walked, a word a day, sometimes two: *fire*, *door*, *mother*, *no*. In exchange Ada taught her the common trade-tongue, and the girl learned it the way she seemed to learn everything, whole and sudden, like ice taking a shape. "This road remembers being a river of people."

"Roads don't remember."

"This one does." Wren crouched and pressed her palm flat to the stone. Her lips moved through sounds that made the hair on Ada's arms lift — not because they were frightening, but because they were *familiar*, the way a lullaby half-remembered is familiar. When she looked up, her eyes were wet. "It says everyone who walked it was going somewhere they hoped would take them in."

Ada looked down the long switchbacking descent toward the walls of Vell, gray-gold in the morning light, and felt the truth of it settle somewhere under her ribs. That was why they were walking it too.

"Well then," she said, and held out her hand. "Let's hope."

Wren took it.

* * *

Vell had no king. That was its pride and its law, cut into the gatehouse in letters deep enough to survive any siege: *NO CROWN PASSES THIS GATE.* The free cities had thrown off their monarchs four hundred years ago, and though the world outside had gone back to crowning men and calling it order, Vell kept its gate-sign polished like a mirror, and every traveler passing under it was made to read their own face reflected in the words.

The guards at the gate were not soldiers so much as clerks with spears. One of them took Ada's name, her trade, and her reason for entry — "carving commission," she said, which was even true, in the way most useful lies are true — while another peered at Wren with the frank curiosity people gave feral children and did not give keys.

"You'll want the Charnel Quarter for bone-work," the clerk said, stamping her paper. "And keep the girl close. There's been notices."

"Notices?"

"Bounty boards." He shrugged, already turning to the next traveler. "Regent's coin spends anywhere, these days."

Ada's mouth went dry. She had left the last bounty notice nailed to a fence post forty leagues south, and she had told herself the roads north were too poor, too crooked, too smuggler-worn for the Regent's writ to follow. But coin spent anywhere. Coin always found a way downhill, like water, like blood.

Inside the walls, Vell was loud in every direction at once. Fishmongers and relic-mongers shared the same arcade. Students in gray robes argued philosophy with butchers over the heads of slaughtered pigs. A funeral procession crossed a market square without anyone stopping their haggling — death in Vell was a neighbor, not an event. And everywhere, under everything, the murmur of the dead, thicker than Ada had ever heard it, a choir of ten generations compressed into six streets.

Wren stopped in the middle of the crowd and put her hands over her ears.

Ada knelt beside her. "What is it? Too many people?"

The girl shook her head slowly. Her eyes were enormous. "They're all *talking to me*," she whispered, in the old tongue, and then again in trade-speech, testing whether the sentence worked in a language built for the living. "Not you. Me."

Ada had wondered about that. For nine days the dead had been silent around Wren — silent to *her*, at least. She had assumed the girl was a stone dropped in a pond, stilling every ripple. Now, watching Wren stand in the roaring market of Vell with her hands pressed to her skull, Ada understood she had it backwards. The girl wasn't silencing the pond. She was the bottom of it. Every voice in the city was falling toward her like rain.

"We need to get you indoors," Ada said, and meant it more than she had ever meant anything about walls.

* * *

Mother Ilex found them before they found her, which Ada would later understand was simply how things worked in Vell.

They had taken a room above a chandler's shop in the Charnel Quarter — a room that smelled of tallow and old incense, where the dead below the floor muttered comfortably, like sleepers — and Ada had barely set her pack down when there came a knock with a particular rhythm to it. Three, pause, one. Not a merchant's knock. A scholar's.

The woman in the doorway was sixty if she was a day, tall and thin as a candle, dressed in the undyed wool of the Ilexian order with ink stains up both forearms to the elbow. Her eyes went past Ada immediately, found Wren sitting cross-legged on the bed, and did something Ada had never seen eyes do before: they *recognized* her.

"You," Mother Ilex said softly, in a language that was not trade-speech, not quite the old tongue, but a cousin of it — the way a fossil is a cousin of the animal. "You speak the First Speech."

Wren stared at her. Then, very carefully, she answered — one sentence, slow, formal, the cadence of a greeting learned rather than lived.

Ilex's hand went to her mouth. When she lowered it, her composure was cracked clean through, and what showed underneath was not fear. It was grief, old and patient, the kind that has waited decades for a door to open.

"May I come in?" she asked Ada. "I think we have a great deal to say to each other, and very little time in which the saying will be safe."

"How did you find us?"

"The dead told me." She said it the way another woman might say *the wind changed*. "A carver who hears them walks into my city with a child who stills them, on the same morning my oldest bones begin singing a name nobody has sung since the Ember was forged. I am a scholar, mistress Ashgrove. I followed the citation."

Ada had given her name only to the gate clerk, hours ago. Word traveled fast in Vell. Or — she thought of the whisper she'd caught on the ridge road — *she's coming* — perhaps word had never needed to travel at all.

She stepped aside and let the priestess in.

* * *

They talked through the afternoon, and what Mother Ilex told them, she told in the manner of her order: precisely, completely, and with the terrible calm of someone who has read the ending of the story many times and only now met its beginning walking through her door.

"There was a god," she began, "before there were kingdoms. Not a god of anything — that is the error every scribe makes, trying to file it under harvest or war or the sea. It was a god of *taking*. It devoured. Souls, light, memory, names — whatever a thing was, it ate until the thing was not. The old texts call it the Hollowing, and they agree on almost nothing else except how it ended."

"It didn't end," Wren said quietly. Everyone looked at her. The girl was picking at the frayed edge of her sleeve, not looking up. "It was *put away*."

"Yes," Ilex breathed. "Yes. Put away. Tell me what you know, child."

"I don't know it." Wren frowned, searching for the words. "I *remember* it. Like — like remembering a story someone told you, except the someone was me." She looked up, and her eyes were the flat gray of a winter sky. "There was a forge. Under a mountain. And there was a man who said yes."

Ada felt the room tilt slightly. "Said yes to what?"

"To be the jar." Wren's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The forging needed a will to hold it shut. Fire doesn't close a door; a hand closes a door. So they asked a king — no. They asked a *man*. He became a king after. Because he said yes." She turned the fraying thread around her finger, tighter, tighter. "His name was Aldric. He was young. He was afraid. He said yes anyway."

The fire in the little hearth popped. Downstairs, the chandler's dead murmured on, oblivious.

"Aldric," Ada repeated. The name of the current king of Thornfell, the Regent's throne, the crown whose armies were eating the south. "That's —"

"That is the name on the throne right now," Ilex finished grimly. "Or the name *worn* by it. Listen, carver, because this is the part the bounty boards will never tell you. The binding was never meant to be permanent. No vessel lasts forever — flesh fails, souls fray. The forging built a release into itself, a seam, a way to let Aldric out and end the Hollowing forever once the devourer inside him had starved. It was supposed to take a lifetime. His lifetime. One mortal span, willingly given, and then freedom for everyone, king included."

"And instead?" Ada asked, though she was beginning to dread the answer.

"Instead, someone found the seam." Ilex rose and paced to the window, her shadow long across the floorboards. "Four hundred years ago, when the free cities threw off their kings, Thornfell's dynasty should have ended too. But Aldric did not die. Aldric does not die. And somewhere in those centuries, something learned that the binding could be fed instead of ended. Fed on other souls — bound, harvested, poured into the Ember like oil into a lamp. Every soul it eats keeps the vessel alive and keeps the Hollowing asleep and keeps the *feeder* alive too. Your Regent is not a usurper who stole a throne, Mistress Ashgrove. Your Regent is a parasite that moved into a prison and made itself the warden, and it has been burning the kingdom's dead as lamp-fuel for longer than any archive admits."

Ada's hands had gone cold in her lap. She thought of the coffin-crate on Brannoc's ferry. Dozens of souls packed like cargo. She had thought it smuggling. She had thought it *trade*.

"And the girl?" she managed. "Why is she —"

"The key." Ilex turned from the window. "The forging required materials that no longer exist. Star-metal. First-fire. And speech — the First Speech, the language the world spoke before it learned to lie, the only language a binding can be spoken in and unspoken in. When the forgers needed a vessel for the unbinding-word, a living carrier of the seam, they made one. Not born. *Made*. As you might carve a whistle, carver. As you might carve a key." Her eyes, old and kind and merciless, rested on Wren. "You are not a person who can open the Ember, child. You are the opening. You are the word, given legs."

The silence that followed was the worst Ada had ever sat inside, and she had sat inside graves.

Wren looked down at her own hands, turning them over, palms and backs, as if checking them for hinges. "Then when the door opens," she said, in a small, terribly reasonable voice, "what happens to the door?"

Ilex did not answer quickly enough. Ada watched the girl watch the priestess fail to answer, and saw something in Wren's face fold closed, quietly, the way a hand folds closed around a thing it has decided to keep.

* * *

Later — much later, after Ilex had gone with a promise of archives and warnings and a guard of her order's quiet gray sisters — Ada sat on the edge of the bed while Wren pretended to sleep.

The dead of Vell murmured below the floorboards. Somewhere in that murmur, Ada knew, were souls who remembered the forging, who remembered Aldric young and afraid saying *yes*, who might remember the seam, the rite, the cost. All she had to do was reach down and listen. It was the one thing she had ever been good at.

She reached down.

And heard, beneath the ten thousand murmuring dead of Vell, a single voice rising toward the surface like a bubble through dark water — thin, exhausted, unmistakably *alive* — and it was saying, over and over, in the cadence of a man writing by candlelight against a clock:

*If anyone hears this. If anyone is coming. Hurry.*

Ada sat bolt upright in the dark, her heart slamming, and did not know — could not know — that three hundred miles south, in a palace where a hollow thing wore his face, King Aldric had just used the last minutes of his stolen hour to seal a letter he had no way to send.

But the dead had heard him. The dead of Vell had heard him, and they had carried it up through the floorboards to the one woman in the city who could not help listening.

Beside her, Wren opened her eyes.

"You heard him too," the girl said. It was not a question.
