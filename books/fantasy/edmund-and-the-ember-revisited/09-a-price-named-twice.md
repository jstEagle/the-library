## 9. A Price Named Twice

The reliquary beneath the Temple of Ninefold Dawn smelled of cedar smoke and old paper, and it was there, among shelves of bones that had been catalogued and silenced by careful scholarly hands, that Mother Ilex finally told Ada what the unbinding would cost.

But that was on the third day. The first two days were spent in argument.

* * *

"You cannot stay in Vell," Ilex said, for perhaps the tenth time since they had arrived, setting down a clay cup of barley tea that neither of them would drink. She was a broad woman, gray-streaked and heavy-shouldered, with ink stains to the second knuckle of every finger — a scholar who had clearly once been a laborer, or never stopped being one. Her study occupied the temple's lowest level, a round room walled with ledgers, and she moved through it with the certainty of someone who had read everything twice. "The Regent knows the Key is here. It is only a question of when the summons comes."

"She has a name," Ada said.

"I know what she has." Ilex's voice did not soften, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly banked. "I have spent thirty years learning what she has. Do you know how rare her language is? There are four texts in this city that contain more than fragments of it. Two are forgeries. One is a hymnal so old the parchment has been eaten to lace. And the fourth—" she touched a shelf behind her, where a slim volume lay wrapped in oilcloth, "—I copied out myself from a stone in the Ashen Steps, before I understood what I was copying. A mourning song. She sang it in her sleep last night. All four verses. In order."

Wren was asleep now, actually — curled on a pallet of temple blankets in the corner of the study, worn out from a morning of being examined, questioned, gently turned like an artifact under lamplight. She had borne it badly. She had borne it the way a wild thing bears handling: still, watchful, saving its strength. Only when Ada had sat down beside her and begun, clumsily, to carve a scrap of antler into nothing in particular — no voice in it, just the motion — had the girl's shoulders come down from around her ears.

Ada looked at her sleeping and felt the strange new weight in her chest that she had been refusing to name for three days. It was not the dead. It was heavier than the dead, and warmer.

"Tell me about the Ember," she said. "All of it. Not the temple version. The version you'd tell someone who was going to have to do something about it."

Ilex studied her for a long moment. Then she pulled a ledger from the shelf, blew dust from its cover, and opened it to a page dense with her own cramped hand.

"The Ember was not forged to imprison a king," she said. "That is the story the crown tells, and even the story most rebels tell, because both stories are useful. The truth is older. Before Aldric's line, before Thornfell, before the free cities had names — there was a god. Or what your people would call a god. The oldest tongue has a word for it that translates roughly as *the hunger that thinks*. It ate souls, Ada. Not bodies — souls. Whole villages would be found alive and empty, walking, working, breeding, and nothing home behind the eyes. The kingdoms of that age could not kill it. Killing, as far as anyone could tell, only gave it more to eat."

"And so they bound it."

"They bound it inside a vessel," Ilex corrected. "A prison of flesh, because the hunger needed somewhere to be contained, and containment required a will strong enough to hold the walls shut from within. They searched for such a will and found it in a dying king — a man named Aldric, though not your Aldric; the name is older than the man, which is part of why the binding took him so easily when his turn came. They forged the Ember at the heart of a mountain vault: a forge-heart of bound souls, thousands of them, given willingly at first and then — as the centuries went on and the willing ran out — less willingly. The Ember holds the hunger shut inside its vessel. And the vessel, in every generation since, has been the crowned king of Thornfell."

Ada's hands had gone cold around her cup. "Your Aldric. The real one."

"Aldric the Ninth. Bound at his coronation twenty-two years ago, as his father was, and his grandfather. The rite is called the Kindling. The king kneels at the Ember and takes the hunger into himself, and the Ember's bound souls form the walls between the hunger and the world." Ilex closed the ledger. "It works. It has worked for nine hundred years. But walls need tending, and the Ember needs feeding, and somewhere in the last generation the feeding became — creative."

"The Regent."

"The Regent." Ilex said the word flatly, the way one says the name of a disease. "No one knows what it is. A construct of the old rites, perhaps, grown out of the ceremony itself — a priest-function made flesh, or something that crawled into the shape of one. What we know is what the refugees know: that it wears the king and rules in his name, that it feeds bound souls into the Ember faster than any rite requires, and that it has been searching for something for years. Something the old texts call the Key."

"Wren."

"Wren." Ilex looked over at the sleeping child, and her voice dropped as if the girl might hear even through sleep — and knowing Wren, Ada thought, she might. "The binding was forged by hearers of the dead, in the days when hearers were common. The Ember answers to their craft. And the first forge-masters, when they built it, built a failsafe into it — a way to unmake the binding entirely, should the vessel fail or the hunger breach the walls. That failsafe requires a living key: a soul forged rather than born, threaded through the Ember's making, able to open it from outside. The last key was used up six hundred years ago. The art of forging another was lost." She paused. "Or so we believed. Apparently it was not lost. Apparently it was merely sleeping, in whatever far place that child came from, until someone woke it."

Ada thought of the vision the bones had shown her: the burning vault, the crowned figure eating light. She thought of Wren's amulet, and the silence it had brought down on every dead voice she'd ever heard, like a hand closing over a bell.

"What happens to her," Ada asked, "when it's used?"

And Mother Ilex, who had argued with bishops and smugglers and once, by her own account, a dragon-cult that had gotten ideas above its station, hesitated.

"That," she said, "is the wrong question."

* * *

They sent Wren to the temple kitchens with a novice and a basket of bread ends to distribute, which was either charity or a stratagem, and Ada suspected both. Then Ilex led her deeper — past the reliquary, down a stair that predated the temple, into a round chamber where the mountain itself had been left bare. A single brazier burned at the center. On the floor, inlaid in brass gone green with age, was a diagram: concentric rings of script in the old tongue, and at the center, rendered in lines of gold wire, a shape like a seed split open, light pouring from the crack.

"The Unbinding Rite," Ilex said. "What survives of it. Which is most of it, thanks be to obsessive monks." She crouched by the diagram, her knees cracking, and tapped the innermost ring. "Here is what matters. The Ember is a wall of souls holding back the hunger. To break the wall without releasing the hunger, you must do two things at once: open the lock — that is the Key, that is the child — and carry away the contents. Every soul bound in that forge-heart. Every voice. They cannot simply scatter; scattered, the hunger drinks them and grows strong enough to crack its own prison from inside. They must be *taken*. Held. Carried out whole."

"By whom?"

"By a vessel," Ilex said. "A second vessel. The rite requires a hearer of the dead — someone whose gift can hold soul-stuff without shattering, the way a cup holds water. The hearer opens themselves and takes the Ember's contents into their own body. All of them. Thousands of souls, Ada. Centuries of them. They pass through you and go free — to whatever waits beyond, or to nothing, but free — and you are the door they pass through."

Ada stood very still. The brazier popped. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, the temple bells rang the third hour, and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from a life she had already half-left behind.

"And the hearer?" she asked. Her voice sounded level. She was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride. "After. When the souls have passed through."

Ilex did not look up from the diagram. "The gift burns out. Completely. No hearer who has carried the Ember has ever heard the dead again. Most have never heard anything again. The ones who survived at all." She straightened, and now she did look up, and her eyes were steady and kind and merciless. "You are the only hearer within five hundred miles who isn't hiding in a hole in the ground. I checked. I've been checking for two years, hoping I'd never need to say this sentence. You are the price, bone-carver. The rite has a cost, and the cost has a name, and I am sorry — truly sorry — but the name is yours."

* * *

She walked out of the chamber and up the stairs and through the reliquary without seeing any of it. Her body performed the motions of walking while the rest of her stood in that round room staring at golden wire.

She had known. That was the terrible thing, the thing she kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it. Some part of her had known since the bones showed her the burning vault — since the moment she understood that the Key was a child who slept beside crates of the dead because the dead comforted her, who spoke a language older than cruelty, who had eaten Ada's bread and learned the word for *more* and used it shamelessly. Some part of her had done the arithmetic in the dark and then locked the answer in a drawer and thrown away the drawer.

Now Ilex had opened the drawer and set the answer on the table, and it was small and simple and shaped exactly like her.

She found herself, without deciding to go there, in the temple's upper cloister, where the afternoon light came down gold through the arcade and Wren was sitting on the edge of the fountain, dangling her feet in the water, showing a novice girl how to fold a reed into the shape of a bird. The novice was laughing. Wren was laughing — a sound Ada had heard perhaps four times total, each one filed away like treasure, like evidence.

*Love beginning,* some dry voice in her observed. *Disguised as logistics.* Because that was what she did next. That was what she did instead of screaming or weeping or running, which were the things she wanted to do. She sat down on the fountain's rim, and she began to plan.

"Little bird," she said, using the name that had started as a joke about the girl's wariness and had become, without either of them noticing, simply her name. "Come here a moment."

Wren came. She always came, now. That was the other unbearable thing.

"Mother Ilex and I have been reading the old rites," Ada said. She kept her voice easy, workmanlike, the voice she used for discussing delivery dates with merchants. "For the unbinding. There's a part I'll have to do — the hearing part. Taking the voices in. It's my craft, so it falls to me, the way carving falls to me."

Wren watched her face with those dark, unhurried eyes. The girl missed nothing; Ada knew that better than anyone. She had seen Wren read a room full of strangers in three breaths. So she kept her face smooth the way she kept a blade of bone smooth, stroke after patient stroke, and she lied.

"It will hurt," she said. "A little. Like a fever. And afterward my ears will ring for a while, and I'll be tired, and you'll have to fuss at me to eat, which you're good at." She smiled. It felt like a mask carved from her own face. "But then it's done. Then the Ember is broken and the hollow thing wearing the crown comes apart like wet paper, and no one ever uses you again. No one ever calls you Key. You'll just be Wren."

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Water trickled. Somewhere a temple cat complained about theology.

"You'll hear them," Wren said at last, in her accented, careful Common Tongue. "All of them. In you."

"For a little while. Passing through."

Something moved across the child's face — too fast, too old, gone before Ada could name it. Wren reached out and took Ada's hand, the scarred right one, the carver's hand, and turned it palm-up, and pressed her own small finger against the calluses there, tracing them the way she traced letters in the old tongue.

"In my language," she said slowly, "there is a word for when one person carries another across a river. It is also the word for carrying a fire through wind. It is also the word for—" she frowned, hunting the translation, "—for paying. For a debt that love makes." She looked up. "There is no word for it that means only one of those things. I think your language is missing words."

"I think your language is showing off," Ada said, and Wren almost smiled, and let it go, and jumped down from the fountain's edge and ran off toward the kitchens, reed bird in hand, calling something to the novice in the old tongue that made the girl laugh again.

Ada sat alone by the water and let the mask fall, briefly, into her hands.

* * *

She worked through the evening. That was her answer to everything, always: work. She borrowed tools from the temple's scriptorium and sat in the cell they'd given her and carved. She had brought few pieces north — travel demanded lightness — but she had the whistle-bone, the miner's femur she had never quite finished, and she took it out now and held it under the lamp.

It had been silent since Vell. Since Wren. Everything was silent since Wren, except when the girl slept, and then the dead crept close to her like moths, and Ada caught edges of them: a woman humming, a soldier counting his sins, an old man repeating a name over and over like a prayer or a grudge. Tonight, though, tonight the femur warmed in her hands and gave up its whisper, and it was the miner again, Halvard, dead in a collapsed shaft eleven winters gone, and he said what he always said:

*South road. Soldiers on the south road. Tell them. Tell—*

"Yes," Ada murmured. "I know. I'm going."

And then the voice did something it had never done. It paused. It gathered. And it said, in the tone of a man leaning close over a grave's edge:

*Hearer. Listen. We know what the rite asks. We have always known what the rite asks.*

The lamp flame bent. Cold walked up Ada's spine.

*We are not only cargo, bone-carver. We remember being carried. The last one who opened the door for us — we passed through her, all of us, six hundred years ago, and she was singing when we went. Singing. Remember that, when your hour comes. Remember that it can be borne, because it was borne, and we helped bear it, and we will help bear it again. You will not be alone in there. That is the one mercy of our number.*

Then silence, ordinary silence, the silence of finished bone, and Ada sat with her forehead in her hand and her eyes burning and did not cry, because crying was loud and the walls were thin and somewhere down the hall a child was asleep who could hear everything.

*A debt that love makes.* She turned the phrase over. She put the whistle-bone away. She took out a fresh scrap of antler and began, with steady strokes, to carve a token — a plain disc, a traveler's marker, the kind smugglers left on doors to say *passed this way, safe, keep faith*. She didn't yet know who it was for. She only knew that the plan forming in her, the real plan underneath the lie she'd told Wren, would need roads, and contacts, and a ferryman with a boat and no questions — and that there was exactly one person in the world who owed her that much and hated owing it.

At dawn, a temple runner knocked at her door with a message that had come up from the river quarter before first light, sealed with black wax.

Brannoc's ferry had been seen at the north docks. Brannoc, who had sworn never to run the Vell stretch again. Brannoc, waiting — the note said — for a bone-carver and a child, and asking, in a postscript that made Ada's blood run cold and warm at once, whether she remembered the way through the tunnels under the Ashen Steps, because the south roads were already closed, and soldiers were asking questions at every gate, and time, the ferryman wrote, was a thing they no longer had.
