## 10. The Regent's Summons

The envoy came to Vell on a morning so bright it felt like an accusation.

Ada saw the procession from Mother Ilex's window: twelve riders in lacquered black, their armor polished to the sheen of beetle shells, bearing no sigil at all. That was the first wrongness. Armies carried banners; kings carried crests. Only the Regent's household went unmarked, as if heraldry were a debt they had stopped paying. The riders moved through Vell's market square at a walk, unhurried, and the crowd parted around them the way water parts around a stone — not in fear exactly, but in that older, animal way, the way sheep part from the smell of the abattoir.

"They've come for her," Wren said.

The girl stood at Ada's hip, small hands pressed to the sill, watching the black riders with the flat calm she reserved for things she already understood. She had been doing that more lately — knowing things before there was any reason to know them. Her amulet, the little carved bone bird, lay against her throat and did not sing. Ada had learned to be grateful for its silence and frightened by it in equal measure.

"Maybe they've come for the fish market," Ada said. "Vell is famous for it."

Wren looked up at her with the weary patience of a child correcting a slow adult. "They came for me."

Downstairs, Ilex was already moving through the temple corridors with her sleeves pinned back, directing novices, sealing the reliquary doors. She paused only long enough to grip Ada's arm. "Whatever they offer — and they will offer — remember that the Regent does not send gifts. It sends hooks."

* * *

The audience was held in the temple's receiving hall, which Ilex had insisted upon rather than the magistrate's palace — holy ground, or at least ground that made the black riders keep their voices down. The envoy himself was a slender man in grey silk beneath his traveling cloak, with a face so smooth and symmetrical it seemed carved rather than grown. He introduced himself as Master Corvel, Envoy Extraordinary to the Free Cities, Voice of the Regent's Grace.

Ada counted the details that didn't fit. His boots were clean after three hundred miles of winter road. He never blinked quite fully. And when he smiled, the smile arrived a half-second before any reason for it did.

"Ada Ashgrove," he said, reading her name from a scroll as though tasting it. "Bone-carver. Daughter of the Salt Road tunnels. Once fined in Harrowgate for selling relics of unverified provenance." He looked up. "You've had an interesting life for a woman who wanted only to be left alone."

"I still want that."

"And you shall have it." Corvel unrolled a second scroll, heavier paper, sealed with plain black wax. "A pardon, full and unconditional, for every irregularity of your past. A house in Thornfell — a real one, with southern light. Two thousand crowns. A commission as Carver to the Court, should you wish it, with apprentices of your own choosing." He let the list hang in the incense-thick air. "All of this, in exchange for one delivery. You bring the child to the palace gates. That is all. No chains, no cages. She will be honored. Worshipped, even. The Regent has great tenderness for what the child is."

"The child," Ada said carefully, "is a girl who likes honey on her bread and cries when the geese are slaughtered. There is nothing else to deliver."

"Mother Ashgrove—" 

"Just Ashgrove."

"Ashgrove." Corvel's voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse. "You have heard the dead. You know better than anyone what waits on the other side of this. The Regent's armies are nine days south of here. Nine. When they come, Vell will burn — not because the Regent is cruel, but because Vell has chosen to shelter something that belongs to the crown. Cities that yield are taxed. Cities that resist are *harvested*. You know the word. You've heard the souls in the crates." He leaned forward. "Deliver the key, and Vell pays a tax instead. Two thousand people keep their homes. Their children keep their children. All of it balanced against one girl who, I promise you, will feel nothing but purpose."

Ada thought of Brannoc's coffin-crate on the river, the whispering weight of it, the dozens of dead packed like salted fish. She thought of how Wren slept beside it peacefully, and how that peace had frightened her more than the whispers ever had.

"You've made this offer before," Ada said. "To other people. Other cities."

"Many times."

"How many took it?"

Corvel's smile arrived again, ahead of his eyes. "Enough."

She stood. "Tell your master the free cities aren't for sale, and neither is the girl. Tell him Vell's answer is the same as mine."

"That is a pity," Corvel said, rising without hurry, gathering his scrolls. "Because the deadline matters less than you think. Deadlines are for negotiation. We are past negotiating." At the door he paused, and for just a moment the smooth face did something it hadn't done all morning — it *listened*, tilted slightly, like a man hearing music through a wall. "Interesting company you keep, Ashgrove. Your king says hello."

He was gone before she could make sense of that.

* * *

In the capital, in a body that was not his own, Aldric woke.

It was always like surfacing through ice — the sudden terrible return of sensation, the weight of his own arms, the taste of his own mouth gone sour with someone else's meals. One hour. The binding granted him one hour each night, and the Regent slept through it, or pretended to, and in that hour Aldric was king of a kingdom the size of a candlelit desk.

He lit the candle with hands that shook until the second minute, then steadied. He pulled the night-letters toward him — the ones already written, folded into the shapes of ordinary prayer tokens, waiting for the laundress who owed him her brother's life and smuggled them out in the linen carts. Then he dipped the pen.

*To the hearer,* he wrote. It was how he'd begun addressing her since the bone token had come back through the intercepts, marked with her sign — a spiral cut into whorled bone, unmistakable, impossible. Someone in Vell could hear him. Someone in Vell was carving answers into tokens and sending them north through channels even he hadn't known survived. For two weeks now they had spoken across four hundred miles, a king and a stranger, in fragments no larger than a psalm.

*The envoy Corvel reached you today. I watched him leave the map room three nights ago. Listen to me carefully: do not trust anything he showed you, told you, promised you. Corvel carries the Regent's sight.*

He paused, flexing his fingers, feeling the hour drain.

*I do not mean he reports to it. I mean the thing looks THROUGH him. Whatever room he stands in, the Regent stands in. Whatever faces he sees, it sees. If he looked upon the child, then the Regent knows her face now, knows her size, knows the set of her shoulders when she is afraid. This cannot be undone. But it can be used.*

He wrote faster.

*It will expect you to run west, to the coast, to the free fleet. Every refugee road runs west. Do not go west. The vault is beneath the palace — beneath ME — and the binding can only be broken where it was forged. I have mapped the old ways. Smuggler roads, pre-dynastic, the ones my grandfather sealed and my father forgot. They come up inside the city, under the tanneries by the river gate. I will leave the third grate unlocked. The one shaped like a fish's mouth. Tell the hearer: the fish's mouth, at the turn of the tide, on the new moon. After that the garrison changes and I cannot promise the ways stay empty.*

His hand was cramping now. The hour turned like a key in his chest, and somewhere far below his ribs he felt the Ember stir, that vast slow hunger turning over in its sleep.

*One more thing. The Regent spoke today — through my mouth, at supper, to a general whose name I refuse to learn. It said the Key grows restless. It said the girl dreams of the vault already, walks toward it in her sleep. If this reaches you in time: tie her to something that loves her. Rope breaks. Love holds, if you swear it properly. My grandmother swore oaths over iron and bread. I don't know if it works. I only know the Regent fears it, and everything it fears is worth trying.*

The candle guttered. His hour was ending. He signed the letter with the small spiral — her mark, borrowed back, a promise that he read her — and folded it into the shape of a prayer token, and slid it beneath the loose brick where the laundress would find it at dawn.

Then the ice closed over him again, and the body that wore his crown opened its eyes, and smiled at nothing, and rang for wine.

* * *

The night-letter reached Ada three days later, carried in a bale of altar linens, tucked between a stole and a funeral cloth. Ilex brought it to her personally, and Ada knew what it was before she unfolded it — she had begun to know the weight of Aldric's handwriting, the cramped urgency of a man writing against a clock.

She read it twice. Then she sat down on the temple step and read it a third time, aloud, softly, while Wren braided grass stems beside her and pretended not to listen with every part of her body.

"*Tie her to something that loves her,*" Ada repeated.

"Is that about me?" Wren asked, not looking up.

"It's about keeping you safe."

"I'm always safe when you're lying to me," Wren said, with devastating mildness, and went on braiding.

Ada looked at the token in her hand — the king's careful, desperate script — and understood at last why Corvel had paused at the door and tilted his head like a man hearing music. *Your king says hello.* The envoy had seen the letters. Had perhaps been present, with those borrowed eyes, when they were written. Which meant the Regent knew about the correspondence. Which meant either the escape plan was already a trap —

— or the Regent believed it was a trap, and had let the letters through precisely because it wanted them running east, into the walls of Thornfell, carrying the key directly to the vault in their own two stubborn hands.

Or both. Or neither. She was a bone-carver, not a spymaster, and every road she turned down ended in the same blind wall: *someone knows what you'll do before you decide it.*

But there was one thing the Regent's sight could not have seen, because it had not happened yet.

That evening, in the workshop behind the temple, Ada took up her knife and a length of antler and began to carve. Not a message. Not a token. An oath-token, the old kind, the kind her grandmother's generation had sworn over iron and bread — a circle broken by two hands meeting, one large, one small. She carved it slowly, speaking the words under her breath in the trade-tongue and then, clumsily, syllable by syllable, in the fragments of the old language Wren had taught her on the tunnel roads. *What is given is given. What is claimed is kept. I am hers and she is mine, until the last bone forgets its name.*

When she finished, Wren was standing in the doorway, watching.

"What's that?" the girl asked.

"Insurance," Ada said, and held it out. "Take my hands."

And far away, in a palace above a sleeping forge-heart, something ancient and hungry felt the oath take hold — and for the first time in three hundred years, hesitated.
