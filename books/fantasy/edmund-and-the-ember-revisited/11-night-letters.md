## 11. Night Letters

The first token arrived sewn into the hem of a fishmonger's coat.

Ada found it three days after the envoy left Vell with her refusal still warm in his mouth — a flat oval of antler, no bigger than her thumbnail, worked so fine she almost mistook it for a button. Only when her thumb traced the grooves did she feel it: not words, but *pressure*, the way a sealed letter presses against the fingers of someone who knows what letters weigh. She carried it to the lamp, turned it under the light, and saw the carving for what it was. A crown. A crack running through the crown. And beneath it, in the smugglers' knot-script she had learned as a girl on the river roads, a single line:

*The envoy's eyes are not his own. Trust nothing he touched.*

She sat down slowly on her stool in Mother Ilex's guest room and laughed once, a short, disbelieving sound. Somewhere behind walls thick enough to swallow armies, a king was passing messages through fish coats.

* * *

It took another week to understand the system, and when she understood it, she marveled at its shabby genius. Aldric could write only during his stolen hour, and he could send nothing out himself; the Regent read every scrap of parchment that crossed the palace threshold. But the palace kitchens bought bone by the cartload — marrow bones, knuckle bones, the cheap white freight of a capital that ate well while its provinces starved. A carver's mark on a soup bone drew no attention. The tokens traveled out in the bellies of commerce: in tallow barrels, in grain sacks, in the false bottoms of fish carts, passed hand to hand along a chain of smugglers who had never met each other and were paid in ways none of them could trace.

Ada's replies went back the same way, slower. She carved at night, after Wren slept, working symbols into slivers of sheep rib that Ilex's people slipped into the next shipment of votive bones bound for the capital's temples. The Regent's clerks catalogued religious goods with bored thoroughness, but they did not read them. Who reads a prayer?

So began the correspondence that would have gotten them both flayed alive if either were caught: a king and a bone-carver, speaking to each other through the dead medium of animal bone, one hour of thought at a time.

* * *

His tokens came roughly every fourth night, though the intervals wandered like a drunkard's path. Ada learned to read more than the words in them. Early tokens were cramped, overfull, the script of a man pouring out a year of silence through a keyhole. He wrote of the palace in the manner of a prisoner describing his cell to someone who might someday come to the window.

*The Ember lies beneath the throne hall,* one said. *Not beneath the throne itself — beneath the floor before it, where petitioners kneel. They built the hall above it deliberately. Every supplication spoken there feeds down into the binding. Clever, cruel men designed this place.*

Another: *There is a way in. The old smuggler roads run under the western wall. Your people cut them, before the city had its name. The Regent bricked most, but the masons' guild kept one open for their own reasons. Ask for the Door of the Ninth Kneeler. It opens from the river side only at low water, and only to a hand that knows the knock.*

*Your people.* Ada ran her thumb over those two words longer than any of the others. He knew. Of course he knew — Ilex would have told him about the woman from the river country, the one who'd grown up ferrying contraband through the hills. But seeing it in his hand, in his careful, clerk-trained script, made it real in a way she hadn't let it be yet. Somewhere south, a king was counting on roads she had run as a child, and expecting her to run them again, toward the thing that wore him.

She carved her reply that night until her eyes burned: *I know the knock. My father taught me. Tell me what guards the vault besides fear.*

His answer came four nights later, and it was the first time his script shook.

*Nothing guards it. That is the horror. The Ember does not need guarding. It calls, and what it calls comes willingly or otherwise. If you bring the girl within a mile of it, you will not need to convince her to walk to it. You will need to convince yourself you can stop her.*

Ada looked across the room at Wren, asleep in the narrow bed with her amulet clutched in one fist, her breath slow and even, her face in sleep unguarded and unbearably young. Then she looked at the token in her hand, and felt the first cold weight of what she was actually planning settle onto her shoulders.

*Understood,* she carved. It was a lie of brevity. Nothing was understood. But the tokens were small, and some truths did not fit.

* * *

The correspondence changed something in her that she did not notice at first.

She had spent her whole adult life listening to the dead, and the dead were easy company in their way — they wanted nothing, needed nothing, asked only to be heard and then released. The living were the ones who frightened her. Living people wanted things. Living people stayed.

Aldric was neither living nor dead, and he wanted nothing from her except information, which was a currency she understood. Their exchanges had the clean shape of trade. He gave her the geography of his prison; she gave him word of the road north, of Ilex's preparations, of the refugee columns moving ahead of the Regent's army. Each token was a transaction. Each transaction was safe.

But somewhere around the tenth exchange, the transactions began to grow margins.

He started asking questions that weren't operational. *Does the girl dream in your tongue or hers?* he wrote once. And: *When you carve for the dead — do you choose what they say, or do they? I ask because I have begun to wonder whether I choose anything at all.* And once, startlingly: *What was the river like? In the letters of my grandfather's reign there is a description of the Ashgrove ferries at festival. Lanterns on every hull. I used to imagine it. I had very little else to imagine.*

Ada caught herself, one gray morning, standing at her workbench with a half-carved token and an answer to that last question already forming — lanterns on every hull, yes, but also the mud smell after, and her father's hands cracked from rope-burn, and the way the whole town sang off-key because the good singers all left for the cities. She caught herself wanting to tell him. Not for strategy. For the telling.

She set down the graver and stared at the little bone in her palm as if it had bitten her.

*You are writing letters to a king,* she told herself sternly. *A king who is mostly not home. This is not friendship. This is reconnaissance.*

Her hands, entirely without her permission, finished the token anyway.

* * *

Wren noticed before Ada did. Children always do; they are barometers of attention, and they fall to pieces long before the storm arrives.

It began with small things. Wren stopped bringing Ada her carvings to look at. She took to sitting in the doorway of the workroom instead of beside the bench, watching with a flat, unreadable expression whenever Ada bent over a token late into the night. When Ada spoke to her, the girl answered in monosyllables, in the common tongue, though lately she had been teaching Ada the old language freely, patiently, delightedly — correcting Ada's botched vowels with a solemnity that made the lessons feel like ceremonies.

Now the lessons stopped too.

"You're tired," Wren observed one evening, standing in the doorway with her arms crossed, in the tone of a much older woman.

"A little."

"From the bones." Not a question. "The night bones. You talk to them when you think I'm sleeping."

"They're not—" Ada paused. Lying to Wren had become appallingly easy since the vault vision, since *a price named twice*, since the afternoon she had smiled at the girl and promised everything would be simple if they just reached the mountain. The ease of it frightened her more than the lying did. "They're letters," she said finally, giving her a piece of truth like tossing meat to keep a wolf from the door. "From a friend far away."

"What friend?"

"A friend who needs help."

Wren's eyes narrowed. "The king."

Ada's hands went still on the bench. "Who told you about the king?"

"Ilex talks." Wren shrugged, elaborately casual, a gesture she had clearly learned from watching Brannoc and performed with a child's disastrous precision. "Everyone talks. Nobody talks to *me* about it." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it immediately — Ada watched the girl hate it, watched her jaw tighten against the wobble — and then Wren turned and was gone, bare feet slapping down the corridor toward the courtyard where the temple children played.

Ada sat with her unfinished token and felt guilt arrive in her chest like weather.

Because the girl was right. That was the poison of it. Ada had spent weeks bent over a king's voice, planning a rescue whose entire mechanism was the child asleep in the next room, and she had told Wren nothing — not the plan, not the danger, not the price. She had decided, in the privacy of her own fear, that Wren was cargo. Precious cargo. Cargo to be protected, informed of nothing, delivered.

And Wren, who remembered being forged, who had asked in the bone-market whether anyone would want her when she was used up — Wren had watched the one person who claimed her spend her nights whispering to somebody else.

* * *

Three nights later, the tokens nearly killed them both.

It was past midnight. Ada had a new token warm from the graver, Aldric's latest spread open beside it — he had written, in unusual detail, of the vault approach, of the Door of the Ninth Kneeler, of the exact number of steps between the river gate and the forge-heart. *Forty-one,* he had written. *I counted them for eleven years before I understood why I was counting. Some part of me was keeping the road ready for you before I knew you were coming.*

She had read that line four times. She was reading it a fifth when she realized the room was too quiet.

Wren's bed was empty.

The blanket was thrown back, the amulet gone from the pillow — gone, Ada's mind supplied instantly, *worn*, because the girl never went anywhere without it — and the window latch stood open to a Vell night that had turned cold and damp, fog pooling in the streets below like spilled milk.

Ada did not remember crossing the room. She was down the stairs and into the courtyard with her cloak half-fastened, calling the girl's name in a whisper that scraped her throat raw, because shouting would bring the watch and the watch brought questions and questions brought attention and attention, now, meant the Regent's people. The envoy had left. His eyes had not. She felt them everywhere, the way you feel a draft from a door you can't find.

The temple precinct was empty. The bone-market lane was empty. Ada stood in the fog at the mouth of the pilgrim's stair, heart slamming, and did the thing she had sworn off doing since the day she'd taken in a feral child who silenced the dead: she reached for her gift, cast it wide, and listened.

Vell's dead murmured back at her — the usual sediment of any old city, merchants and plague-taken and one indignant archbishop — and beneath them, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, something else. Not a voice. A *pull*. A thread of silence shaped exactly like a small girl, moving downhill, toward the river, toward the dark.

Toward the water. Toward the low gate. Toward the Door of the Ninth Kneeler, which opened only at low water, and which Ada had mentioned in exactly one token, to exactly one person, in a city crawling with the Regent's sight.

*No,* she thought, and then, worse: *oh, please, no.* Because there were two explanations, and one of them was that Wren had simply followed the Ember's call the way Aldric warned she would — and the other was that somewhere between the fish carts and the temple stores, a bone token had been read by the wrong pair of borrowed eyes.

Ada ran.

The fog swallowed the stair behind her. Ahead, down where the river breathed against the old stones, something small moved in the dark — a slight figure walking with terrible purpose, arms loose, head bowed, the amulet at her throat catching the lamplight from the quay like a single held ember. Walking the way the sleepwalkers walked in the stories Ilex told. Walking toward forty-one steps she didn't know existed.

"Wren!" Ada's voice tore free at last, discipline abandoned, and the girl did not turn, did not falter, did not hear her at all.

Ada put her head down and sprinted along the quay, boots hammering wet stone, closing the distance in lung-burning strides, and threw herself the last few feet to catch the girl around the shoulders —

— and Wren spun in her grip, eyes open, *awake*, furious and terrified and crying all at once, and shoved her with both hands.

"You're sending me away," the girl said in the old tongue, the words coming out broken and ancient and nine years old. "The king wants me and you're *taking me to him*, everyone keeps talking and nobody tells me, and I heard the bones, I heard them singing under the ground tonight, they called me by my *first name*, the one from before, and you weren't even listening —"

"I was coming for you," Ada gasped. "I will always come for you."

"You promise?" Wren's fists were knotted in Ada's cloak now, holding on even as she accused. "Because everybody promises. The ones who made me promised. The king promised somebody something, the bones say so. Everybody promises until the using-up part."

Behind them, out on the black water, a bell buoy tolled once in the current. Low water. The Door of the Ninth Kneeler was open somewhere below the quay, breathing river-cold air into the dark, and Ada understood with sudden, sickening clarity that the girl hadn't been summoned here by accident at all — that Wren had heard the vault calling and had run not *toward* it, but to the one place she thought Ada's secret road began, trying to see the prison everyone was taking her to before anyone could load her onto the cart.

Guilt and tenderness hit Ada together, indistinguishable, the same weight.

"No more secrets," Ada said, and heard her own voice shake. "Not about this. Not ever again. Come inside where it's warm, and I'll tell you all of it — the king, the vault, the plan, all of it. Even the parts that frighten me." Especially those, she thought. *Especially those.*

Wren searched her face for a long moment, fog beading in her hair, and then nodded once, a small, grave nod, and took Ada's hand.

And it was as their fingers closed — skin to skin, the carver and the key — that every dead voice in Vell went silent at once, the way they had on the blizzard night a lifetime ago, and Ada felt, rising up through the soles of her boots from the deep places under the city, a single new sound.

Not a voice. A knocking.

Patient. Rhythmic. Forty-one steps down, and getting louder.
