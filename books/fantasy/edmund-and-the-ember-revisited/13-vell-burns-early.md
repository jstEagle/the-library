## 13. Vell Burns Early

The bells of Vell rang wrong.

Ada had learned the city's bells in the eleven days they'd sheltered beneath Mother Ilex's roof — the dawn peal that stumbled on its third note, the noon bell with its cracked bronze hum, the evening carillon that played the old harvest hymn badly, as if the ringer had learned it from a grandmother who'd learned it wrong. She'd come to find the imperfections comforting. A city that rang its bells imperfectly was a city that trusted tomorrow enough to be careless in it.

So when the bells began at midnight — all of them at once, no hymn, no pattern, just iron hammered in panic — she was out of bed before her mind finished waking.

Wren was already standing at the window, small and still in her nightshift, watching the eastern sky. The girl didn't scream. She never screamed. But her hand found Ada's without looking, and her fingers were cold as river stones, and she said one word in the old tongue that Ada now knew meant *fire*.

Through the window, past the temple spires and the tiled roofs of the weavers' quarter, the horizon glowed a deep, throbbing orange. Not sunrise. Sunrise came gently to Vell, gray-gold over the river flats. This light climbed and pulsed like something breathing.

"They came early," Ilex said from the doorway. The scholar-priestess was already dressed, already armored in her plain traveling wool, her white braid coiled and pinned as though she had slept expecting this. Perhaps she had. "The envoy's deadline was three days hence. The Regent never intended to honor it. The summons was never an offer, bone-carver. It was a clock."

Ada's hands were shaking as she dressed. She noticed them distantly, the way she noticed weather. *Breathe. Boots. Knife. Wren.* The arithmetic of survival, done in the dark before thought could interfere.

"Where do we go?"

"Down." Ilex was pulling books from a shelf — not reading them, choosing them, weighing each against some private scale of irreplaceable. "Vell has two histories. The one above ground, which will burn tonight. And the one below, which has burned twice already and learned how to survive it. The catacombs run under the whole old quarter and out through the aqueduct galleries to the north cliffs. If we move quickly—"

A sound rolled across the city then, deep enough that Ada felt it in her back teeth. Not thunder. Thunder broke and echoed. This was a single sustained groan, like a giant bending iron, and after it came the crackle — the unmistakable, hungry whisper of fire finding rooftops.

"The east gate," Ilex said quietly. "They've brought a ram. Or worse."

* * *

The streets were already chaos. That was the thing no one told you about the fall of a city — how fast order became a rumor. Ada had imagined armies marching in, gates broken, soldiers in the streets. Instead there was only people: hundreds of them pouring out of doorways with whatever their arms could hold, children carried, grandmothers dragged, a man running with nothing but his cat clutched to his chest as if the cat were the last thing in the world worth saving. Maybe it was. Smoke moved down the avenues ahead of the flames like a tide coming in, thick and acrid, tasting of pitch and burnt thatch.

Ilex led them not toward the main roads but down a stair between a chandler's shop and a shuttered dye-house, into an alley so narrow Ada had to turn sideways. Wren stayed glued to her hip. The girl had not spoken since the bells; she simply moved when Ada moved, stopped when Ada stopped, her amulet — the carved bone key that had started everything — tucked inside her shirt where Ada had insisted it stay, hidden from every eye.

At the alley's end stood a low arch sealed with an iron gate older than any kingdom Ada could name. Ilex pressed her palm to a worn depression in the stone and spoke four syllables in the old tongue. Ada recognized two of them now. *Open* and *remember*. The gate swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, as if the dead who'd built it had oiled them yesterday.

"The catacombs," Ilex said. "Mind the first gallery. The bones are loose there. They shift."

"I'll manage," Ada said. "I've spent my life around bones that talk."

She regretted the flippancy the moment she said it, because Ilex looked at her with an expression that was not quite pity and said, "Then you know better than most what is coming for us. Come."

* * *

Below Vell, the air was cold stone and old tallow. Niches lined the walls floor to ceiling, thousands of them, each holding skulls stacked like fruit or long bones bundled with wire gone green. Oil lamps hung at intervals, and someone — the living city above, in its last confident days — had kept them filled. Their light made the galleries feel almost domestic, a corridor of quiet neighbors.

Ada felt them immediately. She always did, in places like this — the low murmur of the interred, not words exactly, more like the sound of a village heard through a wall. But tonight the murmur was agitated. Tonight the dead of Vell knew what was happening above them, and their restlessness pressed against her hearing like a crowd pressing against a door.

"Mama." Wren tugged her sleeve. "Mama, they're scared."

Ada had stopped correcting the girl weeks ago. She knelt, taking Wren's face in her hands. In the lamplight the child looked impossibly young — six years old, maybe seven, thin as a whistle-bone, eyes too old for the face they lived behind. "Listen to me, little bird. Whatever happens tonight, you stay where I can touch you. You don't stop for anything. Not for voices, not for doors, not for me unless I tell you to run. Do you understand?"

Wren's chin trembled. Then, with the terrible gravity she sometimes wore like an inherited cloak: "You're going to leave me somewhere again."

The words went through Ada like a blade finding the gap in armor. Because it was true — someone had left this child somewhere, more than once; the bones had shown her that much before they'd shown her fire. Someone had set her down in the snow outside Ada's door and walked away, and whatever that person had been fleeing, it had cost them everything.

"No," Ada said, and made herself say it again, firmer, because the first time had cracked. "No. Not tonight. Tonight you are stuck with me like burrs on a sock. Come on."

Ilex was waiting at a junction where three galleries met, holding up her lamp. "The northern route passes under the river district. There's a stair up into the tanneries, and from the tanneries—" She stopped.

From somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of stone, came a new sound. Screaming — but not the screaming of panic. The screaming of a city being *found*.

And beneath it, threading through the stone, Ada heard something else. Something that made her blood go to ice water.

Hooves. Many hooves. And among them, faint but unmistakable, a voice speaking in the cadence of command — a voice with no warmth in it at all, the voice of the Regent's outriders, the hollow men whose orders sounded like sentences read off a slate.

"They're in the streets already," she whispered. "Not at the gates. *In* the streets. How?"

"Sappers," Ilex said grimly. "Or traitors. Vell has always believed itself too clever to be betrayed." She closed her eyes briefly, and for one moment the scholar-priestess looked her age, and older. "The north stair may be watched. We go west instead, under the granaries, and out through the old ferry landing."

"The ferry landing," Ada repeated. "Brannoc."

* * *

They ran. There was no other word for it — an old woman of sixty years, a priestess carrying a sack of books, and a child, moving at a stumbling lope through corridors of the dead while a city died overhead. Plaster sifted down from the vaulted ceilings with every distant impact. Twice they passed side galleries where refugees had already fled — families huddled with candles, faces turned away, pretending not to see the strangers because strangers meant questions and questions meant stopping.

In the third hour — or what Ada guessed was the third hour; time underground was a rumor — they reached the western junction, and Ada heard the fighting before she saw it.

Steel. Shouting. The particular wet sounds that meant steel had finished its argument.

Ilex raised her fist and they froze in the black mouth of a side passage. Ahead, the gallery opened onto the ferry stairs — wide steps descending to the underground dock where the river boats moored in Vell's secret harbor, the smugglers' tithe-road Ada knew better than she wanted to. Lamplight flickered up from below. And across it moved shadows: tall, wrong-jointed shadows in the Regent's livery, moving with that terrible economy the hollow men had, wasting no motion because they wasted no soul on fear.

And holding the head of the stairs — alone, armed with a boat-hook and a boarding axe, roaring curses in three languages — stood Brannoc.

He looked terrible. He looked magnificent. His grey beard was singed, his coat was soaked in something dark, and he fought the way he poled his ferry: with a patient, brutal economy, giving ground grudgingly and making every inch of it cost. Three hollow men lay crumpled on the steps already. A fourth circled him warily.

"He held the landing," Ilex breathed. "He's been holding it. For us. For anyone coming this way."

"No." Ada's mouth was dry. "For the tithe. He always said the road owed him." But her eyes stung, and she knew the lie for what it was.

Brannoc saw them then — saw Ilex's lamp, saw Ada's face, saw the small figure clutching her hand. And he grinned, that gap-toothed ferryman's grin she'd known for twenty years, across a hundred crossings and a thousand secrets.

"There's my bone-witch!" he bellowed, parrying a thrust without even looking at it. "Told you I'd get you north! Didn't say which direction north faced!" He laughed at his own joke, because Brannoc laughed at all his jokes, especially the bad ones, especially now.

"Come *on*," Ada shouted. "Leave them! Get on the boat!"

"Boat's burned, love." He jerked his chin toward the dock, where indeed the ferry hull sat gutted and smoking. "But there's a skiff under the east pilings, and there's a tunnel past the third arch that runs clean out to the cliffs — your tunnel, the one you showed me when we were young and stupid. Take it. Take the girl. Take the priestess and her damn books." Another blow, another hollow man folding. "I'll keep the stairs honest a few minutes longer."

"You'll die," Ada said. The words came out flat and true and unbearable.

"Aye," said Brannoc cheerfully. "Everyone does. Question's only what you're doing when it happens, and I'll tell you, love, I've had worse evenings planned than this one."

The fifth hollow man came up the stairs behind him.

Ada would remember it forever in fragments: the flash of the blade, Brannoc turning too slow, the boat-hook swinging, the axe falling, the way he went down still facing outward, still between the enemy and the stairs. She would remember Ilex grabbing her arm and hauling her forward because her legs had forgotten their work. She would remember Wren crying out — not in the old tongue, but in plain trade-common, one raw word: "*No!*"

And she would remember this: as they pounded down the steps, over the fallen, past the wrecked ferry, Brannoc lifted his head from the stones where they'd left him bleeding, and looked straight at her, and smiled.

And Ada — who had heard the dead her whole life, who had carved ten thousand bones and caught ten thousand last words — heard him one final time. Not through bone. Through nothing at all. Just the last spark of a soul leaving a body it had used well, reaching for her across five feet of bloody stone:

*Worth it, love. Every crossing. Go be somebody's north.*

Then the spark guttered, and the man on the stairs was only meat and memory, and the loudest silence Ada had ever heard roared in where his voice had been.

She ran. Grief could burn later. Right now it was fuel, and the fire was behind them.

* * *

The tunnel past the third arch was hers, in the way that anything is yours when you found it at seventeen with a boy who later hanged for smuggling and a ferryman who later died on stairs. Narrow, unlit, dripping, running up at a cruel grade through the cliff's roots. Ada went first with Ilex's lamp, calling the turns from twenty-year-old memory — *left at the split, mind the shaft, count thirty paces to the ladder* — and the tunnel obliged her, the way old roads sometimes do for the people who loved them badly and honestly.

Behind them, Vell burned. She could hear it even here: a vast, distant crackling, punctuated by the deep boom of collapsing towers. Once, threaded through it, she heard singing — hundreds of voices, high and defiant, some hymn of the free cities rising from the burning streets above, and she did not let herself think about whether the singers escaped.

Twice, Wren stumbled, and both times Ada caught her, and the second time she simply picked the girl up and carried her, because grief makes you strong in stupid ways and she had grief to spare.

Near the top, where gray pre-dawn light leaked through the cliff-mouth ahead, Wren stirred against her shoulder and spoke, very quietly, in the old tongue. Ada knew enough now to catch the shape of it.

*"The man on the stairs. He is still singing. He says the road is paid."*

Ada stopped walking. Stood in the dark with a dead man's last gift in her ears and a living child in her arms, and let herself cry — silently, furiously, for exactly ten breaths. Then she wiped her face on her shoulder and climbed.

Survival's arithmetic was bitter, but it was simple: the living owed the dead movement. Nothing else. Just movement, northward, until the debt could be paid in kind.

They came out onto the north cliffs as the sun rose over a burning city, and Mother Ilex stood beside them, her books clutched to her chest, and said quietly, "The Regent believes terror is a message. Let us make certain the message arrives garbled." She turned to Ada, and her eyes were red-rimmed and hard as flint. "The Ashen Steps, bone-carver. The salt road south. The army will take the long way around the mountains. We cannot afford to."

Ada looked south, toward the capital, toward the vault, toward the Ember and the hollow king and the price she had already secretly agreed to pay. She looked down at Wren, asleep against her chest, the key warm as a coal beneath the girl's shirt.

Three days early. The Regent had struck three days early — which meant the Regent was afraid, and things that were afraid made mistakes.

"We beat them there," Ada said. "Whatever it costs."

Far below, Vell's bells finally fell silent, one by one, like voices going out.
