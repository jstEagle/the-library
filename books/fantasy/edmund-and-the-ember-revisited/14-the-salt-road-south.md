## 14. The Salt Road South

The column found them before they found it.

Ada heard it first — not with her ears but with the dead, who had grown restless in the days since Vell's smoke had risen behind them like a second dawn that refused to fade. The souls in the relics she carried murmured of footsteps, thousands upon thousands, a tide of the living moving north along the salt road while the dead moved south through her bones. Then, around a bend where the road narrowed between two hills stitched with frost-killed gorse, she saw them: a river of people, gray and brown and ash-colored, flowing away from everything they had lost.

Refugees. Hundreds of them. Perhaps a thousand.

Wren's hand found hers without either of them looking. That was new, or rather it was old now — three weeks since Vell burned, and the girl still reached for Ada's hand at every unexpected sight, as if to confirm that the world could produce horrors and Ada would remain.

"They're going the wrong way," Wren said.

"No," Ada said quietly. "They're going the only way left."

Mother Ilex stood beside them, her arm bound in a sling of boiled leather and linen, the wound from the Ashen Steps' edge — no, not yet, that was ahead; Ilex's arm was bound from the catacombs beneath Vell, where a falling beam had caught her shoulder as the city's gates gave way. She watched the column with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she did not want finished. "They'll slow us," she said. "But they'll also hide us. A cart with three travelers is a curiosity. A cart among five hundred is nothing."

Brannoc should have said something crude and wise just then. Brannoc should have spat over the side of his ferry and told them the river always knew best. But Brannoc was under the rubble of the east gate of Vell, and the last thing his soul had said to Ada — *tell the girl the water remembers her* — was a debt she carried now alongside everything else.

"Come on, then," Ada said, and stepped onto the salt road.

* * *

The column absorbed them the way water absorbs a stone: completely, indifferently. Within an hour Ada knew their names and their losses, because the dead who traveled with refugees were generous gossipers. There was Tammas Ovel, a tanner whose wife had stayed behind to hold their shop door shut against the Regent's outriders and had not been holding anything when the outriders finished. There was a grandmother called Sef who carried her entire household's dead in a sack of finger-bones and asked Ada, within ten minutes of meeting her, whether the bones could be made to speak. There were children too many to count, walking with the terrible obedience of children who have learned that questions get people killed.

Ada kept her hood up and her hands in her pockets. Word of what she was — hearer, bone-carver, the woman the Regent's envoys wanted — had followed her out of Vell like a smell. She carved nothing on the march. She let the dead talk only into her own skull, and even then sparingly, because every voice cost something she was beginning to suspect she would need all of.

The salt road ran south along the spine of the hills, wide enough for carts, worn white by centuries of trade. It bent west around the Mirefen lowlands, crossed the Stonebridge at Corrow Ford, and came at last to the capital by the gentlest possible route — twenty-two days, the carter Sef consulted estimated, maybe twenty-four with this many feet and wheels. Safe. Watched, certainly; the Regent's patrols would walk its length like wardens down a prison corridor. But safe from the land itself.

Twenty-two days.

Ada did the other arithmetic at night, scratching lines in the dirt by firelight when Wren slept against her side. The Regent's army was marching south along the same corridor, a day behind the column's tail and closing. The binding ritual needed the Key — needed Wren — and the summons that had come to Vell before it burned made plain that the Regent intended to collect. Every day of safety bought on the salt road was a day the ritual's preparations grew more complete, a day closer to the moment when distance would stop mattering and the Ember would simply reach out and pull.

And there was the other thing. The thing she had not told Ilex. The thing she barely admitted to herself.

At night, Wren walked in her sleep.

It had started small — the girl turning in her bedroll until her head pointed south, like a compass needle finding its metal. Then it had become standing. On the fourth night after Vell, Ada had woken to find Wren upright in the dark, eyes open and empty as coins, facing the capital, her lips moving in the old tongue. Ada had spoken her name and shaken her gently, and Wren had come back to herself shivering, saying, *I dreamed about stairs. Long ones, going down.* Ada had tied a cord between her own wrist and the girl's ever since, and pretended it was for warmth.

The Ember was calling its key home. And the salt road was letting it call.

* * *

On the sixth morning, the road forked.

Not formally — there was no signpost, no crossroads shrine. But the column slowed at a place where the salt road continued south in its broad white certainty, and to the east a track broke away, climbing toward a line of jagged heights that the locals named with their chins rather than their mouths. The Ashen Steps. Even the name arrived half-swallowed.

Ada felt the dead notice it before anyone spoke. The souls in her pocket-relics went quiet in a way she had learned to distrust — the silence of things listening.

A knot of column-folk had gathered where the tracks parted. An old drover was arguing with a younger man whose family had come out of the Mirefen villages. "The Steps are haunted," the drover said flatly. "Everyone knows it. The massacre at Karroch Fen, eighty years back — the dead never settled. You don't take children up there."

"And down here?" the young man said. "Down here we wait for the patrols to catch us, or the army to walk over us, or the winter to finish what the war started? I've got four little ones. I'd rather face ghosts than soldiers. Ghosts only want remembering."

Ada stopped walking.

She had known about the Ashen Steps since she was a girl running Brannoc's smuggler roads — the high pass that cut east over the Karroch massif and dropped into the valley of the capital's river, six days of climbing instead of twenty-two of walking. Smugglers used it in desperate seasons, when the price of speed outweighed the price of blood. Brannoc had taken it twice in his life and spoken of it once, drunk, with a look on his face she had never seen sober. *The dead up there don't haunt you,* he'd said. *They interview you.*

Six days. The army was nine days from the ford at worst. Six days put Ada and Wren in the capital's shadow before the Regent's war machine arrived — before the vault beneath the palace was guarded by anything more than ritual and confidence. Twenty-two days put them there long after, if they arrived at all, if Wren's sleepwalking didn't simply deliver her to the enemy's doorstep like a parcel.

Ilex saw her looking. The priestess had spent thirty years reading faces in confessionals, and she read Ada's now. "No," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said everything. You're counting." Ilex turned to look at the eastern track, at the heights wearing their crown of cloud. "The Ashen Steps are a graveyard with a path through it. And your gift is a lamp in a graveyard. They will see you coming, Ada. Everything dead for forty miles will see you."

"Then they'll see me coming fast."

Ilex closed her eyes briefly, the gesture of a woman praying for patience she suspected was not coming. "And the child?"

"The child is being pulled south every night whether we walk slowly or quickly. The question isn't whether the road is dangerous. The question is which danger we choose instead of having it chosen for us." Ada heard her own voice and recognized something in it she hadn't heard since Vell burned: steadiness. Since the city fell she had been carried — by grief, by the column, by the simple momentum of putting one foot past the other. This was the first decision that felt like hers.

A small tug at her hand. Wren had come up silently, the way she did, and was staring at the eastern track with an expression Ada couldn't parse — fear and recognition braided together.

"I know those mountains," Wren said.

"You can't. You've never—"

"Not with my eyes." The girl's grip tightened. Her voice dropped into the register she used only for the oldest things, the words that predated kingdoms, though she translated as she spoke, a habit she'd developed teaching Ada fragments on the tunnels north of Vell. "*The stones remember the burning. The stones want someone to ask them about it.*" She looked up at Ada, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain all at once. "They've been waiting a long time for someone who could listen. Take the short road. Please. I'm tired of being pulled. I want to *walk* somewhere."

Ada looked at the salt road — the safe road, the slow road, the road where Sef's finger-bones and Tammas Ovel's grief and a thousand ordinary lives flowed at the pace of oxen toward whatever mercy the world had left. She looked at the heights.

For eleven years she had built her life the way she built her whistles: carefully, hollowly, with every passage drilled so nothing could catch inside it. Isolation as craftsmanship. Safety as art. And Vell had burned anyway, and Brannoc had died anyway, and the child she loved was being reeled southward by a forge-heart full of screaming souls regardless of how prudently she walked.

There was no careful route anymore. There was only choosing.

"All right," Ada said. "We take the Steps."

* * *

Telling the column was short work. Most shook their heads and muttered about ghosts; a handful — the young man from the Mirefen, two brothers with a mule, a widow named Kess who had lost everyone worth losing and claimed the dead held no more terror for her than the living — asked to come along. Ada refused them as gently as she could. Nine people could move fast and quiet. Twelve could not, and every extra soul was another one the Regent's scouts might mark.

Ilex came, obviously. She announced it by adjusting her sling and starting up the track without discussion, radiating the moral authority of the gravely injured.

They climbed through the morning, and the salt road fell away below them like a shed skin — the whole refugee column shrinking to a pale thread stitching the valley, the world they'd belonged to for six days dissolving into distance. Ada felt the parting like a physical thing, a small tearing. Those people had hidden her. Fed her. A woman had mended Wren's boot with thread pulled from her own hem and refused thanks because, she said, kindness was the only property the Regent couldn't confiscate.

Now Ada was leaving them to the slow road and taking the fast one, and she carried the guilt of that the way she carried everything: silently, in the same pocket as the bones.

By midday the trees gave out. The Ashen Steps revealed why they bore the name: the rock itself was gray and powdery, veined with streaks of black and rust-red, as if the mountain had burned from the inside sometime in its deep history and never fully cooled. Switchbacks mounted the flank of the massif, cut — Ilex noted, squinting at the masonry — not by smugglers but by something far older, the steps too deep and too regular for human legs, each tread worn smooth in the center as though by centuries of feet that had all been hurrying.

The wind up there had a sound Ada had never heard wind make. It wasn't whistling. It was closer to breathing.

And underneath the wind — faint, rhythmic, patient — she began to hear the dead.

Not voices yet. Not names. Just presence, the way you sense a crowd in the next room: a pressure against the walls of her hearing, hundreds strong, perhaps thousands, spread across the whole height of the pass like frost. They were not agitated. That was the unnerving part. The dead of Vell had screamed and scattered; the dead of the crate had whispered and begged. These dead were *waiting*. Eighty years of waiting, and longer — the massacre at Karroch Fen was only the newest layer. Underneath it she sensed older strata, deaths pressed into the mountain like fossils, going back and back to some burning the stones themselves remembered.

Wren stopped on the two-hundredth step or so, tilted her head, and smiled.

"What?" Ada asked.

"They're excited," Wren said. "They felt you coming. Like—" she searched for the word, found it in the old tongue first and translated clumsily, "—like a door opening in a house where everyone's been sitting in the dark."

Ada's hand drifted to her pocket, to the relics she'd carried out of Vell: a child's knucklebone, a soldier's vertebra, the whistle she'd carved from a stranger's rib on the night the city fell and never blown. The dead inside them had gone utterly silent again. Listening. Waiting their turn.

"They'll want things from me," Ada said. Not a question. Brannoc's drunken warning echoed up out of eight years: *they interview you.*

"Yes," Wren said simply. "They've been keeping their names a long time. Names get heavy." She looked up at Ada with that devastating directness the girl wielded without knowing it. "You'll have to decide whose to carry. You can't carry all of them. Nobody can. That's the test, I think. That's what they're *for*."

Behind them, Ilex paused on the steps, breath ragged, her injured arm cradled close, and said what Ada was already thinking:

"Then let's hope we like her answers."

The wind breathed. Far above, the pass narrowed between two walls of ash-gray stone, and the light took on the color of old bone, and somewhere in the high silence a voice — the first of them, dry as chalk — said a single word that was not a word in any living language, and waited to see if the woman with the lamp in her skull would answer.

Ada squared her shoulders, took Wren's hand, and climbed toward it.
