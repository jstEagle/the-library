## 15. Ashen Steps

The pass announced itself before they reached it.

Ada felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, the way a storm announces itself in an old scar — except this was not weather. It was the dead, thousands of them, packed into the narrow throat of stone like water behind a dam. She had crossed the Ashen Steps once as a girl, running contraband for Brannoc with her hood up and her gift clamped shut, and even then she had heard them: a low murmur under the wind, like a congregation muttering through a closed door.

Now the door was open.

"They're waiting," Wren said. The girl walked at Ada's shoulder without being told to, close enough that their sleeves brushed. Since Vell burned, she had stopped wandering ahead. She had stopped humming in the old tongue. She had become, in three days of hard walking, something smaller and warier than the child who had once slept peacefully beside a crate full of souls. "They've been waiting a long time."

"How long?" Ilex asked from behind. The scholar-priestess moved with a staff cut from a Vellish olive tree, and she had not let go of it since the city fell. Her hands were still gray with ash she refused to wash off. "Do you know?"

Wren tilted her head, listening to something none of them could hear. "Since the mountain was young. Since men first learned to be cruel in this shape." She frowned. "The words are strange. Even my words don't go all the way back."

Ada looked up at the pass. The Ashen Steps were a staircase of switchbacks carved into the flank of the mountains by some forgotten king's ambition — a road built to move armies quickly and abandoned when it became clear that armies died here faster than they could march. The rock was pale, streaked with mineral veins that caught the light like old scars. Nothing grew on it. No lichen, no moss, no scrub pine clinging to the cracks. The refugees' column had split at the fork below, most of them taking the salt road east around the mountains — ten extra days, but a road where the only dangers were wolves and weather.

Forty-one of them had chosen the shortcut. Ada counted them now, strung out along the base of the first switchback: farmers with their children tied to their backs, a one-eyed tanner, two brothers from the Vellish docks carrying nothing but each other. Forty-one people betting their lives on a haunted road because ten days was the difference between outrunning the Regent's army and meeting it.

"Mother Ilex," Ada said quietly, "how much do you know about what happened here?"

Ilex's mouth went thin. "More than I'd like. There was a purge, in the second century of Aldric's line. A rebellion of the mountain clans — the ones who remembered the old faiths, the ones who wouldn't kneel." She touched the ash on her wrist absently, the way another woman might touch a wound. "The crown didn't defeat them in battle. It starved them. Sealed both ends of this pass in winter and waited. When spring came, the soldiers walked in and found—" She stopped. "There is a reason nothing grows here, bone-carver. The ground drank too much."

Ada nodded slowly. She had heard stories too, in the smuggler camps of her girlhood, told low over fires by people who never used the word *massacre* if they could help it. But hearing about the dead and hearing *from* them were different trades entirely.

She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. Beneath her skin, in the place where her gift lived — that quiet room behind her sternum where every voice she had ever carried made its home — she could feel them pressing. Not speaking yet. Not demanding. Just *there*, the way a crowd presses against a gate.

"Stay close," she said. "Both of you. And whatever you hear me say to the air — don't answer it. They'll take any voice offered."

* * *

The first hundred steps were silent.

That silence was worse than noise. Ada kept her eyes on the pale stone and her breath measured, and she felt the dead watching the way one feels eyes between trees. Wren's hand found hers somewhere around the third switchback — not clutching, just resting there, a small cold weight. The girl hadn't done that since the river. Ada did not look down at her. If she looked, she would have to decide whether to squeeze back, and she was afraid of what her face would do.

At the fourth switchback, the pass narrowed into a defile walled by cliffs on either side, and the dead finally spoke.

It came all at once, from everywhere, from the stone itself: not the whisper-voices she knew from relics, small and personal, but a chorus, layered and vast, like wind through a forest of dry reeds.

***Namer.***

Ada stopped walking. Ahead of her, the refugee column faltered; several of them crossed themselves or pressed charms to their lips. The tanner's brother began to pray aloud until his sibling hushed him.

***Namer. Carrier. Bone-speaker.*** The voices braided together, indistinguishable, ancient, hungry. ***We know what walks among us. We smell the door you carry in your chest.***

"I hear you," Ada said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. She raised her head and addressed the cliffs, the pale stone, the empty air. "We're passing through. We mean no disturbance. Let us walk."

A sound moved through the pass then — laughter, or something adjacent to laughter, the way a landslide is adjacent to falling. It rattled pebbles loose from the cliff face.

***Walk?*** said the chorus. ***We have walked these steps for six hundred years. Our feet wore grooves in the stone before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. You ask to *walk*.***

"We ask to pass."

***Everything asks to pass.*** The voices came closer now, or seemed to — she felt them gathering behind her shoulder blades, pressing at the door of her gift like beggars at a rich man's window. ***The soldiers passed. The famine passed. The spring came and the crows passed, fat as bishops. Everything passes through us, Namer, and nothing stays. Except us.***

Wren's grip tightened on her hand. "Ada," the girl whispered. "They want something."

"I know."

***We want our names.***

The chorus dropped its vastness suddenly, and beneath it Ada heard the individual voices again — hundreds of them, thousands, each one distinct the way snowflakes are distinct only when you stop trying to see the field. An old man's rasp. A child's thin pipe. A woman singing half a lullaby over and over because she had died mid-verse and could not remember the rest.

***You carry names,*** they said. ***That is your trade. You carve the bone and hold the voice and give the dead their words back. We have been nameless six hundred years. The crown took our names when it took everything else — struck them from the rolls, burned the clan-records, unmade us so thoroughly that even our ghosts forgot what to call themselves.*** A pause, and then, quieter, almost pleading: ***Carry us, Namer. Carry our names south. Speak them in the capital where the thief-king sits. Make him hear them.***

Ada stood very still.

This was the trap. She understood it the moment the plea finished, the way you understand a snare by the shape of the bait. Every name she accepted would live in her — she knew this from a lifetime of carving, from the miner's whistle, from Brannoc's last word of thanks as the gate came down on him at Vell. Each voice took up residence. Each one cost her something she could not name until it was gone: sleep, appetite, color, memory, pieces of herself paid out like coins to a moneylender who never gave change.

And there were *thousands* here.

"If I carry you," she said carefully, "all of you—"

***You will burst like a dropped jar,*** the chorus agreed, with terrible mildness. ***Yes. We know. We have watched hearers come through before. Two, in six centuries. Both tried to carry everything. Both are part of the floor now.***

"Then what do you want?"

***Choose,*** said the dead. And the word came from every throat in the pass at once, a single syllable spoken by a nation. ***You cannot carry us all. So choose. That is what the living do, isn't it? They choose who matters. Choose us, Namer. Show us how it's done.***

* * *

She sat down on the cold stone right there in the defile, cross-legged, and told Ilex to move the column ahead slowly and wait beyond the narrows. Ilex argued — of course she argued, in the clipped scholar's tongue she used when she was frightened — but Wren put her small body between them and said, in the old language, four words that made the priestess go pale and silent.

"What did you say to her?" Ada asked.

"She needed to hear it in the first tongue," Wren said simply. "Some things sound truer in it. I told her: *the bones must finish their sentence.*"

So Ada sat alone with the dead of the Ashen Steps, and they showed her.

They came forward one at a time, or in knots, the way mourners file past a bier. She couldn't see them — she never saw them, only heard — but she could feel their shapes, the pressure of attention, the particular weight of each grief. A shepherd who had frozen kneeling beside his flock, still counting them. A girl of perhaps nine who had survived the famine months longer than anyone because her mother chewed bark for her, and whose voice ended every sentence on a rising note, still asking permission. A warrior woman who spoke only in commands, even now, even dead: *stand, hold, stand.* A man who had betrayed the clans — opened a gate, sold names to the crown's soldiers — and who had spent six hundred years trying to apologize to people who could no longer hear him.

Each one asked the same thing. *Say my name. Remember me. Carry me.*

And Ada learned, sitting in that white stone throat while the column crawled away above her, exactly how much she could hold.

She took the shepherd first, because he asked politely and because someone should count those sheep forever. His name — the name he remembered, which might not even be his true one, six hundred years being long — settled into her chest like a smooth river stone. She felt it. Felt the weight of it, real and specific, a new tenant in the quiet room behind her sternum.

She took the girl with the rising questions. She took the warrior woman, who barked one last *hold* as she entered Ada and then, astonishingly, went quiet, satisfied, a soldier finally relieved of duty. She took the traitor, because mercy is not only for the innocent, and his apology poured into her like cold water and did not stop apologizing, and she understood immediately that she had made a mistake with that one, that some weights compound interest.

By the ninth name her hands were shaking. By the twelfth, she tasted copper. By the fifteenth, she could no longer remember her mother's face — the name of the traitor had taken its place somehow, had overwritten it, and she sat on the frozen stone of the pass weeping silently for a woman she could no longer picture, while the dead of the massacre pressed in around her, patient, endless, each one certain that *their* name was the one that mattered.

"No," she said.

The chorus stilled.

"No more." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. Her voice cracked and she let it. "I can't carry you all. I won't pretend I can. Fifteen of you come with me, and I will speak your names in the capital if I'm alive to speak anywhere, and the other thousand—" Her throat closed. She forced it open. "The other thousand, I leave to the stone. To the wind. To whatever comes after carrying. I'm sorry. I am so sorry. But if I take you all, I die on this mountain, and the forty-one living people above me die on the other side of it, and fifteen names die with me instead of reaching the capital. This is the arithmetic. I hate it. But it's the arithmetic."

Silence.

The longest silence of her life, stretched across a pass full of the unquiet dead, while Ada Ashgrove knelt on stone that had drunk a nation and waited to learn whether mercy or fury would answer.

Then, softly, from everywhere and nowhere, a voice she recognized — the girl with the rising questions, the one who had died asking permission — said:

***Is that allowed? To refuse?***

"It has to be," Ada whispered. "Or the choosing means nothing."

Another silence. Then the chorus sighed — a sound like snow sliding off a roof, vast and weary and, impossibly, relieved.

***Go, then, Namer.*** The pressure receded from her chest like a tide going out. ***Fifteen is more than zero. Fifteen is more than the last hearer managed. Walk. And tell the thief-king's ghost that the mountain remembers what the crown made it drink. Tell him the bill is coming.***

* * *

She caught up to the column an hour later, moving like a woman twice her age. Wren saw her coming and broke from Ilex's side at a run, and this time Ada did look down, and did squeeze back, and let her face do what it wanted.

"You're crying," Wren said.

"A little."

"The dead did that?"

"The dead did that." Ada knelt so they were eye to eye. Her chest felt crowded, resonant, like a house after a party where too many guests stayed the night. Fifteen voices murmured in her, settling, learning the walls. "I brought guests home. Don't be alarmed if I talk to the air sometimes."

Wren studied her with those too-old eyes. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead to Ada's, the gesture from the old tongue that meant, if Ada had learned anything at all, *I see what you carry.*

Behind them, Ilex limped up, leaning harder on her olive staff than she had that morning. "The narrows are behind us," the priestess said. "Two days to the far side. The scouts say the descent is treacherous with ice, but—"

She never finished.

It happened fast — faster than any of them would ever be able to say clearly afterward. One of the cliff walls shed a slab of stone with a crack like a snapped bone, and whether it was the mountain's ordinary malice or one last test from the dead, no one could determine. Ada heard Wren shout. She turned. The rock was falling, and Wren was standing directly beneath it, frozen, and Ada was too far, three strides too far, her body already knowing it would not arrive in time—

Ilex arrived.

The priestess moved with a speed that had no business living in a sixty-year-old scholar, shoved Wren clear with both hands, and took the glancing edge of the slab across her shoulder and side. The crack of it was very loud in the narrow pass. Then the stone hit the trail and shattered, and the dust rose, and in the ringing quiet Ada heard Wren screaming and Ilex breathing in short, wet, wrong-sounding pulls.

"Help me lay her flat," Ada said, and her voice came out with fifteen dead in it, rougher than her own. "Now. Everyone. *Now.*"

They carried the priestess down the switchbacks on a litter of cloaks and tent-poles, Ilex conscious but gray-lipped, one arm bound tight against her ribs, blood seeping slow and dark through the wrappings. At the day's camp, in the lee of a fallen watchtower, Ada cleaned the wound by firelight while Wren held the priestess's good hand and sang — softly, wordlessly, something in the old tongue that made the fifteen newly-carried names in Ada's chest stir and settle like birds roosting.

"She'll live," Ada said at last, sitting back on her heels. "If the wound doesn't rot, and if we can keep her warm, and if we reach help within the week." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "*If.* Survival's whole ledger is written in ifs."

"Ada." Ilex's voice was a thread. Her eyes, though, were sharp as ever, fixed on Ada's face with scholarly intensity even now. "In the narrows. What did they ask you? The dead. What did they *say*?"

Ada fed another branch to the fire. Around them the refugee column slept in exhausted heaps. Above them the pale cliffs of the Ashen Steps caught the moonlight like the teeth of some enormous jaw.

"They asked me to carry their names," she said. "And I learned something I wish I'd learned cheaper." She looked at her hands — the carver's hands, scarred and steady, that had always seemed to know their own limits until tonight. "My gift has a bottom, Mother Ilex. I always thought it was deep enough. It isn't. And we're walking toward a vault full of bound souls that makes this pass look like a village churchyard."

Wren had gone very still by the fire. When Ada glanced at her, the girl was staring south, down the dark throat of the pass, toward the capital and the Ember and the thing wearing the crown — and she was not staring with fear.

She was staring with recognition.

"Wren?" Ada said. "What is it?"

The girl turned back, and her face in the firelight was the face of a child again, frightened and young — but her voice, when it came, was not entirely her own. Something older spoke through her, briefly, in the old tongue, three words that made the fifteen names in Ada's chest cry out in answer:

*"It knows we're coming."*
