## 16. One Hour of King

The hour came early, and Aldric knew it before he opened his eyes.

He had learned to read the Regent's body the way a sailor reads weather — by small pressures, by the set of things that should not be set. The hollow thing that wore his crown kept his flesh like a man keeps a coat he is fond of: brushed, fed, exercised in the palace yards at dawn so the guards would see their king's shoulders still square. But there were tells. When the Regent was deep in its feeding — when it sat alone in the black chapel beneath the throne room with its hands in the Ember's light and drank — the body it borrowed went slack in a particular way. The left hand curled. The breathing thinned. And for one hour, sometimes less, the cage door swung open, and Aldric came home.

Tonight the door opened while the candles were still burning low, hours before its usual time. He surfaced into his own eyes like a drowning man breaking water, gasping inwardly, and found himself standing in the map room with his fingers pressed flat against the great table of Thornfell, and the Regent's generals still speaking around him as though their king were listening.

He was listening. That was the mercy and the horror of these hours: he could hear everything, feel everything, and do almost nothing. A passenger. A ghost in his own skin, permitted one hour of haunting per night.

"—the Vell road closes within the fortnight," General Corvane was saying. She was a broad woman with a voice like a millstone, and she did not look at him because no one looked at the king anymore unless spoken to. "The Ashen Steps remain passable, barely. If the fugitives take them—"

"They will not take them." The voice came out of Aldric's own throat, and he felt it move his mouth and hated it. The Regent spoke through him even now, even here, a second passenger riding above the first. "The Steps are haunted ground. Bone-carvers' country. Nothing crosses without paying."

"Then we hold the southern passes and wait for winter."

"No." Something moved behind the king's eyes then, something vast and cold turning over like a leviathan under ice. "We do not wait. The Key grows restless. I can feel her pulling at the thread from across the miles, like a fish on a line. We reel her in. Summon her. The binding will be made permanent before the solstice, and every soul in Vell will feed the Ember for a hundred years."

Aldric hung inside his body and memorized every word.

* * *

He had been a passenger for eleven years. He could mark the date precisely: the night the Ember was forged, the night he had knelt in this same vault and consented — *consented*, God help him, that was the poison of it — to let a binding be laid over his soul so that something else could be imprisoned inside him alongside his own spirit. They had told him it would be a partnership. A willing king hosting a devouring god, the two locked together so neither could harm the world. The priests of the old rite had sung, the forge-heart had burned white, and for three days Aldric had felt gloriously, terrifyingly whole.

Then the god had begun to eat.

It ate slowly at first. It ate his dreams, then his memories, then his hours, taking more of each year until the willing king was a tenant in a crumbling house, allowed back only when the new owner slept. Somewhere in those eleven years the arrangement had inverted itself entirely. The devouring god had learned to wear him out into the world, to sit his throne, to sign his decrees, to feed — not on him alone now, but on bound souls drawn up through the Ember like water through a root. The kingdom called him King Aldric the Steadfast. The kingdom had no idea its king had been dead behind his own eyes for a decade, screaming silently at state dinners, watching his own hands sign orders that sent men to burn free cities.

He had tried everything. He had screamed down the binding until his inner voice went raw. He had prayed to gods who either did not exist or had chosen not to hear. He had, in the early years, tried simply to die — to drive the borrowed body off a balcony, into a blade, into the river — but the binding held the flesh together like nails holding a coffin lid, and the Regent had punished each attempt by taking another hour of his night.

So he had learned patience instead. Patience, and the discipline of small acts.

Every night, in his hour, he wrote. Not with ink — ink could be searched, intercepted, traced. He wrote in bone. There was a servant girl, Marta, who brought the king's evening meal, and Marta had a brother who had vanished into the Regent's prisons three winters past, and Marta hated the crown with the clean, patient hatred of the powerless. It had taken Aldric two years of his stolen hours to find her — two years of testing, of coded requests, of watching her face for the flicker that meant *she understands*. Now she carried his night-letters out in the hem of her apron, small tokens of carved bone, each one a sentence scratched in a cipher of his own devising, passed hand to hand through the underworld of Thornfell until they reached whoever might act on them.

Most nights he believed no one read them. Most nights the writing was an act of faith indistinguishable from madness — a king whispering into a well, hoping the splash he heard was an answer and not just echo.

* * *

After the generals left, the Regent withdrew to feed, and the hour deepened. Aldric felt the shift like a change in pressure — the presence that rode him receding toward the vault below, dragging its hunger after it, and suddenly the body was his, all his, clumsy and real and aching.

One hour. Perhaps fifty minutes now.

He crossed the map room quickly, his joints protesting — the Regent kept the flesh strong but never rested it properly — and stood over the great table. The war-map of the northern reaches spread beneath the glass: carved mountains, painted rivers, tiny lead soldiers arranged in their patient thousands. Vell sat near the map's edge, marked with a small ivory tower someone had placed there years ago and never removed.

Three days. That was what the generals' argument had bought — Corvane wanted to wait for winter, the Regent refused, and the compromise was a redeployment: the southern column would swing west to close the Vell road early, arriving in force in three days rather than ten. Three days. If anyone was coming north — if anyone had heard anything — three days was the difference between reaching the capital ahead of the army and walking into its teeth.

Aldric looked at the lead regiments and did mathematics with his heart hammering.

Then he began to sabotage.

He could not move armies. But maps were made by men, and men were vain, and the map room had a flaw he had noticed years ago during another stolen hour: the table's glass cover could be lifted, and the pieces beneath were rearranged nightly by a cartographer's apprentice who copied his placements from the field reports. If the placements changed between the copying and the morning briefing—

He lifted the glass. His hands shook. Fifty minutes.

He worked fast and he worked clever. He did not hide armies — a missing regiment would be noticed instantly. Instead he shifted them. He moved the southern column's marker two days' march east of its true position, so that the morning briefing would calculate the Vell road closing late. He duplicated a decoy regiment in the Ashen Steps — a feint suggesting the Regent already held the pass — knowing the generals, seeing it, would argue for hours about whether to trust the placement, burning time they did not know they were spending. He nudged supply lines, transposed two cavalry markers, tilted the whole western deployment half a degree toward a valley that did not matter, so that every eye at tomorrow's council would bend toward the wrong horizon.

It was theft, this work. Theft of minutes, theft of attention, theft of certainty. Eleven years a prisoner, and his rebellion amounted to nudging little lead men across a painted table. He nearly laughed, and the laugh would have come out as a sob, so he swallowed it and kept moving pieces.

* * *

Twenty minutes left when the intercepts arrived.

They came the way they always came — a clerk with a lacquered tray, documents for the king's signature, left on the side table because no one expected the king to read anything anymore. Aldric drifted toward them with the aimless gait of a man admiring his own map room, and turned the top sheet over with one finger.

Requisition orders. Grain levies. Prisoner transfers. And then, third from the bottom, a report from the Office of Intercepts — the Regent's web of readers who opened every letter that moved through the free cities and burned what mattered.

His eye went down the page out of habit, and stopped.

*Item: bone token recovered from courier seized at the Millbrook ferry crossing. Token bears cipher markings consistent with prior intercepted correspondence (see items 14, 31, 47). Origin unknown. Recommendation: continue surveillance of courier network; sender believed operating from Vell or environs.*

Bone tokens. Cipher markings. *Prior intercepted correspondence.*

Someone was using his cipher. Someone had received his letters — Marta's fragile chain of apron-hems and trusted hands had actually held, all this way, all these years — and someone in Vell or its environs was writing *back*, and the Regent's readers had caught one of the answers in transit.

Aldric stood very still in the middle of his own map room, and something happened in his chest that he had forgotten how to name.

Hope.

It was a dangerous discipline, hope. He had practiced it the way monks practice fasting — strictly, sparingly, because indulgence destroyed you. Every previous flicker of it had ended the same way: a correspondent caught, a helper taken to the cells below the chapel, another silence. He had learned to hold hope the way you hold a live coal — briefly, at arm's length, ready to drop it.

But this was different. This token had come from Vell. And in Vell there was exactly one person who could read bone the way his letters required — Mother Ilex had written to him once, years ago, in terms careful enough to survive interception, of a hearer of the dead. And if the hearer of the dead was corresponding with the prisoner-king, then the hearer of the dead was coming for him. Or coming for the Ember. Either way — either way —

Either way, someone was coming, and he had just bought them three days with a handful of lead soldiers.

Twelve minutes. He took the intercept report to the brazier, hesitated — no. Burning it would draw notice; missing pages drew notice faster than damning ones. Instead he did the smaller, crueler thing: he took his royal seal where it lay beside the tray, and he stamped the report *acknowledged and filed*, which meant it would go down into the archive and not back across the Regent's desk for weeks. One document, buried in bureaucracy, by the hand of the king everyone had stopped watching.

Then he stood at the window and watched the lights of his capital, and let himself hope — properly, dangerously, for a full five minutes — that the coal would not burn him this time.

*Whoever you are,* he thought, pressing his palm flat against the cold glass, *whoever is carving my words into bone and sending them north — hurry. I have bought you three days. I do not know if I can buy a fourth.*

Behind him, faintly, from far below the floor, from the vault where the Ember burned, he felt the presence begin to stir. The hour was ending. The hunger was rising back up through the stone like floodwater through a cellar, looking for its coat, its crown, its kingdom.

Aldric straightened his shoulders, arranged his face into the Regent's accustomed blankness, and prepared to drown again.

But this time, going under, he carried the image with him: a bone token in a clerk's lacquered tray, and somewhere on a dark road, a stranger reading his handwriting, coming toward him through the night.

*Hold on,* he told himself, as the cage door closed. *Someone is coming. Hold the door.*

And far to the south, in a city of ash and catacombs, a bone-carver's hands were already moving.
