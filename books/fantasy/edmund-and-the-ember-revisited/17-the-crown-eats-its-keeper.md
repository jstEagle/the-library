## 17. The Crown Eats Its Keeper

The letter came at dusk, folded inside a hollowed knucklebone that Ada had sent south three weeks before.

She knew it was Aldric's hand before she cracked the bone open — the tight, careful script of a man writing against a clock, each word rationed like water in a drought. The token had traveled by means she did not ask about: pigeon, peddler, priest, the long relay of small mercies that Mother Ilex had stitched together out of Vell's surviving network. That the network still functioned at all was its own kind of miracle. Vell was ash. Its people were scattered along the salt road like seed from a split pod. And yet the letters came.

*They know,* the letter began. No greeting. No preamble. *The envoy you refused carried more than gold — he carried the Regent's sight. Everything said in his presence went back to the palace. Ilex was right to burn her records.*

Ada read it twice by the light of a tallow stub, Wren asleep beside her under a quilt patched from three quilts, Ilex's wounded shoulder wrapped and splinted on the far side of the fire. They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, half a day off the Ashen Steps' southern mouth, and for one evening it had almost felt like safety.

*I heard them speak of it through my own ears,* Aldric wrote. *That is the worst of this body — the Regent uses my hearing when it suits him, my eyes when it needs to see. Tonight it spoke to a circle of hooded men in the map room, and I stood behind my own face and listened. The binding ritual is moved up. Not at the solstice. Not at the new moon. In nine days, at the dark of the dark, when the Ember burns lowest and hungriest. They mean to feed it the Key and make the binding permanent — a king who cannot die, cannot be unmade, cannot be freed. A crown welded shut forever.*

*Nine days. You have nine days, and the army between you.*

*There is more. Forgive me. There is always more.*

*The Regent does not intend to wait for the Key to be delivered. It has begun to call her.*

Ada's hands went cold around the bone.

*It used the word "summon." I do not know what it means in full — I felt the room change when they said it, felt something move through the stones like a current through wire. But I know what it will mean for her. She is not only a key, she is a door, and doors can be pulled open from either side. Watch her sleep. Watch her hands. If she begins to walk in the night —*

The letter ended there, the script degrading into a scrawl, as if the hour had closed over him mid-sentence. One hour a night. Somewhere in Thornfell's capital, a king was being dragged back under the surface of his own life, drowning upward.

Ada sat with the letter until the tallow guttered, listening to the ordinary sounds of the camp: Ilex's slow breathing, the pop of the fire, wind worrying the broken tower's teeth. And beneath all of it, fainter than any of those, the sound she had been trying not to hear for two days.

Wren, in her sleep, humming.

Not a child's tuneless hum. A note. One sustained, patient note, low in the girl's chest, like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Ada had told herself it was dreaming. She had told herself a great many things.

She looked at the child now — the sharp little face slack with sleep, the carved amulet rising and falling on her chest, her fingers curled loose in the quilt. Small. Warm. Hers, in the way that mattered, in the way no paper or blood had ever made anything hers.

And being pulled. Even now. Even here.

Ada fed the fire another stick and began, quietly, to take inventory of the rope in their packs.

* * *

It started on the third night.

Ada woke to cold — true cold, the kind that meant an open flap or a missing body — and her heart understood before her mind did. She turned. Wren's bedroll was empty. The quilt lay thrown back in a neat line, too neat, the way a body leaves bedding when it rises without waking.

The girl stood at the edge of the firelight, facing south, barefoot in the frost, her arms hanging straight as plumb lines. Her breath smoked in slow, even rhythm. Her eyes were open but nothing lived behind them — no Wren, no fear, no child. Just the dark looking out.

"Wren." Ada kept her voice low, the way you spoke to a spooked horse, though she suspected the girl could not hear her at all. "Wren, come back."

The humming answered instead. That single note, deeper now, resonant enough that Ada felt it in her own sternum, in her fillings, in the old bones of the watchtower. And beneath it — because her gift had never fully left her, because the dead still murmured at the edge of her hearing like tide at the lip of a cave — she caught something else. Words. Not the dead's words. Something older, coming *through* the dead, using their murmur as a channel the way floodwater uses a ditch.

She could not translate it. But she knew its shape. It was the shape of a name being called across a great distance, patiently, over and over, the way you call a dog home.

"No," Ada said aloud, to the night, to whatever listened. "She's not yours."

She crossed the frost and took the girl's shoulders. The skin beneath the nightshirt was ice-cold, and yet Wren did not shiver, did not wake, did not register the touch at all except that her feet took one sliding step forward, dragging Ada with her, toward the south, toward the dark road, toward a capital nine days away that her sleeping body seemed determined to reach on foot if it killed her.

Ada hauled her back. It was like hauling a boat against a current — the girl weighed no more than a bundle of sticks, but the pull was in her, not her weight, some tidal thing moving through the small frame. Ada got her arms around the child's chest and walked her backward, step by grudging step, until they were both inside the firelight, and even then the girl's toes kept pointing south like a compass needle straining at its pin.

Ilex woke. The old priestess took one look and was up despite her wound, moving with the brisk economy of a woman who had triaged worse. "How long?"

"Tonight's the first. Aldric warned me three nights ago."

"Then we have less time than he thinks. The calling strengthens. First they walk. Then they stop eating unless woken. Then—" Ilex stopped herself, which was answer enough.

Ada looked down at Wren's sleeping face, at the frost melting on her lashes, and felt something crack open in her chest that was larger than fear. Fear was for yourself. This was the other thing, the thing with teeth, the thing mothers must carry or go mad.

"We tie her," Ada said.

"To what? We have no posts. We have no—"

"To me."

* * *

They used Brannoc's rope.

It was river-rope, tarred and honest, the kind the ferryman had trusted with lives for thirty years, and cutting it felt like a small blasphemy. Ada measured out six feet of it — long enough for the girl to turn in her sleep, short enough that Ada would feel every step — and bound one end around Wren's waist beneath the arms, in a harness knot Ilex showed her, a knot the priestess said had been used to lower bodies into catacombs and raise them back out. "A resurrection knot," Ilex said, without smiling. "It holds what is going down."

The other end Ada tied around her own wrist. Then she looked at it, at the rough tarred line lying between them like a lifeline thrown into dark water, and found it insufficient. Rope could slip. Rope could be cut. Rope held bodies, and what was happening to Wren was not happening to her body alone.

So Ada took the girl's small hands in hers, chapped and bitten-nailed, and spoke the oath in the only language she had that was older than kingdoms — the carver's tongue, the words bone-listeners used to bind a relic to its purpose so that no other voice could wear it. She had learned it from a dead woman whose wrist-bone she had carved into her first awl-handle, twenty years ago, and she had never once spoken it over a living person.

"I hold you," she said. "Bone to bone, breath to breath. Whatever calls you, it calls through me first. Whatever pulls you, it pulls against my holding. You are not a door tonight. You are a child in a workshop in the mountains, and I am the wall around you, and walls do not answer."

Wren slept on. But somewhere in the middle of the oath the humming faltered — just a stutter, just a skipped beat, like a bell catching on its clapper — and Ada chose, fiercely, unreasonably, to believe the girl had heard.

Ilex watched from across the fire, her bandaged shoulder dark in the light. "You know it will test that," the priestess said quietly. "Whatever is calling her. It will test your holding the way water tests a dam. Every night, harder."

"Then every night I'll be harder."

"That is not how dams fail, child."

"I know how things fail, Mother." Ada settled back against her pack, the rope between her wrist and the sleeping girl drawn to a gentle tension, a pulse-line, an umbilical. "I've spent my whole life listening to the ways people break. I'd like, just once, to be surprised."

* * *

The second night, the pull came at moonset, and it was stronger.

Ada woke with her arm already taut, the rope biting a red line into her wrist, and found Wren three feet from the fire's edge, walking — actually walking now, slow and terrible, her bare feet finding the frozen ground with uncanny certainty, each step placed south. Ada braced, hauled, and the girl swayed like a sapling and kept leaning. It took everything Ada had, boots dug into frost, to walk her back. When she finally got her down and wrapped in the quilt, the girl's lips were moving. Silent. Shaping syllables in the old tongue, over and over, a single phrase.

Ada pressed her palm flat against Wren's sternum, feeling for the hum, and the vibration rose up through her own arm like a current, and for one vertiginous moment she *understood* the phrase — not translated it, but understood it, the way you understand a hand reaching for yours.

*Come down. Come down where the light is kept. Come down and be finished.*

Be finished. As if the girl were an unfinished thing. As if all this — the blizzard, the bones, the roads, the rope, the oath, the whole improbable architecture of love that Ada had built around a stranger's child — were merely delay. Drafting errors. Something to be corrected by a forge-heart full of stolen souls.

Ada held her until dawn, rocking slightly, humming back — a different note, a counter-note, a lullaby their mother might have sung in a country neither of them remembered. Toward morning the silent lips stilled. Toward morning the girl's feet warmed.

In the grey light, Wren opened her eyes and found the rope between them and understood instantly, the way children understand everything that matters. Her face did something complicated — shame and fear and a flicker of anger, all the weather of being handled.

"I walked again," she said. Not a question.

"You walked again."

"How far did I get?"

"Three feet past the fire. I'm getting stingier with distance every night. By tomorrow you won't make it out of the quilt."

Something almost like a smile crossed the girl's face and died there. She looked south, the way she always looked south now, the way a compass looks, the way a wound looks toward its knife. "It doesn't shout anymore," she said. "It's gotten quiet. Like someone standing very close behind you, saying your name softly. That's worse."

"I know."

"It knows your name too now, Ada." The girl's eyes came around, and they were clear and frightened and terribly awake. "Last night, when you were holding me. It stopped calling me for a while and it said yours instead. It said it knows what you're planning. It said the tunnels under the palace are already watched. It said—" Wren swallowed. "It said it would let me keep you. If I came willingly. It said it would let you live, and let me remember you, after. It said after wouldn't hurt. It said I'd be finished, and finished things don't grieve."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

"And what did you say?" Ada asked, when she could trust her voice.

"I didn't say anything." Wren's chin came up, stubborn as a fence post. "But I thought at it very loudly. I thought: *she tied the knot wrong on purpose so I could get out of it if I needed to.* Because you did, didn't you. You always leave me a way out. You think I don't notice."

Ada said nothing, which was confirmation.

"Well, stop it," the girl said, fierce and small and shaking. "Stop leaving me exits. If it comes down to it, I want to be tied the way you tie things you mean to keep."

* * *

On the fourth night, the Regent stopped asking.

Ada woke to the rope sawing at her wrist and Wren already at the edge of the dark, moving fast now, not walking but marching, arms pumping, a soldier's stride poured into a child's body, and when Ada grabbed her the girl fought — blindly, silently, with shocking strength, elbows and heels, her mouth open on that sustained bell-note that had climbed nearly out of hearing. It took Ilex's weight as well as Ada's to bring her down, and even pinned, the small body thrashed with the tireless rhythm of something operated rather than living.

"She'll hurt herself," Ilex gasped.

"She's already being hurt." Ada got her knee across the girl's legs, got both wrists in one hand, and put her mouth close to Wren's ear, and shouted the oath into it — not the formal words this time, but the plain ones underneath. "*I hold you. Bone to bone. Breath to breath. Come back. Come back. Come back.*"

And Wren — mid-struggle, mid-hum — burst into tears.

They came all at once, hot and human, breaking the note apart, and the girl collapsed into sobbing consciousness like a diver surfacing, gulping, clutching Ada's shirt with both fists. "It's so loud," she wept. "It's so loud now, it's not quiet anymore, it's *right behind my teeth*, it says it's done waiting, it says if I won't walk it will drag, it says—"

"Hush. Hush. I have you."

"—it says it only needs me *near*, Ada, it says near is enough, it says the vault will do the rest, it says every mile I'm carried closer it can pull harder, it says—"

The sentence dissolved into crying. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent. Southward, nine days' ride and shrinking, something ancient leaned on a door it had spent a thousand years building, and the door was a child, and the child was in Ada's arms, and the rope between them was fraying somewhere no blade could reach.

Ada rocked her and did not let her voice shake. "Then we stop losing miles," she said. "We stop resting. We stop being careful. Ilex — how fast can she travel, wounded?"

Ilex was quiet a moment. "Faster than wisdom allows."

"Good." Ada looked south, at the black line of the hills, at the distant smudge that was a capital and a crown and a forge-heart full of the stolen dead. Terror was in her, vast and cold, but terror was fuel if you burned it right; Brannoc had taught her that with the last hour of his life. "Because we're done running *from*. Tomorrow we run *at*."

Beneath her, still hiccupping, Wren went suddenly rigid — and laughed. One wet, disbelieving laugh.

"What?" Ada demanded.

"It stopped." The girl lifted her head, eyes wide. "When you said that. It *stopped*. Just for a second — like something big turning around to look." She wiped her nose on her sleeve, and grinned through the wreckage of her fear, and it was the bravest thing Ada had ever seen. "It's afraid of you, Ada. Isn't that funny? It's a god wearing a king, and it's afraid of a bone-carver with a rope."

Ada did not find it funny. She found it the most hopeful thing anyone had said in nine days, and she tightened the resurrection knot, and she did not leave the exit in it this time.

They broke camp before dawn, heading south at a pace that wisdom would have forbidden — three figures and a length of tarred rope, running at the dark.
