## 18. Beneath the Palace

The rope at Ada's waist went taut, then slack, then taut again — the rhythm of a child walking behind her who was not entirely awake. She had learned to read it the way she had once read bones: by tension, by tremor. When the rope pulled steady and heavy, Wren was dreaming on her feet. When it jerked, the girl was fighting something in her sleep, and Ada had to stop and hold her until the shaking passed.

They had been under the mountain for six hours.

The tunnel mouth Aldric had described in his night-letters was exactly where he promised it would be: behind the collapsed sluice-gate of an old tannery on the river's south bank, beneath forty years of rubble and one very confused family of otters. The letters had come wrapped in waxed cloth inside a hollowed femur, delivered by a boy who took no payment and asked no questions — one of Brannoc's people, or what remained of them since Vell burned. *The tanners' road runs true,* Aldric had written in a hand that shook like a man writing against a clock, because he was. *My grandfather built it to move brandy and fugitives. It will remember you if you let it.*

Ada had let it.

She knew these roads. Not this tunnel specifically — but roads like it, smuggler veins threaded through the bones of every city on the river, and she had run them as a girl of fifteen with stolen silver in her shoes and her mother's name still warm in her mouth. Her father had been a ferryman before the crown taxes broke him; her mother had carved bone for the pleasure-houses until her hands gave out; and Ada had learned early that every kingdom sits on top of a second country, a country of cellars and culverts and bribed gatekeepers, and that the second country always wins in the end. She had left that life behind deliberately, brick by brick, building her quiet workshop above the tide line where no king's business could reach her.

And now here she was, forty years later, walking back into the first country with a sleeping key tied to her waist and a dead man's map folded against her heart.

*Not a dead man,* she corrected herself, feeling the bone token in her pocket, the one Aldric had carved and sent out through his own prison walls. *A buried one. There's a difference. I've spent my whole life listening to the difference.*

The tunnel sloped downward, and the air changed the way air does when it stops belonging to the world above. It grew warm. Not the warmth of hearths or bodies but a deep mineral heat, as though they were descending toward the mountain's own blood. Somewhere below them, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, something was beating.

Ada did not need her gift to know what it was. She had known since Vell, since Mother Ilex had pressed the old texts into her hands with fingers stained black from centuries of ink. *The Ember is not a fire,* the priestess had said. *It is a forge-heart. A furnace of souls, bound and burning, and it has burned for three hundred years without going out because it is fed. Do you understand me, bone-carver? It is fed.*

Wren was the feeding.

Wren had been the feeding all along — forged, not born, shaped in that burning vault as surely as any whistle or amulet, a living key cut to fit the Ember's lock. And now the Regent had begun to call her home.

Ada stopped and turned. The rope glowed faintly in the dark where her hand held it — she had rubbed it with ash-oil and salt, Ilex's recipe, a ward against things that walked in sleep. By its dim light she could see Wren's face: slack, peaceful, terrible. The girl's lips were moving. No sound came out, but Ada could see the shapes of the old tongue forming and reforming, word after word, like a prayer being recited by someone else's mouth.

"Wren." She kept her voice low and even, the voice she used for frightened animals and grieving widows. "Wren. You're walking with Ada. We're going together. Say it back."

The girl's eyes opened halfway. For a moment they were her own — dark, wary, nine years old, a child who liked honey on bread and hated having her hair combed. "It's singing," Wren whispered. "It got louder when we came under the stone. It knows my name, Ada. It's had my name the whole time and I only just remembered it."

"What is it?"

Wren's face crumpled, and she pressed both fists against her eyes. "I don't want to say it. If I say it, it's mine again."

Ada knelt and gathered her in, rope and all, and held her the way she had held nothing else in forty years of solitary life. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, the capital of Thornfell went about its evening — bells, carts, the distant thunder of the Regent's army drilling in the outer fields. Below them, the heartbeat continued, patient as rot.

*We are between two hungers,* Ada thought. *One above and one below. And we are walking down.*

* * *

The tanners' road ended where Aldric said it would: at a wall of dressed granite with no door in it, only a carving — a stag's skull crowned with oak leaves, worn nearly smooth by time. Ada ran her fingers over it and found, just as the night-letter had instructed, the hairline seam where the skull's left antler met the stone. She pressed. The wall breathed open on counterweights older than the palace above it, exhaling air that smelled of cold metal and candle smoke.

Beyond lay the smuggler country of the palace itself: service galleries, forgotten cisterns, a wine cellar whose racks stood empty since some king's grandfather had drunk it dry. Aldric's letters had mapped it all in a cramped, urgent hand — *left at the third arch, mind the drop, the stairs down are safe but count them, there should be ninety-one* — and Ada followed the map while Wren walked beside her with her eyes half-closed, drawn forward on the invisible line of the Ember's call.

At stair sixty-four, the dead began to speak.

Not aloud. Not in the way Ada had heard them all her life, the voices rising from bone and relic like heat from stone. This was different. This was pressure. As they descended, the silence around her thickened until her ears ached with it, and she understood with a lurch that the souls in the Ember below — hundreds of them, thousands, three hundred years of the Regent's feeding — were so many, and so close, and so loud, that they had stopped being voices at all. They had become a single sound, the way individual raindrops become a storm.

Her gift, which had never once failed her, which had been her livelihood and her curse and her only company for four decades, was drowning.

"Ada." Wren's small hand found hers in the dark. The girl's eyes were fully open now, and full of tears. "You can't hear them anymore, can you. They're too many. That's what it feels like. That's what it's felt like my whole life."

Ada's throat closed. She thought of the whistle she had carved last winter, the miner's last words, the gentle chorus of the dead that had kept her company through every silent evening of her chosen solitude. She thought of how she had told herself loneliness was peace. And she thought of the price Mother Ilex had named twice, in the quiet of the temple at Vell, looking at her with ancient pity: *a hearer must take the weight. The binding cannot be broken from outside. Someone who can carry souls must carry them, all of them, and be emptied of everything else.*

*I lied to you, little bird,* she thought, squeezing Wren's hand. *I told you we'd both walk out of this. I'm sorry. I'd lie again.*

"Ninety-one," she said aloud instead, as the stairs ended. "We're here."

* * *

The vault door was bronze, taller than two men, cast with the image of a kneeling king offering up his own heart in his cupped hands. Three hundred years of candle smoke had blackened it except where countless palms had touched it in prayer or fear, leaving a bright ghost-shape at its center. It stood ajar.

Of course it stood ajar. The Regent wanted Wren inside. The door had probably been oiled.

Beyond it, the vault opened into a chamber that stole the breath from Ada's lungs — not from awe, though there was that, terrible and unwilling, but from the sheer physical weight of the place. It was round, hewn from living rock, and at its center burned the Ember.

She had expected fire. What she saw was worse than fire.

The Ember was a forge-heart indeed: a mass of white-gold light the size of an ox, suspended above a basin of black iron, pulsing with that patient heartbeat she had felt through the stone. And within the light — Ada's eyes watered, but she could not look away — were faces. Souls packed like cargo in a crate, like fish in a barrel, thousands upon thousands, each one distinct and each one burning, their mouths open in a scream that made no sound because sound required space and none of them had any. The light they gave off lit the chamber like a captive sun. It was beautiful. It was the most obscene thing Ada had ever seen.

Around the basin, set into the floor in a ring, were channels — grooves cut in the rock, running inward from the walls like roots toward a trunk. And along those channels, glinting wetly in the Ember-light, moved something dark and slow. Not blood. Older than blood. The mountain's own marrow, perhaps, or the residue of three centuries of feeding.

And there, at the near edge of the basin, small and straight-backed and terribly calm, knelt Wren.

Ada's heart stopped. The rope hung loose in her hand — cut? slipped? — and the girl was ten feet away, kneeling on the channel-stone with her palms flat against the rock, her head bowed, the old tongue pouring from her mouth now audibly, a low continuous song that harmonized horribly with the Ember's pulse.

"No," Ada said, and her voice cracked across the chamber like a whip. "No. Wren. Look at me. You look at me right now."

The song faltered. The girl's head lifted, and her eyes — gods, her eyes were full of light, reflecting the Ember though she faced away from it — found Ada's face.

"I couldn't stop," Wren said simply. "It opened the door in me when we came under the mountain. But—" and here her chin trembled, and she was suddenly, entirely, a nine-year-old child again, kneeling in a hell of light, "—but I waited for you. I made myself wait. I told it I wouldn't kneel all the way down until you came, because—" her voice broke, "—because you said we go together. I remembered you said that."

Ada crossed the distance in four strides and dropped to her knees in front of the girl, taking her face in both hands. The skin was fever-hot. Beneath it, faintly, something pulsed in time with the Ember, as if the key were already half-turned in its lock.

"You waited," Ada said. "Good girl. Oh, my brave girl. Now listen to me, and listen well, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're going to do a very difficult thing." She glanced past Wren's shoulder at the forge-heart, at the screaming light, at the channels in the floor, and felt the truth of Ilex's warning settle over her like a burial shroud. She had known since Vell. She had lied about it for weeks. There was no more time for lying.

"The binding can't be broken from outside. It has to be carried. And I'm the carrier, Wren. Me. Not you. Whatever happens next — whatever you see, whatever it sounds like — your job is to close your eyes and think about honey bread and the workshop and the second chair, and when it's done, I need you to walk us out of here. Can you do that?"

Wren stared at her. Understanding dawned slowly across the child's face, and with it a horror far too old for her years. "No," she whispered. "No, Ada, the priestess said the price—"

"The price is mine to pay. That's what a mother is for." The word was out of her mouth before she could catch it — *mother*, a word she had never once used, a word she had not earned, a word that hung in the burning air between them like a struck bell.

Wren made a sound that Ada would hear for the rest of her life, however long or short that life proved to be.

Above them, far above, a bell began to ring — not the city's evening bells but the palace alarm, urgent and repeating. And beneath the ringing, from the passage behind them, came footsteps. Many. Unhurried. The tread of someone who owned every stone in this place and knew, with perfect certainty, that his guests had arrived.

A voice filled the vault, seeming to rise from the channels themselves, from the black iron basin, from the mouths of every bound soul at once:

*"The carver comes to the forge. How fitting. Bring her to me — and bring the key. The binding will be made permanent tonight."*

Ada rose, pulling Wren behind her, and put her hand into her pocket, around the bone token Aldric had carved — the last piece of him that was still his own.

"One hour," she whispered. "Wherever you are, Your Majesty — I hope it's tonight."
