## 19. The Unbinding

The vault breathed.

That was the first thing Ada understood as she came down the last of Aldric's smuggler stairs — the tunnel walls giving way to a chamber older than the palace above it, older than the kingdom, older perhaps than the idea of kingdoms — that the Ember did not burn. It *breathed*. In and out, slow and vast, like something asleep beneath a hill in all the old stories, and every breath it drew pulled at her skin, at her teeth, at the small bones of her ears where the dead had lived their whole speaking lives.

It hung in the center of the vault: a forge-heart the size of an ox, suspended on chains that were not metal but bone — femurs and ribs and vertebrae fused into cables by centuries of heat — and inside its glass-and-ash shell moved lights. Not flames. Lights with faces. Ada had heard souls for thirty years; she had never once seen them, and now she saw hundreds, thousands, turning slowly in the Ember's core like snow falling upward, each one wearing the shape it had worn when it was happiest.

And kneeling before it, small hands pressed flat to the stone floor, was Wren.

"No," Ada said, and her voice cracked apart in the huge dark. "No, no, little bird—"

Wren didn't turn. Her eyes were open but fixed on nothing, on everything, on the breathing heart of the binding, and her lips moved in the old tongue — the language older than any kingdom, the language Ada had spent weeks learning syllable by stubborn syllable from this same mouth — and Ada knew enough of it now to understand what the girl was saying.

*I open. I open. I open.*

The Regent had called her across distance, had hooked into whatever she was made of and reeled her through half a mountain while she slept, and the rope Ada had tied around both their waists lay coiled and cut on the vault floor ten steps behind them — snapped clean, as if the thing above had simply reached down with two fingers and plucked it apart.

Ada ran.

She got her arms under Wren's shoulders and hauled her back from the Ember, and the girl weighed nothing and everything, like lifting a door that opens onto wind. "Wren. Wren. Look at me. It's me. It's Ada."

The child's eyes focused. Slowly, painfully, like a swimmer surfacing through deep water. And what Ada saw in them broke her heart in the old familiar place, the one that had been breaking since the night a half-frozen girl knocked on her door in a tongue no one living should have spoken.

"You came," Wren whispered. Her voice was hoarse, scraped thin. "I tried not to walk. I tied myself to the bed. I bit my own hand." She held it up; the bite marks were days old, healing wrong, silvered. "But it knows my name. My real one. When it says it I just — I go."

"I know." Ada gathered her close, felt the small heartbeat hammering against her own. "I know, and I'm sorry, and I'm going to make it stop. Whatever it costs. Do you hear me? Whatever it costs."

Because she had known the cost since Vell. Since Mother Ilex had laid it out over candlelight with the gentleness of a woman delivering a death sentence: *unbinding requires a hearer. The souls must pass through a living vessel who can carry their weight, or they scatter and take the king with them.* A hearer of the dead. There was exactly one within three hundred miles.

She had lied to Wren about it for eleven days. She found, now, at the bottom of everything, that she wasn't sorry for the lie. Some mercies are only possible as lies.

* * *

The stairs behind them rang with footsteps.

Aldric came first — Aldric's body, rather, moving with a gait that belonged to no man, spine too straight, head tilted at the angle of someone listening to music no one else can hear. The Regent wore the king like a coat two sizes too large, and up close Ada could finally see the seams: the way the eyes didn't quite track together, the way the breath came without any rhythm a lung would choose. Behind him walked six of his silent guard, and behind them, filling the doorway of the ancient vault like a shadow given rank, the envoy from Vell — the one whose smile Ada had refused on a day that already felt like a previous life.

"Bone-carver," the Regent said. Its voice was Aldric's voice played backward and then forward again, almost right, never right. "You've cost me a city, a ferry, a general, and three days. Do you know how much a day is worth, to a thing that must eat to exist?"

"I imagine you're about to tell me."

Something flickered across the stolen face that might have been amusement. It gestured, and the guards spread out along the curve of the wall, cutting off the stairs, the side passages, the old smuggler ways Ada had run as a girl. Not that she'd been running anywhere. She had walked into the center of the trap with her eyes open, because the center of the trap was also the center of the binding, and there was no unmaking the one without standing inside the other.

"Wren," the Regent said softly, and the girl shuddered in Ada's arms. "Come here, little door. Come home."

"She stays."

"She was forged for this." The Regent drifted closer, and the Ember brightened at its approach, the souls inside pressing toward it like moths, like starving children at a window. "Before your kingdoms, before your languages, there was a god that ate light, and there were people desperate enough to build a cage for it out of a willing man. But a cage needs a lock, carver. And locks are not born. They are *made*. She was made. Every soul in that fire passed through her once, on their way in. She can open the way, and more than open it — she can make the binding permanent. Eternal. A king who reigns until the mountains wear down to gravel. All I require is that she kneel, and feed, and be consumed, as she was always meant to be. That was the design. I am merely — tidy."

"And the alternative?" Ada asked, though she knew. Oh, she knew.

The Regent smiled with Aldric's mouth, and it was obscene. "Here is my offer, since you climbed so far. Keep your gift."

The words dropped into the silence like stones into a well.

"You hear them," it went on, silk-smooth. "The dead. You have heard them since you were a child, alone in the mountains with your bones and your whistles, and I know — I *know* — what that loneliness has cost you. What it will cost you to lose. Unbind the Ember yourself, take the souls into your body, and they will pass through you and go silent forever. You'll be deaf to them for the rest of your life. Empty. Alone in a way you cannot currently imagine." It let that sit. Let it rot. "Or give me the girl. Walk out of here whole. Rich. Honored. Still hearing every whisper, every warning, every last word of every miner and mother and soldier who dies with something unsaid. You keep the voices, carver. You keep the only company you've ever truly loved."

The vault breathed. The souls turned in their prison. Somewhere far above, the palace slept, and beyond it the war-maps, and beyond those the armies marching on the free cities.

And Ada thought about silence.

Not the silence the Regent offered — the silence after. The silence she'd lived in before the gift, if there had ever been such a time; she couldn't remember a world without the whispers. Her whole self was built around that hearing the way a tree grows around a fence post. They were offering to tear out the post and leave the shape of it hollow in her wood.

They thought it was a bargain. That was the joke of it, the black and endless joke. They thought she would hesitate.

"Let me tell you what I hear," Ada said quietly. "Right now. In this room." She lifted her chin toward the Ember. "I hear a woman named Sera who died holding a bridge so her neighbors could cross. I hear a boy who drowned at nine and has been asking for his mother for four hundred years. I hear soldiers who marched under that crown you're wearing and discovered too late what was inside it. Every soul in that fire has a name, usurper. I can't carry them all — Ilex told me that too — but I can carry them long enough. Long enough for them to *leave*."

The Regent's face went still.

"So no," Ada said. "No bargain. No pardon. No gold. You want to know what I choose?" She looked down at Wren, at the bite-scarred hand, at the eyes that had watched her across a winter table and learned, syllable by syllable, what belonging was. "I choose the silence. Gladly. I choose it like a door closing behind me on a warm room."

She kissed the top of Wren's head. "Cover your ears, little bird. And whatever happens — don't open. Don't open for anyone. Not even me. Especially not for me."

Then she stepped forward, alone, toward the forge-heart of the binding, and began the unbinding.

* * *

Mother Ilex had taught her the rite in fragments, wrapped in theology, hedged with warnings, and in the end it came down to something brutally simple. A binding is a knot. Every knot can be untied by the one who holds both ends. The Ember held one end — the god-cage, the devouring light, the reason a good king had consented to be chained. The other end was the king himself, still alive, still fighting, one hour a night, writing letters in smuggled bone.

*Aldric,* Ada prayed, or spoke, or shouted down whatever thread connected the vault to the man imprisoned behind his own eyes. *Now. Pull. Whatever you have left — pull.*

And somewhere above her, in a body he had not commanded in years, King Aldric pulled.

She felt it happen. The Regent staggered — actually staggered, its guards lurching forward — as the stolen body seized against its wearer like a horse against a cruel bit. The perfect posture shattered. Aldric's face contorted, and for one blazing moment the eyes aligned, human and furious and weeping, and a voice that was entirely his own tore out of him:

"*GET — OUT — OF — MY — HOUSE—*"

"Now!" Ada screamed, and put her hands on the Ember.

The shell was hot and cold at once, ash-glass humming under her palms, and she spoke the words Ilex had given her, the old-tongue counter-song Wren had helped her shape through a long terrible autumn of practice — *unmake, unwind, release* — and the knot began to move.

The Ember screamed.

Not the souls — the binding itself, the ancient woven thing, feeling for the first time in a thousand years the possibility of ending. Light poured from the cracks between Ada's fingers. The chains of fused bone groaned. And the souls — the souls turned toward her, thousands of faces, thousands of names, and began to flow.

Into her.

It did not hurt, at first. It was worse than hurt. It was *fullness* — every grief she had ever carried multiplied by every grief there had ever been, every last word, every unfinished letter, every mother calling a name into empty air. The boy who drowned at nine poured through her and she held him, she *held* him, gave him the moment of being carried that his short life had ended without. The woman named Sera crossed her bridge one final time. Soldiers stood down. Farmers saw spring. Ada stood in the center of the vault with her arms flung wide and her mouth open in a soundless cry and became, for ninety seconds, the entire memory of the dead.

The guards fell, weeping, as the flood passed through the room. The envoy collapsed like an emptied coat. The Regent shrieked — a sound like tearing cloth, like a star going out — because every soul it had ever eaten was leaving it at once, pouring past Ada, through Ada, *out*, scattering upward through the vault's ancient chimney into a sky none of them could see.

And then, as Ilex had promised, as the Regent had promised, as Ada had chosen —

the voices went quiet.

Not fading. Quiet. The way a river goes quiet when it reaches the sea. One moment the world roared with the dead; the next, Ada stood in a silence so total it had texture, weight, a temperature. Thirty years of whispered company, gone between one heartbeat and the next. She swayed. Her hands slid off the cooling Ember. She was deaf in the oldest sense there was, and she had never felt so empty or so clean.

Above her, the Ember cracked straight down the middle, and the light that came out was not hungry anymore. It was just light. Then the forge-heart split, folded, and collapsed into a drift of grey ash and a scatter of small white bones — ordinary ones, at last, only bones — and the chains fell slack, and the vault stopped breathing, and the silence settled over everything like snow.

In the middle of the fallen guards, the Regent knelt in a puddle of its own unraveling, Aldric's borrowed face sagging like wet paper, its voice a thread: "*what — are you —*"

"Full," Ada said. "For the first time in my life. And now empty. And both of those things are mine."

Behind her, small feet hit the floor running. Wren slammed into her back and wrapped both arms around her waist, and Ada turned and held on, held on, held on, in a silence that had no ghosts in it at all — just breath, just heartbeat, just a child crying her name in a language older than kingdoms and then, more precious still, in plain trade-tongue, over and over:

"You're empty. You're empty, Ada, I can feel it, the dead aren't touching you anymore—" A hiccupping sob. "Was it worth it? Tell me it was worth it."

Ada opened her mouth to answer — and stopped.

Because across the vault, rising slowly from the floor on shaking limbs, pushing the last grey tatters of the Regent off his shoulders like a man shedding a fever, was a king. Thin, grey-faced, weeping freely, dressed in clothes that had ruled him longer than he'd ruled in them — but alive, and *his*, every line of him his own, looking at his hands as though meeting them for the first time.

His eyes found hers across the ruined vault.

"You're the bone-carver," Aldric said, and his voice was wrecked and rusted and real. "The one who wrote to me. The one who came." He took a step, then another, staring at her with something that had no business surviving what had just happened. "You gave up your hearing. For a stranger on a throne."

"Not for the throne," Ada said.

She looked down at Wren, whose fists were twisted in her coat, whose face was smeared and fierce and, for the first time since the blizzard, wholly, simply a child's.

"For her."

And that was when the doors at the top of the stairs blew open, and torchlight flooded down, and armed men in royal colors filled the entrance — men who had served a hollow thing for years and had no way yet of knowing that the hollow thing was ash on the floor.
