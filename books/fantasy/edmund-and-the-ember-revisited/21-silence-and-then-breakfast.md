## 21. Silence, and Then Breakfast

The silence had a shape.

Ada had expected nothingness — a clean white absence, like snowfall over a field. What she got instead was a room. A vast, echoing room where every voice she had ever carried used to live, and now the walls of it rang with the memory of them: the miner's whistle-song, Brannoc's last *thank you* still warm in her ears from the burning gate at Vell, the massacre-dead of the Ashen Steps murmuring their names into her keeping, her mother's hum, her father's rough laugh, a hundred strangers' confidences gathered over thirty years of carving bone for the grieving.

They were gone. All of them. And the room they left behind was so large that she felt small inside her own skull.

She sat on the floor of the ruined vault with Wren's hand in hers and did not move for a long time. Above them, through the cracked ceiling, dust sifted down in golden threads. The Ember lay in shards around the dais, and the shards were only shards now — dull crystal, cooling, giving off no light and no voices. She could have held one to her ear until her arm went numb and heard nothing but her own blood.

She wept without deciding to. It came up out of her like water finding a crack in stone, quiet and relentless, and Wren knelt beside her and pressed their foreheads together the way the girl did when words failed — which, in the old tongue or any other, was often.

"I can't hear you," Ada said, when she could speak. "I can't hear anything anymore."

Wren studied her face with those too-old eyes that were, day by day, becoming simply a child's eyes. Then she said something in the old tongue — soft, three syllables, rising at the end like a question.

"I don't know what that means," Ada whispered.

The girl took Ada's hand and pressed it flat against her own small chest, over her heart, and said it again. Slower. And though Ada understood nothing — though the whole architecture of her gift had collapsed into rubble — she understood that one anyway. Some things did not need hearing.

*You are still here. I am still here.*

---

They came up out of the mountain into morning.

Aldric was waiting at the vault's outer gate, and he was not what Ada expected, which was itself a strange new experience; for weeks he had existed for her only as carved tokens and smuggled letters, a king reduced to handwriting. In person he was smaller than his coins. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way portraits would never admit to, and he moved carefully, like a man who had been a passenger in his own body for twenty years and was still learning where all his own limbs were parked.

He looked at the ash drifting out of the mountain behind them, then at Ada, and inclined his head — not the shallow nod a king gives a subject, but the deeper bow a man gives someone who has paid his ransom.

"It's done," he said. His voice was hoarse. Of course it was; it hadn't been properly his in two decades. "I felt it let go. Like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying." He flexed his fingers, watching them as if they belonged to a stranger who had recently become an acquaintance. "I'm mortal now. Truly mortal. I'll age. I'll sicken. I'll die on some ordinary day and no binding will drag me back." He laughed — a rusty, disbelieving sound. "It's the finest thing anyone has ever given me."

"Don't thank me yet," Ada said. Her voice came out flat. "I don't know yet what I've lost besides the hearing."

But she knew. She knew the way you know a limb is gone before you look down.

---

They walked down out of the mountains over three days, because Aldric refused a horse until his legs remembered how to be his, and because none of them were in any hurry to arrive anywhere. The Regent's armies, once the Ember cracked, had come apart like wet paper — thousands of bound soldiers waking mid-march with no master's will holding their spines straight, dropping weapons, sitting down in the road, weeping or laughing or simply standing there blinking at the sky like men surfacing from deep water. Refugees going south passed freed soldiers going nowhere, and mostly they ignored each other, united in exhaustion.

Wren walked between Ada and Aldric and chattered. That was the change that undid Ada most. The girl had always spoken — in her old tongue, in fragments of trade-speech learned on the road — but now she spoke *constantly*, greedily, as if making up for years of being a door instead of a mouth. She narrated rocks. She narrated birds. She asked Aldric forty questions about kingship and seemed satisfied by none of his answers.

"You mean you just *sat*," she said, scandalized, "in a chair, while a dead god wore you."

"Not sat," Aldric said mildly. "Sometimes I stood."

"You're a very bad king."

"The worst," he agreed cheerfully. "Fortunately, I intend to retire."

At night they made small fires, and Ada lay awake listening to the silence inside herself, cataloguing its rooms. Grief, she discovered, was not one thing. It came in waves with different names attached. There was the grief for Brannoc, whom she had known thirty years and lost twice — once to the fire at Vell's gate, once to the silence that followed, when she reached for his voice out of habit and found nothing. There was grief for the miners and mothers and murderers whose secrets she had carried, her strange congregation, disbanded now without a word of farewell. And underneath all of it, the strangest grief of all: for the quiet itself. Thirty years of never being alone in her own head, and now she was alone in it, and loneliness turned out to have a taste. Like well water. Clean and cold and a little mineral, and you could drink it, but you wouldn't call it sweet.

On the second night Wren woke screaming from a dream, and Ada held her, and the girl sobbed out a long stream of the old tongue, and Ada understood perhaps one word in ten, and had to simply hold on and rock and say *I'm here, I'm here,* and hate, quietly, that she couldn't hear whatever else the child needed saying.

*This is the price,* she told herself. *You knew the price. You chose it. Stop counting.*

She kept counting anyway. It was the only prayer she had left.

---

They reached the capital on a grey afternoon, and the city had already begun the work of forgetting how afraid it had been. Banners of the Regent's black sun were coming down all along the Processional; children were selling roasted chestnuts beneath the gallows-platforms being dismantled for firewood. Aldric rode ahead to reclaim a throne he no longer wanted, promising — and this promise he made looking directly at Ada, which she suspected was rare for him — that she would want for nothing, ever, and that her name would be spoken in the great hall with honor.

"Spoken is fine," Ada said. "Just don't make me attend anything."

The palace gave them rooms. Real rooms, with beds that didn't sway and windows that weren't arrow-slits, and Ada slept fourteen hours the first night and woke disoriented by how quiet it was, and then remembered, and lay staring at the canopy while the grief came back around for another pass.

It was Wren who broke the cycle. On the fourth morning, Ada found the girl sitting cross-legged on the floor of their shared chamber, surrounded by the bones Ada had carried out of the mountain — the whistle-femur, the token-bones from her correspondence with Aldric, the little carved relics she'd collected across a lifetime of trade. Wren had one in each hand and was turning them over with the fierce concentration of a scholar half her apparent age.

"What are you doing?"

"Listening," Wren said, in trade-speech, and then corrected herself with the old word, which Ada now recognized if not understood. "*Hearing-at.* Trying-to-hear." She frowned. "They are almost empty. Like cups after a feast. But—" She stopped. Started again. Held up the old whistle-femur, the first bone Ada had ever carved for a dead man, the miner's last warning still folded somewhere inside it, or so Ada had always believed.

"This one has something left," Wren said. "Not a voice. An echo of a voice. The last thing it says. Do you want me to—"

"Yes," Ada said, before pride could intervene. "Yes. Please."

Wren closed her eyes. Her lips moved, translating as she listened, the way she'd done since the Ashen Steps, since Mother Ilex first taught her that translation was a gift you could give rather than a burden you carried alone.

"He says—" Wren's brow furrowed. "He says... *the carver heard us all our lives, and we were glad.*" She opened her eyes. "That's all. That's the last of it. They're not sad, Ada. They're not trapped. When the Ember broke, they went *out*. All of them, everywhere, all the bound souls in the world — they went out like sparks off a bonfire. He says the silence isn't empty." She pressed the bone into Ada's palm and folded Ada's fingers over it. "He says the silence is *thanks*."

Ada looked down at the little femur whistle, worn smooth by her own hands, and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched since the vault.

All those years she had thought of her gift as a burden borne for the dead — and it had been, it truly had, but the dead, it seemed, had experienced it differently. To them she had been a window left open in a sealed house. A draft of living air. And now the house was open on every side, and they had poured through it into whatever comes next, and the last thing any of them had bothered to leave behind was gratitude.

"Well," Ada said, and her voice cracked, and she let it. "Well then."

Wren climbed into her lap, all elbows, far too big for laps and entirely unbothered by this, and Ada held her and cried again — but it was a different crying than the vault-floor crying, or even the mountain crying. This one had an ending built into it.

---

Aldric summoned her formally a week later, which meant he sent a servant, which meant the servant got lost twice in corridors the Regent had walled off, and eventually Ada found her own way to the audience chamber out of old smuggler habit, entering through a door the architects had never drawn.

The court was thin. Half the nobility had fled during the war; the other half were watching the restored king with the wary expressions of people trying to determine whether the man on the throne was really the man on the throne. He was. That was the whole point. He was painfully, gloriously, ordinarily himself — tired, middle-aged, mortal — and Ada watched him watch his court watch him, and saw him decide not to care.

He dismissed everyone but her. Even the guards. Even Wren, who went reluctantly, pausing at the door to inform the king that she would be outside listening at it, and that she knew eleven words for *liar* in the old tongue and was prepared to use them.

When they were alone, Aldric came down from the dais. He didn't sit on the steps or lean on anything kingly. He just stood in front of her, in his plain grey mourning clothes — mourning, she realized, for himself, for the man he might have been if a god had never crawled inside him — and said:

"Name it."

"Name what?"

"Whatever you're owed. Land, title, gold, a seat at this table. You unbound a god and gave me back my life. The treasury is full and my conscience is empty, and I find I prefer it that way, but I intend to spend the treasury fixing it. Start with yourself."

Ada had thought about this. She had thought about it on the mountain road, and in the bath the palace provided, and at 3 a.m. staring at the canopy. She had turned the offer over the way she used to turn bones, feeling for the true shape under the surface.

"A place at court," Aldric prompted, when she didn't answer. "Master of Relics. You'd have resources. Apprentices. Your craft preserved, your knowledge honored—"

"No."

Something flickered across his face — hurt, quickly mastered. "No?"

"I spent thirty years being useful to the powerful, Your Majesty, and it nearly killed me twice, and the second time it did kill something." She touched her own ear, briefly, an old gesture. "I'm done being an institution. I'm done being a resource. I was a person before I was a bone-carver, and I'd like to find out if she's still in there."

"Then name anything else."

"Land," Ada said. "In the mountains. North, near the high passes — there's a valley above the old salt road, past the shrine at Coldmere. There's a house on it. Stone, slate roof, probably falling down. Good light for a workshop. A stream." She paused. "Room for a garden. Room for a second chair."

Aldric looked at her for a long moment, and then he smiled, and it transformed his whole face, made him look like a man rather than a portrait. "The Coldmere valley. That's crown land. It's also, if I recall my geography, four days' hard ride from the nearest town and six from the nearest court."

"That's the idea."

"You'll be lonely."

"I'll be *quiet*," Ada said. "There's a difference. I've only recently learned it." She hesitated, and then gave him the truth, because he had earned truth more than most kings ever did. "I spent my whole life alone because the dead never left me room for anyone living. Now the dead have gone, and I find I don't want to be alone anymore. I want to be *deliberately* alone. Alone by choice, with someone who chooses me back. There's a girl in your corridor right now pretending she doesn't know eleven words for liar, and she's coming with me, and I need land enough that she can run and shout and carve terrible whistles without a single neighbor complaining."

"And when she grows?" Aldric asked gently. "When she wants cities, roads, her own life? Will your valley hold her?"

Ada thought of Wren asleep beside the crate of whispering souls, of the girl's hand pressing Ada's palm over a small beating heart, of *you are still here, I am still here.*

"The valley doesn't need to hold her," Ada said. "It just needs to be the place she's always welcome to return to. There's a difference in that, too. I'm learning a lot of differences lately. It's what happens when the noise stops."

Aldric signed the grant that afternoon with his own hand — the first document of his restored reign, though the chroniclers would record otherwise — and pressed the seal while the wax was still soft, and handed it to her like a man handing over a debt he'd been carrying longer than a crown.

"For what it's worth," he said at the door, awkward, unkingly, "the hour a night I was myself — writing those letters, sending them out through cracks in my own prison — it wasn't hope that kept me doing it. Hope is a thing you feel. What kept me was discipline. I wrote because writing was the one act in the world that was mine." He looked at her steadily. "Then your tokens started arriving, and I learned there was a second discipline. It's called being worth rescuing. I intend to practice it for the rest of my life. Both of you made that possible. The valley is the least of it."

---

They left the capital in early spring, on a cart bought with the first installment of a treasury that Aldric insisted on overpaying, loaded with tools and seed and a workshop-lathe and one extremely smug cat acquired from a Vell refugee camp, who appointed himself to the cart's front bench and permitted them to travel with him.

The road north took nine days. Wren talked the entire way. She taught Ada the old tongue in scraps and pieces — not systematically, the way Ilex had tried, but the way children teach languages, which is to say relentlessly and out of order and with much laughter at Ada's accent. By the third day Ada could count. By the fifth she could curse, which Wren found endlessly delightful. By the seventh, riding through the pass where the winter blizzard had first brought a half-frozen child to her door, Ada caught herself thinking a phrase in the old tongue — *cold-here, home-soon* — and realized the language had moved in without asking, the way Wren had.

The house at Coldmere had indeed fallen down somewhat. The roof leaked over the workshop and the garden was a battlefield of nettles and the chimney smoked sideways in a wind. Ada stood in the doorway the first evening, surveying a year of work, and felt something she had no trade-word for: not peace exactly, not yet. More like the moment after setting down a heavy load, when your arms still ache and your hands still remember the weight, but the weight is on the ground, and it is not going to be picked up again.

Wren ran through every room shouting echoes, claimed the loft for herself, and then appeared at Ada's elbow holding the whistle-femur — the miner's bone, the first voice, the last echo.

"You should hang it in the workshop," the girl said. "First tool of the house."

"It doesn't whistle anymore."

"It doesn't need to." Wren hung it on a nail above the lathe herself, crooked, perfect. Then she stepped back and looked at it, and at Ada, and said something in the old tongue that Ada only half understood — but the half she understood was the word for *beginning*, and the tone was the rising question-tone, and Ada answered the only way that mattered.

"Yes," she said. "Beginning."

Outside, the wind came down off the high passes carrying the smell of snowmelt and pine, and the stream below the house ran loud and silver over stones, and the silence in Ada's head — her own silence, her chosen silence, her hard-won and grief-bought silence — filled up, slowly, with the sound of one living voice calling her to come see the stars.
