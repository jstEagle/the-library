## 22. Edmund and the Ember Revisited

The second chair had been Wren's idea, though she'd never said so in words.

Ada had noticed it the way she noticed most things now — slowly, without the dead whispering their observations into her ear. A chair had simply appeared one morning at the far end of the workbench, dragged from somewhere, its legs uneven, its seat worn smooth by some previous life. Wren sat in it that first day with her arms crossed and her chin up, daring Ada to comment. Ada had not commented. She had only moved her lamp a little to the left so the light fell across both of them equally, and that had been that.

A year. It still surprised her, how a year could pass without catastrophe. The workshop above the river bend smelled of cedar shavings and beeswax and, in the cold months, of woodsmoke and the barley porridge Wren had learned to make — badly, then adequately, then well enough that Ada stopped making it herself and let the girl own it entirely. Ownership mattered to Wren. She collected small sovereignties the way other children collected river stones: the porridge, the sweeping of the floor, the south window where she kept her growing hoard of carved things arranged by a logic known only to her.

Ada worked at the bench as the autumn light came slantwise through the east window, turning the dust motes to drifting gold. Before her lay a femur — not human this time, but elk, thick and honey-colored, destined for a shepherd down in the valley who wanted a flute for his daughter's wedding. In the old days, in the years before the Ember cracked and went silent, Ada would have held such a bone and listened to the elk's last hour: the snow, the wolves perhaps, or the hunter's arrow, the animal's final confusion and its strange, wordless peace. She would have carved that peace into the flute's bore, and the music would have carried it.

Now there was nothing. She pressed her thumb against the bone's cool length and felt only bone — dense, dumb, beautiful in the way that stone is beautiful, holding nothing but itself.

She had expected the silence to be a wound forever. For the first months it had been exactly that. She would reach for the voices out of habit, the way a tongue seeks a missing tooth, and find nothing, and the nothing would ache like frostbite. She had grieved the dead as one grieves the dead — not for them, who were gone regardless, but for the conversation. Thirty years of company. Thirty years of being the last stop before whatever came after, the ferry-woman of last words. And then, in a single flooding moment beneath the palace, every soul she had ever carried had passed through her and onward, and the door behind them had closed.

What she had not expected was how much room the silence left.

* * *

Wren came in from the yard with her sleeves pushed up and sawdust in her hair, carrying something cupped in both hands the way one carries a bird or an ember.

"It's done," she said.

Ada set down her knife. "Let me see, then."

Wren climbed onto her chair — her chair, hers by right of conquest — and opened her hands. In her palms lay a whistle. It was small, carved from a knucklebone of the old ox that had died on the mountainside two winters back, sanded until it shone like a river pebble. The workmanship was rough in places; the mouthpiece was a little crooked; the finger holes were not quite evenly spaced. It was, in every technical respect, the work of a nine-year-old with eight months of lessons.

But it was finished. That was the thing about Wren's work now — she finished things. She had begun the year unable to complete even the simplest carving, abandoning each piece halfway through as though finishing it might trap something inside it, as though every object she made might become a door again. Ada understood. You did not spend your first years as a key, as a vessel, as the living hinge of a binding older than kingdoms, without learning to fear containers. But somewhere through the winter the fear had loosened, and in spring Wren had finished a spoon, and in summer a comb, and now, on a bright morning in the last week of harvest, a whistle.

"Blow it, then," Ada said.

Wren raised it to her lips with great ceremony, screwed up her face, and blew.

Nothing. Not a note, not a wheeze, not the thin ghost-hum that bone whistles make even when badly bored. Just the soft sound of a child exhaling through a piece of carved ox-bone, which is to say, almost no sound at all.

Wren lowered it. Her face did something complicated — disappointment wrestling with relief, and relief winning, though it took its time.

"It doesn't sing," she said.

"No," Ada agreed. "It doesn't."

They looked at each other across the workbench, and then, because neither of them could hold the solemnity another moment, they laughed — Wren first, a snorting helpless laugh, and Ada after her, deeper, rustier, the laugh of a woman discovering muscles she hadn't used in years.

"It's just a toy," Wren said, wonder in it. "Ada. It's *just a toy.*"

"That's the finest thing a whistle can be." Ada reached over and turned the little bone instrument in the light. "Whistles are meant for calling sheep and annoying sisters and playing tunes at weddings. They're not meant for carrying the dead. Whoever told you otherwise was lying."

"Nobody told me anything," Wren said. "I remembered." And then, quickly, before the memory could put weight on her: "Can I sign it?"

Ada's knife hand stilled.

Signing was ritual between them now, and it had grown from a single afternoon in early summer when Wren had watched Ada press her mark into a finished piece — the ashgrove leaf, three-lobed, cut deep — and had asked, in the careful old tongue that was becoming her second language and Ada's grudging third, whose name the mark was. *Mine,* Ada had said. *Only mine?* Wren had asked. And Ada had looked at the girl for a long moment, at this child who had been forged rather than born, owned rather than loved, used rather than kept, and had fetched a second knife.

*Then we'll need two marks.*

Now Wren took the small blade they kept for her — blunted, safe, but a real blade, because Ada did not believe in pretend tools — and cut her own sign into the whistle's base beside Ada's leaf. Her mark was a wren, of course, or her version of one: a round body, a tail like a struck match, an eye that was slightly too large and gave every signed piece the look of being mildly astonished. Ada had suggested once that she refine it. Wren had refused with the full authority of a guildmaster. The astonished birds stayed.

Two names. Side by side. On everything that left the workshop now — the flutes and combs and buttons, the memorial pieces for valley folk who wanted their dead honored in bone, the toys Wren made for the miller's children. Two names, and the world outside had begun to notice. Travelers who bought a carving at the market in Dunmarrow would ask after the makers, and someone would say, *the bone-carver and her girl, up past the river bend,* and the phrase *her girl* had settled around Wren like a coat cut to fit.

Ada had never signed anything with anyone in her life. She thought about that sometimes, working late, her thumb resting on the double-marked base of some finished piece. Thirty years of solitary trade, thirty years of careful isolation, of rules against commissions from the powerful, of loneliness mistaken for peace — and all it had taken to end it was a blizzard, a knocking in a language older than any kingdom, and a war for the soul of a king.

She would not have traded a single day of it back.

* * *

The traveler came at midday, riding up from the valley road with the dust of the lowlands still on his boots.

He was a wool merchant by his pack-frame, a talker by his mouth, and he accepted the bowl of stew Wren brought him with the unembarrassed appetite of a man who had been eating trail food for a fortnight. Ada knew him slightly — Master Corvin, he was called, who ran wool between Vell and the coast twice a year and always stopped, because a man who talked needed listeners and the bone-carver's house had become famous for having them.

"You'll have heard already," Corvin said, between mouthfuls. "About the council."

"We hear nothing up here," Ada said. "That's the arrangement we have with the mountain."

Corvin grinned. "Then I'm your arrangement's exception. The free cities stand. All six — ratified at Vell, under the new charter, sealed with the king's own hand." He said *the king* without the old flinch people still sometimes gave the title, and Ada noted it. A year ago, saying *King Aldric* had made folk lower their voices, half in awe and half in fear of what wore the crown. Now it was said plainly, the way one says *the miller* or *the river.*

"They rebuilt the eastern quarter of Vell," Corvin went on. "You'd hardly know it burned. Mother Ilex has a seat on the council now — they made her something grand, First Voice or some such. She asks after you, you know. Every time I come through, she sends the same message: *tell the carver the door is open.*"

"Tell her the door here is open too," Ada said, "and better stocked."

"And the king?" Wren asked, from her chair. She had gone quiet during the talk of Vell — she always did, when the fire was mentioned — but her voice was steady.

Corvin turned to her with the easy warmth he extended to children and dogs and anyone unlikely to buy wool. "Well, miss, they say he's changed. Walks the city walls himself, plain-cloaked, talks to fishwives and dockhands. No more of the old grandeur. Some say diminished." He shrugged philosophically. "I say a king who knows what hunger tastes like is worth ten who don't. He stood at the founding ceremony for four hours in the rain and wouldn't take a canopy. There's a story going round that he keeps a night desk — writes letters till dawn, though nobody knows to whom."

Ada kept her face still. Wren glanced at her, quick and knowing, and looked away smiling.

There were letters. They came perhaps six times a year, carried by circuitous routes through Brannoc's old network — the smuggler roads remembering their dead ferryman the way rivers remember their banks. Aldric wrote in a cramped, urgent hand that had never fully learned leisure, describing the rebuilding, the politics, the slow strangeness of governing a body that answered only to him. Once he had written: *Some nights I sit at my desk and do nothing at all, merely because the hour is mine. You cannot imagine what a luxury an empty hour is to a man who spent seven years as a passenger. I am learning to waste time beautifully.* And once, more quietly: *I remember the vault. I remember you standing in the dark with the whole weight of the dead coming into you, and I have built a kingdom on the debt. If ever the mountain grows too quiet, write, and I will send noise.*

She had written back once, at Wren's insistence: *The mountain is quiet. We prefer it. P.S. — Wren says your handwriting looks like a spider drowned in ink. She is right.*

The letter she'd received in return had contained, folded small, a pressed sprig of mountain ash and a single line: *Tell her the spider agrees.*

* * *

That evening, after Corvin had gone and the dishes were done, Ada sat at the bench with the lamp turned low, doing the work she saved for last light: the signing.

Before her lay the day's finished pieces — the shepherd's flute, three combs, a set of buttons for the innkeeper's wife, and a small memorial tablet commissioned by a family in the valley whose grandmother had died in August at ninety-one years, full of years and opinions. In the old days Ada would have heard the grandmother's voice in the bone she chose; she would have carved the woman's last words into the tablet's border, and the family would have wept at the truth of them. Now she carved instead from what the family told her, and it was enough — different, but enough. The daughter had written that Grandmother Maren's final sentence, spoken clear as a bell an hour before the end, had been: *Don't fuss, I've had a good run, and mind you water the rosemary.*

Ada carved it into the border in her fine lettering, and found she was smiling.

She picked up her knife and cut the ashgrove leaf into the tablet's base. Then she held it out without looking, the way one hands a tool to a partner of long practice, and Wren took it and added the astonished bird.

"There," Wren said, blowing away the last of the dust. "Signed and done."

Ada turned the tablet in the lamplight. Two marks, side by side, cut deep enough to outlast them both. Somewhere in the valley, a family would hang this on their wall and trace the strange double signature and maybe wonder, and the wondering would be a kind of story, and the story would travel, and that was how names became legends — not through trumpets, but through small repeated signs on small beloved objects, passed hand to hand for generations.

*Edmund and the Ember,* the old songs called it now — the ballads had gotten the details wrong within months, as ballads do, giving the bone-carver a sword and the child a crown and the Regent a dragon's shape. Wren had heard a minstrel perform one at the Dunmarrow fair and had come home furious. *"Edmund?"* she'd demanded. *"Who is Edmund? There was no Edmund!"* Ada had suggested that perhaps every legend needs a name it can't explain, a loose thread for listeners to pull. Wren had considered this, unconvinced, and then had gone to her corner and begun carving a small figure of a man with a sword, labeled on the bottom, in her careful letters: *EDMUND (NOBODY).* It sat on the south windowsill now among her treasures, mildly astonished, keeping watch.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the first true cold of the season was coming down off the peaks, and soon the passes would close and the world would shrink to the workshop, the river, the two of them — the way it had shrunk last winter, and the winter before, and how Ada had once dreaded that shrinking and now guarded it like treasure.

Wren yawned enormously, the universal signal of childhood, and began stacking her tools.

"Ada," she said, around the yawn. "Next year. Can I learn the deep cuts? Real ones. Sign-work depth."

"When your hands stop growing," Ada said, for the fourth time.

"My hands aren't going to *stop.*"

"Then you'll wait a long while yet, and be excellent by the time you start."

Wren muttered something in the old tongue that Ada was fairly sure was not complimentary, and Ada, who had learned enough of the language to catch insults and blessings and not much between, raised an eyebrow. Wren grinned, entirely unrepentant, kissed the top of her head — she had grown tall enough to do that this year, a fact Ada was pretending not to mourn — and went to bed.

The workshop settled into silence. Not the silence of absence — Ada knew that silence intimately, had grieved it, had finally made her peace with it — but the silence of a room where everything needed is present: the banked stove, the sleeping child behind the curtain, the finished work drying on its racks, the wind testing the shutters and finding them fastened.

She sat a while longer, her thumb moving over the double signature on Grandmother Maren's tablet. *Don't fuss, I've had a good run.* Good advice from a stranger dead five hundred miles and two months distant. She had had a good run too, she supposed — stranger than most, lonelier than most, and ending nowhere near where it began. The lonely woman who had carved warnings into whistles and called it peace would not recognize this life: the second chair, the shared name, the child's porridge, the king's letters, the legend with the wrong name in it. She hoped the old Ada, wherever dead versions of ourselves go, was listening anyway, out of habit, and approving.

She banked the lamp. On the windowsill, Edmund (Nobody) kept his watch, and beside him Wren's first whistle lay where she'd left it, crooked and silent and perfect — a toy, just a toy, the finest thing a whistle can be.

In the morning there would be porridge, badly-sung songs, work worth signing. In the spring, perhaps, a letter from a king who had learned to waste time beautifully, and a visit from a priestess whose door was always open. And next winter, if the girl's hands ever stopped growing, the deep cuts, and a second knife sharpened true.

Ada blew out the lamp and went to bed in the warm dark, deaf to the dead, fluent at last in the language of the living — and kept.
