## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley woke without magic the way a body wakes without breath—slowly, grudgingly, and with a dull ache it could not name.

Petra Vasquez felt that ache every morning before she opened her eyes. It was not pain, exactly. It was more like an absence pressed against her ribs from the inside, the shape of something that had once lived there. Twenty-three years in Hollowmere, and she had learned to dress around the absence the way other women dressed around old scars: methodically, without looking at it too long.

She rose before the sun cleared the eastern ridge, as she always did, and lit no lamp because there was nothing to light it with but tallow, and tallow was for winter. The floorboards announced each of her steps with a creak she knew by heart—the third one lied about its depth, the seventh groaned like an old man standing up—and she moved through her small stone house in the blue half-dark performing the only liturgy left to her.

The sword hung above the hearth.

It was not a beautiful thing to look at, though strangers sometimes mistook it for one. A plain crossguard, darkened silver. A grip wrapped in leather gone smooth as riverstone from handling. The scabbard was oiled black leather stitched with thread that had once been gold—or so the villagers said, when they said anything at all about it. In the valley's flat grey light, the gold showed only as a dull yellow memory of itself, like everything else here. Like Petra herself.

She took it down with both hands, the way she had every morning for twenty-three years, and carried it to the table by the window where the light would come first.

The maintenance was ritual, and she did not pretend otherwise. Oil first—neat's-foot, warmed between her palms until it ran thin as water. She worked it into the scabbard's seams with a cloth gone stiff and dark from years of the same work. Then the blade-guard: a sliver of beeswax rubbed along the throat of the sheath where steel met leather, because even a sword that never left its scabbard deserved to be ready. Then the hilt, the pommel, the crossguard, each wiped and turned and wiped again while outside the window the valley went about its own morning rituals—the millwheel turning, smoke rising straight up from chimneys because there was no wind worth naming, the slow brown river doing its patient work of forgetting.

Her hands remembered everything. That was the cruelty of it. Her fingers knew the balance point of the blade to the hair's breadth, knew the exact pressure that would free it from the scabbard if she chose to draw, knew the seven forms and the three counter-forms and the wordless cadence that bound them all together into something the old books had called *the speech of iron*. Her hands were still a swordspeaker's hands.

They simply had nothing left to say.

Because in Hollowmere, words were only words. Petra had tested this in her first year, alone in her house at midnight, young enough then to be reckless. She had gripped the hilt and shaped the opening syllable of the First Form—that single consonant-vowel that should have made the blade sing awake in its sheath, should have set the very air trembling with edge-light—and what happened?

Nothing.

The word fell out of her mouth and died on the floor like a moth. No hum in the steel. No warmth in her palms. The hearth-fire hadn't even flickered. And the silence afterward had been so complete, so utterly indifferent, that Petra had understood two things at once, with the certainty of a blade coming down: the magic wasn't broken, and neither was she. Something was drinking it. The valley drank words the way sand drank rain—greedily, silently, leaving nothing behind but the dry fact of having spoken.

She had not tried again. Not out of fear, exactly. Out of something closer to respect. You did not shout into a mouth that size.

* * *

By full light she was done with the sword and hung it back above the hearth, and the house resumed its ordinary shape around her: one room below, a sleeping loft above, shelves of jars, a bed she made with corners sharp enough to cut. Outside, Hollowmere was already at its work. The village sat in the crook of the river where the valley floor widened—a scatter of slate roofs and whitewashed walls, a mill, a smithy whose chimney never quite drew right, a chapel that had been a granary before Petra came and might be a granary again after she left, if anyone remembered how.

That was the thing about Hollowmere that visitors never understood, if visitors ever came, which they mostly didn't: the village forgot things. Not cruelly. Not all at once. But steadily, the way a stream wears a stone. Names of distant towns thinned out. Songs lost their middle verses. Old Aldous the cooper could remember his wedding day perfectly but not his wife's face anymore, and no one found this strange, and that was strangest of all. The forgetting was gentle. It was even, in its way, kind. Grief here had a short half-life. Quarrels died of natural causes. Children grew up light, unburdened, the past sliding off them like water off slate.

And Petra stayed heavy.

She was the one weight the valley couldn't wear away, and she suspected—though she had never said it aloud, never let herself finish the thought—that this was why they watched her. Why conversations paused, just slightly, when she passed the well. Why Maren Cole the miller's widow would give her flour with perfect courtesy and perfect distance, counting out the measure with eyes that never quite met hers. Why children were called indoors when she walked the river path, not with alarm but with a motherly instinct so smooth it might have been nothing at all, if you weren't the one it smoothed against.

Twenty-three years, and she was still *the woman who came over the pass*. Still the stranger. Still the one who kept a sword above her hearth in a valley where no one else owned anything sharper than a bread knife, and no one asked her why, and the not-asking was louder than any question could have been.

She bought her flour.

"Last of the winter grind," Maren said, pouring. "Spring wheat won't be milled till the thaw comes down off the ridge."

"Then I'll come back at thaw," Petra said.

Maren nodded, and the nod held nothing and gave nothing, and Petra carried her sack out into a street where three people had been talking and now were not.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the boundary stones.

There were eleven of them, or perhaps twelve—the twelfth had slipped under the river's edge sometime before Petra arrived and only showed its crown in drought years—and they marked the valley's rim at roughly a mile beyond the last field. Grey, waist-high, older than any wall, older than any road. They bore no markings that the eye could find, though Petra had run her fingers over every inch of every one of them across two decades of walking their circuit, and her fingertips insisted there was writing there, worn down past reading, worn down past feeling, worn down to a smoothness that was itself a kind of text. Something had been written on these stones so long ago, and erased so thoroughly, that the erasure had become the inscription.

Once a month, on the dark of the moon, she walked the whole circuit. Today was not the dark of the moon. Today she walked it anyway, because tomorrow—

Tomorrow.

She stopped at the third stone and stood with her hand flat on its cold crown and looked back down the valley, at the smoke and the slate roofs and the river going its patient way, and admitted to herself what the whole walk was for.

Tomorrow was the anniversary. Twenty-three years since she had knelt on the frost-hardened ground at the valley's mouth, half-dead of wounds she could no longer fully picture, and spoken her oath into the dead air. *I will keep the quiet. I will keep the peace. I will hold this place against all that seeks entry.* She remembered the oath perfectly—of course she did; it was the one sentence in Hollowmere the valley had never managed to drink. She remembered kneeling. She remembered the taste of blood and the weight of the sword across her shoulders. What she could not remember, no matter how she reached, was *why*. What had driven her up that pass. What had been chasing her. Whose blood had dried on her hands besides her own.

The valley had eaten those memories first, before any others. As if they were the sweetest part.

Or—as if someone had wanted them gone.

She pushed that thought down where she kept it, in the deep cellar of herself alongside the untried First Form and the face she could almost see when falling asleep, a face that dissolved every morning like breath off glass. Someone she had loved. Or failed. Or both. The valley kept its counsel, and she kept hers, and the years went by like the river going under the bridge: continuous, unhurried, carrying everything away.

Dusk came early this far north in autumn. She was back within the last field's edge when she saw the storm.

It came down off the western ridge wrong—not rolling in along the wind but dropping, folding over the crest like poured ink, too fast and too low, trailing rain that fell in ropes. The villagers had seen it too; doors were closing along the lane, shutters swinging, a dog giving its one opinion and being silenced. Petra stood in the open and watched it come and felt—for the first time in twenty-three years—a flicker of something under her breastbone that she refused, absolutely refused, to call hope.

Because the storm was crossing the boundary stones.

Nothing crossed the boundary stones. That was the whole architecture of her life. The wards—whatever ancient, wordless mechanism the stones embodied—took a dim view of passage in either direction. Petra herself had tested the line twice in her early years, walking to the third stone and laying her hand across the invisible threshold, and both times had felt a resistance like wading into deep water, a pressure that said *not yet, not you*, and both times she had stepped back, obedient to an oath she couldn't remember making properly. Things came into the valley only when the valley allowed. Wagons of salt. Peddlers, rarely, who always seemed slightly blurred in retrospect. Rain.

Rain crossed freely. Storms crossed freely.

But this storm moved like it had somewhere to be.

Lightning walked down the ridge in long white strides, and the first drops hit the dust like thrown gravel, and Petra turned for home with her collar up and the wind shoving her between the shoulder blades, and told herself firmly that what she felt was only the weather in her joints, only the anniversary sitting heavy on her, only loneliness wearing duty's coat as usual.

She had the door barred and the fire built when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. Knuckles too slight to make more than a child's sound against oak—and yet beneath them, faint as a pulse under skin, the sword above her hearth

hummed.

One clear note, soft as a struck wineglass, hanging in the silent house.

In twenty-three years, in this valley where words went to die, where her blade had been a beautiful corpse on the wall—her sword sang.

Petra stood with her hand on the bar and did not move. The rain hammered. The knocks came again, patient, unhurried, the knocking of someone who knew the door would open. And above the fire, in its black scabbard stitched with dead gold, the sword hummed on—a sound like a held breath finally released after decades, like a word that had been waiting all this time for another voice to answer it.

Outside, small feet shifted in the mud. Small lungs filled with air.

And through the door, in a voice like bells rung underwater—in a language Petra did not know and somehow, bone-deep, recognized—a child began to speak.
