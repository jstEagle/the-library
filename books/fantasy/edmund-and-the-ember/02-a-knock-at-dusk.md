## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The storm came up the valley like something with intent.

Petra felt it before she heard it—a pressure behind her ears, a tightness in the old scar along her forearm that had nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the years she'd spent pretending it did. The sky over the western ridge went the color of wet slate, and the pines on the slopes bent and whispered in a language of their own, and the first fat drops struck the dust of her yard with a sound like small fists knocking.

She was oiling the scabbard when the light died.

Not the daylight—she expected that, with the storm swallowing the sun whole—but the lamp she kept burning in the window, the one she had kept burning every dusk for thirty-one years without once letting it go out. The flame shrank to a blue bead, guttered, steadied. Outside, the wind found its voice all at once, a long howl coming down from the peaks as though the mountains themselves had opened their mouths.

"Early this year," Petra said aloud, to no one. She said things aloud to no one often enough that the habit had worn grooves in her days, the way water wears grooves in stone: the greeting to the empty chair each morning, the report of small failures to the sword on its pegs each night. *The hinge rusted. I mended it. The bread came out flat.* Thirty-one years of talking to a blade that never answered.

She set down the rag. Went to the window.

The valley road was a brown ribbon drowning in rain. Beyond it her garden lay flat under the deluge, the bean rows already standing in shallow lakes, and beyond the garden the boundary stones stood gray and patient in the murk, doing what they had always done: nothing anyone could see, and everything no one could name. The villagers called them *the quiet stones*. Children were taught not to climb them. Dogs refused to pass them. And in thirty-one years, Petra Vasquez had never once seen anything cross them—not bird, not beast, not wind-borne seed—that carried more strangeness than the ordinary world could hold.

That was the point of them. That was why she was here.

She was turning back to the fire when the knock came.

Three knocks. Small ones. A child's knuckles, if she had to guess—and she did have to guess, because no child had knocked on this door since the miller's boy had run away from his father's belt eleven years ago, and he had knocked the way frightened children knock, fast and fluttering, like a bird trapped indoors.

These knocks were slow. Even. Unhurried as the tolling of a bell.

Petra stood very still in the middle of her one room. Rain hammered the roof. The lamp flame leaned hard sideways though there was no draft, leaning toward the door like a dog scenting something it could not decide whether to fear.

No one came up this path. No one came up this mountain except the few who brought her flour and salt twice a year and left as quickly as courtesy allowed, and none of them would be out in a storm like this, and none of them would knock like that.

The knock came again. Three more. Patient.

"I'm coming," she said, and was annoyed to hear that her voice had gone soft, the way it did sometimes, unbidden—the voice she imagined using for someone who might someday answer back.

*Foolish*, she told herself, and took the poker from beside the hearth anyway, because foolishness and prudence had shared this house for three decades and had learned to split the chores.

* * *

The child stood on her doorstep as though the storm had been arranged for his benefit and he had chosen not to attend.

He was perhaps seven, maybe eight—it was hard to tell, because he was soaked to the skin and shivering not at all, and his stillness made him seem older than his body. He was thin in the way of children who have been hungry long enough to stop complaining about it. His hair was dark and plastered flat to his skull. His clothes were odd—she could not have named what was wrong with them, only that the cut of the little shirt belonged to no tailor in the valley or any market she remembered, stitched in patterns that made her eyes slide off them like rain off oiled leather.

He was barefoot. On stone. In water running an inch deep across her threshold. In cold that would turn a grown man's toes blue inside an hour.

And he was looking up at her with eyes so dark they seemed to hold the storm's own color, entirely unafraid, entirely unsurprised, as though he had walked a very long way to arrive precisely here and had never doubted, even once, that the door would open.

"You shouldn't be out in this," Petra said.

The child tilted his head. Then he spoke.

It was not words—or rather, they were words, but they were words from somewhere Petra had no map for. The syllables came out round and resonant, each one shaped like something being set down carefully on a table: *ah-sel, ah-sel, vennu-kai.* Three sounds, four. They fell into the drumming rain and did not drown in it. Instead—

Instead the rain bent around them.

Petra would swear to her dying day, though she had sworn many smaller oaths and broken none of them, that for the length of those syllables the storm grew quieter. Not gone. *Attended.* As if the thunder itself had paused mid-breath to listen.

And on the wall inside her house, on the two iron pegs where her sword hung wrapped in its oilcloth shroud, something stirred.

The sound was faint—a single low note, like the hum a plucked wire makes in the instant after it stops being touched. But Petra had lived thirty-one years in a valley where iron stayed dumb and magic stayed outside the boundary stones, and she knew the difference between a house settling and a blade remembering what it was.

Her sword had just hummed.

She gripped the doorframe. The wood bit into her palm and she welcomed the pain of it, because the pain was ordinary, and she needed one ordinary thing to stand on.

"What did you say?" she asked, and hated how rough her voice came out.

The child looked past her shoulder, into the dim room, toward the wall where the sword hung. He could not have seen it—the wrapping was plain, the shape beneath it ambiguous—but his gaze went to it as surely as a compass needle goes north, and his lips parted again.

*Vennu-kai,* he said softly. Almost tenderly.

The note rose. Longer this time. Deeper. It was not a loud sound; it was a sound that made other sounds feel temporary. The lamp flame stood straight up, unmoving in the gale. The rain against the roof seemed to fall in time with it, drop by measured drop, and Petra felt the hair rise on her arms and a heat bloom behind her sternum that she had not felt since she was nineteen years old and kneeling in a circle of older voices, swearing herself to a duty whose full weight she had understood only later, only slowly, only through decades of carrying it alone.

Recognition. That was the heat. Terrible and sweet and unwelcome as a stranger's hand on her shoulder in the dark.

"No," Petra said. Not to the child—to the sword, to herself, to whatever old machinery had begun to turn. "No, you don't."

But her feet were already stepping backward, opening the doorway wider, and the child walked in out of the storm without hurrying, bare feet leaving prints on her floor that steamed faintly and then were dry.

* * *

She put him by the fire. Of course she did. Whatever else he was—and he was something else, she knew it in her teeth, in the humming wall, in the way the shadows of the room behaved around him—he was also a small soaked child on a freezing night, and thirty-one years of solitary duty had not burned the ordinary kindness out of her, though some nights she suspected it was trying.

He sat where she put him, cross-legged on the rug before the hearth, and let her wrap a blanket around his shoulders. He drank the broth she made him in small careful sips, holding the bowl with both hands, watching her over its rim with those storm-dark eyes.

He did not shiver. She noticed that properly now, cataloging it the way she cataloged everything strange about this valley, everything she kept filed away in the locked room at the back of her mind where she stored the things she could not afford to think about. His skin had warmed but his breathing had not quickened. His wet clothes steamed and dried while he wore them, faster than any fire should manage. And when the lightning flashed through the window, throwing the room into white relief—

When the lightning flashed, the child cast no shadow.

The firelight gave him one: a soft dark twin flickering on the rug beside him. But the lightning—the true light, the clean white light that showed everything exactly as it was—gave him nothing. For one stark instant the room held Petra's shadow thrown huge and wavering against the far wall, the table's shadow, the chair's shadow, the shadow of the wrapped sword hanging on its pegs.

And a small boy sitting in a pool of light with nothing attached to him at all.

The bowl trembled in her hands. She set it down before he could see—or before he could see that she saw, which was different, because those dark eyes missed nothing.

"You're not from the village," she said. It wasn't a question, but he shook his head anyway, a small precise motion. "Do you have a name?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again, and what came out this time was not the round resonant language but a strangled half-sound, a click in the throat, like a word trying to be born and failing. His face worked. Frustration, then something worse underneath the frustration—something that looked, on a face so young, heartbreakingly like grief.

Then he pressed his palm flat against his own chest, just below the collarbone, and held it there, and looked at her with an expression that needed no translation at all.

*Here. Something is here. I cannot say what.*

The lamp guttered. On the wall, the sword hummed—softly now, steadily, a thread of sound woven through the storm's roar, as if it too had turned toward the boy and was listening to whatever burned beneath his hand.

And Petra Vasquez, last oath-bound swordspeaker of a valley that forgot, keeper of a blade she had not drawn in three decades, felt the oath wake in her blood like an old wound reopening, and heard—as clearly as if the words were spoken in her mother tongue, in her mother's voice, in the voice of every swordspeaker dead and gone who had ever knelt in a circle of candlelight and given their lives to the keeping of lost things—

*What comes to your door is yours to answer. What burns is yours to carry.*

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, the boy was still watching her, hand on his chest, waiting. Patient as the boundary stones. Barefoot in her house, shadowless in the lightning, humming her sword awake after thirty-one years of silence.

Outside, the storm redoubled, and somewhere high on the mountain, though she could not yet know it, something else had noticed the wards flicker.

"Stay the night," Petra said hoarsely, and reached for another blanket she would not need.
