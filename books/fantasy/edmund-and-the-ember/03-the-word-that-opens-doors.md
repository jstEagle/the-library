## 3. The Word That Opens Doors

The boy slept in the corner by the hearth like something that had been set down there rather than laid.

Petra had not slept at all. She sat across the room with her back to the wall and the sword across her knees, wrapped in its oilcloth as it always was, unwrapped in her hands as it never was, and she watched him breathe. The storm had spent itself against the shutters around midnight and moved on down the valley, taking its thunder with it, leaving behind that washed, hollow quiet that made every small sound seem like a confession. The fire ticked. The boy breathed. And the sword—

The sword was still humming.

It had not stopped since the moment he stood dripping in her doorway and opened his mouth and said *sennavar* — or whatever it was, whatever impossible syllable had come out of a child's throat sounding less like speech and more like a key turning in a lock older than any door she knew. She had felt it through the floorboards first, before she heard it: a vibration in the soles of her feet, climbing her shins, settling into her teeth like a struck bell she could not stop hearing. Forty years in this valley where magic came to die, forty years of a blade that lay in its wrappings like a corpse she refused to bury, and in one breath of a mute child's voice it had woken.

She had checked the boundary stones at dawn's edge, before full light, walking the perimeter with her lantern hooded. All nine stones stood. No cracks. No seepage. Whatever the boy carried, it had not come through the wards from outside. It had come *in* him, the way a seed comes inside an apple, already part of the fruit.

Now morning was coming up gray behind the shutters, and Petra turned the sword over on her knees and did what she had done every day for four decades and had never once needed to do with her heart pounding: she listened to it.

Swordspeakers were taught to listen. Her mother — no. Not her mother. Her teacher, the woman whose face she could still summon if she worked at it, though the edges had gone soft as wet paper over the years — had knelt her down at eleven years old beside a forge and said, *A blade is a sentence someone finished writing. Your work is to know which words are yours.* Petra had been the last student. There had been no others after her, because there had been no other swordspeakers after her, because Oathscript was a dead language and dead languages do not have children.

Except one was sleeping in her house.

She watched his chest rise and fall. Barefoot even in sleep, feet black with road-dirt, clothes that had been fine once — she could see the ghost of embroidery at the collar, thread gone gray with wear — and no shadow. Even now, with the coals banked low and throwing long unsteady light across the floorboards, the light bent around him strangely. It pooled where he should have cast dark and simply... didn't. As if the darkness itself had decided he was owed nothing, or owed everything, or was too busy looking at what burned in him to bother with the ordinary business of shadows.

Because something did burn in him. She had seen it in the night, when he slept deepest. A glow under the skin of his chest, faint as embers raked over with ash, pulsing slow — slower than a heartbeat, she'd noticed, and that wrongness had kept her awake better than any fear could. A heart beats because it must. This glowed because it *wanted* to. There was a difference, and the difference frightened her more than the wardens ever had.

Petra looked down at the sword.

It was a good blade. It had been a great blade once, before the valley. Narrow, slightly curved, forged — so her teacher claimed — from ore pulled out of a mountain's root, quenched in water that had fallen as rain on ground no one owned. The steel held a color that wasn't quite steel's, a deep smoky blue at certain angles, like the sky an hour after sunset. Along the flat, just below the spine, ran a line of characters etched so fine you needed firelight and patience to read them. She had read them ten thousand times. She had read them aloud, once a year, on the anniversary of her oath, in a voice that grew rustier each season, until the words wore smooth as river stones and meant almost nothing.

They meant something now. They meant something *loudly*, because the etched characters were glowing — barely, a thread of gold-light along each stroke, like veins of ore catching sun — and they were glowing in rhythm with the pulse under the boy's ribs.

Petra set the blade down on the table, flat, and stepped back from it as if it had bitten her.

"All right," she said to the empty room, because talking to herself was one of the few habits the valley hadn't managed to grind out of her. "All right. Think."

Think. She was good at thinking. Thinking was what you did instead of feeling when feeling would kill you, and she had forty years of practice.

Facts. One: the boy spoke a language that woke the sword. Two: the sword had not woken in this valley for anyone, for anything, in forty years — not for lightning strikes, not for the year the river flooded and took the miller's barn, not for her own oath renewed annually at the stones. Three: the valley killed magic. That was its whole purpose, whatever the villagers believed about clean air and honest soil. Magic crossed the boundary and went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. So either the valley was failing —

— and she thought of the flicker at the ford yesterday, the half-second where the world had stuttered like a guttering lamp, and her stomach dropped —

— or the boy's magic was not crossing the boundary at all. It was coming from inside him. From whatever burned in his chest. Something the wards couldn't touch because it had never passed over the stones; it had walked over them wearing a child's skin.

And four: the language. She had to face the language.

*Sennavar.* She turned the word over in her mind the way she'd turn a coin found on a road, checking both faces. It was not a word from any tongue spoken within two hundred miles; she knew all of those, the valley dialect and the lowland trade-speech and the river cant the bargemen used. But it was shaped like a word she knew. The vowels sat in the same places. The terminal consonant fell the way Oathscript consonants fell — soft, but with weight behind them, like a door closing gently on a room you weren't allowed to enter.

Oathscript. The binding-tongue. The language that didn't describe things but *decided* them. Every other language in the world, her teacher had said, is a mirror — it shows you what's already there. Oathscript is a hand. It reaches out and changes what's there. That was why swordspeakers had been feared in the old kingdoms, why the order had been broken and scattered and finally forgotten: because a person who speaks and reality obeys is a person no throne can tolerate for long.

Her teacher had also said, on the last night Petra ever saw her, standing at the boundary stones with her hood up and the wind pulling at her words: *If you ever hear it spoken again — truly spoken, not recited — run toward it or run from it, but decide fast. Because it will only find you for one reason.*

"What reason?" Petra had asked, seventeen and certain the world was a solved problem.

"Because something needs unbinding," the woman had said, "or something needs binding worse."

Behind her, in the corner by the hearth, the boy stirred.

Petra went very still. He turned over, curled tighter around the ache or the dream or whatever children curl around, and murmured — two syllables, slurred with sleep, meaningless to any ear in any kingdom.

The sword rang.

Not hummed. *Rang.* A clear high note off the steel where it lay on the table, a note with harmonics in it, a note that Petra felt in her back teeth and in the scar tissue of her left palm, and the golden thread along the etched characters flared bright enough to throw light on the ceiling beams. The boy slept on. He didn't know what his own mouth did. That was somehow the worst part — that the power moved through him like weather through a valley, indifferent, enormous, unowned.

Petra crossed the room on legs that felt borrowed and picked up the sword.

It was warm. In forty years it had never been warm. Steel takes the temperature of its room; that was physics, that was the valley's whole theology, cold things staying cold. But the blade lay against her palm warm as a living thing, warm as a hand, and the vibration ran up her wrist and into the old bones of her forearm where the oath-scars sat, and the scars — thin white lines, seven of them, one for each clause of the vow she'd taken at nineteen — the scars *itched*. Deeply. Like skin remembering a wound that wanted to reopen and speak.

"No," she told the sword quietly. "No. You wait."

The ringing softened. Did not stop. Softened, the way a dog lies down but keeps watching the door.

She sat back down across from the boy and held the blade across her knees again and let herself feel it, because she had been holding it at arm's length all night and arm's length was no longer honest. Terror, yes. Terror was easy and familiar; terror was a coat she owned in every size. But underneath the terror, shameless and enormous, rising in her chest like water finding its level:

Longing.

Forty years. Forty years of drawing the sword exactly twice a year to oil it, of feeling nothing come up her arm but her own blood, of speaking the old words into air so dead they might as well have been spoken into a grave. Forty years of being the last speaker of a language, which is a loneliness with no name in any living tongue — lonelier than widowhood, lonelier than exile, because exile at least implies somewhere else exists. She had grieved the craft the way you grieve a parent: slowly, in installments, telling yourself each year that this was the final payment. And now here was a barefoot child breathing in her house whose *sleep-talk* made the steel sing, and the longing that rose in her was so violent it was indistinguishable from grief, and she understood with terrible clarity that she would follow this boy anywhere. Off the edge of the valley. Past the stones. Into whatever waited outside the silence. She would follow the sound of her own sword like a hound follows a whistle, and everyone who had ever warned her about oaths and about longing had been right to warn her, and she did not care.

That was the terror. Not the boy. Herself.

* * *

He woke near midmorning, the way animals wake — all at once, eyes open, no drift. He looked at her. She looked at him. Neither of them moved for a long moment, and the fire ticked, and the sword hummed its low patient note between them like a third presence in the room.

"You're hungry," Petra said at last, in valley-speech, and got up to put bread and dripping on the board, because fear was no reason to be a bad host and hunger was no reason to trust anyone.

He ate the way he did everything: without hesitation, without waste, watching her the whole time. When he finished he set the crust down precisely at the board's edge, aligned with it, and something about the precision snagged her attention. Children weren't precise. Precision was trained. Someone had trained this boy in something, recently enough that his body still remembered.

"Edmund," she said. "Is that your name? Edmund?"

He nodded.

"And can you tell me where you came from?"

He shook his head — then paused, and shook it differently, a smaller motion, and she realized the second shake didn't mean *no*. It meant *I cannot*. There was a difference, and he was careful about the difference, and her skin prickled.

"Can you write?"

Another head-shake. Another *cannot*.

"But you can speak." She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice she used on spooked livestock. "You spoke last night. At the door. And in your sleep." She turned her palms up, open, asking. "What language is that, Edmund?"

He looked at her for a long time. His eyes were dark and very old in a young face, the way wells look old, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that the question was being weighed against something she couldn't see — some scale, some ledger, some judgment older than the valley.

Then he pointed.

At the sword.

Petra's breath went out of her slowly. "You know what this is."

He nodded. Then he climbed down from his chair, crossed the floor — bare feet silent, shadowless, the firelight pooling wrong around him — and stopped in front of her, close enough that she could feel heat coming off him that had nothing to do with the hearth. He looked up at her. And he raised one small hand and touched the flat of the blade, just above the etched characters, with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder to wake them gently.

And he said a word.

Not *sennavar*. A different word — shorter, lower, a word that seemed to arrive in the room already complete, as if it had existed before he spoke it and he had merely permitted it passage. Petra's ears told her it was one syllable. Everything else in her told her it was a sentence, a paragraph, a contract. She heard it with her scars. She heard it with the place behind her sternum where her oath lived.

The sword drew itself.

One inch. The blade slid from the sheath-wrapping with a whisper of oiled cloth and a note like a struck glass, one inch of that impossible sunset-blue steel bared to the air of a valley that had killed magic for forty years, and along the exposed edge the metal wept light — actual light, thin and golden, running the cutting edge like dew runs a blade of grass at dawn.

Petra did not move. Later she would be proud of that. In the moment she was aware only that her entire body had turned to something cold and attentive, that her heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt, and that the inch of naked blade reflected her face back at her warped and shining, and in the reflection her mouth was open and her eyes were wide and she looked, for the first time in forty years, like a swordspeaker.

The word hung in the air. Words like that didn't dissipate; they *settled*, like ash, like snow. She could feel it lying on the room, on the table, on her hands.

"That," she said, and her voice cracked and she started again. "That was Oathscript."

Edmund tilted his head. He didn't confirm it. He didn't need to. Confirmation was for people who could doubt.

"Where did you learn it?" she whispered. "Who taught you? Who *are* you?"

He withdrew his fingers from the blade. The sword stayed drawn its one inch, humming, waiting — and she understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like falling, that it was waiting for *her*. For her voice. For the rest of the sentence. The boy had opened the door, and the door would stay open until someone walked through it or closed it, and the only person alive who knew how to close it was sitting in the chair with her hands shaking.

*Run toward it or run from it,* her teacher had said. *Decide fast.*

Outside, far off down the valley, a bell began to ring. Not the chapel bell — the ford bell, the one the village rang when strangers crossed the water. One ring, pause, one ring. The signal for wardens.

Edmund looked toward the sound. Then he looked back at her, and for the first time since he'd arrived, the oldness in his eyes cracked, and underneath it was just a child, a barefoot boy in a stranger's kitchen, and his expression asked the question plain as speech:

*Will you protect me?*

Petra looked at the inch of singing steel. She looked at the door. She reached out with a hand that was not steady at all, and closed her fingers around the grip of the sword her oath had bound her to keep and never draw.

"Hold on to me," she said, "and don't say another word until I tell you."

The blade slid home. The bell rang on.
