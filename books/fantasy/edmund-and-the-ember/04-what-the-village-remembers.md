## 4. What the Village Remembers

Maren Cole had ground flour through forty autumns, and she knew the sound of a village deciding something before anyone said it aloud. It was in the way the millrace ran that morning—too quiet, as if the water itself were listening. It was in the way Tam Osgood's cart slowed outside her door without stopping, and the way he looked at her window and then away. It was in the smell of the meeting-house being swept early, birch smoke and beeswax, the scent of men preparing to be serious.

She set her shawl over her shoulders and did not go to grind anything at all.

* * *

Petra heard them coming up the lane an hour after dawn: six sets of boots on frost-stiffened mud, walking with the particular care of people who had rehearsed their footsteps. She was already awake. She had been awake most of the night, sitting across from the boy who slept by her hearth with his hands curled under his chin like a much younger child, his chest rising and falling with a faint ember-light beneath his shirt that she could still see when she closed her eyes.

Edmund woke when the boots stopped. He didn't startle—that was the thing about him that never stopped unsettling her. He simply opened his eyes, the way a cat does when it has been listening all along.

"They've come about you," Petra said quietly. "Not to take you. Not yet. To talk."

He looked at her with those gray eyes and touched two fingers to his own chest, then to hers. Asking, or promising. She still couldn't tell which language his gestures spoke.

"Stay behind me," she said. "Say nothing. Especially nothing that makes my sword do what it did last night."

The sword hung on its pegs above the mantel, and even now, unspoken-to, it gave off a warmth like a sleeping animal. In thirty-one years it had never done that. In thirty-one years she had polished it every seventh day and told herself that duty to a blade she could not draw was a kind of faith, and she had almost believed it.

The knock came. Three blows, spaced evenly. Aldous Crane's knock—the headman knocked the way he signed things, precisely, expecting compliance.

Petra opened the door.

Six of them stood in the grey light: Aldous Crane, the brothers Vetch, old Sorrel Hake who kept the boundary stones, the cooper's widow Bess, and young Wren Osgood who had no business being there except that his father had sent him to watch and report. Behind them, at a distance that let them pretend otherwise, half the village had gathered along the lane.

"Petra." Aldous took off his hat, which was his version of courtesy and also his version of a preamble. "We'd have words inside, if you'll allow it."

"You're having them whether I allow it or not," Petra said. "Come in. Wipe your feet."

They filed into her single room and arranged themselves the way villagers always did in her house—near the door, none of them touching anything, as though the room might remember them. Her eyes went to the sword on its pegs and she saw Aldous's eyes follow, and saw him look quickly away from it, the way everyone looked away from it, the way they had for three decades.

Sorrel Hake cleared his throat. He was the oldest of them, keeper of the boundary stones, and it was understood that when the stones needed speaking of, Sorrel did the speaking.

"The boy," Sorrel said. "The one that came out of the storm."

"What of him?"

"He shouldn't be here, Petra. You know it same as we do. The valley doesn't hold such as him. The valley doesn't *allow* such as him." Sorrel's voice was kind and immovable, the voice of a man who had walked the perimeter in all weathers for fifty years and believed in the stones the way other men believed in scripture. "There's wardens at the border ford come spring, regular as taxes. The proper thing is to hand him over. Cleanly. Before the valley takes notice."

"The valley has taken notice," Bess said softly, and would not meet anyone's eyes. "My hens haven't laid since he came. The well water tastes of iron."

"Hens stop laying in autumn, Bess," Maren said from the doorway.

Everyone turned. She hadn't knocked; she had simply arrived, which was a liberty only the very old and the very stubborn were permitted. She came in and stood beside Petra's hearth, deliberately between the boy and the delegation, and folded her hands.

"Maren," Aldous said carefully, "this is council business."

"This is village business, and I'm village." She nodded at Edmund, who watched the whole proceeding with the stillness of something carved. "That child walked through the worst storm in nine years barefoot, and didn't so much as shiver. And you want to hand him to the wardens like a misdelivered parcel."

"We want the valley to stay *quiet*, Maren." Aldous's voice roughened. "You of all people know what happens when this valley stops being quiet. There are folk in this room older than the forgetting. My grandmother remembered the year the noise started coming back over the stones—just a little, just birdsong where no bird was—and eleven people sickened in a week. Eleven. The stones held, but not before—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "We keep the silence. That's the whole law here. That's the only law."

"And the law says give the child to the wardens," Vetch said. His brother nodded along, twin hinges of one opinion.

"It says no such thing," Maren said. "There's no law written anywhere in this valley. You know that too, Aldous. There's just habit wearing law's coat."

* * *

Maren had not meant to say any of what she said next. Forty years of keeping her mouth shut had made a deep groove, and she felt herself sliding into it even as she opened her mouth against it. But the boy was looking at her—not at Petra, at *her*—with those grey eyes that seemed to hold a question older than he was, and something in her chest cracked along a line she had thought long healed over.

"You want to remember what remembering costs?" she said to the room. "Then let's remember properly, since we're doing it. Let's remember how Petra got here."

Silence dropped over the room like a stone into a well.

"Thirty-one years ago," Maren said, "in the month the apples came in green because summer left early. A woman came down out of the pass, bleeding through her bandages, carrying a baby wrapped in a swordsman's cloak. She didn't come to the meeting-house. She didn't ask permission. She walked to the boundary stone at the western edge and put her hand flat on it, and the stone sang—one note, low, like a struck bell underwater—and then she fell down."

"That isn't how—" Sorrel began.

"That is exactly how," Maren said. "I was there. I was twenty-six years old and I'd gone out to check the sluice gates in the rain, and I found her, and I brought her to the mill because nobody else would touch her. Do you remember that? Nobody else would touch her. They wanted to carry her back over the line and leave her for whatever was chasing her. It was Old Marta Voss—your grandmother, Aldous—who finally said *let her stay, the stone let her stay, and the stone knows better than we do.*"

Aldous's face had gone the color of ash. "My grandmother never spoke of it."

"No. She didn't. None of us did. That was part of the price, maybe, or part of the habit. But I remember the woman dying in my bed over three days, and I remember what she made me promise." Maren's voice stayed level, but Petra saw her knuckles whiten where her hands were folded. "She said the child must be raised in the quiet, and never taught, and never asked. She said if the child ever drew the sword—if ever the valley heard it sing—we should be afraid. Not of the child. Of what comes for the child."

Petra felt the floor tilt gently beneath her, the way it did when a truth she had built her life around showed a hairline crack. "You knew," she said. "All these years. You knew why I was left here."

"I knew a piece," Maren said. "A woman's dying words are a poor map, Petra. Half of it was names I didn't recognize and the other half was warnings I didn't understand. She said your name once—your true name—and then she said I must never say it, and she made me swear on my husband's grave, and I have kept that oath for thirty-one years while watching you polish a sword you can't draw and thinking: *someone owes this girl an explanation and everyone who could give it is dead.*"

"Why tell it now?"

"Because now there's a second child at a doorstep," Maren said, "and the village is lining up to do to him what it nearly did to you. And I am done letting habit wear law's coat."

* * *

In the silence that followed, Edmund moved.

It was small—he stepped half a pace forward, out from behind Petra's shadow—but every man in the room flinched as if he had drawn a weapon. And perhaps he had. Because the sword above the mantel gave a single soft note, barely audible, a tuning-fork hum that Petra felt in her teeth, and Sorrel Hake made a small involuntary sound and stepped backward into the doorframe.

"Did anyone else hear that?" Bess whispered.

Nobody answered. Nobody needed to. The note faded, and the ordinary sounds of morning crept back in—the millrace, a crow, the creak of frost releasing a fencepost—and the six of them stood in Petra's house looking at a mute child and a silent sword and the shape of everything they had agreed not to know.

"The wardens," Aldous said at last, and his voice had lost its certainty, which somehow made it worse. "They come at the spring ford-crossing. If the boy is still here—"

"If the boy is still here," Maren said, "then this village will decide what it is. Not the wardens. Not the stones. Us." She turned for the door, then paused. "One more piece, since we're spending memory freely today. That night, thirty-one years ago, after the mother died—before we buried her above the tide-line where nothing grows—I went through her cloak pockets. There was a letter. Sealed. I couldn't read it and I didn't dare show it round, so I did the coward's thing and hid it."

"Where?" Petra's voice came out strange, thin.

"Inside the millstone housing. North stone. Where I tell myself every harvest I'll look again, and every harvest I don't, because some doors are easier shut than open." Maren pulled her shawl tight. "But the boy's got a fire in him, by all accounts, and fires have a way of finding dry paper. So think on it, all of you. And think on this too: the wardens don't come till spring. Whatever we decide, we decide as neighbors first."

She left. One by one, without meeting each other's eyes, the others followed, Aldous last, pausing on the threshold with a face full of words that had nowhere lawful to go.

"Petra," he said finally. "Whatever that boy is. Whatever *you* are. This village held you thirty-one years. Remember that holds too."

Then he was gone, and the lane emptied, and the frost began to sweat in the strengthening light.

* * *

Petra stood a long time in her own kitchen. Edmund tugged her sleeve and pointed—first at her, then at the north road, where the mill sat with its wheel turning slow and brown in the shallows.

"No," she said. Then, honestly: "I don't know."

Her oath was a simple thing, worn smooth as river glass from thirty years of handling: *keep what is given into your keeping; carry it home; ask nothing of the road.* Her mother's voice had spoken it over her cradle, they told her—or rather, the dying woman had spoken it over the bundle, and Maren had carried the words to whoever needed carrying until Petra was old enough to carry them herself. An oath-bound swordspeaker with a sword that would not wake, guarding a valley that would not speak, waiting for a charge that never came.

Until a knock at dusk. Until now.

She looked at the boy, and the boy looked back, and beneath his shirt the faint coal-glow pulsed once, twice, patient as a heartbeat that wasn't entirely his.

"There's a letter," she told him, though he could not answer and perhaps could not understand. "In the mill. Thirty-one years old. From a dead woman who knew my true name." She laughed, short and joyless. "And I find that I'm afraid of a piece of paper more than I was of the storm."

Edmund reached up and pressed his small warm hand flat against her sternum—over the place where, these past two days, she sometimes felt a faint answering heat, as though his ember recognized something banked and buried in her own chest. Then he pointed at her, and then, deliberately, at the sword on its pegs.

And the sword hummed.

Not a whisper this time. A clear, low, sustained tone, the sound a bell makes deciding to ring, and both of them stood absolutely still while it filled the little house, while frost slid from the window in a sheet, while down in the lane a dog began to bark and did not stop.

Far off, beyond the boundary stones, beyond the quiet, something answered.

Petra did not know what it was. But she knew the direction. And she knew that spring was four months away, and that whatever had answered was not going to wait for the wardens' ford-crossing schedule.

"Get your cloak," she said to Edmund. "We're going to the mill."
