## 5. The Ember in His Chest

The storm had spent itself by midnight, and the valley lay washed and silent under a sky scoured clean of cloud. Petra could not sleep. She had not truly slept since the boy arrived—three nights now of shallow dozing broken by the sound of his breathing from the pallet by her hearth, a breathing too even for a child, too deliberate, as though he practiced it.

She rose without lighting a lamp. Her cottage needed no light to be walked; she had lived inside its walls long enough to know every board that creaked, every place where the stone floor dipped cold against bare feet. She crossed the single room and stood over the boy the way she had stood over her sword that morning, and every morning for thirty years: with reverence she did not feel and duty she could not set down.

Edmund slept on his side, knees drawn up, one hand curled beneath his chin. In sleep his face lost its uncanny stillness and became merely a child's face—the soft jaw, the chapped lips, the lashes clumped from the storm's wet. He was thin. Thinner than any child of the valley villages, where even the poorest households put meat in the pot at least once a week. His wrists were small enough that Petra's thumb and forefinger could have circled them, and she found herself thinking, with an unease that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone, *someone let him get this hungry. Someone looked at him every day and let him grow thin.*

Then she saw the light.

It came from beneath his nightshirt first—a glow at the collarbone, warm as the last coals raked together before dawn, when the fire is not dead but only sleeping. It pulsed. Slowly. Not the flicker of flame but something more like breath, or like a heartbeat heard through a wall: there, then gone, then there again, patient as tide.

Petra knelt.

She told herself she was checking whether the child burned with fever. She told herself many things in those seconds, and all of them were lies, and some part of her knew it, because her hands were not the hands of someone checking a fever. They were the hands of a swordspeaker approaching a thing of power—and they shook.

*Three years,* she thought. *Three years since the sword hummed. Three years since anything in this valley answered me.* The valley ate magic the way sand ate water. Whatever came in carrying power left drained, dimmed, ordinary. That was its purpose. She had understood that much, always, without ever letting the understanding take shape. The valley forgot magic so that she could forget hers. So that she could live.

And here, in the deepest hour of a storm-washed night, a mute child glowed like a hearth banked for winter.

She drew back the blanket—not the nightshirt, not yet; she was not a monster—and the light strengthened, bleeding up through the coarse linen until his whole chest was visible to her in the dark, a lantern behind flesh. And within the glow, moving like shapes seen through smoked glass, were patterns. Lines. Curves. Characters.

Oathscript.

Her mouth went dry. She knew those characters the way she knew the scar on her palm—not from study but from inheritance, from a childhood spent watching her mother trace them in spilled flour, in steam on windows, in the dirt with a stick, always quickly, always looking over her shoulder after, as though the marks themselves might summon something. Her mother had never explained them. Her mother had died before explaining them, and Petra had spent thirty years telling herself the marks meant nothing, were a game, a superstition, a grief-shaped habit she'd outgrown.

They were not a game. They were written across a child's chest in living light, and they moved, and they were *fading*—she could see that now, see how the outermost characters had gone gray as old ash while the ones near the center still burned amber-bright. Something in him was being used up. Or running out. Or—

Edmund stirred. His eyes opened.

He did not startle. He did not cry out or scramble away or do any of the things a wakened child does when finding a stranger crouched over him in the dark. He simply looked at her, calm as a well, and then—he did the last thing she expected.

He took her hand.

His fingers were cold and small and certain. He drew her palm toward his chest, toward the glow, and pressed it flat against the linen over his sternum, and held it there, and looked into her eyes with an expression she would spend the rest of her life trying to name. It was not fear. It was not trust, exactly. It was the look of someone handing over a debt they can no longer carry alone.

*No,* she thought. *Don't. I don't want—*

She touched him.

* * *

The world went white, then warm, then somewhere else entirely.

She stood—or seemed to stand—in a kitchen that was not her kitchen. A kitchen of low beams and copper pots and a window looking out on terraced hillsides stitched with olive trees. Evening light came through that window, gold and thick as honey. Bread cooled on a board. Somewhere a child laughed.

And a voice said, in the dialect of her mother's village—the dialect Petra had not heard spoken aloud in thirty years, the dialect that existed now nowhere in the world except in her own memory and in the mouths of the dead:

*"Please."*

Petra tried to turn toward the voice and could not. She had no body here. She was only listening, only warmth, only attention.

*"Please, whoever holds me. Please, I am so tired. I have been carried so far and I am so tired, and the dark keeps coming closer, and I don't know whose hands these are. Are they kind hands? Please let them be kind hands. My mother sold me and I do not blame her, I want that known—I do not blame her, the winter was too long and my brothers were starving and the broker paid in grain that lasted until spring. But I don't want to go to the King. Everyone below says the King eats the light, and I am only a little light, I am not enough for a king, I will be gone in a moment and no one will remember—"*

The voice broke. When it resumed it was smaller.

*"I had a name. I know I had a name. I can almost—there was a garden, there was a brother, there was bread like this bread cooling on this board, and my name was—"*

Silence. A long silence, in which Petra felt the presence straining after something just beyond its reach, the way a sleeper strains after a dream already slipping.

*"My name was,"* the voice whispered, *"my name was—"*

And then Edmund's hand tightened over hers, and the vision folded shut like a book, and Petra was kneeling on her own cold floor with her palm pressed to a child's sternum and tears running down her face into her mouth, salt and hot, and she could not remember standing up, could not remember crossing the room, could not remember anything between the last sight of her own ceiling and this.

Edmund watched her cry with grave, unblinking eyes. Then he reached up—slowly, giving her time to pull away—and touched her cheek, one fingertip, catching a tear. He looked at the wetness on his finger with something like surprise, as though tears were a language he had forgotten how to speak and was relearning letter by letter.

"No," Petra said hoarsely. "No, no, no."

She pulled her hand back and pressed it against her own chest, against the ache there, as though she could hold the door shut that had just been opened. Because she knew what she had heard. She knew what slept in the boy's chest, glowing and guttering. She had known since the syllables left his mouth on the first night and her sword hummed like a struck bell; she had known and refused the knowing, the way you refuse a diagnosis, the way you refuse a letter with a familiar seal.

A soul. A sold soul. Carried in a child's body like a coal carried in cupped hands.

And her oath—

Her oath.

She rose on unsteady legs and went to the sword above the mantel. Thirty years it had hung there, and in all that time she had never drawn it, had never been able to draw it, had polished it each anniversary of her oath with oil and a soft cloth and spoken the words of maintenance her mentor had taught her, words that were ritual and nothing more, dead letters, a liturgy for a god who had left the country. The oath she had sworn at nineteen, kneeling on this same floor, her hand on the flat of that blade, her voice cracking on the high vowels:

*I am the keeper of what is sold. What is taken wrongly, I carry home. What burns unbought, I guard until it gutters or is free. Ask no other price of me. Ask nothing else.*

She had thought, at nineteen, that the oath was ornamental. That it was the sort of vow a swordspeaker swore the way a knight swore fealty to a king who would never call upon him—honorable, archaic, safely abstract. *What is sold* had sounded to her young ears like a legalism, a category with no members. Who sold souls? No one she would ever meet. Where would she carry them? Nowhere she would ever go.

The valley had made sure of it. The valley, magic-dead, oath-silent, had been the safest place in the world to swear an oath she believed would never come due.

And now a barefoot child stood in her house with another person's soul burning behind his ribs, fading like a lamp running dry, begging in her mother's dialect for kind hands—and the oath rose in her memory word by word, complete, undimmed, as though it had been waiting thirty years in the dark of her like a seed waiting for rain.

*I am the keeper of what is sold.*

"Who are you?" she asked Edmund, and her voice came out strange, half whisper, half command. "Who gave you this? Whose soul do you carry?"

He tilted his head. He opened his mouth—that beautiful, terrible mouth that spoke the oldest language—and what came out was not Oathscript this time. It was a sound like wind through a keyhole, like a word trying to be two words at once, and the sword above the mantel rang softly, one clear note, and the banked coals under his shirt flared bright enough to throw his shadow huge and wavering across the rafters.

He pointed at the sword. Then at his own chest. Then, with terrible simplicity, at her.

*You,* the gesture said. *This. Me.*

"I know," Petra said. "I know what you're saying. Stop. Stop it."

But he didn't stop, because he hadn't been saying anything cruel—he had only been answering her question, the way children answer questions, plainly, without mercy. And the plainness of it broke something loose in her that she had kept mortared in place for three decades.

She slid down the wall until she was sitting on the cold floor, her back against the stones beneath the mantel, the sword hanging above her head like a judgment. Edmund slid down beside her. He arranged himself carefully, a body-length away, close enough for warmth and far enough for dignity, and pulled the blanket around his shoulders, and waited. He was very good at waiting. He had the patience of someone who had learned early that the world moves at its own speed and shouting changes nothing.

"The voice," Petra said, staring at the dead hearth. "In there. It spoke in my mother's tongue. Do you understand what that means? There are maybe four people alive who speak that dialect. Four. It's a valley dialect—it never traveled. Whoever that soul belonged to came from my mother's country. From the hills above Vell, probably, or the terraces past it. A girl. A girl who liked gardens and bread and had a brother, and whose parents sold her to pay for winter." Her voice cracked. "Sold her *whole*. Not her labor. Not her years. Her. Everything she was, scooped out and carried off in a jar or a lantern or—"

Or a child's chest. Swallowed, the way you swallow a stolen coin to keep it from the thieves.

She looked at Edmund sharply. "Did you steal it? Is that why they hunt you? Did you take her out of wherever they keep such things?"

Edmund's face did something complicated—grief and pride fighting for the same ground—and he pressed both hands flat over his heart, over the glow, and nodded once, firmly, the nod of a soldier confirming a position held.

"You stole a soul," Petra said slowly, "to keep it from the court beneath the mountain."

Another nod. Then he frowned, and tapped his chest twice, and shook his head, and pointed at her, and made a walking motion with two fingers across his palm—*carry*—and pointed down, toward the south, toward the mountains at the valley's end, the direction no one went, the direction the wardens watched.

*Carry her home.*

"I can't," Petra said.

The words came out fast, too fast, the words of a person arguing with a door already open. "I can't. You don't understand what you're asking. I'm not—I haven't been a swordspeaker in thirty years. I'm a woman who oils a sword she can't draw and grows beans and is left alone, and that's all I am, that's all I've *made* myself, and the valley takes whatever power comes into it anyway, it would eat her faster out here, it would—"

Edmund was watching her with those well-calm eyes, and she heard herself, heard the pitch of her own voice climbing, and stopped.

Because the oath was speaking in her, quietly, in the space beneath the argument. It had never stopped speaking, she realized. All these years she had thought it dormant, a contract expired, a law repealed by distance and silence. But oaths sworn in Oathscript did not expire. They waited. They were patient as the characters now fading on a child's chest. *What is taken wrongly, I carry home.*

The soul had been taken wrongly. That was not in question. A girl sold whole to a hungry king was as wrong as wrong got.

*Ask no other price of me. Ask nothing else.*

There it was. The clause she had loved at nineteen, the clause that had made the whole vow bearable: *nothing else.* Not her life. Not her love. Not her name. Just the carrying. Just the walking, the enduring, the putting of one foot before the other in the direction of a wrong until the wrong was righted. She could do that. She had done harder things—she had buried her mother, she had buried her mentor, she had buried her own gift in the flattest soil in the world and tended its grave for thirty years.

But the other clause. *Until it gutters or is free.* The voice in the boy's chest was guttering. She had heard it strain for its own name and fail to find it. Every day it burned, it burned *down*. Souls were not wood; they did not replenish. Whatever fed that light was finite, and the feeding had been going on a long time—longer, perhaps, than the child had even carried it.

Which meant the clock was already running. Which meant the choice had been made for her, three nights ago, by a knock at dusk.

"I swore an oath," Petra said aloud, to the hearth, to the dark, to the girl-voice that might or might not still be listening. "When I was young and stupid, I swore that if anyone ever brought me a stolen soul, I would carry it home. I thought no one ever would." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I built my whole life inside that *thought*, do you understand? I chose the one valley in the world where nothing magical could reach me. I chose it because I wanted the oath to stay a story. And the world walked around every wall I built and put you on my doorstep."

Edmund reached over and, with great solemnity, took the corner of his blanket and laid it across her knees. Sharing warmth. It was such a small gesture, such an absurdly small kindness, that Petra's throat closed entirely.

She sat there a long time, feeling the weight of wool that wasn't hers, watching the faint pulse of light rise and fall beneath the boy's nightshirt. Banked coals. A fire someone had raked together and covered against the night, trusting that someone else would uncover it in the morning and feed it.

*Kind hands,* the voice had begged. *Please let them be kind hands.*

"All right," Petra said finally. Her voice was quiet and it did not shake, and she was grateful to it for that. "All right. Here is what happens now. Tomorrow we talk terms—you and I, and however much of her can hear me. Tonight, you sleep, and I sit up, because I don't trust the dark anymore and I don't suppose you do either." She pushed herself up off the floor; her knees protested; thirty years of bean-farming had not prepared them for destiny. "And tomorrow after that, I start asking questions I should have asked thirty years ago. About the mountain. About the court. About what exactly my mentor knew when she left me here and never came back."

Edmund looked up at her from inside the blanket, a small bright-eyed shape in the gloom, and something passed across his face that she would learn, in the weeks to come, to recognize as his smile: not a curving of the mouth but a settling, a loosening, the look of a held breath released.

"And Edmund," she added, as he turned toward the pallet. He paused. "The girl in your chest. When she finds her name again—if she finds it—I'll help her say it. That's not the oath talking. That's me."

The coals beneath his shirt flared, brief and glad, like a heartbeat skipping with joy, and in the ringing silence after, the sword above the mantel hummed—one low note, sustained, attentive, awake.

Outside, far off at the valley's southern edge, where the boundary stones stood pale under the clearing sky, something answered the note. Petra could not have said what—a pressure, a shiver in the air, a sense of vast attention turning toward her small lit window like an eye opening.

The valley had noticed the ember. And the valley, Petra understood with a dread that settled over her like snowfall, gentle and total, would not be the only thing to notice for long.
