## 6. Terms of the Sale

Sleep in Petra's house is not like sleep anywhere else.

Edmund knows this because he has slept in many places. He has slept in the hollow of a fallen oak where the moss was warm as a hen's breast. He has slept under a bridge with water talking underneath him all night, saying the same word over and over, a word he almost understood. He has slept in the cart on the way to the mountain, between his mother and his father, with Liss's small cold hand in his and everyone pretending she was only tired.

But here, in the swordspeaker's house, sleep comes like a tide that pulls him backward through his own life. He does not dream forward into made-up things. He dreams *back*. The ember in his chest likes it here. It hums against his ribs like a cat settling in, and it opens doors he would rather keep shut.

Tonight it opens the door to the kitchen at home.

* * *

The kitchen smells of barley and smoke and the particular sourness of fear, which Edmund did not know had a smell until that winter, and now cannot forget. His mother stands at the hearth with her back to the room, stirring a pot that has nothing in it but water, because stirring is what her hands do when her mind is somewhere terrible. His father sits at the table with his cap in his fists, turning it, turning it, turning it, so that the brim has gone soft as cloth.

Liss is asleep in the box bed in the corner. She is nine years old and she sleeps like the dead sleep, which is not a thing a brother should have to learn about his sister, but he learned it that autumn, watching her chest rise so shallowly that twice he leaned close to check.

There is a knock at the door.

Everyone in the kitchen stops. Even the pot stops, or seems to. His mother's spoon hangs above the water. His father's hands go still around the ruined cap.

The knock comes again, three times, patient, the way you knock when you know the door will open eventually because it always has before.

His father gets up. His legs are wrong; they move too fast for how afraid he looks, like a puppet worked by someone else's string. He opens the door and the night leans in, and standing in the night is a man in a hood the color of wet ash, and the man is smiling, and Edmund thinks, even then, even at seven years old: *that smile is borrowed. It doesn't fit his face.*

"Good evening," says the hooded man, in a voice like paper being folded. "I believe we have an appointment."

"We don't," says Edmund's father. "We never—there's no appointment."

"There is always an appointment." The broker steps inside without being asked, and the fire dims, just slightly, the way a dog lowers its ears. "That is what a debt is. An appointment that keeps itself."

Edmund's mother turns from the hearth. Her face is doing something complicated, trying to be brave and pleading and furious all at once, and none of them fit, so her face just trembles between them like a candle flame in a draft.

"The harvest was bad," she says. "You know the harvest was bad. Everyone knows—"

"I know everything that is known," the broker says gently. "It is my trade. I do not deal in grain, goodwife. Grain rots. I deal in things that keep."

His eyes find the box bed in the corner. They are gray eyes, the exact color of ash, and they do not blink often enough.

* * *

Here is what Edmund remembers of the table, though he wishes the ember would let him forget:

The contract is not written on paper. Paper can burn, and burning is a kind of escape, and the court beneath the mountain permits no escapes. The contract is written on a sheet of something thin and gray and faintly warm, like skin left out in frost, and the words on it are not ink. They are pressed into the surface, grooves, little valleys of nothing, and when Edmund looks at them too long the grooves seem to deepen, as if the words are slowly sinking into whatever the surface used to be.

The pen is a sliver of char. It writes in ash. When his father signs, the letters appear first as gray smears and then darken, and Edmund hears, very faintly, from somewhere below the floor, below the earth, a sound like a great slow breath being drawn in.

"What happens to her?" his mother asks. Her voice is flat now. All the trembling has been spent. This is worse than the trembling. "Where does she go? What do you *do* with—"

"She will be kept," says the broker. "Kept is a beautiful word. Think of jam. Think of a jewel in velvet. Your daughter will be kept in the King's own light, and she will hold back the dark, and the dark is very large, goodwife, and very patient, and if it were ever to arrive unopposed—" he spreads his gray hands, and the gesture contains winters, famines, the end of every small warm kitchen in the world—"well. You have bought more than you know tonight."

"We haven't bought anything," Edmund's father whispers. "We've *sold*—"

"You have sold one small light," the broker agrees, "and purchased the continued existence of every other light you love. That is the terms of the sale. That is always the terms of the sale. No one reads past the second line, and the second line is the whole of it."

And there — this is the part that makes Edmund's chest ache even now, makes the ember stir and press warm against his sternum like a hand — there is a moment when his mother looks at the box bed, and her face finally finds the expression it has been searching for, and it is neither brave nor pleading nor furious. It is *empty*. And Edmund understands, in the way children understand enormous things sideways and all at once, that his parents are going to do it. That the appointment has kept itself. That Liss is going to be *kept*.

He is seven years old. He is small for seven. But he has already learned the most important lesson of his life, which is that grown-ups will sign anything if they are frightened enough, and that therefore someone who is not frightened must act.

* * *

The ember came to their village three nights before the broker did.

It fell, or descended, or arrived — Edmund has never settled on the word — during the storm that took the roof off the tithe barn. It came down through the rain like a coal dropped from a black sky, trailing sparks, and it landed in the turnip field, and it lay there glowing through the mud, and everyone stayed far away from it because everyone could feel it *watching*.

Except Edmund. Edmund was seven and small and not frightened, or rather he was frightened of all the ordinary things — the dark under the bed, the sound of his parents arguing in low voices about money they didn't have — and the ember was not ordinary, and so it did not frighten him. He walked out into the churned field in his nightshirt and stood over it, and it glowed up at him through the mud like an eye, and it said — not in words, not in any language, but in the way warmth says things —

*I am running.*

And Edmund, who could not speak — who had never spoken, whose mouth made shapes that produced nothing but air — Edmund knelt in the freezing mud and cupped the ember in both hands, and it did not burn him, and he understood that it had been looking for someone who would carry it.

Three nights later the broker knocked. Three nights later his parents signed. And Edmund, lying awake in the loft, listening to the scratch of char on that gray warm surface, understood that the ember in the field and the soul on the contract were the same thing, or nearly the same thing, or enough the same thing that it mattered.

They were going to sell Liss. And something bright and fleeing had landed in their turnip field asking to be carried.

A child's arithmetic is simple and terrible. Edmund did sums with it all night.

* * *

The morning the King's men came for Liss, there was frost on the inside of the windows.

Edmund remembers the frost because he drew on it — a sun, a crooked house, two stick figures holding hands — and then wiped it away with his sleeve, because he did not want the men to see drawings. He did not want the men to see anything of theirs. He already hated them with a hatred so clean and total that it felt less like a feeling than like a bone, something structural, something he would have forever.

They wore ash-colored cloaks and moved like the broker moved, too smoothly, faces like faces seen through water. There were four of them. One carried a lantern that gave no light, only a kind of hungry brightness, a lantern that *wanted*, and Edmund looked at it and knew — again, sideways, all at once — that it was a cage. That the no-light inside it was other children, or pieces of other children, or the parts of people that laugh.

His mother held Liss by the shoulders. Liss was not crying. Liss was nine and brave in the ordinary way, the way that breaks your heart afterward, and she kept looking at Edmund with her eyebrows raised, asking him silently, *is this real? tell me this isn't real,* and Edmund could not answer her because he had never answered anyone anything in his life.

The men took her arms. Gently. That was the worst of it, how gently. As if she were a lamp being carried carefully to another room.

And Edmund did the sum one last time, fast, the way you swallow bread when someone might take it.

If the ember was still in the field, they would find it. If they found it, they would take it too, and Liss would be gone and the bright thing would be gone, and the world would be exactly as gray as the men wanted it. But if the ember were *not* in the field — if it were somewhere no ash-cloak could reach, somewhere inside, somewhere swallowed—

He was seven. He was small. His mouth did not work. None of that mattered.

He ran. Out the door, past the men, who did not stop him because children run all the time and mean nothing, past the frost-killed garden, into the turnip field where the mud had frozen in ridges like the knuckles of a buried giant. The ember lay where he had left it, dimmed now, guttering, three days of rain and neglect having starved it down to a red coin of itself.

*Running,* it had said. *I am running.*

Edmund picked it up. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Behind him he heard his mother scream his name — she had understood, mothers always understand one heartbeat too late — and he heard the ash-cloaks turn, and he heard the hungry lantern rustle like a page being turned toward him, and he put the ember in his mouth.

It tasted like the last day of summer. It tasted like his sister's laugh. It went down like a swallowed star, and it lit him from the inside, and for one white instant he could see the bones of his own hands through his own skin, and then—

Then the men were around him, and their hands passed over his chest, and found nothing. Because the ember was not in the field anymore, and it was not in a lantern, and it was not anywhere their ledgers said a soul should be. It was behind a wall of boy. It was hidden in the one place the court had never thought to look, which is to say: inside someone who loved it.

"He ate it," one of the men said, and his voice was puzzled, genuinely puzzled, as if Edmund had committed a crime against grammar. "The little fool *ate* it."

"No," said the broker, who had come quietly up the path and now stood regarding Edmund with those gray, insufficiently blinking eyes. Something moved behind his face — not respect, nothing so warm, but recognition. Recalculation. "No, he *kept* it. There is a difference, and the difference will matter." He crouched to Edmund's level, and up close his hood smelled of cold stone and old paper. "You cannot digest a soul, child. You can only delay its delivery. And delivery—" the borrowed smile again, fitting worse than ever— "always keeps its appointment."

They took Liss anyway. Of course they took Liss anyway. The contract had been signed; the appointment kept itself. Edmund stood in the frozen field with a star burning behind his ribs and watched the lantern-carrier walk his sister down the road toward the mountain, and she looked back once, at the bend where the road dipped, and raised her hand, and he raised his, and that was the last time he saw his sister wearing her own body.

He did not cry. The men waited to see if he would cry. He did not, because crying was a sound, and sounds were the one thing he had never been able to make, and because — deeper than that, truer — because he had done the sum, and the sum said *one bright thing saved*, and grief could wait. Grief could stand in line behind courage. It is still standing there. It is very patient. It will get its turn.

* * *

Now, in the swordspeaker's house, in the deep of the night, the ember turns over in its sleep against his ribs, and Edmund dreams the last piece, the piece he keeps folded smallest:

After they took Liss, he ran. Not home — home was finished, home was a kitchen where his mother stirred empty water and his father's cap had no shape left — but away, north and west, following a feeling in his chest like a compass needle made of warmth. The ember wanted to go somewhere. It pulled him the way the moon pulls water, patiently, over weeks, over moors and fords and the borders of places whose names he couldn't read. It pulled him toward a valley where the air felt thick and silent, where his chest-glow dimmed to nothing, where he could almost pretend to be an ordinary boy.

And at the edge of that valley, in a storm, at dusk, there was a house with a sword hanging above the door, and the sword was humming — very faintly, a note felt more than heard — and the ember in his chest sat up like a dog hearing its name called from a great distance.

*Her,* the ember said, in the wordless way it said things. *She is one of the ones who speak. She is one of the ones who keep.*

So Edmund climbed the step. So Edmund knocked, three times, patient, the way you knock when you know the door will open eventually.

Because appointments keep themselves. But keepers — keepers have to be found.

In the next room, he hears Petra turn over in her sleep, and murmur something in a dialect he half-recognizes, half-remembers, a woman's voice from a dream that isn't quite hers — and the ember flares once, softly, like a banked coal breathed upon, and Edmund lies in the dark with his hand over his own heart and waits for morning, and for the road, and for the mountain.
