## 9. The Road Sings Back

The first thing Petra learned about the world outside the valley was that it listened.

She had spent forty years in a place where words fell like stones into deep water—where you could curse the rain and bless the bread and neither made any difference to either. She had taught herself, without ever admitting she was teaching herself, to speak carelessly. To let syllables tumble out of her mouth half-formed, unfinished, because nothing was listening and nothing answered.

Three days past the boundary stones, she caught herself mid-sentence and clapped a hand over her own lips.

Edmund looked up from the fire he was feeding—small sticks, dry moss, arranged with the solemn precision of a child performing surgery—and waited. He had learned that about her already, the waiting. He did it with his whole body, the way dogs wait, the way weather waits.

"I said," Petra began slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond, "I said *the wind is cold*."

And the wind was.

It came down off the moor in a long gray rush, bending the gorse flat, finding every gap in her cloak, pressing her breath back against her teeth. Not a gust. A *compliance*. Somewhere behind her ribs, her sword hummed—a low, satisfied note, like a cat settling into a lap it had been promised.

Edmund's eyes went wide. Then, horribly, wonderfully, he laughed.

It was the first time she had heard him laugh. In the valley he had been all watchfulness and small courtesies—the boy who carried water without being asked, who sat at the edge of rooms as though rooms might object. But here, outside, with color sharp enough to cut and sound arriving from every direction at once, something in him had loosened like a knot pulled free. And when he laughed, the fire flared.

Not metaphorically. The fire stood up on its logs, tall and gold-edged, throwing sparks twenty feet into the dark, and for one absurd moment their little camp blazed like a festival.

"Edmund—" Petra said, and stopped, because saying his name with feeling made him *feel* it; she watched it land on him like a hand on the shoulder, watched him straighten under the weight of being named warmly by someone whose words carried.

He pressed both hands over his mouth. His shoulders shook. Above his fingers, his eyes were bright with mischief and apology in equal measure.

"Don't," she warned, and the warning came out as a command, and he went still—not frightened-still, but obedient-still, the stillness of a held note—and she felt the shape of what she'd done settle over them both like frost.

*Oh*, she thought. *This is what I was. This is what they took away when they sent me to the valley.*

She sat down heavily on her pack. The wind, having obeyed her, kept circling as if awaiting further instruction.

"All right," she said aloud, carefully, plainly, wringing the power out of the words by making them boring. "We are going to have rules."

* * *

The rules took two days to build and would take the rest of the road to refine.

Rule the first: no sentence spoken in feeling unless it was meant. Petra demonstrated this by describing the scenery in the flattest voice she could manage—"grass, hill, rock, more grass"—until Edmund giggled and the grass, insulted or delighted, grew an inch around his boots.

Rule the second: names were not to be used casually. She learned this when she said *Edmund, come here* across half a mile of open moor and felt the word travel like an arrow, and felt him turn toward her as if reeled in on a line, stumbling, laughing, slightly breathless when he arrived—as though the name itself had dragged him.

"That's not fair," he signed afterward, hands quick in the gesture-language they'd begun building between them, because Edmund spoke Oathscript the way other children breathed—involuntarily, dangerously, beautifully—and plain speech was a door he could only open when feeling broke it wide. "You get to talk whenever you want. Mine comes out wrong."

"It doesn't come out wrong," Petra said. "It comes out *true*. That's worse, for now. We'll teach it manners."

But privately, watching him sleep that night with the ember-light breathing faintly through his shirt like a lantern behind cloth, she admitted the truth she hadn't said: her own craft was the rusted one. Forty years of silence had done to her sword-arm what rust does to a hinge. She remembered the theory of everything and the practice of almost nothing. She knew the guard-words the way a woman knows a song from childhood—all the notes, none of the breath.

And Edmund, who had never been taught anything, who had simply *arrived* speaking the oldest language there was, kept correcting her.

* * *

On the fourth morning she began teaching him guard-words, and by noon he had become her teacher, and neither of them handled this gracefully.

"The first word is *halveth*," she said, drawing her blade three inches from its sheath so he could see the light along its edge—the light that had not existed in the valley, where the sword had been only old steel and older grief. "*Halveth* cuts a spell in two. It doesn't destroy it. It divides the intention from the working. You say it—"

"*Halveth* means *enough*," Edmund said.

Petra blinked. "What?"

"Not *cut*." He frowned, searching for the words in the trade-tongue, which he spoke well but awkwardly, like a boy wearing his father's coat. "In the old tongue. *Halveth* is what you say when the pot is full. When the rain should stop. When—" he gestured at the horizon, at everything, "—when enough is enough. It doesn't cut. It says: *this much, and no more.*"

She sat with that for a long moment while the moor wind combed the gorse.

Her mother had taught her *halveth* as a battle-word. Her mother had learned it from a grandmother who had learned it in wars Petra had only read about. Four generations of swordspeakers, and somewhere back down the line, someone had taken a household word—a word for stopping the rain, for capping the honey jar, for telling a crying child *that's all now*—and sharpened it into a weapon. Or perhaps no one had sharpened it at all. Perhaps it had always been both, the way a knife is both kitchen and war, and only people who lived in peacetime forgot.

"You're right," she said finally.

Edmund looked alarmed, as if the sky had addressed him personally. "I am?"

"You are. Which brings us to rule the third." She sheathed the sword and rubbed her face. "When I'm wrong, you tell me. Even if I'm your elder. Even if I'm your—" she almost said *master*, and the word snagged on something in her chest, some old splinter of pride, "—even if I'm supposed to be the one who knows."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he nodded once, formally, and stuck out his hand, and she shook it, and the handshake crackled faintly with static, and they both pretended not to notice.

They practiced through the morning. *Halveth* against a thorn-spell some forgotten traveler had left knotted in a hawthorn tree—a petty hex, a *may your path be crooked* curse, the kind farmers used to trade like recipes. Petra's version of the word split the spell cleanly but cost her; she felt it go out of her like a coin from a purse, and couldn't have said what the coin bought, only that her pocket was lighter. That was the way of speech-craft outside the valley: every true word drew on the speaker. She had known this once. Knowing it again, in her body, in the small hollow ache behind her sternum after each working, was different from remembering it.

Edmund's version of *halveth* didn't split the spell. It *finished* it. The crooked-path curse, having been told *enough*, simply concluded—like a story reaching its last page—and unraveled into ordinary air, leaving the hawthorn blooming out of season, white flowers opening all along its black branches while they watched.

"Oh," Petra said softly. "Oh, that's better than mine."

Edmund flushed dark with pleasure and tried to hide it and failed, and the hawthorn blossoms, responding to his joy, opened all at once with a sound like small applause.

* * *

His speech bent fortunes. That was the phrase Petra found herself using, in the privacy of her own head, because *magic* was too grand a word for what he did.

A peddler they walked beside for an hour told them, unprompted, that business had turned good that very day, that he'd sold his last three ribbons at prices he'd stopped hoping for years ago. A lame dog trotted alongside them for a mile and left the encounter walking evenly. Rain that threatened all afternoon went around their camp in a perfect courteous arc, and Edmund, asleep, smiled in a way that suggested he had asked it to.

None of it was commanded. None of it was even intended. He simply spoke, and the world leaned in.

Which was why the guard-words mattered, and why Petra drilled them until Edmund groaned and signed *enough, enough* with such eloquence that the practice session actually ended, the words themselves closing the lesson like a book.

There were seven guard-words in the old training. *Halveth*, the stopper. *Serrin*, the shield-word, which wove a moment's refusal into the air. *Ondu*, which returned a thing to what it was. Three others she remembered imperfectly and one she could not remember at all—she knew there was a seventh, knew it the way you know a stair is missing in the dark, by the lurch of reaching for it—but when she reached for it, it wasn't there. Whether the valley had eaten it or the years had, she couldn't say.

Edmund filled the gap without knowing he was filling it. On the sixth evening, when a marsh-will-o'-wisp came drifting toward their camp with hungry patience, Petra fumbled *serrin*—got the shape of it wrong, got the *breath* of it wrong—and the shield-word guttered like a wet candle. The wisp brightened, interested.

And Edmund, terrified, threw up his hands and shouted the first thing that came into him, which was not *serrin* at all but a word in pure Oathscript, a word with roots in it, a word that meant roughly *you are not invited*—

—and the wisp stopped as if it had struck glass, hung there quivering with offense, and then turned and drifted away into the marsh, sulking visibly.

Silence. Frogs resuming. Edmund's hands still raised, trembling.

"What," Petra said, "was that."

He lowered his arms slowly. "I don't know. It just—it seemed rude? To come in?" He looked at her, stricken. "Was that bad? Was I supposed to use *serrin*?"

"Yes. And yours worked better." She started laughing before she could stop herself—real laughter, unguarded, the kind she hadn't managed since before the valley, and the campfire leapt in response, and Edmund began laughing too, and the frogs fell silent as if listening, and somewhere out in the dark the offended wisp flickered indignantly.

That night she wrote in her travel journal, by ember-light, the first entry she'd made in years:

*Day six. The student has surpassed the teacher in every respect except fear. Note: teach him fear. Note also: do not teach him too much fear.*

She looked at that second line a long time before closing the book.

* * *

On the eighth day, the road began to sing back.

She noticed it first as a rhythm under her boots—a pulse in the packed earth, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Then she realized Edmund was humming. He hummed constantly now; outside the valley, his voice wanted to be used, and humming was the safest outlet they'd found. But this was different. The road itself had picked up his tune.

Old roads did that, if you knew how to listen. Roads that had carried pilgrimages, processions, armies, funerals—roads that had heard ten thousand voices say ten thousand things worth saying. The words soaked down into the clay and stayed. Most travelers never heard the residue. Swordspeakers did. And apparently, boys with embers in their chests did too.

Edmund had stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the road with his head tilted, listening, and the road was giving the tune back to him with harmonics he hadn't sung, layers of old footsteps and old farewells folded into the melody.

"What is it saying?" Petra asked quietly.

He listened longer. His face did something complicated—delight shading into sorrow shading into a kind of wonder that contained both.

"People going somewhere," he said. "Lots of people. For a long time. They were—" his hands moved, searching, "—hoping. All of them hoping. The road kept it." He looked up at her. "Roads keep things?"

"Some of them," Petra said. "If enough people give them something to keep."

He put his palm flat against the ground, and the road sang a little louder, and wildflowers along the verge turned toward him like faces toward warmth, and Petra stood in the middle of an ordinary moorland track with her heart in her throat, thinking: *this is what the world was like before. This is what they sealed away. We lived forty years in a room with the windows painted shut and called it peace.*

Then, because joy this large needed somewhere to go, she did something reckless.

She drew her sword.

Fully. For the first time since the boundary stones—truly fully, the whole length of it, the blade coming free with a sound like a held breath released. And the blade *sang*. Not the thin hum of the valley years but a full chord, a sound with architecture in it, and the road answered, and the gorse answered, and Edmund's ember flared bright through his shirt, and for three glorious seconds the whole moor was one ringing note.

Then Petra attempted a form.

She had known nine hundred forms once. Her body remembered perhaps sixty. She flowed through the opening sequence—*first stance, river-turning, the question asked sideways*—and it was beautiful right up until it wasn't, right up until her balance failed on the fifth movement and she stumbled, caught herself, recovered with whatever grace embarrassment allowed, and finished the sequence in something closer to a stagger than a dance.

Edmund was sitting cross-legged at the roadside, watching with his chin on his fists.

"Well," Petra said, breathing hard, cheeks burning, forty years old and mortified in front of a nine-year-old. "Say nothing."

He said nothing. He was very good at it. But his eyes sparkled, and when she resheathed the sword he patted the ground beside him invitingly, and when she sat, he scooted close and tapped her arm and then, hesitantly, mimed the fifth movement—the one she'd stumbled—the river-turning—with small careful hands, showing her how it had looked from outside.

"You're saying my elbow was wrong," she translated.

He nodded.

"And my weight was on the wrong foot."

Nod.

"And you could see all this, sitting here, having never studied a single form in your life."

Enthusiastic nod. Then, gentler, he touched her sleeve and signed: *show me again. Slowly. I'll hold the shape in my head and give it back to you.*

So they did that. An old woman and a small boy on a singing road, passing a sword-form back and forth like a ball, her body remembering through his eyes, his mind learning through her failures. Twice he gave a movement back to her corrected and she felt the correction land as truth—felt the form click into place like a bone set—and each time the road beneath them hummed approval, and each time she laughed, and each time the laughter cost her nothing and gave her everything.

*Apprenticeship reversed*, she thought, as the sun went down copper behind the hills. *Or maybe there was never a direction to it at all. Maybe that was another lie the valley told—that knowledge flows downhill, from old to young, from master to student, one way only, forever.*

Somewhere below the eastern horizon, past towns she'd only known as names on a map she'd never been allowed to need, lay Hollowbridge Market, and a translator of Oathscript, and answers she was beginning to suspect she did not want.

But that was tomorrow's road. Tonight there was a fire, and a boy humming harmony with the earth, and her sword lying across her knees, warm as a living thing, singing very softly to itself the way contented creatures do.

She was almost asleep when Edmund touched her arm and pointed east.

At first she thought it was a star. Then the star moved against the other stars—steady, deliberate, descending along the line of the road they would take in the morning. Too slow for a falling thing. Too purposeful for a bird. And as it came nearer, low over the moor, she saw that it was neither.

It was a lantern. Carried by a figure walking with the unhurried certainty of someone who knows exactly which door they are knocking on.

Beside her, Edmund's ember pulsed once—twice—like a heart recognizing another heart across a crowded room.

"He's coming," Edmund whispered, and the road itself seemed to carry the words away eastward, delivering them ahead of the light.
