## 10. Hollowbridge Market

The road to Hollowbridge smelled of tallow and river mud, and Petra hated how much she had missed smells.

In the valley there had been bread and woodsmoke and the green rot of the millpond, and that was all—a handful of scents, worn smooth as river stones by forty years of repetition. Out here the world was a riot. Dung and cut hay and tar and someone frying onions in a way that made her stomach clench like a fist. The wind off the water carried salt and fish scales and, beneath it all, something older: the mineral breath of deep stone, though no mountain stood within three days' walk. She filed that away and did not look at it too closely. Some things, once looked at, refused to be unlooked-at.

Edmund walked beside her with his hand hooked in her belt loop, the way he'd done since the ford, since the wardens, since she had lied for the first time in her life and felt the valley's silence shiver around her like a struck bell. He had not let go of her since. Not out of fear, she thought—or not only fear. There was something in the boy's grip that felt like anchoring. As if he were afraid that if he released her, she might drift up off the road entirely, unmoored from everything she'd been.

He wasn't wrong to worry.

"Hollowbridge," she said, and stopped herself before the word could do anything strange. Outside the valley, words had weight. She had learned that on the first morning, when she'd said *good* over a breakfast of hard cheese and Edmund's portion had somehow tasted better—richer, creamier, as if the word had seeped into the food. Small fortunes, small blessings. A careless *cold* had given them both chills for an hour. She was learning to speak the way a drunkard learns to walk again: deliberately, watching each step.

Edmund looked up at her. His eyes were dark and old and utterly childlike, all three at once, which should have been impossible. He pointed ahead with his free hand.

The town announced itself before they saw it: a low roar of commerce, wheels on stone, the complaint of gulls, a bell somewhere ringing the half-hour with more enthusiasm than accuracy. Then the bridge itself, humped and ancient, spanning a brown river wide as a street, and beyond it the town climbing its hill in crooked tiers of slate roofs and plaster walls stained silver by weather. Market day, by the density of carts. Banners hung from upper windows, faded to pastel ghosts of whatever colors they'd once been.

Petra paused at the near end of the bridge and did what she always did now: checked her sword.

It rode her hip where it had ridden for forty years, wrapped in oiled cloth beneath her cloak, never drawn. In the valley it had been dead weight, a relic of a self she'd buried. Here it hummed. Faintly, constantly, like a hive behind the leather. When Edmund laughed, it hummed louder. When he slept against her side on the moor, it had sung so softly she'd lain awake listening, afraid and hungry in equal measure, the way a starving man lies beside a locked pantry.

She touched the hilt through the cloth. The humming rose to meet her fingers like a cat pressing into a hand.

"Not yet," she murmured, and was careful not to put any weight on the words.

They crossed.

* * *

Hollowbridge Market filled a long square between the bridge and the town's lower gate, and it was the loudest place Petra had ever stood. Stalls leaned together like conspirators, awnings overlapping until the light came down green and gold through canvas. Fishmongers cried their catch; a woman sold ribbons in colors Petra had no names for; a knife-grinder's wheel threw orange sparks; somewhere a fiddler played something fast and slightly drunken while children wove between legs and cartwheels and the legs of livestock. Sheep bleated. Geese objected to everything on principle.

Edmund stopped dead in the entrance of the square and stared.

Petra watched him watch it, and her chest did something complicated. The boy had come to her door barefoot in a storm, carrying a stolen ember and no shadow, and since then his world had been moorland and rain and one terrified widow's kitchen. This—noise, color, abundance, human beings in their hundreds going about their mornings as though the world were a safe place—this was revelation. His mouth hung slightly open. The ember under his ribs glowed faintly through his shirt, a coal-warm patch of light, and Petra shifted her cloak to cover him without breaking stride.

"Stay close," she said. "Don't speak."

He nodded. It was the rule they'd agreed on at dawn: outside the valley, his voice was a weapon he didn't know how to hold. Every syllable of Oathscript bent the world a little. On the moor he'd said a word that sounded like *sennet*, meaning nothing to either of them, and a thornbush had bloomed out of season, white flowers opening in the frost like small astonished faces. They'd left it blooming. Petra hadn't stopped thinking about it since. If a throwaway syllable could force spring out of winter, what would happen if the boy ever shouted?

A fruit-seller pressed an apple into Edmund's hand unprompted, laughing at his expression, waving off payment—"For the little lord, look at his face"—and Petra paid her anyway because gifts outside the valley had a way of being binding. The apple was red as a coal. Edmund held it in both hands and looked at Petra for permission, and she nodded, and he bit into it with such ferocious delight that two passing women turned to smile at him.

That was the trouble, Petra thought. Everyone looked at him. He was the kind of child strangers noticed—too quiet, too still, too bright-eyed—and she could not wrap him in canvas the way she'd wrapped the sword.

They worked the edge of the market first, the way she'd worked unfamiliar ground her whole life: perimeter before center. She was looking for a particular kind of stall. Booksellers clustered near temples or universities in most towns; Hollowbridge had neither that she could see, but trade towns attracted the learned the way carcasses attracted crows. Scribes selling letters home for illiterate drovers. Map-copiers. And, if the town was big enough—

There. Third row back, wedged between a chandler and a seller of secondhand boots: a stall draped in paper. Scrolls in bins, ledgers stacked like firewood, a hanging sign bearing a painted eye above an open book. Behind it sat a thin man with ink-stained fingers reading a broadsheet, and beside him, sharpening a quill with a small knife, a woman who looked up as they approached with the flat professional assessment of someone who priced other people's secrets daily.

"You want letters sent?" the man asked without lowering his broadsheet.

"Words read," Petra said. "Old words."

That got both their attention. The woman set down her quill. "What tongue?"

"Oathscript."

The change in them was immediate and total, and Petra cursed herself for saying it aloud. The man folded his broadsheet with elaborate casualness. The woman's eyes went from Petra to the sword-bulge under her cloak to the boy at her side, and stayed on the boy a beat too long.

"Nobody reads Oathscript," the man said. "Not properly. Not since the courts fell quiet." He said it lightly, but his hand had drifted toward a drawer in the counter, and Petra noted that the drawer had a lock.

"Someone reads it," Petra said. "Or someone knows who does."

"Even if we did," the woman said, "words like that cost more than coin."

"Everything does."

They gave her a name, eventually, after she'd bought a worthless map of the coast she didn't need and overpaid for it. A translator. Half a mile uphill, past the shrine district, a door with a brass owl. *Tell her the crows sent you,* the woman said, and then, as Petra turned away, added quietly: "Burn anything she writes for you. That's free advice."

Petra carried the advice away like a stone in her boot.

* * *

They were halfway across the square, aiming for the shrine district, when Petra saw the notice board.

It stood where market squares always kept one: a great frame of blackened oak at the square's heart, papered layers deep, proclamations over tariffs over playbills over lost-dog notices, the whole palimpsest pinned and pasted until it stood an inch proud of its frame. A knot of people stood before it. That was normal. What stopped Petra's breath was the color.

One sheet, newer than the rest, whiter than the rest, bore a border printed in ash-grey.

Ash-grey. The color of the cloaks at the ford. The color of the men who had asked her, politely, terribly, whether she had seen a child pass, a child who cast no shadow, and she had lied and said no and felt the wards of her whole life flicker around her like a guttering lamp.

"Wait here," she told Edmund, and immediately regretted it, because waiting here meant standing alone in a crowd, glowing faintly through his shirt. "No. With me. Don't look at anyone."

She shouldered through the knot of idlers—market crowds part reluctantly for a woman in a travel cloak, and she used an elbow without apology—and reached the board.

The notice was printed, not written, which meant many copies, which meant reach, which meant money behind it. At the top, in formal capitals: **BY ORDER OF THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN**. Below that, a woodcut likeness.

Petra had braced herself for this. Since the ford she had known the harvesters would not simply give up; souls that escaped contracts were hunted the way escaped hounds were hunted, professionally and without heat. She had expected the boy's face. She had expected a reward figure with more zeroes than most houses earned in a generation. She had expected the phrase *PROPERTY OF THE EMBER COURT, RETURN ALIVE AND UNDRAINED*, and there it was, and it turned her stomach exactly as much as she'd predicted.

What she had not expected was the second woodcut beneath the first.

A woman. Tall, straight-backed, cloaked. Her hair bound back. Her jaw set in that particular stubborn line that Petra saw every morning in the water trough and had long ago stopped fighting.

Herself.

And beneath the likeness, in the same formal capitals:

**THE OATH-BREAKER VASQUEZ. LAST OF THE SWORDSPEAKERS. WANTED FOR QUESTIONING REGARDING THE SEAL. REWARD: HER WEIGHT IN IRON, OR EQUIVALENT FAVOR.**

The square went on roaring around her. The fiddler played. Geese complained. A child shrieked with laughter over nothing. And Petra Vasquez stood in front of a piece of paper and could not move.

*Vasquez.*

Not Petra. Not the name the valley used—the valley had never used any name for her, not really; they called her *the swordswoman* or *keeper of the boundary house* or, among the children who dared, *the Iron Lady of the Ford*. She had let that slide for forty years, telling herself it was respect, knowing it was distance. But this name on the page was different. This was a name from before. A name she had traded away at nineteen years old, kneeling on cold stone with a blade across her shoulders, when she had sworn the oath that emptied her sword and sealed her valley and purchased her sanctuary with her own history.

*Vasquez.* Her mother's name. Her mother, whose dialect the soul in Edmund's chest had begged in. Her mother, whose face she could no longer fully assemble in memory—she'd discovered that loss only last week, on the moor, reaching for it and finding a smear where features should be, and had told herself it was tiredness.

The notice knew her name. The court beneath the mountain knew her name. Which meant the court had known about her all along—which meant the valley had never hidden her, had never protected her, had been less a sanctuary than a shelf where something valuable was stored.

*Wanted for questioning regarding the seal.*

Seal. Not *the valley*. Not *the boundary*. Seal. As if the valley were a jar and she were the wax.

"Petra." Edmund's voice, very small, very plain—not Oathscript, just a frightened boy's word—and her hand found his shoulder without her deciding anything.

"We're leaving," she said. "Uphill. Now."

"The picture," Edmund said. "It's you."

"I know."

"It says you broke something."

"I know." She tore the notice off the board. It came away with a sound like a small tear, and half the crowd flinched, and Petra realized she had spoken with weight—that *I know* had landed in the air like a dropped anvil, that several bystanders had actually staggered. She bit down on everything else she wanted to say. Folded the paper. Put it inside her cloak, against her heart, where it lay like a second ember, colder and sharper.

Then she took Edmund's hand and climbed.

* * *

The shrine district was quieter, whitewashed walls and blue-painted doors, old gods keeping modest houses. The brass owl was tarnished green on a door at the end of a lane where cats owned the cobblestones. Petra knocked twice, hesitated, and added the third knock the market woman had described—*the crows sent you*, three knocks, uneven, like a bird's call.

Footsteps. The door opened on a chain, then fully.

The translator was younger than Petra expected and older than she looked, which is to say her face was smooth and her eyes were not. Grey robes, ink on her fingers, the smell of vinegar and old vellum rolling out of the dim interior behind her. She looked at Petra. She looked at Edmund. She looked longest at Edmund, and something moved behind her eyes that Petra did not like—recognition, or appetite, or grief; with the learned, all three wore the same mask.

"The crows sent you," the translator said. It was not a question.

"They did. We need Oathscript read. Spoken words, translated safe. Can you do that?"

"I can." The woman stepped aside, holding the door. "Come in before the lane hears you. Words carry, out here. You of all people should know."

Petra paused on the threshold. "You know me."

"I know the shape of you." The translator's gaze flicked, briefly, to the folded paper inside Petra's cloak—as if she could see through cloth—or perhaps to the sword-bulge beneath it. "Everyone below the mountain knows the shape of you. Come in, swordspeaker. Bring the ember-child. We have a great deal to discuss, and I think—" her mouth curved, without warmth, "—very little time."

Behind her, in the dim of the study, a hundred candles burned in rows like votive lamps, and Petra thought, absurdly, of niches in stone, and stepped inside anyway.
