## 11. The Translator's Price

Sefa had learned to measure her ruin in ink.

There was the good ink, once—violet-black, ground from mountain gall and river-iron, the kind that only the court scribes were permitted to grind, the kind that held a word so firmly that the word could not be unsaid. She had used it for eleven years. She had written harvest tallies with it, and tithe rolls, and the beautiful, terrible contracts of the Ember Court, each clause curling off her pen like smoke given grammar.

Now she used tallow-smoke and oak-gall bought from a chandler who watered his vinegar, and the words she wrote with it—the bills of lading, the marriage lines, the apology letters for other people's sins—faded within a season. Everything faded now. That was the shape of disgrace: not a door slammed, but a slow leaching of color out of everything you touched.

She kept her shop on Cordwainer's Stair, third landing down, where the smell of the harbor came up through the floorboards at high tide like a rumor. Above her hung a sign she had painted herself, badly: *SEFA VELL — WORDS READ, WRITTEN, & SET RIGHT.* The last phrase was a joke only she understood. Nothing she set right stayed right. That was why they had taken her violet ink away.

She was mending a rent in a merchant's lease—careful work, matching a stranger's hand stroke by stroke—when the bell above her door gave its cracked little cough, and the afternoon came in wearing a sword.

* * *

Sefa noticed three things in the first breath, because eleven years in the Ember Court had trained her to notice three things in every first breath, and the habit had outlived the court itself.

First: the woman was armed, and carried the armament wrong—not slung for show, not strapped for war, but worn the way one wears an old grief, close against the body where it can be forgotten until it cannot. The scabbard was plain leather gone gray with age. The hilt was wrapped in cord that had been rewrapped many times, by hands that cared.

Second: the boy. Barefoot still, or barefoot again—there was road dust in the creases of his feet, and newer dust over that, Hollowbridge dust, chalk and tallow and the sweet rot of the fish stalls. He stood slightly behind the woman but not hiding behind her, which was a distinction most adults never managed. His eyes went around her shop the way a thirsty man's eyes go around a well.

Third—and this was the thing that made Sefa's pen stop mid-stroke, made a blot fall onto the merchant's lease like a small black wound—the boy's mouth was moving. He was speaking. And the sounds he made did not behave like sound.

They behaved like *weight.*

She felt them settle on the shop. On the shelves. On the ledger open before her, whose pages stirred though the window was shut. One syllable in particular—a low, rounded thing, like a stone rolled twice in a streambed—made the iron nail-heads in her counter hum, very faintly, a note she had heard only once before in her life, on the day they took her credentials and burned them in a brazier while she watched, and the burning page had sung that same note as it died.

Oathscript. Living Oathscript, spoken freely, unguarded, by a child who clearly did not know—or knew too well—what he was carrying in his mouth.

"Close the door," Sefa said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "And stop talking, little one. Please. Just for a moment."

The woman's hand moved—not to her hilt, Sefa noted, but to the boy's shoulder, drawing him half behind her. Protective. Practiced. "He doesn't speak anything else," the woman said. "Not since—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Sefa watched her decide how much truth this stranger could be trusted with, and admired the economy of it even as she resented being on the far side of the calculation. "We were told you read old scripts."

"Who told you?"

"A man selling eels by the west stair. He said you'd helped his cousin with a funeral text no one else could parse."

The eel-seller. Sefa almost smiled. There was no eel-seller; there was a network of harbor-folk who sent her the strange cases, the desperate cases, and took their cut in favors, and the cousin-with-a-funeral-text was a fiction older than some of the buildings on this street. Someone had pointed these two at her deliberately. She would want to know who, eventually. Eventually was a country she lived in constantly and never reached.

"I read old scripts," she said. "I also charge. Sit. Both of you. If you're going to stand there radiating trouble, at least do it where I've swept."

* * *

The woman sat like someone who had forgotten sitting was allowed. She introduced herself as Petra, and the boy as Edmund, and offered nothing else, and Sefa respected the shape of that silence because she had built identical ones herself.

Tea first. It was bad tea—everything she owned was slightly wrong now, slightly faded—but the ritual of pouring gave the room time to remember it was a room and not a courtroom. Edmund held his cup in both hands and stared into it as if reading leaves. Petra drank hers in three efficient swallows and set it down like a completed task.

"Now," Sefa said, and took out her working slate instead of paper, because some things should not be written even in fading ink. "I'm going to ask you to let him speak. One word. A small one. And I need you both to understand what may happen when he does."

Petra's eyes narrowed. "You know what he's speaking."

"I know what it *isn't*." Sefa turned the slate over, wiped it clean with her sleeve. "It isn't a language of the north, or the deep-folk, or any of the drowned tongues the fishermen pay me to identify. Those languages describe things. This one—" she tapped the slate, "—this one *instructs* things. There is a family of scripts we called, in another life, the binding tongues. Words that don't say what is, but tell what must be. Courts used them. Courts were *built* on them." She looked at the boy, and softened despite herself; he was watching her with the terrible patience of children who have been talked about across tables their whole short lives. "Where did you learn to speak, Edmund?"

He answered. Of course he answered—he always answered—and the shop shivered. The candle flames leaned toward him like courtiers. Somewhere in the walls a beam settled with a sigh, and Sefa would have sworn, would have staked her last coin, that the beam settled *because he asked it to*, gently, the way you'd ask a tired animal to lie down.

But she caught it. Eleven years of transcription had left her with an ear like a fisherman's net: things went in tangled and came out sorted. She caught the root-sounds, the spine of the sentence, and wrote them in her own shorthand, and then—slowly, tasting each piece—she rendered it into common speech.

"He says," she said, and her voice did something embarrassing in the middle, so she stopped and started again, "he says he learned it under the mountain. Before. That everyone spoke it there, in the dark places, and that above ground the words 'get thin.' He says—" she listened to the echo still hanging in the rafters, "—he says he's sorry about your candle."

One of the candles had indeed guttered out. Edmund looked stricken. Petra put her hand flat on his back, and the boy leaned into it the way a post leans into its footing.

"You can translate him," Petra said. It wasn't quite a question, and it wasn't quite wonder either; it was the sound of a person letting hope into a fortified house through a very small window. "Fully? Everything he says?"

"For a price," Sefa said, because kindness without terms was a currency she had been bankrupted by once already, and never again. "Everything has a price. Mine is usually money. Yours—" she hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because the hesitation told her something true before her mouth agreed to say it, "—yours may be more complicated."

* * *

Here is what Sefa did not say, in the hour that followed, while she tested Edmund's speech with careful single syllables and filled four slates with her racing shorthand:

Three years ago, before the fall, she had transcribed a contract. Not one of the great soul-tithes—she had never been senior enough for those, thank every merciful dark—but a lesser instrument, a maintenance covenant, the kind of dry legal bone that keeps a large machine limping. It concerned a valley. It concerned a seal. It concerned, in clause after clause she had copied without comprehension, the obligations of a certain order of women who carried swords and spoke oaths, and the terms under which one such woman might be released from service.

At the bottom of that document, in the space reserved for future countersignature, there had been a blank. And beside the blank, in the margin, in the First Scribe's own hand, a note she had never forgotten:

*This clause will sign itself.*

She had thought it rhetoric. Court scribes were fond of rhetoric; it was cheaper than meaning. But she had copied the note faithfully, because faithful copying was the whole of her virtue and the entire architecture of her pride, and so the words had lodged in her like a splinter, working deeper with every year.

*This clause will sign itself.*

And now a swordspeaker sat in her shop with road-dust in the seams of her boots and a mute child full of living Oathscript at her side, asking for translation, heading—Sefa would have bet her remaining reputation on it—toward the mountain. Toward the court. Toward the table where clauses waited to sign themselves.

She owed the court a debt. That was the ugly heart of it, the thing she circled in her mind the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. Disgrace was not free. They had let her keep her hands, her eyes, her freedom to climb Cordwainer's Stair and sell her watered-ink services to merchants and widows, and in exchange there was a standing instruction, delivered once, quietly, by a courier whose ash-gray cloak she still saw in dreams: *When the court asks you for a service, you will provide it. You will know the request when it comes.*

Three years, and the request had never come. She had begun to hope it never would. Hope, she had learned, was just interest accruing on a debt you'd forgotten.

And here, drinking her bad tea, sat the request. Or the shape of it. Because what use would the court have for a disgraced scribe, if not this—to place her in the path of the ember-bearer, to make her useful to him, to weave her into his company so thoroughly that when the moment came she would be standing exactly where the contract needed her to be?

*You will know the request when it comes.*

She knew it. It had come wearing road dust and holding a teacup in both hands.

* * *

"There's a bounty out," Sefa said at last, because she was not, whatever else she was, cruel by omission. "In the market square. Your likeness, boy—badly drawn, but recognizable. And hers." She nodded at Petra. "Under a name I don't think is yours anymore, but names travel faster than corrections. You shouldn't stay in Hollowbridge past tomorrow's dawn. The ash-cloaks come through on the moor road at first light; I've watched their schedule for three years, partly from habit and partly from cowardice."

Petra absorbed this the way stone absorbs rain. "You're warning us. Why?"

Because I am already entangled, Sefa did not say. Because the web was spun around me years ago and you have just flown into it and I cannot decide whether I am the spider's tool or, perhaps, if I am very clever and very lucky, the moth that chews the thread. Because underneath all of it—the debt, the fear, the faded ink—there is a girl who spent eleven years copying contracts that sold children's light, and who has been waiting to be sorry in a way that costs something.

"Because I'm expensive," she said instead, "and dead clients don't pay."

"And your price?" Petra asked. "For the translating. For everything."

Sefa looked at Edmund. He was watching her with those well-eyes again, and she found she could not hold the look, and looked at the slate instead, at her shorthand crawling across it like tracks of some small determined animal.

"My price is a favor," she said. "Unspecified. To be named later. Owed by you"—she pointed the stylus at Petra—"and honored by you, whatever it turns out to be, whenever I call it. That's the whole of the fee. No coin."

"That's not a price," Petra said slowly. "That's a hook."

"Yes," Sefa agreed. "It is. Take it anyway."

The silence stretched. Outside, the tide was turning; the harbor smell shifted from rot to salt, and somewhere below a gull argued with the world and lost. Edmund tugged Petra's sleeve and spoke—one soft syllable-cluster, gentle as snowfall—and Sefa caught it, translated it before she could stop herself, and heard her own voice deliver it into the quiet:

"He says: 'The ones who owe are the ones who help. She knows the way debt walks.'"

Sefa set down the stylus very carefully, because her hand wanted to shake and she would not permit it.

"Well," she said. "He's not wrong."

Petra looked between them—at the boy, at the scribe, at the four slates of translated speech already stacked like kindling—and Sefa watched her do the arithmetic every traveler eventually does on the road to a mountain: what do I have, what do I need, and what will the difference cost me.

"We leave at dawn," Petra said. "Before the ash-cloaks. Can you be ready?"

Can I betray them, or save them, or both, or neither, depending on what the court asks and what I discover I am? Sefa thought. Yes. I can be ready for all of it. I have been ready for three years, standing on a staircase smelling of tallow, waiting for my masters to remember I exist.

"I'll be ready," she said, and began wiping her slates clean, and did not meet anyone's eyes, and told herself the heat in her face was the bad tea.

Above the door, the cracked bell coughed once, though no one had touched it, and the afternoon light through the window went briefly, unmistakably, the color of violet ink.
