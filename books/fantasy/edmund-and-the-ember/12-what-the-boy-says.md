## 12. What the Boy Says

Sefa had not held a true quill in eleven years, and her hand remembered the weight of it the way a drowned man remembers air.

The room above Hollowbridge's chandler's shop smelled of tallow and old paper, and she had arranged both to her advantage: the candle set low so that the boy's face stayed half in shadow, the papers squared before her with the particular neatness of someone who had once been paid by the court beneath the mountain to make lies look orderly. She was good at order. It was the truth that had ruined her.

The child sat across the table, feet swinging, watching her with the patience of something that had waited a long time already. His chest, beneath the rough shirt Petra Vasquez had buttoned him into, gave off a faint warmth Sefa could feel from three feet away—like sitting near an oven door. Like sitting near a hearth where something lived.

"You understand," she said, "that I'm out of practice."

He didn't answer. Of course he didn't. The Vasquez woman had told her on the stairs: *he doesn't speak anything but that tongue, and it isn't a tongue, it's older than tongues.* Sefa had smiled the way you smile at frightened people, and said she'd know it when she heard it.

She'd been lying. She wasn't sure she would know it at all. She only knew that the favor she owed had a name below the mountain, and that names below the mountain always came due, and that translating for a runaway soul was either the best way to pay it or the fastest way to be collected herself.

"Whenever you're ready," she said.

Edmund looked at Petra. Petra stood by the window with her arms crossed, sword at her hip, wearing the expression of a woman guarding a door against her own curiosity. She nodded once.

And the boy spoke.

* * *

Sefa had expected sounds. What she got was architecture.

The syllables came out of him small and round, each one placed like a stone in a wall—and the room changed around them. Not visibly. Nothing moved. But the candle flame straightened as if standing at attention. The papers on the table lay flatter. Even the dust in the air seemed to arrange itself into patient lines. She had spent six years in the court's scriptorium copying contracts whose words could bind souls, and she had never once heard those words spoken aloud. They were meant to be written. They were meant to be signed. Hearing them in a child's mouth was like hearing a lock being picked by someone humming.

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop.

"Again," she whispered, though he hadn't finished. "Slower."

He went slower. And because slowness is a mercy, she began to catch the shapes of it—the grammar underneath, the load-bearing words. Oathscript. Actual Oathscript, the first-tongue, the binding-language, the thing she had studied from dead copies in a dead scriptorium until the court decided she knew too much about what the copies *omitted*. Her disgrace had been an act of reading. Now reading was saving her.

She pulled a fresh sheet toward her and began to write—not a translation yet, just the bones. Subject. Object. The terrible verb at the center, the one that appeared four times and each time meant something slightly worse.

Petra had come away from the window without seeming to move. "What is he saying?"

"Wait." Sefa's voice came out wrong, scraped. "Please. Let me finish."

Because the message was short. That was the horror of it. Children think in short sentences; the universe makes them pay for brevity. He had compressed everything—the sale, the flight, the hunger behind him, the road ahead—into perhaps forty words, and forty words of Oathscript was a cathedral.

She wrote the last stroke. Set down the quill. Looked at what her own hand had made, and found she couldn't lift her eyes from it.

"Sefa." Petra's voice now, low, the voice of a woman who had learned to hear bad news in the pauses. "What did he say?"

Sefa read it aloud instead, because reading it aloud made it someone else's sentence and not hers to carry alone:

"*The ember is my sister.*"

The candle guttered, though there was no draft.

"*They took her whole. Not a piece—a whole. Mother sold her whole and father let them and I ran with her inside me before the King's men could carry her down.*"

Sefa's own translation stared up at her in her own handwriting, and she hated how steady it looked. "*Carry her home or the King eats the light.*"

Silence took the room the way water takes a dropped stone—all at once, from every side.

* * *

Petra did not move for a long moment. Sefa watched her the way you watch a bridge under weight—looking for the crack, the sway, the place where the structure decides what it is made of.

The swordspeaker's face did exactly nothing. That was the frightening part. Sefa had seen nobles receive death sentences with more motion. Then, very slowly, Petra turned to the boy, and everything in her face rearranged itself around a single question she seemed almost afraid to ask aloud.

"Liss," she said. "Is her name Liss?"

Edmund's whole body answered before his mouth could. He sat up straight, hands flat on the table, eyes gone wide and wet and blazing, and he said one word—one small round word that landed in the room like a coin dropped in a well:

*Liss.*

"That's a yes," Sefa said quietly, though no one needed it said.

Petra closed her eyes. When she opened them again, something had been dismantled behind them and rebuilt in a different shape. Sefa recognized the expression because she had worn it herself, years ago, over a contract page: the look of a person discovering that the story they thought they were in was a smaller story than the real one.

"I thought—" Petra stopped. Started again, more carefully, as if each word were being weighed before release. "I thought we were carrying a stolen ember back to its owners. A debt. A transaction undone. That's what my oath says. *Return what was taken.*" Her hand found the hilt at her hip, not drawing, just resting, the way a man rests his hand on a fence post. "But it isn't a transaction, is it. It's a girl."

"She's seven," Sefa said, and then wished she hadn't, because she had read the age in the contract fragments the broker's office kept, back when she still had access, and saying it aloud made it flesh. "Or she was. Souls don't age in storage. She'll be seven forever, wherever she is, until someone spends her or frees her."

Edmund was watching Petra with an intensity that had stopped being childlike. There was something ancient in it now, something that belonged to the language he spoke rather than the boy who spoke it—as if the words had gotten into him the way dye gets into cloth, all the way through.

Then he said something new. Longer. His voice cracked in the middle of it, and the candle bent sideways, and Sefa caught enough of the shape to translate even as her throat tightened:

"*You have an oath. Oaths are doors. Will your door open for her?*"

* * *

Later—much later, after Edmund had finally slept in the chair by the cold hearth, ember-light breathing faintly through his shirt with each rise of his chest—Petra and Sefa stood at the window together, watching Hollowbridge's few lamps drown in the fog off the river.

"He asked me if my oath would open for her," Petra said. "As if it were a door. As if oaths were things with hinges."

"In Oathscript they are," Sefa said. "That's the part nobody above ground remembers. Your valley people think an oath is a promise. Below the mountain, an oath is a mechanism. Words that do things. Doors that open onto consequences." She wrapped her shawl tighter. "That's why they want translators. That's why they wanted me. A badly translated oath is still binding—it's just binding in ways nobody intended."

"And his message. *Carry her home.* Home to where? He was sold *from* somewhere."

"Yes." Sefa hesitated. This was the part she had been turning over since the candle guttered, the part she had decided, in the end, to give them whether or not it served the favor she owed. Some debts are paid best by betrayal. "Home isn't a house, Petra. In the contract-grammar, *home* for a soul means origin-point. The place it was... decanted. Where the self was poured out of the body and into the vessel." She made herself go on. "For Liss, that would have been the court itself. The harvest-floor. Which means *carry her home* doesn't mean away from the mountain."

Petra went very still.

"It means *into* it," Sefa finished. "He's asking you to walk the ember back down the King's throat and take his sister out of the light before the light gets eaten. That's the message. That's the whole message. A seven-year-old boy swallowed his sister's soul and ran, and the instruction manual he carries in a language older than kingdoms says: bring her back to the mouth of the thing that bought her, and pull her free."

Below them, in the chair, Edmund slept on, unaware—or pretending to be unaware, which children are better at than armies—that his chest rose and fell with another person's light.

Petra's hand had not left her sword-hilt. Now Sefa saw her knuckles whiten around it, and heard her say, so softly it was nearly prayer:

"My oath compels me to return what was taken. It doesn't say *to whom*."

"No," Sefa agreed. "It doesn't. That omission has killed better people than us."

Outside, far off across the moor, thunder rolled—except the sky was clear, and Sefa, who had lived below the mountain long enough to know its weather, felt the hair rise on her arms. That wasn't thunder. That was the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep, taking notice, tasting the air for a runaway ember and the swordspeaker sworn to carry it.

"They know we're coming," she said. "Or they will by morning. The King's harvesters don't chase, Petra. They *wait*. They let the oath do the walking."

Petra looked down at the sleeping boy—at the faint gold seam of light at his collarbone, rising and falling, rising and falling, keeping its small stubborn time.

"Then we'd better teach him some guard-words before dawn," she said. "And Sefa—you're coming with us. Whatever you owe below the mountain, you can pay it on the road."

Sefa opened her mouth to refuse, and found, to her considerable annoyance, that she had already packed.
