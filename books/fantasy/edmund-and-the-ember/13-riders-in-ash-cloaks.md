## 13. Riders in Ash Cloaks

The moor road was the color of old iron, and it sang.

Not the way Edmund sang—his was a language with bones in it, a language that made doors remember they were doors. The moor sang the way a struck wire sings: one long thin note of wind over heather and stone, rising and falling but never quite ending. Petra had walked three days inside that note, and she had begun to understand that it was not empty. It was the sound of a world holding its breath.

Edmund walked ahead of her, barefoot as always, though she had bought him boots in Hollowbridge with the last of Maren's coin. He wore them on a cord around his neck like an amulet he hadn't decided to believe in yet. The light in his chest had dimmed to a coal-glow, visible only at dusk now, and that frightened her more than its brightness had. Ember or soul or sister—whatever burned in the boy was burning down, and the road to the mountain was longer than the road behind them.

"Keeper," he said—not the word, not yet, but the Oathscript syllable that meant *the-one-who-holds*, and she had stopped correcting him days ago. He pointed east, where the moor rose into a ridge of black stone.

Petra looked. The wind changed.

It changed the way weather changes on the moor, all at once and from nowhere: the long note of the wind dropped half a tone, and the heather lay flat in a wave, and out of that wave came a smell she had never smelled and knew instantly—ash. Not woodsmoke, not peat-fire. The ash of things burned all the way down to what they were made of. Names, she thought, before she knew why she thought it. It smelled like names burned to the root.

"Off the road," she said, and her voice came out in the flat tone of command she hadn't used in forty years, the tone of a woman who had once been someone with a rank and a blade and a reason. Edmund went. He didn't question; he read the wind the way she did, or better.

They dropped into the fold of the land where a stream had cut a channel through the peat, dark water ankle-deep, banks high with heather root. Petra crouched, drew Edmund down beside her, and put her hand on the hilt at her hip.

The sword had not left its sheath since the valley. It had not left its sheath, in truth, since before the valley—since the day she had walked through the boundary stones with it bound shut by her own oath, wrapped in words like a wound wrapped in linen. In the magic-dead quiet it had been only metal. On the road it had begun to hum. Now, under her palm, it was *hot*, and it was pulling, the way a dog pulls at a lead when it smells the thing it was made for.

"They've found us," Edmund said. Not in Oathscript. In plain, cracked, ordinary speech—the speech she had coaxed out of him on the third night, word by word, the way you coax a fire in wet wood. He spoke it rarely, and when he did, the world seemed to lean in to listen.

"How many?"

He tilted his head. His eyes had gone the color of the ember-light, faintly, the way water goes the color of the sky. "Seven. Two ahead. Five behind. They ride things that don't breathe."

Petra closed her eyes. She had known, of course. She had known since Hollowbridge, since the bounty notice with the boy's inked likeness and her own name beneath it in a clerk's careful hand. *Petra Halloran. Swordspeaker. Oath-bound.* The past did not stay buried; it rode.

The first of them came over the ridge.

It was not a rider in the way she had imagined riders, all her years in the valley where nothing rode and nothing was ridden. It was a shape of a rider, a suggestion, a cloak the color of cold ash stretched over something that moved wrong—too smooth at the joints, too patient in the saddle. The horse beneath it, if horse it was, made no sound. Its hooves struck the road and the road did not answer. Behind it came the others, strung out along the ridgeline like beads of soot, and the moor's long singing note bent around them and went silent, a silence shaped exactly like five riders and two ahead, closing.

Petra stood up.

"Get behind me," she said. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see. *Behind me.*"

Edmund's hand found the back of her coat and gripped it, small and fierce, and that small fierce weight did more to steady her than forty years of discipline ever had. She walked up out of the stream-cut, out of the heather, onto the open shoulder of the moor, and she put her hand on the hilt of her sword, and she spoke the first word.

Not aloud. Not in the tongue of the valley, the flat practical speech of millers and wardens. She spoke it in Oathscript—the old binding-tongue, the tongue Edmund had carried to her door like a coal carried in cupped hands. She had not spoken it in forty years. She had not been sure she still could.

The word was *wake*.

The sword came out of the sheath like a breath held too long finally let go.

It was not the draw she remembered. It was not a draw at all, really—the blade left the sheath the way light leaves a struck flint, all at once and everywhere, and the moor lit white. Petra stood in the middle of that light holding a sword that was singing, actually singing, a note that answered the moor's long held-breath note and broke it, and she felt—she *felt*, for the first time in four decades, her own power move through her like blood through a limb that had been asleep.

It was joy. She would not lie about that, not even to herself, not even afterward. It was joy, pure and appalling, and it lasted the length of one heartbeat.

Then the first ash-cloaked rider came down the slope at her, and the joy had to become work.

They did not slow. That was the first horror of it—no challenge, no wheeling, no show of force. They came down the slope at a dead, silent gallop as though she were a fence to be jumped, and the leader's hood tipped back and there was no face under it. There was a place where a face should be, and in that place was a slow turning dark, like water over a well, and Petra understood with a lurch that these things were not men in cloaks. They were what was left when the court beneath the mountain took everything a man was and left only the shape of the taking.

The leader's arm came up. In its hand was a hook, black, curved like a question.

Petra spoke the second word.

*Stand.*

The word left her and she felt it leave—not from her mouth but from somewhere deeper, from behind her eyes, from the shelf in her where she kept the memory of her mother's face. It went out of her like a coin out of a purse, and the word became a wall. The air in front of her thickened to a shining pane, and the lead rider struck it and stopped—not thrown, not broken, just *stopped*, the hook a hand's width from her throat, and the thing in the hood leaned against her word the way a man leans against a door.

It leaned, and the word began to bend.

And Petra felt something else, then, something she had not been warned of, not by her mentor, not by any text, not by the oath itself: she felt the word *stand* still existing inside her, still hers, and she felt it *loosen*. It was not spent. It was being *eaten*. The wall was made of her certainty that things could be made to stand, and behind that certainty was a memory—the memory of her mother standing in the doorway of their house on the last morning, hand on the frame, and the wall drank that memory the way the moor drank rain, and the doorway in her mind went empty, and she could not—she reached for the morning, for the frame, for the hand—

Gone. Not dimmed. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone, a smooth absence the tongue returns to again and again.

The rider's hook came on, slower now, grinding against what remained of her word, and Petra did the arithmetic in the space of half a heartbeat: every word is a memory, every memory is a room in the house of me, and I have perhaps a hundred rooms left, and there are seven of them.

She spoke the third word anyway.

*Cleave.*

This one she took from deeper down. She felt it go—her seventh winter, the taste of the honey-bread her mother made once a year, the whole warm room of it, ripped out and forged—and the sword answered. The blade in her hands, which had been a blade, became an *edge*, became the idea of an edge, a line of severance drawn through the world, and when the second rider reached her the sword met the hook and the hook simply ended. Not broke. *Ended*, its length divided into before and after, and the ash-cloak rider came apart along the same line like a page cut with a razor, and what spilled out of it was not blood. It was ash, and a sound—a single thin syllable, a man's name maybe, or the last half of one—and then the wind took even that.

Two more came at once, left and right, silent, patient, and Petra turned in the singing light and understood that she could not win this the way she had once been taught to win things. Her teacher's voice came back to her across forty years—*a swordspeaker does not spend words, she invests them, count the cost before you draw the coin*—and she counted, and the count was terrible, and she spent anyway, because Edmund's hand was on her coat and there were five left and then four.

*Turn.* A memory of rain on the valley stones, the first rain after her arrival, the smell of it—gone, and the rider's charge went wide, its mount slewing as though the moor itself had been told to change its mind.

*Break.* Her mentor's face—she felt it go and grabbed for it and caught only the beard, the grey of the beard, the rest already unraveling—gone, and the third rider's silent horse folded at the knees and did not rise.

*Hold.* The name of the village well-keeper, old Tomas, who had taught her to fish the valley stream and never once asked why she had come—gone, gone, and she was still saying the name in her mind, *Tomas, Tomas*, and it was a sound without a person in it, a shell.

Three riders left. They had stopped. That was worse than the coming. They sat their silent mounts in a half-circle around her at the edge of the sword's light, and the dark where their faces should be turned toward her, and Petra realized they were *waiting*, and she realized why.

They were letting her spend.

The ember-light in Edmund's chest must have been visible over her shoulder now, pulsing with his fear. The hook in the leader's hand turned slowly. They did not need to beat her. They needed only to make her spend herself down to a woman with a sword and no words and no memories, a hollow thing walking, and then take the boy from hands too empty to hold him.

"Petra." Edmund's voice, very small, in plain speech. "Your nose is bleeding."

She touched her upper lip. Blood, hot and ordinary. She was shaking. The sword sang in her grip and she loved it and she hated it, and somewhere in her a room stood empty where her mother's face had been, and she could not even grieve it properly because she could not remember what she was grieving.

*Count the cost before you draw the coin.*

There were three of them. She had perhaps sixty rooms left in the house of herself. And the mountain was still nine days away, and the court beneath it was full of things that had been spending her kind for centuries.

Then the moor's long silent note broke.

It broke from the east—from the ridge behind the riders—and it broke into a word. Not her word. Not Oathscript, exactly, but its older cousin, its wild root, the language Edmund spoke when he wasn't speaking at all. The boy had come out from behind her. Of course he had. He stood on the rise with his arms at his sides and the ember in his chest blazing through his shirt like a lantern, and he said one syllable, and the heather for fifty paces in every direction stood up and *pointed* at the ash-cloaks like ten thousand small accusing fingers.

The riders turned toward him.

And Petra, in the space that their turning opened, made the choice that would define everything after: she took the deepest coin she had, the one she had been saving without knowing she was saving it, the memory at the very bottom of the purse—and she did not spend it on a word of war.

She spent it on a word of running.

*Away.*

She felt it leave, and this time she felt what it took, and she had time, one long falling instant, to know what it was: the memory of why she had come to the valley. Not the fact—she kept the fact, she kept the valley and the years and the oath—but the *reason*, the young woman's burning reason, the face and the promise and the grief that had driven her through the boundary stones forty years ago. It went out of her like heat out of an open door, and the word *away* wrapped itself around her and Edmund like a hand, and the moor folded.

That was the only way she could ever describe it afterward. The moor folded, the way a page folds, and they were not where they had been. They were a mile south and a hundred feet lower, in a peat-hollow the sword's light had never touched, and the last thing she heard from the world above—faint, already receding—was the sound of three silent riders standing motionless on an empty road, and the heather slowly, slowly lowering its ten thousand fingers.

* * *

They walked until dark, because Petra did not trust the folding to hold, and then walked past dark, because Edmund's hand in hers was the only compass she trusted. When at last they made a cold camp in the lee of a stone—one fire, no, no fire, the ash-smell still lived in her nostrils—Petra sat with the sword across her knees and did what she had been avoiding since the ridge.

She counted.

The sword had gone quiet in its sheath, sated and terrible, and she laid her palm flat on it and went through the house of herself room by room, opening doors.

Her mother's face: empty. She knew she had loved her. She knew there had been a doorway, a morning, a hand on a frame. She knew the shape of the absence the way you know the shape of a missing tooth, and the knowing was its own kind of pain, a pain with no picture in it.

The taste of honey-bread: empty. Her mentor's face: empty, except a grey beard floating in the dark like something drowned. The first rain on the valley stones: empty. Old Tomas: a name with no man in it. And at the bottom, the largest absence of all, a whole floor of the house gone dark: the reason. The why. She had come to the valley for a reason that had once been the whole engine of her, and now there was only the fact of her arriving, causeless, like weather.

Edmund watched her from across the dead fire-ring. He was very still, the way he was still when he was listening to something no one else could hear.

"What did it take?" he asked. Plain speech. His plain speech was getting better, and she did not know whether to be proud or afraid.

"Memories," Petra said. "That's the price of the words. Nobody told me. Or—" she laughed, and it came out cracked, "—or somebody told me, and I've spent the telling."

"What did you lose? Just now. The big one."

She looked at him. He was eight years old and barefoot in the cold, with a stolen soul banked in his chest like a hearth-fire, and he was asking her to do the one thing she had sworn, somewhere in the ruins of her oath, never to do: to make him watch her pay.

"A reason," she said at last. "I lost a reason. I don't remember what for."

Edmund was quiet a long time. The ember-light pulsed in his chest, slow, like breathing. Then he said, in Oathscript, one small syllable, and touched the hilt of her sword where it lay across her knees.

The sword hummed. And in the hum, faint as a voice through a wall, Petra heard—she would have sworn it, afterward, though no one could prove it—she heard a fragment of the honey-bread. The warm room. The seventh winter. Held, the way a bank holds a coin. Not returned. *Held.*

The sword was keeping what she spent.

She sat back against the cold stone and looked at the boy, and the boy looked at her, and far off to the north—too far to see, exactly the right distance to feel—the mountain stood under the stars with its mouth closed, waiting.

"We can't fight them every day," Petra said. "There aren't enough of me."

"Then we don't fight," Edmund said. "We get there first."

And in the dark, without meaning to, without deciding to, Petra reached for the memory of her mother's face one more time—and found, in the empty room, a single new thing hanging where the face had been, left behind like a tool after a thief: the knowledge, sudden and total and cold, of *how many words she had left*.

Sixty-one.

She had fifty-nine now. She'd spent two just counting.
