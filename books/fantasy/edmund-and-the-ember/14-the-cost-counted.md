## 14. The Cost Counted

The moor gave them a hollow to shelter in—a fold of earth where the heather grew thick and the wind, for once, had to climb over something before it could reach them. Petra built a fire small enough to hide, cupping the flame with her body the way she'd once cupped it in the valley, back when fire was only fire and not a beacon calling ash-cloaks down the road after them.

Edmund sat close without touching her. He had learned that much about her already: that she did not flinch from him, but that she needed the space around her hands kept clear when she was thinking. He watched her now with those dark, patient eyes—the eyes of a child who had learned early that watching was safer than asking.

She should have asked *him* how he was. His chest still glowed faintly through his shirt when the light dimmed, banked coals under skin. But she knew what she would find if she opened her mouth to speak gently: she didn't trust what came out anymore. Not since the moor road. Not since the harvesters and the words she'd spent like coins from a purse that never refilled.

So instead she took out the little book.

It had been Maren's idea in spirit, though Maren was days behind them on the road with her cart and her map and her stubborn, frightened courage. Petra had found the book in Hollowbridge, a plain thing of gray boards, and had told herself it was for Edmund—for writing down guard-words, for teaching. She had written three words in it. Then she had turned to a fresh page and begun, in small careful letters, an inventory of a different kind.

*What I have paid so far.*

She wrote:

*A face.*

That was all. She could not say whose. She remembered faces the way you remember rooms you've slept in—there had been one, recently lost, that she could almost feel the shape of. A jaw. Something about a jaw, and laughter that came low, from the chest. A man? A woman? The shape of the loss was distinct; the contents were gone. It was like reaching into a pocket and finding the lining torn out. She knew there had been something there. She knew the weight it used to have.

She stared at the word until it blurred.

*Edmund,* she said silently, testing. No—that was intact. His name sat solid in her mind, warm as a hearthstone. She went through others. *Maren.* Intact. Her own mother—she searched, and found the word *mother* and a smell of rosemary and wet wool, but no face attached to any of them. Had the face been her mother's?

Her hand shook. She pressed it flat against her knee until it stopped.

"Petra." He said her name carefully, the way he handled everything of hers, because her name was one of the few plain-speech words he owned. Most of what Edmund said still came out in Oathscript, syllables like stones dropped into deep water, but her name he'd learned to say in the common tongue, softly, with the *r* rolled just slightly wrong.

"I'm all right," she said.

The lie cost nothing. That was the horror of it. Words only cost when they were spoken *with the sword awake*, when they were drawn up out of her like marrow. Ordinary speech was free. She could lie all day for free. It was only truth, shaped into power, that took payment—and the payment was always taken from the same account, the one she couldn't count, the one that held everything she was.

She turned back to the book.

*The taste of bread.*

This one had gone two nights ago, on the moor road, when she'd cut down the first harvester's shadow-blade with a single word that split the dark like a struck bell. She'd known even as the word left her that it was taking something soft and ordinary. She'd woken the next morning hungry, eaten the last of Maren's flatbread, and tasted—nothing. Warmth. Texture. Salt, if salt was counted separately. But bread itself, that particular comfort, that particular *home* of flavors, was simply absent, the way a tooth is absent after it's pulled. Her tongue kept finding the gap.

She had stood in the middle of the moor chewing and trying to remember what she was mourning, and Edmund had watched her do it, and neither of them had said anything.

*My birthday.*

Gone this morning. Gone cheaply, for a ward-word thrown up fast when she'd heard hooves on the far side of the ridge—wasted, really, because the riders had been drovers, not hunters, and the word had hung in the air doing nothing while it ate a day out of her calendar. She no longer knew the day she was born. She knew she was somewhere past forty years. She knew spring had been involved, or she believed it had. Belief is a poor substitute for memory. It's a candle burning in a house where the furniture has been carried out.

She looked at the page. Three entries. Three entries and she had barely begun to fight.

* * *

Edmund had been watching her write for some time before he moved.

He did it the way he did everything—without hurry, without noise. He crossed the space between them and settled beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off his chest through the cooling evening air. The ember's warmth. Liss's warmth. It had a quality no ordinary heat had: it *wanted* things. It leaned toward her like a cat toward a lap.

"What are you writing?" he asked, in the plain tongue, halting. Plain speech still cost him effort the way Oathscript cost her. Each common word seemed to be carried up from somewhere deep, hauled hand over hand.

"It's a list," Petra said.

"What kind of list?"

"The kind merchants keep." She tried to make it light. "Debts."

"Who owes who?"

She almost lied again. The free kind of lie, the cheap kind. *No one owes anyone, it's just practice, just words.* But he was looking at her with his whole face, and lying to him was becoming a thing she did too often, and every lie was a small rehearsal for the larger ones—the ones she'd need below the mountain, or so the old dread whispered.

"I owe," she said. "To the sword. Every time I use it, I pay. And I'm writing down what I've paid so I'll know—" Her voice caught on the arithmetic of it. "So I'll know what's left."

Edmund considered this with the terrible seriousness of children considering adult griefs. Then he said, "Show me."

She showed him.

He read slowly—he read Oathscript fluently, of course, the way fish swim, but her cramped common letters took him longer. She watched his lips move over the entries. *A face. The taste of bread. My birthday.* When he reached the last one he looked up sharply, and there was something new in his expression—not pity, which she could have borne, but a kind of fierce, offended protectiveness, as if someone had stolen from *him*.

"Which birthday?" he demanded.

"I don't know."

"When were you born?"

"I don't know anymore."

"That's—" He stopped. Plain speech failed him; she saw him reach for Oathscript, saw him catch himself, remembering what happened when he spoke it carelessly near her blade. The sword lay across her knees, and at the nearness of true Oathscript it had begun, very faintly, to hum—a sound like a finger run round the rim of a glass. He swallowed the syllables back down. She watched him do it, watched the words die in his mouth for her sake, and something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"You can speak it," she said quietly. "Around me. I'll hold it still." She laid her palm on the flat of the blade, and the humming gentled, sank, became something more like breathing. "I'm its keeper. That's what that means, actually. Nobody ever told me. Keeping means holding things still when they want to sing."

So Edmund spoke.

He spoke in Oathscript because it was easier, and because she had given him leave, and because some part of him—she could see it—needed to say the thing plainly in the only language that felt honest to him. What he said, translated by tone and gesture and the few shared words between them, was this: *It isn't fair. You are paying for me. You are paying for my sister. You didn't sell her. You didn't lose your birthday because of your own choice. Why do the ones who didn't choose it have to pay?*

And Petra, who had spent thirty years believing her oath was a wall that kept magic out, understood suddenly that the oath had never been a wall at all. It had been a promise. And promises, unlike walls, follow you out the door.

"Because that's what keeping is," she said. "Somebody pays. That's the whole of it. The trick—the art—is choosing what you pay *with*. The King below pays with other people's souls. The harvesters pay with other people's lives. I intend to be a cheaper kind of keeper than that."

"How cheap?"

She smiled, and it hurt her face, because smiling was another thing that was starting to feel like it belonged to someone else. "Cheaper than kings. More expensive than I'd like."

* * *

They sat together while the fire burned down to a red eye and the stars came out over the moor, hard and numberless.

After a while Edmund said, "Tell me something you haven't lost yet. So I can help you keep it."

"Keep it?"

"Maren keeps things," he said. "In the valley. She kept my blanket. She kept the bowl I ate from when I first came. If you write things down, I can keep them too. Both of us keeping." He said it simply, the way children say enormous things. "If you start to forget, I'll tell you. Like—" he searched, "—like a second fire. If one goes out."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Then she turned to a clean page and wrote, in careful letters, the beginnings of a different list. Not debts. Holdings.

*I am Petra Vasquez. I am the last swordspeaker. My mother smelled of rosemary and wet wool. I learned my first guard-word at seven, from a woman named Aldith, who had a scar on her left thumb and laughed from her chest.*

She paused at that last line. Laughter from the chest. A jaw. *Oh,* she thought, cold spreading slow through her middle. *Oh, it was Aldith. The face I lost was Aldith.* She had paid away her teacher without ever knowing the coin had left her hand, and only now, reconstructing her from fragments, did she learn what she'd spent. There was a cruelty in it that felt almost designed: the sword took the memory whole, but returned the *fact* of the taking, like a merchant leaving the empty wrapper on the table.

She wrote, beneath it: *Aldith taught me the first guard-word. I no longer know her face. Remember this anyway.*

"There," she said, her voice rougher than she meant it to be. "Now you're keeping her too."

Edmund leaned close and read each line, mouthing them, committing them. Then he pointed at the entry about her mother and made a small gesture—cupping his hands, rocking them. A question.

"Yes," Petra said. "Dead long ago. Before the valley, even." Or she thought so. Or she had always believed so. The candle flickered. She wrote: *Or so I believe. Verify below the mountain, if there is anyone left to ask.*

Below the mountain. The phrase sat on the page like a stone on a grave, and both of them looked at it and then, deliberately, looked away.

* * *

Later, when Edmund slept—the ember in his chest glowing through his shirt like a lantern behind cloth, his breath slow and even, his face in sleep finally looking as young as it was—Petra sat with the book open on her knee and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding.

Three payments made. The road ahead held a court full of harvesters, a King who unraveled, a contract to void, a soul to carry home. How many words would that take? Ten? Twenty? Fifty? She had perhaps—what? She tried to inventory herself the way she'd inventory a larder before winter. Here the memories stood dense as jars: childhood, the valley, the oath, the years of quiet maintenance, the faces of villagers, the taste of forty springs. How many could she spend and still be *her*? Where was the line past which the person paying was no longer the person who'd promised?

She didn't know. That was the worst of it. The sword took randomly, or so it seemed—took the tender things, the homely things, the small warm stones from the bottom of her. It never took the oath. She'd noticed that. It never touched the oath. As if the oath were the one thing nailed down, the one item in the house the thieves respected.

*Because it's not mine,* she thought suddenly. *It never was. It's the mountain's. It's the seal's. It's the reason I was put in that valley like a key hung on a hook.* The thought arrived whole and unwelcome, dressed in the voice of her dead mentor, and she pushed it down into the dark where such thoughts belonged, because tomorrow they would walk again, and tomorrow there might be riders, and tomorrow she might have to buy their lives with another jar off her shelf.

But she added one more line to the holdings-list first, pressing hard enough that the nib nearly tore the paper:

*I chose this. Whatever else is true—whatever the valley was, whatever the mountain wants—I chose to take the boy in. I chose to walk out. Write it down so the sword can't have it. This one is mine.*

She closed the book. For a moment she simply breathed, listening to the moor wind comb the heather, to Edmund's slow sleeping breath, to the ember's faint crackle, almost like a heartbeat, almost like speech.

Then, from the darkness beyond the firelight, came a sound that stopped her breath.

Not hooves. Not voices. A *humming*—low and tuneless, rising from the ground itself, from the old standing stone that jutted from the heather twenty paces off, a stone she'd taken for a natural thing when they'd made camp. It was singing in Oathscript. One word, repeated, patient as water wearing rock.

She knew the word. Every swordspeaker knew it. It was the first word in the first lesson, the word Aldith had taught her at seven, before her thumb had ever held a blade.

The word was *listen*.
