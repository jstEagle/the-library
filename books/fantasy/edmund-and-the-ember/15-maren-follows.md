## 15. Maren Follows

The boundary stones did not stop her. That was the first miracle, though Maren Hollis had stopped believing in miracles somewhere around her thirty-fifth winter and had never quite restarted.

She stood at the edge of the valley with a pack on her back that weighed as much as a sin, and she waited for something to happen — a hum in the teeth, a fog rolling up from the grass, the old familiar pressure that pressed every thought of leaving back down into the chest like a hand on a rising loaf. Forty years she had lived inside that pressure. Forty years she had told herself it was comfort. *The wards keep us safe*, said the elders at every gathering, in the tone people use for prayers they no longer examine. And Maren had nodded along, because nodding was cheaper than thinking, and because her husband had nodded too, right up until the fever took him, and what else does a widow have but the walls her grief was built against?

Nothing happened. The grass bent in the wind. The morning smelled of wet stone and sheep and woodsmoke from the village behind her, already shrinking to a scatter of roofs. She took one step past the last stone and felt — nothing. No wall. No voice. Just the ordinary ache of her knees and the enormous, unedited openness of the world.

"Forty years," she said aloud, to no one, and her voice sounded strange out here, unguarded by the valley's hush, carrying farther than any words she'd spoken in decades. "Forty years of being afraid of an open door."

She walked on before she could talk herself back.

* * *

The road was worse than she remembered, which meant it was better than nothing. She had left in the grey hour before dawn, when even the roosters were ashamed to sing, taking flour, salt pork, dried apples wrapped in cloth, a wool blanket that still smelled faintly of Tam, and the boots he'd worn to his grave because she could not bear to see them idle by the door another season. She had left a note on the table under the salt dish: *Gone after them. Feed the hens or eat them.* Let the elders make of that what they would.

They would make much of it. She knew that. Old Maren Hollis, the miller's widow who kept bees and kept her mouth shut for four decades, walking out of the valley like a girl running off to market. By noon they'd be saying she'd gone mad with grief, grief being the only explanation the village ever offered for anything that broke the pattern. By evening they'd be saying the swordspeaker had bewitched her. By tomorrow they'd have invented a whole liturgy around her departure, and none of it would be true, and all of it would be safer than the truth, which was simply this:

She had seen the boy's chest glowing through his shirt in the firelight, and she had not been able to sleep since.

And beneath that, deeper down, in the silt where a woman keeps the things she has never once dredged up: she remembered Petra Vasquez arriving. Remembered it the way you remember a wound — not the pain of it but the fact of it, permanent as a scar's geography. A young woman, half-dead, standing where Edmund had stood, at the same door, in the same kind of storm. And the elders whispering in the mill that night, and the word they used, the word Maren had carried forty years like a stone in her shoe: *the seal must hold*. As if the woman were weather. As if she were a crack in a dam and not a person bleeding on a doorstep.

Maren had brought bread to Petra the next morning and said nothing about the mill, and had been saying nothing ever since, and there is only so long a silence can be called kindness before it becomes its own kind of betrayal.

The road climbed out of the valley's bowl and into moorland, and the wind came up raw and honest off the grass. Twice she stopped to catch her breath and twice she caught herself listening — for hoofbeats, for wardens, for the ash-cloaked riders the boy drew in the dirt with such careful, terrible precision. But the moor gave her nothing but curlews and cloud-shadow, and so she walked, and the pack straps bit her shoulders, and every mile felt like a tooth coming loose: painful, then strange, then lighter.

Near midday she found the cairn.

It was a small thing, half-swallowed by turf, three stones stacked on the crest of a rise where the road bent toward the river country. Someone had built it deliberately; someone had built it recently. And tucked into the gap between the top two stones, dry despite the night's rain, was a folded square of paper weighted with a pebble.

Maren looked at it for a long time. The wind moved the grass around her ankles. Then she took the paper and opened it, and her breath went out of her all at once.

It was a map. Drawn in brown ink gone the color of dried blood, on paper that had been folded and refolded so many times its creases were soft as cloth. It showed the moor, the river, the ford, and beyond them — beyond anything Maren had ever seen drawn true — the mountain. Not the mountain as the valley's stories drew it, a squat grey molar on the horizon, but the mountain as it must look from close: vast, shouldered, its southern face riddled with galleries marked in a fine, cramped hand. And across the lower slope, in letters pressed hard enough to score the paper, three words:

*THE VALLEY IS THE SEAL.*

Below the map, in the same cramped hand, a note. The writing slanted left, hurried but certain:

*Maren — if this is you, and I think it will be, because you are the only one in that valley who ever looked at me like I was a person and not a problem to be stored — then you've already done the hardest thing, which is leave.*

*Follow the river east to the split willow. We move slow. He's small and she won't say it, but she's frightened, and I know the look because I wore it once.*

*I'm sorry I never told you more. I couldn't. The wards listen — you knew that, didn't you? Everyone knows it and no one says it. But I'm past their hearing now, and so are you, and there are things you deserve to hear. Ask me when we're together. I'll tell you everything I remember, including why your grandmother's name is written on a wall below the mountain.*

*Burn this after reading.*

*A.*

Maren sat down on the cold turf without meaning to. Her legs had decided for her.

Aldous. Aldous Wren, who had taught letters to half the valley's children and vanished one autumn twenty years ago, leaving his cottage swept and his inkwell full and not so much as a bootprint pointing anywhere. The elders had said he'd gone to die among strangers, as old teachers sometimes did. The children had made stories: taken by fairies, fallen down a well, turned into a heron. Maren had always privately suspected something duller and sadder.

But here was his hand. She would know it anywhere; he had corrected her letters when she was nine years old, patient as rain. *Your grandmother's name.* Her grandmother, Sorrel Hollis, dead sixty years, who had grown herbs and hummed songs in a dialect nobody in the valley spoke and had died quietly and been buried quietly and never once been mentioned in any story worth telling.

*Including why your grandmother's name is written on a wall below the mountain.*

Maren folded the map along its soft creases and put it inside her coat, over her heart, because that seemed suddenly like the correct place for it. Then she stood, and shouldered the pack, and looked east along the road where the land fell away toward the glittering thread of the river.

"Burn it," she said to the empty moor. "Yes. After."

She was, she reflected, already becoming a liar out here. It took remarkably little time.

* * *

She caught sight of them at dusk, and did not announce herself at once.

Not from caution — or not only. From something shyer. For forty years Maren Hollis had watched people from the edges of rooms: the mill floor, the well, the back pews at gatherings. Watching was her craft and her shelter. But watching these two was different, the way looking at a fire in a grate is different from looking at a burning house.

Petra had made camp in the lee of a drystone wall, a small fire going, and she was standing very still with her sword drawn — actually drawn, out of its sheath, the blade catching the firelight — while Edmund stood opposite her holding a stick like a sword, moving through some slow pattern of steps. Every few movements the child would speak a syllable, two syllables, that round old-sounding language that had made the elders' faces go grey, and each time he spoke, the sword in Petra's hands would flare faintly along its fuller, like a coal breathed upon. And each time, Petra would wince. Not at the light. At something behind her eyes. Maren watched her shake her head slightly after one word, watch her mouth the same word silently, testing, and come up with nothing, and set her jaw against the loss.

Paying for something. Maren didn't know what. But she knew the arithmetic of cost when she saw it; she'd done it herself for forty years, subtracting pieces of herself to keep the peace of the valley.

Edmund saw her first. Children always did; they hadn't yet learned to stop looking at horizons. He went rigid, stick lowering, and tugged Petra's sleeve, and Petra turned with a speed that would have been frightening if Maren hadn't spent a lifetime reading fear in people's bodies and recognizing, now, its cousin in the swordspeaker's stance.

"Maren?" Petra said. The sword went back in its sheath with a click that sounded almost embarrassed. "What in the world—"

"Hens ate," Maren said. She came down the slope, joints complaining, and dropped the pack by the fire with a thump that announced her intentions better than any speech. "I fed them before I left. Well. Some of them. The rest are the village's problem now." She held out the sack of provisions like a tithe. "Flour. Salt pork. Apples. Tam's boots, if either of you can fit them, which the boy might, growing like a weed."

"You left the valley," Petra said slowly, as if translating from a difficult language. "You. Left."

"I did notice that, yes."

"The wards—" Petra began, and stopped, because whatever she'd been about to say was clearly a thing she herself did not believe anymore. Maren filed that away. The wards. Something had happened at those stones, then, or failed to. Something Petra had expected and Maren had feared for forty years and both of them had walked through anyway like women stepping over a threshold that turned out to be painted on.

"The wards let me pass," Maren said. "Or there were never any wards worth the name. Or—" and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, roughening, "—or I've finally stopped caring, which took me forty years, and I'd thank you not to make me defend the decision while my knees are still shaking."

Edmund laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, like a gate opened after a season — and Maren saw Petra flinch again at the sound of it, gently, as if laughter itself had become a thing the child rationed. He came and stood beside her, close enough that she could see the faint warmth under the skin of his throat, the ember-light banked low like a lamp trimmed for sleep.

"You knew him," he said. Not a question. Children and their terrible directness. "The teacher. You came for him too."

Maren crouched, which cost her, so that their eyes were level. Up close the boy smelled of road dust and woodsmoke and, underneath it, something faintly mineral, like a sun-warmed hearthstone. His eyes were the brown of deep water.

"I came for you," she said. "Both of you. Him too, if he'll have me, but mostly you, because somebody followed that woman off a cliff once and I wasn't going to watch it happen twice." She glanced up at Petra, whose expression had gone carefully neutral in the way of people who have just been seen straight through. "Aldous Wren left a map on the moor cairn, addressed to me, mentioning my grandmother, whom I have never once mentioned to a living soul. So either the man has been dead twenty years and is still teaching lessons, or I've gone mad, and I'd prefer company in my madness."

"He's alive," Edmund said.

Petra said nothing, which was louder.

"He's alive," Edmund repeated, with the flat certainty children deploy against adult disbelief. "He's below. In the dark part. He talks to the lamps."

"Edmund," Petra said softly, warningly, and the boy shut his mouth, but not before Maren had seen the shape of the sentence he'd swallowed: *he told me you'd come.*

The wind moved the fire sideways. Somewhere far off, a fox screamed once and thought better of it. Maren lowered herself onto Tam's blanket by the flames, unpacked the apples, and began cutting one with her belt knife into slices, because there was no situation so dire that a sliced apple didn't improve the margins of it. This had been her mother's doctrine and it had survived two funerals and one apocalypse-adjacent Tuesday and it would survive this too.

"Eat," she said, handing slices around. "Then you'll tell me everything, starting with why the boy glows, and ending with why drawing that sword makes you look like someone's picking coins out of your pocket. And then—" she pointed the knife at the darkness eastward, where the mountain would be if the land weren't in the way, "—then we'll decide how far this foolish walk goes, and whether old women count as soldiers or baggage."

"We're not soldiers," Petra said. There was something raw under the flatness of it. "We're a debt collector and a debt, walking uphill."

"Then I'm the interest," Maren said. "Eat your apple."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Edmund giggled — actually giggled, a real one, brief and startled out of him — and the sound cracked something open in the little circle of firelight, and Petra sat down heavily on the ground across from her, and for a while the three of them ate apples in the dark like the simplest family in the world.

Later, when Edmund slept with his head against Maren's pack and the ember in his chest pulsed slow as a sleeping heart, Petra fed sticks to the fire and talked. About the oath. About the voice in the boy's chest begging in a dead woman's dialect. About the wardens at the ford, and the lie, and the flicker in the valley's silence that had told her the valley knew. About the word-costs of the blade — a birthday gone, a face gone, the taste of bread gone, recited in a voice that tried very hard to be an inventory and mostly succeeded.

Maren listened the way she had listened her whole life, fully and silently, and when Petra finished, Maren reached over and put her hand briefly on the younger woman's wrist.

"My grandmother hummed songs in that dialect," she said. "Sorrel Hollis. And when I asked her where she learned them, she looked at the mountain and said, *from before the quiet*, and never said another word about it for thirty years." She withdrew her hand and looked into the fire. "This valley doesn't forget, Petra. It's *made* to forget. There's a difference, and I think you've started learning it, and I think Aldous knew it before either of us was born."

Petra stared at her. In the firelight her face was stripped of everything but the question.

"What did Aldous know?"

Maren reached into her coat and drew out the map, and unfolded it along its soft old creases, and turned it toward the firelight, and watched Petra read the words scored across the mountain's flank.

*THE VALLEY IS THE SEAL.*

The fire popped. Edmund stirred in his sleep, and murmured something in Oathscript — one small round word — and along the wall behind them, unseen in the dark, every stone in the old drystone dyke rang once, faintly, like a struck bell, and went silent again.

Petra rose slowly to her feet, staring south, back the way they had come, toward the valley neither of them could see anymore.

"That," she said quietly, "was not supposed to follow us."

Maren looked at the sleeping boy, at the warm gold light breathing under his skin, faster now than it had been, like a heart quickening toward something it remembered.

"No," she said. "I don't suppose it was."
