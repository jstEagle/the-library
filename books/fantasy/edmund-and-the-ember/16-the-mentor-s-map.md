## 16. The Mentor's Map

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Maren unwrapped the bundle.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border ruins that dotted the moor like broken teeth. Petra had chosen it for the sightlines—two hundred years of habit did not leave a body just because the body left the valley—and Edmund had chosen it for the way the wind moved through the empty arrow-slits, whistling low notes that he answered with syllables of his own until the tower seemed to hum along. The boy was asleep now, curled against Maren's cloak with his chest rising and falling in slow ember-light, each breath dimming and brightening like something breathing under ash.

Maren sat across the fire from Petra, her hands folded around a roll of oilcloth tied with waxed cord. She had been holding it since sundown. Petra had watched her not mention it through supper, through the banking of the fire, through an hour of silence that had once, in another life, been comfortable between them.

"You've been carrying that since the ford," Petra said.

"Since before the ford." Maren's voice was rough from the road. Forty years of mill-dust and quiet had given her a voice like a stone dragged over stone, but there was something underneath it now that hadn't been there in the valley—something that had woken up when she'd crossed the boundary stones behind them, weeping and laughing at once because the world outside was so *loud*, so *colored*, so much more than forty years of fear had let her believe. "I carried it for three days before I followed you. Sat on my kitchen floor with it in my lap and told myself I was too old, too tired, too afraid. Then I got up and packed bread."

"What is it?"

"Yoran gave it to me." She said the name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "The winter before he walked out of the valley and never came back. He said—if she ever leaves, if she ever crosses the line, give her this. He said you'd know what to do with it." Maren's mouth twisted. "I thought he meant a letter. A confession. Something I could burn and be done with. It took me twenty years to open it myself, and when I did—" She stopped. Her thumb moved over the cord. "It wasn't for me at all. It was just waiting. Like everything he ever did. Just waiting for someone else to need it."

Petra's chest tightened. Yoran. Her mentor. The man who had found her half-dead at the valley's edge thirty years ago, who had taught her the forms with a wooden sword because steel would have answered, who had taught her silence instead of speech, patience instead of power. Who had told her, on the last night she saw him: *The oath is not a chain, child. It is a hinge. Remember which side of the door you're standing on.*

She had spent decades thinking she understood him.

"Give it here," she said.

* * *

The oilcloth was stiff with age and smelled of tallow and cedar, the smell of Yoran's workroom, and for a moment Petra was thirty years younger, sitting cross-legged on those cold flagstones while an old man with ink-stained fingers showed her how to hold a blade without wanting it.

Inside the cloth was a map.

Not parchment—skin. She knew it at once, the fine grain of it, the way Oathscript scribes had once prepared their surfaces, and her stomach turned even as her fingers recognized the craft. Someone had loved this document enough to make it permanent. The map was drawn in iron-gall ink gone brown with years, and then overdrawn—overdrawn again and again—in a dozen different hands, some faded to ghosts, some still sharp. Yoran's hand she knew immediately: the small precise letters, the little hooked serifs. But beneath his writing were older layers, and beneath *those* were older still, lines so faint they might have been drawn in breath.

And the mountain—

Petra had seen the mountain every day of her life. It stood at the valley's southern rim, black against the sky, and everyone in the valley called it the King's Keep, or the Old Fortress, or simply the Rock, the way people name things they have decided not to think about. On every map she had ever seen—the ones in the wardens' station, the ones in Maren's almanacs—it was drawn as a fortress. Square. Walled. Crowned with towers.

This map drew it true.

The mountain was a mouth. There was no other word for what the cartographer had rendered: a vast open jaw of stone, lips of ridgeline, a throat descending in rings into darkness, and around the whole of it, drawn in careful cross-hatching, the suggestion of teeth—not rock formations, not crags, but *teeth*, long and curved and closed almost shut, the way a mouth closes around something it means to keep.

"The seal," Petra read aloud, and her voice came out strange.

Her finger had found the valley. There it was, rendered small and exact, the river, the mill, the boundary stones—and written across it in Yoran's hand, in letters pressed deep enough to score the skin: **THE SEAL**. Not *the sanctuary*. Not *the refuge*. Not any of the words the valley used for itself, the gentle words, the words that had let thirty generations sleep soundly inside their circle of forgetting.

The seal.

Below it, smaller, in the same deep-pressed hand: **AND THE KEY STANDS IN THE DOORWAY.**

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the moor a nightbird called, a long falling note, and Edmund stirred in his sleep and murmured a word in Oathscript—a guard-word, one of the ones Petra had taught him, spoken soft and wrong and perfect—and the embers flared briefly green before settling.

Petra did not look up from the map. She could not have looked up if the tower fell.

She turned the skin over. The reverse held more: columns of notes, dates, names, a chronology scratched down by successive keepers of whatever truth this was. The oldest entries were in a script she only half recognized—early Oathscript, the ceremonial hand, the kind carved into sword-hilts and door-lintels in the age before the valley forgot itself. She could read fragments. *Bound.* *Fed.* *Sealed at cost of—* the rest eaten by time or deliberately scraped away.

Then the hands changed. Later scripts. Later centuries. And always, generation after generation, the same refrain appearing like a chorus:

*A keeper is placed within the seal.*
*The keeper must not draw.*
*The keeper must not speak.*
*While the key holds still, the mouth stays shut.*

Petra read it four times. Each time the words arranged themselves the same way, and each time she hoped, desperately, stupidly, that they would arrange themselves differently.

A keeper is placed within the seal.

Not called. Not chosen. *Placed.* The way you place a wedge. The way you place a lock-bolt. She thought of herself at nineteen, feverish and half-mad at the valley's edge, and Yoran finding her, and the village taking her in with that particular wariness she had mistaken all these years for distrust of outsiders. They hadn't distrusted her because she was strange. They had distrusted her because they *knew*. Some part of the village's long memory, deeper than the wards, deeper than the forgetting, had known exactly what had been installed among them, and had kept its distance the way you keep your distance from a blade lying edge-up on a table.

She thought of the ritual. Every morning, the maintenance of a sword she could not draw. Every morning, oiling the scabbard, checking the bindings, speaking no word of power, keeping the blade asleep. She had called it discipline. She had called it devotion. She had built her entire self upon it—*I am the last swordspeaker; my restraint is my service*—and it had felt like meaning, it had felt like the purest kind of purpose, and all the while—

All the while she had been a bolt slid home in a lock. A key kept still. Her whole life of renunciation had not been her sacrifice offered up. It had been her function performed. Someone had needed a swordspeaker who would never speak, planted in the valley above the mouth, holding the door shut with the sheer weight of her unused self, and Yoran—or whoever Yoran answered to—had made sure she never learned it.

Because a key that knows it is a key can turn.

* * *

"Petra."

She started. Maren was watching her, and the widow's face in the firelight was older than forty years, older than the valley, lined with the specific grief of someone who has just watched another person be robbed.

"How long have you known?" Petra asked. Her voice sounded like someone else's.

"I didn't. Not truly." Maren leaned forward. "But I remembered things, after I opened it. Little things I'd shoved down where I couldn't hear them. Yoran coming to the elders' meeting the year you arrived—I wasn't supposed to be there, I was young, I was fetching ale—and him saying, *the arrangement holds, the girl suspects nothing, and it must stay that way.* And old Tam Voss asking, *and if she draws? If she ever speaks?* And Yoran going quiet, that terrible quiet of his, and saying, *then the mountain wakes, and God help us all.*" Maren's hands were shaking. "I told myself I'd dreamed it. I told myself for thirty years I'd dreamed it. Because the alternative was that our whole valley, our whole *peace*, was built on a lie and a locked-up girl, and I couldn't—"

"You could have told me."

"When?" Maren's voice cracked, sharp with old guilt. "You'd have crossed the line the same day. You'd have gone straight to that thing below the ground with your sword singing, and either it would have eaten you or the seal would have broken, and either way—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. When she spoke again it was barely a whisper. "Either way, Edmund's sister burns a little faster. That's what I know now that I didn't know then. Now I know what's down there. Now I know why they built the cage."

Petra looked at the sleeping boy. The ember-light in his chest pulsed, slow and patient, and she thought of the voice she'd heard through it—that thin begging voice in her mother's dialect—and of the contract waiting somewhere below the mountain with her own signature aged backward on it, and of the harvester captain's offer on the harbor wall, and of every step of this road that had led, with the inevitability of water finding its level, toward the mouth in the stone.

The map lay open between them. In the firelight the mountain's teeth seemed almost to gleam.

"They didn't build the valley to protect us," Petra said. "Or to protect me. They built it to protect *him*—from himself, maybe, or from us, I don't even know yet. And they put me inside it the way you put a stone on a grave. To keep something down." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Thirty years. I thanked them. I *thanked* them, Maren. I knelt at the anniversary rite every year and gave thanks for my sanctuary, for my quiet, for the gift of a place where my curse couldn't follow me—"

"It wasn't all a lie," Maren said fiercely. "Listen to me. The peace was real. The bread was real, the seasons were real, the people were real. Whatever they built it *for*, we lived in it, and it was good, and you were good in it. Don't let dead men's ink take that from you too."

But that was precisely the wound, Petra thought. Not that the valley was false—but that it was real, and had been real *as a cage*, and she had loved it. You could forgive a lie. It was harder to forgive a beautiful truth that had been built around you like a wall, furnished like a home, warmed like a hearth, so that you would never once feel the bars.

Yoran's face rose in her memory: patient, tired, ink-stained, kind. *The oath is not a chain, child. It is a hinge.* She had thought it wisdom. Now she heard it for what it must have been—a handler's instruction to a lock. *Remember which side of the door you're standing on.* As if her whole existence were a matter of doors. As if she had never once been asked whether she wanted to be a door at all.

The betrayal was not that he had lied to her. The betrayal was that every kindness had been true, and every kindness had also been a bar.

* * *

Edmund woke near midnight.

He woke the way he always did—suddenly, completely, no drifting—and sat up and looked at the map spread between the two women, and then at Petra's face, and whatever he saw there made him crawl across the cold ground to sit against her side. He didn't ask questions. He never asked questions. He simply leaned his warm weight against her arm, ember-light seeping through his shirt, and waited.

Petra looked down at him. At this child who had swallowed his sister's soul to keep it from being taken, who had walked barefoot out of a storm to the one door in the world that was built to stay shut, who had spoken the old tongue without knowing its price. The wards had pulled him in. Of course they had. The seal had felt a soul pass its boundary and had reached for the nearest keeper, the nearest key, the way a lock reaches for the shape that fits it. Even his arrival had been the mechanism working. Even his small burning life had been, in some sense, the door testing its hinges.

Unless.

She picked up the map again and held it close to the coals, tilting the skin so the light raked across the oldest layer, the ghost-script beneath everything. And there—so faint she had missed it twice before, hidden in the cross-hatching of the throat—she found a line she had not seen. Not Oathscript. Older. Drawn in something that glittered faintly, as if mixed with metal, or with ash that had never fully cooled.

She could not read it. But she could see what it pointed to: a mark deep in the lowest ring of the throat, a small circle with a line through it, repeated three times. And beside it, in Yoran's hand, one final note—the last thing he had written on the skin, the ink still sharp after all these years, as if he had added it the very week he walked out of the valley and into the dark:

**If she reads this, then the key has turned already, and I am sorry, and it was always going to come to this. What is bound in the third ring is not what the ledger says. Go down and see. Do not sign anything.**

*Do not sign anything.*

Petra sat very still. The fire ticked. Out on the moor, far off, something howled—or sang; at this distance, on this road, she could no longer tell the difference—and the sound rolled over the ruined tower and died among the hills.

"Maren," she said quietly. "How many days to the mountain?"

The widow followed her gaze to the map, to the jaw of stone with its ringed throat, and Petra watched her arrive at the same arithmetic, watched her shoulders square themselves the way they did when the millstone jammed and there was nothing to do but lean.

"Four days, if the weather holds," Maren said. "Three, if it doesn't and we don't stop to care."

Edmund tugged Petra's sleeve. When she looked down, he was pointing—at the map, at the third ring, at the small circled mark—and then at his own chest, where the ember glowed steady and gold. His eyes were wide and awake and utterly unafraid, and he said one word in the old tongue, soft as a coal settling:

*Home.*

Petra folded the map along its old creases, wrapped it in its oilcloth, and tied the cord the way Yoran had tied it, thirty years and a lifetime ago. The dead man had left her a lock, a lie, and an apology. It would have to be enough.

"We walk at first light," she said.
