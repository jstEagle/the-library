## 17. City of Ledgers

Grayharbor announced itself by smell before sight: brine and tar and something sweeter underneath, like candlewax burning in a closed room. Petra smelled it from the crest of the last hill, where the road bent around a stand of wind-bent pines, and her stomach tightened in a way that had nothing to do with hunger.

"There it is," Maren said beside her, shifting the pack on her shoulders. The widow had walked three days without complaint, but her feet were blistered and her patience was a thinning soup. "Bigger than I imagined."

"Bigger than anyone imagines," Sefa said. The scribe had not spoken much since the moor; she walked like a woman returning to a house she'd been thrown out of, eyes down, hands folded into her sleeves as though to keep them from touching anything that might remember her.

Below them the city spread along its bay like spilled ink drying. Grayharbor did not gleam. Its roofs were slate and shingle, its walls granite furred with salt-moss, and even at this distance Petra could see the fog that never fully lifted from its streets—a low, woolen haze that softened every edge and made the whole city look like something remembered rather than seen. Ships crowded the harbor mouth, masts bare as winter trees. And rising from the city's heart, taller than any temple spire, stood a single tower of black stone, windowless, crowned with a roof of green copper gone to verdigris.

Edmund stopped walking. He had been chattering all morning—that strange, weighted speech of his, syllables that made the air feel thicker after they passed—but now he went silent, and his hand found Petra's without looking for it.

"What is that tower?" she asked.

Sefa followed their gaze. "The Hall of Debts," she said. "Every contract ever struck in the northern kingdoms is written there. Every debt of coin, of labor, of blood." She paused, and when she continued her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reciting something they'd once been forced to memorize. "And of soul. There are ledgers below the waterline, in vaults cut into the harbor rock, where they keep the pages no one is meant to read. Soul-contracts. Sales. Pledges. The court beneath the mountain keeps copies, but the originals—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"The originals are ours," Petra said. "If we're going to void Liss's sale, we need the page itself. You said so on the moor."

"I said it was impossible," Sefa said.

"You said it was impossible *legally*."

Sefa's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first she'd shown in days. "Yes. Well. I was a legal creature once. It didn't end well."

* * *

They came down into the city through the Salt Gate in the late afternoon, joining a river of carts and fishwives and pilgrims, and Petra felt the change immediately—the way she always felt it now, outside the valley, where words had weight. A beggar called "coin, kind souls" and the coins in Petra's pocket actually shifted, rattling toward her hand before she caught them. A mother scolded her child and the child's lip stopped bleeding. Nothing dramatic. Everything slightly *obedient*. In Grayharbor, it seemed, language had been taxed so long that even casual speech paid a small tithe.

"Careful what you say here," Sefa murmured, noticing Petra's flinch. "The city has customs. Compliment a merchant's wares and you may find them in your pack, charged to your account. Curse a rival and the curse gets filed. There are clerks whose whole office is listening at corners for unguarded speech." She nodded toward a narrow man in gray robes standing beneath an awning, quill poised over a slate, head tilted toward a pair of arguing sailors. "Debt-listeners. They transcribe anything that sounds like a promise."

Petra touched Edmund's shoulder and steered him close. "Say nothing you don't mean," she told him quietly. "Not here."

He looked up at her with those dark, ember-lit eyes—there was a glow behind them now, faint but constant, visible only when the light was wrong—and he pressed his lips together solemnly. Then, because he was eight years old and could not help himself, he whispered: "*Small words. Safe words.*"

The nearest lamplight flickered brighter. Somewhere across the street, a shutter banged open and a woman leaned out, sniffing the air, confused by a sudden smell of rain that wasn't coming.

"*Edmund.*"

He grinned, entirely unrepentant, and Petra felt the familiar ache-and-warmth that had become her constant companion since the valley: terror and tenderness braided so tight she could no longer tell which was holding up which.

* * *

They lodged at an inn called the Drowned Bell, chosen by Sefa because, she said, its owner owed her nothing and hated everyone equally, which made him safe. The room was small and smelled of tallow and old rope, and through its one window Petra could see the black tower against the darkening sky, its copper crown catching the last light like a flame about to gutter.

She sat up long after the others slept—Maren snoring gently, Edmund curled against the widow's side as though he'd known her forty years instead of forty days—and studied the tower until her eyes watered.

"It watches back, doesn't it," Sefa said softly. She hadn't slept either; she sat cross-legged on the floor with her back to the wall, sharpening a letter-opener on a whetstone with the absent rhythm of a habit older than thought.

"Does it?"

"Everything in this city watches. But the Hall—" Sefa set down the blade. "I worked there eleven years. Third rank, contracts of conveyance. Do you know what I did the day they revoked my credentials?"

"No."

"I corrected an error." She laughed, short and bitter. "A merchant's daughter had been pledged for her father's debts. Standard practice. But the term was written wrong—ninety-nine years instead of nine. Clerical slip. I flagged it. I brought it to my superior with the correction drafted in proper form." Her whetstone hand had begun moving again, though the blade lay still. "Ninety-nine years is harvestable. Nine is not. Someone wanted that girl harvested, and it wasn't her father who signed the amendment. I never learned who. I only learned that by morning my seal was ash, my name was struck from the rolls, and there was a notice with my face on it offering ten crowns for information regarding my whereabouts."

Petra said nothing. Outside, the fog thickened, swallowing the tower's base first, then its middle, until only the green crown floated above the white like a lily pad on milk.

"So when I say the Hall cannot be breached," Sefa went on, "understand that I say it as a woman who spent eleven years learning exactly how it cannot be breached. Four gates. The outer two are guards and paperwork. The inner two are worse. The third gate opens only to living credentials—mine are dead, and dead credentials are kept on file, which means the door will know me and refuse me *by name*. And the fourth gate, the one down to the soul-vaults under the harbor—" She shook her head. "That one predates the city. It predates the kingdom. The stone it's cut from isn't stone. I've stood in front of it. It hums."

Petra's skin prickled. She glanced at the small shape asleep against Maren's side, at the faint coal-glow pulsing under his nightshirt with each breath, slow and patient as a sleeping heart.

"Hums," Petra repeated.

"In Oathscript," Sefa said. "Old dialect. I recognized perhaps one word in twenty, and the word I recognized was *open*."

* * *

In the morning, fog lying in the streets like spilled milk, they went to see the thing they intended to rob.

There was no way to approach the Hall of Debts casually—it dominated every avenue the way a scar dominates a face—but Sefa took them the long way regardless, through the fish market and the ropewalks, past the counting-houses with their brass plaques, until they stood in a cobbled square directly beneath the tower's western face and Petra could crane her neck at it properly.

Up close it was worse. The black stone drank the daylight. No windows, no ornament, only the great bronze doors at ground level, thirty feet tall, worked with reliefs of figures handing things to other figures: coins, sheaves, children, flames. Every figure giving wore a smile. Every figure receiving had no face at all.

Above the doors, in letters of hammered silver, ran the city's oldest motto. Petra couldn't read it, but Edmund could—or rather, his lips moved silently over it, translating, and his hand crept into hers and gripped hard.

"What does it say?" she asked.

Sefa answered for him, her voice very quiet. "*All That Is Owed Is Kept.*"

"And we want to take something out of there," Maren said flatly. She had her arms crossed, her widow's shawl pulled tight, and she was glaring up at the tower with the expression of a woman who had spent forty years being afraid of things and had recently decided to stop. "A page. One page, out of a building full of every debt in the world, guarded by clerks and listeners and a door that hums in a dead language."

"Yes," Petra said.

"And if we're caught?"

Petra watched a line of petitioners queueing at the bronze doors: a farmer clutching a mortgage, a shipwright with a lien, a thin young man holding a paper that trembled in his grip like a live bird. All of them waiting, patient as livestock, to be recorded. To be *kept*.

"If we're caught," Petra said slowly, "they'll take Edmund. Or whatever's in Edmund. And they'll take me, because there's apparently a bounty with my old name on it, and I'd very much like to know why before anyone collects it." She turned to Sefa. "You said your credentials are dead but on file. Dead things can be resurrected. Can a revoked seal be forged?"

Sefa stared at her. "You're asking me to counterfeit the authority of the Hall of Debts."

"I'm asking what you'd need."

For a long moment the scribe said nothing. Then, slowly, she reached into her sleeve and drew out a stub of wax-stained wood—a seal-handle with the impression broken off, carried eleven years like an amputated limb. "Wax of the right grade. A press cut from memory—I remember every line of my mark, I drew it ten thousand times. And access to the second gate, which means bribing or deceiving a gate-warden, which means money we don't have and a story good enough to survive a debt-listener's ear." She looked down at the broken seal, then up, and something in her face shifted—the disgraced clerk falling away and something harder, hungrier, taking its place. "It can be done. It should not be done. Those are different things, and I have spent eleven years learning the difference, and I find I no longer care."

Maren snorted. "Welcome to the company."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and the city spent the day watching them, or so it seemed to Petra. At the wax-chandler's, a debt-listener lingered too near while Sefa asked—in careful, meaningless phrases—about grades of sealing wax. In the tavern at dusk, a gray-robed man sat too long over a cup, quill idle, eyes moving. Petra learned to speak in weather and prices, to make every sentence a locked box, and she hated it: the whole city was a throat, and every word risked becoming a hook in it.

But it was Edmund, at the window of their room that night, who found the crack in the wall.

"Petra." His voice, when he used plain speech, was rare and precious and always slightly startling—rougher than his Oathscript, more human, more *his*. "Come look."

She came. Below, in the fog-bound street, torchlight moved in procession: six figures in ash-gray cloaks, walking in perfect step, and between them a seventh figure stumbling, wrists bound, hooded. The queue of petitioners by the bronze doors parted without being asked. The great doors opened for the procession alone, and swallowed it, and closed.

"A debtor," Sefa said behind them, grim. "Or a thief. Or someone whose name was on a list. It happens weekly. The Hall sends collectors for those who default on... unusual obligations." She was silent a moment. "Or for those who run."

Petra's arm was around Edmund's shoulders. She could feel the ember's heat through his shirt, steady, alive, impatient. Somewhere under that black stone, in a vault cut into harbor rock, a page described everything that had been taken from this boy—and somewhere above that page, in galleries she refused yet to imagine, the thing that had taken it was waiting.

"We go in tomorrow," she said.

Sefa looked at her. "Through four gates."

"No." Petra watched the fog close over the place where the procession had vanished, and felt her sword, wrapped and bound in her pack, give a single low hum—like a held breath. Like anticipation. "Through three. The fourth door hums in Oathscript, and I know someone who speaks it better than the people who built it."

Edmund looked up at her. In the dark, the glow behind his eyes brightened—not fear, not quite. Recognition. As if some part of him had known, since the night he arrived barefoot at her door, that every road in this world ended at a door, and that doors were what the two of them were for.

"Tomorrow," he echoed, in plain speech, and the word settled over the room like a vow.

Outside, the green crown of the Hall of Debts burned through the fog, patient as a creditor, keeping all that was owed—including, soon, Petra understood, whatever price they would have to pay to take one page of it back.
