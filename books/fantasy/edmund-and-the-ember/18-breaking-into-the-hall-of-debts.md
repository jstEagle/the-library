## 18. Breaking Into the Hall of Debts

The Hall of Debts did not look like a place where souls were bought and sold. It looked like a place where rain was counted.

That was Sefa's first thought every time she saw it, and she had seen it ten thousand times in eleven years—first as a scribe of the third bench, then the second, then, for one brief and ruinous season, the first, before her name was struck from the rolls and her seal was broken in front of her on the marble floor. The building squatted at the head of Grayharbor's ledger-square like a fat grey ledger itself, its walls dressed in slate so fine-grained that in wet weather the whole façade seemed to be writing something. Water ran down it in thin ruled lines. The harbor folk said the Hall drank the rain and recorded it. Sefa had once believed that was a joke.

Now she stood across the square in the shadow of a chandler's awning, hood up, watching her old life drip.

"You're certain about the door," Petra said beside her. The swordspeaker's voice was low and even, the voice of someone who had learned to make questions sound like statements so that no one could refuse them.

"I'm certain about *my* door," Sefa said. "The east entrance, the one for clerks and carriers. My credentials were revoked, not erased. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole reason I'm here." She flexed her right hand inside her glove, where the ghost of a signet ring still itched. "They break the seal. They don't break the *habit*. The door remembers me whether the Hall wants it to or not."

"That's either very reassuring or very frightening," said Maren, the miller's widow, from Petra's other side. She had a bundle of rope and oiled canvas on her back and the expression of a woman who had spent forty years being afraid and had recently decided to spend the rest of her life being something else instead. "Which is it?"

"Yes," Sefa said.

Maren snorted. Sefa found, to her ongoing surprise, that she liked the widow. There was no transaction in her anywhere. Sefa had spent her whole career among people who were always, in some ledger or other, buying or being bought, and Maren was the first person she'd met in years whose accounts all balanced at zero. It was restful. It was also, Sefa suspected, a kind of madness. Nobody balanced at zero. Not for long.

The third member of their company said nothing at all, because the third member of their company never said anything that Sefa could understand.

Edmund stood at the edge of the awning's shadow with his bare toes just touching the wet cobbles, staring at the Hall with an intensity that made the back of Sefa's neck prickle. The boy had no shadow in the ordinary sense—she had checked, twice, in the lamplight of the safehouse, and what fell behind him was less a darkness than a *hesitation*, as if the light couldn't quite decide what he was. In his chest, behind his ribs, something burned with a slow banked light that showed faintly through his shirt when he breathed in. The ember. The soul. Liss.

He was looking at the Hall of Debts the way a man looks at the house where his sister is being kept.

"All right," Sefa said, and heard her own voice go crisp and official, the bench-voice, the third-bench-then-second-bench-then-first voice, and hated how good it felt. "The rules. We go in at the change of the night-watch, which is in"—she squinted at the bell tower—"a quarter hour. The east door takes my old credentials for exactly as long as it takes the door-warden to cross the vestibule for his tea, which is ninety seconds, because I counted for six years. We are in the vestibule, through the second door, and down the record-stair before he sets down his cup. After that, no one looks at the deep stacks until morning. The night-clerks are three: one asleep, one gambling in the cellar, one honest, and the honest one is blind and works by touch, so we do not speak in the deep stacks. *No one speaks.*" She looked at Edmund. "Especially not you."

Edmund turned his head and looked at her. He had grey eyes, the boy, grey as slate, and when he looked at you directly it was like being read aloud from.

"I know," Sefa said, defensive. "I know what your words do. I translated your guard-word for the swordspeaker, didn't I? I know a word of yours can open a lock or break a knee or make a fortune turn. That's why you're going to keep your mouth shut and let the doors that still remember me do their work." She hesitated. "Except—"

"Except the deep door," Petra said quietly.

"Except the deep door." Sefa exhaled. "There's one door my credentials won't touch. The Old Stack. The contracts that predate the city. Predate the *Hall*, if you can believe the rumors, and after eleven years down there I believe them. It's sealed with script older than the King's own ledger-tongue. The wardens have tried to re-key it for two hundred years and the door just—" she made a small, helpless gesture— "declines. It declines politely. That's the worst part. It's very polite about it."

"And the boy's language," Petra said, "is older than kingdoms."

"So the mentor's map says," Sefa said. "So the favor I owe says." She did not say who held that favor over her, or what it was, because she had promised herself she would not think about the hooded broker until it was unavoidable, and it was not yet unavoidable. It would be unavoidable soon. Everything in Grayharbor was eventually unavoidable. That was the city's whole religion.

The bell tower began to toll the change of watch.

Sefa moved.

* * *

The east door of the Hall of Debts was black oak banded in lead, and it had known Sefa Halloran's hand for eleven years, and it had not forgotten.

She pressed her bare palm to the lead band and spoke her name—her *whole* name, the one on the revoked credentials, the one they had struck from the rolls, Sefa Halloran of the Third Bench, Second Bench, First—and felt the door consider her the way an old dog considers a master who went away and came back smelling wrong.

The bolt drew back.

"Down," she whispered, and they were through, all four of them, into the vestibule, and the door sighed shut behind them with a sound like a ledger closing.

The vestibule was exactly as she remembered it, which was the cruelest thing about it. Grey marble. Grey light from grey lamps. Along the walls ran the great public rolls, ten thousand brass spindles holding ten thousand ribbons of vellum, every debt in Grayharbor recorded in triplicate and humming faintly with the particular silence of paper that has been written on in blood. Somewhere ahead, the door-warden's kettle was just beginning its small complaint.

"Ninety seconds," Sefa breathed. "Move."

They moved. Petra went first, sword flat against her spine under her cloak, and Sefa watched the woman's feet and marveled, not for the first time, at how a person could be so quiet while carrying that much iron and that much grief. Maren came third with the rope and the canvas, breathing like a woman climbing her own stairs, muttering a prayer to a harbor saint under her breath. Edmund came last, barefoot on the marble, making no sound at all, and Sefa did not look back at him because looking at the place where his shadow should have been made her arithmetic go wrong.

Through the second door. Past the tally-room, where the night-clerk who gambled was indeed absent and his chair was indeed warm. Past the alcove where the blind clerk sat with his fingers resting on a spindle of ribbons, reading debts by touch, his face serene as a sleeping child's. Sefa held her breath as they passed him. His fingers twitched on the vellum. He tilted his head, listening to something in the paper, and Sefa realized with a cold slide in her gut what he was listening to: *them*. The Hall recorded everything, and the blind man read the recordings, and somewhere in the ribbon under his hand a new entry was being written—*four entered, four descended, one of them burning*—

He did not raise the alarm. He turned his face toward them, sightless eyes wide, and his mouth moved around a word he didn't say aloud, and Sefa, who had spent eleven years reading the micro-expressions of clerks, read it anyway.

*Finally.*

Then they were past, and down, and the record-stair swallowed them.

* * *

The deep stacks went down farther than the city went up.

Sefa had always loved that and been ashamed of loving it. Grayharbor's towers were famous—nine of them, white and green, crowned with signal-fires—but the Hall's undercrofts were nine times nine, gallery under gallery under gallery, and the deeper you went the older the debts became, until at the bottom the paper wasn't paper at all but bark, hide, slate, and once, on a shelf she had never been cleared to touch, something that looked horribly like a shoulder blade. The air changed as they descended. It went from lamp-oil and dust to something colder and sweeter, like the air off a glacier, like the inside of a sealed letter.

The lamps down here burned without flame. Little spheres of held light, one to a gallery-end, and each one dimmed as they passed, Sefa noticed. Dimmed, and leaned. She had never noticed it before, in eleven years. But she had never descended with a burning soul in the company before, and the lights down here were not lamps at all. She had known that, of course, in the way you know the meat on your plate was an animal. Knowing and *knowing* were different ledgers.

"Don't look at them," she murmured, and then, because Maren had stopped anyway: "Don't. They notice being noticed."

"What are they?" Maren whispered.

"Collateral," Sefa said, and hated the word, and used it anyway, because the word was the crime and the crime needed saying in its own tongue.

They came down the last flight into the gallery of the great ledgers—the modern ones, the King's tongue, the ones Sefa had spent her career keeping—and here she allowed herself to stop and breathe and be, for ninety seconds, competent.

"The contract we want is eleven years old," she said, running her finger along the shelf-ends. "Soul-class, rural filing, which means it's not in the main sequence. Rural soul-sales get shelved in the annex ranges because the Hall never expected volume from the countryside." Her mouth twisted. "The countryside surprised us. The last decade, the annexes filled. There's a whole shelf-range of valley children nobody wanted to count." She found the range. Pulled the index ribbon. Her fingers knew the code before her eyes did—she had *written* half these codes. "L. Liss. There."

The ledger was where it should be, fat and grey and cold, its clasp a small lead seal stamped with the Hall's device: a hand holding a quill, and beneath the quill, almost too small to see, a chain.

"Eleven years," Petra said. Her voice had gone strange. "She's been down here eleven years. In a book."

"In a book *and* elsewhere," Sefa said. "The book is just the receipt. The—" she gestured upward, at everything, at the dimming lights, at the mountain she could feel through nine floors of stone, far to the north and east, a pressure like a headache on the horizon— "elsewhere is where the payment went."

Maren made a small sound. Edmund put his hand flat on the ledger's cover, and the cover warmed under his palm, and for one moment Sefa saw the lead seal glow through with a light like a coal breathed on, and every held-lamp in the gallery leaned toward the boy like grass toward sun.

"Later," Sefa said gently, and covered his hand with hers, and took it off the book. "We can't take the ledger. Too big, too missed. We take the *page*." She looked at Petra. "The original contract page. It's in the Old Stack. Everything older than the King's ledger-tongue is cross-filed there—the Hall keeps the deep originals because a debt isn't real unless the oldest copy says so. To void a sale, you void the first writing. The first writing of your boy's sister is behind a door that has been declining politely for two hundred years."

Petra looked down at Edmund.

Edmund looked at the far end of the gallery, where the shelves stopped and the stone changed—older stone, fitted without mortar, bearing marks that were not letters in any language Sefa could read and were not *not* letters either. And set into that ancient wall, a door.

It was smaller than a door should be. Child-height, almost. Black wood gone silver at the edges, and across its face a lock that was not a lock but a *knot*—a carving of interwoven script, dense as a bird's nest, that Sefa's trained eye could not untangle into any beginning or any end. It had no keyhole. It had no handle. It had a small worn hollow at its center, at the height of a child's hand, as if generations of something small had rested its palm there.

Sefa had stood in front of that door a hundred times over eleven years, on errands, on dares, on the one terrible night when her career had ended, and it had never once done anything but decline.

Edmund walked to it.

"Sefa," Petra said, low. "What happens if it opens?"

"Then everything the Hall is afraid of is true," Sefa said. "And I stop being the most dangerous thing in this building."

Edmund raised his hand and fitted it into the hollow, and it fit the way a hand fits a hand.

He did not speak. Sefa had braced for the speaking—for the Oathscript, for the word that had drawn a blade an inch from its sheath in a quiet valley, for the language older than kingdoms—and instead the boy simply *breathed out*, slow, the way you breathe on a window to draw on it, and the knot of script on the door began to move.

It moved like ice going out on a river in spring. The interwoven strokes slid, loosened, found new alignments, and Sefa—disgraced scribe, first of the fallen benches, a woman who had spent her life believing language was a tool—stood with her mouth open as the carving read *itself*, letter by letter, backward through its own knot, unwinding two hundred years of polite refusal into a single plain sentence that no living warden had ever managed to make it say.

Sefa could not read it. But she understood it, the way you understand a door is open by the draft on your face.

The door swung inward, child-height, onto darkness that smelled of rain on hot stone.

Edmund looked back at them. In the light of the leaning lamps his chest glowed through his shirt, and his grey eyes were wet, and he was, Sefa realized with a lurch, the bravest person she had ever seen, and also the most frightened, and both of those things were true about the same small boy at the same time.

"Eleven years," Petra whispered, to no one, to the book, to the girl in it. "Hold on, Liss."

They went through, one by one, into the Old Stack, and the door swung shut behind them with a sound like a held breath finally let go—and in the darkness ahead, faint and patient, something in the deep shelves turned a page.
