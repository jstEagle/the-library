## 19. The Page That Bleeds

The Hall of Debts did not want them to leave.

Petra felt it in the way the corridors bent—subtly, politely, the way a host steers a guest back toward the table. They had come down through nine floors of shelving that rose beyond sight into darkness, ladders on wheels long enough that a climber could die of thirst before reaching the top rung. The air smelled of vellum and cold iron and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed jar. And everywhere, everywhere, the whisper of pages turning themselves.

"Stay close," Sefa said, without looking back. Her revoked credentials hung from her neck on a fraying cord—a slate tablet etched with her name and, beneath the name, a word struck through so hard the slate had cracked. *Revoked*, but not erased. The doors still knew her. "The Hall rearranges for thieves. It's not malicious. It's *tidy*."

"It's alive," Edmund said.

He had not spoken since they'd entered, and when he did, it was in plain words—the rough, halting trade-speech he'd been practicing on the road, each consonant placed like a stone across a stream. He saved Oathscript now for doors. Petra watched him touch the next one, a slab of black oak bound in lead, and murmur three syllables that made her back teeth ache. The lock exhaled. The door swung inward like a man stepping aside.

Beyond it: the Contracts of Persons.

The room was smaller than Petra expected, and colder. No shelves here. Instead the documents hung from hooks along curved walls, hundreds of them, thousands, each page suspended in its own glass sleeve like a moth pinned for study. Lantern-light came from nowhere and touched nothing directly; it pooled beneath each contract as though the pages were lit from within by whatever they contained. Petra had seen soul-ledgers described once, in a treatise she no longer fully remembered reading, and the treatise had been wrong about everything except this: that being near them felt like standing in a room full of sleeping people.

Maren stopped just inside the doorway and would come no farther. The widow's face had gone the color of tallow. "Forty years I told myself the world below was stories," she said quietly. "To keep the children quiet at night. To keep *myself* quiet." She gripped her bundle of supplies against her chest like a shield. "I'll watch the door. Someone should watch the door."

Petra squeezed her shoulder—once, briefly, all either of them could bear—and went in.

The pages turned slowly on their hooks, restless as moths. Some glowed warm amber. Some had gone gray as ash, their light long since drawn out and spent. And some—

Some flickered.

She saw it from across the room. A page near the wall's far curve, guttering in a rhythm that was unmistakably a rhythm, unmistakably a *pulse*. Not the steady burn of a whole soul. Not the dead gray of a finished harvest. Something between. Something clinging.

Edmund saw it too. He was moving before Petra could catch his sleeve, threading between the hanging contracts with the terrible single-mindedness of a child, and by the time she reached him he stood before the flickering page with both hands pressed flat against the glass, and the light inside the sleeve had begun to reach toward him—thin filaments of gold pressing against the inner surface like roots against the bottom of a pot.

"Liss," he said. Plain word. Plain voice. It broke in the middle.

The contract was written in ash-ink on skin-pale vellum, and it was beautiful, which was the first obscenity of it. Court script, flowing and ornate, every clause looped like ivy. Sefa came up beside them and read aloud in a low, professional murmur, translating as she went, her scribe's cadence flattening the horror into inventory:

"*Contract of Sale, Person Entire. Seller: Aldous Merrin and Wife, Yeva Merrin, of the village of Thornhollow, being of sound mind and desperate circumstance.*" Sefa's voice caught slightly on *desperate circumstance*; even the court's clerks, it seemed, had occasionally allowed themselves irony. "*Purchased: one soul entire, female child Liss Merrin, age seven years, condition unblemished. Price rendered: remission of debt, passage granted, one life permitted to remain above ground.*"

"One life," Maren whispered from the doorway. "They sold two children and bought back one."

"The buyer," Sefa continued, and her professional calm finally fractured around the name, "*the Court Beneath the Mountain, its heirs and assigns, in perpetuity, until such time as the debt is voided by countersignature of an oath-bound authority or the purchased soul is returned whole to flesh.*"

"Returned whole," Petra repeated. "It says there's a way."

"It always says there's a way." Sefa's laugh had no humor in it at all. "That's how contracts work, swordspeaker. A door is only a door if it opens. They write the way out into every agreement because a soul that believes it might go home stays sweeter longer." She touched the clause gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "But look here. Look at what voids it. *Countersignature of an oath-bound authority.* Do you understand what that means? Not any clerk. Not the King himself, even. It has to be someone bound by the old oaths. One of yours."

"I'm the last," Petra said.

"Yes," said Sefa. "I know."

And that should have been the end of it—that should have been almost *hope*, the terrible bright shape of it, because if the contract could be voided by an oath-bound hand then Petra's presence here was not coincidence but requirement, and all the roads of her life had been bending toward this glass sleeve since before she understood she was walking. But Petra was not listening anymore. Because she had looked down, past the clauses, past the price, past the elegant cruelty of the terms, to the foot of the page where the signatures lived.

Aldous Merrin had signed in a laboring hand, each letter formed with the care of a man who knew he was damning himself and wanted at least to spell damnation correctly. Yeva's mark beside it, a small pressed thumbprint, because she could not write—and Petra thought of Edmund's mute precision, of a household where language had been sold off piece by piece, and her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat in heavy water.

And beneath them both, in the space reserved for witnesses and authorities:

A swordspeaker's mark.

Not a signature. A sigil—the blade-and-braid cipher that every sworn sword bore burned into the meat of their sword-hand, the mark they pressed to wax and stone and skin to seal what they spoke. Petra knew its geometry the way she knew her own pulse. She had traced it ten thousand times in the dirt of the valley as a girl, practicing, dreaming of the day it would be hers to press.

This one was pressed into the vellum itself, burned there, and the burn was old—but not as old as it should have been. That was the thing that stopped her breath. The ink had not faded evenly. Time eats the edges of a mark first, blurs the fine work, softens the braid into smudge. This mark was crisp. Fresh-edged. *Aged backward*, the way river stones are worn smooth not by years but by water—as though it had been signed not decades ago but at some point that had not yet finished arriving.

"No," Petra said. She didn't know she'd said it until the word was already out, hanging in the cold air among the sleeping pages.

"Swordspeaker?" Sefa asked.

Petra reached out and laid her bare right hand against the glass, over the mark. The lantern-light beneath the contract shifted, warmed, gathered toward her palm. And the Hall of Debts, tidy and patient, did something Petra had never heard of a ledger doing.

The page began to bleed.

Not metaphor. Color welled up through the vellum from beneath the swordspeaker's mark—deep red, then brighter, then a red so saturated it seemed lit from inside, spreading outward through the fibers like frost blooming across a window. The clauses darkened. The elegant loops filled. And where the blood touched them, the words lifted off the surface—not floating, exactly, but rising into legibility, sharpening, the way a face sharpens when it turns toward you out of a crowd.

Edmund made a small sound and stepped back. Even the hanging contracts around them had gone still, their slow restless turning arrested, every page in the room angled now—Petra was sure of it, sure with the certainty of nightmare—toward the one that bled.

"What is it doing?" Maren called from the door, her voice thin.

"Recognizing," Sefa breathed. She had gone very pale. "Gods under everything. It's *recognizing* her."

The blood-red spread until the whole lower half of the contract glowed like a coal, and within the glow the swordspeaker's mark pulsed once, twice, and then—there was no other word for it—it *answered*. Light answered it, rising out of Petra's own scarred palm where the blade-and-braid had been seared into her at nineteen years old, her own oath-mark waking for the second time in her life, the first having been the night a mute child knocked on her door. The two marks faced each other through the glass. The same geometry. The same proportions, down to the small flaw in the third braid-strand where the burning iron had slipped—

Where the iron had slipped. Where *her* iron had slipped. She remembered the pain of it, the master-of-oaths cursing softly, the smell of her own skin, the strange private shame of carrying an imperfect seal for the rest of her sworn life. A flaw no one else's mark should share. A flaw as individual as a fingerprint, as a way of walking, as the particular hesitation in a signature's final stroke.

The mark on the page had the flaw.

Petra took her hand off the glass as if the sleeve had bitten her. The bleeding slowed, dimmed, settled into a dull ember-glow at the heart of the contract—but it did not stop. It pulsed. Slowly. Patiently. Like something with all the time in the world.

"That's impossible," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, a stranger's voice reporting from another room. "That mark is mine. My hand. My flaw. But I have never—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, more carefully, laying each word down like a plank over a chasm. "I have never signed anything below this sky. I left the order before I ever descended. I have never *been* here. I would remember."

"Would you?"

Sefa asked it gently, which made it worse. The disgraced scribe drifted closer, arms folded, studying Petra with the narrow attention she usually spent on clauses.

"You told me on the road that your memory has holes. Gaps. Years you can't account for between your leaving and your arrival in the valley." Sefa tapped the glass, just beside the mark, careful not to touch the glow. "I told myself it was trauma. It usually is. But look at the aging on this signature, swordspeaker. Ink doesn't do that. Burn doesn't do that. Unless—" and here her voice dropped to nearly nothing, the way voices drop in the Hall of Debts, as though the pages were listening, which Petra now believed absolutely they were, "—unless the signature isn't waiting to be found. Unless it's waiting to be *made*."

The words landed in Petra's chest like stones dropped down a well. She counted the seconds before the splash and heard none.

"You're saying I will sign this."

"I'm saying the mark is yours and the ink is young, and both of those things are true at once, and in my forty years of record-keeping I have learned that when a document contradicts time itself, the document wins." Sefa straightened, and for a moment the disgraced court scribe looked less disgraced than anyone Petra had ever seen—straight-backed, clear-eyed, delivering a verdict. "There are oaths that bind forward. Every swordspeaker knows the old tense-craft. A vow sworn in the future tense holds harder than one sworn in the past, because the future cannot be escaped, only walked toward."

Petra looked at the page. At the mark with her flaw in it. At the blood-glow pulsing its slow patient pulse, and at the thin gold filaments of Liss's soul pressed against the glass, reaching, always reaching, toward the brother who had swallowed her light to keep it safe.

Edmund tugged her sleeve. When she looked down, he was pointing—not at the signatures, not at the mark, but at a line of the contract that had darkened when the blood passed through it, a clause so small she'd skipped it entirely. He couldn't read the court script. But he didn't need to. His finger rested beneath it, and his eyes came up to hers, and he mouthed the trade-words he'd practiced so carefully on the road:

*What does it say.*

Sefa read it. Her voice, when it came, was barely a thread:

"*Countersignature to be rendered at the Signing Table beneath the Mountain, by the oath-bound party, upon presentation of the soul entire.*"

Beneath the Mountain. The Signing Table. *Upon presentation.*

Which meant the contract was not merely a record of a sale long concluded. It was an instruction. A route map written in someone else's blood. Everything—the knock at the door, the oath that dragged her from the valley, the wardens, the ambush, the ledger, this room with its corridors that bent thieves gently back toward what they were meant to find—had been arranged with the courtesy of a host steering a guest back toward the table.

The page pulped once more, red and gold together, and in the pulse Petra heard—or dreamed she heard, and could not afterwards swear which—two voices braided into one. A woman's voice, faint and flickering, speaking in a dialect she hadn't heard since childhood. And beneath it, older and colder and terribly amused, something that was not a voice at all but the sensation of being *read*.

From the doorway, Maren said, "Petra. Your hand."

Petra looked down. Her oath-mark, the blade-and-braid seared into her sword-hand nineteen years ago, was glowing. Softly. Steadily. In perfect rhythm with the page.

And on the glass of the sleeve, where her palm had pressed, her handprint remained—burned into the surface as though the glass had been wax, five fingers and a palm, overlaid precisely on the swordspeaker's mark below.

Waiting for the rest of her to arrive.

* * *

They did not take the page. Sefa explained why on the stairs, in the clipped voice of a woman rationing her own fear: a contract of persons could not be stolen, only voided, and it could not be voided anywhere but where it was kept. The Hall would simply grow a new one from its roots, the way a tree grows around a nail. "You don't cut the clause out," she said. "You answer it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

So they climbed back up through nine floors of turning pages, and the corridors, tidy and patient, let them go—bent them, if anything, *toward* the exits, toward the gray harbor light, toward the mountain waiting at the edge of the world with its mouth open. Toward the Signing Table.

At the top of the stairs, Edmund stopped. He looked back down into the dark of the Hall, at the faint red glow rising from the Contracts of Persons like heat off a forge, and he said one word in Oathscript. Petra felt it land in her chest, in the scarred hollow where her memories paid for her blades, and for one disorienting moment she almost understood it—almost felt its meaning settle into place like a key finding its lock—and then it was gone, leaving only its shape behind.

Later, much later, she would ask him what he'd said. He would tell her. But standing there on the threshold of the Hall of Debts, with her hand still burning and the mountain waiting, Petra felt the word pass through her and understood only this much:

Whatever it meant, it was a promise.

And promises, below the mountain, were currency—and she had just spent one she couldn't afford.
