## 20. Interrogating Herself

The page had not stopped bleeding.

It lay on the table of the safehouse Sefa had led them to—a shuttered counting-house above a chandlery in Grayharbor's debt-quarter, all dust sheets and dead ledgers—and from its center, where the countersignature curled like a sleeping serpent, a thin red stain crept across the parchment's grain. Not ink. Ink did not move. This crept and pooled and paused, as if listening.

Petra stood over it and could not stop looking at her own hand.

The mark was hers. She knew it the way she knew the scar on her left thumb, the way she knew the particular crook of her own signature's final stroke—the way it hooked down at the end like a man bowing out of a room. Forty years she had signed nothing but supply manifests and village tithes, and still the hand remembered itself. The hand on the page was younger. That was the worst of it. The loops were confident, unweathered, drawn by fingers that had never hauled water or buried friends. A hand from before the valley. Or after it.

Or not yet.

"Stop staring at it," Maren said quietly. She sat near the cold hearth with Edmund asleep against her shoulder, his ember-light dimmed to a faint rose glow through his shirt. "Staring doesn't change what's written."

"Reading does." Sefa was bent nearly double over the contract, a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, three of her own scribe's lamps—rescued from her ruined career—angled to throw light across the countersignature without casting shadow onto it. Shadow, she'd explained, made Oathscript lie. "And I need everyone quiet. The tense is doing something strange. It's doing something I've only seen in condemned records."

"Tense," Petra said. "You mean past or present."

"I mean neither." Sefa adjusted the loupe with a finger gone gray with old graphite. "Swordspeaker oaths are written in what we called the binding mood. Not *I swore*. Not *I swear*. A third thing. *I am swearing*, continuously, forever, the verb never allowed to finish. That's how an oath holds. It never gets to be over." She traced the air an inch above the mark, not touching it. "But this—this isn't in the binding mood. This is in the future tense. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Nobody writes contracts in the future tense. You can't hold someone to something they haven't done."

"You're saying it hasn't happened yet," Maren said flatly.

"I'm saying the document believes it already has." Sefa straightened, wincing, pressing a hand to her back. Her eyes found Petra's over the lamplight, and there was something in them Petra had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a physician delivering a diagnosis he wished he could swallow. "The countersignature reads: *I, who will be called oath-breaker, sign this in the deep court so that the seal may renew.* Signed with your mark. Dated—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because Sefa was a woman who had learned that plainness was the only currency left to her, "—dated forty days from now."

* * *

The silence afterward had texture. It pressed against Petra's ears like deep water.

Edmund slept on, mercifully, the ember in his chest rising and falling with his breath. Outside, Grayharbor went about its midnight commerce—the groan of cranes, the ledger-clerks' bells marking the hours of debts come due, a foghorn moaning somewhere out past the harbor wall where Maren had been taken and returned and taken again in their nightmares. Forty days. The harvesters had given them terms; the parley had bought time; and now the paper itself told her the price had been agreed long before any of them walked into the Hall of Debts.

"No," Petra said finally. "No. That's not how time works. That's not how *oaths* work. An oath is spoken by a throat, witnessed by a blade, bound by—"

"By what?" Sefa asked softly. "By what, precisely? Say it."

"By the speaker meaning it."

"And do you know, beyond any doubt, that some version of you won't mean it?" Sefa spread her hands, and the gesture was almost gentle. "I served the court beneath the mountain for eleven years. Eleven years transcribing contracts for beings who experience every moment they will ever have as though it were happening now. To them, Petra Vasquez, the future is just a place they've already visited. If the Ember King's clerks recorded your signature, it's because somewhere ahead of us, in the dark under that mountain, you put pen to ash and signed." She let that sit. Then, quieter: "Or because they are very good at making documents that make themselves true. I've seen both. I honestly couldn't tell you which frightens me more."

Maren shifted, and Edmund murmured in his sleep. His lips moved around syllables that were not words—not quite—and the bleeding page flinched. Actually flinched, the red stain contracting like a pupil narrowing against light. Everyone saw it. No one spoke of it for a long moment.

"He answers it," Petra said at last. "Even dreaming. Even mute. He answers Oathscript in his sleep."

"He's been speaking it since before he had teeth," Maren said. "Of course he answers. The question is whether *you* do."

* * *

They argued until the third bell, and then they argued past it, in the low fierce register of people trying not to wake a child. Maren wanted to run—north, away, anywhere the harvesters' ash cloaks would take seasons to search. Sefa wanted to burn the page, though she admitted within two sentences that burning a soul-contract voided nothing and might, if the court held the original, actually accelerate the harvest. Petra listened to both and heard, underneath each voice, the same sentence repeating like water under ice:

*Forty days.*

She stepped out onto the counting-house's narrow balcony to be alone with it, and the city gave her no peace even there. Grayharbor at night was a ledger made visible: every window a figure, every bell a sum coming due, the whole glittering town built atop the certainty that everything owed would eventually be collected. Somewhere below the mountain, a court full of beautiful starving things had already watched her sign. Had perhaps *filed* her signing. She thought of the King's voice from the parley—that vast dry patience, like wind through a tomb—and wondered if he had smiled when the harvester captain offered terms, knowing the outcome was already inked.

Her sword hung at her hip. It had been humming since the Hall of Debts, a note too low to hear properly, more felt than sound, the way you feel a storm before the sky admits it. She drew it an inch—only an inch, the discipline of a lifetime—and looked at the steel in the city's orange light.

"What do you know?" she whispered.

The blade answered the way it always answered now: not in words but in weight. It felt heavier than it had in the valley. It had felt heavier every day since the moor road, since the first speech-blade, since she'd begun paying for power in pieces of herself. She had lost the taste of bread. She had lost her mother's face—she knew she'd lost it because she kept reaching for it and finding a warm shape where features should be, like a portrait left out in rain. She had lost, three nights ago, her own birthday, and had spent an hour lying awake inventing one, choosing a date the way you'd choose a stone to stand on in a river.

And now the page told her that whatever remained of her was already committed. That the traitor in the record wore her name and moved with her stride and was, day by day, forty steps away and closing.

"Then I'll interrogate her," Petra said to the blade. Her breath fogged. "Wherever she is. Whatever she thinks she's doing. I'll ask her why."

The sword hummed. It sounded, absurdly, like approval.

* * *

She went back inside and found Edmund awake.

He sat up on the pallet, hair sleep-tangled, the ember glowing through the thin fabric of his shirt like a coal wrapped in linen. He didn't speak—he rarely did anymore, saving his Oathscript the way misers save coin—but he watched her cross the room, and his watching had a gravity to it that children should not possess. He patted the pallet beside him. An invitation. A summons. With Edmund it was hard to tell the difference.

She sat.

He took her hand—small fingers, ember-warm—and turned it palm-up, studying it as though comparing it to something. Then he reached out with his other hand and touched, very lightly, the bleeding page.

The stain stilled. And in the silence of the counting-house, in Petra's mind rather than her ears, came a sensation she had no better word for than *reading*: a pressure of meaning, brief and enormous, like a wave passing under a boat. For half a heartbeat she was standing somewhere cold and vast, and there was a table, and there was a pen, and her own hand was moving toward it with terrible calm—

Edmund snatched his fingers back and shook them as if he'd touched a stove. His eyes were huge. He looked at her and mouthed something, and this time she caught it, because it wasn't Oathscript at all.

*Don't.*

"I don't intend to," she said.

He frowned. He tapped his own chest, where the ember lived. Then he pointed at her, at the space behind her ribs where a soul would sit if souls kept house there. Then he made the two hands meet: his chest, her chest, joined.

*Same,* that gesture said. Or *soon*. Or *you and Liss, the same fate, sold and signed*. With a child's grammar and a prophet's aim, he had cut through every argument of the night and laid the truth bare: the question was never whether she would betray them. The question was whether the woman who signed that page would still be her—still the one who carried him, taught him guard-words, counted her own memories like coins—or whether signing would be simply the last transaction in a long sale, the way his parents' desperation had ended in a broker's hooded smile.

"I'm not your parents," she said.

Edmund considered this with the grave thoroughness he brought to everything. Then he shook his head slowly, and reached up, and pressed his small hot palm flat against her sternum, right over her heart, and left it there until she felt the ember's warmth pass through skin and bone and settle somewhere deeper than either.

Not *same*, then. Something kinder. *I'm keeping this one*, maybe. *This one stays mine.*

Maren appeared at the doorway with tea nobody had asked for, which was Maren's way of ending arguments. "So," she said. "The page says you sign. Sefa says the page might be wrong, or worse, might be right. What does the swordspeaker say?"

Petra looked down at Edmund's hand over her heart, and at the sword across her knees, humming its low storm-note, and at the page on the table, patient as a trap, patient as a promise.

"The swordspeaker says a contract needs two signatures to bind and one refusal to break." She stood, and the old ritual rose in her unbidden—the stance, the breath, the settling of weight that had been beaten into her by a mentor whose face she could no longer picture. "If there's a version of me under that mountain holding a pen, then I want to meet her before she meets me. I want to look her in the eye and find out exactly what she thinks she's buying."

Sefa laughed once, short and disbelieving, from her nest of lamps. "That's not defiance, that's a death wish with footnotes."

"It's an interrogation," Petra said. "I told you. I intend to question myself thoroughly."

* * *

They spent the remaining dark hours preparing, because preparation was the only prayer any of them trusted. Sefa copied the contract's clauses into her working cipher, hunting for loopholes with the hunger of a woman redeeming a ruined life. Maren packed food as if packing could ward off fate—hard bread, salt fish, a jar of honey "for the boy's spirit," though everyone understood whose spirit needed feeding. And Edmund sat by the window with his legs dangling, watching the harbor lights, and practiced, barely audible, a single Oathscript syllable over and over—the one, Petra realized with a chill, that opened doors. As if he already knew which doors lay ahead.

Near dawn, the page began to bleed again.

Petra saw it first. The stain spread from the countersignature in a thin deliberate line—not spreading randomly now but traveling, writing, drawing itself letter by letter across the parchment's empty margin in a hand she recognized because she had spent the whole night refusing to recognize it. Her hand. Young and sure and hooking downward at every stroke like a man bowing out of a room.

Sefa read the fresh words aloud in a voice gone thin as wire:

"*Thirty-nine days.*"

Below it, after a pause long enough for all four of them to stop breathing, a second line crawled into being:

"*Bring the boy. Bring the blade. Come and see.*"

The lamp flames bent sideways, all at once, though every window in the counting-house was shut. Down in the street below, hooves struck the cobbles—many hooves, unhurried, in no need of haste—and out through the fog along the harbor road came a sound like ash falling on ash: the King's riders, wearing the night like a cloak, riding toward the debt-quarter with all the patience of a document that has already been signed.

Edmund stood. The ember in his chest flared bright enough to throw his shadow against the wall—his shadow, which he had never had, tall and wavering and shaped almost exactly like a swordspeaker holding a drawn blade.

"They're early," Maren breathed.

"No," Petra said, and drew her sword fully, and the steel sang. "We are."
