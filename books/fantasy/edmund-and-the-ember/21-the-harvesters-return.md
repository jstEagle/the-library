## 21. The Harvesters Return

The harbor wall at Grayharbor was built for war and repurposed for commerce, which meant it was built twice and maintained by neither. Petra walked its spine in the blue hour before dawn, when the fish-carts had not yet come up from the strand and the gulls were still arguing over the bones of yesterday. Below her, the tide worked at the pilings with patient teeth. Above her, the sky was the color of a blade that had been sharpened too many times—thin, bright, and tired.

She counted exits the way she used to count heartbeats. Three ways off the wall: the stair by the salt-house, the ramp behind the chandlery, and the long drop to the mudflats that would break an ankle but not a neck. She had Maren's map folded against her ribs. She had Edmund's hand in hers, small and warm and faintly luminous at the wrist where the ember sat beneath his skin like a coal wrapped in paper. And she had Sefa, walking three paces back with her scribe's ink-stained fingers tucked into her sleeves, pretending to be nobody.

Maren came last, as she always did now, hauling the pack with the bread and the lamp-oil and the little tin of honey she refused to leave behind because, she said, a person could survive anything except sweetnesslessness.

They should have been on the road north by now. They were not, because Petra had made a mistake, and the mistake had a name, and the name was *one more hour*.

She had wanted to see the harbor wall at dawn. She had wanted, just once since leaving the valley, to stand somewhere high and open and let the wind take the smell of ash-cloaks out of her hair. The moor ambush was nine days behind them. Nine days of sleeping in ditches and waking at every owl-call. Nine days of Edmund flinching at gray fabric. She had told herself the harvesters would not find them in a city of forty thousand souls, that Grayharbor's noise and stink and endless ledger-keeping would swallow them the way the valley once swallowed her.

But harvesters did not hunt by sight. They hunted by weight. A soul like Liss's—a soul sold whole, still burning after years in a child's chest—had a gravity all its own, and every hour they lingered, the city tilted toward them by degrees.

Petra felt it now. Not heard, not saw. *Felt*, in the place behind her sternum where the sword-speech lived: a slow cold pressure, like weather coming off the sea.

"Stop," she said.

Edmund stopped. He had learned to obey that word without question, and something about the ease of his obedience broke her heart a little each time.

Sefa went still too, head cocked, listening the way scribes listen—with their whole spines. "What is it?"

"The air's wrong." Petra turned slowly. The wall stretched away in both directions, empty, gull-haunted, innocent. The salt-house stair stood dark. The chandlery ramp was a black slot between buildings. "It's too quiet. Even for this hour."

"It's a harbor," Sefa said. "It's never quiet."

"That's what I mean."

Maren had caught up and was breathing hard, one hand pressed to the pack strap across her chest. "Petra, love, if you're going to say run, say it quick, because my knees have opinions and none of them are brave."

Petra looked at Edmund. He was looking past her, down the long curve of the wall toward the seaward end, where the old beacon tower stood gutted and leaning. His face did what it always did when the ember spoke to him—went smooth and absent, like a pond when something passes beneath.

"What is it?" she asked him softly. "What does she say?"

His lips moved. No sound. He had not spoken plain words since the night on the moor road, since he'd asked her *will you fail too* and she had answered honestly, which was the worst thing she could have done. But his hands spoke now, quick and certain, the way they'd begun to speak when Oathscript failed him: two fingers pointed at his own chest, then swept out toward the sea, then closed into a fist.

*She feels them. Coming. Many.*

"How many?" Petra asked, though she already knew the answer was *enough*.

Edmund held up both hands, spread all ten fingers, then folded them shut again and shook his head. More than counting. Beyond counting.

Then the lamps along the wall began to go out.

Not all at once. One by one, in sequence, from the landward end toward them, each flame guttering down to nothing with a sound like a sigh drawn through cloth. Petra watched the darkness walk toward her along the wall's spine and understood that she was watching patience. Whatever came up those stairs was in no hurry, because nothing in the world could outrun it. It had been paid to wait.

"Maren." Petra kept her voice level, the way you keep a cup level when it's full to the brim. "Take Edmund. Salt-house stair, down to the strand, into the fish-market—the crowd will be thick by mid-bell. Don't stop for anything. Don't look back."

"And you'll be—"

"Right behind you."

It was the first lie she had told since the wardens at the ford, and the wards of her own body knew it; something in her chest flickered, a candle guttering in a draft. Maren looked at her for a long moment with eyes that had raised children and buried a husband and survived forty years in a valley built on a lie, and Petra saw that she knew. Widows always know.

"You come down that stair," Maren said, "or I will come back up it after you, and I am heavier than I look and twice as stubborn."

"I know," Petra said. "Go."

They went. Edmund resisted—one heartbeat, two, his hand tightening on hers with a strength no child should have, the ember flaring gold through the skin of his throat—and then Maren took his shoulders and turned him, gently, the way you turn someone who has frozen, and Petra understood with a lurch that this was the moment. This was the shape of it. She had seen it in dreams she hadn't told anyone about: the boy freezing, the widow turning him, the dark coming up the stairs.

She drew her sword.

It came free of the sheath with a sound like a word being spoken, and the world leaned in. Even here, even half-rusted with disuse and drunk on everything she'd fed it on the moor road, the blade woke singing, its edge shedding a light that wasn't quite light—more like attention, like the feeling of being *read*. Along the wall, in the doorways and shadows where the lamps had died, shapes detached themselves from the dark.

Ash-cloaks. Eight of them. Then twelve. They moved wrong—not quickly, but *completely*, each step arriving exactly where it intended, no waste, no hesitation, as though the space between steps had been negotiated in advance and found agreeable. Their hoods were down. That was new. On the moor they had kept their faces hidden; here, in the gray pre-dawn, they showed her what they were.

They had been people once. You could see it in the architecture of the cheekbones, the set of the jaws. But the hunger had been at them a long time, and hunger, given enough years, becomes a kind of face. Their eyes were banked coals, dim orange, and they did not blink, because blinking was a habit of bodies that expected to keep living.

The captain came last. She was taller than the others, and her ash-cloak was newer, and where the others' coals guttered hers burned steady, and Petra recognized the discipline in her stance the way you recognize a dialect—inherited, trained, worn smooth by generations of use.

"Swordspeaker." The captain's voice was dry paper. "You've cost us two harvests already. The court remembers costs."

"Then remember this one too." Petra set her feet. The wall was narrow here, four feet of stone between the drop to the mudflats and the drop to the strand, and she put her back to neither, because a swordspeaker's back belonged to the enemy. "The boy goes free. That's the whole of my terms. Say yes and we part ways. Say no and I start spending."

Something moved through the ash-cloaks like wind through wheat. *Spending.* They knew what it meant. On the moor road she had spoken three words of true speech-blade and forgotten her mother's face for it, and the harvesters had felt every syllable land.

"You misunderstand your position," the captain said. "We were not sent for the boy alone."

"I know. You were sent for the ember."

"We were sent for the *oathbreaker*." The captain's coal-eyes did not waver. "There is a page in the Hall of Debts with your mark on it, swordspeaker. A contract countersigned in a hand that has not been born yet. The court has been waiting for you longer than you have been alive. Everything else—the boy, the ember, the widow—is logistics."

The words went into Petra like a blade finding the gap in armor. *Waiting for you longer than you have been alive.* She thought of the ledger page that bled, of her own handwriting aged backward, of the signing table waiting below the mountain with its pen and its ash. She had thought she was running *toward* that fate. She had not understood she was being *escorted*.

"Last chance," Petra said, and lifted the sword, and let the first word rise in her throat.

She felt it there like a swallowed coal: *sunder*, the guard-word, the oldest one, the one that cut connections rather than flesh. If she spoke it at the wall itself, the ancient stones would crack and fall, and the gap would buy them thirty seconds. Thirty seconds was a life. Thirty seconds was Maren reaching the market crowd.

She opened her mouth—

—and Edmund screamed.

Not a word. Not Oathscript. A raw, animal sound, high and terrible, torn out of him from somewhere below language, and Petra's whole body turned toward it before her mind agreed, and in turning she saw them: two ash-cloaks on the salt-house stair, silent as spilled ink, and between them Maren, and in front of Maren, Edmund, rooted to the step, frozen the way he froze when the ember's fear flooded him and his body simply *stopped*, and the ash-cloaks were not reaching for him at all.

They were reaching past him.

For the widow.

"No—" Petra was moving, running along the wall's spine with the sword blazing, and she was fast, she had always been fast, but the wall was long and the stair was far and the harvesters moved *completely*, without waste, and one of them had already taken Maren's arm. Not cruelly. That was the horror of it. The grip was gentle, almost courteous, the grip of a bailiff or a nurse, and Maren was striking at it with her free fist, with the tin of honey she'd snatched from the fallen pack, and she was shouting—Petra heard her voice crack across the water, furious and unafraid:

*"You take your hands off me, you great gray gravecloth, I've buried better men than—"* 

The second harvester caught her other arm.

Petra spoke the word.

*SUNDER.*

The speech left her like blood leaving a wound. She felt it go—felt it take a piece of her with it, some small bright thing she would spend the rest of the day hunting for and never find—and the wall answered. Stone cracked from end to end of the seaward reach, a jagged seam splitting the ancient masonry, and twenty feet of the harbor wall groaned, tilted, and collapsed inward with a roar that woke every gull in Grayharbor and half the town besides. Dust boiled up white in the dawn. The ash-cloaks nearest the breach staggered, and two of them went down with the falling stone, buried, gone.

But the ones on the stair did not so much as turn their heads.

That was the lesson, and Petra learned it in the space of a single heartbeat while the dust was still rising: the collapse had never been the point. The captain had walked her onto the wall, had spoken the word *oathbreaker* to root her in place, had let her see the threat and choose—and whatever Petra chose, the harvesters had already chosen for her. Sunder the wall, and they took the widow. Charge the stair, and they took the boy. Stand and fight, and they took everything, patiently, the way the tide takes a sandcastle, not because the sea hates the castle but because the sea has time.

She charged anyway.

She was three strides into it when the captain stepped into her path—not blocking her, merely *arriving* where Petra was going to be, the way water arrives in a low place—and the other ash-cloaks closed around them in a ring that was not a ring, more a suggestion of enclosure, a geometry Petra's sword-hand understood and despaired of.

"Look at her," the captain said quietly, and it took Petra a moment to understand she was speaking not to her but to Edmund, frozen on the stair, watching with the ember's light pulsing visibly through his shirt now, gold as a struck match. "Look at what she chose. She spent a word of power to save a stone wall's worth of nothing, and now the old woman pays the interest. This is what oaths do, child. This is what they have always done. They make good people into arithmetic."

"Let. Her. Go." Each word a separate blow. Petra's voice didn't sound like her own anymore; it sounded like the sword.

"The court has no quarrel with the woman," the captain said. "Deliver the ember voluntarily—walk with us, today, unhindered—and she walks free within the week. Refuse, and she comes below anyway, as inventory. The court is not cruel, swordspeaker. It is merely *thorough*." The coal-eyes steadied on Petra's. "You have until the tide turns. Ask the harbor-master when that is. Everyone in Grayharbor knows the tides."

And then, like a tide themselves, they withdrew—backward, unhurried, Maren borne between two of them as gently as a bride, her voice still ringing off the water, cursing them with a fluency that would have made a dockhand blush, until the dust and the distance took them and the stair stood empty and Edmund was alone on it, on his knees now, his fists pressed against the stone, shaking.

Petra reached him. She didn't remember crossing the distance. Her sword was still drawn and she couldn't recall drawing it fully, only that it was bare and singing and that her hand ached from gripping it, and she knelt in the rubble-dust and pulled the boy against her chest and held him while the ember beat against her ribs like a second heart, wild, terrified, grieving.

"They took her," Edmund said.

Plain words. Plain, small, broken words, the muteness cracking clean through, and Petra would have wept at the sound of his voice under any other sky.

"They took her because of me," he said. "Because I froze. She told me *run* and I couldn't, I couldn't, it was like the ember went cold and heavy and I couldn't—"

"Listen to me." Petra took his face in her hands. Behind them the breach in the wall smoked; below them the tide worried the pilings; above them the thin bright sky went on being indifferent. "You listen to me, Edmund. What happened here was not your failing. It was a trap with two doors, and both doors led to the same room. I chose a door and I chose wrong, and Maren is paying for my arithmetic, not yours. Do you hear me? *Mine.*"

He was crying silently, the way children cry when they've learned that sound brings things.

"But we're going to get her back," Petra said, and heard the oath-forming in her own mouth before she could stop it, felt the words settle into her bones with the old familiar weight, the weight that had defined her entire life and broken her entire life and was, right now, the only thing holding her upright. "I don't know how yet. I don't know what it costs. But I have a map with a mountain on it, and a debt-page with my name on it, and apparently I have an appointment below the ground that I made before I was born. So we're going to keep it. We're going to walk into that court, and we're going to put Liss's contract on the table, and we're going to take Maren home."

Edmund looked at her. Through the tears, through the terror, something in his face shifted—the ember-light steadying, gathering, focusing into something that looked, for one uncanny moment, like hope wearing a child's features.

"She says thank you," he whispered. "Liss. She says the tide turns at mid-morning."

Petra closed her eyes. Of course it did. Of course the dead girl in his chest was keeping time for the enemy.

"Then we have until mid-morning," she said, and rose, and sheathed the sword, and turned to face the smoking breach in the wall, and the road beyond it, and the mountain at the end of the road that had been waiting for her longer than she had been alive.

Behind her, small and steady, Edmund got to his feet.

* * *

They found Sefa waiting at the foot of the salt-house stair, pale as her own parchment, the abandoned pack clutched to her chest like a shield. Her eyes went from Petra to Edmund to the breach in the wall above, where gulls wheeled screaming through the settling dust.

"I heard it from three streets away," she said. "The whole city heard it. By noon there will be wardens asking questions, and by evening there will be a bounty notice, and by tomorrow—" She stopped. Drew breath. "Where's the widow?"

Petra said nothing. She didn't have to. Sefa was a scribe; she read silences the way she read script, and this one was written large.

"Oh," Sefa said softly. "Oh, no."

"They gave me until the tide turns." Petra's voice came out flat and strange, scraped hollow by the word she'd spent. "So we're going below the mountain. Today. All of us. And Sefa—you're going to tell me everything you know about the Ember Court, and you're going to start with how a disgraced scribe gets an audience with a king."

Sefa hugged the pack tighter. For a long moment she looked at the sea, and the sea looked back with all its gray patience, and somewhere beneath the harbor the tide began, minute by minute, to turn.

"There's a door," Sefa said at last, "in the Hall of Debts. A door that predates the city. Edmund's language opens doors like that—I've seen it do it." She swallowed. "But Petra. That door doesn't lead around the court. It leads *through* it. Straight down. There's no path in the world shorter to the Ember King, and none more fatal."

Below them, the tide sighed against the pilings, patient as everything else that served the mountain.

"Good," said Petra, and started walking.
