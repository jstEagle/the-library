## 22. Parley With a Warden of Souls

The harbor wall was older than the city it guarded, and it had never been meant to hold back the sea.

Petra had understood that only in the last hour, standing among its lichen-eaten stones while gulls screamed overhead and the tide went out and out and out, exposing miles of black mudflat glittering like hammered tin. The wall faced the wrong way. It had always faced the wrong way—faced inland, toward Grayharbor's crooked streets and counting-houses, as if the true danger had never come off the water at all. As if the builders had known something the city had forgotten: that what needed holding back would come walking down the moor road in ash-colored cloaks, patient as arithmetic.

They had come at dusk, six of them, and they had not fought her.

That was the worst of it. She had expected violence—the clash and flare of speech-blades, the cost counted in memories, everything she now understood about her own craft laid out in blood and forgetting. Instead the harvesters had simply *taken* Maren. One moment the widow was shouting at Edmund to run, her stick raised like a lance, and the next a tall figure in gray had folded her up with terrible gentleness, the way one gathers a sleeping child from a cart, and the ash-cloaks had withdrawn to the far end of the wall, unhurried, and waited.

Now they were still waiting. And Petra stood between them and Edmund, with Sefa pressed white-knuckled against the parapet behind her, and the tide hissing out across the flats below, and she understood that this too was a kind of ledger. Everything, in the end, was.

The captain came forward alone when the last light failed.

She was taller than the others, or seemed so because she did not stoop; the harvesters moved like men wading through deep water, but this one walked upright, boots ringing on stone with a sound too clean for the wall's decay. Her cloak was ash-gray like the rest, but beneath the hood Petra caught the gleam of a face that might once have been beautiful in the way court portraits are beautiful—symmetrical, composed, and utterly without warmth. When the hood fell back, Petra saw why the others kept their distance.

The captain's eyes were lamps. Not glowing—not the vulgar shine of magic—but lit from within, small and steady, the way niches full of votive souls had burned in the galleries of the mountain. Someone else's light, banked and tended, looking out through borrowed sockets.

"You are the swordspeaker," the captain said. Her voice was low and even, a voice for reading terms aloud. "Vasquez. Of the valley."

"I'm no one of the valley," Petra said. "The valley is empty. You know that better than I do—you built the silence there."

"We maintained it." The captain stopped ten paces away, close enough that Petra could see the fine stitching of the cloak, thread of spun gray that drank the lamplight from the harbor beacons rather than reflecting it. "Maintained it for a very long time. Longer than your life. Longer than your oath." A pause, delicate as a scale finding balance. "You have crossed the seal. You carry an escaped ember. And you have cost us two harvesters' worth of work on the moor road, and a page torn from the Hall of Debts, which cannot be replaced, though it can—" the lamp-eyes flickered, "—be rewritten."

Behind Petra, Sefa made a small sound, like a mouse deciding whether to exist.

"Where is Maren?" Petra asked.

"Safe. Comfortable. Fed." The captain said each word as if placing coins on a counter. "We do not damage what we intend to keep. She is old, and old vessels crack, so we have been careful with her. You may verify this yourself, presently, under terms."

"There are no terms," Petra said. "There is nothing to negotiate. You took her. Give her back."

"Everything has terms." For the first time the captain's composure shifted—not breaking, but settling, like silt in still water. "You above all people should know that. You were made of terms. Your valley was a term. Your sword is a term, written in a language you can barely speak anymore, and every word you speak from it costs you a piece of yourself, does it not?" The lamp-eyes tilted, considering. "We watched you on the moor road. We heard what you spent. A face. A birthday. The taste of bread—I could tell you which memory went for which blow, if you'd like an accounting. We keep excellent accounts."

Petra's hand found her sword hilt. The blade hummed against her palm, faint and hungry, remembering things she no longer could.

"Say what you came to say," she said.

* * *

The captain drew a fold of gray cloth from within her cloak and spread it open upon the parapet stone, weighting the corners with four smooth pebbles that had not been in her hands a moment before. On the cloth lay three objects.

A lantern, unlit, its glass smoked dark.

A strip of vellum covered edge to edge in Oathscript, dense as woven cloth.

And a single dried petal, red as a coal gone out, which Petra recognized with a lurch before she knew why—and then knew: it was from the climbing rose that had grown over her cottage door in the valley. The rose she had planted the first spring, the rose whose scent she could no longer quite summon. Someone had been inside her house. Or someone had carried a piece of it out long ago, waiting for exactly this night.

"The terms are simple," the captain said. "Three clauses. Hear all three before you answer, because the answer must be whole or it is nothing."

Petra said nothing. Behind her, Edmund's small hand had found the back of her belt and gripped it, and she could feel the ember in his chest beating faster—a warmth against her spine like a second heartbeat, frightened and bright.

"Clause the first." The captain touched the dark lantern. "You deliver the ember voluntarily. Unbroken, ungutted, still warm. The boy walks down from this wall on his own feet and goes where he is told, and no hand is laid on him beyond what the contract requires. In exchange, the woman called Maren Odell is released at the ford by morning, whole, with her stick and her supplies and her forty years of fear returned to her undiminished."

"She's not a clause," Petra said quietly. "She's a person."

"All persons are clauses," the captain said, without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Some are simply longer than others. Clause the second." Her finger moved to the vellum. "Your memories. The ones you have already spent—the face, the day, the bread, the rest—we cannot return. Spent words go into the blade, and the blade belongs to you, and we do not traffic in what is already owned. But the ones you have *not yet* spent remain yours. Every word you speak from this hour until the ember is delivered home will cost you nothing. The sword will drink from the ember's margin instead. The boy's sister has already been sold whole; there is surplus in her. Enough to carry you through a war, if it came to that."

Petra felt the offer slide into her like a key into an old lock. *Enough to carry you through a war.* How many nights had she lain awake doing the arithmetic since the moor road—counting the memories left against the memories the road would demand, wondering which piece of herself she would feed to the blade next, dreading the morning she reached for her mother's voice and found the shelf empty? And here it was, offered freely: permission to spend without becoming less. All it required was delivering a child's soul to the thing that ate souls.

"And the third clause?" she asked, because her voice would do nothing else.

The captain lifted the dried petal between two fingers, delicately, as one lifts a relic.

"Clause the third. You keep your name."

The wind came off the mudflats then, cold and smelling of salt and old iron, and for a moment nobody on the wall moved.

"You don't understand what you're holding," Petra said.

"We understand precisely." The captain turned the petal in the lamplight. "You think we offer you trinkets. But we have read your file, swordspeaker. We wrote half of it. We know what you are: the last oath-bound keeper of a seal built on a lie, carrying a stolen soul toward a court that will consume it, armed with a craft that unmakes you word by word. We know that somewhere ahead of you stands a table with a pen and a dish of ash, and that either you sign or you break, and both of those roads end with you hollowed out and nameless. We are offering you the only version of this story in which you arrive at the end still *being* someone." The lamp-eyes held steady. "Deliver the ember. Keep your memories. Keep your name. Live out your life in the valley—which will remain sealed, and silent, and safe—for as many quiet years as you have left. The boy will grieve. Grief passes. Names do not come back."

From behind her, Edmund spoke.

Not Oathscript. Not the old syllables that bent fortunes and opened doors. Just a small, cracked sound, the first plain word Petra had ever heard him make:

"No."

The captain's gaze slid past Petra's shoulder, and something moved in those borrowed lights—something that might, in a living face, have been surprise.

"You don't get to decide," the captain said to him, gently. "The contract names the bearer. The bearer decides."

"He can't decide," Petra said. "He's eight years old. He swallowed his sister to keep her from you. That's not a decision—that's a child doing the only brave thing available to him, and the fact that it was *available* is the crime here, not the choice." She stepped forward, putting herself fully between the captain and the boy, and her voice dropped into the register she had not used since the moor road, the one that made the air tighten. "So I'll decide. And my decision is no."

"Consider the cost of that word," the captain said. "Not the sword's cost. *Yours.*"

"I've been counting costs since before you found me," Petra said. "Count mine."

* * *

The captain sighed. It was a practiced sound, almost tender, the sigh of a clerk closing a ledger on a bad debt.

"So be it. The refusal is recorded." She began folding the cloth, taking up the pebbles, the lantern, the vellum—everything except the petal, which she extended toward Petra on her open palm. "But understand how our accounts work, swordspeaker. Nothing is free. Not even refusal. You declined the terms, so the terms decline you—and the collateral reverts."

"What collateral? I signed nothing."

"You signed everything, thirty years ago, at a door in a valley, in a language you were too young to know you were speaking." The petal lay gleaming on the gray palm like a drop of dried blood. "Your oath is on file below the mountain. Every oath is. And oaths have margins, and margins can be collected against when the principal defaults. You defaulted tonight."

"That's not how oaths work."

"It is how *our* oaths work," the captain said. "It is how they have always worked. Why do you think your mentor left you the valley and vanished? Why do you think the wards knew you lied to the border wardens at the ford, and let you go anyway? Because the seal was already collecting. Slowly. Kindly. A memory here, a season there. You thought the valley was protecting you from magic." The lamp-eyes dimmed, and in their dimming Petra saw, horribly, the shape of a smile. "The valley was protecting the mountain from *you*. And now the protection is withdrawn, and the collection comes due, and I am authorized to take it myself, here, humanely, rather than let it be taken from you piecemeal on the road."

"Take what?" Petra's hand was on her sword. "Take *what*?"

"The oldest item on the account," the captain said. "The first thing you ever pledged."

Petra never saw the mechanism. There was no gesture, no spoken word, no flare of power—only a sensation like a page turning somewhere behind her eyes, soft and final, and the world tilted sideways on an axis she hadn't known existed.

She was still standing on the harbor wall. The tide was still going out. Edmund was still gripping her belt, and Sefa was still pressed against the parapet, and the captain was still before her with the petal on her palm. Nothing had changed.

Except that Petra could no longer remember why she had come.

No—that wasn't right. She remembered the quest. The ember, Liss, the mountain, the signing table. Those stood fast, carved into her like the boundary stones. What was gone was smaller and deeper: some first thing, some founding thing, the seed from which all the rest had grown. She reached for it and found a smooth blank place, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth lived for decades. Something had been there. Something essential. She could feel the edges of the absence aching.

Her mother's face. No—she had lost the face on the moor road; that ache was familiar, worn smooth by now. This was different. This was—

The day she took the oath. The original day. The door, the words, the reason. She had pledged herself to the keeping-oath at some point in her life—of course she had, the oath defined her—but the moment itself, the scene of it, the girl who had spoken it and *why*—

Gone. Collected. Reverting to the creditor.

"There," the captain said softly, and closed her fingers around the petal. "Paid in part. The remainder stays outstanding." She drew her hood back up, and the lamp-eyes sank into shadow, two small graves of borrowed light. "You will find the loss inconvenient. You may even find, further along, that it is fatal—oaths are strange architecture, and you have just pulled out the cornerstone to see what holds the roof. But that is not my concern. My concern is the ember, and the woman, and the account." She stepped back, and the five other ash-cloaks detached themselves from the darkness at the wall's end and fell in around her like a sentence closing. "Maren Odell sleeps tonight in comfort. She will sleep in comfort tomorrow night, and the night after, for as long as it takes you to reconsider. Refusal is not a conclusion, swordspeaker. It is a rate. It accrues."

"And if I never reconsider?"

"Then she becomes a long-term asset," the captain said, "and assets are eventually harvested. Good evening, Keeper."

The word landed like a knife laid flat against skin—not cutting, but promising to. *Keeper.* Her future title, the King's word for her, the name the court had already written against her in a book she hadn't signed yet. They knew. They had always known. Every step of this road had been walked before her, mapped and margined and entered into the ledger, and the only thing anyone had ever left to her was the illusion of choosing.

The ash-cloaks went down the inner stair of the wall, unhurried, and the dark took them the way water takes ink.

* * *

For a long time nobody moved.

The tide had turned. Far out on the flats the water was coming back, thin sheets of silver sliding over the mud, erasing the glitter mile by mile. Somewhere in the city below a bell rang the hour, and another answered it wrong, and a dog began to bark and could not stop.

"Petra." Sefa's voice, hoarse. "Petra, what did they take? What's missing?"

"I don't know." It was the truth, and the truth was unbearable. "That's what's missing. I don't know what it was. I just know the shape of the hole." She turned, slowly, and looked at Edmund.

The boy stood in the shadow of the parapet, small and straight-backed, the ember-light bleeding faintly through his shirt like a coal wrapped in linen. He was looking up at her with an expression she had never seen on him before—not fear, not the mute watchfulness he wore like armor. Something rawer. He had heard the whole parley. He had heard *Keeper*, and heard the terms, and heard her say no, and he was looking at her the way a child looks at an adult who has just proven, one way or the other, whether the adult can be trusted with the truth.

"Edmund," Petra said, and knelt, because her knees decided before she did. The blank place behind her eyes ached. The cornerstone was gone and she could feel the whole house of herself shifting, settling, groaning in the wind. "I need you to know—I don't remember why I took the oath. I don't remember the day, or the door, or what I was before it. Whatever I promised, whoever I was when I promised it—they took the beginning of me, and left me the middle." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I'm telling you because you deserve to hear it from me and not find out some worse way. I'm still coming. Do you understand? Whatever's missing, I'm still coming. To the mountain. To Liss. All the way."

The boy looked at her for a long moment. The ember pulsed in his chest, slow and deep, like something turning over in its sleep.

Then he opened his mouth, and what came out was not Oathscript.

"They're lying about Maren," he said.

His voice was rough, rusted, a hinge unused for years—and it was *plain*. Plain words, plain grammar, a child's voice scraped clean of the old language, and Petra felt the sword at her hip go utterly still, as if the blade itself were leaning in to listen.

"They were never going to give her back," Edmund said. "I heard them talking on the road, before you found us. The tall one told the others—" his breath hitched, and he pushed through it, "—told them the old woman was *better bait than cargo*. Told them a hostage in hand was worth more than any clause." His small fists clenched. "I didn't say anything before because the words wouldn't come. They only come now when it matters more than being safe." He wiped his nose with his sleeve, fierce and furious and eight years old. "You gave up your beginning for nothing. So we have to get her back ourselves. And I know how."

Below them, the sea slid in over the flats, patient as a creditor, and the first lamp of the returning tide touched the wall's drowned foundations—and Edmund took Petra's scarred hand in both of his, and said the thing that would send them down from the wall and into the dark before dawn:

"I know where they keep the hostages. Because I've been there before."
