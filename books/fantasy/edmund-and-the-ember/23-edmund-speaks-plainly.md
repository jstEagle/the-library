## 23. Edmund Speaks Plainly

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Edmund woke, and the first thing he saw was Petra sitting with her back against a stone, her sword across her knees, counting.

He knew she was counting because her lips moved without sound. He had watched her do it for three nights now. She would touch her temple and then her wrist and then the hollow of her throat, as if checking that all of herself was still there, and sometimes her mouth would shape a word — *bread*, he thought once, or maybe *birthday* — and then stop, like a door closing on an empty room. The harvester's blade had taken something from her on the moor road. He had seen it go: a thin gray thread pulled out through her lips when she spoke the word that killed the first ash-cloak, unraveling into the dark like smoke off a snuffed candle.

She did not know he watched. Or she pretended not to know, which was worse, because it meant she was ashamed, and Edmund knew about shame the way he knew about hunger — from the inside, where no one could take it from you.

The cave mouth showed a slice of night sky, bruise-colored at the edges, one star caught in it like a fishhook. Below, the moor went on breathing its long cold breath. Somewhere down there Maren was in a cage on wheels, being carried toward the mountain, and every hour they sat still was an hour the harvesters had on them.

Edmund knew this. He understood it the way he understood most things now: late, and all at once, and with his whole chest hurting.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he had not said.

He had not spoken plainly since the day Liss was taken. He hadn't known that was what he was doing — going quiet, folding himself up into the old words, the deep words, the ones that predated kingdoms and made Petra's sword hum and opened doors that men had sealed with iron and prayer. Oathscript was safe. Oathscript was a place to live instead of a thing to say. When he spoke it, the world bent a little, small fortunes turned, doors remembered they were doors — and nobody had to hear what he actually meant. Nobody had to hear him say *I ran*. Nobody had to hear him say *she called my name*.

But tonight the old words felt far away. Tonight they sat in him like stones at the bottom of a well, and the water above them was shaking, and he was afraid that if he didn't say it soon — say it plain, in the flat tongue of markets and mothers — it would harden into him forever, the way ash hardens into a grate.

Petra fed the fire another branch. Sparks climbed. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the first thing he'd learned about her, standing barefoot in her doorway with rain running off his chin while she looked at him like he was a question she'd been dreading her whole life.

"Edmund." Her voice was low, roughened by the day's riding. "You should sleep. Tomorrow's hard country."

He didn't move. The ember in his chest shifted — Liss shifted — warm against the inside of his ribs, the way she used to press her back against his in the narrow bed they'd shared, before. She had been restless lately. Flickering. He'd felt her dimming at the edges like a lamp low on oil, and he knew what that meant, because he'd heard the harvesters talk over their cookfire on the moor, casual as men discussing weather: *the King takes the half-harvested first, they keep better.*

His sister was being kept. Like butter in a cellar. Like coin in a vault.

And he had swallowed her to save her, and saving her had gotten her caught anyway, and now Maren too, and Petra was spending pieces of herself like a woman breaking up a heirloom necklace to pay for bread, and none of it — none of it — had been enough. None of it had ever been enough, not the night the broker came with his hood and his contract of ash, not the night he'd run with the ember hot as a swallowed coal and his parents' faces already smoothing over behind him like water closing on a dropped stone.

"Edmund?" Petra said again.

"I couldn't save her."

The words came out cracked, ugly, in plain speech, and they seemed to startle even him — as if someone else had borrowed his mouth. Petra went very still. The fire popped. Down the mountain of the dark, some night bird called and got no answer.

Edmund's hands were shaking. He looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger, a coward, a boy who had hidden in the grain loft while men in gray took his sister by the arms, who had heard her say *Edmund, run* and had run, who had come back at dawn to an empty house and parents who wouldn't look at each other, and found the broker's leftover page on the table, the terms half-erased, the price paid in a currency he hadn't understood until he pressed the ember to his lips and it tasted like his sister's laugh.

"She told me to run," he said. His voice sounded young. It *was* young. He hated it. "I'm eight. I know I'm eight. But I ran, and then I came back, and they were gone, and there was just the ember on the table where the broker left it for the King's men to collect, and it was—" He pressed his fist against his sternum, against the warmth. "It was flickering. Like it was scared. And I knew if the King's men took it up the mountain it would never come back down, so I—"

He swallowed. The gesture was absurd and complete: the same swallow, four years ago, of a soul the size of a coal.

"I ate my sister," he said, plain and terrible, "to keep her from being eaten."

The silence after was the loudest thing Edmund had ever heard. Louder than the storm at Petra's door. Louder than the wardens' horns at the ford. The fire crackled and spat and Petra did not move, and Edmund realized with a distant, floating horror that he had finally done it — broken the muteness, cracked himself open like an egg — and now everything inside was going to spill out and there was no Oathscript in the world that could put it back.

"I don't remember her face anymore," he whispered. "I remember the ember better than her face. Isn't that — isn't that the worst thing? I have her *here*—" his fist ground against his chest, "—and I can't remember what she looked like when she laughed, only how the light moved when she did."

Then Petra moved. Not toward him — she was careful, always careful, as if he were a wild thing that might bolt — but she turned, drawing her cloak around her, and sat facing him across the red bones of the fire.

"No," she said. "It isn't the worst thing."

"You don't know. You don't—"

"I forgot the taste of bread today." Her voice was flat, but Edmund had learned to hear the floor under people's voices, the way you learn to hear ice under snow. "Three days ago I forgot my mother's face. I have a sword that drinks my memories every time I speak a killing word, and I keep speaking them, because the alternative is watching Maren die or watching you be harvested, and I will keep paying, Edmund. That's the arithmetic. You're not the worst thing in the world either. We're both just — paying."

"But you chose it," Edmund said, and his voice rose, cracked, went high and thin with a grief that was four years old and finally finding air. "You chose your paying. I was *eight*. They sold her. My mother signed. My father held the pen steady for her because her hand was shaking, and I *watched*, and I did nothing, and then I did the one big thing I could do and it wasn't enough, it's never been enough, and now Maren's in a cage because I froze on the harbor wall, I froze, I just stood there while they took her—"

"Edmund—"

"And you!" It burst out of him, the true thing, the thing underneath all the other things, hot as the ember. "You're going to fail too!"

The words rang in the cave. The star in the cave mouth trembled, or seemed to. Edmund stood there panting, fists clenched, tears cutting hot lines down his face, and Petra looked at him across the fire with an expression he had never seen on her — not the guarded stillness, not the tired kindness, but something raw and startled, like a woman shown her own reflection in a window at night.

"You'll fail," he said again, quieter now, the fury guttering into something smaller and more frightened. "Everyone fails. My parents failed. The village failed me — they wanted to give me to the wardens, I heard them, I understand the words even if I don't use them. Sefa failed somebody below the mountain, that's why she owes them. Even the King fails, doesn't he? That's why he eats. He's failing forever and eating souls to cover it." His breath hitched. "So you'll fail too. You'll spend all your memories and we'll climb the mountain and it won't be enough, and Liss will gutter out, and I'll have eaten her for nothing, and I'll be alone again, and I want to know — I need to know *now*, before I love you any more than I already do — are you going to fail me too?"

The fire settled. A knot in the wood exploded softly, throwing sparks.

Petra was silent a long time. Long enough that Edmund's question began to curdle in him, long enough that he wished for Oathscript, for any word that could reach into the air and pull the question back down his throat.

Then she said, "Yes."

Edmund flinched.

"Yes, I'll fail you," Petra said. "I've already failed you, probably, in ways you'll find out later. I lied to the wardens and the valley knows. I've spent memories I can't get back. I'm taking you toward the thing I should be taking you away from, because the oath in me won't let me do anything else, and oaths are just failures waiting to happen with good posture." She leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her face, and Edmund saw that her eyes were wet and that she was not hiding it. "I will fail you, Edmund. I promise you nothing else. But I will fail you *slowly*, and *loudly*, and *on purpose*, and I will still be failing when there's almost nothing left of me, because that's what keeping is. Keeping isn't succeeding. Nobody ever told you that? Keeping is staying in the room after the winning stops being possible."

The ember pulsed under Edmund's ribs. Warm. Restless. Listening.

"Liss isn't gone," Petra said. "Half-harvested, flickering, kept in a niche like a votive lamp — but not gone. I've read the contracts. I've seen the ledger. There's a page with her name on it and pages can burn, and marks can be voided, and I am the last swordspeaker alive, which means I am the last person in the world whose word, spoken right, can unmake a sale." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as a knife's edge, brave as a boy swallowing a coal. "I might fail. Probably I'll fail. But I'm going to stand in front of the Ember King and open my mouth, and whatever comes out is going to cost him more than it costs me. That's the only promise I have. Take it or leave it."

Edmund stood there a long moment, shaking, wrung out, empty in the way that comes after crying — the clean kind of empty, the kind with room in it.

Then he crossed the space between them and sat down beside her, close, shoulder to shoulder, the way Liss used to sleep against him, and Petra didn't pull away, and neither of them said anything for a while, and the fire burned, and the moor breathed, and somewhere north a cage rolled slowly toward a mountain that was shaped, though they didn't know it yet, like a mouth.

"Keeper," Edmund said at last — testing the word, not knowing yet what it would become, only that it fit her better than anything he'd tried.

"Hm?"

"When we get her back. If we get her back." He stared into the coals. "Will you teach me the guard-words? Properly. So next time I don't freeze. So next time I'm the one who stands in the room."

Petra was quiet. Then he felt her nod, a small motion of her shoulder against his.

"Dawn," she said. "First lesson at dawn. First word costs nothing. After that—" she huffed something that was almost a laugh, "—after that we negotiate with ourselves."

Edmund almost smiled. Almost. And then the ember in his chest flared — sudden, sharp, bright as a struck match — and he gasped, hand flying to his sternum, and Petra was on her feet with her sword half-drawn before he could speak.

Because the ember wasn't just warm anymore.

It was pulling. Northward. Like a compass needle made of heat, like a hook set in his ribs, and through the flare of it Edmund felt — faint, flickering, unmistakable — his sister's voice, thin as thread:

*Hurry.*

Far to the north, past the sleeping moor, the mountain waited with its mouth open. And now it had stopped waiting politely.

* * *

Dawn came gray and fast. They broke camp without speaking much — there was a new urgency in Petra's hands as she kicked earth over the coals, and Edmund felt the ember tugging harder with every step north, a warm insistent hand closed around his heart. The moor gave way to stony foothills, the foothills to a road of packed ash-gray gravel that wound upward through country that grew stranger by the mile: trees with leaves like hammered tin, streams that ran silent as spilled milk, and always, growing on the horizon, the mountain.

By midday it filled half the sky. Edmund had thought it a fortress. From here he could see it was nothing so kind. The slopes curved inward like a throat, the peak split by a dark cleft, and the whole mass of it had the look of something leaning down to drink.

They stopped at noon in the shadow of a leaning waystone, sharing hard cheese and the last of Maren's bread — bread, Edmund noted, that Petra ate without a flicker of recognition, chewing steadily at something her tongue no longer remembered loving. He looked away so she wouldn't see him look.

"We'll reach the galleries by tomorrow dusk if the road holds," Petra said. "Sefa's map shows a pilgrim stair on the western face. Old. Pre-court. It may not be watched."

"And Maren?"

Petra was quiet a moment. "Maren bought us time. We don't waste it feeling guilty. We spend it climbing."

They walked on. The road climbed. And as the afternoon slanted long and gold across the ash-gravel, Petra stopped at a flat stretch of stone, drew her sword, and laid it flat across her palms like an offering.

"All right," she said. "First lesson. Guard-words. The word that turns a blow. Watch my mouth, not the blade — the blade is just where the word lands."

She spoke a single syllable, soft as a lullaby, and the air above the sword shimmered like heat over a forge. Edmund felt it in his teeth. Felt the ember answer, a slow glad pulse, as if Liss were leaning closer to listen.

"Now you," Petra said, holding the sword out to him, hilt first. "Don't be afraid of it. It's only a word. The worst it can do is make the world listen."

Edmund reached out. Wrapped his small fingers around the leather grip, still warm from her hands. Opened his mouth.

And far above them, at the mountain's crown, something vast exhaled — a sound like a door opening at the bottom of the sea — and every lamp-niche in the galleries of the dead flickered once, all together, like ten thousand held breaths released.

The ember pulled so hard Edmund staggered.

Petra caught his shoulder. Looked up at the mountain. Looked down at him.

"It knows we're coming," she said quietly. "Good. I'm tired of knocking."

She took the sword back, turned north, and walked into the mountain's shadow — and Edmund, ember hammering, sister calling, followed his keeper up the throat of the world.
