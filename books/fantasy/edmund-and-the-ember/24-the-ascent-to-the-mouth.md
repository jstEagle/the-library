## 24. The Ascent to the Mouth

The mountain did not have a door. It had a mouth.

Petra had seen it first from the moor road, three days' hard walking behind them now, when the mentor's map had finally made sense against the horizon. Not a fortress with walls and gates, not a crown of stone like the storybooks drew—just a cleft in the mountainside, low and dark, ringed with rock worn smooth as a palate. Wind came out of it at intervals, slow and regular, like breath. She had told herself it was only a draft moving through some deep chimney of the world.

She stopped telling herself that somewhere around the second hour inside.

The passage began wide enough for six to walk abreast, floored with stone so old it had gone soft-edged underfoot, and lit by nothing they carried. That was the first wrongness: Sefa's lantern, unlit since Hollowbridge, glowed faintly green in her hand without ever having been kindled. The second wrongness was the sound. Every footstep echoed twice—the once of boot on stone, and then again, a half-beat later, from somewhere far above or far below, as if the mountain were repeating them back to itself, learning their rhythm.

Edmund walked closest to Petra. He had not spoken—not in Oathscript, not in plain words—since the harbor wall. But he kept his hand fisted in the hem of her coat, and every time the wind exhaled from the deep places he pressed closer, and she let him. There was no dignity in pretending she didn't want him there too.

* * *

The first gallery opened without warning. One moment the passage was throat-narrow, pressing on both shoulders; the next it yawned out into a chamber tall as a cathedral and wide as a village square, and Petra's breath left her all at once.

Niches. Thousands of them. They honeycombed the walls from floor to unseen ceiling, each one a shallow alcove the size of a bread loaf, and in each one burned a light.

Not fire. Nothing so honest as fire. Each niche held a small, slow radiance, the color varying—amber, rose, one far up in the heights a deep bruised violet—and each light pulsed. Slowly. Gently. In and out, brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming, at its own private tempo.

Like breathing. Like sleeping. Like hearts.

"Oh," Maren said, very quietly, beside her. The widow had not said much in three days. She had walked with her jaw set and her pack straps creaking and her eyes fixed on the road ahead, as if the valley were a debt she was finally paying off by leaving it. Now her voice cracked down the middle. "Oh, Petra. What are they?"

Petra didn't answer. She couldn't yet. Her sword-hand had gone cold, and the sword itself—sheathed across her back, awake now in a way it had never been in forty years of valley silence—was humming. A low, continuous note, barely below hearing, the way a struck bell goes on ringing long after the ear gives up on it.

Sefa stepped forward into the gallery light, and the disgraced scribe's face did something Petra had never seen it do. It went young. All the transactional sharpness fell away, and what remained was a girl who had once, apparently, stood in this same room and felt exactly what Petra was feeling now.

"The harvest galleries," Sefa said. "I copied the ledgers for twenty years and I never—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, flatter. "Every soul the court takes comes through here first. They're stored. Rationed." Her eyes moved along the wall, niche to niche to niche, and Petra watched her do the arithmetic every scribe does without meaning to. "There are more lights in this one room than people in Grayharbor."

The pulse of the nearest niche caught Petra's eye and would not release it. Amber-gold, close to the color of the ember she'd first seen burning through Edmund's ribs on a storm-night a lifetime ago. It swelled, held, faded. Swelled, held, faded. And beneath the sword's hum, faint as a word spoken through a wall, she heard something that might have been humming—or might have been someone, very far away, singing to themselves because they were lonely and could think of nothing else to do.

"They're alive," Petra said. Her voice came out strange, scraped. "They're not stored. They're *waiting.*"

"Is there a difference?" Sefa asked, and it was not cruelty. It was the question of a woman who had spent two decades filing other people's suffering into neat columns, and knew precisely how much difference the world allowed.

Petra looked at her. "For the one waiting," she said, "it's everything."

* * *

They climbed.

The galleries did not sit side by side; they stacked, connected by stairways cut into the living rock, spiraling upward through the mountain's throat toward whatever waited at the summit of the dark. Some stairs were broad and worn concave by centuries of feet. Others were narrow ledges above drops that swallowed Sefa's lantern-light whole, and on those Petra went first with one hand on the wall and Edmund's grip locked in hers behind her, and Maren behind him reciting, under her breath, the names of every saint the valley had ever pretended not to believe in.

The niches followed them up. That was the worst of it. However high they climbed, the walls bloomed with lights—thinner here, sparser, some niches empty and dark as eye sockets—but always present, always pulsing, always *breathing*. And the longer Petra walked among them, the more she began to notice what she had refused to notice in the first gallery.

The lights were not all the same brightness.

Some blazed steady and full, confident as noon. Others flickered, guttered, recovered. And some—she counted eleven before she made herself stop counting—were dimming even as she watched. Slower pulses. Longer darks between the brights. A rose-colored glow near the third landing that had been strong when they entered was, by the time they crossed the gallery, spending longer and longer moments nearly gone.

She stopped in front of it. She didn't decide to. Her feet simply halted, the way they had once halted at a boundary stone, at a knock at dusk, at every threshold her oath had ever dragged her toward.

Up close, the niche was carved with marks. Not decoration—script. Oathscript, the old binding-tongue, cut deep and precise into the stone around the light like a frame around a portrait. Petra read it the way she read her own sword's hilt, half by knowledge and half by instinct, and what it said was simple and administrative and unbearable:

*Held in trust. Account current.*

"Account current," she repeated aloud. The words tasted like ash and ink.

"It means the payments are up to date," Sefa said from behind her. Her voice had gone back to its ledger-flat register, but her hands, Petra noticed, were shaking where they gripped the lantern. "Whoever sold this soul has been making their installments."

"Installments of *what?*"

"Prayers. Offerings. Names." Sefa's shrug was brittle. "The court is flexible about currency. It's the only mercy it has."

And then, because Petra was who she was—an oath-bound thing, a keeper, a woman whose entire self had been built out of obligation—she leaned close to the niche and spoke to the light inside it.

"I see you," she said, in plain speech, because her Oathscript was still rusty and this felt too important to get wrong. "I don't know your name. But I see you, and I'm going up there, and I'm going to try."

The rose light pulsed. Once. Twice. And on the third beat it flared—briefly, wildly, brighter than any niche they'd passed—before settling back into its slow dying rhythm.

Maren made a small sound. Edmund's hand tightened on Petra's until the bones ground together.

"Don't promise them things," Sefa whispered urgently. "Petra. Don't. They remember promises. Down here, a promise is a contract, and contracts are how all of this started—"

"I know," Petra said. "I signed one anyway."

* * *

They camped in the fourth gallery, because Maren's knees gave out and there was no arguing with a widow who had abandoned forty years of safety to follow you into a mountain. They ate cold bread and harder cheese in the light of ten thousand sleeping souls, and none of them suggested building a fire. It would have felt like an obscenity—a small hot mortal flame burning in a room full of the real thing.

Edmund sat apart, cross-legged, facing the wall of niches. He had been doing that for hours: sitting, watching, his lips occasionally moving around syllables he never released. Petra settled beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and followed his gaze to a niche at his eye level. Its light was white-gold, small, quick-pulsed. Young, she thought. Whatever rhythm souls kept, this one beat fast, like a child's heart.

"Liss?" she asked softly.

He shook his head. Then, after a moment, he shrugged—which was worse, somehow. Which meant: *maybe. maybe. maybe.* There were thousands of them. His sister might be anywhere in the dark above them, or already spent, already rationed out in drips and drabs to keep a king stitched together, and the boy had swallowed her ember and run and climbed and frozen on a harbor wall while Maren was taken, and now he sat in front of a wall of strangers' souls looking for one face in a country of lamps.

Petra put her arm around him. He let her. That was new, too—since the harbor wall he had flinched from touch, as if his body blamed itself for reaching and failing.

"We'll find her," Petra said. And then, because Sefa was right and she knew it and she said it anyway: "That's not a contract. That's just me, telling you the truth as best I can see it."

Edmund turned and looked at her. In the soul-light his eyes were enormous and ancient and six years old all at once. His lips parted. For one suspended moment Petra thought he was going to speak—that the trauma-cracked silence would break here, in this gallery, and he would say something plain and human and devastating.

He didn't. He closed his mouth. But he lifted one small hand and touched it, gently, to the hollow of her throat, where a swordspeaker's oath-mark lay faded under the skin, and then he touched his own chest, where the stolen ember slept, and then he pointed upward, toward the summit, toward the court.

*You. Me. Up there.* The oldest grammar there was.

"Yes," Petra said. "Both of us. Together."

Behind them, unnoticed, the white-gold niche at Edmund's eye level pulsed a little brighter, and held it a little longer, before it dimmed.

* * *

Sleep came in fragments. The mountain breathed around them, and every exhalation stirred the gallery lights so that thousands of pulses rippled in slow waves across the walls, and Petra dreamed—or half-dreamed—of standing in a wheat field at night where every stalk glowed, and something vast and starving moved through the rows harvesting handfuls, and the field bent toward it gratefully, gratefully, because being eaten was warmer than being alone.

She woke with her sword half-drawn and her mother's dialect on her tongue, words she hadn't spoken in decades, a lullaby's opening line dissolving before she could catch it. The blade slid home. Around her the gallery breathed on.

It was during the middle watch that she noticed the cost.

She had drawn the sword on reflex, dreaming of harvesters. And the blade, awake and hungry after its long valley fast, had drunk from her the way it drank whenever it worked now—word by word, memory by memory, the price of power she had spent a lifetime not paying. She sat cataloguing in the dark, the way she'd taught herself to after the moor road. The taste of bread: still there. Her birthday: gone, and she couldn't even feel the shape of the hole. Her mother's face—

Her mother's face was there. Faded, watermarked, but there. Petra clung to it like a drowning woman to a rail, and made herself say the details aloud, silently, over and over. The crooked tooth. The burn scar on the left wrist. The way she'd said Petra's name—

The way she'd said—

Petra's name.

She had it. She had it. She reached for it and it was there, it was *there*, four syllables of her mother's dialect wrapping around three letters of her name, and then it wasn't. Not gone the way a room goes when you leave it. Gone the way a room goes when it burns. She knew there had been something. She knew its exact shape and weight and warmth. She knew it had been hers.

She could not make it come back.

Around her, ten thousand souls pulsed in the dark, patient, breathing, waiting, and Petra Halloran—who was not sure anymore that Halloran was anything but a word she'd found written somewhere—sat in the throat of the mountain and wept without sound, because the child beside her was asleep, and grief that wakes children is a luxury guardians can't afford.

* * *

Dawn meant nothing underground, but they rose anyway, because bodies keep their own calendars, and climbed.

The galleries changed as they ascended. The niches grew older—Petra could read it now in the script frames, in the weathering of the stone, in the color of the lights themselves, which shifted from warm golds and roses to stranger hues: greens, blues, one entire gallery washed in silver so pale the occupants seemed less like souls than like reflections of souls, remembered rather than resident. These were the ancients. Sold or taken centuries ago. Some of their account-marks had crumbled to illegibility, and those lights burned lowest of all, barely stirring, barely there.

"Nobody's paid for these in generations," Sefa murmured, trailing her fingers an inch from the wall, not quite touching. "No descendants. No debtors. They just... persist. On nothing."

"On hope," Maren said.

"On habit," Sefa said.

"On being seen," Petra said, and both women looked at her, and she had nothing to add, because it wasn't an argument. It was just what she believed, standing there, in a place built to prove her wrong.

Near the top of the silver gallery, Edmund stopped dead.

He had drifted ahead—his fear transmuting, mile by mile, into something harder and more terrible, a child's grief annealed into purpose—and now he stood before a niche set slightly apart from the others, higher than his head, and he had gone absolutely still in the way that stones are still.

Petra reached him in three strides and looked where he was looking.

The niche was empty.

Not dark—empty. The script frame around it was intact, freshly cut, the edges still sharp: *Held in trust. Account current.* But the space within held nothing at all. No light. No pulse. Just smooth stone, polished, waiting, recently vacated.

"No," Edmund said.

It was the first word he had spoken since the harbor wall. Plain speech. Cracked down the center, small and ragged and unmistakable, and it broke Petra's heart more thoroughly than any Oathscript ever had.

"No," he said again, louder, and his voice echoed twice the way footsteps did, the mountain learning him, and he spun to face the others with his fists clenched and the ember-light bleeding through his shirt like dawn through a curtain. "No, she's not—she wouldn't be *spent*, she's strong, she's *strong*, she used to carry me on her shoulders up the whole hill—"

"Edmund—" Maren started toward him.

"She's not *gone!*" And the word came out in Oathscript, full-throated, the binding-tongue rolling off the boy's tongue with a force that made the air itself flinch—and every light in the silver gallery flared at once. Ten thousand souls, waking, blazing, answering him, and the mountain shuddered around them like a throat clearing, and somewhere far above, something that had been sleeping for a very long time turned over and took notice.

The lights sank slowly back to their pulses. The trembling stilled. And in the ringing quiet, Sefa said, in a whisper stripped of everything but awe:

"He called them. They answered. Do you understand what that means? The King's own harvest has never—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "The court will have felt that. Everything above us will have felt that."

Petra took Edmund's shoulders. He was shaking, tears cutting clean tracks through the dust on his face, and she made him look at her.

"That niche was empty," she said, gently, firmly, holding his furious terrified eyes. "Empty isn't spent. Spent leaves ash, and the ledgers mark it, and Sefa knows the marks—don't you?"

"Spent niches are scored through," Sefa said quickly. "Crossed out. This one's clean. Clean means... transferred. Moved. They've taken her deeper. To the court itself. To the King's own—" She faltered. "To wherever the King keeps what matters most."

"To the table," Petra said, and did not explain, because she had seen the signing table in no dream she could prove and every dream she couldn't.

She lifted Edmund. He was light, too light, a bird-boned child worn down by running and grief, and he wrapped his arms around her neck and buried his face against her shoulder and shook, and she carried him as they climbed the last stairs out of the silver gallery, Maren's hand steady on Petra's back, Sefa's lantern glowing its unexplained green, the sword singing its low endless note against her spine.

Above them, the passage ended. Not in stone—in light. A warm, golden, living radiance spilled down the final stairway like honey poured from a great height, and with it came music: distant, delicate, many-voiced, the sound of a court at its ease. And beneath the music, so faint she might have invented it, a voice like banked coals speaking her name.

Not *Halloran*. Not the name her mother had said.

The other one. The future one. The title written backward in a ledger page that bled.

Petra set Edmund on his feet. Took his hand. Squared her shoulders beneath the sword that remembered everything she was forgetting.

"Stay close," she said. "Whatever greets us—it lies beautifully."

They climbed the last steps together, out of the throat of the mountain, into the Ember Court.
