## 25. Court Beneath the Mountain

The galleries ended where the light began.

Petra had climbed for what felt like a day and a night through the mountain's throat—past niches of dimming souls, past stairways carved by hands that must have been very patient or very afraid—and at the top of the last stair there was no door at all, only a curtain of warmth that parted when Edmund touched it, the way a mother's hand parts a bedcurtain to wake a child.

Beyond it, the Ember Court opened like a wound full of jewels.

Petra stopped on the threshold and forgot, for three whole breaths, how to breathe.

The cavern was vast beyond reason—a cathedral hollowed from black stone that drank every sound and gave back none. And everywhere, *everywhere*, was light: not torchlight, not lamplight, but something older and more personal, a glow that rose out of the walls themselves as if the mountain's heart had been cut open and its blood ran luminous. Rivers of it ran along channels in the floor, slow and gold-red, pooling in basins of glass. Chandeliers hung from a ceiling lost in dark—wrought iron cages, Petra realized with a lurch, each one cradling a knot of light that pulsed gently, rhythmically, like something sleeping.

Like something breathing.

*Those are not lamps,* she thought. *Those are not lamps.*

Beside her, Edmund made a small sound—not a word, not quite a whimper—and his hand found hers. His fingers were hot. The ember in his chest glowed through his shirt now, unhidden, casting his face in amber from below so that he looked like a child lit by hearthfire, which in a way he was, which in a way they both were.

"Don't look up," Maren whispered behind them. She didn't follow her own advice; no one could. The widow had gone gray around the mouth since the galleries, since the niches, since the long climb through all those little dying lights, and her grip on her walking stick had gone white-knuckled. But she had come. Forty years of fear, and she had walked into the mouth of the thing she feared, carrying bread and salt and a dead man's map. Petra loved her for it in a way that frightened her, because love was a debt too, and this place collected debts.

Sefa said nothing at all. The scribe stood with her arms wrapped around herself, staring at the court with an expression Petra couldn't read—until she could. It was homesickness. Sefa had served here once, before her disgrace, before her revoked credentials, before someone below the mountain had bought her favor with a hook. This was the shape of her exile's opposite. She was looking at home.

*Careful,* Petra told herself. *It will do that to you too.*

Because it was beautiful. That was the trap of it, the first and deepest tooth of it. The court was beautiful the way a spider's web is beautiful if you have never seen what webs are for. Black stone polished until it mirrored the light-rivers, so that walking felt like wading through stars. Pillars carved in spirals that seemed to turn slowly when you weren't watching them directly. Music somewhere—or not music, exactly, but resonance, a low chord sustained by the cavern itself, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than heard. Warmth soaked into Petra's shoulders like a bath after years of cold. Her sword hummed against her hip, low and content, and she did not trust it.

And the people—the courtiers—were beautiful too, and that was worse.

They came drifting out of the side galleries as the company advanced, unhurried, silent, their footsteps making no sound on the stone. There were dozens of them, perhaps a hundred, robed in colors Petra had no names for: shades that existed only here, only in this light, dyed in it. Their faces were fine-boned and ageless in the way of things that had stopped counting age because counting had become unbearable. They wore jewelry of glass and iron. And they wore light.

That was the phrase that came to Petra unbidden and refused to leave: *they wore borrowed light.* It moved beneath their skin. It pooled in the hollows of their throats and the backs of their hands, a soft under-glow, brighter in some than others. The brightest ones moved most slowly, most gracefully, like people carrying full cups. The dimmest ones—Petra saw them at the edges, pressed back into shadowed alcoves—moved quickly, restlessly, and their eyes followed the bright ones with an expression she recognized from Hollowbridge Market, from Grayharbor's debt-halls, from every poor street she'd ever walked.

Hunger. Polished, patient, elegant hunger.

"They're wearing them," Edmund said. Plain words, hoarse. He hadn't spoken plainly since the night on the moor when trauma had cracked his Oathscript muteness open, and each plain sentence still cost him visibly, like pulling splinters. "The lights. They're wearing people."

"Yes," Petra said. There was no other answer.

A courtier drifted near—close enough that Petra could see the light moving under the skin of her collarbone, pulsing in time with the chandeliers overhead, in time, Petra understood with a cold rolling sickness, with the sleeping souls caged in the iron above. The woman smiled at Edmund. It was a kind smile. That was the worst part. It was genuinely, warmly kind, the way a person is kind to a meal they intend to savor.

"He brought it back," the courtier murmured, to no one, to everyone, her voice like water over stones. "He always brings them back."

*He.* The word moved through the court like wind through wheat. Heads turned. The music-chord deepened. And down the length of the great cavern, along a causeway of black glass that spanned one of the rivers of light, figures began to gather at the far end—courtiers parting, bowing, folding themselves aside like curtains—

—and the Ember King came to meet them.

* * *

Petra had imagined him wrong, in the way you imagine everything wrong about a thing you've only met in stories. She had pictured a monster: vast, horned, crowned in fire, something to be fought with a drawn sword and a shouted word. Her whole life had trained her for that picture. Her oath, her valley, her rusted blade—all of it built to face a monster.

What came down the causeway was a tall man in a robe the color of banked coals, walking slowly, leaning on no staff, needing nothing. His hair was white as ash and fell loose past his shoulders. His face was old—not ancient, not monstrous, just *old*, the way a tree is old, the way a river is old—with lines around his eyes that looked less like cruelty than like exhaustion worn so deep it had become architecture. His crown was a thin circlet of black iron, unadorned, and beneath it his skin gave off no light at all. In a court of borrowed radiance, the King was the one dark thing. He moved through all that glow like a hole cut in a bright cloth.

And yet everything leaned toward him. The light itself leaned toward him. Petra watched the rivers in the floor bend fractionally from their courses as he passed, the way grass bends toward a man walking through it, and she understood that this was not devotion. It was gravity. Everything in this place was falling toward him, slowly, endlessly, and had been for longer than kingdoms.

Edmund's grip tightened on her hand until the bones ground together.

"It's all right," Petra murmured, and knew it wasn't, and said it anyway, because some lies are load-bearing.

The King stopped ten paces away. Up close, the darkness of him resolved into detail: eyes like the space between stars, deep enough to fall into; hands folded before him, long-fingered, scarred at the wrists with marks that made Sefa inhale sharply beside her—Oathscript, Petra realized. Old Oathscript, cut into flesh instead of written on paper, binding-marks of a kind she had never been taught, of a kind maybe no one alive had been taught.

The King looked at them. At Maren, standing stubborn with her stick planted like a spear. At Sefa, whose chin lifted in defiance of whatever history stood between them. At Edmund, glowing, trembling, refusing to look away.

Then at Petra.

And the Ember King smiled, and it was a tired smile, a *relieved* smile, and that was the moment Petra's stomach dropped through the floor of the world—because you do not smile like that at an enemy. You smile like that at a letter you have waited decades to receive.

"Petra Vasquez," he said. His voice was quiet, and the whole court went quiet to hold it, and it reached her anyway, soft as ash settling. "Last of the swordspeakers. Keeper of the western seal. I know your mother's dialect. I knew your grandmother's." A pause, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "I have been waiting for you a very long time."

"You have me confused with someone," Petra said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I'm nobody's keeper. I keep a sword I can't draw and a house with one chair."

"Do you?" The King tilted his head, and the gesture was almost playful, and the playfulness in it was the most frightening thing yet. "Then tell me why your blade has sung since the boundary stones. Tell me why the seal-galleries opened for the boy and not for my harvesters. Tell me—" and here his voice dropped, went intimate, went *kind*, which was infinitely worse than cruel— "why the wards of your valley have kept you safe all these years, Petra Vasquez. Sanctuaries are not free. Someone pays for them. Someone *is* paid."

"I don't know what you mean."

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't. Not yet." He unfolded one hand from the other, and the wrist-scars caught the light. "But you will. It's already in the ledgers. It's been in the ledgers since before you were born." His gaze slid, then, to Edmund—to the ember burning in the boy's chest—and something moved across the King's exhausted face that Petra would spend the rest of her life trying to name. It looked like hunger. It also looked, horribly, like grief.

"And you must be Liss's brother," the King said to Edmund, gently. "You've carried her so far. You're such a brave child. Braver than anyone who signed for her."

Edmund flinched as if struck. Petra stepped half in front of him without deciding to, her body moving on an oath older than her memory of taking it, and the sword at her hip sang one clear note into the resonant dark—a note that ran through the court like a crack through ice. Courtiers recoiled. Light-rivers shivered. For one heartbeat, a hundred borrowed faces turned toward her with naked want, the way starving men turn toward bread.

The King raised one finger, and the court stilled.

"There she is," he murmured, and his tired eyes were warm, were *welcoming*, were the warmest thing in all that gilded dark. "There's the swordspeaker. Do you feel it, Petra? How loud it is here? How awake?" He spread his arms slightly, encompassing the pillars, the rivers, the caged and breathing chandeliers, the whole gorgeous starving engine of the court. "Your valley was built to make you quiet. This place was built to let you sing. Every word you speak here has weight. Every word can build or burn. You've spent your whole life rationing yourself in a room designed to muffle you—and here, at last, here you could be *everything you were made to be.*"

"That's not a gift," Petra said. "That's bait."

"Everything true is bait," the King said, "to the right hunger." He began, then, to walk—slowly, past them, gesturing with one scarred hand for them to fall in beside him, and the terrible thing, the thing Petra would hate herself for later, was how natural it felt to walk with him, how the court closed around their little company like water around stones, how the warmth and the light and the low sustaining chord all seemed to say *you're expected, you're welcome, you're finally home.* "Come. Walk with me. My nobles will feast us. You'll want to refuse—I can see it in your shoulders, swordspeaker, you wear refusal like armor—but eat anyway. Strength matters, where we're going, you and I. We have such a great deal to discuss."

"We're not staying."

"You're absolutely staying." He said it without menace, the way one says *the sun will rise*. "The contract requires it. But we needn't speak of contracts tonight. Tonight you are guests. Tonight the court is glad." He glanced sideways at her, and the between-stars eyes held something that was almost, almost pleading. "Let me be a good host, Petra Vasquez. I have had so few chances. The ones who come below rarely come willingly, and the ones who live here—" a small, bleak gesture at his shining, hungry nobles— "cannot leave. Let me practice hospitality while I still remember what it was for."

Maren made a sound low in her throat, half prayer, half curse. Sefa had gone pale as paper, her scribe's eyes flicking from the King's wrist-scars to the pillars to the chandeliers, cataloguing, translating, and Petra saw the exact moment the scribe read something in the architecture that made her stop walking for two full steps before forcing herself onward.

But Edmund—Edmund looked up at the King with his ember-lit face, and asked, in plain broken words, the question that Petra had been circling and refusing:

"Why do you want my sister?"

The King stopped.

For a long moment he said nothing, and the court held its breath with him, a hundred borrowed lights pulsing in silence, and Petra watched the oldest creature she had ever seen stand in the middle of everything he had built and struggle—visibly, humanly struggle—for an answer that wasn't a lie.

"Because I am coming apart," he said at last, quietly, "and she is very bright." He knelt, then—knelt, the Ember King, down to one knee on the black glass before a barefoot child, his ash-white head nearly level with Edmund's own, and his voice dropped to something raw. "I won't pretend otherwise to you, boy. Everyone else in this room I may lie to gracefully. Not you. Not the brother. I am unraveling, thread by thread, year by year, and the seal your friend's valley holds keeps me whole only so long as it is fed, and I have fed it with everything I had, and I am running out of everything." His gaze lifted, then, past Edmund, and fixed on Petra with a weight like a hand pressing her down into the earth.

"So you see why I waited for you," the Ember King said. "Why I have *always* been waiting for you. The ledger says you are coming to save the light, swordspeaker. The ledger says it in your own handwriting."

The chord beneath the court swelled, soft and sweet and suffocating, and all around them the beautiful starving court bowed as one, and somewhere far above, through miles of stone, Petra thought she could almost hear the valley—the seal, the lock, the lie—holding its breath, waiting to see what its key would do.

"Feast first," the King said gently, rising. "Ledgers after. Come."

And Petra, because her legs were shaking and Edmund was swaying with exhaustion and Maren had not eaten in two days and refusal, tonight, would win them nothing but a cell or a knife—Petra walked.

The court closed behind them like a hand.
