## 26. The King's Unraveling

I have worn many shapes in the long dark beneath the mountain, but never once have I worn a lie well.

That is the first thing you should know of me, before you judge what I have done. Kings are expected to lie—about their strength, their appetite, their endings. I have told the truth so long, and to so few, that the truth has gone strange in my mouth, like water kept too long in copper. And now the swordspeaker stands in my court with her hand on her singing blade and her eyes full of fire she does not yet understand is hers, and I find that I want, more than anything, for one person in all the world to hear me plainly.

So I will show her. It is kinder than telling. Truth shown cannot be disbelieved; truth spoken can always be called a trick of the light.

"Come," I say to her, and my voice comes out as it always does now—in pieces, like a bell cracked down its middle. "You have climbed through my galleries and counted my lamps. Come and count me."

Petra Vasquez does not move. She stands among the niches where the harvested souls hang dimming, and behind her the boy Edmund presses close to her side, his small chest glowing through his shirt like a door left open onto a lit room. The widow Maren is not here—the harvesters took her down toward the lower cells, and I have given orders that she be fed and warmed and untouched, because I am not the monster the road-songs would make of me, or because I am tired, or because even a starving king can afford one kindness and call it strategy. All three are true. That is the trouble with being old: every motive arrives carrying its siblings.

"You will forgive me," I say, "if I do not rise to greet you. Rising has become an act of arithmetic."

She says nothing. Good. Swordspeakers were always better at silence than speech—that was the whole tragedy of their order, though they never knew it. They bound the world with words and then forgot how much each word cost them.

"I know what you think you have found," I tell her. "A court of gilded hunger. A king who eats children's souls to keep his crown bright. You walked past my nobles in their borrowed light and saw parasites." I lift one hand—it takes effort; everything takes effort now—and gesture down the long gallery behind us, where the lamps burn low in their stone cradles. "And you are not wrong. But you are looking at the meal and calling it the table."

The boy flinches at that. I regret it immediately. Children should not be spoken to in metaphors; they take them literally, which is either their wisdom or their wound.

"Then show us the truth," Petra says at last, and her voice is rougher than I expected, worn at the edges like a blade kept too long in its sheath. "If you're capable of it."

* * *

There is a door at the heart of my court that no noble may pass and no harvester may touch. It is not locked. It has never been locked. It is simply true, and lies cannot cross it, and my entire court is built of lies—beautiful ones, necessary ones, the borrowed light and the powdered bone and the etiquette of a civilization pretending it is not dying. My nobles could no more walk through that door than smoke could walk through rain.

I take Petra and the boy there myself, walking slowly, leaning once upon the wall when my knees forget their office. The boy watches me do it. He does not look away, and he does not pretend he did not see. There is a courtesy in that I have not been offered in four hundred years.

The door opens onto a stair going down—not carved, but grown, the way a tooth grows, the way a scar grows. The walls here are warm. That is the first thing Petra notices; I see her palm flatten against the stone as we descend, testing, and then withdrawing quickly, as if the mountain had licked her.

"This is the oldest part of me," I say. "Older than the court. Older than the oath. Older than your valley."

"My valley was a sanctuary," she says. "It was built to keep magic out."

"Yes," I say. "Say it again, slower, and listen to yourself."

We come down into a chamber that is not a chamber. It is a wound. That is the only honest word for it. The floor dips inward like a bowl pressed into dough, and at its center the rock itself is wrong—thin, translucent, shot through with veins of gold light that pulse too slowly, like a heart that has learned to ration its beats. Beyond the thin place, if you press your face close as I have done ten thousand times, you can see the other side: a darkness that is not empty but *full*, packed dense with something patient and formless that has waited since before kingdoms to pour through.

The seal. The first seal. The one from which all others were copied.

"What am I looking at?" Petra whispers.

"The reason for everything," I say. "Look longer."

And because I am still, in the ruined remnant of what I was, a king—I command the truth to show itself. I press my palm against the thin place, and I let her see.

* * *

What she sees, I will set down here as plainly as I can, because this is the part of the story that has never been written anywhere except in my own unraveling flesh.

Before the valley, before the oath, before the order of swordspeakers bound the wild magic of the world into law and language, there was a rupture. Not a war—a rupture. Something on the far side of the world's skin tore loose, and the tear did not heal, because some wounds are doors. The things beyond it were not demons. Demons can be bargained with. These were older than bargaining. They were hunger without shape, appetite without name, and they poured toward the tear the way water pours toward a crack in a dam.

The first swordspeakers came—twelve of them, in the age when twelve was possible. They could not kill what was beyond the tear. Nothing kills hunger; you can only feed it or fence it. So they fenced it. They sang the Oathscript into the stone and pulled the mountain down over the wound like a lid over a boiling pot, and the mountain became a mouth, and the mouth became a prison, and the prison needed a keeper.

They asked for a volunteer. That is the part the songs get wrong, if any songs survive up there—they imagine a tyrant chained, a monster bound screaming. No. I stood forward. I was young then, in the way mountains are young, and I loved the world the way only something about to leave it can love. I said: *put me between the hunger and the light.* And they did.

But here is what none of them understood, and what I did not understand until the first century had taught me its slow lesson: a seal is not a wall. A wall stands on its own. A seal is a living thing, a held breath, a muscle clenched across a wound—and muscles tire. The Oathscript they laid into the mountain needed fuel. It drank magic, yes, but magic alone was not enough; the tear fed on something deeper, something with a name inside it. The seal wanted souls. Not their meat, not their breath—the *name* of them, the selfness, the burning particular light that makes a person one person and no other.

The twelve looked at their work and understood what they had built: a lock that ate keys. And they were ashamed. And shame, in the hands of good people, becomes architecture.

So they built the valley.

They scooped it out of the world above like a thumbprint in clay, warded it dead to magic, filled it with quiet fields and honest work and forgetting, and they seeded it with families who would never know why their grandmothers hummed certain songs, why the border stones made their teeth ache, why no one ever quite wanted to talk about the world outside. The valley was not a sanctuary. The valley was a *farm*. A slow, gentle, unknowing farm, where lives lived out their whole soft lengths and drifted down through the roots of the mountain at the end, nameless and unresisting, to feed the seal. Every peaceful death in that valley for four hundred years has been a tithe. Every contented old man closing his eyes in his bed was the seal taking its supper.

"And the peace you loved," I say to Petra, whose face has gone the color of the stone, whose hand has found her sword-hilt without her noticing, "the quiet you guarded, the forgetting you called mercy—that peace was purchased. I purchased it. With this." I spread my arms, and let her see all of me.

* * *

This is what four hundred years of feeding a seal does to a king.

I was not always this. In the beginning I had a body the way you have a body, and a name the way you have a name, though I gave the name to the seal in the first year—it was the down payment, the proof of good faith—and I have not been able to remember it since. What remains is a framework. Light stretched over obligation. When I stand in my court, robed in the harvested glow of a thousand tithes, I look solid enough. Here, against the raw wound of the world, you can see what I actually am: a lattice of vows with nothing left inside them, a lantern-shape, a king-shaped absence that the Oathscript holds upright because someone must hold it upright.

Every soul I harvest goes two ways. A portion burns to feed the seal—to be pressed into those golden veins, to become mortar in the lock. The rest keeps *me* coherent. Do you understand? If I stop eating, I do not merely die. I *unravel*. The vows that give me shape will slough off me like wet cloth, and the thing beneath—no, not a thing, a *loosening*, a king-shaped hole with the wind of the far side whistling through it—that loosening will fall against the seal from within, and the seal, already starving, will fail, and the hunger beyond the tear will come through into a world that has forgotten it was ever in danger.

"The court above," I say, "the nobles in their borrowed light, the harvesters in their ash cloaks, the brokers who buy souls from desperate parents in desperate villages—all of it, all of it, is a supply line. I am the worst kind of king, swordspeaker. I am a king whose kingdom exists to feed him, and he exists to feed the lock, and the lock exists to keep the dark from eating everyone you have ever met. I have committed four hundred years of atrocities against the only enemy that matters: time."

Edmund has crept forward during all of this. Children do that; they go toward the wounded thing while adults back away. He stands at the edge of the bowl now, his ember-heart lighting the golden veins brighter where he nears them, and he looks at the thin place with an expression I recognize, because I wore it once, at the beginning: the look of someone measuring whether they are big enough to stand in front of a door.

He speaks. One word of Oathscript, soft, the way you'd speak to a frightened animal.

The seal answers him. The veins flare. And for one moment—one unbearable, unrepeatable moment—I feel the pressure ease. The clenching muscle relaxes a fraction. Somewhere in my lattice of vows, a frayed thread pulls taut again, and I make a sound I have not made since my first decade here, and I am ashamed of it, and I do not care.

"He speaks the old tongue," I manage. "Do you understand what that means? The Oathscript was dying. Has been dying for centuries—each generation of swordspeakers fewer, each binding weaker, the words wearing smooth as river stones. Your valley was built also to hide the last speakers until they were needed." I look at Petra. "You were not exiled there, Keeper. You were *stored*."

"Stop calling me that."

"It is what you are. It is what you signed yourself to be, before you were old enough to sign anything." I let that land. Cruelty and honesty have shared a bed in me so long I can no longer tell which is which. "Your mentor did not vanish. Your mentor descended, and stood where you stand, and heard what you are hearing, and went back up, and spent thirty years arranging for you to be ready. And when the arrangement was complete—" I shrug, a gesture that costs me. "The seal took its fee."

"No." Petra's voice cracks on it. "No. She wouldn't—"

"She would, and she did, and you will do worse, because that is what keepers are for." I turn from her, because I cannot watch her face while I say the next part; even a king is allowed one cowardice per century. "Now hear the last of it. The part I have never said aloud."

* * *

"I am failing."

The words echo in the bowl of the wound, and the golden veins flicker as if the mountain itself flinched.

"Not soon. Not tomorrow. But the arithmetic is done, and I have done it honestly, which is the only virtue I have left. The tithes grow thinner—your valley's deaths, fewer and gentler as the forgetting deepens. The sold souls, smaller each year, because the world above forgets how to believe in souls at all. I ration myself now. Did you wonder why my court looks starved? Why my nobles wear light like beggars wear another man's coat? I have been stretching four hundred years of supper across five hundred years of hunger, and the ledger does not balance, and the seam is starting to show." I press my hand flat against the thin place, and beneath my palm the darkness stirs, slow as a sleeper turning over. "When I go, the seal goes. Not in a day. In a season. Long enough for the wise to flee and the foolish to disbelieve. And then the tear opens, and everything your oath ever protected ends, and it will end *because I kept my word*. Because I stood where I was asked to stand and held the door for exactly as long as I was able."

"So what do you want from me?" Petra asks, and her hand is on her sword now, truly, and the blade is singing low and constant, a note like a held breath. "Confession? Pity?"

"I want what every dying king wants. An heir." I face her fully, and I let my borrowed light gutter, deliberately, so she can see the lattice beneath, the vow-work, the hollow king-shape held together by duty the way a scarecrow is held together by its coat. "Not my crown. My *post*. Someone to stand here. Someone with a name worth spending, and years worth burning, and the old tongue alive in their throat. Give me your name, swordspeaker, and I will teach you the holding of the door, and your valley sleeps safe for another four hundred years, and the boy keeps his sister's ember, and no one else need ever be sold."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I unravel, the seal thins, and you may spend your remaining years fighting the dark with a sword, one memory at a time, until the memories run out and the dark steps through. I am offering you the merciful arithmetic, Petra Vasquez. It is the only inheritance I have."

For a long moment she says nothing. The boy's ember pulses. The dark beyond the thin place turns over again, listening.

And then Edmund does the thing that changes everything.

He steps past me—past the king, past the wound, past four hundred years of careful arithmetic—and lays both his small hands flat against the seal, and speaks a sentence of Oathscript I have not heard since the twelve first sang it: the opening of the third clause, the clause about *release*. About what happens when a prisoner's term is served, or ended, or forgiven.

The seal flares white. The whole mountain shudders. And in the light of it I see the boy's face, and it is not afraid.

It is reading.

"He isn't guessing," I breathe. "He *knows* the script. Child, where did you—" But I already know. The ember in his chest. Liss. Sold whole, sold *young*, a soul that lived its first years in a house where the old tongue was still spoken by lamplight. She has been teaching him. From inside him. All this road, all this way, the little lamp has been tutoring her brother in the language of locks.

Petra's sword comes free of its sheath at last, singing full-voiced, and she levels it—at me, at the seal, at the whole rotten beautiful architecture of necessity.

"Show me," she says, "exactly what happens if we break it instead."

And far below us, in the galleries where the harvested souls hang dimming in their niches, every lamp begins, very softly, to sing.
