## 27. Liss Among the Lamps

The galleries went deeper than the mountain should have allowed.

Petra had stopped counting the turns of the stair an hour ago—or what felt like an hour; time down here moved like honey off a cold spoon—and still the niches came, cut into the living rock on either side, each one holding its small captive light. They passed in procession: Maren with her bundle clutched to her chest like a shield, Sefa with the revoked seal-ring hidden under her glove, Edmund walking close enough to Petra that his shoulder brushed her hip at every step. And above them all, or below them—direction had gone soft—the Ember King's court glittered somewhere in the dark, patient as a held breath.

The lamps were souls.

Petra had known this since the second gallery, since she'd made herself look instead of looking away, and knowing had not made it easier. Each niche was a shallow alcove no bigger than two cupped hands, and each held a flame that was not quite a flame—a trembling, person-shaped brightness that leaned toward the corridor when they walked past. Leaning. All of them leaning. Like flowers toward a window, like the starving toward a smell of bread.

*Don't look at their faces,* Maren whispered, though none of them had faces anymore, not exactly. *Don't look.*

But Petra looked. She had spent a lifetime learning to hold hard things without flinching, and if these were the last hours of these small lights' captivity, someone ought to witness them properly. She saw one flame shaped like a young man mid-laugh, frozen at the height of some joke he would never finish. She saw another small and curled like a sleeping cat, and hated herself for finding it almost sweet. She saw one flicker in rhythm with Sefa's footsteps, keeping time, and wondered how long a soul could keep itself company before it forgot there had ever been anyone else to talk to.

"How many," she said quietly, to Sefa, because Sefa had been a scribe here once, before disgrace, before the ledger closed over her name like water.

Sefa didn't answer at first. Her gloved hand rose and touched the wall, and the stone was warm—not warm like sun-warmed rock but warm like skin, like something feverish and alive. "When I served," she said finally, "the galleries ended at the third stair-landing. The King's harvesters brought up maybe forty souls a year. Enough to keep the seal fed. Enough to keep him—" She stopped. Recalculated. "There are ten thousand lamps now, Keeper. Perhaps more. He's been unraveling faster."

Ten thousand. Petra let the number sit in her chest until it found its own weight. Ten thousand people, sold or stolen or bargained away by the desperate, burning in little rooms of rock so that a valley full of farmers could live in peace and call it innocence.

So that *she* could live in peace. That was the part she kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a cracked tooth. Forty years of quiet mornings. Forty years of bread and rain and the sword she never drew. Bought and paid for, lamp by lamp, and she hadn't even known the currency.

Edmund tugged her sleeve.

He didn't speak—had barely spoken since the harbor wall, since Maren had been taken and given back only through the harvester captain's grudging terms—but he pulled, insistent, and pointed deeper into the gallery where the passage bent around a rib of black stone.

Petra felt it before she understood it. Warmth. Not the feverish ambient heat of the walls but something brighter, quicker, a pulse in the air like a second heartbeat just out of sight. And beneath the warmth, faint as smoke, a sound: not Oathscript, not the King's court-language, but something simpler. A child's humming, threadbare and wandering, the tuneless song children make to fill silence.

She knew that tune. She'd heard it in the ember's voice the first night, pressed against Edmund's glowing chest—that begging voice speaking her mother's dialect, the voice she'd sworn to carry home.

"Liss," Edmund said. Plainly. His first plain word in days.

They rounded the stone together.

* * *

The niche was larger than the others.

Not much larger—a hand's width more in every direction—but it mattered, somehow, that it was different, that whoever had set this soul in place had judged it worth slightly more room. The alcove was cut into the face of the black rib, framed by grooves worn smooth, and inside it burned a light that made all the other lights seem like embers to a bonfire.

She was small. That was Petra's first thought, absurd and piercing: *she's so small.* A girl-shape of gathered light, perhaps seven years old by the proportions of her, sitting with her knees drawn up in the niche the way children sit when they've learned that taking up less space means fewer hands reach for you. Her brightness guttered constantly, flaring and dimming in an arrhythmia that hurt to watch, and along one side of her—her left side, her left arm, her left shoulder—there were places where the light simply wasn't. Hollows. Harvested patches, gray and cold, like snow-shadow on a field.

Half-harvested. The phrase from Sefa's translations came back to Petra with new and terrible literalness. The King had been feeding from her slowly, spoonful by spoonful, drawing out enough to slow his unraveling without consuming the whole—for the whole, of course, was promised elsewhere. The whole was collateral. The whole sat in a contract in Grayharbor, signed in ash, waiting for a countersignature that would never come unless a swordspeaker carried it below the mountain.

Which was, Petra understood with a lurch, exactly what she had done.

"Hello," she said softly, and her voice sounded wrong in the gallery, too loud and too alive.

The light turned. There were no eyes in it, not really, but the brightness gathered at its upper edge in a way that Petra's whole body read as *looking*, and then—then the guttering changed. It steadied. For three heartbeats the flame burned clean and tall and steady, and in that steadiness Petra saw her clearly: a girl with a stubborn chin and a cowlick she'd have fought any comb about, a girl who looked—

She looked like Edmund. That was the thing Petra couldn't unsee. Not identical—Liss's light held angles his round face hadn't grown into yet—but the same architecture underneath. The same tilt of the head. The same way of going very still when something mattered.

"Maren made a noise behind her," Petra would remember afterward. "A small one, grief-shaped. But in that moment there was only the niche, and the light, and the boy."

Edmund stepped forward.

"No—" Sefa said, reaching. "Wait, the contract—"

But he was already moving, and he was a child, and no one on this earth or under it could have stopped him. He crossed the gallery floor in four quick steps and knelt before the niche, and he raised both hands—not touching, not yet, palms hovering a finger's width from the light the way you'd warm yourself at a fire you were afraid to claim—and he spoke.

Not Oathscript. Plain words, cracked and clumsy, the words of a boy who had traded his voice for a language older than kingdoms and was now spending his hoarded plainness all at once:

"Liss. It's me. It's Ed. I came. I'm sorry it took—I'm sorry. I ate your ember so they couldn't get it, and I know that was scary, Mama said don't ever put things in your mouth, and I did it anyway, and I'm not sorry, because they didn't get you. They didn't get all of you. See? See, you're still—you're still here—"

His voice was coming apart. Petra stood rooted, her hand on her hilt out of pure reflex, watching a child try to build a bridge across a year of horror using nothing but broken sentences.

And Liss—Liss leaned toward him.

The whole gallery responded. Every lamp in every niche flared at once, ten thousand small fires surging in their alcoves, and the sound that went through the corridors was not a sound Petra heard with her ears—it arrived in her teeth, in the marrow of her arms, in the scar-tissue of her oath. A chord. Ten thousand voices finding one note. Hope, Petra thought dizzily. This is what hope sounds like when it has been locked in the dark for years and someone opens the door a crack.

Then the stone spoke.

* * *

It was not the mountain. It was not the King. Petra knew both those voices now, and this was neither. This was thinner and colder and infinitely more patient—the voice of the contract itself, of the ledger-page bleeding in a drawer three hundred miles away, waking to find its terms approached.

Grooves in the niche's frame began to glow.

Petra had seen Oathscript written before—in her mentor's hand, decades ago, in diagrams she'd memorized and half-forgotten. This was Oathscript, but rendered in the old high style, the legal style, each character interlocked with the next so that no single word could be altered without breaking the sentence entire. The characters crawled around the frame of Liss's niche like frost forming in reverse, and the voice that accompanied them came from everywhere and nowhere, dry as paper, exact as arithmetic:

**THE SOUL DESIGNATED LISS, DAUGHTER OF HALL AND VESNA, SOLD WHOLE BY CONTRACT OF ASH, IS HELD PENDING COUNTERSIGNATURE. APPROACH BY BLOOD KIN IS NOTED. RELEASE REQUIRES THE MARKED HAND. LET THE MARKED HAND PRESENT ITSELF.**

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"The marked hand," Sefa repeated. Her face had gone the color of the harvested hollows in Liss's side. "That's countersignature language. It wants—"

"It wants me." Petra heard her own voice as if from a distance. Flat. Certain. She looked down at her right hand—at the sword-calluses, the burn scar from her oath-day, the fingers that had signed nothing in forty years except the village grain-ledgers because someone had to. "The contract in Grayharbor. The page that bled. My mark was already on it, aged backward. Future-tense." She flexed the hand. It obeyed. Hands always do; that's the trap of them. "It's been waiting for me to arrive. We didn't find the contract. It fished for us."

Maren caught her arm. "Petra. *Keeper.* Whatever it's asking—if you sign anything down here—"

"I know what it's asking." She did. She'd understood it in the Hall of Debts and understood it better every mile since: the loop, the renewal, the machinery of the seal grinding forward on rails greased with names. Sign, and Liss goes free into—into what? A soul released below the mountain, in the King's hungering dark, with no body to return to and no road home. Sign, and become the betrayer of record, the swordspeaker whose mark doomed the next child and the next. Refuse, and Liss burns in her niche forever, spoonful by spoonful, until there is nothing left to harvest and the King takes the rest in one swallow.

Love meeting law. Law had such good posture. Love just knelt there in the dust with its hands out.

Edmund hadn't moved from the niche. His palms still hovered at the edge of the light, and now Liss's brightness had gathered itself against that boundary, pressing outward as far as the Oathscript frame allowed—close, so close, the two of them separated by a finger's width of ancient legal language. Where her glow met the invisible wall of the contract, the air shimmered, and Petra saw tiny characters flare and strain, holding.

Holding. But for how long? The contract was old, and contracts are only as strong as the fear backing them, and everything in this mountain was unraveling—the King most of all.

"She can hear him," Petra said. Not a question.

"Siblings," Sefa said softly. "Kin-blood approaches are noted. It said so. Which means the contract tracks blood." Her scribe's mind was moving visibly behind her eyes, cataloguing clauses. "If kin-blood is *noted*, then kin-blood might also be... weighted. Some of the oldest sale-contracts had mercy-clauses. Release provisions. If the seller's line performs restitution—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "I'd need the page. The original. Clauses mean nothing until you read the actual ink."

"We have the copy in my pack," Maren said. "From the Hall."

"A copy shows the terms. Only the original shows the *exceptions*. The margins. What was struck through and rewritten." Sefa's gloved hand curled into a fist. "And the original is in the King's archive, which is to say in his fist."

The contract-voice pulsed again, impatient now, its patience revealed as performance:

**LET THE MARKED HAND PRESENT ITSELF. THE SOUL DIMINISHES. THE DEBT STANDS.**

And it was true—they could all see it. Liss's steady burning was failing, the effort of leaning toward her brother costing her more than she had. The hollow places on her left side seemed wider than they'd been minutes ago. Or perhaps that was only terror. Terror does that; it makes losses grow while you watch.

Edmund turned. He looked up at Petra, and his face in the lamplight was the oldest thing in the mountain.

"You said you'd carry her home," he said. Plain words again, spending them like a miser forced open. "You said it at my door. In the storm. You put your hand on my chest and you said *I swear it keeps.*"

"I did." Her throat was tight as a drawn wire. "I keep what I swear, Edmund. That's the whole of what I am."

"Then keep *her*." He pointed one small finger at the niche, at the guttering girl-light straining toward them all. "Not the contract. Not the oath. *Her.* The contract doesn't get to say what keeping means. It's just—it's just words that somebody wrote, and you told me words are tools, you said a tool doesn't choose what it builds—"

"He's quoting you," Maren murmured, wonder and dread in equal measure.

He was. Petra stared at the boy—at this boy she'd taught guard-words on the moor road, whose speech bent small fortunes, who had swallowed his sister's soul to save it and carried it in his chest like a coal and never once complained of the burning—and felt the shape of the trap and the shape of the way out occupying, impossibly, the same space.

The contract wanted her mark. Fine. But she had learned, over one long terrible autumn, that Oathscript was not the court's property. It was older than the ledger. Older than the King. It was the tongue in which promises became physics, and it answered to meaning, not to authority—and a counter-oath spoken truly could void a false one. Sefa had told her that in Hollowbridge. *Words that open nothing and mean everything,* the woman had said, bitterly, about the valley children. But these words would open something. These words would mean everything.

They would also cost. She knew that too. Every word of power she'd spent since the moor had taken a memory with it—the taste of bread, a birthday, her mother's face dissolving like sugar in rain. A counter-oath strong enough to break a contract of ash would take... more. Perhaps everything.

Later. Petra put the thought down gently, the way you lay down a blade you'll need again soon. Later, at the signing table, when the choice crystallized. Right now there was a girl flickering in a niche and a boy on his knees and a room full of witnesses, and the contract was demanding her hand, and she was not ready—she was not *ready*—to give it what it asked.

"Not tonight," Petra said aloud, to the contract, to the King listening somewhere in the dark, to the whole patient machine of the mountain. "You'll have your marked hand when I choose to give it. Not when a groove in a wall glows."

**THE SOUL DIMINISHES,** the voice said.

"Then dim with her," Petra said, "because I don't bargain on your schedule."

And she knelt beside Edmund, and put her arm around him, and together they watched Liss's light fight to stay tall.

The niche-frame pulsed. The characters regrouped. Somewhere far above, doors of state were opening, and footsteps like falling ash were beginning to descend.

The signing table, Petra knew, was already being prepared.
