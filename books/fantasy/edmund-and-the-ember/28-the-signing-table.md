## 28. The Signing Table

The room had been waiting for her.

That was Petra's first thought, and it was not a comforting one. Not a chamber built for an occasion, but a chamber built *around* an occasion—the way a scabbard is shaped around a blade, the way a grave is dug before the dying begins. The walls curved inward like cupped hands. The ceiling was low and ribbed with pale stone, and along every rib ran channels of faint gold light, as if the mountain's veins surfaced here, carrying something precious toward a heart that was not a heart at all.

And in the center of it: the table.

It was black wood—or something like wood, grown rather than cut, its grain swirling in patterns too deliberate to be chance. It stood on no legs she could see; it simply rose from the floor as though the floor had decided, long ago, to offer up this surface and nothing else. Upon it lay two objects, arranged with the neatness of a place setting at a funeral feast.

A pen. And a shallow dish of ash.

The pen was long and slender, its barrel dark as the table, its nib a sliver of something that caught the gold light and refused to give it back. The ash was fine as flour, gray-white, and it did not sit still. Even in air that did not move, the ash breathed—rising a hair's breadth, settling, rising again—in a rhythm Petra recognized because she had felt it against her palm not three days past, through a child's nightshirt, warm as banked coals.

*It breathes,* she thought. *The contract breathes.*

Behind her, Edmund made a sound. Not a word. He had not spoken since the galleries—not in Oathscript, not in plain tongue, not even the small wordless noises children make when the world presses too close. He had gone silent the way a house goes silent when someone dies inside it, and that silence was worse than any wailing, because it meant he had understood everything Sefa had translated for him, and had chosen to carry it without letting it out of his mouth where it might become real.

Petra turned. The boy stood in the doorway with Maren's hand on his shoulder, and the ember-light in his chest shone through his shirt like a lantern behind wet paper. His face was the face of a child trying very hard to be brave in front of adults who needed him to be, which is to say it was the saddest face Petra had ever seen, and she had seen her mother's face on the morning of the oath, and she had seen her own face in the wardens' polished shields when they came asking questions she could not answer.

"It wants me," Edmund said. Plain words. His voice cracked down the middle like river ice in spring. "It's been waiting for me since I swallowed her."

"No." Petra crossed the room in four strides and knelt, so that her eyes were level with his. Up close she could see the flicker of his sister moving beneath his skin—a pulse of light at his throat, another at his wrist, traveling, restless, looking for a way out that was not a way home. "It doesn't want you, little brother. It wants my name."

She said it and heard how it sounded in the curved room, how the walls took the words and gave them back softer, kinder, as if the mountain itself were agreeing with her. That frightened her more than the pen did.

* * *

Sefa had told them what the countersignature meant while they climbed the last stair, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own death warrant aloud because someone had to. A future-tense oath. An oath written from the far side of itself. *I, who will have signed.* The grammar of a thing already done, waiting only for the doing.

Now Petra stood before the table and saw the rest of it, and the seeing came all at once, the way ice lets go of a branch.

There were marks on the table's edge. She bent close. They were worn nearly smooth, grooves in the black grain, dozens of them, overlapping—and each groove was the ghost of a signature. Not hers. Not anyone's, anymore. Names scraped away by time or by the table's own slow digestion, signatures given and spent and erased, and beneath the erasure, faint as breath on glass, the same countersign repeated in every generation's hand:

*A swordspeaker keeps the seal. A swordspeaker signs the sale. A swordspeaker carries the ember home.*

Carries it home. She thought of the oath she had spoken at nineteen, kneeling in the boundary stones' cold shadow, her mother's hand heavy on her crown: *to keep what is sold, to return what is taken, to hold the line between the valley and the dark.* She had believed, all these years, that the oath meant the valley. That home was the quiet place, the safe place, the magic-dead sanctuary where her sword slept in its sheath like a bird with a broken wing.

But home, to an ember, to a soul sold beneath the mountain—

Home was here.

Her whole life, her whole oath, had been aimed at this room like an arrow loosed forty years ago. Every step of the road—Hollowbridge, Grayharbor, the Hall of Debts, the page that bled—had not been her fighting her way toward this table. It had been the table reeling her in, patient as a fisherman, courteous as the King. The contract did not need to chase her. It only needed to exist, and let her oath do the walking.

"I sign," she said slowly, testing the shape of it against the room, "and Liss goes free."

"Free into the galleries," Sefa said from the doorway. Her scribe's eyes were red-rimmed; she had not slept, and neither had any of them, though sleep was the least of what none of them had had. "Released from harvest, restored to the lamps. She'll burn there, Petra. Dimly. Decades, maybe a century, if the King's court holds. That is what the contract calls *home*."

"And I become—" Petra touched the worn grooves at the table's edge. "The betrayer of record."

"The keeper of record," Sefa corrected gently, and the gentleness was crueler than correction. "That's the trick of it. No one who signs this thinks they're betraying anything. They think they're keeping their oath. *Return what is taken.* You'd return her. To a lamp. And you'd walk out of here believing you'd done the right thing, because the contract will make sure you believe it, and then you'll go back to your valley, and you'll raise the wards, and someday another child will knock on your door with light in his chest, and your oath will pull you down this road again, and you'll stand at this table again, and you'll sign again. Because you always have. Because you always will."

The future tense. Petra felt it settle over her shoulders like a cloak cut exactly to her measure. *I, who will have signed.* Somewhere below the mountain, in some ledger older than kingdoms, her signature already existed—aged backward, waiting for her hand to catch up to it. She had seen her own mark on Liss's page and told herself it was a trick, a forgery, a cruelty of the court. Standing here now, with the pen within reach and the ash breathing its slow coal-breath, she understood that it was neither trick nor forgery.

It was a promise the mountain had made about her. And the mountain kept its promises. That was the whole horror of the place—it was the one thing in creation that had never once broken its word.

* * *

Maren pushed past Sefa into the room.

Petra had watched the widow change on the road—had watched forty years of fear burn off her like morning fog off a field—but nothing had prepared her for the sight of Maren planting herself between Petra and the table, arms folded, chin up, in the manner of a woman defending her own kitchen from a debt collector.

"You're not signing," Maren said.

"Maren—"

"Don't you *Maren* me, girl. I've known you thirty years. I fed you soup through your first winter in that valley when you were so hollow-eyed the dogs wouldn't come near you. I watched you mend fences and sharpen a sword you never drew and stand at every funeral like you owed the dead something personal. And I'll tell you what I never said out loud, because out loud would have made it true: I always knew you were waiting for something. Some duty. Some summons. Well, here it is, and it's a table with a pen on it, and I am telling you as the closest thing to family you've got left—" her voice broke, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, by force, "—that pen is not your duty. It's your cage. They built the valley around you, Petra. They built the *oath* around you. And now they've built this room around you, and if you pick up that pen you'll be exactly what they made you to be, forever, and I will not stand in this ugly rock hole and watch it happen."

The gold light in the ribs of the ceiling pulsed once, softly, like a patient heartbeat. Listening. Weighing. The room wanted her to sign. The room had always wanted her to sign. Every kindness of the valley—the forgetting silence, the safe dull years, the sword that would not wake—had been the long courtesy of a jailer keeping its key well-oiled and close at hand.

Edmund slipped past Maren and stood beside Petra at the table.

He looked at the ash. He looked at the pen. Then he looked up at her, and in his eyes was the question he had asked her on the moor road, after the harvesters, after the parley, after she had failed him in every way that mattered: *Will you fail too?*

He didn't ask it now. He just looked. And Petra understood, finally, why the child had swallowed his sister's soul instead of letting the King's men take it—because he had known, even then, even at whatever tender terrible age he'd been, that the world runs on tables like this one. Contracts. Sales. Signatures. Adults trading children's light to keep their own lamps lit. And he had put his small body between the machinery and his sister and said, in the only language that has ever mattered, *not this one. Not her. Not today.*

He had refused the table when he was six years old and mute and alone.

What excuse did she have?

* * *

Petra laid her hand flat on the black wood.

The table was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything below the mountain was warm—the galleries, the lamps, the starving beautiful court—all of it heated by the same fire, the slow combustion of souls being spent to hold a seal together, to keep a king from unraveling, to keep a valley quiet. Warmth everywhere, and none of it free.

Beneath her palm, the wood hummed. And in the hum she heard—not words, not yet, but the *shape* of words, the grammar of the thing, the future-tense architecture of it. *I, who will have signed.* She felt the sentence reaching for her the way water reaches for a crack in stone, patient, inevitable, finding every weakness she had ever shown it: her loneliness dressed as duty, her obedience dressed as love, her forty years of standing at funerals owing the dead something personal.

If she signed, Liss would burn in a lamp for a century. Edmund would grow old above ground knowing his sister was a votive flame in a thief's cathedral. Petra would walk back to the valley with her memories intact, her oath renewed, her name clean in her own mouth and filthy in the record, and the wheel would turn, and turn, and turn.

If she refused—

She looked at the pen. At the ash. At the worn grooves of every swordspeaker who had stood exactly here, feeling exactly this, and picked it up anyway, because the alternative was unthinkable, because the contract had made sure the alternative was unthinkable, because that was what contracts were *for*.

The alternative was unthinkable. So she would have to unthink it. She would have to reach past the edge of the possible and find something there, the way Edmund had found Oathscript in his mouth before he had words for anything else. There was a language older than kingdoms, older than contracts, older than the mountain's promises. A language that bound magic because magic had no choice but to listen. She had spent her life unable to speak it, guarding a sword that would not wake, hoarding syllables she did not understand.

But her sword was awake now. It hung at her hip, drinking the gold light, humming a note she felt in her teeth. And it remembered things she had forgotten—faces, birthdays, the taste of bread—and it was hungry for more, and it had been waiting, all this time, for her to stop thinking in sentences the mountain had written for her.

"Step back," Petra said.

Maren didn't move. "Petra—"

"*Step back.*" And the word came out wrong—came out *right*—with a weight behind it that made the ash jump in its dish and the gold light stutter in the ribs overhead. Oathscript, or the edge of it, rising in her throat like water from a well she'd been standing on all her life without knowing.

Maren stepped back. So did Sefa. So did Edmund, though his hand found Petra's sleeve first and held it for one breath—one small human breath, plain and wordless and worth more than every contract under the mountain—and let go.

Petra looked at the pen. She did not touch it.

"You want my name," she said to the room, to the table, to the patient listening dark. "You've had my whole life. My valley. My years. My mother's dialect and my mentor's map and every friend I never let myself keep. You've had all of it, and it wasn't enough, and now you want the name too."

The ash rose and fell. Rose and fell. Breathing. Waiting.

"Well," Petra said, and her hand closed around the hilt of her sword, and the blade sang awake against the sheath like a struck bell, "I've got a counteroffer."

She began to speak.
