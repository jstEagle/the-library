## 29. Refusal in Oathscript

The pen waited on the signing table like a patient animal.

It was made of black bone—some long-winged thing Petra could not name—and its nib had been cut to a point finer than any quill she had ever ground herself, back when she still ground her own ink, back when she was a woman with a trade and not merely a title. Beside it lay the shallow dish of ash, grey and fine as sifted flour. The contract itself stood open between them: Liss's page, torn from the Hall of Debts and carried all this way beneath the mountain by hands that were not hers, glowing faintly at its edges where the ember's light leaked through Edmund's chest and answered something in the ink.

Petra did not touch the pen.

She looked at it instead, the way she had once looked at her sword through thirty years of mornings she could not draw it. There is a particular kind of attention you learn from a thing you cannot use. You learn its weight without lifting it. You learn the shape of your own refusal so well that it becomes a second skeleton.

Around her, the Ember Court held its breath.

* * *

The court was beautiful the way a frozen river is beautiful—all that motion, stopped and gleaming. Nobles lined the gallery above the signing floor, wearing their borrowed light like silk: one wore dawn, another wore the green-gold shimmer of dragonfly wings, another wore the last hour of a wedding feast. Their faces beneath the glow were grey and hungry and terribly polite. None of them spoke. They had learned, perhaps, that words cost more down here than they were worth.

Edmund stood at Petra's left, his small hand fisted in the fabric of her coat. He had not let go since they found Liss among the lamps. His breath came quick and shallow, and each exhale carried a thread of warmth that smelled, absurdly, of summer bread—the smell of a hearth in a house Petra could no longer picture, though she knew she had once known it.

Liss hung in the niche behind him, half-harvested, flickering. She had been brighter an hour ago. Petra had watched her dim by degrees while Sefa read the contract aloud in the flat cadence of legal language, and every degree of that dimming had lodged somewhere under Petra's ribs like a swallowed stone.

*Carry her home or the King eats the light.* That was what the boy had said, back on the road, when Sefa finally cracked his Oathscript open. And here was the home: a court of starving nobility. Here was the light: guttering in a niche like a votive lamp abandoned by its god.

Maren stood at Petra's right. The widow's knuckles were white around her walking stick, and there was ash in the creases of her face from the descent, but her chin was up, her feet planted, and she had arranged herself between Edmund and the nearest noble with the unselfconscious ferocity of a woman who had spent forty years being afraid and had decided, somewhere on the moor road, to be done with it.

And the Ember King sat upon nothing at all—a throne of absence at the far end of the floor, a shape of darker dark—and watched Petra with eyes like banked coals seen through smoke.

"The table is set," he said. His voice filled the chamber the way water fills a cup: gently, completely. "The page is open. The terms have been read. All that remains, Keeper of the Valley, is your mark."

Petra looked at the pen. She thought about picking it up.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic Sefa had laid out for her, in whispers, during the climb:

If Petra signed, Liss went free. The contract would be satisfied, countersigned by a swordspeaker's hand, renewed for another cycle, and Maren—who had been taken by the harvesters and ransomed back only on condition of this signing—would live out her days unmolested. The valley would keep its quiet. The seal would hold. Edmund would grow old above ground never knowing what his sister's freedom had cost.

And Petra would become the traitor of record.

Because that was the loop, the ugly machinery at the heart of everything: the original countersignature on Liss's page was Petra's own hand, aged backward. A future-tense oath. Somewhere ahead of her, or beside her, or inside this very hour, Petra Vasquez signed this page, and the signature rippled backward through time until it sat in the ledger like it had always been there, waiting for her to arrive and catch up to her own betrayal. Every step of the journey had been her walking toward the moment the record already remembered.

Sign, and the cycle renewed. Sign, and some other swordspeaker, decades hence, would find a mute child on her doorstep and begin the walk toward this same table.

Unless—

Sefa had said *unless* too, quietly, in the dark of the stair, when even Edmund slept. *Unless you don't sign. Unless you counter-oath. But Petra, no one has spoken full Oathscript in three hundred years, and the collateral for voiding a soul-contract isn't coin.*

*What is it?*

*You,* Sefa had said. *Your name. Your history. Everything the oath can reach.*

Petra had not answered then. She answered now, with her hand still flat on the table, nowhere near the pen.

"No."

* * *

The word fell into the court and did not echo. Down here, sound behaved strangely; the walls drank it. But every head turned, every borrowed light flickered, and the Ember King leaned forward upon his throne of nothing.

"Keeper." Gently. So gently. He had been gentle with her since the moment they entered, and Petra understood now that his gentleness was the most frightening thing about him—that a creature who consumed souls to keep a seal intact had learned tenderness the way a caged animal learns pacing. "Consider what refusal costs. The child's sister gutters. The widow returns to the harvesters' keeping. And the seal—" he spread his hands, and the darkness of him spread with them, "—the seal thins. I do not want your defiance, Petra Vasquez. I want your signature. It is such a small thing. A curl of ink. Half a heartbeat. You have given away larger pieces of yourself for less."

"That's exactly why," Petra said.

She lifted her sword from her hip—not drawing it, not yet, just lifting it, laying it across the signing table beside the pen, so that blade and instrument lay together like two answers to the same question.

"I spent thirty years in that valley doing maintenance," she said. "Oiling a blade I couldn't draw. Sweeping a doorstep for visitors who never came. Tending wards I didn't build for a prison I didn't know I kept. Do you know what my oath asked of me, down to the last word? *Keep the quiet.* Three words. And I built my whole life inside them like a house with no windows." She looked up at the gallery of grey faces. "I gave you larger pieces of myself for less. Every day. For decades. I'm done selling cheap."

The Ember King's coal-eyes dimmed and brightened. "Then what do you propose? Sentiment? The ledger does not take sentiment."

"It takes collateral." Petra turned to Sefa. "Read me the counter-form. All of it. Don't spare me the hard clauses."

Sefa came down from the steps slowly, her revoked credentials still hanging at her belt like a wound she'd decided to stop hiding. Her hands shook as she opened her satchel—but her voice, when it came, was the voice of the court scribe she had once been, precise and carrying, trained to read contracts aloud in rooms full of people who wanted her dead.

"'Whereas the sale of the soul designated Liss, daughter of Aldric and Wren, stands recorded,'" Sefa recited, "'any party bound by prior oath may lodge a counter-oath sufficient to void the instrument, provided collateral be offered of equal weight to that which the instrument encumbers.'" She paused. "Petra. Equal weight. It encumbers a whole soul. An entire human life, everything she was and would have been. The collateral has to match that. Not part of it. All of it."

"All of it," Petra agreed. "My name. My memories. My history, word by word, as far back as the oath can reach." She looked down at the sword lying on the table. Its steel had begun, very faintly, to sing—the same note it had found at the boundary stones, the morning the world came back. "And I know just the vessel."

* * *

"You don't understand what you're offering," the Ember King said. For the first time, something moved beneath his gentleness. Something almost like alarm. "A swordspeaker's memory is not a purse of coins to be emptied neatly. It is load-bearing. Pull enough stones and the wall comes down. You will stand here having won and not know why you fought. You will look at the boy and see a stranger. You will—"

"I'll look at him," Petra said, "and love him anyway. Because whatever the sword takes, it takes *into itself*. It doesn't burn what it drinks. Sefa told me that too." She met the King's smoldering gaze. "Nothing is lost that the blade remembers. You taught these people to hoard light because you're afraid of going dark. Well—I'm putting mine somewhere it can't go out."

Silence. Then, from the gallery, one of the nobles laughed—a high, broken sound, quickly strangled—and Petra realized with a jolt that they were laughing because no one had ever spoken to their king that way. In three hundred years beneath the mountain, perhaps no one had.

The Ember King rose. The darkness of him gathered like a storm deciding.

"Then speak your counter-oath, swordspeaker," he said. "But know that Oathscript is not kind. It does not accept paraphrase. It will take you at your word, word by word, and it will not stop when it hurts."

"Good," said Petra. "I've been hurt before. It's how I know I'm still here."

* * *

Sefa read the form entire.

It took a long time. The counter-oath was forty-one lines of Oathscript, and Oathscript was not a language you could rush—it had been built, back when the world was younger, to bind things that did not wish to be bound, and it required of its speakers a precision that left no room for trembling. Each clause named a category of self: name, lineage, craft, grief, joy, the faces of the dead, the taste of bread, the color of a door, the sound a mother makes saying your name in the dark of a fever.

As Sefa read, Petra repeated each line after her, and the sword began to drink.

* * *

It started with her name.

*"Vasquez,"* she said, in the old tongue, and the word left her mouth and did not come back. She felt it go—felt it slide out of her like a splinter drawn clean, and the blade on the table brightened, drinking, and somewhere in the galleries a lamp flared. When she tried to think *my name is*, the sentence ended in fog. She reached for *Vasquez* and found a smooth blank place where it had been, the way a tooth leaves a hollow the tongue cannot stop exploring.

*"Halloran,"* she said, because the oath wanted both names, the given and the earned, and that one went harder—it had been Maren who first called her by it, forty years ago, at a boundary stone, in a voice gone soft with fear. Petra gave it anyway. The sword sang a note higher.

Her mother's face went next.

She had been holding onto it, she realized—holding onto it the way you hold a door shut against wind. *"Lineage,"* Sefa intoned, and Petra said the line, and her mother's face came up out of her like a fish rising through dark water: brown eyes, a scar on the chin, flour on the wrist. Petra saw it one last time, whole and warm, and then it drained out of her and into the steel, and she was left knowing only that someone had loved her first, once, before the valley—knowing it the way you know a room was warm after you've left it, by the cold on your skin.

She did not stop speaking.

That was the terrible discipline of it, and also the strange mercy: Oathscript required her to keep talking. There was no pause in which to break down. The form was a road and the road had no shoulders, and if she faltered mid-line the whole counter-oath would collapse and Liss would gutter and the harvesters would come for Maren and the cycle would close over all of them like water. So Petra walked the road. Word by word. Clause by clause.

Her craft went—the thousand hours of drill, the forms her mentor had beaten into her arms and shoulders, the exact angle of a speech-blade's release. She felt her swordwork leave her and marveled that her body stayed standing, that the knowledge of *how* could drain out and leave the *having-done* behind like footprints in sand.

The taste of bread went, and she laughed—a short, cracked sound—because she hadn't expected that one to sting, but it did, it did, some small daily pleasure pulled out by the roots leaving a raw place.

The anniversary of her oath went, and with it the date itself, and with it the number of years, so that she stood in the court of the Ember King with no idea whether she was thirty or fifty or ninety, only that she had been keeping something a long time and was finished now.

Edmund's arrival went—the storm, the knock, the barefoot child with no shadow—and Petra felt that one tear loose and cried out despite herself, because it was the hinge of her life, the door everything else had swung on, and it was leaving, it was leaving—

—and the sword caught it. Held it. She felt it there, in the steel, not destroyed but *kept*, the way a riverbed keeps the shape of the water, and suddenly she understood what she had told the King was true: nothing was burning. Everything she surrendered passed through her and into the blade, and the blade grew brighter with every clause until it lay on the signing table like a bar of captured daylight, humming, singing, *remembering*—every forgotten thing gathered into itself, her whole history held safe in a vessel that had waited thirty years for exactly this purpose.

Perhaps that was why the mentor had forged it so. Perhaps that was why the oath had chosen a keeper whose heart, whatever else it lacked, had never once learned how to let go of a duty halfway.

*"Final clause,"* Sefa read, and her voice broke on it. "'The speaker offers all that she is, freely, as collateral against the voiding of the instrument, that the soul designated Liss may return to rest unencumbered, and no power beneath the mountain nor above it shall reclaim her.'" She looked up. Her eyes were wet. "Say it, Petra. Say it and mean it, because it will take you at the meaning."

Petra looked at Edmund.

He was watching her with those grave dark eyes, his hand still fisted in her coat, and she could not remember—for one vertiginous, bottomless instant she could not remember—how he had come to her door, or why she loved him, or what his name meant to her. She only knew that her body had turned toward him without asking her mind, the way a compass finds north, and that her hand, released from her coat-sleeve, had risen of its own accord to hover near his shoulder. Whatever the sword took next, it would not take that. Some things lived below memory, in deeper rooms.

"Hey," she said to him, in plain speech, plain and rough. "Watch this."

And she spoke the final clause in full Oathscript, every syllable, meaning every word.

* * *

The world went white at the edges.

Not pain—she had braced for pain—but subtraction, total and instantaneous, like a candle pinched out. Petra felt the last of herself pour down her arm and out through her fingertips into the steel: her history, her griefs, the smell of rain on the boundary stones, the millwheel's turning, the thirty years of swept doorsteps. She felt the sword take her whole and *hold* her, and she heard—she was nearly sure she heard, in the ringing instant before the silence—a voice in the steel that was her own voice and yet vaster, saying *kept, kept, kept* like a bell saying it.

Then it was done.

Petra Vasquez stood at the signing table of the Ember Court, and she was no one. Nameless. History-less. A woman-shaped space with steady legs and empty hands and, impossibly, no fear—because fear needed a past to frighten, and hers had all been poured into the light on the table before her.

The sword rose off the table on its own.

* * *

Later—though *later* was a word belonging to people with histories, and she would have to relearn it—there would be arguments about what happened in the next hundred heartbeats. Sefa swore the blade split into a thousand shards of remembering. Edmund, in his plain, blunt way, insisted it simply *opened*, the way a window opens, and everything it had drunk came pouring out at once.

What Petra saw—what the woman-shaped space saw, standing witness to her own sacrifice—was light.

Every memory she had given came back out of the steel as radiance, and it did not stay politely contained. It flooded the signing floor. It climbed the gallery walls. It poured into the niches along the mountain's throat, and the harvested souls there—thousands of them, centuries of them, dimming in their votive rows—took the light and *brightened*, each one recognizing in Petra's surrendered history a piece of the world it had been stolen from. Bread. Rain. Mothers. Names. The founding of a valley that was never a sanctuary at all.

And in that flood of truth-light, the court saw itself.

The nobles cried out, their borrowed glows stripped away, their grey hunger exposed to their own eyes for the first time in three hundred years. The great ledger-pages lining the hall began to turn by themselves, showing every entry, every sale, every lie the quiet had been purchased with. And the founding lie itself—the valley, the seal, the prisoner, the key—stood revealed in letters of light tall as doors, so that no one present, mortal or otherwise, could ever again claim they had not known.

The Ember King stood alone in the middle of it, his throne of nothing dissolving around him, his coal-eyes wide, lit for the first time by a brightness he had not eaten.

"You," he said to the nameless woman. His voice had lost its gentleness. What remained underneath was older, and wearier, and almost hopeful. "Do you have any idea what you've done?"

Petra looked at her empty hands. She looked at the sword hanging in the air, blazing with everything she had been, and at Edmund running toward her through the light with his arms out and tears cutting tracks through the ash on his face, and at Liss's niche, where a small flame had begun, impossibly, to climb.

"No," she said honestly. It was, she suspected, the truest thing she had ever said. "But I think we're about to find out."

Above them, deep in the mountain's throat, something vast and ancient shifted—and the first crack ran up through the foundations of the seal, thin as a hair, singing.
