## 32. Liss Rekindled

The mountain was quiet now, and Edmund had never heard so much.

That was the strange arithmetic of it. All his life—since the ash contract, since the swallowing, since the wordless years—he had lived inside a silence that other people mistook for emptiness. They looked at him and saw a boy with no voice, as if muteness were a room with nothing in it. But it had never been empty. It had been full of everything he could not say: Liss's laugh, the shape of her name in his mouth before he lost the mouth for it, the weight of the ember when it first slid down his throat like a swallow of molten honey. The silence had been crowded. It had pressed against his teeth.

Now the mountain was truly silent—the King spent, the galleries dark, the harvesters fled or kneeling in the ash—and Edmund's silence had finally emptied out. And what rushed into the space where the crowding used to be was *sound*. Small sounds. The tick of cooling stone. Maren's boots on the stair, unhurried. Petra's breathing, shallow and careful, the breathing of someone carrying a body she could no longer remember the weight of. Somewhere below, water finding its way through channels the King's people had cut and abandoned for centuries.

And above all of it, faint as breath on winter glass, a light humming.

He knew that hum the way you know your own heartbeat. He did not hear it with his ears.

*She's awake,* he thought. *She's awake and she's waiting for me.*

* * *

They found her in the third gallery, in one of the niches that lined the walls like the cubbies of an enormous dove-cote, except these had held souls instead of birds. Most of the niches were dark now. When the King had chosen to spend himself—to pour his unraveling vastness out through every lamp-niche in the mountain, lighting them one last time rather than letting them gutter in the dark—most of the harvested souls had gone up like sparks off a bonfire. Brief, bright, gone. Freed by burning.

Liss had not burned.

Edmund understood why before anyone said it. She had been sold whole; she had not yet been eaten down to nothing. There had been enough of her left to hold a flame instead of becoming one. So while the other lamps went up in glory and vanished, Liss's niche had filled with something steadier—a small gold light, round and patient, sitting in the stone cup like an egg in a nest.

Maren gasped when they came around the corner. Petra made a sound too, low in her throat, though Edmund noticed she did not seem to know what she'd said. Since the counter-oath, since the sword drank her history word by word, Petra spoke like a woman walking on river ice—every sentence tested before she trusted it to hold her. She remembered how to talk. She did not always remember what the words meant until they were already out.

But Edmund only had eyes for the light.

It was warm. That was the first thing, the thing that undid him: it was *warm*, the way her hands had been warm when she pulled him out of bed on cold mornings, the way her palm felt pressed to his forehead when he was feverish. He knelt at the lip of the niche and the warmth reached out and touched his face like fingers, like recognition, and something in his chest answered—a flicker, a banked coal turning over, the sliver of ember-light he still carried where his sister's soul had once sat whole.

*Hello,* said the light, without words. *Oh. Hello, little brother.*

His name for her, the one from before, rose up in him and broke apart on his tongue because he had no tongue for it anymore. That was the cruelty of it, and he had known this cruelty was coming, and knowing had not helped at all. He opened his mouth and what came out was Oathscript—the old language, the binding language, the language that had lived in him since before kingdoms and had taken his plain speech away when the ember settled into his chest.

*"Sessah,"* he said. *Beloved. Kin-of-my-kin.*

The light trembled. Gold rippled along its edges like the surface of a pond touched by wind.

*"Sessah,"* it answered—or the closest a soul could come to answering—and Edmund wept, because it sounded like her. Not her voice; her *way*. The tilt of it, the upward lilt at the end that meant she was smiling, that had always meant she was smiling, even when she was scolding him for tracking mud across the swept floor.

*You swallowed me,* she said, and there was wonder in it, and grief, and no blame at all, which somehow made it worse. *You were seven years old and you swallowed me. Do you know how that felt, from the inside? Like being carried. Like being sung to. I have been carried in my little brother for three years, Edmund. I heard every word you couldn't say.*

He hadn't known that. He hadn't let himself hope for it. He curled forward over his knees and the sobs came out of him in the old language, which was worse than sobbing in plain words, because Oathscript made true things *truer*, and each syllable of grief landed in the gallery and rang and echoed and would not die.

Petra crouched beside him. He felt her hand hover near his shoulder—the careful hand of someone who had forgotten whether she had ever comforted a child before, who checked each gesture against a memory she no longer owned—and then settle, warm and certain, anyway. Some things lived deeper than memory. Her arm came around him and held.

"She can't go back," Petra said quietly. Not a question. She had asked it already, or the sword had, or the mountain had, in the hours after the seal broke. "Into her body."

He shook his head against Petra's sleeve. He had asked the mountain too. The mountain had answered the way mountains answer: with the truth, unsoftened. Flesh was a door that swung one way. A soul could leave it; a soul could not reenter it, not after three years, not after being handled and sold and half-harvested and freed. Liss's body lay in a grave behind the house their parents had sold their daughter out of, and the door had long since shut, and no word in any language, not even Oathscript—not even the language that opened doors older than cities—opened that particular door backward.

But the light had more to say.

*I don't want to go back,* she told him, and the light brightened as she said it, gold going almost white at the center. *Listen to me. Listen properly, because I've had time to think in here, more time than I ever wanted. If I went back—if some miracle put me back in that thin hungry girl—I would be afraid again. I would be cold again. I would spend my whole life flinching from men in ash cloaks. This—*

The light pulsed, and for a moment Edmund saw her. Truly saw her: not a flame but a girl-shape woven out of gold, nine years old forever, barefoot the way he had been barefoot the night he knocked on Petra's door, standing inside her own brightness with her arms open.

*This is mine,* she said. *Nobody sold this. Nobody bought this. This is the part of me they couldn't reach, and it is *warm*, Edmund, and I am tired of doors.*

* * *

They carried her out of the mountain at dawn.

Not carried—there were no hands for what she was now—but Maren wrapped a lantern in wool and Liss consented to rest inside it, dimming herself to a coal-glow, and Maren bore the lantern against her chest the way you carry a sleeping child. Petra walked behind with her sword drawn and upright, though it needed no drawing anymore; it had become the kind of blade that is always unsheathed, a vessel of everything forgotten, singing very softly to itself as it drank the morning air. Edmund walked between them with his hand resting on the lantern's wool-wrapped side, feeling his sister's warmth seep through into his palm.

Outside, the world was doing something it had not done in living memory: it was raining light. The King's last spending had not stayed in the mountain. It had gone up through the broken seal and out into the sky, and now the dawn over the valley road was gold-shot, every cloud edged with fire, every drop of dew on the moor grass holding a small sun. The villagers far below would wake to a valley that magic could enter at last, and some of them would pack their carts in terror, and some would stand in their doorways with their faces turned up, and Maren's garden-over-the-boundary-stones was still only a plan, and none of that mattered to Edmund just then, because his sister was humming against his hand.

She hummed the way she used to hum while shelling peas. He hadn't thought of that in two years. The memory arrived whole and complete, unasked-for, a gift: the kitchen, the bucket between her feet, the *snap-snap-snap* of pods and the tune under it, half-nursery-rhyme and half-invention, the song of a girl making up her own music because the radio had broken. He stopped walking. Petra stopped. Maren stopped. And Edmund stood in the gold-shot morning and hummed back.

Liss's light flared with something that was unmistakably laughter.

*You remember it wrong,* she said. *It goes like this.* And she corrected him—three notes different, a turn at the end he had lost—and they sang it together, brother in plain broken humming and sister in pure light, while Petra watched them with an expression Edmund could not read and did not need to. Some things needed no translation. That was the secret the translators never learned.

* * *

They buried nothing, because there was nothing to bury. Instead they built a cairn.

It was Maren's idea, in her practical way. "Folk need somewhere to put their hands," she said, kneeling in the moss at the foot of the mountain where the trees began. "Grief's like water. It needs a channel or it floods the house." So they gathered stones—grey ones, white-veined ones, one flat slab Petra pried up from the streambed that rang faintly when struck, like a bell—and they stacked them into a small tower taller than Edmund, and Liss poured a thread of herself into the flat ringing stone at the top so that when the wind moved through the trees it would touch the stone and sing.

*A grave that isn't an ending,* she said, settling into the stone. Edmund could feel her thinning—not dying, never dying, but spreading out, becoming less a person-shaped flame and more a presence, the way a river becomes the valley it runs through. *A place you can come and argue with me. You'll need it. You're going to be stubborn. You get it from Mother, and Mother got it from granite.*

He laughed. It came out cracked and rusty, half Oathscript and half sob, but it was a laugh.

Then it was time, and he knew it was time, and she knew he knew, and that was the worst kindness of the whole terrible year—that neither of them had to pretend.

He knelt at the cairn and put both hands flat on the ringing stone, and he said goodbye twice.

First in plain words. Plain words were hard for him now, awkward, like walking on a leg that had been asleep; the Oathscript had crowded them out for so long that simple human speech felt like a borrowed coat. But he dug them up one at a time, out of the deep places, the way Maren dug potatoes—carefully, so they wouldn't break.

"Goodbye," he said. His voice cracked on the second syllable. He kept going. "I miss you. I'm sorry I couldn't— I should have run faster. I should have—" Petra's hand found his shoulder and squeezed, once, and he breathed, and started over. "I love you. I ate your soul so the bad men wouldn't get it. I would do it again. I'm sorry it wasn't enough to save you. Goodbye, Liss. Goodbye, goodbye, goodbye."

Each *goodbye* landed on the stone and rang, and the light in the stone pulsed with each one, receiving them, keeping them.

*Oh, my darling boy,* the light said, and the girl-shape appeared one last time above the cairn, translucent gold, arms crossed the way she crossed them when she was about to say something true. *You listen to me now. You didn't fail. Failing is when you stop. You carried me three years through a world that wanted to eat me, and you gave me to a keeper, and the keeper broke a kingdom for us. That's not failing. That's the longest act of love anyone has ever done for me, and I was there for all of it, from the inside, and I am telling you as your older sister who knows everything:* the girl-shape leaned down and pressed her forehead to his, and the warmth of it went all the way through him like summer, *you saved me. Not my body. Me.*

Then, because plain words were not enough—because plain words were never enough, not for this—Edmund spoke the second goodbye.

In Oathscript.

The old language rose in him whole, and this time he did not fight it, did not mourn the plainness it displaced. He spoke the binding-tongue slowly, formally, the way Sefa had taught him oaths were spoken in the courts of the dead, and every syllable was precise and every syllable was true:

*"Sessa-vahn. Ille morath, ille kest."* — Beloved-of-my-blood. Gone from sight, gone from hand. *"Tor ille vahn-seth."* — Never gone from the oath. *"Ka sessa. Ka ille-morath. Ka vahn."* — I hold you. I release you. I keep you.

Three clauses. Hold, release, keep. The oldest structure in the language, Sefa had said—the shape every oath took, the shape the Ember King himself had been bound in. To hold without releasing was possession. To release without keeping was abandonment. Only all three together made love lawful.

The last syllable left his mouth and hung in the clearing like a struck bell, and the light in the cairn-stone swelled once, blindingly, tenderly—

*Kept,* it sang. *Kept. Kept.* 

—and then spread itself out into the stone, into the roots, into the ground, and the girl-shape dissolved into a thousand motes of gold that drifted up through the branches and were gone, or were everywhere, which might be the same thing.

The stone still rang, faintly, when the wind touched it. It would always ring. That was her now: not gone, not here, but *kept*—in stone, in language, in the sliver of warmth behind Edmund's breastbone that had been hers first and would stay his forever, not a debt anymore, a keepsake.

* * *

They stood a long time at the cairn. Grief does not finish; it only changes shape, like water finding new channels. But Edmund found that his grief had a channel now, a place to flow, and it did not flood the house.

Petra sat beside him on a fallen log, sword across her knees, watching the stone ring. After a while she said, in her careful ice-testing way, "I don't remember if I ever lost someone. Before. I think maybe I did. The sword has it, if I want to ask it. I haven't decided."

Edmund looked at her—this woman who had given her whole history to a blade so a child's sister could rest—and did the only thing he could think of to do. He took her hand and pressed it flat against his chest, over the ember-sliver, so she could feel it: small, steady, warm.

*Kept,* it seemed to say, in the language under all languages. *You are kept too.*

Petra's face did something complicated and quiet. She did not cry. But she did not let go of his hand for a long time either.

When they finally turned back toward the road—the long road home, to the waking valley, to Maren's garden and Sefa's school and the door that stood open for whoever came next—the sun had cleared the ridgeline and the light came down through the trees in long gold columns, and every column was full of drifting motes, and Edmund chose to believe some of them were her.

He walked with his hand on his chest, on the keepsake, and ahead of him Petra suddenly stopped and turned, frowning at the treeline with an expression he had learned to read as *memory trying to surface*.

"There's someone coming," she said slowly. "Down the road. I think— I think I almost remember why that matters."

Far off, where the gold light met the shadow of the trees, a figure had appeared on the road to the valley.
