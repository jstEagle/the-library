## 33. Aftermath in Falling Light

The mountain went out the way a hearth goes out—not all at once, but in a long slow settling, ember after ember folding itself into ash-colored dark, until what had been a throat of burning galleries became only stone, and the stone remembered it was only stone.

Petra stood at the mouth of the great court and watched the last of the light go.

She did not weep. She noticed that about herself the way you notice weather: there was grief in her, vast and structural, load-bearing grief, but it had no face attached to it. Grief needs a name to pull on, and she had given hers away word by word into the drinking dark of her sword, and the sword had spent it all, every syllable, buying back a child's sister and unmaking a king's prison and flooding a hundred galleries with truth-light that had burned away the lie at the center of the world.

The lie was gone. So, it seemed, was she—or the shape of her that had been called something once. *Petra*, she thought, testing it, the way you test a tooth that might be loose. *Was that mine?* It fit her mouth like a borrowed glove. Warm from someone else's hand.

"Keeper."

Edmund's voice, small and plain and certain, at her hip. He had taken to calling her that since the naming—not as an oath-word, not in Oathscript, but simply, the way children name dogs and stars and the people they intend to keep. She looked down at him. His chest glowed faintly under his shirt, the sliver of Liss that remained, banked now instead of desperate, a coal that had been told it could rest.

"I'm here," she said. Her voice worked. That was something. Words still came when she called them; they simply carried nothing of her history in them anymore. She was a woman made entirely of present tense.

Above them, where the Ember King had stood—where he had chosen, at the very last, to spend himself rather than gutter—the air still shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stones, though there was no heat in it. He had gone out lighting lamps. That was the truth of it, and Petra held onto it the way a drowning sailor holds a spar: he could have taken his unraveling quietly, collapsed inward, let the seal's death take the whole mountain down around their ears. Instead he had walked the galleries one final time, this vast starving beautiful ruin of a king, and touched each niche as he passed, and every harvested soul he had hoarded against his own dissolution had flared up like dawn.

A thousand small lights. A thousand souls no longer fuel, no longer harvest, but *lit*—the way you light a candle for a traveler, the way you light one for the dead.

"He liked the lamps," Edmund said.

"Yes," Petra said. "I think he did."

* * *

They began to descend in the hour that had no name, because time below the mountain had been one of the things the truth-light unmade. Sefa went first, her scribe's lamp held high—a real lamp now, oil and wick, but its flame had been lit from one of the King's last spendings, and it burned without flickering, steady as a held breath. The light it cast was strange and gentle, gold shot through with something older, and where it fell the walls of the throat-road gave back not shadow but memory: faint impressions in the stone, handprints, small worn places where knees had rested, the marks of all the souls who had been carried down these galleries and set in niches like votives.

Now the niches were empty and shining. The souls had gone wherever freed light goes. But the stone remembered them, and in the lamp's strange radiance Petra could see them all, an audience of absences lining the descent, and she found she could not look away from them, and she found also that she was not afraid.

That was new. For forty years she had been afraid in the specific, disciplined way of a woman guarding a secret she didn't know she was keeping. Now the fear was gone and what remained underneath was just a tired body, an aching knee, a sword that hummed contentedly at her back like a cat fed too well.

Maren came behind her, breathing hard on the switchbacks, refusing all offers of help with a wave of her stick. The widow had walked out of forty years of fear to follow a map drawn by a dead man, and she had stood in the Ember Court with her chin up while reality came apart around her, and now she descended the mountain muttering complaints about the state of her boots, and Petra loved her with a fierce uncomplicated love that required no memories at all. Some things, it turned out, were made fresh each day, like bread.

"The lamps," Maren said, for perhaps the sixth time since they'd begun down. "We're carrying them up to the valley? All of them?"

"As many as we can," Petra said.

"And the folk will want to know why. They'll want to know what we did up here, and I'll have to tell them the valley was a lock, and they were the bolt, and nobody asked their leave." Maren snorted. "Half of them will be furious."

"And the other half?"

"The other half will be furious too, but they'll help me plant the garden anyway." The old woman shifted her bundle of lamps, wrapped in wool, cradled like eggs. "Fury keeps a body warm. Gardens keep a body fed. We'll manage both."

Sefa laughed from up ahead—a short, surprised sound, as if laughter were a language she'd studied but rarely spoken. "You should write that down," she said. "I used to write down everything, in my other life. Debts, mostly. I burned my page." She said it lightly, but her hand went to her breast pocket, where the ash-marked contract had been, the debt owed to someone below the mountain—and the someone below the mountain was now a warmth dispersing through a thousand lamps, and the debt had died with him, and Sefa was still walking with her hand pressed flat over her heart as if checking that it, too, hadn't been collateral.

"You're free," Petra told her. "All of you. Whatever that costs."

"It costs everything," Sefa said. "That's how you know it's freedom."

* * *

They rested in the gallery of the small lights, halfway down, where the niches were sized for children.

Petra did not want to stop there. She understood, in the wordless way her body understood things now that her mind had been simplified, that this was the gallery where Liss had been kept, or ones like her, and that resting among those little empty shelves would be like sleeping in a churchyard. But Edmund was already sitting down beneath a niche, cross-legged, looking up at the small hollow in the stone above him with an expression of terrible calm, and so they stopped.

"They were warm," he said. "When I came through. Coming up, I mean. When they took her." He spoke plainly now; the trauma had cracked his Oathscript muteness days ago, and plain words came easier to him than they once had, though sometimes, in sleep, he still spoke the old tongue, and the walls answered. "I was cold the whole way except here. Here it was warm, because of everyone burning. And I thought—" His hands turned over in his lap, palms up, the gesture of a boy weighing something invisible. "I thought maybe it wasn't so bad, being a lamp. Isn't that awful? I thought, at least she isn't alone."

"It isn't awful," Petra said. She sat beside him. The stone was cold through her clothes. "It's the truest thing anyone's said on this mountain in a thousand years. He kept them because he was alone. That was the whole horror of it, and the whole pity. Loneliness with a crown on."

Edmund leaned into her side. Under his shirt, the sliver of Liss pulsed gently, twice, like a sleeper turning over.

"She likes you," he said.

"How can you tell?"

"She gets bright when you talk. She never got bright for anyone else. Not even me, lately." He said it without jealousy, which was its own kind of grace, and Petra put her arm around him and felt the small warmth answer against her ribs, and thought: *this is what I bought. Word by word, memory by memory, this.* She had forgotten her mother's face—she knew she had forgotten it, the way you know a book is missing from a shelf by the clean rectangle of absence—and she had forgotten the taste of bread and the color of her own front door and whether she had ever, anywhere, been kissed. But this boy's weight against her side was being written into her even now, in whatever ink remained, and she resolved to guard it jealously. Let the sword drink the past. This was the present, and the present was warm, and she would defend it with the ferocity of the memoryless.

"Tell me your name again," she said. It had become a ritual between them, twice a day, morning and dusk. "Slowly."

"Edmund." He said it the way he always did, patiently, as if handing her something breakable. "Edmund. You say it wrong, though."

"Do I?"

"You say it like it's precious. It's just my name. Say it like you're calling me for supper."

"Edmund," she said again, flatly, supper-voiced, and he grinned, and the niche above them caught the grin and held it a moment in the lamplight, and somewhere far above, wind moved across the mountain's crown, the first wind, perhaps, in an age.

* * *

Near the bottom, the road opened into the chamber of the boundary stones—the far anchor of the seal, the twin of the valley's ring, and here the truth-light had left its deepest mark. The stones themselves had cracked. Not shattered; *cracked*, cleanly, like eggs, and through the fissures came a soft grey radiance, and out of the cracks, impossibly, grew grass.

Grass, underground, in the dark, fed on nothing but the death of a prison. It grew right up through the seam of the world, pale and new and utterly indifferent to everything it had cost, and Maren knelt beside it and touched it and said nothing for a long moment, and then said, "Well," in the voice of a woman revising forty years of certainty, and stood up, and walked on.

Beyond the broken stones, the passage sloped toward the outer door, and the outer door stood open.

It had always stood open, Petra realized. That was the last cruelty and the last joke of the place: the door out of the mountain had never been locked. Only the door *in* had been sealed, and sealed from the inside, by the prisoner himself, because a king who eats souls cannot bear to be seen eating them. All those centuries of the court beneath the mountain, the nobles in their borrowed light, the ledgers and the brokers and the contracts signed in ash—and any soul who wished to leave need only have walked to this door and pushed.

Nobody ever had. Or if they had, they'd come back, because outside was the valley, and the valley forgot you, and forgetting is its own kind of hunger.

"We're leaving," Edmund said, at the threshold. Not asking. Announcing. He looked up at her. "All of us. Together."

"All of us together," Petra agreed.

She drew her sword.

Not for danger—for witness. The blade came out singing, low and full, a chord rather than a note, and in its polished length she caught her reflection and studied it frankly, the way you study a stranger on a road. A woman, middle-aged, grey coming in at the temples, eyes the color of weather. No name behind the eyes. Just the present tense, and the boy beside her, and the lamps.

"One more thing before we go," she said, and spoke to the blade in Oathscript—not a counter-oath, not a binding, nothing with teeth. Just a question, in the oldest grammar: *What do you carry of me?*

And the sword, which had drunk her history word by word, answered—not in words, for swords do not speak, but in images that rose through her like water through a reed:

A door in a storm. A knock. Herself, younger, opening it to a bedraggled stranger with rain in her hair and terror in her eyes, and saying, *Come in, you're soaked through,* and meaning it, meaning it with her whole unbroken heart—

No. Wait. That was not her memory. That was the memory of the valley's founding, the mentor's memory, the first Keeper's—the sword held everything now, every forgotten thing, and it was showing her the moment the sanctuary became a lock, the moment a woman at a door chose kindness and built a cage out of it.

But the woman at the door had her face.

Petra lowered the sword slowly. Her hands were steady. Inside her, something vast and quiet rearranged itself, like furniture in a dark room.

"That was me," she said aloud. "At the beginning. Wasn't it."

Edmund looked from her to the blade and back. "You don't remember?"

"No." She slid the sword home. It settled against her spine, warm as a hand. "But it remembers me. And I think—" She paused at the open door, at the thin line of pre-dawn grey beyond it, at the smell of rain and pine and living earth pouring in like a tide. "I think that's enough. To be remembered by your own sword. Other people forget you, kings forget you, the valley forgets you—but steel keeps what it's given. I gave it everything. It'll hold."

Maren came up beside her, lamps bundled, breath steaming. "Are we standing here philosophizing," she said, "or are we walking home?"

* * *

They walked out of the mountain into the last hour of night, and the world above was doing what worlds do: continuing.

Rain had come and gone; the moor smelled of wet stone and gorse. Stars stood thick overhead, reckless with light, and Petra counted them idly and found she did not know their names either, and found that this, too, was bearable. A star does not need you to know its name to burn.

They made camp at dawn in the lee of the mountain's shoulder, and lit the first of the King's lamps.

Sefa did the honors, unwrapping the wool with a scribe's careful hands, setting the little vessel on a flat stone. The flame caught at a touch—no tinder needed, no spark, just the King's last spending answering the call of wick and oil—and rose up golden-and-old, and threw its light across all four of their faces, and none of them spoke for a while.

It was Edmund who broke the quiet. He was lying on his back, watching the sky go from black to iron to the color of a mussel shell, and he said, without turning his head:

"When we get home. Will you still not remember? Even then?"

"Even then," Petra said. "It doesn't grow back. Names don't. The sword drank deep."

"But other things grow back. Feelings. Habits. You'll learn the taste of bread again, Sefa says. New bread. It won't be the same taste, but it'll be yours." He rolled onto his side and propped his head on his hand, and the sliver in his chest glowed through his shirt, a second small lamp. "So maybe someday you'll be somebody new. And maybe that somebody will be glad she's not you. And maybe that somebody will still keep me."

"Definitely that last one," Petra said. "That part's not memory. That part's load-bearing."

He smiled. Then his brow furrowed, the particular furrow he got when Oathscript stirred in him, when the old tongue pressed at the backs of his teeth wanting out. He sat up. He looked at her very seriously, the way he had looked at her the first night, barefoot in a storm, carrying a stolen fire.

And he spoke one word in Oathscript.

It was short. It was simple. It was, she understood instantly, a guard-word—the first one, the root one, the word her own mentor must have spoken over her cradle or her oath or her door, the word that means, in the tongue older than kingdoms: *kept*. Not keeper. Kept. The thing and its guardian folded into a single sound, so that neither could be lost without the other knowing.

The word went into her like rain into dry ground. It settled nowhere in particular and everywhere at once. It did not return her name—that was spent, spent truly, spent buying back the light. But it gave her a place to stand. A word-shaped hearth in the empty house of herself, with a fire already laid.

"What was that?" Maren asked sharply. "What did he do? Girl, are you all right?"

Petra found she was smiling. Her face did it without consulting her, the way faces do.

"I'm kept," she said, and heard the word land true. "He guarded me. Turnabout." She looked at Edmund, and Edmund looked back, grave and glowing, apprentice and master and neither and both. "Don't teach that one to just anybody."

"I'll teach it to the valley," he said. "Everyone should get to be kept. That's what he got wrong, the King. He thought keeping meant holding on so tight nothing leaves." He cupped his hands around the lamp-flame, carefully, feeling its warmth without smothering it. "Keeping means making room."

* * *

They struck camp mid-morning and turned their faces west, toward the valley, toward the boundary stones that no longer bound anything, toward a village waking into magic like a sleeper waking into a house that has rearranged itself in the night. The road ran green and wet under a rinsed sky. Behind them the mountain stood dark and silent at last, its long hunger ended, its galleries empty, its king dispersed into a hundred small flames that rode in their packs like passengers, like pilgrims, like seeds.

Petra walked at the rear, as she always had, keeping watch over her strange small company: the widow with her stick and her fury and her garden plans, the scribe with her empty pocket and her unbought future, the boy with his sister's light and his older-than-kingdoms tongue. Her sword sang softly at her back. Her name was gone. Her hands were warm.

Somewhere ahead, over the hills, a valley that had forgotten itself for a thousand years was beginning, stone by stone, field by field, frightened and free, to remember.

And Petra—who had been many things, oath-bound and oath-broken, key and keeper and traitor-of-record and savior, and was now, gloriously, none of them—walked toward it carrying fire, and could not wait to see what they would all become.

Then Edmund, walking point with Sefa, stopped dead in the road.

He raised one hand. The company halted. Ahead, where the track bent around the shoulder of the first hill, something glinted—metal, moving, deliberate. Figures on the road between them and home, half a dozen, closing at a walk. Not ash-cloaks; the ash-cloaks were ash. These wore travel-stained leathers and the badges of no court Petra knew, and at their head rode a woman on a dun mare, and even at this distance Petra saw the woman's eyes fix on Edmund's glowing chest, and widen, and fill with tears.

Edmund made a small sound. He grabbed Petra's sleeve with both hands.

"Keeper," he whispered. "I know her."
