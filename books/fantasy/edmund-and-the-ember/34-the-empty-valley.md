## 34. The Empty Valley

Maren had lived forty years in a place that smelled of nothing, and she did not know it until the day she came home and the air bit her.

That was how she thought of it afterward, though *bit* was too sharp a word for what happened at the boundary stones. It was more that the valley's air had always been bread left out overnight—soft, stale, safe—and now, as their little company crested the last ridge with lamps still guttering from the King's final spending, the wind coming up the slope tasted of rain on hot stone, of pine sap, of something green and impatient pushing through old ground. The world's breath, unfiltered. She stood at the ridgeline with her bundle of supplies digging into her shoulder and wept without quite knowing why, the way you weep at a wedding or a funeral, when the body understands a thing before the mind consents to.

"Are you all right?" Edmund asked. He was taller than when he'd knocked on Petra's door—no, that wasn't true, it had only been weeks—but he carried himself differently, shoulders squared like a boy practicing at being a man. The ember-light under his collarbones pulsed gently when he spoke, a keepsake now, warm as a hearthstone seen through a window on a cold night.

"I'm old," Maren said, which was not an answer, and he had the grace not to say so.

Behind them the mountain was dark. Truly dark, for the first time in anyone's memory—not the brooding, watchful dark of the seal, but the honest dark of any dead thing, cooling. The lamps they carried had been lit from the King's last burning, and Maren kept hers cupped close as if it might go out if she thought about it too hard. Petra walked ahead of them all, silent, straight-backed, her sword slung across her back like a yoke. She had not spoken since the galleries. When Edmund took her hand at the ford, she'd let him, and looked at him with the patient blankness of a woman waiting to be told who she was.

Maren had watched enough of the world by then to know: that was not emptiness. That was a room swept clean, waiting to see what would be brought into it.

* * *

The valley did not look empty. That was the first wrongness.

From the ridge it lay below them exactly as it always had—the mill, the meeting hall, the crooked lanes, smoke rising from chimneys in the gray hour before full morning—and yet every line of it seemed drawn again, inked over, the way a faded letter looks when someone traces the words in fresh pen. The willows along the stream were greener than willows should be in that season. The water caught light and threw it back in colors Maren had no names for, colors she had only ever seen in dreams she'd been embarrassed to describe. And beneath everything, faint as a pulse under a wrist, ran a hum she felt in her teeth rather than heard.

"The wards are gone," Sefa said quietly. The scribe stood beside her, travel-worn, her revoked credentials long since burned but her hands still moving in the small habitual gestures of a woman cataloguing the world. "No. Not gone. *Open.* There's a difference. A door unlocked is still a door."

"It's been open since she broke it," Maren said.

"Yes." Sefa's eyes went to Petra's back. "And everything on the other side is going to want to walk through."

They descended. The village saw them coming—of course it did; there were watchers on the meeting-hall roof, and word ran ahead of them down the lanes like spilled water finding its channels. By the time they reached the well at the center of things, half the valley had gathered, and Maren read their faces the way she'd learned to read weather in forty years of widowhood: there was fear in most of them, and under the fear, worse, something hungry and bewildered, the look of people who have been promised safety all their lives and have just discovered the promise was a cage.

Old Tam the smith pushed to the front. His arms were bare and his forearms black to the elbow, and behind him his forge roared hotter than Maren had ever seen it, flames standing up straight and blue-hearted, fed by something that was not bellows.

"What did you do?" he demanded. Not to Maren—to Petra.

Petra said nothing. She never said anything now. It was Edmund who stepped forward, and Maren's heart clenched to see it, a boy of perhaps ten years putting himself between a crowd and the woman who had saved them all.

"She set us free," Edmund said. "The valley was a lock. She broke the lock."

"A lock keeps things *out*," Tam said.

"A lock keeps things *in*," Sefa said, "when it's built around a prisoner. Ask your grandmothers what the founding charter actually says. I've read it. Most of you can't—that was rather the point."

The crowd murmured. Maren watched faces turn away and come back, watched a young mother pull her child close and then, shamefacedly, let the child wriggle free to stare at Edmund's glowing chest. Fear and wonder, wonder and fear, churning together like cream before butter. This was the thing no one had warned her about in forty years of quiet: that freedom arrives looking exactly like catastrophe, and only afterward, much later, do you learn which one it was.

"We don't know what comes in," Tam said, quieter now. "We don't know what we are anymore. My fire does things. Hesta's wellwater sings. The barley on the south field came up *overnight*, Maren, overnight, knee-high, and it hums when the wind isn't blowing. People are packing carts. The Aldous family left before dawn. The Brennans are loading theirs now."

Some flee, Maren thought. Some stay. It had always been thus, in every story worth telling; the only question was which side of the door you'd be standing on when it swung.

She surprised herself with her answer.

"Then let them go," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and certain, the voice she'd used once, decades ago, to defend a strange silent woman and a stranger silent boy before the elders. "Let them run to some other valley and spend thirty years convincing themselves this was a bad dream. But I buried a husband here. I buried a son here. I am not leaving their ground because the ground has started talking back."

Tam stared at her. Around him the crowd did that thing crowds do, rippling with the opinions of individuals too afraid to voice them alone.

"And what does it say?" someone called from the back. "The ground? What does it say?"

Maren knelt, right there in front of everyone, her knees cracking like green wood, and pressed her palm flat against the packed earth of the lane.

It was warm. Forty years she had known this soil as cool and dumb as potter's clay, and now it was warm as a sleeping animal, and beneath her hand she felt—or imagined, and decided she didn't care which—a slow deep rhythm, patient, vast, like the heartbeat of something turning over in its sleep after a very long night.

"It says good morning," Maren said, and stood up, and dared anyone to laugh.

Nobody laughed. But nobody cheered either. The crowd thinned the way fog thins—not deciding, just drifting—and by noon perhaps a third of the carts that had been loaded were unloaded again, and the rest were gone down the south road, and the valley belonged to the frightened and the stubborn, which is to say, to the beginning of every new thing.

* * *

Petra's house—no. Maren corrected herself. The house on the hill where Petra had lived her whole exile was empty now, its door standing open to the wind, and Maren could not bring herself to call it by the old woman's name because the old woman had traded her name away, and names, Maren was learning, were not the small things she'd once believed.

So she called it *the house on the hill*, and she went there on the third evening after their return, when the others were asleep in the meeting hall, and she stood in its doorway and looked at the cold hearth and the sword-marks on the floorboards and the dust lying over everything like a second snow.

Forty years she had lived two lanes down from this house and never crossed its threshold. Forty years of nodding to Petra at the well, of leaving bread on the step in hard winters without knocking, of watching the one true mystery of the valley tend her unwakeable sword and keep her unbreakable silence. Maren had told herself it was respect. She knew better now. It had been fear, dressed in its Sunday clothes.

"I'm sorry," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the absent woman, to herself. "I should have come sooner. I should have come decades sooner. I should have come the first winter and dragged you to my table and made you eat soup until you remembered how to be a person among persons."

The house did not answer. Houses don't. But the wind came through the open door and stirred the dust, and for one absurd moment the motes rose and hung in the lamplight like a held breath, and Maren chose—deliberately, the way you choose to plant a seed—to believe the house had heard.

She claimed it that night. Not by law; there was no law left to claim it by, and the elders who remained had lost their appetite for decrees. She claimed it the older way: she slept beneath its roof, and in the morning she swept the floors, and she put her husband's carving knife and her son's wooden bird and her own forty years of mended linens into its cupboards, and when Tam came up the hill a week later, hat in hand, to ask delicately whether the property ought to revert to the commons, she handed him a cup of tea grown in the garden she'd already started and said, "Revert it yourself, if you've the stomach. I'll pour you another cup while you try."

He drank the tea. He stayed an hour. He never raised the question again, and in the autumn he forged her a new spade, unasked, and left it leaning against her door, which in the grammar of that valley was as good as a deed and a blessing both.

* * *

The boundary stones were the last thing, and Maren saved them for spring.

They stood in their broken ring at the valley's mouth where the road narrowed between the hills—seven stones, six of them cracked clean through when the seal shattered, their runes gone dull as old scars. For months the villagers had detoured around them even in passing, giving the broken ring the wide berth once given to graves and gallows. Children dared each other to touch one and ran shrieking when they did, reporting that the stone was *warm*, that it *hummed*, that a dandelion growing in the crack had opened *out of season*. The stories grew legs. A stone was said to have wept. A stone was said to have sung a lullaby in a dead woman's voice.

Maren went out on the first mild morning of spring with a basket of seed potatoes, a spade, and no audience—which was to say, an audience of three, because Edmund followed her, and where Edmund went Petra went, and where those two went Sefa went taking notes.

"You're going to dig them up?" Edmund asked. He crouched by the largest stone, the keystone, tracing its fractured rune with one finger. The ember in his chest glowed brighter near it, Maren had noticed; whatever the King had spent himself becoming, some of it recognized the old architecture of his prison.

"No," Maren said. She spat on her palms and took up the spade. "I'm going to plant on top of them. There's a difference. Digging up says the past is a mistake to be erased. Planting says the past is ground, and ground is for growing."

Sefa's pen scratched. "I'm writing that down."

"Don't you dare make me sound wise. I'm an old woman playing in the dirt."

"You're an old woman performing an act of Oathscript," Sefa said mildly. "Unbinding by cultivation. The oldest form there is. The founders used it to raise the wards; you're using it to bury them. Everything I know about the craft says your garden will grow strangely, Maren Vasquez—"

"Maren Halloran," Maren said automatically, her married name, and then stopped, spade in the earth, because she realized she had chosen it. Chosen it freely, for the first time in forty years, not because the valley required a name of her but because she wanted one. Her eyes stung. "Well," she said gruffly. "There you are. First magic I've ever done and it's only naming myself."

Edmund laughed—a bright, startling sound, rare as a comet—and even Petra's mouth moved at its corner, that ghost of a smile she wore these days like a garment borrowed from someone kinder.

Maren turned the first spadeful of earth over the keystone. The soil came up dark and rich and *warm*, steaming faintly in the cool air, and when it fell back over the broken rune she felt the hum beneath her feet change pitch—something easing, something settling, a long-held note resolving at last into rest. She planted potatoes in the crack of the second stone and beans against the third, and onions along the ring's whole circumference, because onions keep, and a woman who had buried a husband and a son had earned the right to plant things that keep.

By midmorning a few of the braver children had drifted up to watch, and by noon there were a dozen of them, kneeling in the dirt, asking questions faster than she could answer—*what does this one grow, why this spot, can I have a row, will it really come up strange*—and Maren found herself teaching, found the words coming easily, found that forty years of silence had stored up in her like rain in a dry cistern and was pouring out now all at once.

"Everything grows strange here now," she told them, tamping soil around the last seed potato. "That's not a warning. That's the whole point. Strange is just a word the frightened use for alive."

* * *

It was Edmund who brought the news up the hill that evening, running the way boys run when the news is too big to walk with, and Maren knew before he spoke, from the shape of his excitement, that the world beyond the valley had begun to move.

"Travelers!" he gasped, hands on knees, ember blazing through his shirt like a lantern. "On the south road—three of them, maybe four—a cart, and they've stopped at the stones, at your garden, they're staring at the beans—they're asking if anyone lives here, if the valley takes people—" He straightened, breathless, lit from within. "They're asking at *your door*, Maren. They're asking at the door where I knocked."

Maren set down her hoe. She looked west, where the sun was going down fat and gold over the hills that had kept her prisoner and her sanctuary both, and she felt the old fear rise in her throat, the forty-year fear, the shut-door fear—and she let it pass through her and out, like weather.

"Well then," she said. "I suppose someone had better answer it."

Down in the gathering dusk, the travelers' lamp flickered at the broken ring, small and far off and coming closer—and beside her, Petra turned toward the light with her empty, waiting face, and Edmund reached up and took her hand, and Maren understood that whatever name the boy was going to give her, whatever word he'd been carrying all this way like a seed in his pocket, he was going to speak it soon.

She hoped, with her whole planted and rooted heart, that she'd be there to hear it.
