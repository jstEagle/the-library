## 36. Sefa's Ledger Closed

The page did not want to burn.

Sefa had expected it to go up like any other paper — she had burned a thousand pages in her years beneath the mountain, contracts voided by death or default, the ash swept into the same gray bins as candle stubs and chicken bones. A debt was only ink. She had told herself that for thirty years. Ink was ink was ink.

But the ledger page lay on the flagstones of the old schoolhouse and refused the flame, curling away from it like a hand withdrawing from a stove, and in the firelight the countersignatures seemed to writhe, the seal of the Hall of Debts pulsing faintly, a heartbeat made of wax.

"You have to mean it," said the boy.

Edmund sat cross-legged on the floor beside her, chin propped on his fists, watching with the patience of someone who had once swallowed his sister to keep her safe. He was nine, perhaps ten; it was hard to tell with children who had been traded. His chest gave off a warmth Sefa could feel from where she knelt, a hearth-warmth that had nothing to do with fire. The sliver of Liss that remained in him glowed through his shirt when he breathed out, dimming when he breathed in, like something learning again how lungs worked.

"Mean what?" Sefa asked.

"The burning." He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "Words only do what you mean. That's what Keeper says."

*Keeper.* Petra Halloran, who no longer had a name to be Petra with, who walked the valley lanes these three weeks since the mountain went dark, answering to a word that had once been a chain. Sefa still caught herself thinking of her as *the swordspeaker*, or *the woman from the records*, because Sefa had spent so much of her life knowing people as entries — names, debts, dates of sale — that a person without an entry felt to her like a room without walls.

She looked down at the page again. Her page. HEREFIN SEFA, SCRIBE OF THE THIRD GALLERY, DEBT OUTSTANDING: ONE FAVOR, PAYABLE TO THE COURT BENEATH THE MOUNTAIN. She had carried it out of Grayharbor folded against her heart, through the pass, down into the waking valley, telling herself she would burn it at the first opportunity, and then finding opportunities and declining them, one after another, the way a prisoner declines the open door because the corridor beyond is dark.

The favor had never been named. That was the elegance of it, the horror of it. Somewhere below, a broker or a king or a king's clerk held a blank claim on her future, and every choice she'd made since signing — the translation for strangers on the road, the theft in the Hall of Debts, the walk into the mountain's throat — she could not swear had been her own wanting. Perhaps she had wanted them. Perhaps the page had pulled her like a hook pulls a fish. She would never know now. That was the part no one warned you about, when they spoke of freedom as if it were simply a door swinging wide: you came out of servitude unable to tell which of your loves had been yours.

"I mean it," she said.

"Then why won't it burn?"

"Because I've meant things before that turned out not to be mine."

Edmund considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Keeper says you can borrow meaning. Like a cup." He cupped his hands to demonstrate. "You hold it out and somebody pours."

"And who would pour for me?"

"Me," he said simply. "I owe you. You found my sister's word. *Liss*, in all that Oathscript, hidden in the contract like a seed." He leaned forward and blew gently across the page, the way one blows on embers — and of course, Sefa thought, of course, that is exactly what he is, an ember-keeper, a boy with banked coals behind his ribs — and the flame took.

It went up green first. Then gold. Then it was only paper burning, ordinary and quick, and the ash lifted on the draft from the broken window and scattered across the schoolhouse floor among chalk dust and last year's leaves, and Sefa felt — nothing dramatic. No thunderclap. Just a loosening under the breastbone, subtle as a knot slipping, as though a hand that had rested on the back of her neck for thirty years had finally lifted away.

She sat back on her heels. Outside, the valley went on being impossible: birdsong where there had never been birdsong, the smell of Maren's garden — actual growing things, beans and marigolds, planted over the boundary stones — drifting in sweet and green through the shattered glass. Magic had seeped into the valley like water into dry ground, slow and uneven, pooling in low places. The millstream ran silver at dusk now instead of gray. Old Tomas swore his bad hip sang on damp mornings. And in the schoolhouse, which had stood empty for forty years because no one in the valley had dared teach children anything but silence and caution, Sefa had set out seven desks.

Six were empty.

The seventh held a girl named Odile, the miller's granddaughter, eight years old, gap-toothed, fearless, waiting.

* * *

Sefa had been a scribe of the third gallery for longer than she cared to count, and a court scribe before that, and a girl with quick hands and a hungry mind before that, in Grayharbor, where cleverness was currency and her family had none. She knew how she had been recruited — the hooded man who admired her penmanship, the apprenticeship offered like a gift, the small print she'd been too young and too proud to read. *One favor, payable to the court beneath the mountain.* She had signed herself into service with the same flourish she later used to record the selling of souls, and for thirty years the flourish had been the most honest thing about her.

In the galleries she had copied contracts. That was all, she had told herself. Copying is not selling. The pen moves; the hand does not choose. But there had been an afternoon — she could date it precisely, because she remembered everything about it, having spent decades trying to forget it — when a couple came in with their daughter's name already chosen for them, a name that sounded like bells, and the father's hand shook so badly over the signature that Sefa had reached across the desk and steadied his wrist. Helped him. Made the letters clean. Made the sale *legible*.

The mother had looked at her with such gratitude.

That was the memory that had finally driven her out of Grayharbor, more than any scandal, more than the revocation of her credentials. Not guilt at what she'd done, but the discovery that they thanked her. That in the economy of the court, she was the kind face. The mercy at the counter. People brought their children to be weighed and sold and left lighter than they came, and they pressed her hand at the door because her voice was gentle, because her ink was black and even, because she wrote the word *irrevocable* in a beautiful hand.

When the mountain fell — when the King spent himself lighting ten thousand lamps and the galleries went dark and the ledgers' power died with him — Sefa had stood in the ruins of the Hall of Debts holding a single page and understood that she was the last living person who knew how those words worked. Every scribe above ground was dead or scattered or pretending. Oathscript itself, the tongue that had bound magic into law and law into chains, survived in exactly two places: in a boy's mouth, and in her memory.

A language is a tool, she had learned. But a tool kept locked in a chest rusts into a weapon for whoever finds the key.

So: desks. Seven of them, dragged from the cellar, scrubbed, set in rows facing a slate board she had salvaged from the old schoolhouse wall, where some vanished teacher generations ago had written, in fading chalk, *the alphabet of the valley-tongue* — the plain speech, twenty-two letters, harmless as bread. Beneath it Sefa had written nothing yet. She kept wiping the slate clean each evening and leaving it blank each morning, because the first thing she wrote there mattered, and she hadn't earned it yet.

Odile raised her hand, though there was no one else to compete with. "Teacher. If the words don't open anything anymore—"

"They might," Sefa said. "Some day. Some words will always work. That's why we learn them carefully."

"But Mama says the mountain's dead and the words are just sounds now."

"Mama may be right." Sefa picked up the chalk. It felt strange in her fingers, this instrument of erasure, after a lifetime of instruments of record. "But listen. Before the court, before the ledgers, before anyone thought to sell a soul, Oathscript was how people talked to things that don't have ears. To doors. To rivers. To the dead." She drew the first character, the one that meant *witness*, its long descender like a root going down. "We are not going to use it to bind anything. We're going to use it to say hello."

"To a river?"

"To whatever's listening."

Odile leaned over her desk and repeated the character's sound, and got it slightly wrong, and laughed at herself, and tried again, and Sefa felt something move in her chest that she recognized, distantly, from the far side of her life: the feeling of teaching a child something true. She had helped unmake thousands of souls with that same hand. There was no arithmetic that balanced those scales. She had stopped looking for one somewhere on the moor road, watching Petra pay for a single spoken blade with the taste of bread.

But there was this. There was a gap-toothed girl saying *witness* to the morning, wrong and then less wrong, while bean plants climbed the bones of the boundary stones outside the window, and the valley — the valley that had been built as a lock, a lid, a lie — filled up slowly with birdsong.

* * *

Maren came by on market days with bread and opinions, in roughly equal measure.

"You'll ruin them," the widow said, setting a loaf on Sefa's table without being asked, the way she did everything, as though the world were already hers by right of having waited forty years to claim it. "Fill their heads with the tongue that built the prison. First thing they'll do is go knocking on doors that should stay shut."

"The doors are open anyway," Sefa said. "The mountain is dark. The wards are garden beds. Better they learn the words from me than find them scratched on a wall somewhere."

"Hm." Maren cut the loaf, handed Sefa the heel — the widow's idea of tenderness, giving away the crusty end she knew Sefa loved — and settled onto the bench by the window with the sigh of a woman whose knees had opinions. "You know what I can't get past? Forty years I lived here afraid of my own shadow crossing the boundary line. Forty years, and it turns out the whole quiet was one poor devil's hunger, wearing us as a lock around his wrist." She shook her head. "I keep waiting to feel angry. It hasn't come. Just this — lightness. Like a debt paid off I didn't know I owed."

Sefa's hand went, without her permission, to her breastbone, where the page had lived folded for so long there was still a crease in her shift where it used to sit.

"You too," Maren observed, sharp-eyed as ever. "Yours wasn't the valley's doing, though. Yours you signed yourself."

"Yes."

"And now you stay here teaching letters to millers' granddaughters. Redemption by chalkboard." Maren snorted, but her eyes were kind. "It's as good a way as any. My husband used to say a debt's not paid till it's forgotten — and then he'd add, quick as lightning, *and that's the one payment nobody wants, so pay it forward instead.*"

"He sounds like he'd have liked the valley awake."

"He'd have hated it for a month and then planted something." Maren rose, brushed crumbs from her apron, and paused at the door. "They're coming, you know. Travelers. Word gets out. A valley that was a prison for a century goes silent overnight and then starts singing — people will come to look. Some of them will bring children." She let that land. "Best have more than six desks."

After she was gone, Sefa sat a long while with the heel of the loaf and the slate board, and finally, deliberately, picked up the chalk and wrote, in careful Oathscript, the character that meant *welcome*. Not the binding-word, not the opening-word — those she taught last, guarded, with warnings attached like thorns on a stem. This one was older. It predated contracts. It had been scratched on waystones and doorposts in the time before anyone thought to charge admission to a human soul.

It meant, literally translated: *this place knows your name, and will keep it safe.*

She looked at it a long time. Then she left it on the board overnight, unfinished business, a promise she hadn't decided whether she could keep — because keeping names safe was a skill she had never once practiced, and practicing began tomorrow, at dawn, with six empty desks and one girl who deserved better teachers than fear had ever provided.

* * *

On the evening of the first frost, Petra came to the schoolhouse.

Sefa heard her before she saw her — the soft ring of a sword carried loose in its sheath, a sound the valley had never known until this autumn and was already learning to love. The swordspeaker stood in the doorway with frost melting in her dark hair, and behind her, half-hidden by her cloak, Edmund peered in with the particular eagerness of a boy arriving somewhere he's been promised a secret.

"You wanted to see me," Petra said. Even her voice had changed since the mountain — quieter, less armored. A woman speaking without a name learns to speak softly, Sefa supposed, because a loud voice belongs to someone.

"I wanted to ask a favor. A real one. Freely given or freely refused." Sefa set down her marking. "No hooks."

Petra's mouth twitched. "From you, that's nearly a vow."

"I'm out of practice at clean dealing. Indulge me." Sefa stood and gestured at the slate board, at the welcome-character still ghosted there in chalk. "I'm teaching. Six desks, one student. But travelers are coming — Maren's seen smoke on the north road, three fires' worth — and some of them will carry children who've heard whispers of Oathscript and want it for the wrong reasons. Or the frightened reasons. Or the desperate ones." She met Petra's eyes. "I know the grammar. I know the law. Thirty years of law. But I never once learned what you learned in that valley: how to give the words away without chaining anyone. How to teach a tongue that opens nothing—" she glanced at Edmund, who had crept to the front row and was tracing the welcome-sign in spilled flour on a desktop, his lips moving soundlessly, getting it almost right— "and means everything."

"You want me to stand at the front of a room?"

"I want you to sit in the back of one. Once a week. Correct me. Tell me when my sentences smell of the court." Sefa laughed, short and rueful. "I can't always tell anymore. The ledgers got into my blood. When I write *promise*, part of me still reaches for the clause that makes it enforceable. You're the only person alive who ever broke an enforceable promise with her whole history and lived."

For a moment Petra said nothing. Frost ticked against the window. In the flour, Edmund finished the character and looked up at them both, and the ember-slight glow behind his ribs brightened once, twice, like a pulse remembering itself.

"There's a condition," Petra said at last.

"Name it."

"Teach him too. Formally. He speaks it native, but native speakers make the worst grammarians — he'll need the rules for when the rules matter. And—" her voice did something complicated— "I'd learn beside him. What's left of my craft lives in the sword now. The sword remembers everything. But I'd like to remember some of it myself, in my own head, in my own hand. Writing it out. Like it's mine."

Sefa nodded slowly. "First lesson is tomorrow. Dawn's late this time of year — we'll start when the frost burns off."

"No." Petra smiled, and it was the old smile from the road, the tired, stubborn, unbowed one. "Start at dawn anyway. I've spent enough of my life sleeping through beginnings."

She left. Edmund lingered at the door, and turned back, and pointed one finger at the slate board, at the welcome-sign, and spoke it aloud — correctly, perfectly, the way water is wet — and every desk in the room warmed slightly, as if the wood itself had been greeted by name.

Then he ran after his keeper into the blue cold, and Sefa stood alone in her schoolhouse with six empty desks soon to be fewer, a slate full of welcome, and, pinned inside her coat where the debt-page used to live, a fresh sheet of paper on which she had begun, that very morning, to draft a new kind of document — not a contract, not a record, not a chain.

A lesson plan. Lesson one: *the difference between a word that holds and a word that keeps.*

Outside, on the north road, the first of the travelers' fires had come close enough to see — and with them, faint on the wind, a sound that made Sefa's blood stop and then start again faster:

a child's voice, far off, speaking Oathscript.

Not Edmund's voice.
