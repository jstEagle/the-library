## 39. Edmund and the Ember

The storm came up from the west the way storms always did now, dragging its gray skirt over the hills and muttering to itself in a language no one had bound. Edmund stood at the boundary stones with his hands loose at his sides and let it come.

He was seventeen this autumn, though he had stopped counting the way other people counted. In the valley they measured time by harvests and by lamps, and by the slow green creep of magic back into places that had been empty of it for a hundred years. The old mill wheel turned again on its own power some mornings, groaning like an old man asked to dance. Moss grew in colors that had names only in Oathscript. And at night, if you walked out past the last fence and stood very still, you could hear the mountain breathing—or what was left of it breathing, which was less a sound than a memory of one, a long tide of quiet rolling down from the dark peak where the Ember Court had been.

Edmund had been standing there since before noon, because today was a day for standing.

The ember in his chest was warm. It was always warm—it had been warm for seven years, ever since the King's last light had spent itself through the galleries and a sliver of it, unasked, had settled into the hollow where Liss's soul had been sold away. But some days it burned differently. Some days it felt like a hand resting flat against the inside of his ribs, patient, waiting for him to notice something.

Today was one of those days.

He could feel the storm before he could see it properly: a pressure behind his teeth, a taste like struck flint on the wind. His sword—the sword Keeper had given him three winters ago, when his shoulders had finally grown into its weight—hummed against his hip. Not singing yet. Just humming. The way a dog hums before it barks.

"You'll catch your death," Maren had said that morning, pressing a heel of bread into his hand at her kitchen table, her garden gone riotous and half-wild around the house she'd claimed over the buried boundary stones. "Standing out there in weather."

"I won't," Edmund had said, and it was true, and they both knew why. The ember kept him warm the way a hearth keeps a room warm. He hadn't been cold since he was ten years old. He sometimes missed being cold—the sharp clean ache of it, the excuse to huddle—but he had never once wished the warmth away. You did not wish away the last piece of your sister.

Now he stood at the boundary and watched the storm build itself out of nothing, and thought about doors.

* * *

Keeper found him there an hour before the rain, as he'd known she would. She came up the path from the village with her hood down and her gray hair loose, and she moved the way she always moved, unhurried, like someone who had learned that hurry cost more than it bought. She had learned it expensively. He knew that too. Everyone in the valley knew it, though almost none of them knew what it had cost, because the price had been paid in the only coin that couldn't be shown: everything Petra Vasquez had ever remembered about herself.

She carried two cups of something hot wrapped in cloth, because she was Keeper, and Keeper's whole self was made of small provisions. Bread at the right moment. A blanket without being asked. A name, when you needed one.

"You're doing it again," she said, handing him a cup. Barley and honey. Steam curled up between them and the wind took it sideways.

"Doing what?"

"Waiting." She sipped from her own cup and looked out at the storm with him, her profile calm as a boundary stone herself. "You get that look. Like the horizon owes you something."

"It might." Edmund blew across his drink. "It's the anniversary."

"I know what day it is." Her voice didn't change, but the ember in his chest flickered, just slightly, the way it did when something true passed near it. "I don't remember much, Edmund. You know that. But I remember the shape of days. This one has a shape."

"A knock-shaped one," he said.

That earned him the ghost of her smile. "A knock-shaped one."

They drank in silence. Below them the valley lay green and lamplit, chimneys beginning to smoke against the coming rain, and beyond it the world went on being remade—roads that travelers used now, caravans with wardens' sigils painted over, Sefa's school with its windows full of children learning words that opened nothing and meant everything. Seven years since the mountain went dark. Seven years since he had knelt in a gallery of guttering lamps and told his sister goodbye in two languages, plain words and Oathscript both, because some things deserved to be said in every tongue you had.

*Rest,* he had told her. *You're home. I carried you home.*

And the lamp that held what remained of Liss had flared once, bright as a struck match, and gone soft, and stayed soft, and that was the whole of the answer. Grief wasn't fixed. Keeper had taught him that early, not with words—with the way she touched the hilt of her own sword some evenings, like touching a grave. Grief was carried. That was all. You carried it until carrying became a kind of love, and then it was just love, heavier, but yours.

"Tell me about it again," Keeper said. "The night you knocked."

Edmund looked at her. She never asked for stories twice; she knew how memory worked, how telling wore a groove into a thing until the thing became mostly groove. But she asked for this one, most years, and he had learned why. She asked because she couldn't remember her own arrival, forty years before his, barefoot and oath-bound at a door in a valley that forgot. His story was the closest thing she had to hers.

So he told it. He always told it the same way, because it was true, and true things kept their shape.

* * *

"There was a storm," he said, "like this one. Bigger. The kind of storm that makes the air feel solid, like you could lean on it. I'd been walking for—I don't know how long. Days. Maybe more. When you swallow an ember, time goes strange. It burns slow inside you and everything outside gets slow too, like the world is holding its breath around you."

"And you weren't afraid."

"I wasn't afraid." He smiled a little at that, at the boy he'd been, ten years old and hollowed out with grief and so far past fear that fear couldn't find him anymore. "That's what nobody tells you about the worst thing happening to you. Afterward you're not brave. You're just past the place where brave lives. I walked through that storm barefoot and I remember thinking my feet were somebody else's feet, and the rain was somebody else's rain, and the only thing in the whole world that was mine was burning in my chest."

"The ember."

"Liss." He said the name plainly. He always said it plainly first, and then, under his breath, in Oathscript, the way you set a stone on a cairn: *Liss-vaen, sister-of-my-house, keeper-of-my-light.* The syllables made the air shiver faintly, the way they still did, after all these years. Oathscript didn't open anything anymore—not here, not since the seal broke and the valley woke. But it still meant everything. Sefa said that was the whole point of teaching it. Words that open nothing and mean everything. A language made entirely of love with nowhere left to go.

"And then I saw the house," he went on. "Your house. One window lit. And I remember thinking—I remember this exactly, Keeper, I've kept it like a stone—all I thought was: *someone is awake.* Not *someone will help me.* Not *someone will feed me.* Just: someone is awake. Someone is keeping watch. And that was enough. That was the whole reason I knocked."

Keeper was quiet for a moment. Out over the moor the storm had grown a spine; lightning moved inside it like something turning in its sleep.

"When you knocked," she said, "my sword hummed for the first time in thirty years. Did you know that?"

"You've told me."

"It scared me half to death." She laughed softly, an old sound, worn smooth. "Thirty years in a valley where magic couldn't follow me, where I kept a blade I couldn't draw and an oath I couldn't speak aloud, and one knock on my door and the steel started singing. I thought the wards were failing. I thought—" She stopped. Shook her head. "I thought a great many wrong things that night. The right thing was standing on my step getting soaked, carrying my sister home in his chest, and I nearly sent him away because the elders were frightened and I was frightened and I had spent so long being careful that I'd forgotten careful isn't the same as good."

"But you didn't send me away."

"No." She looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes were the same eyes he'd seen through the crack of a door at dusk seven years ago: tired, wary, and underneath the wariness, lit. "I opened the door. Best decision anyone ever made on my behalf, and I made it myself, which I consider the single exception to every rule I know."

* * *

The rain reached them at dusk.

It came across the moor in a gray wall, and Edmund turned his face into it and let it hit him, cold and enormous and alive, and the ember in his chest answered it the way it always answered weather now—with a slow brightening, coal-deep, banked-fire deep. Lightning walked the ridgeline. Thunder followed, rolling down into the valley like a barrel down stairs, and somewhere below a dog began to bark, and a lamp in Sefa's school window flared as someone hurried to shut it against the wet.

His sword sang.

Not fully—not the way it had sung on the moor road, the day he'd frozen and Maren had been taken instead of him, the day Keeper had drawn it whole and spoken blades out of the air and paid for each word with a piece of herself. But it sang low, a thread of sound under the thunder, harmonizing with the storm the way it had begun to do lately, as if the blade were learning the weather's language. As if the blade were learning *his* language.

Because that was the truth he hadn't told anyone yet, the truth that had been growing in him all summer like a seed under snow:

The humming wasn't random anymore. It came when he thought in Oathscript. It came when he grieved. It came, three nights ago, when he had dreamed of Liss and woken with the word for *home* sitting on his tongue like a live coal, and the sword had rung once, clear, in the dark, and Keeper had appeared in his doorway with a candle and said, very quietly, *"Say it again."*

He hadn't said it again. Not yet. Because he knew what words cost in this family. He had watched his master pay in memories—in a mother's face, in birthdays, in the taste of bread—and he had sworn, silently, in the grammar of children who have already lost too much, that he would never spend himself that way. That he would find another way or no way.

But the sword kept humming. And the ember kept warming. And tonight, on the anniversary, at the boundary, in the storm—

Tonight it felt less like a warning and more like a door.

"Edmund." Keeper's voice cut gently through the rain. She was watching him watch the lightning, and her face had gone carefully still, the stillness she wore when she was afraid and refusing to show it. "What are you hearing?"

"The storm," he said. "And under it—" He put his hand flat on the pommel. The vibration ran up his arm and into his chest and met the ember, and for one heartbeat the two warmths recognized each other, steel and soul, and the valley seemed to hold its breath the way the world had held its breath around a walking boy seven years ago.

"Under it," he said, "I think it's knocking."

* * *

They stood together while the dark came down.

This was the part he loved best, though he'd never say so: the standing together. Keeper beside him, nameless and unbound and utterly certain of exactly one thing, which was him. Maren's garden glowing faintly down the slope, because the vegetables she grew over the old boundary stones had never quite agreed to be ordinary. The lamps of the valley strung out below like a constellation someone had arranged by hand, which, in a way, someone had—a dying king's last spending, carried down the mountain in glass and given to the waking world.

Seven years ago he had been the stranger at the door. Now the valley knew his step on every path. Children trailed him through Sefa's schoolyard asking him to say the shining words. Travelers arriving at the remade world were greeted, more often than not, by a young man with a warm chest and an old soul's patience, standing where Keeper had once stood, holding the door.

The story handed itself on. That was what Sefa said, burning her ledger-page and staying anyway. That was what Maren said, planting things over locks. That was what the valley itself said, every spring, pushing green up through the seams of its hundred-year silence.

The story hands itself on. Your job is only to be awake when it knocks.

Lightning split the sky above the mountain—from this angle, in this light, the peak looked less like a fortress than a mouth, and less like a mouth than a closed eye, sleeping at last—and the thunder that followed was long and rolling and strange, and beneath it, threaded through it, unmistakable, the sword at Edmund's hip rang like a bell struck once by a careful hand.

And then, thin under the rain, from the direction of the moor road, from the direction of the wide remade world:

A knock. Faint. Twice. Then again, more urgent.

Not on wood. On the boundary itself—the oldest door any of them knew, the seal broken from inside, the line where the valley met everything else. Someone was out there in the storm, on the far side of the stones, knocking the way you knock when you are past the place where brave lives and the only thing you have left is the knowledge that someone, somewhere, keeps a light in the window.

Someone is awake.

Edmund's heart slammed once, hard, and the ember flared against his ribs like a hand pressed flat from within, and he looked at Keeper, and Keeper was already looking at the darkness, her cup forgotten, her face lit by the last flicker of the lightning—wary, tired, and underneath it all, unmistakably, lit.

"Well," she said quietly. "You heard it too."

Edmund set down his cup, and put his hand on the hilt of his singing sword, and turned toward the door.
