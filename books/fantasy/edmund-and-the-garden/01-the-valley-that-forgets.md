## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgets the way old men forget names — slowly, politely, and with a small apology each time. It has forgotten the shape of the mountains that ring it, calling them simply *the walls*. It has forgotten the river's original name, which even the oldest gravestone doesn't know. It has forgotten, most thoroughly and most conveniently, why any of us are here at all.

I know, because I've asked. I've asked the way you ask when you're twenty-six and the question has been sitting in your chest since you were nine, going soft and heavy like a stone in a streambed. I've asked the elders, who answer in the tone people use for children and the very old: *the valley is safe, Felix, the valley has always been safe.* I've asked my mother's grave, which answers in the tone people use for the dead, which is to say it doesn't.

What the valley has not forgotten — what it cannot forget, because I won't let it — is the drill yard.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin, the kind of light that seems to be apologizing for showing up at all. I was in the yard before it, the way I always am, running the forms with a practice blade because the real one is for oaths and I am not permitted to waste an oath on muscle memory.

Swordspeech. That's what they call it, when they call it anything at all. The art of putting words into steel. My grandmother taught my mother taught — no. My mother taught no one. My mother is the gap in the sentence, the name on the grave I tend, and everything I know of the speech I learned from her teacher's teacher's notes, copied out in a hand that got shakier with every page, as if the words themselves were dying of old age.

Here is what the notes say the speech should do: a swordspeaker names a blade's purpose and the blade agrees to it. *Cut rope, not flesh.* *Turn the falling stone.* *Hold the bridge until the last soul crosses.* The steel listens. The steel remembers. The steel, according to three generations of marginalia, is more faithful than any person who ever held it.

Here is what the speech does in the valley: nothing.

I stood in the frost that morning with my practice blade raised, and I said the opening word of the First Form — the true word, the one with the weight in it, the one that tastes like struck iron on the back of the teeth — and the blade hung in the cold air and did nothing at all. It did not sing. It did not warm. It was a length of wood and my voice was a voice and the valley sat around us both like a hand pressed flat over a mouth.

*The valley where magic cannot follow.* That's the phrase the elders use, when they use any phrase at all. As if magic were a dog we'd once owned, that chased us here and was finally left panting at the ridge line. As if we should be grateful.

I finished the form anyway. All nine movements, the turn, the kneel, the sheathing that is supposed to mean *the work is done and the world is safer.* I sheathed my practice blade in the empty yard, for an enemy that has never come, before an audience that has never watched, and I bowed to the walls of the valley because they are the only thing here that ever listens.

"You'll ruin your knees, kneeling in frost."

Tam Vess, of course. He appears at the fence every morning with the punctuality of a bad conscience, carrying two cups of barley tea and the expression of a man who has decided, after long deliberation, to be fond of me. He's the cooper's son and my oldest friend and the only person in this valley who has never once told me the valley is safe.

"Knees are the least of what I'm ruining," I said, and took the tea, because even a woman at war with her inheritance drinks tea when it's offered.

"You did the kneel again." He nodded at the yard. "The last movement. You know the notes say the kneel is only for—"

"For when the work is done and the world is safer. Yes." I drank. The tea was terrible. It is always terrible. "And when is the work done, Tam? When does the world get safer? Because from where I'm standing, the world has been exactly as safe as a held breath for two hundred years, and held breaths end."

He looked at me the way he looks at barrels he suspects are about to fail. "You asked the elders again."

"I asked the elders again."

"And?"

"And Elder Maren told me the Founding Bargain was a matter for the Founders, and the Founders are dust, and dust keeps its own counsel." I turned the cup in my hands. "She said it like she'd said it before. Like it was a speech she keeps oiled for exactly this occasion."

"She has had occasion," Tam said quietly. "You've been asking since you were nine."

"Nine," I agreed. "The year my mother died. The year I started noticing that everyone in this valley answers questions the same way — with a longer version of *don't.*" I looked up at the walls, at the pass through them, the notch of paler sky where the mountains allow the world to remember we exist. "You know what I think? I think we're not the ones who were saved. I think we're the ones who were *kept.*"

Tam didn't answer. He's good at that — the silence of a man who agrees with you and wishes he didn't. We drank our terrible tea and watched the frost burn off the drill yard, and somewhere behind us the valley went about its morning, forgetting.

* * *

The graves come next. They always come next. Duty in this valley has an order to it, like the forms: yard, graves, walls' walk, evening meal, sleep, and begin again. I have done it every day since I was old enough to be told it was mine, and I have never once been told *why it's mine.*

There are forty-one graves in the family plot behind the meeting house. Forty-two, counting the empty one my grandmother had cut and dressed and left waiting beside my mother's, with her own name on it, because she believed in preparing. She'd have liked me to lie down in it eventually too, I think, in the orderly way of things. The stonecutter keeps it weeded. I keep it empty.

My mother's stone says her name, her years, and — by her own instruction, cut before I was born — a single line: *She paid what was asked.*

Not *she gave.* Not *she was taken.* *Paid.* As if there had been a transaction. As if somewhere there was a receipt.

I pulled the winter weeds — early this year, the weeds were early, everything is early, the swallows left a month ago and nobody wants to say it aloud — and I talked to her the way I always do, which is to say I talked to myself in a voice pitched to carry.

"Morning, Mama. The forms are clean. Tam's tea is worse than ever, which means business is good. The swallows left early and Elder Maren says it's nothing, and Elder Maren has never once in her life told the truth about something that frightened her." I sat back on my heels. "I found a new page in the notes last night. Torn half away. There's a word on it I don't know, and the word is in the old script, the pre-Founding script, and you know what that means. It means someone thought this page was worth destroying and did a poor job of it."

The stone said nothing. The stone is very good at its work.

"The word might be *court,*" I told her. "Or it might be *debt.* The ink's gone the color of old blood, which is a thing I noticed and then wished I hadn't."

A crow landed on the empty grave — my grandmother's, the waiting one — and looked at me with the frank assessment of its kind. I have decided over the years that the crow is on my side, in the way that crows are on no one's side and therefore everyone's.

"Don't look at me like that," I said. "I'm not going to do anything foolish."

The crow did not believe me. Crows are rarely wrong.

* * *

The walls' walk is the part of the duty I hate least, which is to say it's the part where the valley's cage most resembles a view. The path climbs the eastern shoulder to a lookout the Founders built — fitted stones, no mortar, older than any record — where you can see the whole valley cupped in its mountains like water in two hands. Fields going gold. The meeting house. The smoke of forty hearths. The pass, far to the north, a gray notch in the gray stone, closed by snow eleven months of the year and open the other one like a held breath of its own.

The oath is kept at the lookout. That's the one part nobody disputes, the one piece of the inheritance everyone agrees on. Once a season, the oath-keeper — me, the last one, the only one — stands on the Founders' stones and speaks the Oath of Keeping to the valley entire. I have done it since I was sixteen. I know every word. I know the weight of every word, the iron taste of them, the way they make the air go still and attentive, the way even the valley — even here, even where magic cannot follow — seems to lean in and listen.

And every season, when I finish, the silence afterward has a shape. It has the shape of a door being closed. And every season I stand there with my hand on my sword hilt, waiting for the valley to answer, and every season it does not, and I tell myself that's how oaths work — they're spoken *to* something, not *with* something — and every season I believe it a little less.

That afternoon I climbed to the lookout and did not speak the oath. I only stood, and looked north at the pass, and noticed what I had been not-noticing for a week.

The notch of sky in the pass was white.

Not the white of distance. The white of snow. In the season when the pass should be open stone and thin grass and the small brown birds that come through in the autumn. Snow already sealing it, weeks — no, more than weeks — before the first snow has ever come, in any record, in any grandmother's memory of her grandmother's complaints about weather.

The swallows. The early frost. The weeds. The word in the old script that might be *debt.*

I stood on the Founders' stones with my hand on my hilt and let the dread arrive, because I have learned that it always arrives anyway, and it's better to be holding the door when it does.

*Something is coming down out of the pass,* I thought. Or: *something has already come, and the valley is forgetting it even now.* And beneath both thoughts, older and colder, the one I never say aloud, not even to the crow:

*We are not the ones who were saved. We are the ones who were kept. And keepers come to collect.*

* * *

I saw him at dusk.

I was coming down from the walls' walk with the last light going copper on the fields, and I saw him stagger out of the tree line below the pass road — a dark shape, wrong-shaped, moving the way things move when they are not walking so much as falling in installments. I was running before my mind finished deciding to, which is the one inheritance I've never questioned: the body of an oath-keeper answers faster than the mind of a skeptic.

He went down at the edge of the lower field, and I reached him as the light failed, and I knelt in the frost — the kneel, the ninth movement, *the work is done* — and turned him over, and knew at once that he was wrong in the way I had feared without having words for it.

Frostbitten, yes. His lips were gray, his fingers black at the tips, his breath coming in small torn pieces. But frostbite is a wound, and wounds have a logic to them. This man was wrong the way the pass was wrong. The frost on him was not melting by the fire of my hands. It was *holding on.* Little rimes of white clung to his beard, his lashes, the collar of his coat, and they glittered, and I swear on my mother's stone that they glittered like they were *arranged.* Frost does not arrange itself. Frost does not grow in patterns like script.

His eyes opened. They were the pale washed blue of ice over deep water, and they found my face with terrible precision, as if he had been walking out of the pass for days toward one particular face and had finally, at the end, arrived at it.

He knew me. That was the first horror — before the frost, before the wrongness, before anything — he *knew* me. His cracked lips moved, and what came out was not a stranger's question, not *where am I,* not *help me.* What came out was my name.

Not *Felix.*

The other one. The name no one uses. The name I have heard exactly twice in my life — once at my mother's grave-side, murmured by my grandmother in the old script, and once by myself, alone, at the lookout, trying it on like a coat that didn't fit. The full name. The oath-name. The name that is supposed to mean something *to* something.

He said it the way you read a label.

"Debt," he whispered, and his black-tipped fingers closed on my wrist with a strength that had no business surviving in him, and his other hand — I saw it then, had been seeing it without seeing it — pressed something against my palm. Stiff. Stained. Folded many times, the creases dark.

"The court beneath," he said. "It's early. It's early, and the valley forgot to—" His breath tore. His eyes went past my shoulder, up the eastern shoulder of the valley, to the lookout, to the Founders' stones, and something moved through the ruin of his face that I will spend the rest of my life not having a word for. Recognition. Pity. *Pity,* aimed at me, by a dying man who knew my name.

"The garden," he said. "Find the keeper. He remembers—"

The first snowflake landed on his cheek.

It was weeks early. It was the first of the season, and it landed on the cheek of a dying man who had come down out of a pass that should have been open, and it did not melt.

Around us, gently, without malice, without any sound at all, the snow began to fall on the valley that forgets — and I knelt there in the ninth movement, holding a bloodstained map and a stranger's cooling wrist, and understood with a certainty that went through me like a blade finding its word:

The long winter had not been coming.

It had been *sent.*
