## 2. A Map Pressed Into Unwilling Hands

The stranger did not die quickly, which I will be honest about resenting.

There is a version of this story where he gasps out his secrets and slips away between one breath and the next, tidy as a candle going out, leaving me free to carry a clean mystery to someone else's door. That is not what happened. What happened was that he lay in the mud of the training yard with my sword-form half-finished around us like a dropped sentence, and he held on, and he looked at me with eyes that were already frosting over at the edges, and he said:

"Say it back."

"Say what back?"

"The garden. The keeper." His fingers tightened on my wrist — a grip far too strong for a man whose body had apparently decided to stop being a body. "He remembers. Say you'll find him."

"I don't know who *he* is," I said. "I don't know what garden. There's no garden here. Nothing grows past the—"

"There's always a garden." He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see, that smile, because it meant he had something left to be amused about, and people with something left to be amused about are not finished dying yet. "Every valley has its edge. Every edge has its keeper. Ask the elders why they never let you walk the boundary line."

"They let me walk the boundary line."

"Do they?" he said, and let go of my wrist, and for one lurching moment I thought that was it, that he'd spent his last coin on a riddle, but no. He was only reaching into his coat.

I want to describe that coat properly, because I have thought about it every day since, and because getting it right matters more than most of what happened after. It was a traveler's coat, oilcloth, good quality once, now worn through at the elbows and patched at the shoulders with thread that didn't match — mended by hand, by someone patient, or by the same person many times. The kind of coat you buy when you intend to arrive somewhere. Not the kind you wear out into a mountain pass in the first week of autumn.

From inside it he drew a folded square of leather, and from the leather a paper, and the paper was stained brown in long streaks that I did not need anyone to identify for me. Blood maps itself differently than wine. Wine soaks. Blood travels — it moves the way a dying man moves, in fits, dragged along by whatever is carrying it.

"Your name," he said.

"Felix Almeida."

"No." His voice had gone thin, papery, a wind through a crack. "Your other one."

"I don't have another one."

"You do." And then he said it.

I am not going to write it down. Not here, not anywhere, not ever again if I have any say in the matter, which I am beginning to suspect I do not. But I will tell you what it did, because that part belongs to me whether I want it or not.

It went through me like cold water finding a crack in a wall. It was four syllables long and shaped wrong for our valley — our names are short, practical, built for calling across fields — and it sounded old in a way that made my teeth ache, the way a word sounds when it has been buried a long time and has opinions about being dug up. My mother used to hum when she worked. I suddenly understood, with a sick lurch, that some of those humming-sounds had been this word, broken into pieces and scattered through a melody so I'd never notice I was learning it.

Nobody in the valley called me anything but Felix. Nobody had ever called me anything but Felix.

"How do you know that," I said. It came out flat. Somewhere behind my ribs my heart was doing something I'd have called panic if I'd had time to be polite about it.

"Everyone down there knows it." He pressed the bloodstained paper into my hands — not offered, *pressed*, folding my fingers closed over it with both of his, and his hands were so cold that my skin flinched away from them on instinct, the way it flinches from a stove. "The debt is called. The court beneath has opened its ledger. They are coming to collect, girl, and the collection is *you* — no. No, listen." My face must have done something, because his grip tightened. "Not your body. Your body is safe. Bodies are cheap. It's the *name* they'll come for. Names are how they hold what they're owed, and what they're owed—" He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it stopped there was blood on his lips and frost on his eyelashes and both of those things were wrong in ways I couldn't count fast enough.

"What are they owed?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Snow was gathering on his shoulders now, on his chest, settling into the hollow of his throat. Around us the yard had gone white and quiet, and my footprints and his drag-marks were filling in like the valley was erasing evidence.

"Ask the elders," he whispered. "Ask them what the Founding Bargain bought. Ask them what surety was given. Ask them why the winter stays outside and what it costs to keep it there." Each question landed like a stone dropped down a well, and I heard each one hit bottom, and none of them made a splash I could see. "They won't want to tell you. Tell them the collector sent you. Tell them—" and here his voice cracked open, and what came through the crack was not anger exactly, though there was anger in it, banked and ancient, the way a peat fire is angry — "tell them Marta's daughter finally asked."

Marta.

My mother's name was Marta.

I did not say anything. I could not. There is a particular kind of silence that happens when the ground shifts under you and you haven't fallen yet — you just stand there, weightless, waiting to find out which way down is. I stood in it. The snow fell through it.

"Take the map," he said. "Follow it down. Find the keeper first — he'll get you through the pass alive, and nothing else will. And Felix—" my own name, plain and warm and ordinary, sounded strange coming after the other one, like a coat put back on inside-out — "whatever they told you your oath was for? It isn't. An oath that binds only the ones who keep it isn't an oath. It's a leash. Somebody's been holding the other end your whole life."

Then he shivered, once, all over, like a horse shedding water, and said, "Cold," in a small voice with no prophecy left in it at all, just a man, just freezing, and I remembered myself.

I got him under the arms. I hauled him toward the hall — he weighed nothing, that was the horror of it, he weighed what a bundle of sticks weighs — and I shouted for help in the voice we use for emergencies, the one that carries across fields, and people came, because people always come, that was the whole point of the valley, that was the bargain, wasn't it, that we came for each other.

We got him to the hearth. We did everything right. Old Sefa wrapped him in wool heated by the fire; Tam brought the willow-bark and the honey; I chafed his hands until my own burned. And he faded anyway, steadily, politely, the way a guest fades who does not wish to inconvenience anyone, and somewhere in the middle of it he gripped my sleeve one last time and said, "Don't let them pack it away in a drawer," meaning the map, meaning, I think, everything, and then he said, "She'd be proud. She fought too," and I didn't ask who *she* was because I was afraid of the answer, and then—

And then he died. Between one breath and the next after all, in the end. Tidy as a candle. The universe apparently saves its tidiness for when it will hurt most.

* * *

Here is what I did in the hours after, and I record it without pride because there isn't any to record.

I sat with the dead man while the others made arrangements, because someone had to, and because sitting with the dead is a duty and duties are the shape my fear takes when it doesn't know where else to go. I watched the snow through the window. It kept falling. First snow, weeks early, falling thick and unhurried, and every flake that landed on the sill and did not melt felt less like weather and more like correspondence.

I opened the map.

It was drawn on paper that had been good once and had traveled hard since: creased into eighths and refolded along new lines so often the old creases had given up. The ink was fine and precise, surveyor's work, and it showed our mountains from above the way a hawk would see them — and then it showed what was under them. A descent. Pass after pass marked in a hand that grew hurried, then a spiral of switchbacks drawn tight as a snail shell, winding down and down beneath the tallest peak, to a mark at the center like a seal pressed in wax. Beside the mark, in the same fine hand, two words:

*The court.*

And below that, smaller, in a different hand — a rounder, softer hand that made my stomach drop through the floor before my mind caught up with why — a single line:

*If you are reading this, the debt has come due. I'm sorry. I ran out of road.*

That handwriting. I knew that handwriting. It was on the spice jars. It was in the front of the songbook. It was on the letter tucked into my mother's grave-marker, the one line she'd left for me, the one I had memorized at nine years old and recited to myself on bad nights like a charm: *Grow strong, little blade, and stay where it's safe.*

Stay where it's safe. Written by a woman who had, according to a dying stranger, run out of road fleeing *down*-mountain. Who had known about courts and debts and ledgers. Who had hidden a word inside a lullaby and a warning inside a blessing and then died and left me holding all of it like a hot coal passed hand to hand down twenty years.

I sat in the hall of my ancestors' house, in the valley that forgets, holding a map written partly in my dead mother's hand, and I laughed. Once. It came out wrong. Sefa looked over at me with kind worried eyes and I shook my head and she let it go, because in the valley we are gentle with grief, we have practice, grief is the one thing that has always been allowed to follow us in.

Unwanted inheritance. That's the phrase for it, I suppose, if you want a phrase. The house gets the land, the eldest gets the title, and Felix Almeida gets a bloodstained map, a secret name, a debt she never signed for, and a stranger's corpse to bury in frozen ground. Every inheritance comes with taxes. Mine just happened to be collected by a court beneath a mountain.

But here is the other thing I did in those hours, and this one I will take a sliver of pride in, small and stubborn as a crocus through snow.

I did not burn the map.

Understand: I thought about it. I stood at the hearth with the paper in my hand for a long time — long enough that the heat started to curl its edges — and I thought about how easy it would be. How the valley would close around the loss of it like water around a stone. How in a year I'd barely remember the stranger's face, and in five I'd have talked myself into believing the whole day was a fever, and in ten I'd be teaching some child the ninth movement of the sword-form and telling her it was for protection, protection, protection, the word we use instead of *fear*.

Burning it would have been staying. Staying is what I'm for. It's the family trade.

Instead I folded it along its tired creases, slid it back into its leather, and pushed the leather deep into the lining of my pack, under the spare shirt, under the whetstone, in the place where you put things you intend to lie to yourself about later.

Then I went out and dug the grave myself, because the ground was already freezing and I wanted it done while my hands still worked, and because I owed him that much — he'd carried my mother's handwriting across mountains to bring it to me, and you pay your couriers.

The ground came up hard and black under the new snow. I dug until my shoulders screamed. Nobody came to help, which I noticed, and noticed noticing, and filed away with the rest of the day's strange inventory: the elders' windows stayed lit and their doors stayed shut, all evening, every house on the row, like a street of people pretending not to be home.

When the grave was filled I stood over it and realized I had nothing to mark it with. No name — he'd never given one. No prayer — I didn't know any that weren't oath-words, and oath-words suddenly felt like currency I wasn't sure I wanted to spend.

So I said the only true thing I had.

"Marta's daughter asked," I told the fresh-turned earth. "Whatever that was worth to you — it's yours. It's spent. I hope it buys you better road than she got."

The snow fell on the mound and did not melt, and did not melt, and did not melt.

Behind me, up the lane, lamplight bloomed in the elders' meetinghouse — all of them, gathered, at this hour, in this weather — and I watched the light in their windows the way you watch smoke rising from a fire you can't yet smell.

They had questions to answer. I had a map in my pack and a name in my mouth that no one was supposed to use.

I picked up my shovel and started walking.
