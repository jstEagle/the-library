## 3. What the Elders Will Not Say

The elders convened in the meetinghouse before I even reached it, which told me two things: first, that someone had already run ahead with the news of the dead man in the snow; second, that the valley's talent for moving quickly had not, as I'd sometimes suspected, atrophied entirely. It had merely been reserved for emergencies. And for gossip, which in this valley were often the same thing.

I stood outside the door for a long moment, the stranger's map folded against my ribs where I'd tucked it inside my coat, and listened to the murmur within. Seven voices. All seven elders, then. They hadn't gathered all seven for anything since the mill flood, and that had been eleven years ago.

They knew what he was. Or what he meant.

I pushed the door open.

The meetinghouse smelled of tallow smoke and old cedar, the way it always did, the way it had smelled my entire life, and there they sat in their crescent of chairs — Elder Maren with her hands folded like a woman praying to a god she'd stopped believing in, Elder Tomas beside her picking at the seam of his sleeve, and the rest arranged in their usual order of gravity. The fire was lit, though the day wasn't cold enough to need it. Nerves, then. Someone had wanted something to do with their hands.

"Felix." Maren's voice was gentle, which was how I knew I was in trouble. Maren saved gentleness for funerals and for me. "Sit down, child."

"I'm not a child."

"You're twenty-six years old and you've never left the valley," said Elder Tomas, not unkindly. "In every way that matters, you are a child, and we have all conspired to keep you one."

That stopped me. Not because it was cruel — it wasn't, quite — but because it was honest, and honesty from the council was rarer than snow in high summer.

"The man in the pass," I said. "You know who he was."

Silence, of the particular texture that means *yes*.

"He died holding my wrist," I went on, when no one offered anything. "He died saying my name. Not the name everyone uses. The other one. The one nobody uses." I pulled the map from my coat and held it up, bloodstained corner and all. "And he gave me this. So before you tell me it's nothing, before you tell me he was a lost traveler or a madman off the mountain, understand that I watched him die, and his last act on this earth was to make sure *I* got this. That's not nothing. That has never once been nothing."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them again, she looked older than she had a moment before, as if the truth were a weight she'd been setting down and picking back up for forty years and her arms were tired of it.

"Close the door, Felix."

I closed the door.

* * *

What they told me came out in pieces, the way a wall comes down — not all at once, but stone by grudging stone, each one pried loose only because I refused to stop asking.

The Founding Bargain, they called it. Three hundred years ago, give or take a generation nobody wrote down, the valley had been dying. Not from war, not from famine — from magic itself. The world beyond the ridgeline was full of it then, wild and hungry, and it ate valleys like ours the way fire eats dry grass. My ancestors had been swordspeakers, oath-bound, the last defenders of a line that stretched back further than any record. And they were losing.

"So they made a bargain," Elder Maren said. She spoke slowly, like a woman walking on ice she didn't trust. "With the court beneath the mountain. The court that keeps the winter."

"What court? What winter?"

"The long winter," said Elder Tomas quietly. "The one that comes when the bargain ends. It falls once, Felix, and it does not lift. Snow until the valley is a grave with a lid on it. Every child here learns the rhyme about the winter that doesn't end. We let them think it's just a rhyme."

My mouth had gone dry. "And the price?"

Another silence. This one had a shape to it, and the shape was a person.

"A soul," Maren said at last. "One soul, given into the court's keeping, as surety that the bargain would be honored. As long as the soul sits unclaimed on its shelf beneath the mountain, the valley is safe. The magic stays outside the ridge. The winter stays a season instead of a sentence."

"Whose soul?" I asked, though some cold, clever part of me had already begun to guess, and hated itself for guessing.

Maren wouldn't look at me. It was Elder Sefa, oldest of them all, half-blind and sharp as a fishhook, who answered.

"Your mother's."

The word landed in the room like a dropped stone. I heard myself laugh — one short, ugly sound — because surely I'd misheard. Surely.

"My mother has been dead fourteen years," I said. "She's buried under the rowan tree. I tend her grave. I've tended it since I was twelve years old."

"Yes," said Sefa. "Her body is. Her body we kept. What the court took was the rest of her — the part that hums, the part that dreams. It was taken the night she fled down-mountain trying to leave, and your father turned back rather than follow, and the bargain needed renewing, and—" Sefa's voice caught, actually caught, on something that might have been grief or might have been shame. "It was taken so that you could grow up safe, Felix. So that all of us could. Your mother understood that. In the end, she agreed to it."

"She *agreed*—"

"She was given a choice," Maren said, too quickly, and the quickness told me everything the words didn't. "And she chose the valley. She chose you."

I stood very still. Outside the meetinghouse window, the light had gone strange and thin, the light of a day deciding whether to become winter weeks early. Somewhere a dog barked, ordinary and stupid and beloved, and I thought: *all of you knew. All of you have known my whole life. You watched me plant flowers on an empty grave and none of you said a word.*

"The stranger," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, like someone else speaking through a wall. "The man in the pass. He knew. He came to tell me."

"He should never have crossed the ridge," said Tomas. "Whatever he was — a runner, a sympathizer, a fool — the court does not let its business walk around loose. If he came to you, then the court knows the debt is stirring. And if the debt is stirring—"

"Then the winter comes early," Sefa finished. "As it is doing. Look outside, girl. First snow, and it's barely autumn. That's not weather. That's a collection notice."

* * *

They forbade me to leave, of course.

They did it gently, which somehow made it worse. Maren took both my hands in hers, and her palms were soft with age and dishonesty, and she said, "You must stay in the valley, Felix. You must never cross the ridge. The bargain holds as long as the soul sits unclaimed — but if the collateral's daughter walks out from under its protection, if the court can reach you, they will use you. They will leverage you against the debt. Everything your mother gave will have been for nothing."

"You mean everything my mother *paid*," I said.

"Felix—"

"No." I pulled my hands free. "You don't get to soften it. You've been softening it for fourteen years. You watched me grow up in a cage and called it safety. You taught me sword-forms for enemies that could never reach me because you'd already sold the war away, and you let me believe the oath meant something, and the whole time—" My voice cracked, and I hated that too. "The whole time, the price was sleeping under a rowan tree and humming lullabies to herself in the dark somewhere beneath a mountain."

No one contradicted me. That was the worst part. Not one of them said *you're wrong*, or *it wasn't like that*. They just sat in their crescent of chairs wearing the faces of people who had done arithmetic with a human soul and gotten the answer they wanted.

"The council forbids it," Maren said finally, formally, the old words dragging out of her like roots out of clay. "By the authority of the Founding Charter, no oath-bound member of this valley shall pass the ridgeline while the bargain stands. Felix Almeida — hear us. Stay."

"I hear you," I said, and walked out.

* * *

That night I sat in my house with the map spread open on the table and a candle burning low, and I did not burn it.

I want it noted that I considered it. There was a version of me — the dutiful one, the one who drilled forms at dawn and tended graves and swallowed the cage and called it home — who knelt at the hearth and fed the map to the flames and went back to sleep and lived another fifty quiet years. I felt that version of me standing at my shoulder, reasonable and tired, whispering that the elders were right, that the valley needed its walls, that one soul on a shelf was a small price for three hundred years of peace.

But I kept thinking about the shelf.

I kept thinking about my mother — not the grave-mother, the flower-and-stone mother, but the real one, the one who used to sing off-key and burn the bread and laugh with her whole ribcage — sitting in the dark under a mountain, bottled and labeled and *warm*, warm enough that a stranger had frozen to death trying to tell me about it. Fourteen years of flowers on a headstone. Fourteen years of the whole valley lying to my face with kind voices.

If I burned the map, I wasn't protecting the valley. I was protecting *them*. The seven in their crescent of chairs, and their parents before them, and theirs before that — three hundred years of people choosing comfort over courage and calling it love.

I looked at the map a long time. Then I folded it along its old creases, careful as folding a letter to someone living, and put it inside my coat where it had been all day, against my ribs, where it had started to feel less like paper and more like a heartbeat.

Then I packed.

Not much. A valley life fits in a satchel — that's either tragic or efficient depending on the hour. Whetstone, flint, dried meat, the good wool socks my mother knitted the winter before she died, which I had never once worn because they were hers and wearing them felt like spending her. I packed them now. If I was going to do this, I was going to do it with her on my feet.

My sword I wrapped in oilcloth, though I didn't know why. The oath was inert past the ridge — everyone knew that, the way everyone knew the rhyme about the winter. Swordspeech didn't work outside the valley. Whatever I carried up that mountain, it wouldn't be magic.

It would just be me.

Somewhere past midnight, the first flakes of the early snow began ticking against the window, soft as fingertips, patient as creditors. I blew out the candle and stood in the dark listening to them fall, and I thought about the garden at the valley's edge — the impossible one, green in every season, that the children dared each other to touch and the adults pretended not to see.

Nobody ever talked about who kept it.

I slung the satchel over my shoulder and opened the door onto the falling snow, and stepped out toward the edge of everything I had ever known.
