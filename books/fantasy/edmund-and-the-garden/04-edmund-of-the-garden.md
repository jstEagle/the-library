## 4. Edmund of the Garden

I left before dawn, which felt appropriately dramatic and also meant I spent the first hour walking into things.

The elders had forbidden me to go, which in the valley's particular dialect of love meant they'd told me exactly where to look for the truth: anywhere but here. They'd given me a speech about duty, about the Founding Bargain, about how a soul was a small price for three generations of peace — as if my mother were a coin someone had pocketed at a market stall, and everyone had agreed not to mention it because mentioning it would be rude.

So I packed everything, which is to say: two shirts, my sword, the map with its bloodstain gone brown and stiff, a wheel of cheese that would regret coming, and my grandmother's boots, which fit no one in our family properly and never have. I left a note on the kitchen table. It said *gone to commit theft* and then, after some thought, *back by spring*. I am not a cruel person, but I wanted them to sit with the sentence a while.

The valley let me walk out of it the way it lets everything leave: without comment. That's the thing people don't understand about a place where magic cannot follow. It isn't peaceful. It's indifferent, the way a locked room is indifferent to the person inside it. The valley doesn't hate magic. It simply declines to acknowledge that anything outside itself exists, and if you grow up inside that refusal long enough, you start to wonder whether you exist either.

I drilled sword-forms at the boundary every morning of my life. I want to say that now so you understand what I was doing when I found him: standing at the edge of the world with a blade in my hand, speaking words into air that couldn't hear them, practicing a defense for an enemy that had never come, against a threat that had already been paid off in the only currency my family had.

That was when I saw the green.

* * *

You have to understand what green means at the valley's edge in late autumn, weeks after the frost has taken everything down to gray stalks and brown memory. Green means nothing. Green is impossible. The pines keep their color, yes, but this was not pine-dark; this was the soft, shameless green of growing things, lettuce-green, new-leaf green, the green of a season that hadn't been invited.

It sat maybe two hundred paces from the boundary stone, tucked into a fold of the hillside like a secret kept badly. As I got closer I could pick out shapes: bean vines on trellises. A row of something leafy. Roses — roses, in November, blooming like they'd never heard of winter, which I suppose they hadn't.

And in the middle of it all, kneeling in the dirt with his sleeves rolled up, a man.

I did what any sensible person does when confronted with an impossibility: I drew my sword and announced myself.

"Stop where you are," I said, "and explain the beans."

He looked up. He had a face you'd forget on purpose — not plain exactly, but unurgent, the kind of face that seems designed to put people at ease, with brown hair going gray at the temples and dirt on his knuckles and an expression of mild, genuine pleasure at seeing me, as though I were a friend he'd been expecting rather than a stranger pointing a blade at his cabbages.

"The beans are climbing beans," he said. "They do that."

"You know what I mean."

"I imagine I do." He rose slowly, brushing soil from his knees, and spread his hands in a gesture that managed to be both surrender and welcome. "You're one of the valley folk. Almeida girl. Felix." He pronounced it carefully, like a word in a language he was still learning. "Though I expect nobody uses your whole name much anymore."

My grip tightened. Nobody used my full name. My father had died before I could ask him why we kept it folded away like linen saved for an occasion that never comes. Two days ago a dying stranger had said it to me with blood on his teeth, and now a man made of November stood in an impossible garden saying it back to me like a key trying doors.

"How do you know my name?"

"I know most things that grow near me," he said. "People count, more or less. You've walked past here four hundred and eleven times since you were tall enough to see over the wall grass. You always stop at the same spot by the third trellis. You never come in." He smiled, small and apologetic. "Until today."

"That's not knowing someone. That's surveillance."

"It's gardening," he said, with such gentle conviction that I actually lowered my sword an inch. "Things grow toward what pays attention to them. I pay attention. It's the whole of my method."

We looked at each other across the bean rows. Frost lay silvered on the fields behind me. Here, the soil was dark and warm-looking, and the air smelled of thyme and turned earth, and somewhere under all of it, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, I felt something I had never once felt inside the valley.

Magic. Old, quiet, rooted. Not approaching. Just *present*, the way weather is present.

"You're outside the boundary," I said.

"I'm at the edge of it," he corrected. "There's a difference. The valley refuses magic, and I refuse to be refused. We've reached an understanding over the years." He gestured at a low stone bench beneath an apple tree that was simultaneously in blossom and heavy with ripe fruit, which should have been obscene and instead just looked tired, somehow, like a man working two jobs. "Sit. You look like someone who hasn't slept in a barn."

"I slept in a barn."

"Then you know how barns sleep you." He produced a clay cup from somewhere — gardeners produce things from nowhere the way priests produce blessings — and poured something steaming from a dented kettle. "Chamomile and honey. Drink. Then tell me why the Almeida girl finally came past the third trellis, and I'll decide whether to lie to you."

* * *

His name was Edmund. He offered it the way he seemed to offer everything — freely, and with the slight air of a man handing you something fragile and hoping you'll be careful with it.

I told him almost nothing, and he heard almost all of it anyway. I told him a stranger had come out of the pass and died holding my wrist. I told him about the map, the debt, the court beneath the mountain. I told him the elders had admitted — finally, cornered like cats in a sack — that my family's safety was bought with a soul, and that the soul was my mother's, and that the long winter coming early meant the bill had arrived.

He listened the way he gardened, I think: completely, without hurry, giving each word its own row to grow in.

When I finished, he didn't gasp or cluck or reach for comforting platitudes. He watered a tray of seedlings while he thought, which struck me as either very rude or very wise.

"And you're going up the pass," he said finally. "Alone. In the first snows. After a man who knew your name died of cold on the very route you mean to take."

"When you put it that way it sounds stupid."

"It is stupid," he said pleasantly. "It's also correct. Those often arrive together." He set down the watering can. "The court beneath the mountain keeps ledgers, and ledgers don't care about courage. They care about terms. Do you know the terms?"

"Some. One soul per generation, renewable if unclaimed. If she stays claimed past the renewal—" I stopped. Saying *my mother dies forever* aloud in a stranger's garden was harder than I'd expected. Everything about her had been hard to say aloud for years; grief in the valley is conducted quietly, like prayer, like housework.

"If she stays claimed," Edmund finished gently, "she becomes property outright. And the winter seals the valley, and whatever's written next in the ledger gets written about you." He studied me. "You carry the oath. The swordspeech. It sleeps in there, doesn't it? In the valley. Like a bird with a cloth over its cage."

"How did you—"

"Four hundred and eleven times past my wall, doing forms that make no sound. Sword-forms aren't silent unless the words in them are being swallowed." He tilted his head. "Outside the line, they wouldn't be. Have you ever wondered why your family kept an oath none of them could speak?"

"I've wondered why my family kept anything," I said. "A valley, a name, a debt. It's like inheriting a house where every room is rented to somebody else's secrets."

Something moved through his face then — quick, buried fast, but I caught it. Recognition. The expression of a man hearing his own biography read back to him in a stranger's voice.

"Yes," he said. "That's what it's like."

* * *

I stayed the day. I tell myself it was tactical — I needed information, rest, a guide who knew the pass — but the truth is that the garden held me the way warm water holds you, and I didn't struggle much.

Edmund worked while he talked, and talked while he worked, and I learned him in pieces the way you learn anyone worth learning. He remembered every plant. Not the way a good gardener remembers — *that's the sage, third year, needs splitting* — but completely. There was a plum tree at the far end, half-wild, and he told me it had been planted by a woman with a burn scar on her left hand, sixty-one years ago, on an evening that smelled of woodsmoke, and that she had cried while she tamped the earth down and laughed by the time she finished, and he had given her cuttings of rosemary for the road. He said it plainly, without pride, the way you'd report rain totals.

"And you remember all that," I said, "but not how old you are."

"I remember years the way the tree remembers them," he said. "In rings. From inside, they all feel like now." He shrugged. "I stopped counting because counting started to feel like hoarding."

"Who planted you?" I asked, and immediately winced, but he only laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, warm as the garden air.

"That's nearly the right question." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Someone left me here, a long time ago, the way you leave a lamp lit for someone who may or may not come home. I've tended the edge ever since. Kept a little summer burning where the valley's refusal runs thin." He looked at me then, directly, and his eyes were the exact brown of good soil, and for a moment I had the strange vertiginous sense of looking into something much older than the face around it, something patient and buried and enormous, like roots under a lawn. "You asked how I know your name. Here is part of the answer, freely given: I was left behind too, Felix Almeida. Whatever the bargain cost your family, it cost the ones who kept its edges as well. I am what remains when a debt is paid and no one collects the change."

I didn't fully understand him. I want to be honest about that. But I understood *left behind*, and I understood *remains*, and there is a kinship between people that gets built out of those two words alone — flimsy at first, and then, without your permission, load-bearing.

"The elders never mentioned you," I said.

"The elders mention the weather more often than they mention true things." He said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "They know the garden exists. Some of them bring me their dead plants and ask me to argue them back to life. I always do. They never ask how." He smiled. "Protection makes people incurious. Curiosity feels like ingratitude, when your safety was purchased."

That landed somewhere bruised in me. I drank my chamomile and didn't answer.

* * *

Toward evening he walked me to the garden gate — a gate, absurdly, made of woven living hazel, still leafing — and stood with his hands in his pockets while the first stars came out over the pass.

The pass. I looked at it longer than I meant to. By day it had seemed merely difficult. At dusk it looked like a wound in the world, white already at its throat, waiting for snow to finish the job.

"I'm leaving at first light," I said.

"I know."

"You can't talk me out of it."

"I wouldn't dream of it. Talking people out of correct stupidity is a waste of good breath." He was quiet a moment. "But you'll die on it alone, and I find I mind that, and I've been trying all afternoon to work out why."

"Because you're kind?"

"Because kindness is a habit and mine has deep roots, possibly." He turned to face me fully. "Here is the other part of the answer I owed you. I know these mountains. I know where the snow lies soft and where it lies hungry. I know which springs stay open in hard winters and which caves the wind respects. I know the pass of Small Mercies the way I know this garden — plant by plant, stone by stone, mercy by mercy." He paused. "I will guide you over it, if you'll have me. And when we come to the door beneath the mountain — and we will come to it, your map is honest even if its messenger wasn't — I will stand at the threshold with you. What happens past it, I can't promise. But I can promise you won't face the ledger alone."

I stared at him. "Why? You don't know me. Four hundred walks past your wall doesn't make us friends."

"No," he agreed. "But I watched a child stop at the third trellis every morning and practice words no one could hear, for years, without ever once skipping a day. Do you know what that is, Felix?"

"Stubbornness."

"Duty," he said. "Duty with nowhere to go. I know that condition intimately. I've had it for a very long time." He held out his hand — soil in the creases, warmth in the palm, a gardener's handshake, offered like a cutting: take this, plant it, see what grows. "Two leftovers, then. Walking together."

Wary kinship, the elders would have called it, disapproving. I called it the first sensible decision I'd made in days, which should tell you something about my week.

I shook his hand.

* * *

I went back to the barn he showed me — dry, sweet-smelling, with a lantern and blankets that appeared with suspicious readiness, as though he'd been expecting guests for years and had simply been keeping the lamp lit — and I lay awake listening to the wind test the pass like a singer warming up.

Somewhere out there, beneath the snow that hadn't fallen yet, a court was opening its ledgers. Somewhere above me my mother's soul sat on a shelf, warm, waiting to be collected or claimed. And somewhere closer than either — close enough to hear, if I pressed my ear to the ground — I could have sworn the garden hummed.

Not the plants. Something underneath them. Patient as roots, old as rings.

I decided I was too tired to think about it, which was a lie, and fell asleep anyway, which was not.
