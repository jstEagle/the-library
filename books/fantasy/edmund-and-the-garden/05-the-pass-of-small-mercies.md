## 5. The Pass of Small Mercies

*Felix*

The valley ended the way most good lies end: abruptly, and with a view.

I had walked to its edge a hundred times in my life. I had never once stepped past it. There was always a reason — drills at dawn, graves to tend, an elder's cough that needed seeing to, my own cowardice dressed up as duty. The boundary itself was nothing dramatic. No wall of fire, no whispering ward-stones. Just a line where the grass stopped growing quite so obediently, and the air changed the way air changes when you step out of a crowded room into weather.

Edmund stood beside me with a pack on his back and a walking stick he hadn't needed until now, which I suspected was theater. Everything about Edmund was slightly theatrical in the gentlest possible way, like a man performing the role of *harmless gardener* for an audience of one, and enjoying it.

"You can still turn around," he said. "The elders will forgive you eventually. Elders are built for it."

"They'll forgive me the way frost forgives a window."

"That's still forgiveness. Of a sort." He nodded at the pass ahead — a gray seam stitched up the mountain's shoulder, already dusted white though autumn was barely limping along. "The Pass of Small Mercies. That's what they call it locally."

"Locally meaning you?"

"Meaning me. I named it. Someone had to." He started walking before I could ask what small mercies it actually offered, which I would learn was his signature move: answer everything except the question.

The first hour was ordinary misery. My boots were wrong for scree. My pack was wrong for anything longer than a long afternoon. Edmund set a pace that was either considerate or condescending, and I couldn't tell which, and resented him mildly for both possibilities. The valley shrank behind us with insulting speed — all those years of drills and grave-tending compressing into a green smudge between two ridgelines, as if the whole place could be folded up and put away.

Then we crossed the line.

I didn't feel it happen. That's the strange part. I'd braced for something — a shudder, a sound, the sensation of passing through cold water. Instead there was just a moment when the wind came from a different direction than it should have, and Edmund glanced sideways at me with an expression I couldn't read, and kept walking.

It happened an hour later, at the first rest stop, when I drew my sword to clear a tangle of dead brush from the trail.

In the valley, my sword was a sword. Good steel, honest weight, twenty-two years of forms drilled into my arms. Swordspeech — the thing my family had been oath-bound to carry, the inheritance nobody would explain — was a word written in invisible ink. I'd spoken the words ten thousand times. They made shapes in the air and did nothing else. The elders said patience. The elders said *in its season*. The elders said a great many things, I was beginning to understand, precisely so that no one would ask follow-up questions.

So when I spoke the opening form — *edge remember, edge return* — I expected the usual nothing.

The blade sang.

Not rang. *Sang.* A note climbed out of the steel and hung in the air like a struck bell that had decided to be a choir, and the dead brush fell apart into pieces too clean to be cut, more like *edited*, and every hair on my arms stood up and paid attention.

"What," I said.

"Ah," said Edmund.

"*What.*"

"I wondered how far we'd get before—" He gestured at the sword with his stick, the way a man gestures at a dog that has just done something alarming and impressive. "Magic can't follow you into the valley, Felix. But you're not in the valley anymore. And whatever your family carried down the mountain all those years ago — it isn't magic. It's older than the distinction."

I spoke the second form. The note changed key. Frost on a nearby boulder lifted off the stone in a fine silver mist and hung there, arranged, *listening*. I spoke the third and the mist spun into a spiral and I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, a sound I didn't recognize coming out of my own mouth — because it was like this: it was like spending your whole life mute and discovering the muteness had been a room, and you'd only ever tried to speak inside it.

I spent two hours doing forms I'd done ten thousand times, and they were all new. The sword pulled light along its edge. It hummed harmonics when I moved it fast. At one point I cut a falling leaf into four quarters without touching three of them, and I want it noted that I did not stop grinning for some considerable time afterward.

When I finally lowered the blade, breathless, cheeks aching, I found Edmund sitting on his pack watching me with an expression I took a while to name, because it wasn't the one I'd have expected.

He looked happy for me. Underneath that, unmistakably, he looked sad. And underneath *that* — this was the part that unsettled me — he looked like someone remembering.

"You knew," I said. "You knew it would wake up out here."

"I hoped," he said. "Knowing is a strong word for a man in my position."

"And the face?"

He smiled, and the sadness folded itself away somewhere neat, like linen. "Every gift has a previous owner," he said. "That's all. Come on. We want the saddle before dark, or the pass will introduce itself less politely."

We climbed. I hummed the sword-note under my breath the whole way, quietly, like a girl with a new song she doesn't want to finish yet.

*Edmund*

There is a particular sorrow in watching someone open a door you once nailed shut from the other side.

I don't mean that literally. I mean it in the way I mean most things these days, which is loosely, and with an eye toward the plants. Felix Almeida stood in the wind doing sword-forms her ancestors had carried out of the world like embers in cupped hands, and she laughed, and the mountain listened, and I sat on my pack and thought: *yes. This is what it costs. This is also what it buys.*

She asked about my face. I gave her the linen-folded answer. It was even mostly true.

Here is what I did not say: that I recognized the note her blade sang, because I had heard it before, decades ago, sung by a woman moving fast down-mountain in the wrong direction, with a sleeping child wrapped against her chest and terror worn smooth over her features like river stone. That woman had paused at the garden's edge — my garden, though *mine* is another loose word — and looked at the green things growing in impossible snow, and said, "Can you keep a secret?" and then, without waiting, "Keep him safe. Keep him *ordinary*. Whatever comes asking."

I had said yes. I am constitutionally incapable of saying otherwise; it is one of the few parts of myself the court left unfiled. And so I had watched her climb back up toward the pass alone, and I had kept the child ordinary for twenty-odd years, from a distance, badly, by not interfering, which is the hardest kind of keeping there is.

Felix does not know any of this. She knows I was "left behind too," which is true the way a map is true — accurate, and missing everything that matters.

The pass earned its name that evening, as promised. Small mercies: a shelf of flat stone out of the wind, a spring that ran sweet instead of mineral, a stand of mountain pine whose roots had woven a natural windbreak taller than a man. I had known about all three for a very long time. Growing things tell you where the world keeps its kindnesses, if you listen. Most people don't listen. Most people are in a hurry.

Felix listened without knowing she was doing it. She chose the flat shelf over softer-looking ground, tasted the air near the spring before drinking, banked our fire in the lee of the pines. Oath-trained, all of it. Her body knew things her elders had never told her.

"Ask me," she said, across the fire. She was turning her sword slowly in the firelight, watching the note-shimmer run along the fuller. "Whatever you're not saying. Ask me something instead, so I know what it looks like when you do it."

That caught me off guard, which takes some doing. "What do you mean?"

"You've answered four questions today with scenery. I counted. So show me the correct technique, and I'll learn by observation. You seem like the type who teaches that way."

I laughed. It surprised us both. "All right," I said. "A question." I considered. "What will you do if the debt can't be paid? If the court beneath won't trade, won't bargain, won't give her back?"

Firelight. Wind in the pines, going through their one long vowel. She was quiet long enough that I regretted the question, and then not quiet long enough for me to take it back.

"I'll steal her," Felix said. "I've been practicing theft since I was six. The elders' honey, the tithe-wine, extra bread at midwinter. I'm extremely qualified."

"It's rather larger than a pantry."

"So was the lie they fed us." She sheathed the sword, and the singing faded down to a thread, and then to memory. "Your turn. Answer honestly: what happens to you if we fail?"

And there it was — the correct technique, demonstrated. I looked into the fire and gave the honest answer, or the shape of one.

"I've failed before," I said. "It turns out to be survivable. In the way that a tree survives being turned into a fence post. You go on being useful. Nobody asks you what you were."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she said, "Edmund. What *were* you?"

"Tomorrow," I said, "we cross the scree fields, and I'd rather you sleep."

It was a coward's answer and she knew it, and she let me have it anyway, which was its own small mercy, and I lay awake half the night adding it to the ledger of debts I keep, which has only one column, and no bottom page.

* * *

*Felix*

The avalanche came on the third morning, and it taught me the difference between hearing a mountain and listening to one.

We were crossing the scree fields below the saddle — gray slopes of broken stone fanning out beneath cliffs the color of old teeth — when Edmund stopped mid-stride, tilted his head, and said, "Off the path. Now. Up-slope, to the left, that rib of rock. Quickly, please, and don't argue beautifully the way I suspect you're about to."

I argued briefly and unbeautifully, but I went, because everything in my oath-training was screaming in agreement with him. We gained the rock rib — a spine of stone jutting from the slope like a knuckle — and thirty seconds later the mountainside above us exhaled.

I have no better word for it. The snowpack let go all at once, a white throat clearing itself, and the sound was not loud so much as *total* — a sound that left no room for any other sound, including thought. The slide came down the gully we'd been walking, hit the fan of scree, and spread, and roared, and the world became white noise and white motion, and I pressed against the rock and felt the whole mountain shudder like a struck drum.

Then it was over. Dust and powder hanging in the air, glittering. Our path gone. The silence afterward enormous.

"We'd have been—" I started.

"Under it, yes." Edmund was brushing stone grit off his sleeves with maddening calm. "Though 'under' flatters it. Avalanches don't bury so much as *redistribute*. You'd have become a feature of the lower slope. Possibly scenic, come spring."

"How did you know?"

He crouched, laid his palm flat against the snow, and held it there. "Listen," he said.

I listened. Wind. Settling stone. My own heartbeat doing something undignified.

"Now listen the way I mean."

I crouched beside him, put my hand on the snow, and reached — the way I'd reached all morning with the sword, the way the forms had taught me without teaching me, some listening muscle I'd owned all my life and never been allowed to use. And the snow *spoke*. Not in words. In weight, and strain, and the slow cold arithmetic of layers — the crust, the depth hoar beneath it like rotten timber, the whole slope a sentence waiting for a period. I snatched my hand back.

"It's been ready to fall for days," I said. "All of it. The whole pass is—"

"Holding its breath. Yes." He stood, and there was the sorrow again, brief as a bird's shadow. "Growing things and dying things and things in between — they're all talking, all the time. Your valley is deaf by design, child. Out here, everyone hears eventually. Some of us just heard sooner."

"Because of what was done to you." I said it plainly, because I was tired of scenery-for-answers, and because the snow had just shown me its teeth and I felt entitled to a little plainness in exchange.

Edmund looked at me for a long moment. Powder-dust settled in his hair and made him look, absurdly, like a man aging in fast-forward.

"Yes," he said. "Because of what was done to me. And because I intend for you to reach the court beneath alive, and the court beneath is *worse* than avalanches, and every tool you have, you will need." He picked up his pack. "Including the questions I haven't answered. They're tools too. Trust me."

"The trouble, Edmund," I said, falling into step beside him as we picked our way around the slide's debris field — trees snapped off at the knee, stones sorted by size like a giant's laundry — "is that trusting you currently requires believing a man who admits he's lying by omission."

"I prefer 'narrating in installments.'"

"You would."

Above us, the saddle showed against the sky, a notch of hard blue. Below us, the world fell away southward into country I had never seen — valleys I didn't know the names of, rivers I couldn't point to on the elders' deliberately vague maps, and somewhere out there, days distant, a border town where people sold pieces of themselves, and beyond it the mountain that held the court, and beneath *that*, on a shelf in the dark, something warm that hummed lullabies.

My mother. Or what the court had kept of her.

I touched the hilt of my sword, and it hummed back, faint and glad, like a dog hearing its name.

That night, camped in the saddle itself with the whole world laid out below, I dreamed of the stranger from the pass — the dying man with the map and my unused name in his mouth — and in the dream he wasn't dying, he was *waiting*, and when he saw me he smiled with terrible relief and said:

*"You woke it up. Good. They'll have felt that. They always feel it."*

I woke with the stars overhead and Edmund's fire burned down to red eyes, and I lay still, heart hammering, telling myself dreams are dreams.

But far to the north, past the town of sold names, the mountain stood black against the stars — and I could have sworn, could have sworn on my oath and my sword and my mother's unfinished song, that its shadow was deeper than the night around it.

As if something down there had opened a door to look up.

And felt me looking back.
