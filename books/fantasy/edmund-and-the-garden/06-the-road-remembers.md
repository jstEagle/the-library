## 6. The Road Remembers

I have kept a garden for longer than I can honestly claim to remember, which is a strange thing to say, because remembering is the one thing I do without effort. Names of plants, the year the frost came late, the exact shade of green a leaf turns when it has decided to die — all of it stays. What slips from me is time itself. Years pass through me like water through a sieve, and I am left holding only what grew.

So when I say that I remember a woman walking this road, I cannot tell you how long ago it was. A decade. Two. The road does not care for calendars either; it only remembers feet.

We had made camp that first night in the lee of a granite shoulder, out of the wind that comes down off the pass like an unpaid debt collector. Felix slept badly — she had slept badly since the stranger died in her arms, and I suspected she would sleep badly until whatever was owed was paid — and so I sat up and kept the fire small and let my mind wander where it liked, which is usually backward.

The woman came down the mountain at dusk. That much I am sure of. Dusk, because the light was doing that thing it does in autumn, turning everything the color of an old promise, and she was backlit by it so that I saw her mostly as outline: a cloak too thin for the altitude, hair escaping its braid in gray-streaked wisps, and in her arms a bundle that breathed.

A sleeping child. Small. Maybe three years old, maybe four. Wrapped in a blanket that had been fine once and was now more mending than wool.

She did not walk like a traveler. Travelers pace themselves; they have destinations and the good sense to arrive at them intact. This woman walked like someone being chased by something that could not be outrun but must be anyway. She kept glancing over her shoulder at the mountain — not the road behind her, mind you, but the mountain itself, as if the danger were above rather than behind. And she was going down. Down-mountain, toward the valley, toward the place where magic cannot follow.

I stood at the edge of my garden, which even then stood green and impossible at the valley's mouth, and I watched her come, and something in my chest — I had a chest then, and things in it — recognized the shape of her fear.

"Please," she said, before I had said a word. Her voice was scraped raw. "The garden. They said there's a garden that's always green. Please, just — let us rest inside it. One night."

"They?" I asked, though I knew who they were. There is only ever one they, if you go deep enough into any story.

"The ones beneath." She would not look at the mountain while she said it. "They let me go. That's the worst part. They *let* me go, and I keep waiting to understand why."

I let her rest. Of course I did. I gave her soup and a corner of the greenhouse where the basil grew thick enough to sleep in, and she laid the child down among the leaves, and the child slept on with the terrible completeness of the very young, who trust the world because no one has yet shown them the ledger.

And here is the thing I have carried ever since, the stone in my shoe of memory: she talked. People talk to gardeners. We are priests without the sermons. She talked in fragments, half-asleep, and I pieced together what she never quite said whole — that she had been given to the court beneath the mountain, that she had been permitted to bear a child first, that the child was spoken for in some way she could not bring herself to name aloud, and that she had run anyway, knowing it was useless, because running is what mothers are for.

"I'm not stealing her back," the woman whispered, in the hour before dawn, watching her daughter breathe among the basil. "You can't steal what they already own. I know that. I just wanted her to see the sky once. From the inside. Not through a window."

Then morning came, and she was gone, and the child was gone, and I never learned their names.

Except.

Except that I have spent the years since learning everything else about them, haven't I? Because the child grew. The child became a woman who drills sword-forms at gravesides and chafes against an oath she doesn't understand, and last week a dying man pressed a map into her hands, and yesterday she walked out of the valley carrying her mother's old stubbornness like a second blade.

Felix Almeida slept ten feet away from me, breathing in the rhythm of someone dreaming about running, and I sat with the fire and did not wake her, and did not tell her, and hated myself with the quiet efficiency of long practice.

* * *

The bandits found us on the third day, at a place where the road narrows between two rockfalls and every sensible traveler feels the prickle on the neck before they hear the whistle.

There were five of them, which is four more than you want and one fewer than they'd need if Felix had been anyone else. They were gaunt, winter-early men with mismatched armor and the particular desperation of people whose usual prey had stopped traveling. The long winter was coming early — we'd both felt it, the light going wrong at noon, the cold arriving like a creditor — and hungry men feel it first.

"Gentlemen," I said, stepping forward with my hands visible and my voice pitched to the register I use for frightened animals and frightened people, which is nearly the same register. "We've little worth taking, but you're welcome to—"

"We'll take the swords," said the leader, "and anything else."

He was looking at Felix when he said it, and I understood immediately that he had made the mistake every sensible person makes upon meeting her, which is to assume that the slight young woman with the wary eyes is the one to negotiate with and the pleasant middle-aged gardener is the harmless one. It is a reasonable assumption. I have worked hard to earn it.

"Felix," I said mildly, "perhaps we should—"

She drew.

I want to be fair to her here. She didn't draw to kill. I don't think she even drew to fight, exactly. She drew because five armed men had stepped onto the road in front of her, and somewhere under her skin something older than her judgment answered before she could.

Her sword woke up.

That is the only word for it. In the valley, her gift lies dormant — magic cannot follow past the boundary, and swordspeech is magic wearing steel — but we were two days beyond the pass now, high and cold and outside, and when the blade cleared its scabbard it *sang*. Not rang. Rang is what metal does. This sang, a low note that started in the steel and climbed into the air and then into your teeth, and I watched the bandits' faces change as it reached them, watched the hunger drain out and something older move in, the animal knowledge that says *run*.

Felix didn't run. Felix spoke.

Not words, exactly — or words, but words the way a landslide is geology. The oath-language, the swordspeech her family had carried for generations and never needed, came out of her in a torrent, and the air around her blade bent, and frost leapt up along the ground in a spreading fan, and the nearest bandit's axe-head bloomed with ice crystals like something flowering in fast-forward.

"*Yield,*" she said, and the word hit them like weather.

Four of them yielded. The fifth — young, stupid, or simply too hungry to be wise — swung his blade at her anyway, and I will give Felix this: she did not kill him. Her sword met his with a sound like a struck bell and a crack of blue-white light, and his weapon sheared off a hand's width above the hilt, and he sat down hard in the snow and stared at the stump of his sword and then at her, and began, very quietly, to cry.

"It's the winter," he said. "It's coming early. Everyone knows it's coming early. We heard the court beneath is collecting, and there's nothing left to eat, and—" He stopped himself, looked up at her with red-rimmed eyes. "You're one of them, aren't you? You're one of the oath-keepers. The valley folk."

"I don't know what I am," Felix said, and her voice shook, and the sword in her hand was still singing faintly, and I could see that it frightened her more than the bandits had.

"Go," I told the five of them, gently, gathering up the broken sword-hilt and pressing it back into the crying man's hands. "South, if you can. The border towns have work. Avoid the high passes after dark." And because I am what I am, I added: "There's rosemary growing wild by the stream back a mile. Chew the needles if the hunger wakes you at night. It won't feed you, but it will remind your body it's alive, and that helps."

They went. Bandits, in my experience, are rarely evil; they are mostly early casualties of whatever winter is coming, and this lot had the look of men who had been casualties for years.

Felix stood in the middle of the road with her sword still drawn, breathing hard, staring at the frost-fan on the ground as if it might explain itself.

"That wasn't me," she said.

"It was you."

"It *wasn't.*" She turned on me, and her eyes were bright and scared. "Edmund, I have drilled forms my whole life and never once — never *once* — has anything like that happened. The words just came. I didn't choose them. I didn't even know them, and I knew them." She looked at her own hand on the grip. "What is this? What am I carrying?"

*A debt,* I thought. *A birthright. A bargain's interest, compounding.* And underneath all of it, unspoken: *Your mother ran down this same road with you asleep in her arms, and the reason you don't know what you're carrying is that everyone who could have told you decided you were safer ignorant, and look how that's gone.*

"You're carrying a sword and an oath," I said instead. "The rest we'll learn as we walk."

She sheathed the blade slowly, and the singing faded, and she looked younger suddenly, and tired, and I felt the weight of my silence settle onto my shoulders like a pack I'd been carrying for years and had somehow convinced myself weighed nothing.

We walked on. The road climbed. Somewhere behind us the valley sat sealed under its bargain, and somewhere ahead the mountain waited with its court and its ledgers, and between the two of them Felix Almeida marched with her chin up and her gift awake and her mother's fear written all over her in handwriting she couldn't read.

I could read it. I had seen it before, on a dusk-colored road, in a woman who glanced over her shoulder at the mountain and asked only that her child see the sky once from the inside.

That night, making camp, Felix practiced. She thought I was asleep. She stood at the edge of the firelight with her blade unsheathed and whispered syllables of the old language, testing them, and each one made the steel hum, and each hum made her flinch and try again, and I lay with my eyes closed and listened to her teach herself the thing that had been taken from her mother, and I thought: *when she learns what was traded, whose soul sits on whose shelf, she will ask me why I said nothing.*

And I will deserve the answer I have to give.

But that was tomorrow's weight. Tonight there was only the fire, and the girl practicing her inheritance alone in the dark, and far below us — so far below that neither of us could hear it — the sound, if you pressed your ear to the stone, of something beneath the mountain turning a page.
