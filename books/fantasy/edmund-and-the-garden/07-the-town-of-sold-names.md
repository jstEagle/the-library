## 7. The Town of Sold Names

The town was called Vespergate, which sounded like the name of a place where something ended, and it turned out to be exactly that.

We came down out of the foothills at dusk with our boots full of snowmelt and my head still ringing from the pass — from the way my swordspeech had sung through me like a struck bell, like a thing I'd been carrying all my life without knowing it was alive. Edmund walked beside me with his hands in his pockets and that particular patience of his, the kind that isn't calm so much as *old*, and said nothing while I kept flexing my fingers like a girl who'd just discovered her hands could do tricks.

"You'll wear yourself out," he said finally.

"I'm wearing myself out *on purpose*. Do you know how long I've been—"

"Yes."

That shut me up. Edmund had a talent for one-word answers that landed like stones in a well. We'd known each other four days and I'd already learned to stop asking him things I didn't want stone-cold answers to.

Vespergate sat in the crook of the border like a coin someone had dropped and decided not to pick back up. It was bigger than anything I'd seen — I'd grown up in a valley of two hundred souls who all knew each other's business down to the last embarrassing childhood incident — and it was loud, and lit, and smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and frying onions, and I loved it immediately and completely, the way you love a stranger's dog.

"Brokers' Row," Edmund said, when I asked where we'd find information about the mountain road. "But we should talk about what it costs first."

"It costs money. Everything costs money. I have three coppers and a button."

"It doesn't cost money." He stopped under an awning and looked at me with those eyes that were brown the way tree bark is brown, if you looked too long. "Here, memory is currency. Real currency. The brokers buy pieces of your past and sell them to people who want to feel things they never lived. You walk out lighter than you came in, and you don't always notice what's missing until you reach for it."

I laughed. I actually laughed. "That's absurd."

"Mm," said Edmund, which was how he said *you'll see*.

* * *

The broker's shop on Brokers' Row was called The Fair Exchange, which should have been warning enough. It was warm inside, warmer than any shop had a right to be, and it smelled faintly of honey and old paper. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves sat jars — hundreds of them, thousands — each holding a curl of light like smoke caught mid-dance, each labeled in a small neat hand.

*First snow, age six.* *My father's hands teaching mine.* *The taste of plums off the tree.* *A wedding morning.*

I stood in the doorway with the cold at my back and felt something in my chest go tight and strange.

"They're not dead," said a voice. "Before you ask. They're not even sleeping, exactly. They're *elsewhere*. Being enjoyed."

The broker was a small woman with silver hair cropped close and spectacles on a chain, and she looked at me the way a fishmonger looks at a trout — kindly, professionally, with an eye to weight. Her name, according to the little brass plate, was Maren Quill.

"I'm not selling," I said.

"No one ever is, at first." She poured tea without asking whether I wanted any, which is either hospitality or a trap depending on the town. "You're the girl asking after the under-mountain road. Third one this season."

My tea paused halfway to my mouth. "Third?"

"Mm." She settled behind her counter. "There was a surveyor in autumn, a big loud man named Corvin Hale. And before him a young woman traveling alone, wouldn't give her name. Both of them bought passage-knowledge the same way everyone does." She smiled. "Both of them paid in memory."

"And they went beneath the mountain."

"They did."

"And came back?"

Maren Quill took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on. All of it very slowly. I began to understand this was her version of Edmund's one-word answers.

"No," she said.

* * *

Edmund found me twenty minutes later sitting on a barrel outside the shop, watching the jars glow through the window like a jarful of captive sunsets.

"You went in anyway," he said.

"I went in to *look*."

"Felix."

"I didn't sell anything." Yet. The word hung there unspoken between us, fat as a raindrop. I rubbed my arms. "Edmund, there were people in there buying memories. A man paid for someone else's wedding morning. He closed his eyes and smiled like — like it was happening to him, right then, for the first time. And the woman who sold it will wake up tomorrow and know she got married but never remember being happy about it."

"That's the trade," Edmund said quietly. "It's honest, in its way. Everyone signs. Everyone knows the price going in. It's more honest than most bargains I've—" He stopped himself. He'd been doing that a lot lately, stopping himself at the edge of things, and I was starting to collect these unfinished sentences like coins I couldn't spend.

"The road," I said. "The under-mountain road. That's why we're here."

"There's a guide," he admitted. "Old Tomas. He knows the shepherd paths down to the Doorward Stair. But he won't take coin anymore. Not since the ones who didn't come back."

"Corvin Hale," I said. "And the unnamed woman."

"You've been busy."

"She also told me something else." I stood up off the barrel. My legs had gone cold and stubborn, which is how my legs do fear. "She said the third person asking this season would be along soon, and she hoped whoever it was would be smarter than the other two. She said it like she was reading it off a ledger."

Edmund was quiet for a long moment. Snow had started again — fine, dry, wrong-feeling snow, the kind that falls like ash. "Felix, whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," I said, "that I need to know the way down, and I need to know it tonight, and the only currency in this town that buys it is a piece of my own past. So help me choose which piece."

* * *

Choosing a memory to lose is a strange arithmetic. I sat in The Fair Exchange's back room with a cup of cold tea and tried to inventory my life like a merchant counting stock.

Not the oath-day — I needed the oath; it was apparently a key, though I hadn't told Edmund that part yet. Not my mother's face. Not the lullaby, though I couldn't have explained why the lullaby mattered so much except that it was the only thing of hers I had that wasn't a grave. Not the day I buried the stranger whose map had started all this, because guilt is a terrible thing to lose track of; guilt keeps you honest.

That left the good days. Which was the whole horror of it, I realized. The currency here was happiness. Nobody pawned their grief — grief was worthless, heavy, common as gravel. They pawned joy, because joy was rare and bright and burned long in another person's chest.

Maren Quill laid out my options with the gentleness of a woman who'd done this ten thousand times. "The strong ones fetch the best prices. First love. A triumph. A day when everything, briefly, made sense."

I thought about my life in the valley. Drills at dawn. Graves tended. An oath recited to no enemy. There hadn't been many days when everything made sense.

But there had been one.

The summer I was fourteen, before I understood what the valley was, before I chafed — there was a morning after a storm when the whole valley steamed in the sun, and my mother was still alive, and we climbed the ridge together to check the boundary stones, and she taught me the names of every flower coming up through the scorched grass, and at the top she said, *one day this will be yours, and I hope it's a gift and not a cage,* and I didn't understand her, and we laughed about something stupid — I've forgotten what, even now, or maybe I lost it later — and for one whole morning the valley was not a sanctuary or a sentence. It was just home, and she was just my mother, and I was just a girl with muddy boots.

I had carried that morning through everything. Through her death. Through the elders' evasions. Through the stranger dying with my name in his mouth. It was the proof, tucked somewhere behind my ribs, that the valley had once been worth loving.

"I'll take that one," I heard myself say.

Maren Quill looked at me over her spectacles. "Are you certain? Once it's decanted, the original is gone from you. You'll know it happened — there'll be a shape where it was, a fact without furniture. But the warmth goes into the jar."

"How much for the road knowledge?" I asked. "Tomas, the paths, the Doorward Stair. All of it."

Her eyebrows rose slightly. "That's a great deal of information."

"Then it's fortunate," I said, and my voice only shook a little, "that I'm offering you a whole morning of perfect happiness."

* * *

The taking itself was almost nothing. That's the obscene part. She had me hold a glass bell the size of an egg, close my eyes, and go to the memory — really go there, walk through it, which meant I had to live it one last time on purpose, knowing it was the last time. The steam rising off the valley. Her hand in mine, callused from the garden. *One day this will be yours.* The light going gold on the boundary stones.

The bell grew warm. Then it glowed. Then it held a curl of light like smoke caught mid-dance, and Maren Quill corked it, labeled it in her small neat hand — *a mother and a daughter on a ridge, age fourteen* — and set it on the shelf between *first snow, age six* and *the taste of plums off the tree*.

And I knew it had happened. I knew it the way you know a fact from a book. Mother. Ridge. Flowers. Age fourteen. The words were all there.

The warmth was gone.

It's hard to describe a hole. It doesn't hurt. That's the trick of it, the cruelty dressed as mercy — it doesn't hurt at all. It's just that when I reached inward for that morning, the way I'd reached for it a thousand times across a thousand bad nights, my hand closed on smooth empty air. There was a fact-shaped room in me with no furniture in it, and I stood in the middle of that room and understood, suddenly and completely, every gray-eyed quiet person I'd ever passed on the road.

"Oh," I said. Small voice. "Oh, that's — that's what it's like."

"Yes," said Maren Quill, and for the first time she sounded tired. "Now you know what half this town is carrying. Or not carrying." She pushed the payment across the table: a folded paper of instructions, a token for Tomas, and a look I couldn't read. "For what it's worth, girl — the woman who came before you, the one who wouldn't give her name. She sold a memory too. Right before she left."

"What was it?"

"I can't say. Broker's confidence." She hesitated. "But she cried the whole time, and afterward she thanked me. Said she'd rather carry the loss than the weight. Said the mountain would take it from her anyway, better it go to someone who'd enjoy it." She took off her spectacles. "People say strange things when they're paying."

* * *

Outside, the snow was falling harder, and wronger. Edmund was waiting under the awning, and he read my face in one glance, the way he reads weather and plants and apparently people.

"You did it," he said.

"I did it." I patted my chest like a man checking for a wallet. "There's a hole in me shaped like a morning. It doesn't even hurt. That's the worst part, Edmund. It doesn't hurt. I keep waiting for it to hurt and it just — sits there. Being absent."

He put his coat around my shoulders without asking, which was becoming a habit, and we walked toward the inn through streets that were filling fast with white. People hurried past us with their collars up, and I watched them differently now. Wondering which of them had holes. Wondering what they'd traded and whether they missed it, whether they reached for it in the night the way I already knew I would.

"Two people went down that road this year," I said. "Neither came back. And the broker talks about it like weather. Like a fact of the landscape." I stopped walking. "Edmund, the court beneath the mountain has been doing this a long time, hasn't it? Taking people. Taking pieces of people. This whole border runs on it — the memories, the gray-eyed refugees, the roads nobody returns from. It's not a court. It's a *harvest*."

Edmund was silent so long that snow gathered on his shoulders and didn't melt.

"Yes," he said at last. "It is."

"And you know that how?"

Another silence. Another unfinished sentence gathering behind his teeth. But before he could decide whether to spend it, a boy came pelting down the street toward us, breathless, blue-lipped, shouting the news to anyone who'd listen:

"The pass! The high passes are closing — closed, they're saying, closed a month early! Winter's come down off the peaks like it's got somewhere to be! Refugees on the south road, hundreds of them, and half of them walking wrong, eyes all gray, not saying a word—"

Edmund and I looked at each other over the boy's head.

The long winter wasn't coming.

It had already begun to fall.
