## 8. Winter Finishes Falling

I have kept a garden at the edge of a valley that forgets for longer than I care to count, and in all that time I have learned one iron law of the seasons: they are rude, but they are punctual. First frost comes when the elderberries drop their leaves. Deep cold comes when the last of the rowan berries have been stripped by thrushes. Winter does not send word ahead. It simply arrives, like a bill collector who has lost his patience with polite letters.

So when the light began to fail in the middle of an afternoon in what should have been late autumn — not dimming, mind you, but *shortening*, as though some enormous hand were pinching the days closed like a purse — I knew the punctuality had ended.

I was pruning the quince when it happened. One moment the sun sat low and gold over the western ridge, throwing my shadow long across the beds; the next, that shadow was gone, and the ridge itself was gone, and the sky had gone the color of pewter left out too long in the rain. The temperature fell so fast that the dew on the kale froze mid-bead. I heard it — a faint crystalline tick, tick, tick, like a clock deciding to stop.

"Well," I said to the quince tree, because one must say something. "That's ahead of schedule."

The quince did not answer. Trees rarely do, and this one less than most, being dead of heartwood and living only by my stubbornness. But the other plants told me things in their own ways. The roses pulled their hips tight. The last beans curled their leaves like fists. Even the comfrey, which has the survival instincts of a particularly optimistic turnip, lay down flat against the soil as if hoping not to be noticed.

Everything growing knows before we do. Everything growing always knows.

This was not weather. Weather is indifferent. This was *administrative*.

* * *

Felix came down from the graves at dusk, or what passed for dusk now — a gray hour that arrived two hours early and smelled of iron. She had her sword wrapped in sacking over her shoulder, which she did whenever she'd been drilling at the cemetery and didn't want the elders to see her carrying it. A habit of hers. A foolish one, but habits are how people hold themselves together, and I am not in the business of taking apart anyone's stitching.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question. She stood at the garden gate with snow beginning to gather in her dark hair, and she looked at my green world sitting fat and impossible in the white like a lie told well enough to become true.

"I felt it," I said. "Come inside. There's tea."

"There's always tea."

"There is always tea," I agreed. "It's one of the few reliable institutions left."

She came in, and I put the kettle on the little stove in the potting shed where I do most of my living, and while it heated she unwound her sacking and laid the sword across the bench with the tenderness most people reserve for sleeping children. In the valley the blade was quiet — it was always quiet here, magic being unable to follow past the ridgeline, whatever else followed — but Felix still touched it the way you touch a thing you're afraid will wake.

"The elders are saying it's an early winter," she said. "A hard one. Bad luck."

"Mm."

"They're saying it happens. Once a generation, maybe."

"Mm."

"Felix," she said, mimicking the eldest elder's dry rasp, "*the mountain does not care about our calendars.*"

"He's right about that," I said. "He's wrong about nearly everything adjacent to it."

She looked up at me then, and I saw her doing the thing she does — turning a suspicion over in her hands like a stone, looking for the seam. She'd found the map three weeks ago pressed into her grip by a dying man who knew her name, and since then she had been a woman walking around inside a question. I confess I had not helped. There are things I know about her mother that I keep the way a wall keeps its mortar: not proudly, but structurally. If I pull them out, something falls.

But the winter was not going to wait for my courage, apparently. Winters never do.

"Edmund." She said my name flatly, the way you set a card face-up on a table. "What is it?"

I poured the tea. Two cups, because there are always two cups, even in years when no one comes. I handed her one and watched the steam rise off it and thought: *the steam rises straight*. No wind. No draft. The air itself had gone heavy, like a held breath, like a room where someone important is about to speak.

"It's not weather," I said. "Weather doesn't shorten the days. Days are a matter of astronomy, and astronomy keeps excellent books. What shortens the days is a court that has decided the year is over and begun putting the light away."

"The court beneath."

"The court beneath," I said. "They're harvesting early."

* * *

We went up to the pass road the next morning, because Felix insisted and because I have never once won an argument with her by pointing out that she could die. It is remarkable, really, how little that argument accomplishes. We took the switchbacks above the valley mouth, and from the high shoulder of the road we could see down the long descent toward the lowlands, and what we saw made me glad, in a grim way, that Felix saw it too, so that neither of us would have to describe it to the other.

Refugees.

Not a trickle. A river. Carts and sledges and families on foot, coming up out of the low country toward the valley passes, because word travels faster than sense and somewhere in the towns below a rumor had taken root: *the valley is safe, the valley is spared, go to the valley.* They came with everything they could drag. Oxen with frost in their nostrils. Children tied to their mothers' backs under quilts. An old man pulling a handcart with a goat in it, and the goat looking, if anything, more resigned than the man.

And among them — this was the part that stopped Felix's breath, I watched it stop — among them walked the quiet ones.

You can tell. I could always tell; it is one of the few advantages of having been filed away myself. The half-claimed move differently. Not slower, exactly. More *economically*, as though someone has gone through them and removed the wasted motions, the fidgets, the small human extravagances. Their eyes go gray the way water goes gray when the sediment settles. They answer when spoken to, promptly and correctly, and they smile at the correct moments, and every part of them is present except the part that would have minded.

"That woman," Felix said quietly. A young mother by the roadside, sharing bread with her children, doing everything correctly. "There's something wrong with her."

"She's not wrong," I said. "That's the horror of it. She's been *improved*. Streamlined. Someone in the court beneath looked at her life and decided which parts were essential, and it wasn't many parts."

"Why her? Why any of them?"

"Because a harvest doesn't ask permission, Felix. It asks *yield*." I watched the column of the dispossessed grind upward through the falling snow. "The bargain your ancestors made bought the valley's safety with one soul per generation. One soul, held as surety, renewable if unclaimed. That was the terms. But surety is only ever the down payment. Courts beneath mountains keep ledgers, and ledgers accrue interest, and interest is collected in whatever currency is nearest to hand."

"You're saying they're taking these people because—"

"I'm saying the debt has come due, and the debtor is late, and creditors do not remain patient forever. Your family's soul was the collateral. These—" I gestured at the gray-eyed woman, at the whole shuffling river of humanity hauling its grief uphill, "—these are the collection notice. Sent early. Sent wide."

Felix stood very still. Snow gathered on her shoulders and she did not brush it off, which for her is roughly equivalent to screaming. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone level in the way I had already learned to fear, because level was what happened just before she did something irreversible.

"How long do we have?"

I considered lying to her. I want that on the record. I considered it seriously, the way a man considers jumping a fence he knows has a dog behind it. But the day was shortening as we stood there — visibly, the light withdrawing up the eastern sky like a tide going out — and lies take time, and time was the one commodity the court had stopped selling.

"Before the last snow seals the passes?" I said. "Days. Perhaps a week. Perhaps less. And after the sealing—" I looked north, toward the mountain whose roots went down further than geology admits, "—after the sealing, nothing goes in, and nothing comes out, until the court decides otherwise. Which, given current management, may be never."

* * *

That evening the first refugees reached the valley gate, and the elders met them the way elders meet everything: with committees.

I stayed near the edge of it, in my capacity as nobody-in-particular, which is a capacity I have cultivated with great care over a very long time. The elders formed their anxious semicircle by the gatehouse fire. The eldest — dry old Marta Voss, who has been old since I was merely ancient — spoke the valley's position in the tone of a woman reading terms aloud to a flood.

"We have grain stores for two hundred more mouths through a normal winter. This will not be a normal winter. We can shelter perhaps forty families in the lower barns, no more, and we cannot promise—"

An old man in the crowd interrupted her. He had a woodcutter's shoulders and a voice like a door off its hinges, and he said, "Lady, we've seen what's following us. You'll promise whatever we need you to promise."

And the crowd murmured agreement, and the elders exchanged the particular glances of people discovering that charity has arrived wearing armor, and I turned away and looked instead at the quiet ones.

There were eleven of them in the first wave. I counted. Counting is a habit of mine; when you have been catalogued yourself, you develop a professional sympathy for the practice. Eleven men and women standing at the edges of the firelight, not quite in the warmth, not quite out of it. Their families fed them. Their families spoke to them. Their families had not yet entirely noticed, because the change is gentle at first — the way rot is gentle at first, all softness and no smell.

One of them, a young man with a farmer's hands, caught me watching him. And he smiled at me — correctly, at the correct moment — and something behind his gray eyes moved like a fish under ice, and I understood that he was still partly in there. Watching. Waiting. Perhaps hoping.

Perhaps reporting.

I did not sleep much that night. I sat in the potting shed with the lamp turned low and listened to the valley breathe, and I took stock the way I used to take stock in the old days, in the court, when inventory was a form of prayer.

Here is what I know about the court beneath the mountain, and I know it the way you know the house you were raised in, including the rooms you were locked out of. It is beautiful. That is the first thing and the worst thing. It is lit by stored summers, bottled light, and everything in it moves slowly because nothing in it needs to hurry — courts that live for centuries mistake patience for virtue. Its steward is courteous the way a knife is clean. And it keeps its accounts with a thoroughness that would bring tears to a tax collector's eyes.

When my own soul was taken — the first sold, the first given leave to walk the valley's edge instead of sitting on a shelf, an arrangement the steward called *efficient placement* and everyone else called mercy — what struck me most was not the loss. Loss announces itself. What struck me was the tidiness afterward. The rage I had carried all my life was gone, and in its place was a smooth surface, and on that surface I could grow anything except anger. Gentleness remained. Gentleness is what remains when a court files away your teeth.

The gray-eyed ones at the gate were at the beginning of that road. Give them a season under the mountain and they would be smooth as river stones, pleasant as spring water, and empty in the specific way that a bottle is empty — shaped perfectly to hold something that is no longer there.

And the court was harvesting *early*. That was the sentence I kept turning over like a coin I suspected of being clipped. Early meant afraid. Early meant the ledger had a deadline written in it that I couldn't see. Early meant—

Early meant Felix's mother's soul might not be sitting patiently on its shelf waiting to be reclaimed. Early meant the whole arrangement might be about to be called in, collateral and interest and all, and the long winter wasn't a season at all. It was a repossession.

* * *

On the third morning, the pass sealed.

Not with a storm — storms announce themselves, build, spend themselves honestly. This was different. The snow simply began at dawn and did not stop, and it fell *upward* along the northern heights, I saw it myself, flakes rising against gravity into the low clouds like sparks drawn back into a fire. By noon the Pass of Small Mercies — the route Felix and I would need, the only route down-mountain that didn't end in a cliff — had vanished under forty feet of new white, and the wind on the ridge sang in a voice I recognized, a slow patient voice with excellent diction, reciting something that was almost certainly terms and conditions.

Felix stood beside me at the garden gate, watching the white wall where the pass had been. Her breath came out in short furious clouds.

"So that's it," she said. "Sealed. Done. My mother stays on her shelf until the court decides the invoice is settled, and the valley gets to watch its neighbors get harvested, and everyone lives safely ever after."

"Not necessarily."

She turned. "Edmund, I watched the snow fall *upward*."

"Yes. Impressive, isn't it? Very showy. The court loves a display." I nodded at the buried pass. "But consider: why seal it now, before the deadline, unless the deadline matters to *them*? A door locked early is a door the locksmith worries about. Something down there is hurrying, Felix, and courts beneath mountains do not hurry unless the ledger is about to change hands. Which means there is still a ledger. Which means there is still a window. Sealed doors are only permanent if you believe the seal."

"And if I don't believe it?"

"Then we should probably discuss," I said, "how you feel about avalanches."

She looked at me for a long moment. Snow came down between us, unhurried, proprietary, each flake landing with the confidence of a thing that owns the ground it touches. Behind her the refugee fires smoked along the valley's lower fields, and somewhere among them eleven gray-eyed watchers counted what they saw, and beneath us, far beneath, something patient turned a page.

"Edmund," Felix said, and pulled the bloodstained map from inside her coat, "tell me everything you know about the door beneath the mountain. And don't be gentle about it. I've had enough gentle."

I looked at the map — at the crest inked at its foot, her family's crest, worn thin by a dying man's grip — and I thought of a woman I had once watched flee *down* this very mountain with a sleeping child in her arms, and I thought: *not yet. Soon. But not yet.*

"First," I said, "there's tea."

"There's always tea."

"There is always tea," I said. "Drink it quickly. I don't think we'll have the luxury for long."
