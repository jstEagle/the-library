## 9. The Door Beneath the Mountain

The map had been wrong about everything except the important things, which was either sloppiness or mercy on the part of whoever drew it.

It showed a road through the high country that did not exist anymore — washed out, Edmund said, some forty years before either of us was born, though how he knew that and why he phrased it as *before either of us was born* when I was fairly certain he'd been born sometime around the invention of dirt, I didn't ask. It marked a village where there was only a burned foundation. It promised three days of travel that took five. But when it came to the mountain itself — the one with no name on any map we'd seen, the one the refugees wouldn't look at directly, the one whose peak wore snow like a crown it hadn't earned yet — the map was precise in the way of things drawn by someone who loved them or feared them. There was no difference, I was learning, between those two kinds of love.

*A door beneath the mountain,* the stranger had died saying. And the map showed me where: not the summit, not a cave mouth, but a fold in the southern face where two ridgelines met like hands pressed together in something that wasn't quite prayer.

We climbed to it on the fifth morning, through snow that squeaked underfoot like new leather, and found exactly what the map promised, which frightened me more than finding nothing would have.

There should have been nothing there. That's what mountains do with their faces; they keep stone and scree and the occasional stubborn pine, and they do not keep doors. But set into the rock at the meeting of those two ridges was an archway, tall as three men and carved so cleanly it might have been grown rather than cut, and above it, worn shallow by centuries of weather but unmistakable once you knew what you were looking for —

I knew what I was looking for.

A sword crossed over a sheaf of wheat, with roots running down from both into a single knotwork line. My family crest. The one stitched into the collar of every coat my mother ever made me. The one carved small and secret into the lintel of our house in the valley, so old the grooves had filled with paint and no one remembered putting them there. The one on the ring the elders kept locked in a box and would not let me touch, *because you'll lose it, child,* which was a lie so lazy it was almost restful.

"It's ours," I said. My voice came out strange in all that white silence. "Edmund. It's *ours.*"

"I can see that." He stood a few paces back from the door the way a man stands back from a horse he doesn't trust. His breath made little clouds and the clouds hung in the air longer than breath should. I'd noticed that about him since the pass. Things around Edmund lingered — his footsteps stayed printed in snow too long, shadows leaned toward him. As if the world couldn't quite decide whether to let him go.

"Does it frighten you?" I asked. "That it's mine?"

"Everything true frightens me," Edmund said mildly. "It's why I garden. Dirt doesn't surprise you. You put a seed in and you know what comes out."

"And what comes out of this?"

He looked at the archway for a long moment. Snow settled on his shoulders and did not melt, which I chose not to think about.

"A debt," he said finally. "Debts come out of doors like this."

* * *

Here is the thing about oaths: nobody tells you what they're for.

In the valley we recited ours twice a year, at first frost and last, the whole town standing in the square with candles going soft in the cold, and the words poured out of us like water from a jug — *by root and blade, by bread and grave, by the long quiet that keeps us safe* — and then everyone went home and ate too much and complained about their neighbors, and the oath sat inside us the way a key sits in a drawer: present, polished, purposeless.

I'd been speaking those words since before I could understand them. That's how oaths work. They're not promises you make; they're promises made into you, poured molten down the generations until they cool into the shape of your bones. I could recite mine drunk, concussed, or dead. I'd tested the first two. Not the third. Yet.

So when I stood in front of a door carved with my family's crest, in a place where my swordspeech woke up singing the moment we left the valley behind — when I looked at that archway and felt the oath stir in my chest like a bird turning over in its sleep — I understood, with the particular clarity of someone standing at the edge of something, that the words had never been a shield.

They were a key.

Nobody builds locks into shields. Nobody carves crests above doors that are meant to be defended. You carve crests above doors that are meant to be *opened*, by the right people, at the right cost.

"Say it," Edmund said quietly. He'd come up beside me without making any sound at all, which was another thing I'd stopped remarking on. "If you're going to say it, say it now, while you still think you might turn back."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not in the comforting business. I'm in the *accurate* business. It pays worse."

I laughed. I don't know why — nerves, maybe, or the absurdity of standing in a snowfield arguing philosophy with a man who smelled faintly of rosemary and had no shadow I could find when I looked for it straight-on. Laughing felt like theft. Somewhere under this mountain, if the map and the dying man and the elders' careful silences were all telling the truth, my mother's soul sat on a shelf in a bottle, humming lullabies to no one. What right did I have to laugh?

Every right. That was the point. That was the whole rotten, glorious point. She'd given her soul so I could grow up laughing in a valley that forgot how to be afraid, and if I went down there grim and joyless I'd be spending her gift exactly wrong.

"By root and blade," I said.

The snow around us went very still.

Not quiet — still. There's a difference. Quiet is an absence of sound. This was sound holding its breath, every flake suspended mid-fall, the wind stopping mid-word. Even Edmund's lingering breath-clouds froze where they hung.

"By bread and grave—"

The archway began to warm. I could see it happening: the frost on the carved stone retreating like a tide going out, lines of gold appearing in the grooves of the crest, the sword and the sheaf and the roots all catching light from somewhere below, somewhere deep, somewhere that had been waiting a very long time for these particular words in this particular voice.

"—by the long quiet that keeps us safe."

The last word fell out of my mouth and into the mountain, and the mountain swallowed it, and the door opened.

Not swung. *Opened* — the way a hand opens, the stone folding inward along seams I hadn't been able to see, revealing a passage lit with a warm amber glow that spilled out across the snow like something generous, like an invitation, like a trap that had learned good manners.

And the warmth — gods, the warmth. After six days of cold that lived in your joints and made your thoughts move like syrup, the air breathing out of that doorway was unbearable in its kindness. It smelled like summer. Like cut grass and ripening apples and sun on warm stone, a whole compressed season exhaling from the dark, and I understood suddenly why people went beneath this mountain and did not come back. Not because they couldn't leave.

Because leaving would mean walking out of summer forever.

"Oh," I said. It was not a profound observation but it was the only one available.

"The court beneath keeps summers," Edmund said. His voice had gone carefully neutral, the way it did when he was handling something fragile — seedlings, other people's griefs, truths he wasn't ready to share. "Stored ones. Bottled vintages. I told you the world down there runs slow. Slow enough to save what the surface wastes."

"You've been down there."

It wasn't a question and he didn't pretend it was. He just stood at the threshold, at that clean dividing line between the blue-white world and the amber one, and I watched him do something I'd never seen him do in seven days of travel: hesitate.

Edmund did not hesitate. Edmund stepped over streams without testing the depth and walked into blizzards like visiting neighbors. He had steered us through the avalanche by knowing, somehow, which slope was *lying* about being stable, and when I'd asked how, he'd said, *the ground told me, we're old friends,* and smiled, and I'd let it go because the alternative was asking questions I might not survive the answers to.

But now he stood at a warm open door and did not go through it, and his face — his kind, patient, unlined face — looked for a moment like the face of a man reading his own name on a gravestone.

"Edmund?"

"There are places," he said slowly, "where I am known."

"Known how?"

"Let's say," and here he smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, "that if anyone down there asks you who's traveling with you, the honest answer is *no one you need to worry about,* and I'd take it kindly if you gave it."

"That's the least reassuring sentence anyone has ever spoken to me."

"I know." He flexed his fingers, looking down at them as though checking they were still attached. "Felix. Once we go in, we're committed. Not by law. By *attention*. Places like this notice what enters them. They remember. Whatever you're carrying — the oath, the map, the—" he paused, choosing, "—the hope — it stops being yours alone the moment you cross. The court will have opinions about it."

"My mother's soul is on a shelf down there."

"Yes."

"Then the court can have whatever opinions it likes. I'm not leaving without her."

Something moved through Edmund's expression then, quick and buried, like a fish under ice — grief or recognition or memory, gone before I could name it. He looked at me the way he sometimes looked at plants that had come up stronger than he'd expected: surprised, pleased, and already braced for the frost.

"No," he agreed softly. "You're not."

He stepped across the threshold first.

I want to be honest about what happened in me when I followed him, because honesty is the only inheritance the valley ever gave me that turned out to be worth anything. When I crossed that line — one boot in snow, one boot on warm amber stone — something closed gently around my decision like a hand closing around a coin. Not a trap. A *receipt*. Somewhere far above, the oath in my chest turned over again, and this time it did not go back to sleep.

I had spent my whole life practicing sword-forms against enemies who would never come, guarding graves that no one threatened, keeping watch over a bargain I didn't know I'd inherited. Every drill, every dawn patrol, every night I'd stood at the valley edge listening to a silence that felt too deliberate to be natural — all of it had been rehearsal for a play no one told me we were staging. And now here was the stage, warm-lit and waiting, smelling of stolen summers.

My mother sold her soul to buy this valley's safety. The bill had come due in early snow and gray-eyed refugees. And I was her daughter, oath-keyed and oath-kept, walking down into the court beneath the mountain to collect what was hers.

Committed. The word fit like a coat tailored for someone else who turned out to be my exact size.

The passage sloped downward, smooth-worn, glowing from within. Behind us the door began to fold itself closed with a sound like a long exhalation, sealing out the wind, the snow, the whole bright cold world where I had been merely restless.

Ahead, the amber light deepened toward gold, and I heard — faintly, from far below — music. Strings. Something patient and slow and terribly beautiful, played by hands that had all the time in the world.

Edmund glanced back at me, and for just a moment his face was not gentle at all.

"They'll offer you hospitality," he said. "Whatever they offer you — eat nothing, drink nothing, promise nothing. And Felix —" he hesitated, that rare, terrible hesitation — "when you see the shelves... don't read the labels out loud."

"Why not?"

"Because some of them will answer."
