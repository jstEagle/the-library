## 10. The Court of Quiet Hours

The door closed behind us with a sound like a book being shut by someone very patient.

I want to say I turned around. I want to tell you that some part of me — the part that had drilled sword-forms at dawn for fifteen years against enemies who never came — heard that sound and understood it for what it was. But the truth is that I stood in the warm amber light of the corridor beyond and thought, absurdly, *it's warmer than home.*

That should have been my first warning. Warmth, in a place like this, is not comfort. It's inventory.

* * *

The passage opened into a hall so large I couldn't see its ceiling, which was strange, because I could see everything else with terrible clarity. The walls were stone the color of honey held up to firelight, veined with gold that moved — actually moved, slow as sap, brightening and dimming on some rhythm I couldn't count. And the light itself didn't come from torches or lamps or any of the ordinary cheats.

It came from summers.

I don't know how else to describe it. Along both walls, set into niches carved at intervals like windows in a cathedral, glowed what could only be called weather. One niche held a June afternoon, all green-gold slant and drifting pollen. Another held the last hour before a thunderstorm, purple-heavy and humming. A third was simply heat — the flat white shimmer over a wheat field in August — and standing near it made my skin prickle with sweat and memory at once.

"Stored," Edmund said quietly beside me. He'd stopped hesitating at the threshold the moment we crossed it; whatever fear had gripped him outside had been folded away somewhere, neat as linen. "They harvest them. Whole seasons, from places that won't miss them. Or from people who can't object."

"How do you bottle a summer?"

"The same way you bottle anything." His voice did something complicated on the last word. "You find the thing that makes it itself, and you take that."

I looked at him. He looked at the wall of stolen afternoons with an expression I couldn't read, and I decided — not for the last time, and never successfully — to leave it alone.

The hall was not empty. That was the other thing. Figures moved through it, unhurried, dressed in grays and browns and colors so muted they seemed embarrassed to be seen: courtiers, I supposed, though they moved less like courtiers and more like people wading through deep water. No one hurried. No one spoke above a murmur. When one of them passed us, she inclined her head with perfect courtesy and I saw that her eyes were gray — not blue-gray, not storm-gray, but the gray of ash, of things already burned down to what doesn't change.

Edmund touched my elbow. "Don't stare."

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring the way you stared at the avalanche."

"That was a reasonable amount of staring. Mountains falling on you deserve staring."

"Felix." Low, almost gentle. "Everything here deserves staring. That's how it gets you. Stare later."

So I fixed my eyes on the middle distance, which was occupied by a fountain that poured upward — water rising in a slow silver rope, pooling against the unseen ceiling, and raining back down somewhere I couldn't hear — and I walked, and I tried to look like someone who belonged in a court beneath a mountain, which is a skill I did not have and would not be acquiring that day.

* * *

They were waiting for us at the far end of the hall, though I never saw them arrive, which I suspect means they were always there and simply permitted themselves to become visible when it suited them.

The steward was tall in the way that furniture is tall — correct, proportioned, without any particular interest in height as an experience. Their face was pleasant and smooth and I genuinely could not have told you their age, their gender, or anything else about them except that they wore a robe the exact color of the last snow before spring, dirty-white going gray at the hem, and that when they smiled, the smile arrived a half-second before the rest of the expression caught up, like a messenger sent ahead of the army.

"Welcome," the steward said, and their voice was the temperature of the room: warm, even, stored. "Felix Almeida. Of the valley. Of the oath." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "The collateral's daughter."

The words went through me like cold water through cloth.

"My mother," I said, and was proud of how level it came out, "has been dead eleven years."

"Yes," the steward agreed pleasantly. "And yet here you are, asking after her accounts. Grief is such a durable instrument. We've found few things keep their edge longer." They spread their hands, and the sleeves fell back from wrists that were, I noticed, unmarked, unlined, unbothered. "But forgive me. You've crossed the pass in winter. You've opened a door that has not been opened in a generation. Hospitality is owed, and hospitality will be given. Will you walk with me?"

It was phrased as a question. It was not a question. Behind the steward, two figures in ash-gray waited with the stillness of furniture, and I understood that *will you walk with me* had roughly the same relationship to choice that a cell door has to architecture.

"We'll walk with you," Edmund said, before I could answer, and his hand found my elbow again, light as a moth. *Stare later,* he'd said. *Walk now.*

* * *

The steward led us through corridors that I am fairly sure rearranged themselves behind us, because I have an excellent sense of direction — years of patrolling a valley the size of a soup bowl will do that — and within ten minutes I had completely lost the way back to the door. Whether this was magic or architecture or simple exhaustion I couldn't say, and the steward, walking ahead with their hands folded, offered no commentary whatsoever.

They talked instead. That was the horror of it, looking back: how much they told us, freely, cheerfully, because none of it mattered once you were inside. Knowledge is only power if you can leave with it.

"The court keeps the quiet hours," the steward said, gesturing at a window — an actual window, set into solid rock, showing a view of a hillside under a sky I knew did not exist anywhere above us. "Above, everything runs. Seasons chase each other. People are born, spend themselves, die, and call it living. Here, we do not run. We *keep.*" They glanced back at me, smile arriving early. "Your valley learned this, once. That is why it survives. Stillness. Boundaries. A bargain kept is a kind of quiet, wouldn't you say?"

"A bargain kept," I said, "implies both parties know the terms."

"Oh, the terms were known." The steward's tone didn't change at all, which somehow made it worse. "Terms are always known. Understanding them is a separate service, and one we do not provide."

We passed through an archway hung with bells — dozens of them, bronze and glass and bone — and every single one was silent. Not stilled. Silent, the way a held breath is silent. As we passed beneath them, I felt the silence lean toward us, attentive, and then, reluctantly, let us go.

"Do not touch the bells," the steward said, without turning around.

"I wasn't going to touch the bells."

"Everyone touches the bells eventually. It is why we mention them." A beat. "The alarms of this court are not sounds, swordspeaker. They are silences. When something is taken that should not be taken, the quiet spreads, room by room, until the thief stands in a silence so complete that her own heartbeat betrays her position. We find it more reliable than bells. Bells can be ignored." They turned their head just enough that I caught one eye, gray as banked coals. "Silence cannot."

My hand had drifted, without my permission, to the hilt of my sword. I made myself take it away. Made myself smile. It felt like wearing someone else's face.

* * *

They fed us. That's the part I keep circling back to, the part that shames me most, because I was hungry — three days of hard travel and mountain cold will hollow you out — and the food was extraordinary. Bread that tasted like the smell of baking rather than the eating, fruit out of season and out of country, wine that tasted the way the word *amber* sounds. We sat at a table in a small room lit by a bottled autumn, all copper light and the ghost-smell of woodsmoke, and the steward watched us eat with the satisfaction of a host whose hospitality is doing exactly what hospitality does.

Because that's what it was. I knew it even as I ate. Hospitality is custody with better manners. Every bite made me softer, sleepier, more inclined to stay. The warmth wasn't comfort; it was inventory, and I was being shelved.

"This is very good," Edmund said mildly, helping himself to more bread. He ate like a man who had done this before. I remember thinking: *of course he has. He was sold to them. He knows the menu.*

"We are pleased," the steward said. "The collateral's daughter honors us. Few come below. Fewer leave." Another pause, calibrated to the half-second. "Though I should say — since you have come so far, and since curiosity honored is courtesy — that nothing here is hidden from you. Ask, and you may see."

There it was. The trap with the door left open, because a trap you have to be pushed into teaches you to resist, and a trap you walk into willingly teaches you only about yourself.

I should have said no. I want that on the record, wherever records go. I should have finished the bread, thanked them for the wine, and asked to see nothing at all.

Instead I said: "Show me the shelves."

* * *

The archive was long and low and utterly silent, and it was lit by a single stored summer — a noon, I think, or close to it, white and total, the kind of light that leaves no shadows for anything to hide in. Shelves ran the length of the room, floor to ceiling, row after row after row, disappearing into distances the light refused to measure.

And on the shelves: bottles.

Not bottles, exactly. Vessels. Glass blown thick and old, each one cradled in a stand of dark wood, each one holding a light of its own — and no two alike. Some burned steady as candles. Some flickered. Some pulsed, slow, patient, rhythmic, and it took me four rows of shelves to admit to myself what the rhythm was.

A heartbeat. They pulsed at the pace of a heartbeat, because they had been heartbeats.

Each vessel bore a label. Small, neat, written in a hand so regular it might have been printed: a name, a date, and a number. Nothing else. No history, no crime, no reason. Just the name of a person, the day they were filed, and a figure that I understood, with a lurch, was probably a value.

I walked the rows. My boots sounded obscene in there, loud, alive. The vessels brightened faintly as I passed — I told myself it was the light playing tricks, and then I stopped telling myself that, because some of them turned. Not physically. But the light inside them leaned, the way a sunflower leans, following something warm walking past.

"They know I'm alive," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended.

"They know many things," the steward said, from the doorway, where they had stationed themselves with the ease of a jailer who owns the keys and the building and, frankly, the concept of doors. "Souls are not dead things, swordspeaker. A soul in storage is not extinguished. It is *kept.* Preserved. Safe from wear, from grief, from the indignities of embodiment. Some of our residents consider it a mercy." A pause. "Some do not. We do not ask. Asking is not part of the service."

I stopped in front of a vessel labeled *Maren, daughter of Ilse.* The light inside it was the color of lamplight through a window on a snowy night, and it flickered fast, quick, nervous, like a bird in a cupped hand.

"How many?" I asked.

"In this room? Eleven thousand, four hundred and six." The steward said it the way a farmer says how many sheep. "In the deeper rooms, more. The court has been keeping for a long time. Your valley alone has contributed—" they consulted nothing, needed to consult nothing, "—one per generation, as agreed. Nine, counting yours."

"Counting mine?"

"Counting your mother's." The smile arrived early again, and this time I watched it come and hated it. "Which is, I believe, why you have come. You have the look of a collector, Felix Almeida. Or the daughter of one." They stepped aside from the doorway, gracious as death. "The shelf you want is in the next room. Row seven. We have kept it warm."

*I have kept it warm.* Four words, and they undid me more thoroughly than the whole silent hall of bells, because I heard what lived underneath them: *we expected you. We have always expected you. The debt remembers your face.*

Edmund's hand found mine in the dark between shelves. His fingers were cold. For the first time since the garden, the man who remembered every plant and no years was trembling, and he pressed something into my palm — small, hard, a seed, I realized, a seed of all things — and closed my fingers over it, and leaned close enough that his breath stirred my ear.

"Row seven," he whispered. "Whatever you see there — whatever you feel — it will want you to stay. Everything in this room wants you to stay. Don't listen with your heart open."

And down the aisle, past the leaning lights, through the stored noon, the next room waited, and somewhere in it, on row seven, something warm was humming a tune I hadn't heard in eleven years and had never once forgotten.
