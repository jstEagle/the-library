## 11. A Soul on a Shelf

The steward left me alone with the shelves.

I want to be clear about that, because later — when I tell this story to volunteers around the fire, and I do tell it, though I edit shamelessly — people always assume there was a guard, a locked door, some dramatic obstacle between me and the thing I'd come to steal. There wasn't. There was a corridor of bottled souls arranged in alphabetical order by name, and a steward who said, "Take whatever time you need," in the tone of a librarian who has already catalogued every book you might check out and knows exactly which ones you'll bring back late.

That's the horror of the court beneath the mountain. Not cruelty. *Efficiency.*

* * *

I had asked to see the collection. That was the word he used — *collection*, like stamps, like pressed flowers. We had finished a meal I couldn't taste because my throat was closed with dread, and I'd said, "I'd like to see where they're kept." And the steward had set down his cup, folded his hands, and smiled the way you smile at a child who has finally asked the question you've been waiting all evening for.

"Of course," he said. "She would want you to see."

He hadn't said *your mother*. He didn't need to. The whole court had been saying it obliquely since we arrived — the collateral's daughter, the guest of the surety, the heir of an account long dormant. Everyone here spoke about souls the way bankers speak about money: politely, precisely, and never once touching the actual thing.

So now I stood in the archive, and the archive breathed.

That was the first thing. You expect a vault to be cold. This wasn't. The air held a warmth like the inside of a loaf, like a hand that has been holding yours a long time, and it came from the shelves themselves — row upon row of glass bottles, each one glowing faintly, each one *humming*. Not sound exactly. More like standing near a hive. Like pressing your ear to a door and hearing someone singing on the other side, too soft to make out the words, too present to pretend you hadn't heard.

Thousands of them. I stopped counting at four hundred because counting felt like agreeing to something.

Each bottle sat on a little wooden cradle, and under each cradle was a label written in a hand so beautiful it looked like it had been paid to be legible. Names. Just names, in ink the color of dried blood, or maybe actual dried blood; I decided not to investigate. Some labels were old enough that the ink had gone brown and the wood had gone silver. Some were new. One, three rows down, still smelled of pine sap when I leaned close, and I stepped back from it as if it had bitten me.

"Almeida," I said out loud, to no one, because if I said nothing I was going to start reading names, and if I started reading names I was going to find someone I knew, and I could not afford that yet. "A-l-m-e-i-d-a. Where are you."

My voice did that thing voices do in big quiet rooms — came back to me smaller than it went out, apologetic.

And then I heard it.

Not my echo. Something else, threaded through my echo, coming from the far end of the third aisle: a melody. Four notes, then a pause, then four notes again, patient as water wearing down stone. A lullaby. My lullaby. The one my mother used to hum when the valley storms shook the shutters and I was small enough to believe she could hold the roof on through sheer stubbornness. She died when I was nine. I had not heard that melody in twenty years, and I would have known it if it were hummed by a stranger in a market in a country whose language I didn't speak, because some songs don't live in your ears. They live somewhere lower, somewhere the body keeps its accounts.

I walked toward it. I don't remember deciding to walk. My feet made that decision without consulting me, which, in hindsight, should have been a warning about how the rest of the night would go.

Third aisle, seventh shelf, second bottle from the left.

It was smaller than I expected. That's the detail that undid me, more than anything else — more than the warmth, more than the light. It was the size of a preserve jar. The kind my mother kept plums in. It fit in two hands, and it glowed gold-amber like honey held up to a window, and inside it something moved the way light moves on the bottom of a clear stream.

And it was warm. Warm through the glass, warm like a forehead, warm like the back of her neck when she carried me up from the fields half-asleep. Twenty years cold in the ground above us, and here her soul was warm as bread.

The label read: **ALMEIDA, MAREN. SURETY. RENEWED.**

Renewed. I turned the bottle over looking for what that meant and found only the date of the last renewal, which was eleven years ago, which was the year I turned eighteen, which was the year the elders had come to our house and spoken quietly with my father behind a closed door while I pretended not to listen through the wall. Eleven years. Whatever renewal meant, it had a schedule, and the schedule had recently been kept.

The lullaby faltered.

Just for a moment — the melody stumbled, caught itself, went on. But I felt it the way you feel a sleeper shift beside you. And I understood, with a certainty that arrived whole and unwelcome, that it had faltered because I was close. Because after twenty years of humming to an empty room, something in that bottle had noticed *me*, the way a plant notices the sun coming around the edge of a wall.

"Don't," I whispered. To the bottle. To myself. To whoever was listening, which in this court was probably everyone. "Don't do that. Don't know me. I can't —"

The lullaby swelled anyway, gentle and unmistakable, and I put my forehead against the shelf and cried the way I hadn't cried at her funeral, because at her funeral I'd been nine and furious and everyone kept telling me she was at peace, and I had thought: *this is what peace looks like? A hole?*

This is what peace looked like. A jar on a shelf, singing to her daughter.

* * *

"You found her quickly."

The steward's voice came from the end of the aisle, soft as dust settling. I wiped my face with the heel of my hand before turning around, an instinct of dignity that lasted approximately one second, because his expression wasn't pitying or smug. It was tired. Genuinely, deeply tired, the tiredness of someone who has watched this exact scene play out more times than he can number and has never once found a version of it he could feel good about.

"They're easy to find," he said, before I could ask. "When they're yours. The bottles call their kin. It's not policy. It's just what souls do." He came closer, stopping a respectful few feet away, hands folded. "Most visitors take hours. Wandering. Working up courage. You walked straight to her."

"She was humming."

"Yes." He looked at the bottle with something I couldn't name — fondness, maybe, or grief's well-mannered cousin. "She does that. Has done, all these years. Most of them go quiet eventually. They settle. She never settled." A pause. "I've often wondered if it was because she left something unfinished."

"I'm the unfinished thing," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "Aren't I. That's why she sings. She's waiting for me."

The steward didn't confirm it, which was its own confirmation. He gestured instead to the cradle, to the label, to the neat brown ink. "You've read the terms?"

"I read 'renewed.' I don't know what that means."

"Then let me explain the bargain properly, since your elders apparently explained it badly or not at all." He drew a breath, and for a moment he looked less like a steward and more like a schoolmaster facing a class he'd taught too many years. "When your valley was founded — when your ancestors fled the wars of the speaking bloods and needed a sanctuary magic could not enter — they did not simply *find* such a place. Places like that are made. And making them requires material."

"Material," I repeated.

"A soul," he said. "One soul, freely given, woven into the boundary stone. The soul becomes the lock. Magic cannot follow into the valley because the valley's threshold is, itself, a soul that says *no*. It says no forever, tirelessly, the way a heart beats without being asked." His eyes flicked to the bottle. "Your grandmother's mother gave herself for the founding. When a giver's strength wanes — and souls do wane, even here, even tended — the debt must be renewed. One soul per generation. Freely given. Your mother came down the mountain nineteen years ago and gave hers freely, and the valley forgot her within the season, because that is part of the working. The lock must not grieve its keeper."

Nineteen years ago. I was nine. The winter my mother died — the winter they told me she died — she had been walking *down* the pass, Edmund had seen her, fleeing down-mountain with a sleeping child. With me. And somewhere between the garden and the valley's edge, she had turned around. Gone back up. Given herself.

For the valley. For the lock. For me.

"The long winter," I managed. "The elders said —"

"— is the collection notice, yes." The steward inclined his head. "Every sixty years or so, the soul weakens past renewal-by-willing-gift, and the court calls in its interest. Frost first. Then the passes seal. Then, if no new soul is offered, the valley itself —" he spread his hands, delicately, "— stops. Not violently. Valleys don't die violently. They simply stop growing, stop thawing, stop remembering. A sanctuary that has forgotten it is a sanctuary is just a grave with good acoustics."

"And if I claim her." My hand had drifted to the bottle without my permission. It was trembling. So was the bottle, faintly, the light inside quickening like a pulse. "If I take her off this shelf and carry her out of here. What happens to the lock?"

The steward was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere deep in the court, a bell that was not a bell rang — a silence spreading outward, the way I'd learn silences worked down here, each one a door opening or closing in a house made of hush.

"Then everything she bought comes undone," he said. "The boundary fails. Magic enters the valley for the first time in three hundred years — every speaking blood, every old debt, every enemy your ancestors fled, all of it free to walk in through a door that no longer says no. The long winter finishes falling regardless; a broken lock protects no one. And the people you love — the elders, the children drilling sword-forms at enemies who will now actually come, the gardener who waits for you at my threshold even now —" his gaze rested on me, gentle and merciless, "— they inherit a war they were promised they'd never have to fight."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Bargains rarely are, for the generation that inherits them. Your mother knew that better than anyone. She signed knowing you'd stand exactly here someday, exactly as you're standing, wanting exactly what you want." He tilted his head. "Do you know what she said, when she gave herself? I was here. I always am, for the giving. She said: *'Keep it warm for my daughter. She'll come for it. She's stubborn — she gets it from me.'*"

The lullaby rose. Four notes, pause, four notes. Stubborn. Patient. Singing to the daughter who had finally, finally come down the mountain, twenty years late, carrying nothing but an oath and a stolen map and a heart full of the exact dilemma her mother had foreseen.

Because that was the trap, and I saw it now with perfect clarity, the way you see the bottom of a lake only once you're drowning in it. If I left her here, the valley lived, and I spent the rest of my life as the woman who chose a bargain over her own mother — loyal to the living, yes, safe, sensible, and hollow in a way no oath could fill. If I took her, I got my mother back for one breath at a graveside, and I handed every person I'd grown up with to the winter and the wars. Love against loyalty. The dead against the living. And both choices cost everything, because bargains built on souls are designed to make sure someone always pays.

"There's a third option," I said. "There's always a third option. People just price it high."

The steward raised an eyebrow — the first genuinely curious expression I'd seen on him. "Is there?"

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "But my mother bet on me once. Nineteen years ago, on a frozen pass, she bet that I'd come, and that I'd be stubborn enough to matter." I wrapped both hands around the bottle — warm, warm, impossibly warm, the lullaby stumbling again, quicker now, like a heartbeat hearing footsteps. "I'm not going to decide anything standing in your archive. But I'm not leaving without her either. So you may as well tell me what it costs to try."

Something shifted in the steward's tired face. Pity, maybe. Or respect. Or the particular sorrow of a man who has just realized the ledger is about to get interesting.

"That," he said, "is a conversation for tomorrow. And Felix Almeida —" he used my name deliberately, fully, the way no one in the valley ever had, "— I would sleep well tonight, if I were you. Because I suspect that whatever you decide, you will want your strength. And because your gardener has been asking the door to let him in for the last hour, and doors down here, unlike gardens, do not grow things."

Behind me, in its cradle, the bottle hummed on — four notes, pause, four notes — and for the first time since I'd entered the court beneath the mountain, I hummed back.
