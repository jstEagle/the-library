## 12. Edmund's Confession

I have kept a great many secrets in my long, unlabeled life, and I will tell you the truth about them: keeping them is easy. It is the carrying that wears grooves into you. A secret is a stone you swallow whole, and it sits in you and grows heavier by seasons, until one day you bend to prune a rosebush and find you cannot straighten again.

Felix Almeida slept by my fire with her sword across her knees and her mother's map folded against her heart, and I sat across from her with my stone shifting in its groove, and I knew — the way I know when frost is coming three days before the sky agrees — that I could not carry it past this fire.

We had made camp in the lee of a shrine so old it had forgotten which god it served. The pass of Small Mercies lay behind us, two days gone. Ahead, the road bent down toward the border towns, where names were currency and memories were pawned like wedding silver. She would learn soon enough what things cost out here. It seemed only fair she should learn first from someone who had already paid.

She woke the way she did everything — suddenly, completely, hand finding the hilt before her eyes finished opening. Then she saw me, and something in her unclenched. That unclenching did more damage to me than any suspicion could have. Trust is a terrible thing to be handed when you are planning to confess.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm an old man watching a young woman sleep. Staring is the least of my crimes."

"Old." She snorted and sat up, working the stiffness out of her shoulders. "You don't look a day over forty."

"That's because I stopped counting at forty."

"Felix," I said. "There are things you should know before we go further. Things about me. About the garden."

She went still in that way of hers — not frightened stillness, but the stillness of someone who has spent her life waiting for doors to open and has learned to listen for hinges.

* * *

Here is what I told her, though I confess I took the long road around it, because even now I flinch from the short one.

"You asked me once why I remember every plant but no years," I said. "You thought it was a joke. It wasn't."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, wind moved through pines that had no business growing this high, and I felt each tree like a finger laid on the back of my neck, because they were mine — or I was theirs; by that point in the story the distinction had worn away.

"The garden at the valley's edge is not a garden," I said. "It is a grave that decided to keep living. And I am not its keeper. I am its rootstock."

Felix's eyes narrowed. "Explain."

"When your ancestors bargained with the court beneath the mountain — when they bought their sanctuary with a soul — they were not making the first payment." I turned my hands over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there. It wasn't. It never is. "They were renewing one. There was a keeper before me. Or rather—" I smiled, and it hurt, "—there was a man before me, and then there was me."

"You're saying you're the first soul sold."

"I'm saying I was given leave. A shelf in the court is for souls the court wants to *keep*. Mine they wanted to *use*. So they filed away everything inconvenient — the rage, mostly, and the grief, and the memory of my own name — and left me the gentleness, and set me at the valley's edge to tend the proof of the bargain. Green grass in the snow, Felix. Every traveler who passes sees it and thinks: *what a blessed place, what merciful gods.* They see the receipt and mistake it for a gift."

Her face had gone very carefully blank, which I recognized, because I had invented that expression four hundred years ago, give or take a season nobody wrote down.

"And your rage?" she said quietly. "Where is it filed?"

"In a drawer somewhere beneath the mountain, in a bottle labeled with a name I can't pronounce anymore because I can't remember it." I fed a stick to the fire. "That's what they do down there. They don't destroy you. Destruction is wasteful. They *organize* you."

* * *

She stood then and walked to the edge of the firelight, arms crossed, looking north toward the mountain we were walking toward, and I let her stand there while I finished the part I dreaded most.

"There's more," I said. "And you're not going to like it."

"I haven't liked anything since the stranger died holding my wrist."

"No," I agreed. "But you'll dislike this particularly, because it's about your mother."

That brought her around. Fast.

"What about my mother?"

"She came down this road. Years ago. Fleeing — not toward the court, but away from it, downhill, with a sleeping child in her arms." I watched the words land. "You were the child."

"You *knew* her." Her voice didn't rise. It went flat and cold, which was worse. "You've known all along, and you said nothing."

"I knew a woman running with a baby. I didn't know she was your mother until the stranger spoke your name and the pieces fit. By then—" I spread my hands. "What would telling you have changed?"

"Everything!" Now it rose. "I grew up thinking she abandoned us. Thinking she chose the debt over her own daughter, or couldn't face what she'd done, or — Edmund, I built my whole life on the shape of her leaving, and you *knew*—"

"I knew she ran *downhill*," I said. "Toward the valley. Away from the court. Whatever she fled, Felix, she was trying to reach safety — yours, hers, I don't know whose. But no one flees *toward* the thing they sold themselves to."

She stared at me, and I watched the fury move through her like weather, and I did not flinch from it, because some small stubborn root in me believed she deserved to aim it somewhere true.

"Why tell me now?" she demanded.

"Because tomorrow we reach the town where people sell pieces of themselves to buy bread, and after that, the door beneath the mountain. And once you're inside, you'll meet the steward, and he will be kind to you. That's the worst thing I can tell you: he will be *kind*. He'll offer you tea and hospitality and reasonable terms, and every kindness will be a hook. And when he shows you the shelves — and he will show you the shelves — you need to understand what you're looking at."

"Bottled souls."

"Bottled *people*. Every one of them still warm. Still humming whatever song they hummed last. And every one of them got there the same way your mother did — through a bargain someone else signed on their behalf, or one they signed themselves out of love or fear or simple hunger." I leaned forward. "So before you go charging in with that blade singing on your back, I am begging you — *begging* you, Felix, and I have not begged since before your valley had a name — weigh what theft will cost. Not to you. You've already decided what you're willing to pay. Weigh what it costs *her*, if you pull her soul off that shelf and the bargain breaks and the winter comes down on everyone she tried to save."

"The elders said claiming it voids everything."

"The elders told you fragments. Let me tell you the shape of the whole: the valley survives because the debt is paid. Steal the payment back, and the collector comes. Not just frost, Felix. *Collection.* Gray-eyed quiet walking through your streets. People half-claimed before they know they're being harvested. I've seen the leading edge of it on the roads these past weeks — refugees with winter already sitting behind their eyes."

"So we do nothing?" Her jaw set. "We turn around? Let her sit on a shelf forever humming a lullaby to nobody?"

"I didn't say that."

"Then what are you saying?"

I looked at her — this fierce, stubborn, oath-bound girl who drilled sword-forms at graves no enemy ever came for — and I felt the old rage stir in its drawer, far beneath the mountain, rattling its bottle. Even filed away, it knew its kin.

"I'm saying there may be a way that isn't theft," I said slowly. "A rite. An old one. If the court offers terms — and it will, courts always offer terms — read them. All of them. Especially the parts written small. Because I signed terms once, written small, and I have been tending roses at the edge of the world ever since, remembering every plant and no years."

* * *

Silence, then. The fire burned low. Above us the early stars came out, hard and sharp, and I felt the mountain's attention like weather coming.

Felix crouched by the fire across from me, and for a long moment she just looked at me — not with pity, which I would have forgiven, nor horror, which I would have understood, but with something more dangerous. Recognition.

"They didn't take your gentleness," she said at last. "They left it. On purpose. Because a gentle keeper makes the cage look like a garden."

"Yes."

"And you stayed anyway. Four hundred years. Why not walk away?"

"Where would I walk?" I smiled. "My name is in a bottle. My rage is in a drawer. What's left of me grows best in that soil. Besides—" I looked north, toward the mountain, and let her see it, let her see the thing under the gentleness, the thing they'd missed or hadn't bothered to file. "Someone has to show the next fool where the small print is."

Something shifted in her face. Pity turning into resolve, the way frost turns into ice — same water, harder purpose.

"Then you'll come with me," she said. Not a question.

"To the door, yes. Past it—" I hesitated, and the hesitation cost me, because she caught it the way she caught everything.

"Edmund. Past it?"

"The court knows me," I said quietly. "I told you — they gave me leave. Leave can be revoked. And there are rooms down there I have not entered in a very long time, and one of them holds a bottle with my name on it, and I do not know what happens to a man who stands in front of his own rage after four centuries of being gentle." I met her eyes. "But I know what happens to a valley that loses its surety, and I know what happens to a girl who goes alone, and between those two fates, mine is the cheapest."

She was quiet a moment. Then she reached across the dead fire and gripped my wrist — the same grip, I thought, that a dying stranger had put on hers, passing the weight along, hand to hand to hand.

"Weigh it together, then," she said. "Whatever it costs."

Above us, the first flakes of the long winter came down out of a sky that should have held stars for another month, and far below, in a drawer beneath the mountain, something with my name on it began — very faintly, very patiently — to hum.
