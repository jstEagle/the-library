## 13. Terms of Hospitality

The steward did not have a name.

I know how that sounds — everyone has a name, even the stranger in the pass whose grave I'd dug with numb hands and less grace. But when I asked him, he tilted his head like a bird considering a worm and said, "Names are for things that might be lost," and I understood two things at once: that I would get nothing further from him on the subject, and that he considered me very much a thing that might be lost.

We sat in a room lit by stored summer. That's not poetry; it's architecture. The walls were honeycombed with glass cells, each one holding a sliver of season — a June afternoon, thick and gold as syrup; an October morning with frost still clinging to it like lace someone forgot to take down. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, and it made everything look kinder than it was, including the steward, including me.

Edmund stood by the door. He had not sat since we entered. He hadn't spoken much either, which for Edmund was its own kind of alarm bell. Edmund talked to plants, to doors, to horses we passed on the road who were mostly interested in his pockets. Silence from Edmund meant he was doing arithmetic he didn't like.

The bottle sat between us on a table of black wood that drank the light instead of reflecting it. My mother's soul. Still warm. Still humming, faintly, if you put your ear close enough — a lullaby in a key I knew better than my own heartbeat, because it was the key my heartbeat had been set to before I could remember setting it.

"You understand," the steward said, "that you cannot simply take it."

"I understand that I'm going to."

He smiled. It was a good smile, well-made, the kind of smile that had been practiced on people with far more leverage than me until it wore smooth. "Yes. The daughter of the collateral. You have her temper, I think. Or perhaps she had yours first; time is flexible down here." He folded his hands. They were long hands, careful hands, the hands of someone who had shelved ten thousand fragile things without breaking one. "Let us speak plainly, then, since plainness seems to be your native tongue. There is a procedure. We call it the Rite of Return. It is not offered often. It is not offered kindly. But it is *offered*, which is more than most visitors to this court may say."

"Most visitors don't leave, is what you're telling me."

"Most visitors are never told they may." He spread his hands, and somewhere in the honeycomb walls a jarred summer flickered, as if the room itself flinched. "The terms are these. You will carry the soul up through the falling winter. Out the door. Up the mountain. Across the boundary where magic cannot follow — your valley, yes, I know its history better than its current residents do. If the soul crosses that boundary while still held, the debt is discharged. The bargain breaks. Your valley stands naked under whatever sky it finds, and this court closes its ledger on the Almeida line forever."

"And if I fail?"

"Then both souls are forfeit." He said it the way a man reads a weather report. Rain expected Tuesday. Both souls forfeit. "Yours and hers. The court will hold them side by side, and I will label them neatly, and in a hundred years no one will remember there was ever a question about it."

* * *

I want to tell you I hesitated. I want to tell you some part of me weighed it properly — the living against the dead, the valley full of breathing people against one woman already twenty years gone, one soul already paid into a system older than my grandmother's grandmother. I want to tell you the arithmetic was hard.

It wasn't. That's the shameful truth of it. The arithmetic took half a second, and what filled the other half was fear, arriving late to a decision already made, like a guest who missed the ceremony but insists on giving a toast.

"What does 'forfeit' mean?" I asked anyway, because I've learned that when a word is left vague in a bargain, the vagueness is the weapon. "For a soul. Specifically."

The steward regarded me with something that might have been approval, or might have been appetite. Those can look similar on old faces. "It means it stops being yours. A soul is not a candle that goes out, swordspeaker. It is a song. Forfeit means the court keeps the song and sings it however it likes. Some souls make fine lamps." He gestured at the walls. "Some make fine winters."

My mouth went dry. On the table, the bottle hummed its little lullaby, oblivious, warm as bread.

"And the winter itself," I said. "The long winter. The collection notice. If I carry her out — if I succeed — the winter stops?"

"The winter is the sound of the debt being called in. Discharge the debt, and the sound stops." He leaned forward, and the stored summers bent their light around him like water around a stone. "But hear the shape of it, Felix Almeida, because I will not repeat it and I do not lie: the last snow seals the door. When it falls, this door does not open again for a hundred years, and anything on the wrong side of it belongs to whichever side it stands on. You will be climbing through the teeth of the season we sent. Every hour, the cold will ask you to stop. Every mile, the mountain will suggest you lie down. And the soul you carry is warm, and tired, and *wants* to rest, and it will whisper to you in your mother's voice that just a little farther is far enough."

"That's cruel."

"That's *terms*," he said. "Cruelty would be not telling you."

* * *

They gave us a room to decide in. That's hospitality for you — a small chamber with a brazier that burned without heat, because heat was apparently being saved for more important customers, and two chairs, and a window that looked out onto rock, because even courts beneath mountains understand that people trust a view.

Edmund didn't sit in his chair. He stood at the window looking at nothing, and I watched his reflection do the same, and for a while neither of us spoke.

"Say it," I finally said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been not-saying since the steward used the phrase 'both souls.' You've got a face like a man reading his own eulogy. Out with it."

He turned. In the lightless brazier-glow he looked older than he ever let himself look on the road, and I realized with a small cold shock that I didn't actually know how old that was. I didn't know anything certain about Edmund except that he remembered every plant he'd ever tended and no years at all.

"If you fail," he said quietly, "you lose everything. Not just death — death I could talk you out of fearing, you're too stubborn to die properly. Everything. The song of you, filed away, sung by *them*. And your mother's song too, and then the whole errand becomes a story the court tells its new acquisitions to frighten them." He crossed to me. "And if you succeed, the bargain breaks, and the valley loses the only wall it has ever had between it and the world. You'll have unmade the Founding Bargain with your own hands. Do you understand what that makes you, to those elders? To everyone sleeping safe behind a promise you tore up?"

"A traitor," I said. "Or a founder. Depends on whether spring comes."

"Felix—"

"I'm not finished." I stood up, because sitting felt like agreeing. "You think I haven't done this sum? I've been doing it since the pass. Here's what I know. The valley bought its safety with my mother's soul, and nobody told me, and everybody benefited, and every year the interest comes due as snow. That's not safety. That's a mortgage with my family's name on it, and the bank is *underground*, and the bank has decided to foreclose early. There is no version of leaving that bottle on that shelf where I get to live with myself. So the real question isn't whether I sign. The real question is whether I sign with my eyes open or my eyes shut, and I am so tired of the elders' valley, Edmund. The valley where we don't ask. The valley where the oath is a lock and they lost the key on purpose." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "They told me the oath was to protect us. It was a *key*. It opened their door. Everything I was raised to be was a hinge on somebody else's cage."

Edmund was quiet a long moment. Then, gently, in the voice he used on seedlings and frightened animals and, evidently, me: "And what happens to the cage when you open the door, Felix? Sometimes the thing outside is the sky. Sometimes it's the wolf."

"Then I'll learn swordspeech that works on wolves."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him. It faded fast. "There's something else. About the Rite. Something the steward won't say because it isn't in his interest to say it." He hesitated, and I watched him choose his words like a man crossing ice. "A carried soul wants to rest. It will speak in voices you love. And the cold will offer you bargains of its own — small ones, kind ones, *rest here, sleep here, just an hour* — and every one of them is the mountain's mouth. People don't fail the Rite because they're weak. They fail it because it's designed to be failed by exactly the kind of person strong enough to attempt it. The ones who care. Caring is the failure mode."

"So the test is whether I love her enough to keep walking away from her voice."

"Yes."

"That's obscene."

"That's a contract," Edmund said, "with your fear. And you're going to sign it anyway, aren't you."

I looked at the door, beyond which lay the black-wood table and the humming bottle and the steward with his smooth smile and his neat labels. I thought about my mother, whom I knew only as a shape in other people's silences, a lullaby in a borrowed memory, a name the elders wouldn't say above a mutter. Twenty years on a shelf. Warm the whole time. Waiting for someone to come collect her, and no one coming, because everyone who knew had agreed to forget.

"Yeah," I said. "I'm going to sign it anyway."

* * *

The steward received us back in the summer-lit room as if we'd merely stepped out to admire the rock formations. Perhaps for him we had. I got the sense that his hours ran long and slow, that our entire desperate journey had occupied, in his reckoning, roughly the space between one sip of tea and the next.

"Well?" he said.

"I accept the terms. The Rite of Return. I carry her out before the last snow seals the door, or both souls are forfeit." I kept my voice level, which cost me something. "I want it said aloud. All of it. In front of witnesses."

"Witnesses." His gaze slid to Edmund with mild curiosity, the way a man looks at furniture that has unexpectedly expressed an opinion. "Very well. Let the record show: the daughter of the collateral claims the surety. She undertakes the Rite of Return under the old terms. Failure forfeits both songs to this court, irrevocably, eternally, in the manner of songs." He paused. "Do you so swear?"

Here is the thing nobody warns you about oaths: they don't feel like anything when you make them. All that weight, all that consequence, and the moment itself is just air moving over your tongue. I thought of the oath I'd been born into, the swordspeaker's vow I'd drilled beside graves my whole life, words I'd never understood until they turned out to be a key. This was different. This one I understood completely. That was somehow worse.

"I so swear."

The steward nodded once, and the walls of jarred summer brightened fractionally, as if the room approved, or as if somewhere a ledger page turned. "Then we are agreed. Preparation begins at once. You will be given provisions, a route, and instruction in the carrying — the soul must be held against the skin, close to the heart, or it will gutter in the cold. You will leave within the hour. The winter does not wait on sentiment." He rose, and rose, and kept rising slightly past what seemed reasonable, unfolding like something that had been patient about being tall. "One matter remains."

"Which is?"

"The keeper." He indicated Edmund without looking at him, the way you indicate a coat rack. "He is court property. Old property, granted unusual latitude, but property nonetheless. He does not leave with you. He returns to his garden, such as it now is, and waits out the century as he has waited out others."

The room went very quiet. Even the bottled summers seemed to lean in.

"He's coming with me," I said.

"He is not."

"He guided me here. He's protected me across a mountain range. He—" I stopped, because the true sentence was right there and saying it felt like handing the steward a knife. *He's mine,* I wanted to say. *You don't get to file him.* "He's under my protection now. That's how it works up where I'm from."

"How charmingly provincial." The steward finally looked at Edmund directly, and something passed between them — old, complicated, the look of two things that had been shelved in the same cabinet for a very long time. "Keeper. Tell her."

Edmund stepped forward. My stomach dropped, because I knew that walk, that set of the shoulders. It was the walk of a man about to agree with the enemy for reasons that were going to sound like sense.

"Felix." His voice was gentle. It was always gentle. I suddenly hated how gentle. "Listen to me. If I come, I slow you. What I am — what's left of me — the cold knows it. The mountain knows it. I burn years to do the things I do, and I have fewer years than you'd believe, and every one spent on that climb is one not spent holding the garden, and the garden is the *doorway*, Felix. It's the way the soul crosses the boundary. If the garden dies, the Rite dies with it, no matter how far you climb." He held my eyes. "Stay alive. Carry her home. I'll be at the edge. I'm always at the edge. That's rather the whole of what I am."

"No," I said.

"Felix—"

"No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to stand there being reasonable and selfless and *gentle* while that—" I pointed at the steward, and my hand was shaking, and I let it shake, "—while that filing cabinet with a face decides who walks out of here. You told me on the pass you were left behind too. Well, I'm not leaving anyone behind ever again. Not my mother. Not you. Not anybody. That's the whole point of me, Edmund. That's the oath underneath the oath." I turned to the steward. "Amend the terms. He comes. Whatever it costs, put it on my page."

The steward studied me for a long moment. Somewhere deep in the honeycomb walls, a jarred autumn rustled like paper.

"There is no amendment," he said at last. "There is only the Rite, as written. But the Rite says *the claimant and such company as the claimant can keep alive to the boundary*." The smile returned, thinner now, sharper. "His life is not my gift to give or withhold, swordspeaker. It is yours to spend. Spend it wisely. The mountain takes payment in advance."

He swept toward the door, then paused, and without turning added, almost softly:

"The last snow is falling on the upper passes as we speak. It falls faster than you climb. I would begin."

And behind us, in the silence he left spreading through the summer-lit room like frost through a windowpane, the bottle on the black table hummed its lullaby — and for the first time, I heard it change. Just slightly. Just enough.

Like something waking up. Like something that knew it was about to be carried home.
