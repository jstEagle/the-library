## 14. Theft of a Small Warm Thing

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives in the Court of Quiet Hours, and I had spent three days learning its grammar. Not silence — silence is just absence, an empty room. This was quieter than that. It was a quiet with intent, a held breath, a court listening to itself think.

It was also, I was beginning to suspect, an alarm system.

"You keep looking at the ceiling," Edmund murmured beside me. We were walking the long gallery of stored summers at the pace of honored guests, which is to say slowly enough to seem unhurried and quickly enough to seem obedient. "Is there something up there?"

"Maybe," I said. "Or maybe I've just been in this place too long and I'm starting to hear the walls."

"That's not paranoia," Edmund said mildly. "That's scholarship."

Three days of hospitality. Three days of meals served by attendants whose faces were pleasant and whose eyes were elsewhere — filed away somewhere, I thought, like everything else in this court. Three days of the steward's courteous custody: your rooms are prepared, your comfort is our concern, the Rite of Return may begin whenever you feel ready. Which is to say never. The court was very good at *whenever*. It had all the time in the world and it wanted me to know it.

But three days is also enough time to learn where things are kept. And on the second evening, escorted past the archive hall on the way to supper, I had seen through an open door a wall of small glass bottles glowing faintly warm, each one labeled with a name written in a hand so neat it looked printed.

One of those names had been my mother's.

* * *

The plan, if you could call it that, had four parts, and Edmund had contributed two of them, which worried me more than the steward did.

Part one: etiquette. The court ran on courtesy the way a mill runs on water, and Edmund — who had been a soul here once, before whatever mercy or accident had set him tending gardens instead of shelves — knew its currents better than anyone. He could bow correctly. He could ask for things with the exact phrasing that made refusal rude. He could, as he put it over supper the first night, "be so politely inconvenient that they'll want us anywhere but here."

Part two: the map. The dying stranger's bloodstained gift marked not just the door beneath the mountain but the ways through the court itself — corridors drawn in a hand that had clearly walked them often, and one route out that didn't pass the steward's hall at all. Whoever he'd been, he'd planned his own theft and died before using it. I tried not to think about what that meant about his odds.

Part three was mine. Swordspeech. My oath, sung backward through steel, could cut anything I understood well enough to name — and I understood glass, wax seals, and iron brackets. What I couldn't cut was time.

Which brought us to part four: the silences.

"The alarms aren't bells," Edmund had said, the night before, sitting on the edge of his borrowed bed with his hands folded like a man praying to no one in particular. "When something goes wrong here, the court doesn't shout. It stops. Room by room. You'll hear the quiet get *thicker*. When that happens, we run, and we don't stop running until we're above ground or dead."

"How thick is thick?"

He'd considered the question seriously, because Edmund considers everything seriously. "Thick enough that your own heartbeat sounds like an intruder."

* * *

We went at the hour the court used for rest, though I don't believe anyone here truly rested; the attendants simply dimmed, like lamps turned low. Edmund walked ahead of me down the gallery with his hands clasped behind his back, wearing the expression of a man with every right to be wherever he was, and I followed half a step behind and to the left, which apparently meant *attendant* in court grammar and bought us both the privilege of being ignored.

The archive door stood open.

I want to say I felt brave. What I actually felt was cold in my hands and a lullaby lodged behind my teeth that I hadn't consciously remembered in ten years — the one my mother used to hum while she braided my hair, the one that turned out, now that I'd heard her bottled soul humming it through the glass, to be the same song note for note. Souls remember what bodies forget. Somewhere under the mountain, my mother was still singing me to sleep.

Edmund stopped at the corner where the gallery bent toward the steward's wing and turned back to me. He didn't say *good luck*. In a court like this, words have weight, and he wasn't going to spend any on my behalf.

"I'll give you as many minutes as politeness can buy," he said. "If it becomes fewer than that—"

"Then I'll be fast."

"Felix." His voice dropped out of court cadence into something plainer, older. "If it comes to choosing between the bottle and yourself, choose yourself. She would say the same. I'm certain of it."

I looked at him for a moment. There was something in his face I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to. Then I went through the open door, and he turned the corner toward the steward's wing, spine straight, hands folded, a gentle man going off to be magnificently rude.

* * *

The archive smelled like candle smoke and apples.

That was the worst part, I decided later. Not the shelves, not the labels, not even the hundreds of small warm lights breathing in their glass. It was the smell. Someone had built a room to hold stolen souls and had given it the smell of a harvest kitchen, and either that was sentiment or it was craft, and I genuinely couldn't decide which was worse.

The bottles sat in rows on iron brackets, each sealed with wax the color of late honey, each labeled. Names I didn't know. Names from no valley I'd ever heard of. Some of the bottles were old — the wax gone dark, the light inside them dimmed to embers. Some were new. One, near the end of the third row, glowed steady and gold and hummed, so faintly I felt it in my molars rather than heard it.

*Maren Almeida,* said the label. *Surety. Renewable.*

My mother's handwriting. Of course it was. The court hadn't written her label; she had signed herself, the way you sign anything when the alternative is worse. I thought about my mother teaching me to hold a knife, to plant beans, to stand with my weight on my back foot. I thought about her hands. They were in this room. They had signed this room.

"Don't touch it yet," I told myself, out loud, barely a breath. "Cut first. Grieve later. That's the order."

I drew my sword.

Sound behaves strangely in the Court of Quiet Hours. The scrape of steel leaving its sheath should have rung down every corridor, and instead it fell into the hush like a stone into deep water — swallowed, noted, filed. I felt the court notice. I felt it decide, for the moment, that guests were permitted their eccentricities.

I laid the flat of the blade against the bracket and began to sing.

Backward, the oath goes like this: not *I will hold the line* but *the line will unhold me*, not protection but release, not the word that binds but the word that opens locks. In the valley it was useless — magic cannot follow past the ridge, and my whole life my swordspeech had been a beautiful language spoken to people who'd never learned it. Down here it woke like a fire given air. The blade drank the song and the song came out the other side as understanding, and the bracket understood itself being opened, hinge by hinge, weld by weld, the way a knot understands being untied.

The iron parted without a sound. That frightened me more than a clang would have. Even destruction here was polite.

I caught the bottle before it could tip. Warmth soaked straight through my glove into my palm — body-warm, hand-warm, the warmth of someone sleeping — and the humming sharpened, and for one unbearable second I was eight years old and feverish and my mother's palm was on my forehead telling me *you're safe, you're safe, you're safe*.

"Hi, Mama," I whispered, like an idiot, like a daughter, and tucked the bottle against my chest inside my coat, where it beat faintly against my heartbeat like a second, slower pulse.

And then the silence began.

* * *

It started behind me, in the far end of the archive — a thickening, a pressure, like the moment before a storm breaks except there was no wind, only less and less of everything. The apple-and-candle smell faded. My own footsteps stopped making sound, then my own breathing, and I understood suddenly what Edmund had meant: my heartbeat sounded like an intruder. Because it was one.

Room by room, I could track it. The hush rolled outward like a ripple in reverse, eating sound ahead of it, and I stood in the archive holding my mother's soul and listened to the court close its throat.

Somewhere across the palace, Edmund had run out of minutes.

I ran.

The map in my head — the stranger's route, memorized in stolen hours — took me left out of the archive, down a servants' corridor narrow enough that my shoulders brushed both walls, past doors labeled with seasons instead of rooms. The silence chased me. I could feel it gaining, the way you feel weather gain, and behind it, worse, came footsteps — no, not footsteps. Presence. The attendants moving through their silenced halls, faces pleasant, eyes elsewhere, converging with the terrible unhurried patience of a court that has never once needed to hurry in its entire existence.

Turn here. Stairs down, then up — the stranger's route doubled back on itself deliberately, I realized, designed by someone who expected pursuit. My breath smoked in air that was getting colder, or maybe that was the bottle, cooling as it left its shelf, as if my mother's soul didn't know yet whether she was being rescued or repossessed.

"Hold on," I gasped at my own chest. "Hold on, I've got you, I've got—"

The corridor ended in a door, and beyond the door stood Edmund, and beyond Edmund stood six attendants, and beyond them, seated in a chair that hadn't been there before, was the steward.

"Well," said the steward pleasantly. "How unfortunate."

* * *

Here is what I learned about Edmund in the next thirty seconds, and I have had a year since to turn it over and I still learn something new: a man whose rage has been filed away does not become harmless. It becomes *precise*.

He bowed. Of course he bowed — full court depth, perfect form, the bow of a keeper addressing a superior, and the attendants slowed because courtesy demanded they acknowledge it, and courtesy, in this place, was law. And into that bow he poured words — I caught fragments, *guest-right*, *escorted passage*, *the terms of hospitality*, *surety freely carried* — court-grammar deployed like a blade, each phrase a lock turning, each lock buying seconds. The steward's smile thinned by a degree I wouldn't have noticed a week ago. A week ago I hadn't known that smiles could thin.

"Walk," Edmund said to me, without turning his head, without breaking his bow. "Don't run. Running releases them. Walk like you're bored."

I have never in my life been less bored. I walked.

Past the attendants, whose pleasant faces tracked me with eyes that weren't anywhere at all. Past the steward, who watched me with the mild interest of a ledger-keeper watching an entry walk off the page. The bottle against my chest pulsed faster — my mother, feeling my fear through the glass, humming louder, a lullaby becoming a warning becoming, I swear, a battle-song.

At the corridor's mouth I risked one look back.

Edmund was still bowing. Still talking. But his eyes found mine over his folded hands, and what was in them was not fear. It was arithmetic. He was counting what he had left to spend and finding it enough, and that scared me more than the closing silence did, because I had seen that look once before — on the pass, when he burned years of himself to hold a door against a flood, and smiled afterward like a man who'd finally gotten to be useful.

"Edmund," I said.

"*Walk*, Felix."

I walked. Behind me the silence swallowed him mid-sentence, and the last thing I heard was his voice going not silent but *quieted*, taken into the court's great held breath, and then I was through the arch, into the dark, alone with a sword, a stolen soul, and a map drawn by a dead man.

Behind me — distant, muffled, unmistakable — the silence began, room by room, to follow.

* * *

The stranger's route ended at a wall.

Not figuratively. An actual wall, smooth black stone, unmarked, exactly where the map showed a stair climbing toward the door beneath the mountain. I stood there with my palm flat against stone cold enough to burn, my mother humming against my ribs, the silences closing in behind me like a fist, and I laughed — one short, ugly bark of a laugh, because of course. Of course the dying man's map led to a wall. Either he'd lied, or the court had moved the walls, or—

Or the map was older than the wall.

I pressed my hand harder against the stone and felt it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards: a seam. A door, walled over, forgotten — or hidden, hidden by whoever had redrawn the corridors, hidden from the court itself. And doors, my oath reminded me, singing up through my arm like a struck bell, doors are just walls that haven't been introduced to the right words yet.

I drew my sword and sang the oath backward at the stone, loud now, past caring who heard, past caring about anything except the climb above and the sky above that and the valley above that where graves needed tending. The blade bit. The stone understood. And the wall opened onto cold rising air and the first grey hint of upward dark, and I pulled my mother's soul tighter against my heart and stepped through—

—and heard, from the silence behind me, one sound the court could not swallow:

Edmund, singing my oath forward.
