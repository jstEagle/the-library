## 15. Flight Through Stored Summers

**FELIX**

The alarm was not a bell.

I want that understood, because everything else about the court beneath the mountain had bells in it somewhere — the chime of glass bottles on their shelves, the steward's voice like a struck wineglass, the little silver tongs he used to handle souls as if they were sugar cubes. But when we stole a small warm thing off a shelf labeled with my mother's name, the court did not ring. It *hushed*.

It started in the Hall of Quiet Hours, where the steward had poured us tea and offered us hospitality the way a spider offers a web. Edmund had been talking — I'd never heard him talk so much, such lovely useless talk about grafting techniques and the proper depth for tulip bulbs — and then his voice stopped mid-sentence, and I understood that the silence had reached him. Then it reached me: a pressure in the ears, like diving into deep water, like the moment before lightning.

"Run," Edmund said, very calmly, and knocked over the tea.

We ran.

The map — the dead stranger's map, bloodstained at one corner where his hand had gripped it too hard for too long — showed the way out as a series of chambers. The cartographer had drawn them with the patience of a man who expected to die slowly, each room labeled in a cramped hand: *spring. summer. autumn. the door.* No room marked winter. I'd told myself that meant nothing. Rooms full of stored seasons, and no winter — because winter was coming to us free of charge, outside, above, everywhere. The court didn't need to bottle what it was already pouring over the world.

Behind us, silence spread like frost across a window, room by room, eating our echoes.

"Which way?" I shouted, and then hated myself for shouting, because shouting is how you find out how much silence there is. The sound fell dead a foot from my lips.

Edmund pointed left, toward an archway carved with running water and new leaves, and mouthed something. I read his lips wrong twice before I got it. *Hold your breath.*

I thought he meant the smell.

* * *

**EDMUND**

She did not hold her breath. Of course she didn't. Felix Almeida held her sword, which in her personal grammar amounted to the same thing, and went through the archway first, because she was constitutionally incapable of letting anyone else be brave ahead of her.

The spring room was beautiful the way a trap is beautiful. Water stood knee-deep over the whole chamber, clear as glass, and from its surface grew things that had no business growing underground under any light at all — willows trailing green fingers, crocuses opened wide, a cherry tree in absurd full bloom, petals drifting down onto water that never rippled. The light came from nowhere. The air smelled like the first hour of the first warm day you can remember.

And the flood was rising. Slowly — this court did nothing quickly; haste would have been vulgar — but rising, fed by channels in the walls that gushed meltwater in patient ropes.

"Felix." My voice carried strangely here; the spring room had its own soundscape, birdsong without birds, and it let me speak. "The water remembers being snowmelt. It wants to go downhill. Give it a direction."

She looked at me the way she looked at me when I said things like that, which was the look of a woman deciding whether her guide was wise or merely mad. She'd been giving me that look since the garden. I had come to treasure it.

"How," she said, "do I give *water* a direction?"

"You've been speaking to steel your whole life. Steel is only ore that learned discipline. Water has no discipline at all — so don't command it. Ask."

I watched her wade to the center of the room, the stolen bottle wrapped against her chest inside her coat, and I watched her do the thing she does, which is take an oath-shaped thing and bend it until it fits through a keyhole it was never made for. Her swordspeech was made for duels. Instead she knelt in the rising water, laid her blade flat on the surface like a plow, and spoke to the flood in a low, almost apologetic voice.

The water listened.

I will not pretend I wasn't afraid. I am always afraid; fear is what they left me after filing away the rest. But watching her — watching the whole chamber tilt, gently, its stored spring finding an old channel behind the far wall and pouring itself out in a roaring green ribbon, carrying petals and crocuses and one extremely startled-looking carp into the dark — watching that, I felt something that was not quite fear, and had forgotten the name of.

"Ask nicely," she said over her shoulder, breathless, soaked to the waist, grinning like a girl. "That's the trick? Ask *nicely*?"

"It works on most things," I said. "It worked on me."

She didn't hear it. Or she heard it and filed it away, which was worse, or better. Behind us, the silence finished the spring room and moved on, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

**FELIX**

The summer room tried to kill us with kindness.

Understand: I grew up in a valley where magic cannot follow. Everything I knew about magic I knew secondhand, from elders' warnings, which are mostly just fear wearing a nice coat. So I had a theory about the court's season rooms, and the theory was *tests*. Spring tested us with drowning. Summer, I figured, would test us with burning.

Wrong. Summer tested us with a picnic.

The room was a meadow under a honey-colored sky, warm as a hand on the back of your neck. There was a blanket spread on the grass. On the blanket: bread, cheese, stone fruit blushing like scandal, a jug of something dark. And the heat — the heat was the trap, I understood that much, because the warmth came down on you like falling asleep in the sun on purpose, and every step deeper into the room made my legs argue for lying down. Just a rest. Just a minute. You've been running so long. Look, there's food. Look, there's shade under that oak. Look how tired you are.

Edmund stopped at the edge of the meadow and did not step in.

"No," he said quietly.

"It's fine," I said, and heard my own voice come out slow and syrupy, and hated it. "Edmund, sit down. We should sit—"

"Felix." He caught my wrist — the same wrist the stranger had gripped, funnily enough; apparently it was just a wrist people grabbed — and his hand was cool, and through his fingers I felt the bottle against my sternum give one small indignant pulse of warmth, my mother's soul objecting, and that was enough. That was the pinprick. I gasped like surfacing.

The picnic was gone. Or rather, it hadn't ever been; the blanket was a slab of pale stone, the fruit were pebbles, the jug a knot of root. The heat remained. The heat was real and it was doubling and tripling, and now that I wasn't drunk on it I could see the meadow grass going gold, going brown, going up — little threads of fire walking through it toward us, unhurried, the way the whole court did everything.

"The stored summer's been disturbed," Edmund said. "It's spilling. It doesn't know it's killing us. It's just — being July."

"So we ask it nicely to stop?"

"I don't think July knows how to listen." He was already moving along the room's edge, reading the walls, and I saw him find it: a seam where two great stones met, and behind the seam, faintly, moving air. A draft. Cool air, from the direction of autumn. "But fire eats. It's all it does. So we feed it something faster than us."

"What's faster than us?"

"Forty years," he said, "of a garden."

I didn't understand. Then I did, and I grabbed his sleeve. "No. Absolutely not. Whatever that means, whatever it costs—"

He smiled at me, and it was his usual smile, gentle and slightly rueful, the smile of a man who remembers every plant and no years, and he stepped backward into the meadow, into the fire-walking grass, and he *opened*.

There's no other word. He opened like a book, like a gate, like a granary in famine, and what came out of him was green — memory-green, garden-green, forty years of springs I had walked past at the valley's edge without once wondering what kept them alive. The fire turned toward it the way starving dogs turn toward a thrown joint. Grass flared, and where it flared, Edmund gave it roses, and hedgerows, and an orchard's worth of blossom, decades of Octobers and Mays, and the fire ate and ate and grew fat and slow, and Edmund walked backward out of the meadow looking —

Looking smaller.

Not bent. Not gray. Just — less, the way a candle is less after a birthday. His hands had gone translucent at the edges, faintly, like paper held to sun.

"That was years," I said. My voice shook. "Those were your *years*, Edmund, those were—"

"Years of watering," he said. "Felix. Years of *watering*. There are worse things to spend." He took my arm and steered me toward the seam in the wall, and behind us the fire settled into the ash of an eaten summer, drowsing, sated. "Besides. I'm owed some. I intend to collect them back. Now — through here, quickly, and whatever happens next, keep your grip on your mother and don't let autumn get in your ears."

* * *

**EDMUND**

Autumn was the worst, because autumn was honest.

Spring had lied about safety and summer had lied about rest. Autumn lied about nothing. It was a long gallery of wind, a corridor of moving air that smelled of leaf-smoke and rain coming, and the wind did not lull us or burn us. It simply took things. It went through us like a customs officer, and where it found something loose, it took that.

It found my loose things first. I felt them go — little leaves of myself lifting off, drifting back down the corridor toward the court, and I could not even resent it, because the court had a claim on every part of me and always had. Pieces of forty more years, torn away like pages. I let them go and kept walking and said nothing, because she was carrying enough.

It found hers too. I heard it happen — a hitch in her breathing, a stumble, and when I caught her elbow she was staring at her own empty hand as though it had been holding something.

"There was—" she started. "I had — there was a *thing*, I was keeping it—"

"What thing?"

"I don't—" She pressed her fist to her mouth. "I don't remember. That's the point, isn't it. I had a thing and now there's a hole shaped like it."

The wind had taken a memory; I couldn't tell her which, because I didn't know, and telling her she'd lost something unnamed would only make the hole bigger. So I did what cowards do and called it strategy.

"Hold your oath tighter," I said. "Wind can't carry what's sworn."

And she did — I felt it, felt the words of her ancestors' bargain close around her like a coat, and the next gust broke around her instead of through. But here is what I did not know, walking that corridor, holding my own edges down against the wind: some of what it stripped from me did not go home to the court. Some of it — the pieces nearest her, torn loose in the summer room, in the spring floods, in every mile since the garden — had already lodged in her, quiet as seeds, and she carried them unknowing between herself and the bottle at her breast. Two souls, I thought, watching her fight the wind. Hers by theft, her mother's by right. If only the ledger were that simple. Ledgers never are.

The corridor ended at a door.

Not the great door — not yet, not the carved exit with her family crest that the map promised, the one we could see now at the end of a final short hall, blessedly ordinary, wooden, iron-banded, *real*. Between us and it stood one last chamber, small and round, and the silence that had chased us through three seasons arrived at its threshold and stopped.

Because the door out of the round chamber was closing. Slowly — this court does nothing quickly — but grinding shut, stone upon stone, and beyond it I could see the hall, the crest-carved door, freedom with a wooden face.

"The silence can't cross," Felix breathed. "Look — it stops at the threshold. This room is still ours. But that door—"

"Is leaving," I agreed. "Rooms in this place keep their own hours. It's nearly the hour for closed."

She did the arithmetic instantly, because she is her mother's daughter, whoever her mother turned out to be. "How long?"

"Minutes. Perhaps fewer."

"And the only way to hold a door open in a country where everything closes is—"

"Don't," I said.

"—to *be* a door," she finished, and turned on me with fire in her face, the good fire, the one they could never shelve. "No. No. You've paid twice today already. Find another way."

"There isn't one." I said it gently, which somehow made her angrier. "Felix. Listen to me. A door only needs to be held, not held *well*. I'll stand in the frame and I'll put my back against the stone and I'll fill the hinge with — with whatever I have left that's stubborn. It won't kill me. Nothing here kills; it only keeps." I smiled at her. "You'll come back for me. You're constitutionally incapable of doing otherwise. I'm relying on it."

She swore at me. It was a good oath, earthy, unmagical, entirely human, and I treasured it more than any swordspeech.

Then the gap was shoulder-width, and she went through — she had to, someone had to reach the far side and be ready to run — and I stepped into the frame of the closing door, and set my back against the stone, and began to pour.

I gave it the year the plum tree finally fruited. I gave it the decade of learning her grandmother's name. I gave it mornings, hundreds of mornings, dew and shears and the small vanity of a well-kept hedge. The stone drank and slowed and stopped, quivering, and I held it the way I had once held a garden through a late frost: not with strength, with *attention*, with love applied as pressure, and the gap stayed open — narrow, ragged, exactly the width of one desperate woman and the small warm thing she carried.

She turned back once, on the far side, silhouetted against the crest-carved door with its iron bands. Her face did something complicated.

"Edmund—"

"Go," I said, through gritted everything. "Through the wooden one. Quickly. And Felix — when you get to the valley — the garden gate sticks. Lift, then push. It's been sticking for sixty years and no one ever—"

The stone shuddered, hungry again, taking another handful of me to stay persuaded.

She ran at the gap. She turned sideways, protectively, the way you turn when carrying something breakable — and I saw, with the last clear sight I had, that she was cradling not one bundle against her chest but two, her arms curved around her mother's bottle and around a soft green glow she didn't know she was holding, glow that bled from her sleeves, from her hair, from the spaces between her fingers.

My pieces. Riding to freedom in the coat of a thief who thought she'd stolen only one soul.

The door took the last of what it wanted. The gap sealed. The silence rolled over me like a tide, and somewhere beyond the stone, muffled, fading, running, I heard her boots on the flagstones and her voice — not swordspeech, not an oath, just her, shouting my name at a mountain that had never once answered anyone.

I leaned against the closed door, lighter than I had been in centuries, and waited to see if she would prove me right about coming back.

Above us both, I felt it begin: the first flakes of the final snow, sifting down through the dark of the world, sealing the passes one by one.

We were out of stored summers. All that was left was winter, and the climb, and whatever she decided a promise was worth.

The snow was falling early. The snow was falling fast.

And the door at my back had no handle on this side.
