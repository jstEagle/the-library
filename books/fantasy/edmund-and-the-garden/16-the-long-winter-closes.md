## 16. The Long Winter Closes

The last corridor of the court was made of autumn, and it was dying.

I could hear it going as we ran — the slow collapse of stored seasons behind us, room after room exhaling their borrowed warmth into stone. The spring room had flooded at our heels. The summer room had tried to burn us, and Edmund had walked through it like a man greeting old friends who'd turned cruel, murmuring apologies to the fire as it snapped at his coat. Now the autumn wind chased us down a hallway of amber light and bare branches, tearing at my hair, moaning through the rafters with a sound I chose to believe was grief rather than pursuit.

The bottle rode against my chest, wrapped in Edmund's scarf, warm as a sleeping bird.

*Still humming,* I thought, for the hundredth time since I'd cut it from its shelf. *She's still humming.*

My mother's soul sang her lullaby in a register below hearing — I felt it more than heard it, a vibration in my sternum, in my teeth, in the hilt of the sword strapped across my back. The swordspeech had gone quiet since the theft. Not silent, exactly. *Watchful.* Like a dog that knows it has done something extraordinary and is waiting to find out whether it will be punished or praised.

Edmund stumbled beside me, and I caught his arm before I caught myself doing it.

He was lighter than he should have been. I'd noticed it first in the summer room, when he'd held open a door of woven heat while the court's silences came hunting us — those spreading absences that ate sound room by room, that had swallowed three corridors behind us already. He'd stood in the doorway with his arms spread like a man holding back a tide, and when he'd finally let go and run, something had stayed behind with the door. Some weight of him. Some years.

"You're spending yourself," I said, pulling him along. "I can see it. You're—"

"I'm fine," he said, which was what people said when they were not fine, and had been saying since the invention of doors.

Ahead, the amber light thinned. Ahead, I could see it: the door.

It looked smaller than I remembered, which was absurd, because I had only seen it once, from the outside, hours or days or one entire stolen lifetime ago. Carved stone, tall as two men, my family's crest cut deep into its face — the sword and the growing thing, crossed. My ancestors' mark. My inheritance, pressed into rock by hands that had also pressed a soul into a ledger.

And beyond it, through the hairline gap where the door had not quite sealed behind us, a sliver of gray.

Snow.

The final snow was beginning.

I felt it before I saw it — a change in the air pressure, a stillness in the stone itself, the whole mountain drawing a breath it did not intend to release. The long winter, finishing its fall. The collection notice coming due. Somewhere above us, weeks early and miles wide, the sky was opening over the pass, over the valley, over everything my family had traded a soul per generation to keep soft and green and forgetful.

"Edmund." I stopped him with a hand on his chest. His heart was beating slower than it should have. I pretended not to notice that too. "How much time?"

He looked past me at the sliver of gray light, and his face did the thing it always did — the gentle arithmetic of a man who remembers every plant and no years, calculating something he wished he didn't know.

"Less than you want," he said. "More than nothing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind the mountain gives."

We ran the last stretch together, our footsteps loud in the dying autumn, and the wind screamed behind us, and somewhere far off — so far off it might have been imagination — I heard the steward's footsteps. Unhurried. Patient. A court does not chase. A court simply arrives where you are going, slightly before you, holding out its hand.

* * *

The door was sealed.

Not locked. Sealed — grown shut, the way frost grows across a window, the way scar tissue grows over a wound. The hairline gap I'd seen from thirty paces away was, at ten paces, no gap at all. Stone had flowed like water around the edges of my family's crest, smooth and seamless, and the gray sliver of daylight was gone, and the cold coming off the door was the cold of the world above, pressing in.

"No," I said. It came out very small. I said it again, louder, and put both palms flat against the stone. "No. No, it opened *for me.* It opened for my words. It can open again."

"Felix."

"It opened for the oath. I spoke the oath and it opened like a—" My voice cracked. I hated that. I hated every crack in it, every tremor, all of it evidence of how afraid I was, laid out for the mountain to catalog. "Edmund, help me think. You know things. You know *growing* things. Doors are just walls that haven't decided yet—"

"Felix." He took my wrists, gently, the way he handled seedlings. "Look at the snow."

"There is no snow, there's a *door*—"

"Look."

I looked. Where my breath met the seam of the door, frost was forming. Spreading. Delicate fern-patterns of ice crawling outward from the point where the outside world touched the inside one, and beneath the frost, faint as memory, the crest was beginning to glow — not with welcome but with *closing*, the way a mouth closes, the way a book closes, the way a hand closes around a thing it means to keep.

The final snow had reached the threshold. The long winter was sealing the door from the other side.

"We're on the wrong side," I whispered. The truth of it went through me like the swordspeech used to, cold and total. "We're on the wrong side of winter. If this shuts—"

Behind us, down the corridor of dying autumn, footsteps stopped.

Unhurried. Patient. Close enough now that I could hear the particular quality of them — the sound of someone who has never once needed to hurry, because everything comes to them eventually. Leaves drifted down around the approaching figure like confetti at a funeral. The amber light bent toward him, the way light bends toward anything that owns it.

The steward came walking out of the falling season with his hands folded, and he was smiling, and I understood suddenly why the court didn't need bells for alarms. Why would it? Everything in the dark under the mountain already belonged to it. Silence wasn't an alarm here. Silence was the natural state, and noise was the intrusion, and we had been noise from the moment we entered.

"The collateral's daughter," he said warmly. He always said it warmly. That was the worst part — the genuine courtesy of it, the hospitality that was also custody. "You've given us a memorable evening. The shelves will speak of it for a century, which for us is roughly a long weekend."

"Stay back," I said, and drew the sword.

The bladesong woke reluctantly, a thin note, tired. Even it knew how this looked: one girl, one spent garden-keeper, one stolen bottle, standing between a court and its property.

The steward regarded the blade with polite interest, the way one regards a child's drawing.

"I'm not here to take it," he said. "Put that away before you hurt yourself. Swordspeech in the court's own halls — do you know what that costs you? Every word is a withdrawal. And you've been making withdrawals all night." His eyes moved to Edmund, and softened, and I hated that they could soften. "Both of you."

"What do you want?" Edmund asked. His voice was steady. His hand, on my shoulder, was not entirely.

"To offer terms," the steward said. "One last time. The court admires audacity, and it honors hospitality even toward thieves — *especially* toward thieves; audacity is a form of payment, in its way. So. Hear me."

The autumn room shuddered. Somewhere behind him, another season died. The frost on the door crept wider, and through the stone I could feel it — the weight of the finished winter stacking itself against the far side, drift upon drift, silence upon silence, the whole white tonnage of the long winter leaning on the only door out.

"The Rite of Return requires passage," the steward said. "Passage requires the door. The door requires the winter to be unfinished. The winter," he spread his hands, apologetic, "is finishing. These are not cruelties. They are arithmetic. But the court is not without mercy, and I am empowered to extend it. So: a trade."

"Name it," I said, before I could stop myself, and immediately wished I hadn't, because naming trades is how courts get their hooks in.

The steward smiled again. Gently. He looked at Edmund.

"Him," he said.

The word dropped into the corridor like a stone into a well, and I listened to it fall for a long time.

"He was ours first," the steward said, softly, reasonably, the way you'd discuss returning a borrowed tool. "The first keeper. The first soul sold. We gave him leave to tend the valley's edge instead of sitting on a shelf — a kindness, though I understand he may not have framed it that way. He has wandered outside his terms for a century, and now he stands in our halls again, and the ledger remembers him. Return him to us — willingly, and the bottle goes with you. The door opens. The Rite completes. Your mother rests. The valley keeps its bargain, unbroken, and everyone," he folded his hands, "goes home."

Home. The word did something to me. Home was the valley. Home was graves and drills and an oath I'd carried like a stone in my mouth for twenty years. Home was the garden at the edge, green in the snow, kept by—

Kept by him.

I looked at Edmund.

He was already looking at me, and his face was terrible in its calmness, because I could see that some part of him had been expecting this offer since the moment we entered the court — maybe since the moment he'd first offered to guide me over the pass, a leftover recognizing a leftover, kinship between two things the world had filed away. He knew what he was worth here. He'd told me himself, in the garden, in chapter after chapter of a life I was only beginning to read: *his gentleness is what remains when a court files away your rage.*

"It's a fair trade," Edmund said quietly.

"It's not," I said.

"Felix—"

"It's *not.*" The bladesong flared, wild, and I reined it in. "You don't get to decide you're a fair trade. Nobody gets to decide that about themselves. That's the whole—that's the entire *point* of everything wrong with this place. It puts prices on people and then calls the pricing mercy."

"The alternative," the steward said, patient as geology, "is that the door seals with you on this side. Both souls forfeit, per the terms you agreed. Your mother's bottle returns to its shelf. You become a guest of the court — indefinitely. And the long winter finishes falling on a valley that has lost its last oath-bound swordspeaker, its garden-keeper, and its future, all in a single evening." He tilted his head. "I mention this not as threat but as clarity. The court deals in clarity."

The frost reached my boots. Cold climbed through the leather like fingers counting toes.

And Edmund stepped forward.

"Don't," I said.

"Felix Almeida." He used my full name, the name no one used, the name the stranger had died speaking, and it landed on me like a hand on my hair. "I have been a leftover for a hundred years. I have been *leave* — leave to exist at the edge of other people's safety. Do you know what you gave me these past weeks? You gave me a road. A purpose. Someone to walk beside who saw the garden-keeper and not the shelved thing." His smile was the gentlest thing in that whole underground country, gentler than any stored summer. "Let me be worth something once, at the end, on purpose."

"No," I said, and my voice broke clean through, and I let it. "You want to know what I learned in the town of sold names? What I learned watching people pawn their memories for passage, small piece by small piece, until they were gray-eyed and quiet? *Nobody survives by selling the warm things.* Nobody. You don't trade a person for a person. You don't trade a soul for a soul. That's not mercy, that's just the bargain wearing a kinder face, and I am *done* with bargains. My whole life was a bargain. My family's whole history is a bargain. And I am standing here, at the bottom of the world, telling the entire court beneath the mountain—"

I turned to the steward. The bladesong rose through me, exhausted, ragged, and I let it rise anyway.

"—*no.*"

The word rang off the stone. Off the dead autumn. Off the door.

The steward regarded me for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Courts are patient. Perhaps interest. Perhaps the faint, dry pleasure of a clerk encountering an entry that doesn't fit the ledger.

"So noted," he said. "Refusal is recorded. The terms stand as written: failure forfeits both souls. The door closes in"—he glanced upward, consulting some clock I couldn't see—"*now.*"

The frost surged. The stone groaned. The crest blazed white, and the seam began to close like an eye.

"Then we go *through* it," I snarled, and did the only reckless thing left in me: I raised the sword and spoke the oath backward.

I don't know where the words came from. That's a lie — I know exactly where they came from. They came from the same place the lullaby came from, the same place the map came from, the same place mothers keep things for daughters they'll never meet: the deep shelf of the blood. The oath ran through me reversed, unmade, a key turning the wrong way, and the bladesong tore out of the tired steel like a hawk off a wrist, and I struck the closing seam with the flat of the blade and *spoke it open* —

The door split. Gray light poured in. Snow came through the gap in a single long exhalation, swirling into the corridor, and beyond it I saw the pass, white and blind and howling, the climb of our lives stacked against a sky the color of unwritten paper.

"GO!" I grabbed Edmund's sleeve.

He went. Of course he went. He hesitated — one heartbeat, half a heartbeat, looking back at the steward, at the court, at whatever the steward's face was doing — and then he went, through the gap, into the snow.

I went after him.

And just before the stone closed, I heard the steward's voice behind me, unhurried, patient, almost kind:

"The court thanks you for your visit. Do come again."

The door ground shut.

* * *

The silence afterward was total.

We stood in the snow, twenty feet from a wall of mountain that showed no door at all — just cliff face, just crest-less stone, just the ordinary geology of a world that had decided we were done with it. Snow filled my hood, my collar, the space between my racing heart and my ribs. Edmund leaned against a boulder, breathing in a way I pretended not to count.

"We made it," I said. "We're out. Felix Almeida, master thief, strikes again."

Edmund didn't answer.

"Edmund?"

"The door," he said slowly. He was looking at the cliff face, and then up, at the pass, and then — I watched him do it — at the valley, far below and far away, invisible under the falling white. "Felix. Which way is home?"

I turned to point.

And the pointing hand stopped, because the answer wasn't there.

I knew the shape of the way home the way I knew my own hands — up the pass, along the ridge, down through the boundary where magic cannot follow, into the valley that forgets. I had walked that shape in my mind ten thousand times, bored at graveside, restless through drills. It was the first map I ever owned, drawn before the stranger's, inked into me by twenty years of wanting out.

I reached for it, and it was gone.

Not fogged. Not hidden. *Gone*, the way the oath had been a key and was now an unmade thing, the way the door behind us was now a wall. The long winter had finished falling, and it had fallen not just on the passes but on the knowing — the valley sealed itself the way the court sealed its doors, and the seal ran through me like a suture. I was outside my own home's memory. I was a stranger at my own border.

"Edmund," I said. My voice sounded very young. "I can't remember the way home."

The snow fell. The bottle hummed against my chest, warm, oblivious, singing its lullaby to no one. And somewhere beneath my fear, beneath the cold, beneath the dawning size of what we'd done, one thought surfaced and refused to sink:

*I brought her out. Whatever happens now — however we climb, however we fail — she is out. She will never sit on a shelf again.*

I pulled the scarf tighter around the bottle, turned my back on the blank wall where the door had been, and looked up at the white blindness of the pass.

"Climb anyway," I said. "We climb anyway."

Edmund pushed off the boulder and came to stand beside me, and together we stepped into the finished winter, toward a home neither of us could find.
