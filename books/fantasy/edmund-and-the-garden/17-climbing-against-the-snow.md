## 17. Climbing Against the Snow

I have died before. I want that on the record, filed somewhere official, because what we were doing on the mountain was not dying, and I resented the difference.

Dying is a door. This was a staircase — long, white, and staffed by weather with opinions.

The winter had finished falling while we were still beneath the mountain, and now it fell upward too, if such a thing can be imagined: snow rising off the drifts in slow gray ribbons, as though the world were being unwritten from the bottom. The court's collection had begun in earnest. Every flake that passed us going the wrong way was a small receipt for something taken.

Felix climbed ahead of me. She always climbed ahead of me now. She had learned, somewhere between the season-rooms and the door, that if she let me set the pace we would both die politely, and Felix Almeida did not do anything politely.

"Edmund." Her voice came back to me thin and scraped. "Talk."

"About what?"

"Anything. Your voice keeps people warm. It's the only useful thing about you."

This was her way of checking whether I could still speak, which was her way of checking whether I was still alive, which was her way of saying things she would deny under oath. I gave her my best sentence.

"In my garden," I said, "there was a rose called 'Céleste' that bloomed only when it was about to die. The gardener who named it thought this was romantic. I thought it was a plant with poor timing."

Silence. Then, faintly, from somewhere above me: "That's not a story, that's a complaint."

"I'm a very old man. Complaints are all that's left after the stories are used up."

She laughed — one syllable of it, frost-cracked — and kept climbing, and I counted that laugh the way a miser counts coins, because I had been spending myself since the door beneath the mountain and my purse was nearly empty.

Let me explain what I mean by *spending*, since I spent most of my life pretending I didn't know.

A soul sold to the court is not taken all at once. That would be theft, and the court does not steal; the court *files*. What remains of you is left at your post, tending whatever you were assigned to tend, and the court draws on the remainder as needed — interest, they would call it, if courts used honest words. In the season-rooms I had burned decades holding doors open against spring floods and summer fire. I had felt each year leave me the way you feel a tooth go loose: familiar, then absent, then a hole the tongue keeps returning to.

I was lighter than I had ever been. Lighter than the day I was first sold.

It occurred to me, somewhere around the third switchback, that this might be the court's design. Not the steward's malice — the steward did not seem capable of malice, only of arithmetic — but the shape of the bargain itself. Let them run. Let them climb. Everything they spend getting free is everything the ledger gains. We were not escaping the debt. We were paying it off at altitude.

I did not tell Felix this. There is a difference between honesty and cruelty, and I had already crossed that line once, and the crossing was coming, and I dreaded it more than the cold.

* * *

We sheltered that night in the lee of a stone that had once, I believed, been a waymarker. I knew its cousins — stones like it had lined the road when I walked it carrying seedlings and someone else's grief. The snow had eaten their inscriptions. The snow was eating everything, methodically, like a clerk working through an inbox.

Felix sat with the bottle cradled against her chest, inside her coat, inside her arms, wrapped in every layer she had. Her mother's soul glowed through the glass the way a coal glows: not light exactly, but the memory of light, insisting.

It was dimmer than it had been in the court. I noticed. I was almost certain she noticed and was pretending otherwise, which is a family trait, apparently, inherited along with the jaw and the stubbornness.

"How is she?" I asked, though I meant the bottle, though I meant *how much time*.

"Warm," Felix said. "Still humming. She does the second verse wrong — she always got the second verse wrong, she'd skip a line and I never told her because she'd stop singing." A pause. The wind filled it with something sharp. "Edmund. How long can a soul stay out of its shelf?"

"A soul isn't a pickle," I said. "It doesn't spoil on a schedule. But it fades when its bearer fades, and its bearer—" I gestured, generally, at the mountain, the winter, the whole arrangement. "Cold is a kind of forgetting. She's forgetting herself a little more each hour we're up here."

Felix looked down at the bottle for a long moment. Then she began to sing.

Not the lullaby. The oath.

I had heard the oath spoken in the valley — the elders droning it at midwinter, duty-heavy, a rope they held rather than a song. Felix sang it backward. Last word to first, unmaking it as she went, and where the spoken oath bound, the sung reversal did something else entirely: it loosened. Warmth moved through her voice like water through dry ground, and the bottle brightened against her chest, and for a moment the snow around us fell the correct direction, as if embarrassed.

"That's not how the oath works," I said, stupidly, because I am sometimes stupid in the presence of genius.

"It works however I say it works," Felix said. "I'm the last oath-bound swordspeaker. If there's no manual, I'm writing it." She tucked the bottle away. "Sleep, Edmund. You look like something I dug up."

"I was something you dug up."

"Then rest. Compost needs its sleep."

I slept, or did whatever I do instead, and dreamed — I still dream, that is one mercy the court has never managed to repossess — and in the dream I was standing in my garden in high summer, and a woman came down the path carrying a sleeping child.

I knew her face before I knew her name. I had known it for years, the way you know a word you've read but never said aloud. Mirabel Almeida. Felix's mother. Fleeing *down*-mountain, toward the valley, with a bundle that breathed.

In the dream she stopped at the gate and looked at me with eyes gone gray at the rims — half-claimed, already signed for — and she said, *Keep the gate open behind me. Whatever comes. Keep it open.*

And I had said yes. Of course I had said yes. I say yes to everyone; it is my entire personality, and it is also, I understood even then, the reason I ended up filed.

She had gone down into the valley, given birth or already given it, raised a daughter among graves and sword-forms, and never once told that daughter why the valley was safe, or what the safety cost, or that her mother's own name sat on a shelf beneath the mountain gathering dust and interest. Protection, the elders would call it. I had another word, and I had been carrying it up the mountain like a stone in my boot for two days, and tomorrow — I decided in the dream, and dreams are binding for my kind — tomorrow I would take the stone out and hand it to her.

* * *

The storm found us on the fourth day, or perhaps we found the storm; by then the distinction had stopped mattering. The finished winter did not blow so much as *arrive*, wholesale, in place, the way bad news arrives. Visibility became a rumor. The cold stopped being temperature and became presence — something walking beside us, matching our pace, patient as the steward.

Felix sang the oath backward until her voice went raw, then mouthed it, then hummed it, then I heard her lips moving in silence and saw the bottle glow anyway, faintly, fed on nothing but intention.

"We need shelter," I shouted into the white.

"There's no shelter!"

"There's always shelter. Shelter is a decision plants make." And I put my hand against the mountainside, under the snow, and asked.

You may laugh. Everyone laughs until it works. Growing things listen — that was my first assignment, my original posting, the job description they wrote into what was left of me after the filing: *tend, keep, listen.* And the mountain's scree-slopes were threaded with roots, old ones, patient ones, alpine shrubs that had survived ten thousand winters by knowing exactly when to bend. I asked them to bend now, together, over us.

They did. Slowly, groaningly, a lattice of living wood arched above our heads and caught the snow, and we crawled beneath into a dark that smelled of bark and resin and was, relative to everything else, paradise.

Felix pressed her back against the root-wall, breathing hard, and looked at me across the bottle's dim glow, and her face in that light was terrible and young and absolutely done with me.

"You've been holding out," she said. "All this time. On the pass you 'knew the growing things.' In the avalanche you 'had a feeling.' Under the mountain you knew the etiquette. Edmund. What else do you know?"

Here it was, then. The stone in the boot.

I am a gentle man. I want to be clear that my gentleness is not a virtue; it is a scar. When the court took my rage — and they did take it, carefully, the way you'd core an apple — what remained was kindness, the way a wall remains when you remove the house around it. For three hundred years I have been kind because I had nothing else in stock. And kindness has one law above all others: *do not add weight to what another carries unless they ask.*

But she was asking. She just didn't know she was asking yet.

"When your mother fled," I said, "she came down this mountain. Toward the valley. With a child asleep against her chest."

Felix went very still. Even the bottle seemed to hold its breath.

"She stopped at my gate. She was already half-claimed — gray-eyed, quiet, the way the refugees on the road were quiet. But she had enough of herself left to make me promise something. She said: *keep the gate open behind me.*" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I promised. I have kept that gate open for twenty years, Felix. I kept it open the day you walked out of it without telling anyone, and I kept it open tonight, in a manner of speaking, by growing you a roof. But I should have told you on the pass. I should have told you the first night. I knew what she was fleeing *from*, and I knew what she was fleeing *with*, and I did the arithmetic, and I said nothing, because I thought silence was a kindness, and—"

"Stop." 

I stopped.

Felix stared at the bottle. Her hands were shaking, and she wrapped them tighter around the glass as if it were the only solid thing in the universe, which, that night, it may have been.

"She knew," Felix said finally. "She knew what she was running from. She knew the debt existed. She carried me *toward* it — no. Away from it. Into the valley. She bought me twenty years inside the walls and never—" A sound came out of her that was not quite a laugh. "She sang the lullaby wrong on purpose, didn't she. So I'd remember it. So someday I'd hear the right version and know something was missing."

"Felix—"

"Why didn't you tell me?" And here it was, the real question, stripped of everything else, and it hit harder than any blade I had ever watched her speak awake. "Not the court. Not the elders. *You.* You watched me drill forms at graves for years. You watched me chafe. You knew. Why didn't you *say*?"

"Because you were happy," I said. "Or near enough. And because I am a coward with excellent manners, and because every reason I gave myself was a reason, and none of them were excuses." I made myself meet her eyes. "I'm sorry. Not for keeping the gate open. For keeping my mouth shut while you stood in the doorway wondering why you couldn't leave. I'm sorry, Felix. I'll carry it however you tell me to carry it."

The wind screamed over our root-roof. Snow sifted down through the branches in fine gray threads, receipts still arriving.

Felix cried. Quietly, furiously, the way she did everything — as if grief were a chore to be completed efficiently. She cried for maybe a minute, and then she wiped her face on her sleeve, and looked at me, and what was in her face was not forgiveness, not yet, but something underneath forgiveness, something older and more load-bearing.

Anger, burning down. And under the anger, gold.

"You're forgiven," she said, "because being angry at you is a luxury and I don't have time. But Edmund? Never again. Not one true thing held back. Not to protect me. Swear it."

"On the garden," I said. "On what's left of me."

"Good." She shifted the bottle higher against her heart and stood, and the root-lattice groaned and opened for her like a door. "Because we're climbing, and I don't climb with liars."

* * *

We climbed.

I will not pretend those last days were anything but ruin. My decades were spent; what remained of me burned fast and low, candle-end mathematics. Felix's hands cracked and bled and she sang through it. The oath backward, over and over, her voice a thread of warmth in a world that had forgotten the concept. Sometimes she sang it in whispers. Once, delirious with cold, I caught her singing it to *me*, and pretended to be asleep, and was warmer for an hour afterward, which is not how warmth works, and happened anyway.

The bottle faded. I will say it plainly because she deserves plainness: each morning the glow was less. The lullaby slowed, gaps opening in it like missing teeth. Felix filled the gaps herself, humming her mother's wrong version, stitching the holes with error, with memory, with love doing repair work far above its pay grade.

On what I believe was the sixth day — the sun had become a theoretical object — Felix stumbled, caught herself on ice, and stayed down a moment too long.

"Get up," I said.

"I'm getting up."

"You're resting."

"I'm *strategically* horizontal."

"Get up, Felix." I hauled her arm over my shoulder, and we stood together, two ruined things propping each other upright, and I looked past her shoulder at the ridge ahead — black rock, white sky, and beyond it, barely visible, a smudge of green that had no business existing in the finished winter.

The garden. My garden. Dead, probably. Frozen, certainly. Waiting, absolutely.

"There," I said.

Felix turned. And I watched her see it — the last impossible green thing at the edge of a world gone white — and I watched something move through her face that I had seen once before, twenty years ago, on a woman with gray-rimmed eyes looking back down the mountain at a gate she was leaving open behind her.

Hope, and the price tag attached.

"The boundary's just past the ridge," I said. "Where magic cannot follow. Where the soul can't cross unless—" I stopped. Because the end of that sentence was mine, and I had known it since the season-rooms, and I was not ready, and readiness is a luxury I had already spent.

"Unless what?" Felix said.

The wind answered for me, howling down off the ridge, and below us, far below, faint as a heartbeat through stone, I felt the mountain shift — the court, patient, closing its ledger page by page.

"Unless someone becomes part of the valley itself," I said. "Come on. I'll explain at the top. It's better with a view."
