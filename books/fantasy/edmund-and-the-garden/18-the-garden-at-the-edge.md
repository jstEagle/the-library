## 18. The Garden at the Edge

I had imagined this moment a hundred times on the climb. In most versions I was weeping. In several I was laughing. In one particularly humiliating version Edmund carried me the last stretch and I was unconscious, which tells you something about how much faith I had in my own legs.

In none of them was I standing in the snow, holding my mother's soul against my chest like a loaf of bread gone cold, staring at a dead garden.

The garden at the valley's edge had been green when we left. Impossible green — the kind that made your eyes argue with themselves, roses blooming fat and shameless beside frost-burned pines, grass so vivid it looked lit from underneath. It had been the first thing I ever saw that didn't belong to the valley's gray arithmetic of survival, and I had hated it a little for that, before I loved it entirely.

Now it was gray. Gray as everything else. The roses were black sticks in white drifts. The trellises stood bare as ribs. Snow lay over the beds in smooth, unbroken sheets, the way snow lies over graves.

"It's dead," I said, because someone had to say it out loud or I'd keep pretending otherwise until pretending became a way of dying.

Edmund said nothing. That was worse. Edmund always had words — gentle, sideways, patient words, the kind you could lean against without them splintering. He'd been rationing them for hours, though. Since the door ground shut behind us with all three of us on the wrong side of it, he'd spoken maybe forty times, and each word had cost him something visible. His color had been going since the court. Not pale — *drained*, like watercolor left in rain. The edges of him had started to look negotiable.

"Edmund." I turned. "Tell me it's sleeping. Tell me gardens sleep."

He looked at the garden the way you look at an old friend's face across a coffin. "It isn't sleeping," he said. "It's waiting. There's a difference, and the difference is me."

* * *

Let me back up, because my hands are shaking and if I don't put things in order they'll come out of me in the wrong shape.

We had climbed for two days through winter that had finished falling — real finished, capital-F Finished, the long winter the elders used to scare children into bringing in the firewood. The sky above the pass was the color of old pewter and it did not move. Snow fell upward in places. I am not being poetic; it fell *upward*, drifting back toward the mountain like it was being called home, and every flake that went up felt like a minute coming off our lives.

My mother's soul rode against my sternum in its bottle, wrapped in my scarf, wrapped in Edmund's coat over that, wrapped in every layer we owned, because the steward's parting courtesy had included a detail I hadn't asked for: souls cool when they travel far from their shelf. Cool toward quiet. Quiet toward nothing. She had hummed her lullaby almost constantly at first — I could feel it through the glass, a vibration more heard than heard, if that makes any sense, a song played on the inside of my own ribs. By the second day the humming came in shorter phrases. Rests between the notes grew longer. I found myself humming along out loud, badly, filling the rests, like keeping a conversation alive with someone who keeps drifting off mid-sentence.

And Edmund was fading. He told me it was the altitude. He told me it was the cold. He told me, once, with a small crooked smile, that he had never been built for distance — that he was a kept thing, a planted thing, and planted things do poorly when you dig them up. I had called him a liar then, gently, and he had let me believe his letting me believe was the same as being wrong.

Now, standing at the boundary where the valley began — I could feel it, a faint pressure on the skin like walking into a warm room, the exact place where magic stopped following — now I understood what he hadn't said.

Because here was the problem, laid out plain as a map with only one road on it:

The soul could not cross.

Not wouldn't. Couldn't. The valley was the valley precisely because magic could not follow past its edge — no magic in, no magic out, that was the whole architecture of the Founding Bargain, the moat that had kept my family alive for generations by keeping everything else out. And a bottled soul, it turned out, counted as magic. Of course it did. What else would a soul be?

I learned this not from Edmund but from the bottle itself. At the boundary line — I could mark it exactly, a line in the snow no wider than a hair, where the air changed texture — the lullaby simply stopped. Not faded. Stopped, like a door closing on it. I stepped forward and the bottle went silent as stone. I stepped back and my mother hummed against my heart, faint and frightened-sounding, and I stood there doing that, forward and back, forward and back, like an idiot, like a woman knocking on a door knowing nobody's home, until my knees gave and I sat down hard in the snow and the crying I'd postponed through two days of climbing finally arrived, all at once, with interest.

"Felix." Edmund crouched beside me. His hand on my shoulder weighed almost nothing. That should have frightened me sooner than it did.

"She's right *there*," I said. "She's right there and she can't come home. Do you understand? I stole her out of a court beneath a mountain, I outran a winter, I burned—" my voice cracked on what we'd burned, what *he'd* burned, decades of himself holding doors open while silences hunted us room to room, "—and I can't carry her ten feet."

"I know."

"There has to be a way. There's always a way, there's a rite, there's a key, there's a bargain—"

"There is," Edmund said quietly. "You're right. There is a way. I've known it since the pass."

I looked up at him. Snow was landing on him and not melting, which it should have done, which it had done for the entire journey, because Edmund ran warm the way hearths run warm, and I thought: *oh.* I thought: *oh, no.*

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard it yet."

"I don't need to. Whatever it is, whatever look you're making — no."

He smiled. It was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was completely happy. "The valley takes no magic across its line. But the line doesn't keep out what's already part of the valley. It never has. That's why the garden stood there, Felix. Why do you think a garden grew at the edge of a place where magic cannot follow?" He spread his hands, those gardener's hands, dirt in the creases of knuckles that never seemed to hold dirt for long. "The garden wasn't defying the rule. The garden *was* the rule. Something rooted deep enough stops being magic crossing a border and starts being the border itself. The valley doesn't carry my mother's roses across the line. They grow from inside it."

I was already shaking my head. "You're not a rose."

"No," he agreed. "But I was a soul on a shelf once, and the court gave me leave to be soil instead. I've been half-rooted my whole second life. I only ever needed a reason to finish the job." He said it lightly. He said everything lightly; lightness was his native tongue, the accent survival leaves you with. "And here's a reason standing in the snow, holding a bottle, refusing to go home."

"Find another reason."

"There isn't time for another reason. The winter's still falling behind us, and it falls toward the valley too — you've seen the sky. When the last snow seals the season, the door beneath closes for a generation, and your mother cools past warming, and everything we spent down there buys exactly nothing." He took the bottle from my hands with a tenderness that undid me more than any argument could have. He held it up to the pewter light, and inside, faintly, my mother hummed three notes toward him. Recognition. Souls remember other souls that were shelved near theirs. "Hello, little sister," he murmured. "I know the shelf you slept on. Let me walk you home."

"Edmund—"

"The Rite needs a carrier already inside the valley. You can't do it from the edge; the words won't reach. Someone has to bring her over the line and set her in the ground at her grave, and the only person who can cross carrying her is someone who *is* the valley." He looked at me then, fully, and his eyes were the gray of the garden under snow, and they were kind, and kindness was somehow the cruelest thing about all of it. "I was the first keeper of this garden, Felix. Before the bargain, before the shelves. Rooting me back into it isn't a death. It's a return. People spend their whole lives looking for one of those. I get mine at the end of a rescue mission, in excellent company. There are worse endings."

"Don't," I said. My voice came out small and vicious. "Don't you dare make this sound reasonable. Don't you dare make it sound like a *gift*. You don't get to decide—"

"Who does, then?"

"Me! Somebody! Not — not the math!" I was on my feet without remembering standing, shouting at a man who was mostly snowlight. "Everyone in my life has decided things *for* me. The elders decided my grandmother's grandmother's debt. My mother decided to run rather than tell me. The court decided I was collateral with a pulse. And now you're going to stand there and decide to *plant yourself* like it's gardening, like it's just another Tuesday among the roses, and expect me to thank you—"

"Yes," Edmund said.

"—because that's what you *do*, you absolute—" I ran out of words. All the swordspeech in me, all the waking wild music of the blade, and I had nothing. Just breath and fury and the bottle sitting in his hands, humming its thin brave thread of a lullaby.

He waited me out. He was good at that. He'd had lifetimes of practice waiting for things to grow.

"When you were small," he said, "a woman came down the pass carrying a sleeping child. She stopped at my gate. She was starving and terrified and she asked me if the valley forgets, and I told her the truth: it forgets everything except what it loves. She looked at you — asleep, drooling on her shoulder, utterly unimpressed by mountains — and she said, 'Then she'll be fine,' and she walked on. I have thought about that sentence for twenty years, Felix. *Then she'll be fine.* She wasn't talking about safety. She was talking about you. About what you're made of." He held the bottle out to me. "I'm not deciding for you. I'm deciding with you, whether you like it or not, because that's what leftovers do. We stick together. Now take your mother. And dig."

* * *

Here is what taking root looks like, in case anyone ever asks you, in case it's ever your job to watch:

It looks like digging.

That's it. That's the miracle. No light, no choir, no thunder. Edmund took off his boots — of course he took off his boots, he apologized for the state of his socks — and stood barefoot in the ruined garden between two black rosebushes, and he said, "Dig me a hole. Deep as you can. Deeper than seems polite."

So I dug. With my hands, then with the sword, using the flat, then — when he nodded — with the edge, cutting into frozen ground that opened for the blade like it remembered being cut by better gardeners than me. The hole went down and down and the deeper it got the warmer the earth became, which was wrong for winter and right for everything else, and by the time I climbed out my arms were shaking and the hole smelled like summer. Like stored summers. Like the court's season-rooms, but honest, growing instead of hoarded.

Edmund handed me the bottle first.

"Hold her a moment," he said. "Before I go. So she knows the way."

I held my mother's soul and it hummed against my palms and I said all the things I'd carried up a mountain to say at a grave and couldn't say at a grave because the grave was empty and the words were too heavy for a hole that size. I said I was sorry it took me so long. I said the lullaby was terrible and I loved it. I said, "Then she'll be fine," and laughed and sobbed in the same breath, because apparently I was my mother's daughter down to the sentences.

Then I gave her back to him.

He stepped into the hole. He stood in it up to his knees and looked around at his dead garden with an expression I will keep until I die — fond, exasperated, proprietary, the look of a man surveying work he intends to resume immediately. Then he looked at me.

"Rules," he said, and held up fingers. "One: water the roses. They'll pretend they don't need it. They lie. Two: the gate sticks in wet weather; lift, don't shove. Three—" and here his voice finally, finally wavered, "—come sit with me sometimes. Not to talk. Roots get lonely for voices. Read me your drafts. I'll hate the endings. I always hated the endings."

"You never read anything I wrote."

"I will now. I'll have nothing but time." He grinned, and it was the old grin, the gate-grin, the grin that had made me trust a stranger at the valley's edge because nobody faking gentleness bothers to be *funny*. "Felix Almeida. Last oath-bound swordspeaker. Terrible singer. Worse liar. Best hole-digger I ever employed."

"Employed?"

"Consider your wages paid in lessons." He sank. That's the only word. Not fell — *sank*, the way a swimmer lets the water take them, deliberate, easy, arms loose. The earth closed over him the way water closes, smoothly, without violence, and the last thing visible was his hand, rising out of the soil, palm open, offering — and then the snow above the hole shivered, and settled, and was only snow.

For a long moment I stood in a dead garden holding an empty space where a man had been, and I felt absolutely nothing, which is how I knew I was feeling everything, all of it at once, compressed past the point of sensation, a star collapsing.

Then the garden moved.

Not dramatically. A rosebush shivered off its snow. Grass came up through the drift in a slow green ripple, spreading outward from the filled-in hole like a dropped stone's rings, and the ripple reached the trellises and buds swelled along the bare canes, tight and green and impatient, and the snow slid away from the beds all on its own, retreating like a servant who'd misjudged the room. The smell hit me next — loam and sap and green growing things, thick enough to taste — and under it, faint, unmistakable, a lullaby. Rising out of the ground. Out of *him*. My mother's song, taken up in a new voice, hummed by roots, carried across the line like a lantern through a doorway.

The bottle in my hands went quiet.

Not silent — *quiet*, the difference between a door shut and a door held open. Warmth pulsed in the glass, steady as a heartbeat, and I understood: he was carrying her now. From inside. The way he'd promised.

"Show-off," I whispered, to the garden, wiping my face with a sleeve already destroyed beyond redemption.

The gate, when I lifted it — lift, don't shove — swung open onto the valley, gray and familiar and mine, and somewhere down the slope a light was moving in the dusk, and a bell was ringing the alarm the elders had sworn would never matter, and my mother's soul was humming in the ground beneath my feet, and I had a rite to perform and a grave to reach and possibly, if the elders had any sense at all, a few heads to remove from a few shoulders.

I stepped over the line. Magic stayed behind me, rooted at the border, singing.

I went home to finish the job.
