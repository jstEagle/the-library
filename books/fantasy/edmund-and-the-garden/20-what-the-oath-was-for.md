## 20. What the Oath Was For

The first thing I learned about teaching is that nobody warns you it's mostly watching people fall down.

"Again," I said, and Tam Vess went down again, his practice blade skittering across the frozen mud of the training yard, his dignity skittering after it. He got up the way the young do — all at once, offended by gravity — and spat and picked up the sword.

"You could just tell us the words," he said. "You know them. You've *used* them."

"I've used them wrong more times than right," I said. "The words are the last part. The words are what you say when everything else has already happened." I walked over and took the blade from him, turned it in my hands. It was a good practice sword, valley-made, honest oak and iron, no magic in it at all. That was the point. "Your feet knew where to go before your mouth did. Your mouth just panicked."

"My mouth didn't panic," Tam said. "My mouth said a word and nothing happened and *then* it panicked."

Around him, the other volunteers — eleven of them this morning, which was ten more than the first morning — shifted and grinned and pretended not to. Maren Hale, who was fifty-three years old and had outlasted two husbands and one flood, stood at the end of the line with her arms crossed like she was grading me. She had come on the second day and never missed a session since, and she had never once managed to make her blade sing, and she had never once complained about it either, which made her, in my private accounting, the bravest of the lot.

"The word isn't a spell," I told them, because I'd told them before and I'd tell them again, because that was teaching too. "It's a promise you make out loud. The oath our ancestors swore wasn't *please keep us safe.* It was *we will stand.* Everything else grew out of that. If you're standing when you speak, the sword hears you. If you're begging, it doesn't."

"And if we're scared?" asked little Odile Brandt, who was sixteen and had hands like her father the farrier and a temper like her mother the brewer.

"Then you're scared and standing," I said. "That's most of courage, as far as I can tell. Nobody's ever shown me the rest of it."

I set Tam's sword back in his hands and stepped back, and they ran the form again, and this time three blades hummed — not sang, not yet, but hummed, a low note like a struck fence wire — and Tam's was one of them, and the look on his face was the look I'd been chasing since the day the stranger died in the snow with my name in his mouth.

I had spent my whole life drilling forms no enemy would ever come for. Now there were enemies worth drilling for — or at least the possibility of them, patient and cold beneath the mountain, keeping their ledger with my name at the top of a fresh page — and instead of dread I felt something I hadn't felt in the valley before.

Purpose. Mine. Not inherited. Not sworn on my behalf by dead people who'd traded my grandmother's grandmother for safety. Chosen, at dawn, in the cold, with my feet in the mud.

* * *

The elders came to watch on the third week. They didn't announce it; they just appeared along the fence line the way elders do, wrapped in wool and disapproval, like crows who'd decided the scarecrow needed supervising.

Old Sera Voss stood at the front of them. She had forbidden me to leave the valley with one hand and burned nothing with the other, and she had spent the winter since looking like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to. Which, to be fair, she was. Without the bargain, the numbers were different now. No soul owed. No winter guaranteed to stop at the ridge. No certainty of anything except that whatever came for us next, we'd meet it standing — or we wouldn't.

"Felix Almeida," she said, when the volunteers had gone and I was stacking practice swords alone in the gray light. "A word."

"Several, probably," I said. "Go ahead."

She looked at the yard. The mud was churned dark where twenty feet had learned the same form. Someone had scratched tally marks into the fence post — Odile's doing, counting the days — and someone else had hung a strip of red cloth from the armory door, an old soldier's charm that meant *we are here.*

"When your great-great-grandmother signed the bargain," Sera said, "the elders of that day believed they were buying forever. A soul per generation seemed a small price against extinction. They were not wicked. They were tired, and frightened, and they chose the living over the dead." Her voice was flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this in front of a mirror and hated every pass. "We have repeated that choice without making it. Every year we kept the oath locked, the forms empty, the truth unspoken, we chose it again. We let you carry a sword you weren't allowed to use so that we wouldn't have to admit the sword was real."

"I know," I said.

"We are asking—" She stopped. Started again, which I had never seen an elder do. "The council will yield its seats. Authority over the valley's defense passes to whoever can hold it. The records open. All of them. Including the ones we hid from ourselves." She held out a heavy iron key, old enough that its teeth had gone soft with handling. "This opens the undercroft where the Founding Bargain is written out entire. Your family's copy. You should read it before anyone else does. There are things in it even we didn't know we were keeping."

I took the key. It was warm from her pocket, which felt like a small betrayal of how cold it should have been.

"Why now?" I asked. "You've had a whole winter to do this."

"Because the snow stopped at the ridge," she said, "and everyone in this valley saw where it stopped, and everyone knows why, and every day we keep the seats, we insult what you paid to make that true." Her jaw worked. "And because my granddaughter is in your volunteer line, Felix, and she hums her blade better than I ever hummed anything, and I would rather she learn from a teacher than inherit from a coward."

She left before I could answer, which was wise of her, because I don't think I'd have managed anything kinder than silence, and silence between us had done enough damage for one family of strangers.

That night I opened the undercroft. The bargain was there, ink brown with age, and I read it by lamplight with my breath fogging, and I learned the shape of the whole cage at last: the clause, the surety, the renewal, the long winter as collection notice. And at the bottom, in a different hand, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the page: *forgive us, we were afraid, we loved you anyway.*

My ancestors. Cowards and lovers, both at once, which is most people if you read them closely enough.

I closed the book and sat in the dark a while, and then I went home and slept better than I had any right to.

* * *

Spring came late but it came, and with it the garden — or what was left of it, which was a dead brown tangle at the valley's edge and, at the center of it, Edmund.

He had taken root in the way he'd chosen, and I still don't have better words for it than the ones I used that terrible afternoon: he was the garden now, alive and bound, his body gone into bark and soil and his self gone into everything growing. Where he'd stood, there was a young tree that hadn't existed before, silver-barked, leafless through the dead winter, and around it the ground stayed soft no matter how hard the frost bit.

The gardeners' guild — three women and a boy who had tended the valley's practical plots for generations — wanted nothing to do with the edge garden. Too strange, they said. Wrong season, they said. Dead, they said, and then couldn't quite meet each other's eyes, because everyone in the valley had heard the story by then, and stories make liars of us all when we repeat them flatly.

So I did it myself, at first. Pruning shears and a wheelbarrow and a stubbornness I recognized as newly mine. I cut back the winterkill and found, underneath, green. Not everywhere. But here and there — a shoot of something like rosemary that had no business surviving, a bulb swelling in ground that should have been iron, a vine climbing the young tree's trunk with small determined leaves.

"He's dreaming," I told the vine, because talking to plants was now a family tradition of a sort. "Dream loud, then."

And sometimes — I want to be careful how I say this, because I am a swordspeaker and not a poet, and precision matters — sometimes the garden answered. Not in words. In arrangements. I'd wake and find the new shoots had grown overnight into a pattern: a spiral, a wave-form, once unmistakably the opening stance of the third sword-form, rendered in stems. Birds gathered in the young tree in configurations that felt less like chance and more like punctuation. Once, after a bad night, after a dream of shelves and labels and quiet hours, I came out at dawn and the whole east bed had bloomed white, every flower facing the mountain, like a sentry line. Like a promise: *still here. Still watching. Still yours.*

I started leaving him reports. Silly, maybe. Necessary, definitely. I'd sit against the silver trunk with bread and tea and tell him about the volunteers, about Tam's humming blade, about Odile's temper finally finding its proper shape, about Maren Hale who still couldn't make her sword sing and had begun teaching the younger ones footwork instead, better than I could, because she'd watched forty years of valley fights that never happened and understood balance the way I understood grief — intimately, and from the inside.

"They yielded the seats," I told him one evening, the light going gold and long across the dead-and-living beds. "Sera handed me the key herself. I keep waiting to feel triumphant. Mostly I feel like a woman holding a very heavy key wondering who's going to steal it." I pulled a weed. "Maren says purpose is just inheritance you get to pick yourself. She says a lot of things like that. I'm going to put her on the council whether she likes it or not."

The leaves moved. There was no wind. I chose, as I had chosen all spring, to take it as agreement.

* * *

Midsummer, the first real test came, and it wasn't the court at all. It was wolves.

A hard winter up-mountain had pushed a pack down past the ridge — the ridge, the line where the long winter had stopped, the line the whole valley now watched the way sailors watch weather — and they took two sheep from the Brandt pasture and would have taken more. The old response would have been to bar the gates and wait, the way the valley had waited for four hundred years, safe and small and slowly forgetting why.

Instead, twelve of us stood the pasture wall at dusk. Volunteers. Farmers, a farrier's daughter, a brewer, a stonemason, me. And when the wolves came over the rise — lean, desperate, more frightened than frightening — Odile Brandt spoke the second form's word, and her blade *sang*, clear and high across the twilight, and six other blades hummed with it, and the sound rolled over the pasture like a bell being rung inside the world.

The wolves stopped. Looked at us. Looked at the singing steel, which is older magic than any court beneath any mountain, the plain animal truth that these creatures here will stand and bite back. And they turned, and flowed away up the slope, and were gone.

Nobody cheered. We all just stood there breathing, listening to the hum fade out of the steel, and then Maren Hale laughed — one short bark of a laugh — and said, "Well. That's the first time in my life the valley's fought back at anything," and Odile burst into tears and Tam clapped her on the shoulder so hard she nearly went over, and I stood at the center of it feeling something I had no form for, no word, no drilled stance.

It wasn't triumph. Triumph is for finishing things. This was the other thing — the thing that comes before all the unfinished work, the moment you understand the work is real and yours and endless.

That night I carried the report to the garden. Sat against the silver trunk in the dark with the stars coming out over the ridge, and told him all of it, the singing and the wolves and Odile crying and laughing at once.

"You'd have liked it," I said. "You'd have stood at the back being gentle at everybody." I rested my head against the bark. Under my ear, faint as a pulse, something moved. Sap, maybe. Or a heartbeat. Or a man dreaming at the bottom of a garden, keeping his promise the only way left to him. "Thank you," I said, because I'd never said it properly, because there hadn't been time, because there is never time. "For the door. For the summers. For the pieces of yourself I carried and didn't know. I'm still finding them. I find one and suddenly I'm brave in a place I used to be afraid. I don't know if that's me or you. I've decided it doesn't matter."

The east bed rustled. Every white flower turned toward me at once, and then, gently, turned back to the mountain.

Watch kept. Watch shared.

* * *

Autumn came early — of course it did; the court keeps its own calendar and it runs short — and with it the council ceremony, such as it was. No crowns. No oaths on stone. Just the seven elders standing down in the meeting hall while the whole valley watched, and Sera Voss reading the Founding Bargain aloud from beginning to end, every clause, every shameful surety, every *forgive us, we were afraid,* until the room had heard the whole shape of the cage and could never unknow it again.

Then she set the iron key on the table between the old seats and the new empty ones, and said, "The valley defends itself now. Come forward, whoever means to hold it."

They came forward. Maren Hale, scowling, dragged by her granddaughter. Tam Vess, grinning. Odile, solemn as a funeral. The stonemason, the brewer, the shepherd who'd lost the sheep and wanted standing say in what the walls were for. Twelve seats filled, and me at the center of them not because I was the last oath-bound swordspeaker but because I was the first teacher, which is a different office entirely and a harder one and the only one I've ever actually wanted.

Afterward, walking home through the first cold snap, I passed the meeting hall's old honor board, where the founders' names were carved, and I stopped, because there was space at the bottom. Blank space. Four hundred years of blank space, because no one had believed the valley would ever need another name carved there.

I didn't carve anything. Not yet. But I stood there a long time with my bare hand flat against the cold stone, learning its grain, the way you learn a blade before you speak to it.

* * *

The letter came at first snow — real snow, ordinary snow, the kind that falls because of weather and nothing else, and I stood in my doorway and laughed at it like a madwoman before I could help myself.

It wasn't a letter, exactly. It was a leaf. Silver-barked-tree leaf, out of season, unfallen, delivered somehow to my doorstep in the dead of the year. And across its surface, in veins and discolorations no gardener could have planned and no accident could have produced, in the careful script of a man who remembered every plant and no years:

*I dreamed of the shelf room. Something is missing from it that the steward has noticed. The birds brought me the shape of a seal breaking. Wake the sixth form, Felix. Teach it before midwinter. And plant something by the gate — the stranger deserves a name, even a made-up one. Even yours.*

I read it three times. Then I tucked it inside my coat, over my heart, where I kept the map and the key and every other inheritance I'd chosen, and I went to find Odile and Tam and Maren, because the sixth form was the one my ancestors had locked away hardest, the one the undercroft called *the answering stroke*, and I had a suspicion — growing all winter, fed by dreams of ledgers and patient courts — that we were going to need it sooner than midwinter.

Outside, the snow fell soft and honest on the valley, and somewhere beneath the mountain, a patient hand turned to a fresh page.

But that page was mine now too. And I intended to be standing when it was read.
