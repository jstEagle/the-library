## 21. The Court Beneath Waits

I dream in root-time now, which is to say slowly, which is to say honestly.

When I had legs, my thoughts ran ahead of me like water down a hillside — quick, shallow, always finding the easiest path. Now that I am oak and hawthorn and a stubborn rowan at the garden's heart, my thoughts go down instead of out. They seep. They take their time. They find the deep water.

And the deep water, it turns out, connects to everything.

I felt the steward's ledger turn before I heard it — felt it as a change in pressure far below, the way you feel a storm coming in your knees, if your knees are two hundred miles of mycelium. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in a court lit by bottled summers, a pen touched paper, and a new page opened, and on that page was written a name I have carried in my bark all winter like a splinter of sunlight:

*Felix Almeida.*

Courts are patient. That is not a threat; it is simply their nature, the way slowness is mine now. A court does not rage. Rage is for creatures with short lives and shorter memories. A court *files*. And somewhere below us, filed under losses recoverable, there is a page with her name on it, and the ink is still wet, and the wetness is the only thing about it that will ever hurry.

But I am getting ahead of myself. Root-time. Let me start where roots start: in the dark, with the cold, with the waiting.

* * *

The winter that fell on the valley was not weather. I want to be clear about that, because the elders — those dear, frightened, well-meaning gatekeepers of other people's secrets — spent the whole season telling each other it was an early snow, a hard frost, a bad year, as if the sky were merely clumsy. It was not clumsy. It was *collecting*. The long winter was the sound of a contract being read aloud, line by line, by something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

It failed. Felix saw to that. Felix, who walked into the Court of Quiet Hours carrying nothing but an oath she'd spoken backward until it became a lullaby, and walked out carrying her mother's soul and most of what was left of me, and refused every trade offered, including the one that would have bought her mother back at the price of returning me to a shelf.

She refused it without hesitating. I know because I was there, half-frozen, half-dissolved, watching her through eyes that were already forgetting how to blink. She said no the way she does everything — too loudly, too honestly, with her chin up and her sword-hand loose, ready to be wrong but never ready to be polite about it.

I loved her for it then. I love her for it now, from underneath, which is a stranger geography but the same country.

Here is what no one tells you about becoming a tree: it does not hurt. I expected it to hurt. Instead it was like being set down after a very long walk — every joint easing at once, every debt of standing suddenly forgiven. Edmund the man had been walking for three hundred years. Edmund the garden finally got to stop.

What they don't tell you either — what I am telling you now, because someone should — is that you keep dreaming. Trees dream. Or this one does. My dreams run on sap and season, and they are slow and enormous, and in them I hold the whole valley the way a hand holds a sleeping bird: carefully, and with a constant low astonishment that anything so small and warm would trust you.

All winter I held the valley. All winter the long winter pressed down on both of us, the court's collection notice written in white across the ridgeline, and all winter I pushed back the only way a garden pushes — patiently, cell by cell, keeping the thaw alive in the ground like a coal kept under ash.

The bargain broke when Felix gave her mother's soul back to the earth above her grave. I felt it break. It went through my roots like a bell through water. One moment the valley was a held breath three hundred years old; the next moment it exhaled, and the long winter stopped at the ridge as if it had hit glass, and the first thing I thought — the first undignified, unrooted, entirely human thing I thought — was:

*She did it. The reckless, stubborn, magnificent girl actually did it.*

The second thing I thought was: *the steward is writing her name down right now.*

Roots do not let you lie to yourself. That is their great cruelty and their great kindness. So let me say it plainly, the way I could never quite say it when I had a mouth: we won. And winning against a court is not the same as being safe from one. Those are different things, and the space between them is exactly wide enough for a ledger page.

* * *

Deep below the mountain — deeper than the season-rooms, deeper than the shelves of bottled souls, in an archive where the light comes from stored noons — the steward sat at a desk older than my first spring, and catalogued our theft.

I know this because the archive's floor is stone, and stone has roots too, if you are patient enough, and I have nothing now if not patience. My awareness reached down that far only once, and it cost me a month of dreaming to get there and another month to climb back, and what I found was so quiet it made the valley look like a marketplace.

No anger. That is what unsettled me most, and I was a creature who had spent centuries learning what unsettled meant. The steward did not rage at the loss of a soul-bottle, or the voided bargain, or the escape of the collateral's daughter with her mother's warmth tucked inside her coat. The steward simply wrote. The pen moved with the unhurried precision of something that has never once needed to hurry, because hurrying is for mortals, and courts have solved mortality the way farmers solve hunger — by keeping stock.

*One soul, female, returned to origin,* the steward wrote. *Claimed by rite. Bargain dissolved.*

Then, after a pause in which the stored noonlight dimmed by a degree — the court's version of a sigh:

*Note: subject displayed unregistered capability. Swordspeech, oath-keyed. Recommend observation.*

And then, on a fresh page, in the same patient hand, the letters forming one letter at a time like frost forming on a window:

*F. Almeida.*

That was all. No sentence. No sentence is the point. A court does not write sentences; it writes *entries*, and entries wait to become chapters, and chapters wait to become accounts settled. Somewhere in that archive there are pages three hundred years old with my own name on them, and I know exactly how little those pages have aged.

I pulled my awareness back up through the stone as fast as roots can travel, which is to say I began a journey I would finish sometime around the thaw, and all the way up I carried the image of that wet ink, and I made myself a promise in the old language, the one before names, the one roots speak:

*Grow faster.*

* * *

Spring came late. Of course it did. You cannot spend a winter as a collection notice and expect the ground to forgive you quickly.

Above me — above all of us — the snow lingered on the ridge into a month when the crocuses should have been gossiping, and the valley waited under its white shroud with the particular tension of a household after a funeral: life going on, but quietly, and everyone listening for something.

Felix came to the garden every day. I knew her footsteps before I knew anything else about the world — that particular stride, half-soldier's march and half-girl-skipping, the stride of someone who learned to drill alone in a graveyard and never fully saw the point of walking any other way. She would come through the gate — the new gate, the one the volunteers built from the pass timber — and she would stand among my dead brown branches, and she would talk.

She talked about the training yard. About the volunteers — the miller's daughter with quick wrists, the old shepherd who'd secretly wanted to fight his whole life and was furious with joy about it, the twins who bickered over footwork. She talked about swordspeech, which she was teaching now, properly, openly, the way it should always have been taught: not as an inheritance locked in one bloodline, but as a language anyone with the patience could learn to sing. She talked about the elders, who had yielded their seats with the grace of men handing over a heavy load they'd forgotten they were carrying, and about the valley's book, and about a grave beside the gate that she tended for a stranger whose name none of us ever learned.

And sometimes she talked about her mother. Not much. A sentence here, a memory there, offered to the air the way you leave bread out for birds — not expecting anything to come, just leaving it, because leaving it is its own kind of prayer.

I drank all of it. Every word went down through my bark and into my rings, and I grew them into myself, and if my buds swelled a day earlier wherever she stood, well. Trees are allowed their small vanities.

She couldn't hear me yet. That frustrated her enormously, and I confess I found her frustration delicious in a way I could never have admitted when I had a face capable of smirking. She would press her palm flat against my trunk — my trunk! I had a *trunk* — and demand answers, and I would give her everything I had: a creak of branch, a drop of sap, one memorable occasion on which I shed an entire limb directly onto the head of a volunteer who was teaching her students to prune incorrectly. (She laughed for a full minute. I have replayed that laugh through every ring I've grown since.)

But leaf-patterns and birdsong are slow alphabets, and she was a fast reader being asked to parse a book that turned one page per season. Patience, I wanted to tell her. Patience. I have three hundred years of practice and I'm telling you, it gets easier after the first century.

She would learn. She was already learning — I could feel it, the way her questions changed over the weeks, from *are you in there* to *what do you need* to, eventually, the question that made my sap run warm clear through a frosty April night: *what do you remember?*

Everything, Felix. I remember everything. That is the gift and the bill for what I am now. I remember your mother coming down this mountain with a sleeping child in her arms, fleeing the wrong direction, away from the court, choosing the cage of the valley over the shelf. I remember the bargain, and the garden I was before I was the keeper of it, and the rage they filed away from me, shelf by shelf, until what remained was gentleness — gentleness and the memory of rage, which is its own strange kind of strength. I remember the door grinding shut behind us with our way home inside it. I remember the climb. I remember giving you the truth of your mother's flight halfway up a mountainside in the finished winter, and I remember that you were furious, and I remember that you kept climbing anyway, singing the oath backward, keeping two souls warm with nothing but your voice and your stubbornness.

I remember that you forgave me. Slowly. In the way of thaws.

And I remember — this is the part I hold deepest, the part I grow my strongest rings around — I remember the moment you chose to stay. Not because the bargain bound you. Not because exile or duty or inheritance demanded it. You stood in a valley that no longer owed you anything and that you no longer owed, and you looked at the graves and the drills and the frightened elders and the broken garden, and you said, in effect, *mine.* You signed your full name in the valley's book. You planted a stranger's unnamed grave beside my gate. You stayed the way people stay when a place stops being a sentence and becomes a home.

You stayed the way I rooted. Freely. Which is the only way anything down here or up there truly counts.

* * *

But.

There is always a *but*, and roots have taught me that pretending otherwise is how gardens die. So let me tell you about the last thing I saw in the deep archive, before I climbed back up toward the light, because it matters, and because I have decided that honesty is the whole of my remaining job description.

As my awareness withdrew from that stone floor, that patient lamplit quiet, I brushed against the edge of the steward's desk. And there, stacked in a neat column beside the ledger, I saw the other pages. The future pages. Blank, all of them — except that they were not blank, not quite. Each one bore a faint watermark, pressed into the paper at the making, the way courts watermark everything: a small crest, a family crest, carved long ago into a door beneath the mountain.

Her crest. Her family's. Page after page after page of them, blank and waiting, printed years in advance, the way a printer prints a run of invitations before he knows who will answer.

The court had always known, you see. Had always known that a bargain breaks, that souls get claimed, that collateral grows opinions. The pages weren't prepared for punishment. They were prepared for *correspondence*. For whatever Felix Almeida chose to do next, the court had already set aside the paper for it.

That is not menace, exactly. Menace is hot; this was room temperature. It was worse than menace. It was *accommodation*. Somewhere below, something vast and unhurried had looked at the girl who stole back her mother's soul and broke a three-hundred-year contract, and had responded not with wrath but with stationery.

I have been afraid many ways in my long life. I have been afraid of avalanches and bandits and doors and my own reflection in the season-rooms' polished floors. But I had never, until that moment, been afraid of being *expected*.

So I did what roots do. I took that fear down into my deepest rings, and I wrapped it in springwood, and I let it feed me. Fear makes excellent fertilizer, if you're honest about it. And I turned my growth upward, toward the thaw, toward her, toward the training yard where the miller's daughter was learning to make a blade sing, toward the valley that was learning — clumsily, bravely, for the first time in three hundred years — to defend itself honestly rather than survive on a purchased lie.

Because that is the answer to a patient enemy. Not haste. Haste is the court's game; they have infinite time and we do not, and racing them is losing. The answer is *depth*. Grow deeper than they can dig. Root wider than they can file. Become so thoroughly, so stubbornly *alive* — a whole valley of swordspeech and spring planting and children learning forms in the mud — that no ledger page, however patiently ruled, can find the edge of you to write on.

They are patient. Fine. So is a forest.

* * *

The last frost broke on a morning in late April, and I felt it happen in every twig at once — that loosening, that green insistence, the whole underground network of the valley waking and stretching and saying *now*.

Felix came through the gate at dawn, as she always did, and stopped, and I felt her stop, the way you feel a held breath.

My rowan was budding. My hawthorn was budding. Along the border of the garden where the long winter had stopped at the ridge like a wave frozen mid-break, the first green was showing — not the desperate, borrowed green of the impossible garden I used to keep, magic-fed and out of season, but honest green. Slow green. Green that had earned itself.

She walked to my trunk and laid her palm against the bark, and this time she didn't ask *are you in there*. She just stood, and breathed, and watched a bud unfurl at the height of her heart — one bud, opening petal by petal while she waited, because she had finally learned my alphabet's first word, which is *wait*.

And far below us — far below the thaw, below the graves and the training yard and the valley's open book — I felt the steward dip a pen, and turn to a page bearing her name, and write the first entry of the new account. I could not read it from this distance. I did not need to. Courts begin every ledger the same way.

They begin with *received*.

Above, the sun cleared the ridge, and the garden bloomed, and Felix Almeida — soulless of power, whole of heart, teacher of swordspeech, planter of strangers' graves, signer of her own name — rolled up her sleeves and started the day's work, whistling a lullaby she didn't know she'd finished.

Let them write. We would give them something worth the ink.
