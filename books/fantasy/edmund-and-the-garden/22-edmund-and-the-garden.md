## 22. Edmund and the Garden

The first thing you should know about a garden that has eaten a man is that it does not behave like other gardens.

Other gardens grow in rows. They mind their business. They put out blossoms in spring and drop them in autumn like well-mannered citizens paying their taxes. My garden blooms out of season, leans toward me when I walk past as if it wants to be spoken to, and once — I swear this on my oath, which is to say on nothing at all now, since I no longer have one — rearranged its whole eastern bed overnight so that the foxgloves spelled something that might have been *good morning* or might have been *you're late*, depending on how charitable I was feeling at dawn.

I have decided to feel charitable about it. It costs nothing, and Edmund spent enough on my behalf already.

* * *

One year. That's what it's been since the door beneath the mountain ground shut behind us, since my mother's soul rose from her grave and finished a lullaby she'd left half-sung for twenty years, since the long winter stopped at the ridge like a dog called home mid-lunge. One year since Edmund knelt down in his own dead garden and took root.

I still don't know if I watched a miracle or a funeral. Some mornings it's both before breakfast.

He had explained it to me on the climb, back when we were both too frozen to lie convincingly. The valley's boundary — the line where magic cannot follow — would not let a soul carried by an outsider cross. It had never been designed to let anything cross; it was a wall, and walls don't care whether what they're keeping out is a curse or a cure. But the valley itself could carry things inward. The soil. The roots. Anything that became part of the valley's own body.

"I was the garden once," he'd said, breath steaming, hands blue, voice as patient as ever. "It isn't so bad, being a place. You get to hold things."

"You'll die," I said.

"I'll *stay*," he said, with the mild correction of a man who had filed away his own rage centuries ago and knew exactly where he'd put it. "There's a difference. You taught me that, actually. You stayed in this valley your whole life and nobody ever called you dead."

I didn't argue. Arguing with Edmund was like arguing with weather — technically possible, spiritually pointless. And besides, some part of me had known since the court of quiet hours, since I saw him burn decades of himself to hold a door open against a flood of stolen spring, that he had been spending himself all along. He was simply choosing, at last, where the coin went.

So he knelt. He pressed his palms into the frozen earth of the garden he'd kept green through every impossible winter, and he said something in a language I didn't know but recognized anyway — it had the shape of my oath, the cadence of it, the way a key and a lock share a silhouette. And the ground took him the way ground takes rain: completely, without ceremony, without any drama whatsoever.

The snow around him melted in a circle ten feet wide. By morning there were crocuses.

By spring there was a garden.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about talking to a man who is now approximately four acres of perennials: you get very good at reading leaves.

His dreams come up through the roots and out into the canopy, and if you know how to look — and I have had a year to learn, which makes me possibly the world's only expert in a field of study with a population of one — the patterns say things. A spiral of new growth means contentment. Elm leaves curling inward means he dreamed of the court again, of shelves and labels and the steward's soft, patient voice cataloging losses without anger. Once, in high summer, every apple tree in the garden dropped its fruit in a single hour, and I sat down among them and cried, because I knew what that pattern meant. He had dreamed of my mother. Of the woman who walked down the mountain carrying a sleeping child, the child who grew up to be me, and of the silence he'd kept about it until a frozen mountainside wrung the truth out of him.

Forgiveness took longer than the climbing did. But apples ripen. So do I.

Mostly, though, the dreams are small ones. Ordinary ones. He dreams of watering cans and of the particular satisfaction of a weed coming up roots-and-all, and the garden expresses these opinions in birdsong, and I drink my morning tea under his largest plum tree and listen to a wren deliver what I can only assume is a horticultural complaint, and I answer it, because I am not proud, and because the alternative is talking to no one, and I have done enough solitary duty for one lifetime.

"Tell him the beans need staking," I tell the wren.

The wren tells him. The next day, the bean poles have migrated three feet to the left, entirely on their own, into a configuration that is either better support or a sardonic comment on my technique. With Edmund it is always both.

* * *

The valley itself has changed less than I expected, which is to say it has changed exactly as much as people do when no one is forcing them anymore.

Without the bargain, we survive honestly now, which mostly means we survive clumsily. The elders gave up their seats in the autumn — not all at once, nothing so dramatic, but in the slow way that certainty dies when it meets a winter that stopped at the ridge because a girl refused a trade. Maren went first, bless her, standing up in the meeting hall and saying, "We told her the debt was hers to pay and then forbade her to pay it or refuse it. I am old and I was wrong and I would like to stop being wrong while I can still remember being right about anything." Then she sat down next to me and asked if I'd teach her daughter sword-forms.

So I teach. Swordspeech, real swordspeech, the kind that woke in me on the far side of the pass singing through steel — except here's the thing I learned in the year since: it doesn't sleep in the valley anymore. Not entirely. The bargain broke the wall's reason for being, and magic, which turns out to be less like water and more like ivy, has begun finding its way over. Slowly. Tentatively. The children's blades hum when they get a form right, faint as a struck glass, and their faces when it happens are the reason I get up in the morning, or one of them, anyway. The other reasons include tea, and the garden, and the fact that no one in this valley will ever again be collateral for anything.

We drill for enemies that may yet come. The court beneath waits — I know it does; Edmund's roots go deep enough to feel the patience down there, vast and unhurried, a ledger with a fresh page bearing my name. Let it wait. We are not hiding anymore. There is a difference between a cage and a keep, and I intend for this valley to learn which one it lives in.

* * *

But I said I'd tell you about the grave.

The stranger — the dying man from the pass, frostbitten and wrong, who pressed a bloodstained map into my hands and died speaking a name no one used — never got a name. I searched for a year. I asked the broker in the town of sold names, who examined my coin and my face and told me, gently for a man who traffics in other people's pasts, that some debts arrive unmarked. I asked travelers on the road. I asked the map itself, which had nothing further to say, having delivered its one message the way maps do.

In the end I stopped looking for his name and started looking at what he'd given me instead. A stranger who died in the snow so that a girl he'd never met could steal her mother back from a court of quiet hours. Whatever his name was, his work was named after him from the start. It was mine.

So today I planted his grave beside the gate.

Not in the graveyard up the hill with the tidy rows and the elders' old stone — beside the gate, where everyone entering the garden passes him, where the first thing you meet on your way in is a man who paid for someone else's homecoming. I dug the hole myself, which took most of the morning, because the garden's soil is rich and stubborn and, I suspect, opinionated. I set the stone myself too, rough granite from the ridge, and I chiseled the only words I had:

*HERE LIES A MAN WHOSE NAME WE NEVER LEARNED, WHO CARRIED A MAP AND A DEBT THAT WERE NOT HIS, AND WHO DIED SO THAT SOMEONE ELSE COULD GO HOME.*

Then, because it seemed right, and because I have learned from a rooted man that staying is its own kind of speech, I added:

*HE IS HOME NOW.*

The wren sang something from the plum tree while I worked. When I stood back and looked at the fresh-turned earth, I noticed — and I want it noted that I checked twice, because I have been fooled before — that the border plants along the gate had, sometime between yesterday and this morning, leaned inward slightly, framing the new grave like cupped hands.

"Thank you," I said to the garden.

The foxgloves rustled. It might have been wind. It might have been *you're welcome*. I have decided to feel charitable about it.

* * *

Which brings me, I suppose, to the book.

Every valley keeps a book; ours is older than the meeting hall, older than the elders' seats, a fat ledger bound in leather gone dark with a century of hands. For generations it recorded one thing above all others: who was born inside, who married inside, who was buried inside. Who belonged by blood, by bargain, by the accident of a wall that kept everything out including hope. My name has been in it since birth — *Felix Almeida, born to Sera Almeida*, entered in a clerk's careful hand, an inheritance I never asked for, a line in a ledger written by people who were also lines in someone else's ledger, all the way down to the court beneath the mountain.

Today I signed it again.

Maren held the book open for me on the meeting hall table, and half the valley crowded in to watch, because a valley that survived nearly being sealed under ice forever will find occasion to celebrate almost anything, including paperwork. The children came too, my students, still smelling of drill-yard dust, and the youngest — Tam, seven years old, the one whose blade hums loudest — asked me in the piercing whisper of the very young whether signing a book hurt.

"Only if you sign it lying," I said.

I dipped the pen. I thought about what to write. Under my old entry, in my own hand, larger than the clerk's careful script, I wrote:

*Felix Almeida. Swordspeaker. Daughter of Sera, who is free. Keeper of the garden at the edge, with Edmund, who stays. I was born here. I choose here. Let the record show the difference.*

Then I signed it, and the pen scratched, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was the loudest silence I have ever heard, and then Maren laughed and someone brought out cider and the moment dissolved into the ordinary beautiful chaos of people who intend to keep living.

* * *

Evening now. I'm writing this at the garden gate with my back against the warm stone, because the stone is warm — the whole garden runs a degree or two warmer than the valley, always has, even in the dead of last winter, and I have stopped pretending I don't know why. The stars are out. The long winter sits beyond the ridge where it belongs, a story we tell children along with the one about the man who became a garden, and the two stories are the same story, and someday I will tell them properly, beginning to end, to anyone who asks.

For now, the wren has gone quiet. The beans are staked. The stranger's grave has fresh flowers on it that I did not put there, white ones, blooming out of season, because of course they are.

And from deep under the flowerbeds, faint as a struck glass, I feel it — not words, not yet, but the shape of contentment rising through the roots like sap, like a lullaby finally finished, like a hand resting briefly on my hair.

"Goodnight, Edmund," I say.

The foxgloves rustle. The gate stands open, the way it always will.

I stay. Not because I must. Because it's mine — and, at last, I am its.
