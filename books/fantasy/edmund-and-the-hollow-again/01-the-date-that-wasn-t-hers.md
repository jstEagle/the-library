## 1. The Date That Wasn't Hers

The key to the bookshop's back room had not turned for eleven years, and it did not turn easily now.

Jonah Eastwick braced her shoulder against the door of her grandfather's old shop and leaned into the stubborn iron lock, breath fogging in the cold that pooled in the stairwell like something poured. The key was hers by right — the shop itself had been hers since the reading of the will, along with its debts, its dust, and its reputation as the most disreputable den of paper in Thornfell. But right and willingness were different animals, and she had been unwilling to open this particular room since the day they buried him.

*Just inventory,* she told herself, the way she'd told herself every night this week. *Count what's worth selling, burn what isn't, be done before the first snow.*

Thornfell took its winters seriously. The town sat at the knee of the mountains like a beggar at a rich man's gate, and when the passes closed — and they always closed — the smugglers' routes closed with them, which meant Jonah's other livelihood closed too. She had perhaps three weeks of good weather left to sort through a dead man's secrets, and she had spent six days of them finding reasons not to climb these stairs.

Tonight she had run out of reasons. Or rather, tonight the reasons had run out of her.

The lock gave with a crack like a knuckle popping, and the door swung inward on a smell that stopped her heart for half a beat: pipe smoke and leather and the particular dry sweetness of old vellum. Eleven years sealed, and still the room smelled like him. Like the inside of his coat when she was small enough to ride on his shoulders, before she learned that her grandfather's kindness had edges, and that one of those edges was a locked room he never explained and she never asked about.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, letting the dark adjust around her. Moonlight came thin through a single high window, laying a pale stripe across a desk buried under ledgers, across shelves sagging with books whose spines had gone the color of old teeth. A chair pushed back from the desk at an angle, as if he'd risen from it mid-thought and simply never come back down. Which, in a way, he hadn't.

"You're being ridiculous," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "It's a room. It has books in it. You've robbed better rooms than this."

That was true, at least. Jonah Eastwick had picked locks in border houses where the owners slept upstairs with loaded crossbows, had lifted ledgers out from under the noses of Lowmark customs men, had once talked her way past a tollgate guard while a stolen reliquary ticked away inside her coat like a second heart. She was, by trade and temperament, a woman who opened doors other people wanted shut.

So why did her hands shake now?

Because this door wasn't locked against thieves, some quiet part of her answered. It was locked against *you.*

She lit the lamp on the desk — the wick caught on the third try, the oil still good after all these years, which meant he'd filled it recently before he died, which meant he'd expected someone to come up here eventually. Her, probably. The thought settled over her like a wet cloak.

She began with the desk, because beginning anywhere else felt too much like trespassing. Ledgers mostly, in his cramped sideways hand: purchases, sales, sums owed and forgiven. And then, beneath the bottom drawer's false floor — because of course there was a false floor; she came from a family that put false floors under false floors — a single book wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed string.

It was heavier than it looked. When she unwound the string and peeled back the cloth, the lamplight slid across a cover of dark green leather gone soft with age, stamped with no title, no maker's mark, nothing but a small blind-embossed device near the spine: a gate, standing open, with a road running through it.

Jonah knew books. Knew them the way a farrier knows horses — by their mouths, their tempers, what they'd cost you if you handled them wrong. This one was old. Not old the way Thornfell was old, all timber and habit, but old the way the mountains were old, old in a way that made her fingertips prickle. The pages were ragged-edged vellum, dense with a hand she didn't recognize, ink gone brown as dried blood.

And on the flyleaf, in a different, older script:

*A true account of things to come, set down in the year 1123, for the instruction of those who will need it.*

A hundred years ago. Prophecy books were ten a penny in the border towns — every hedge-witch and defrocked priest seemed to have scribbled one, and collectors in Lowmark paid good silver for the convincing ones. Jonah had smuggled a dozen. She'd read most of them out of professional curiosity and found nothing inside but fog: *a king shall come, a crown shall fall, the rivers shall run backward,* all of it vague enough to fit any year and any fool who wanted it to.

She flipped the book open at random, meaning to give it thirty seconds and move on.

The page fell open on its own weight, as if the binding had a favorite place, and her eye went straight to a passage marked with a thin line of red thread sewn through the margin.

*In the hundredth winter, when the last coach comes down before the snows, a stranger shall walk into Thornfell carrying the last map of Vellharrow. With him shall come the finding or the ending of all things.*

Jonah frowned. Vellharrow. She turned the word over and found nothing behind it — no country, no duchy, no ruined hold she'd ever heard of, and Jonah had heard of everything worth hearing of between here and the sea. Smugglers kept better geography than kings. There was no Vellharrow. There had never been a Vellharrow.

Probably. The not-quite-sureness of it bothered her more than the sentence did.

She read on.

*And the beast-speaker of Thornfell, daughter of the shop, keeper of small voices, shall know the stranger by his burden, and the door shall know her by her name.*

Her name.

Not *a* name. Not *the name of one yet unborn.* Not the usual prophecy fog. The next line, in the same brown ink, written by a man a century dead:

*Her name is Jonah.*

The lamp flame leaned hard to one side, though there was no draft.

Jonah sat very still. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters the way a dog worries a bone, and somewhere down the street a cart rattled over cobbles and faded away, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary evening went on happening, indifferent to the fact that a dead man's book had just said her name in a dead man's voice.

"Trick," she said finally. Her voice came out flat and steady, and she was grateful for it. "Somebody wrote it in later. Ink's fresh. It's a trick."

But even as she said it she was tilting the page toward the lamp, angling the light low across the surface, the way she checked suspected forgeries. The ink lay *in* the vellum, sunk deep, oxidized to the same brown as the rest. No scratch-through, no ghost of erasure. Whoever wrote *her name is Jonah* had written it a hundred years ago, before her grandfather was born, before her mother was born, before anyone alive could have known there would be a Jonah Eastwick sitting in a locked room in Thornfell reading about herself by lamplight.

Unless it was a common name. Unless it was coincidence. Unless the world was full of Jonahs, and this one belonged to somebody else.

She almost believed it. She wanted to believe it, the way you want to believe the noise in the night is only the house settling.

Then the cat spoke.

Moss had followed her up — of course he had; he considered himself supervisor of all her questionable decisions — and now he flowed out of the shadow of the doorframe and onto the desk, a big grey tom with a torn ear and opinions. He was her grandfather's cat before he was hers, and like everything else the old man had left behind, he came with unexplained habits. He talked more than most cats, in his way: chirps and trills and the long complaining vowels cats use on people who understand them. Jonah had spoken beast since she was four years old, and in twenty years she had learned that animals mostly said the same few things. *Food. Warmth. Go away. Come here. Something moved in the grass and I don't like it.*

What Moss said now was none of those things.

He sat down in front of the open book, put one paw deliberately on the edge of the page — a gesture so nearly human it raised the hair on her arms — fixed her with his yellow eyes, and said, in the clear, level tone beasts reserve for absolute truths:

*"Do not answer the knock."*

Jonah's mouth went dry. "What knock?"

Moss looked at the window. Then at the stairs. Then back at her, and repeated it, slower, the way you'd speak to someone being deliberately slow: *"When it comes. Do not answer."*

"Cats don't warn about knocks." She heard how thin that sounded. "Cats warn about dogs. About weather. What are you—"

But Moss had turned his head toward the high window again, ears forward, tail wrapped tight around his feet, and Jonah realized with a cold slide in her stomach that he wasn't listening to the street below. He was listening to the road. The coach road. The one that came down out of the pass, the one that would carry the last traffic of the season before the snows shut Thornfell off from the world.

The last coach. *When the last coach comes down before the snows.*

"No," she said, to the cat, to the book, to the whole arrangement of the evening. "No, I'm not doing this. It's a smuggler's trick, it's a collector's forgery, it's my grandfather having a joke from beyond the grave, and I am going to close this book, lock this room, and go home and sleep like a person with sense."

Moss said nothing. Cats are terrible at arguing, but excellent at waiting, and he waited with the patience of a creature who had watched her grandfather fill that lamp and knew, somehow, that she would read to the end.

She told herself she wouldn't. She read to the end.

The final passage on the page was shorter than the rest, and written larger, as if the hand had pressed harder:

*The door opens for two and keeps one. Let the one who reads this remember: what is given freely cannot be taken back, and what is traded will curdle. The mountain does not bargain. The mountain only asks.*

Below it, in her grandfather's cramped sideways hand — unmistakably his, she'd have known it in the dark, she'd have known it drowned — a single line added in fresher ink:

*If you found this, little wren, then it's your winter. I'm sorry. I ran out of time to tell you properly.*

Jonah put the book down very carefully, the way you put down something that might be sleeping.

Her grandfather had called her *little wren* exactly twice in her life: once when she was seven and brought a fledgling back from the dead with nothing but attention and want, and once the day before he died, gripping her wrist with strength he shouldn't have had, saying *wren, listen to me, when the time comes, don't let them make you trade—* and then coughing, and waving it off, and asking for tea instead.

She had thought it was the fever talking.

Down in the street, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she had been dreading without knowing she dreaded it: wheels on the frozen mud of the pass road. The rattle and groan of a coach working downhill in the dark, late, too late, the last coach of the season pushing hard to beat the snow.

Moss rose, stretched, and looked at her.

*"There it is,"* he said. *"The knock."*

"It's a coach," Jonah whispered. "Coaches aren't knocks."

*"Everything's a knock,"* said the cat, *"if it's looking for you."*

And far below, at the foot of the stairwell, in a shop that had been locked and dark and empty for eleven years, someone tried the front door handle — once, gently, the way a guest knocks — and the sound came up through the floorboards like a question asked in a language Jonah had never learned but recognized anyway.

* * *

She should have stayed in the dark. Every instinct she'd sharpened in fifteen years of smuggling said *stay down, stay quiet, let the night pass, decide in daylight.* Daylight made everything smaller. Daylight was where tricks went to die.

Instead Jonah found herself descending the stairs with the prophecy book tucked inside her coat, against her ribs, warm as a stolen thing, with Moss flowing ahead of her through the shadows like spilled mercury, and with absolutely no story she could have told anyone about why.

The shop's front room was a museum of her grandfather's respectable face: fine bindings behind glass, first editions arranged to look accidental, prices chalked high enough to keep out everyone but collectors and liars. In the gloom it all floated grey and ghostly, and the figure at the front door was silhouetted against the frosted pane — tall, travel-worn, hat in one hand, snow already melting on his shoulders.

He knocked again. Softly. Politely. As though the shop were occupied, as though he *knew* it was occupied, as though he had walked a hundred miles to stand precisely here on precisely this night.

Jonah stopped at the edge of the lamplight, one hand on the banister, and made herself breathe.

Through the frosted glass she called out, keeping her voice bored, border-flat: "Shop's closed. Closed for years. Try the inn."

A pause. Then, through the door, a voice — tired, courteous, and carrying something underneath it that made the hairs on her arms rise, something that sounded almost like relief:

"I'm sorry to trouble you. My name is Edmund." A pause, and then, almost apologetically, the words she had read by lamplight not ten minutes ago, arriving at her door in a stranger's mouth: "I'm looking for Thornfell. And I've lost my map — or rather, my map has lost its country. Perhaps you could help me find it."

Against her ribs, the old book seemed to grow warmer.

On the stairs beside her, Moss said quietly, *"Too late now, wren. You answered."*
