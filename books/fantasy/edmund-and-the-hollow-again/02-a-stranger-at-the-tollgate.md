## 2. A Stranger at the Tollgate

The last coach of the season came up the Thornfell road at dusk, and Jonah knew it was the last because the horses told her.

*Cold coming down off the mountain tonight,* said the near-side bay, a mare named Bess who had carried the mail for eleven years and complained about it in the flat, put-upon tone of an old soldier. *Cold like teeth. Driver wants to be home. Wants his stew.*

Jonah stood at the tollgate with her collar up and her hands in her pockets, collecting the winter road-tax from travelers too tired to argue about it. It was honest work, or honest enough — her grandfather had held the toll concession before her, and his father before him, back into the gray stretch of years where Eastwicks did what Eastwicks had always done: kept the gate, kept the shop, kept their mouths shut about the things they could hear.

She'd slept badly. The cat's words from the night before sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

*The book is awake. Do not answer it.*

Cats spoke in warnings often enough — *dog, dog, dog; rain; the tall one kicks* — but never like that. Never with weight behind it, never as though the animal were passing along something older than itself. Jonah had spent half the night telling herself she'd dreamed it, and the other half lying awake listening to the mice in the walls, who said nothing at all, which was somehow worse. Mice always had opinions.

Now the coach came rattling through the blue dusk with its lamps lit early, and Bess's voice reached her before the wheels did.

*Tired. So tired. He smells wrong, the man inside. Not bad-wrong. Old-wrong. Like a room that's been shut.*

Jonah straightened. "What man?"

Bess flicked an ear at her — the driver wouldn't notice, but the mare knew perfectly well that Jonah asked questions — and said nothing more, because horses were like that. They gave you the shape of a thing and left you to fill in the rest.

The coach ground to a halt in a spray of road-grit and frozen mud. The driver, a heavyset man called Ollis with a beard full of last week's porridge, leaned down and jerked a thumb at the passenger door. "One through," he said. "Going all the way to Thornfell proper. Says he's got business."

"Business," Jonah repeated. "At the end of October. On the last coach."

"That's what he says." Ollis lowered his voice without lowering it enough for anyone to miss. "Paid double not to be put out at the crossroads. Double, Jonah. You know what kind of man pays double?"

"A fool."

"Or a rich one." Ollis spat thoughtfully into the ruts. "Same thing, mostly."

The door swung open, and the stranger climbed down.

He was younger than she expected — mid-twenties, maybe, with the kind of face that looked like it had been assembled from spare parts: sharp nose, soft mouth, dark hair cut by someone in a hurry. His coat was good wool gone threadbare at the cuffs, and he carried everything he owned in two bags, one leather and one canvas, slung across his body so that neither hand was ever quite free. He moved like a man who'd been sitting still for three days and whose bones hadn't gotten the news yet.

But it was his eyes that stopped her. Not their color — gray, ordinary gray — but the way they went over the tollgate, the road, the tree line, the mountain shoulder rising black behind it all, quick and thorough, the way a merchant counts coin. Mapping it. She'd seen surveyors do it, engineers, army scouts. This man did it the way other people breathed.

"Evening," he said. His voice was roughened by travel but the accent underneath was lowland, southern, educated. "Toll's what — four coppers?"

"Six, after first frost." She held out her hand. "Road tax goes up when the ice does. Gate rules."

"Gate rules," he agreed, and counted six coppers into her palm without complaint, which marked him immediately as either very rich or very tired, and possibly both.

Ollis was already climbing back onto the box, muttering about stew. The coach lurched, rolled, gathered itself, and went on south toward the low country with its lamps swinging, and then there was just the two of them and the mountain and the wind coming down off it like a warning.

"You're walking into Thornfell on foot," Jonah said. "It's three miles. There's no inn between here and town except the Drowned Lamb, and Marta charges non-locals double."

"I heard." He shifted his bags. "I can pay double."

"Everyone can pay double. That's why she charges it." She looked him over again, and this time let herself do it properly, the way her grandfather had taught her: hands, eyes, baggage, boots. The hands were ink-stained along the right fingers. The boots were city boots, badly worn at the heels, wrong for mountains. The bags were ordinary except that the canvas one rode against his chest rather than his hip, the way a man carries something he cannot afford to lose. "You're not a trader. Traders ask about roads. You're not a pilgrim — pilgrims ask about shrines. What are you?"

Something flickered across his face — surprise, quickly banked, and under it something that might have been relief. "A cartographer," he said.

"There's nothing left around here to map."

"That," he said, "is exactly what I've come to check."

The wind gusted down the pass, carrying the first dry tick of snow with it, and every hair on Jonah's arms stood up under her sleeves. Behind them, in the bare hedge along the tollhouse, a wren began to scold — *cold cold cold, go go go* — and then stopped mid-word, as if someone had put a hand over its beak.

The stranger didn't notice. But Jonah did, and the stone in her chest turned over.

* * *

They walked the three miles together because there was nothing else to do with him. The road dropped through bare birch and old field-walls, and the snow-tick thickened until it wasn't ticks anymore but real flakes, fat and slow, the first true snow of the year. Jonah kept half a step ahead and half an ear cocked, and the animals along the way kept up their usual commentary — crows arguing over a dead fox, a farm dog giving them the once-over (*stranger, stranger, smells like paper*) — and none of it meant anything, and she made herself believe that.

His name was Edmund. He offered it at the second mile-marker with the air of a man paying out rope slowly. Edmund Vane, he said, late of Carrowmere, lately of everywhere else. He asked careful questions — how long the passes stayed open, whether the high villages emptied in winter, who owned the land above the tree line — and answered none of hers, and Jonah found herself doing the thing she hated, which was liking him despite it. He had a dry, self-deprecating humor that surfaced sideways, like fish rising. When she told him the Drowned Lamb's landlady watered the beer, he said, gravely, "Then I'll drink fast, before it notices it's been cheated."

By the time the town lights showed below them — Thornfell stacked up its hillside like crockery, chimneys smoking, the shop-windows already shuttered against winter — the snow had settled into a steady whisper, and Edmund had stopped twice to look back up the valley, and both times Jonah had caught him doing it.

"What's up there?" she asked finally. "Whatever it is you're checking."

He was quiet for six or seven steps. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off.

"Do you know how many countries there are?" he said.

"That sounds like the opening of a terrible sermon."

"Humor me. How many?"

"I don't know. Forty? Fifty? Depends who's counting and what they call a country."

"Mm." He nodded as if she'd passed a test. "And maps are supposed to show all of them. That's the whole point of a map — it's a promise. Everything that exists is on it somewhere." He glanced at her, and in the light spilling down from the high windows of the first farmhouse, his gray eyes looked almost silver. "My family made maps for a hundred and forty years. Six generations. And in all that time, we kept one promise above the others."

"Which was?"

"That there was one country we would never put on paper." He smiled, and it was a strange smile, tired and frightened and proud all at once. "Because it isn't lost, Mistress Eastwick. Lost things can be found by accident. It's hidden. And my family hid it."

The name arrived in her head before he said it, arriving the way the cat's warning had arrived, from somewhere outside her own thinking:

*Vellharrow.*

"Vellharrow," Edmund said.

Jonah stopped walking.

The world did not stop with her — the snow went on falling, the farmhouse dog went on barking its bored two-note bark at the horizon, smoke went on rising — but something underneath the world went very quiet, very fast, like a held breath. Because she had heard that word. She had read it, eleven hours ago, in a locked book in a locked shop, in a list of names written in a dead woman's hand a century ago, and she had told herself it was a smuggler's trick, a cipher, a nonsense-word, anything.

"I don't know that name," she said.

"No," Edmund agreed mildly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point."

"It's not on any map."

"No map shows it. No chronicle mentions it after the winter of—" he paused, calculating, "—ninety-nine years ago. No song, no ruin, no grave. A whole country, Jonah — may I call you Jonah? — a whole country with rivers and towns and, by my family's account, some forty thousand people, and it went out of the world like a candle pinched between finger and thumb. And everyone alive simply agreed, without ever discussing it, that there had never been such a place."

"That's not possible."

"No," he said cheerfully. "It isn't. And yet."

They had come to the top of the town steps, where the street lanterns began, and the snow fell through their light in slow gold spirals. Below them Thornfell glowed and muttered to itself. Somewhere a fiddle was being tuned badly. Ordinary life, going on. Jonah gripped the cold iron rail and made herself breathe.

"Why are you telling me this?" she said. "You met me four miles ago."

"Because you're the tollkeeper, and the tollkeeper knows everyone who comes and goes, and I need someone who knows the passes." He shifted the canvas bag higher on his chest. "And because—"

"Show me the map."

She hadn't planned to say it. The words came out of her the way the wren's scolding came out of the wren, and she heard her own voice as if from a little distance, and hated it, and could not stop it. "You have it with you. In the canvas bag. Show me."

Edmund went still. Not alarmed — worse than alarmed. Watchful. The way a man goes still when a trap he half-expected turns out to be real.

"Why would you think—"

"Show me," Jonah said, "or I'll wake the constable and tell him you're smuggling something, and he'll open that bag on the customs table, and whatever your family promised, it won't survive that."

It was a bluff. Mostly. Constable Bray was asleep by eight and slow before ten. But Edmund looked at her for a long moment, snow melting on his dark hair, and then he laughed — a short, unsteady breath of a laugh — and said, "You're not at all what the letters described."

"What letters?"

"Later." He knelt right there on the top step, in the snow, and unslung the canvas bag, and drew out a tube of oilcloth, and from the tube a roll of paper tied with a cord. "Don't touch it yet," he said. "Just look."

He unrolled it across his knees.

Even by lantern-light, even half-curled, Jonah could see it was beautiful. Fine rag paper, dense with ink and wash-color, hills shaded in feathered strokes, rivers in blue so deep it was nearly black. A cartouche in the corner bore a crest she didn't recognize — a door within a circle — and lettering in a hand like frost ferns. It was, unmistakably, a map. And it was, unmistakably, of nowhere she had ever seen, and yet the mountains in it had a shape she knew in her spine, the way you know the shape of your own hand in the dark.

"Vellharrow," Edmund said quietly. "All of it. Every river, every road, every town. The last map in existence. My grandfather carried it out. Everyone else who could have drawn it is a hundred years dead."

Jonah reached out and touched it.

She meant only to touch the edge. Her fingertip landed on the mountains instead, on the shaded flank of a range with no name she knew, and the moment her skin met the paper the world changed key.

Every animal within earshot went silent.

Not gradually. All at once, completely, the way a room goes silent when a door opens onto it. The farmhouse dog, mid-bark, stopped. The crows in the birches along the wall, who had been muttering their evening grievances, stopped. The stable cats, the penned geese, the mice in the granary walls, the owl that hunted the churchyard and was even now opening its wings somewhere above the rooftops — all of them, every mind she had ever brushed against in her life, every small bright voice that had murmured in the background of her hearing since childhood, went out like windows slammed shut in a storm.

Jonah had never known silence. Not once, not since she was old enough to listen. The silence of beasts was the silence of deafness, of blindness, of a bell rung dumb. She made a sound she was not proud of and snatched her hand back, and the silence held for one heartbeat, two, three—

—and then the world rushed back in, a flood of small voices, the dog saying *cold cold stranger cold*, the owl saying *mouse mouse mouse*, and Jonah realized she was on her knees in the snow beside Edmund, and that he was holding her elbow, and that his face was very close and very pale.

"What happened," he said. It wasn't quite a question. "When you touched it. What happened to you?"

"Nothing." Her voice came out cracked. She cleared her throat. "Nothing. I slipped on the step."

"You were standing on flat ground."

"Then I'm clumsy." She pulled free of his grip and stood, and made her knees work, and looked down at the map as if it were a snake she'd stepped near. Under the lantern-light the ink seemed to have dried darker where her finger had touched it. Or perhaps not. Perhaps. "Put it away. Put it away now, before someone sees."

"Jonah." Edmund rolled the map with careful hands, but his eyes never left her face. "On the coach, for three days, I rehearsed what I'd say in this town. I had a whole speech about surveying commissions and border grants. And then you stood at that tollgate and asked me what I was, and I told you the truth, and I don't tell people the truth. Ever. It's the first rule of my family." He tied the cord, slid the roll into the oilcloth, the oilcloth into the bag, and rose, snow sliding off his shoulders. "So I'll break the second rule too, and ask you plainly: what did you hear, when you touched it?"

"The question," Jonah said, "is what it heard."

Below them, the fiddle in the tavern found its tune. Above them, the snow came down thicker, erasing the road they'd walked, erasing the road ahead, laying the first white page of winter over everything. And in the hedge by the tollhouse, three miles back, the wren that had stopped mid-scold found its voice again and said, in its small fierce spark of a mind, words Jonah had never heard any bird say and hoped to God she never would again:

*Too late. Too late. The door knows your name.*
