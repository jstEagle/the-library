## 3. What the Ravens Knew First

Edmund Vell had learned to read a room the way his father taught him to read terrain: by what was missing from it.

The back room of Eastwick's bookshop was missing nothing so much as honesty. There were ledgers on the shelves that had never held accounts, charts of coastlines that existed only in the dreams of smugglers, and behind every false spine, he'd have wagered his last coin, something truer and more dangerous than any of it. The girl who owned the room — or was owned by it; the distinction seemed thin in Thornfell — stood across the table from him with her arms crossed and her weight on her back foot, like a woman deciding how far the door was.

The ravens had not stopped looking at her since she walked in.

That was the thing Edmund could not stop noticing, and could not explain politely. Three of them perched along the beam above the shop's back window, feathers ruffled against the cold that came through the glass like water through a net. Ordinary birds huddled. These birds watched. Their heads turned when Jonah turned. When she spoke, they went still in the particular way birds go still before weather changes.

His grandmother would have known what to make of them. His father too, probably, though his father had died before telling him half of what he should have. Edmund only knew enough to be afraid of it, which was, he suspected, exactly as much as the map wanted him to know.

"You said you needed a guide," Jonah said. "Through the Thornfell passes. Before winter closes them." She said it flatly, the way people repeat a sentence they've decided not to believe. "So say the rest of it. Why me. You've been circling that all night like a moth at a lamp."

Edmund set the map tube on the table between them. He didn't open it. Not yet.

"My family were cartographers," he said. "Not the kind who draw pretty maps for merchants' daughters. The other kind."

"There are other kinds?"

"There's one other kind." He turned the tube slowly under his fingers, feeling the seam of the cap. "My family kept a country. That was the work. Not drawing it — keeping it. A country called Vellharrow."

He watched for the flicker. Everyone had a flicker. Either they knew the name and flinched, or they didn't know it and their faces did the small honest arithmetic of *strange word, foreign word, none of my business*. Jonah Eastwick did neither. Her face did nothing at all, and the nothing was so deliberate that it might as well have been a shout.

"You've heard the name," he said.

"I've never heard the name."

"Then your face is better trained than most soldiers' I've met."

"I grew up in a smuggling town." She unfolded her arms just enough to gesture at the room, the shelves, the century of careful lies around them. "We train our faces before we learn to walk. Say what Vellharrow is."

"It isn't anything now." He heard his own voice go quiet, and hated it a little, and let it anyway, because some sentences only worked quietly. "It was a country in the high country east of here — past the passes, past the Stair, in the bowl of mountains where no road goes anymore. It vanished a hundred years ago. Not destroyed. Not conquered. Vanished. One autumn the trade roads stopped coming down, and the next spring the surveyors went up to find out why, and there was nothing to find. No walls burned. No graves. No ruins. A valley with orchards gone wild and empty houses with bread still in them, going stale. And no one who remembered it clearly. People who'd traded there their whole lives could describe the wine but not the name of the town that made it."

"And the maps?"

"Went blank." He tapped the tube. "Not torn out. Not inked over. Blank. My family drew maps. We were good at it — three generations of royal cartographers before the vanishing, and after it, we were the only ones who could still write the name down. Ink wouldn't hold it anywhere else. Paper, vellum, stone. You could carve VELLHARROW into a cliff face and come back in a week and find smooth rock. But on our maps, the name stayed. So we became the memory of a place everyone else forgot. And we made a promise."

"To whom?"

"That's the part I keep coming back to." He laughed, short and without humor. "You'll appreciate this. The promise was written down, and then the paper it was written on —"

"— went blank," Jonah finished.

"The oath survived as a story. Sworn words survive as stories. My father told it to me the way you teach a child a prayer: *we keep the door findable*. Those were the words. Findable. As long as a Vell lived who could draw the way in, the door could be found by someone worth finding it." He looked at her. "My father died in the spring. I'm the last Vell. This is the last map."

The ravens shifted on their beam. All three at once, wings half-lifting, settling again. Jonah glanced up at them, and Edmund caught the glance — quick, guilty, familiar — the glance of someone checking whether witnesses had noticed something.

*She talks to them,* he thought. *Or they talk to her.* In Lowmark they'd have burned her for less. In Thornfell, apparently, they sold her smuggled books and let her be.

"Why tonight?" she asked. "Why come now, on the last coach, at the edge of winter? If this has been sitting in your family for a hundred years."

"Because of the book." He reached into his coat — slowly, letting her see the movement, letting her decide not to put a knife in him for it; she watched him do it without moving a muscle, which told him she'd already decided she didn't need a knife — and laid the folded page on the table. He'd copied it out himself, twice, because he hadn't trusted his own eyes the first time. "A prophecy book surfaced in a private collection in Lowmark this summer. Dated a hundred years back. Most of it's the usual fog — kings, comets, a river running backward. But there's a passage about the door. About two names."

Jonah looked at the page. She didn't touch it. Her eyes moved over the copied lines once, fast, the way she must have read stolen ledgers all her life — taking the whole shape first, hunting for the trap.

"A beast-speaker of Thornfell," she read aloud, and her voice stayed level but her knuckles whitened against the table's edge, "and a son of the line of maps. Two hands, one door. What one opens, the other must close—" She stopped.

"You can finish it," Edmund said. "I've read it forty times. It doesn't get kinder."

"*What one opens, the other must close,*" she repeated, slower, "and neither walks free of the Hollow again." She looked up. "This is why the hunters want it. This is why your coach got searched at the tollgate. There are men in this town who would sell their mothers for a line like this."

"There are men in every town who would sell their mothers for a line like this. It's why I need to leave tomorrow. Tonight, if the snow holds off." He leaned forward. "I need a guide through the Thornfell passes. Not a merchant guide — they won't go near the high trails in late autumn, and half of them would sell me to the hunters by morning. Someone who knows the smuggler roads, the ways that don't exist on any chart. Someone the mountain trusts."

"The mountain doesn't trust anyone."

"Then someone the mountain tolerates." He nodded upward, at the beam, at the three black heads tracking her every breath. "Someone the birds watch like that."

For the first time all night, Jonah Eastwick looked genuinely off balance. It lasted maybe two seconds. She covered it well — folded her arms back into their fortress, tipped her chin down — but he'd seen it. He'd spent his whole life reading faces the way other people read maps, and hers had shown him a flash of raw country: fear, and under the fear, something that looked almost like grief.

"They're just crows," she said.

"Ravens. And they haven't taken their eyes off you since you came downstairs. Birds don't do that for no reason. Birds especially don't do that for *no reason* in a room where a man is talking about a prophecy that names a beast-speaker." He spread his hands. "I'm not asking what you are. I'm asking if the passage is about you. The date matches tonight. The town matches. The gift, if you have one—"

"If." She let the word hang there, sharp as a hook. "You're very generous with ifs, cartographer. Here's one back. Suppose I believed even half of this. Suppose there's a door, and a debt, and a prophecy old enough to vote in this town's council. Why would the door want *me*? I'm a smuggler. I move contraband brandy and forged deeds through frozen goat-tracks. I am not the heroine of anything. Prophecies don't waste their ink on women like me."

"Prophecies," Edmund said, "waste nothing. That's the worst thing about them. Every word costs somebody everything." He pulled his hand back from the map tube. "And I'm not asking you to believe. I'm asking you to walk. Guide me to the Stair, to the high valley beyond the last pass. After that, you can turn around, go home, and forget my name along with everyone else's forgotten Vellharrow. One week's work. I'll pay in gold, and I'll pay in truth — everything I know about the door, everything in the oath, all of it, yours to keep or sell."

"Sell," she repeated softly. "You'd give me something like that to sell?"

"I'd give it to you to *have*, and trust you not to sell it. Which is either foolishness or faith, and I honestly don't know which yet."

Something moved behind her eyes at that — something that might have been the beginning of an answer — and then the lamp guttered, and in the stutter of light all three ravens cried out at once. Not the dry rattle of ordinary birds. A sound like a word bent double, like speech pressed through a throat never built for it, and the hair rose on Edmund's neck, because he knew — he *knew*, the way his family had always known things they couldn't prove — that those birds were trying to say something, and that whatever it was, it was aimed at the woman across the table.

Jonah had gone white.

"Get out," she said. Quietly. Not angry. Frightened people often sounded calmest right at the edge.

"Jonah—"

"You heard the birds same as I did, so don't pretend you didn't." She was around the table now, hand flat against his chest, steering him toward the stair — firm, not rough, and trembling faintly, though he'd have missed it if he hadn't been watching for exactly that. "I'm not your beast-speaker. I'm not anybody's beast-speaker. I'm a woman with a shop full of debts and a crew expecting me at the tollhouse before dawn, and if I'm not there, they'll start asking questions, and if they ask questions about *you*—"

"Then come tell them you're taking a paying job. One client, one route, seven days."

"They'll want to know what the client carries." Her hand fell away. At the top of the stairs, with lamplight climbing the wall behind her and the ravens silent again above, she looked at him for a long moment — measuring him, he realized, the way he measured coastlines: for depth, for currents, for where the hidden rocks lay. "Everyone wants to know what you carry, Edmund Vell. The hunters at the gate wanted to know. My crew will want to know. And I'll tell you what I told myself an hour ago, when a stranger sat down in my grandfather's shop and said a dead country's name like it was a password: whoever finds out what's in that tube will own the winter. Maybe own more than that."

"So don't tell them."

"I can't not tell them. They're my crew. We've kept each other alive six winters." She shook her head, and some of the fortress came down, just a crack — enough that he saw how tired she was underneath, how young, how thoroughly alone inside a town full of allies. "I'm sorry. Truly. Find another guide."

"There is no other guide. The passage names Thornfell. It names you."

"Then the passage," Jonah Eastwick said, "can learn to be wrong."

She went up the stairs ahead of him, and the dark of the closed shop swallowed her, and Edmund stood alone in the back room with a blank-named map and a copied prophecy and three ravens who would not stop looking at the stairwell where she'd been, as if they expected her back. As if they knew something about doors that he didn't yet.

He slept badly in the chair by the cold stove, and woke before dawn to a sound he couldn't name — a scratching, soft and rhythmic, at the shuttered window. When he opened it, the cold poured in blue and silver, and the first true flakes of the winter were coming down fat and slow over Thornfell's rooftops. On the sill, in the grey hour, stood one raven. It looked at him. It looked at the map tube leaning against the wall. It looked back at him.

Then it flew toward the mountain, and after a moment's argument with himself that he lost completely, Edmund took the map, wrapped it in oilcloth, strapped it inside his coat, and followed.

* * *

He found her at the tollgate, and he was not the only one.

The last coach of the season had left an hour before dawn, and the gate yard was empty except for sleet and mud and four figures gathered under the tollhouse eave — three men and a broad-shouldered woman in oiled wool, and Jonah among them, her back against the tollhouse wall, her face doing its excellent trained nothing while a wiry man with a scarred lip talked close to her ear. Even at thirty paces, through falling snow, Edmund could read the geometry of it: not a quarrel. An accounting. The woman kept glancing at Jonah's coat pocket, and Jonah kept her shoulders square and her hands visible and her eyes fixed on the middle distance, and every line of it said *this conversation is not going the way she planned*.

The raven on his shoulder — it had ridden the whole climb down from the ridge like a hired scout, and he'd stopped questioning that somewhere around the second mile — croaked once, low.

"Yeah," Edmund murmured. "I see it."

He should have walked away. That was the sane arithmetic: a guide who refused, a crew closing ranks, hunters somewhere behind him in Lowmark already buying descriptions of his face. Every instinct his father had drilled into him said *a map is worth nothing to a dead man; go around; wait; come back in spring.*

But the door opened once, the oath said, when the frost opens the way — and the frost had opened tonight, and the passage named two names, and one of them was currently being backed against a wall by people who wanted to know what was in her coat.

He crossed the yard.

Four faces turned. The scarred man's hand drifted somewhere beneath his coat with the lazy professionalism of long habit, and Jonah pushed off the wall and stepped — not away from Edmund, Edmund noted, and filed that away like a contour line on an unmapped slope — *between* him and the crew.

"You," she said.

"Me," Edmund agreed. He pitched his voice for the whole eave-full of them, easy, bored, a merchant's voice, and let his coat fall open just enough to show the purse. "Madam Eastwick. I'm told you reconsidered my offer of employment. Seven days' guiding, high passes, generous terms. I do hate negotiating twice."

"We haven't negotiated anything," the scarred man said.

"No," Jonah said. Her eyes came up and met Edmund's, and what was in them was fury wearing a mask of calculation wearing a mask of fury — layered, like everything in Thornfell, like everything in this whole cursed beautiful business. "We haven't. Give me a moment with my client, Tam."

"Your client," Tam repeated.

"My *client*," Jonah said, "who is paying for the winter's grain and the roof repairs and half this crew's debts, unless someone at this tollgate intends to explain to me why he shouldn't." She smiled. It was a magnificent smile, all teeth and no warmth, and Edmund understood suddenly why the smuggling crews of Thornfell were legends in the lowland taverns. "Inside. Now. Both of us. And Tam — nobody follows, or our client develops a limp and a bad memory about payment."

Inside the tollhouse, with the door barred and the single window fogged with their breathing, Jonah rounded on him.

"Are you insane?"

"You were about to lose control of the situation."

"I was about to *manage* the situation, which is what sane people do, and instead you strolled into a crew meeting smelling like gold with a raven on your shoulder like some kind of— of prophet in a mummer's play, and now they think I'm hiding something, which means now I *am* hiding something, which means—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. Breathed. When she lowered them, she looked ten years older and, somehow, more resolved. "They'll want the map. Or whatever you're carrying. Tam already sent a boy toward Lowmark — I saw the horse go out. By nightfall, someone will have told the hunters exactly where you're sleeping."

"Then I shouldn't sleep here."

"No," Jonah said. "You shouldn't sleep anywhere." She stared at the fogged window, at the snow coming down thicker now, erasing the road, the yard, the year — and Edmund watched her weigh it, watched the scales tip, watched a woman make the exact kind of choice his family's stories were full of and had never once prepared him to witness. "Seven days," she said finally. "To the Stair and back. You carry your own pack, you eat what I eat, and if I tell you to drop something in a ravine, you drop it in the ravine without opening it first."

"Agreed."

"And Edmund." She held out her hand — palm up, an old bargainer's gesture, sealing terms older than contracts. "Whatever's in that tube stays in the tube until we're past the last pass. Whatever's in your head stays in your head. And whatever the birds keep saying about me—" her jaw tightened, just barely, "—you keep to yourself. Deal?"

Above the tollhouse, muffled by snow, three ravens settled onto the ridgepole in a row, patient as judges.

"Deal," Edmund said, and shook her hand, and outside the window the winter came down on Thornfell like a curtain falling on the first act of something neither of them yet knew how to survive.
