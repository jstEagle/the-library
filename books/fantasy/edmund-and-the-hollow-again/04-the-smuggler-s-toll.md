## 4. The Smuggler's Toll

The Salt Cellar smelled the way it always did before a run: tallow smoke, wet wool, and the sour bite of cheap gin sweating out of the walls. Jonah came down the crooked stair with her hood still dripping and found all four of them already there, which meant they'd been waiting long enough to get impatient, and impatience in the Cellar had teeth.

Maren sat at the head of the table like she'd been born to it, which was a joke nobody laughed at anymore because Maren had stopped finding it funny. Doss was sharpening a knife he didn't need to sharpen. The twins, Pell and Piet, occupied one bench between them the way they'd occupied their mother's womb — pressed together, finishing each other's silences.

"You're late," Maren said.

"The coach was late." Jonah shook the rain from her shoulders and took the empty chair at the far end, deliberately far. "Last one of the season. After tonight, the pass closes and we're done till thaw."

"That's what I heard." Maren turned something over in her fingers. A copper token, toll-gate stamped. "And I heard who came off it."

Jonah kept her face still. She'd learned that skill young, in this room, at this table: how to be a stone when every part of you wanted to be weather. "A traveler. Bookish sort. Going nowhere worth going."

"A traveler," Maren repeated. She set the token down and slid it across the table with one finger, slow, until it stopped at the edge. It didn't fall. It never fell; that was the trick of Maren's hands, things stayed where she put them. "Toll-keeper's boy says he asked after guides up the Thornfell passes. Says the woman who answered him went white as milk and walked him home herself through the rain, like he was made of sugar."

"I was being neighborly."

"You don't know the word." Maren leaned back. Tallow light caught the scar on her jaw, the one from the Kettlebridge job, the one Jonah knew better than her own reflection because she'd stitched it shut with sail-thread and shaking hands. "Here's what else I hear, Jonah. Here's what my boy at the tollgate says this bookish traveler carried. Rolled oilcloth, strapped crossways, guarded like a christening. And here's what the toll-keeper's boy says he saw when it unrolled for half a breath on the platform." She let the silence sit. She'd always been good at that. "He saw a country."

Doss stopped sharpening his knife.

"He saw a country," Maren said again, quieter, which was worse than louder, "that doesn't exist."

* * *

They argued about it the way they argued about everything — in circles, with knives of tone. Jonah gave them nothing. She said Edmund was a scholar, that scholars carried old paper, that half the maps in grandfather's shop showed countries that had never drawn breath, Cathay with its golden roofs, the Sea of Worms, the Terrestrial Paradise marked neat as a parish. Old maps lied. That was most of what old maps were for.

But Pell had seen the name, or the boy at the tollgate had and Pell had paid him two bits for it, and he said it now into the gin-smelling dark like setting down something heavy:

"Vellharrow."

Nobody breathed. Even the twins, who shared everything, shared the not-breathing.

"There's no such place," Jonah said.

"There wasn't," Piet said. "That's the point, isn't it. Wasn't."

She should have laughed. That was the move, the obvious move, the stone's move — laugh at the fools chasing ghost-countries, pour her gin, change the subject to the winter run along the salt road. And some part of her did laugh, some thin dry thing that came out wrong, and Maren watched her do it with those flat grey eyes and Jonah understood, with a lurch like a stair missing, that Maren already knew. Had known since the word Vellharrow left Pell's mouth. Had perhaps known since Jonah came down the stairs looking like weather instead of stone.

Because Jonah had touched that map. On the walk from the tollgate, in the lee of the chandler's wall, Edmund had unrolled a corner of it to show her the pass routes and she had laid her fingertips on the ink without thinking, the way she touched everything, cats and crows and the noses of tired horses, and every living thing within earshot had gone silent at once. Every bird in the eaves. Every dog behind every fence. The whole town holding its breath around her hand.

She hadn't told anyone that either.

"Lowmark pays," Maren said, "for prophecy. You know this. Everyone knows this. The collectors there would pay more for a map of a vanished country than we'd make in ten years of salt runs. More than we'd make alive, maybe." She spread her hands, reasonable, terrible. "I'm not saying we take it off him rough. He's your guest, Jonah. Your find. But tomorrow morning I'm sending word ahead to Lowmark, and if you've got an objection, now's the hour to make it."

Every eye on her. The knife finally still in Doss's hand.

Say something, Jonah told herself. Say it's worthless. Say he's mad. Say anything but the truth, which is that when you touched the paper the world went quiet as a held grave, and that last night a cat spoke to you in a voice no cat ever owned, and that a hundred-year-old book in your grandfather's locked shop has your name written in it like an appointment.

"I need to think," is what she actually said.

Maren smiled, and it was the worst smile Jonah had ever seen on her, warm as a trapdoor. "Course you do. We're family. Family thinks together." She stood, gathering the tallow lamp, and paused at Jonah's shoulder on her way to the stair. Her voice dropped to just the two of them, under the twins' sudden loud talk of Lowmark prices. "You touched that paper. Didn't you. I watched your face when Pell said the name, love, and I've watched your face twenty years. Whatever it is you're carrying — the beasts, the books, whatever your grandfather left rotting in that shop — you carry it alone or you carry it with us. But you don't carry it against us."

Then she was gone up the stairs, and the lamplight went with her, and Jonah sat in the dark with four people who had fed her and bled beside her for fifteen years, understanding that she had already chosen, and hating herself for how easy it had been.

* * *

She went first to the shop.

Grandfather's lock was a joke; she'd been picking it since she was nine, back when picking it felt like trespassing and not like inheritance. Inside, the dust hung in the lamplight like something waiting. The prophecy book lay where she'd left it, weighted with a horseshoe against its own eagerness, and she didn't open it again. She didn't need to. She could see the page burned behind her eyes: *the speaker and the bearer*, and beneath it the date, and the date was today.

Instead she took other things. Rope. The good flint. Grandfather's old field-lantern with the shuttered sides. And from the drawer where he'd kept the things too valuable to sell and too dangerous to show, a slim knife with a bone handle that had never once been used for anything but cutting apples, because grandfather had believed — loudly, often, to customers who hadn't asked — that a blade's first job was fruit.

The cat was on the counter when she turned to go. The grey one, the one that wasn't quite a shop cat and wasn't quite anything else, sitting with its tail wrapped like a judge's chain.

"Don't," Jonah said aloud, though the street outside was empty. "Whatever you're going to say. Don't."

The cat looked at her with eyes the color of pond ice. When it spoke, its mouth barely moved, and the voice came from somewhere behind the purr, somewhere older: *"The door remembers the hand that opens it. It does not remember the hand that sells the key."*

"I'm not selling anything."

*"No,"* the cat agreed, and leapt down, and was gone between the shelves, and Jonah stood alone among a dead man's lies about geography with her heart going like a bird in a box.

* * *

Edmund had taken a room at the Drowned Bell because it was the cheapest bed in Thornfell and the only one whose landlord asked nothing but coin and silence. Jonah climbed the outside stair in the black hours, rain come round again, harder now, with a taste of iron in it that meant snow on the high ground. Snow early. Snow weeks early. Everything running ahead of schedule, the season and the prophecy both, as if the year itself had read the same book she had.

She knocked the signal they'd agreed on their walk from the tollgate — three, pause, two — feeling absurd, feeling like a girl in a ballad, and the door opened and there he was, sleep-rumpled and wary, candle in hand, the oilcloth roll already clutched against his chest like he'd slept holding it. Maybe he had.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. "It's not safe."

"No," Jonah agreed, shouldering past him into the room. "It really isn't. Listen fast. My people know what you're carrying. By morning they'll have sent word to Lowmark — the collectors, the prophecy-hunters, men who buy fragments of the future the way other men buy land. They won't hurt you. That's not how it works. They'll pay you so much money you'll forget why you ever needed the thing itself, and then it'll sit in a glass case in Lowmark while some fat collector tells his guests it's authentic, and your country stays lost, and whatever debt brought you here dies in a display cabinet."

Edmund had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Something else. "Your crew," he said carefully. "These are the partners you vouched for me with."

"They're my family," Jonah said, "which is different, and worse. Family knows where you sleep." She started pulling his coat off the peg, throwing it at him. "Get dressed. We're leaving Thornfell tonight, up the old drover's stair before the pass seals, because once Maren's letter reaches Lowmark there will be men on the mountain who can read a trail the way priests read scripture."

"And if I refuse?" Quietly. Testing. "If I say I'll take my chances, sell to these collectors myself—"

She crossed the small room in two steps and put her palm flat on the oilcloth roll against his chest, and through the canvas and the leather she felt it again, that vast held breath, the whole night leaning in around her hand. Outside the window, in the yard below, the Drowned Bell's mule stopped chewing. Somewhere down the lane a dog cut off mid-bark. Silence spread from her fingers like frost from a nailhead.

"Do you feel that?" she whispered. "That happens when I touch your map. Do you understand what that means? It means the thing isn't paper. It means the thing is *listening*. And I will not hand something listening to men who collect ears."

Edmund looked down at her hand, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, and reach for his boots.

* * *

The trouble was that Maren had posted Piet at the tollhouse, because Maren posted someone everywhere, and Jonah had known that too and gone anyway, because there was no other road and because some stubborn animal part of her refused to sneak past her own family like a thief.

Piet stepped out of the tollhouse doorway with a lantern and a look of genuine sorrow, which was the worst part. Piet had taught her to swim in the millpond. Piet had held her hair back the winter of the fever.

"Don't," he said. "Please, Jo. Just give it up and come inside and we'll work it out. Maren's already writing the letter. Nobody has to lose anything."

"Someone loses something either way, Pip. That's what a toll is." She shifted the pack on her shoulder and kept the lantern shuttered low between them. Behind him, up the dark throat of the pass, the wind had changed again, and now it wasn't a taste of snow it carried but something warmer, impossible, a thread of air that smelled of cut grass and blossom in the teeth of November, and she saw Piet smell it too and frown.

"Where's your scholar?" Piet asked.

"Walking," Jonah said, which was true — Edmund was already on the drover's stair with the map and her rope, moving fast, trusting her to hold the rear — and then, because lying to Piet sat in her chest like swallowed stone, "I'm sorry. Tell Maren I'm sorry. Tell her—" What? Tell her what? There was nothing that fit. "Tell her I'll send word from the high shepherds' hut."

Piet's jaw worked. His lantern trembled, just slightly, just enough. "She'll send Doss after you by dawn."

"Then I'll be higher by dawn."

"Jo." His voice cracked down to the boy he'd been. "What's on that map?"

And Jonah, halfway turned toward the mountain, toward the freezing stair and the impossible warm wind and the rest of her life, thought of the cat's voice, thought of the silent dogs, thought of a hundred-year-old page with her name on it, and gave her family the only honest thing she had left.

"A door," she said. "I think it's a door, Pip. And I think somebody's got to open it before winter does."

She ran. Behind her Piet didn't call out, didn't raise the alarm, stood with his lantern raised like a man watching a ship take water, and whether that mercy would cost him his place at Maren's table Jonah couldn't stop to wonder, because the drover's stair was already climbing steep and slick beneath her boots, and above her — faint, absurd, unmistakable — she could hear the birds starting up along the pass, hundreds of them, rooks and choughs and one screeching thing that might have been an owl or might have been nothing of the kind, waking in the frozen dark and singing toward the mountain as if something up there had called them by name.

The first flakes came down while she climbed, fat and slow, weeks too early.

By the third mile she'd caught up with Edmund, and neither of them spoke, and the snow thickened, and behind them Thornfell's lights shrank to a handful of embers in a black bowl — one of which, she knew, was already burning down onto a letter addressed to Lowmark.

Above the tree line, in a voice that belonged to no bird she had ever heard, a chough said: *"Two come up the stair. One comes down."*

Jonah climbed faster.
