## 5. Up the Frozen Stair

The snow began at the treeline, the way Jonah had promised it would.

Edmund felt it first as a change in the light — the grey afternoon thickening, softening, until the pines ahead of them stopped being individual trees and became a single dark suggestion. Then a flake landed on the back of his hand and did not melt. Then another.

"That's it," Jonah said, not turning around. She was three steps ahead of him on the switchback trail, as she had been all day, moving through the pass like someone reading a book she'd memorized in childhood. "First true snow. Everything below this line will be mud by morning. Everything above will be ice until spring."

"Then we keep above it," Edmund said.

She glanced back at him then — just long enough for him to see the expression she wore when he said something she hadn't expected. It happened more often than she seemed to like. "You've never climbed the Thornfell passes in winter."

"No."

"And you want to go *up*?"

"I want to go where the map says." He tapped the oilcloth tube strapped across his back. They had argued about it for two days now, since Lowmark, since the smugglers' betrayal, since the night he'd woken to find her gone from the safehouse with his family's last inheritance under her arm and come back an hour later breathless and furious and carrying it anyway. He still didn't entirely understand what had happened in that room. He suspected she didn't either. "And the map has been very clear since we crossed the river. The road goes up."

"The road," Jonah said flatly, "is a line drawn by dead men who wanted their country found. The mountain doesn't care what the map wants."

But she turned up the trail anyway, and Edmund followed, and the snow came down between them like a curtain being drawn across the world they were leaving.

* * *

By full dark they had gained the shoulder of the ridge, and Jonah called a halt in the lee of a granite overhang where the wind couldn't strip the heat from their bones quite so efficiently. Edmund got a fire going small and shielded while she vanished into the trees with the stated intention of finding water and the unstated intention, he was fairly sure, of getting away from him for ten minutes.

He let her go. There was a rhythm to her already, three days into knowing her: she could stand his company in close quarters for roughly the length of a hard climb, and then something in her went taut as wire and she needed the dark and the distance or she'd start saying things she'd regret. He recognized it because he had the same flaw, worn differently. His version was maps. When the world pressed too close, he drew one.

The fire crackled. Snow hissed softly into it, a sound like whispering.

That was when he heard the fox.

It started as a yip somewhere upslope — ordinary enough, a red fox announcing territory in the winter dark. But the yips came too evenly spaced, too deliberate, and then they changed, and Edmund's skin went cold in a way the fire had nothing to do with, because the fox was no longer barking.

The fox was speaking.

"*Two come up the frozen stair,*" it said, in a voice like dry leaves dragged over stone, each word shaped with terrible clarity. "*One to open. One to remain.*"

Edmund was on his feet before he knew he'd moved, knife out, staring up into the black lattice of the pines. The voice came again, closer now or maybe just louder, and it was definitely a fox's throat making the sounds but it was not anything a fox had ever meant.

"*The door knows its own. The door takes its own.*"

"Jonah!" he shouted into the dark.

* * *

Jonah heard him call her name and came back at a run, and what she found was Edmund standing rigid at the fire with his knife drawn against the night, and the night answering him.

Because she could hear it too. That was the horror of it. For twenty-six years her gift had been hers alone — the low murmur of animal minds, the cat's opinions, the mule's grievances, the wordless warm pressure of creatures thinking at her in colors and hungers and simple present-tense needs. What came down the slope now was nothing like that. It was language, human language, wearing an animal's mouth like a stolen coat.

"*Two come up the frozen stair,*" said something in the pines — a fox, she was almost certain, though the voice was old and dry and patient as frost. "*One to open. One to remain.*"

"Stop," Jonah whispered, and then louder, in the way she used with frightened animals, pitched low and even: "Stop. That's not yours. Whatever you're carrying, it isn't yours. Give it back."

The silence that followed was worse than the voice.

Then, from further off, a raven answered — and ravens talked, she knew that, ravens always talked, but not like this, not in meter, not in prophecy. "*When the frost seals the high passes, the way opens once and only once.*" And beneath the raven, deeper in the dark, a chorus she couldn't parse — deer, maybe, or something larger moving through the timber — murmuring fragments in overlapping voices: "*the price*... *the keeper*... *before the long winter*..."

Every beast along the whole spine of the ridge, she realized, every creature within miles, was repeating pieces of the same text. Like a hillside full of bells all struck by the same wind.

"They've been doing this since the river," Edmund said quietly. He hadn't lowered the knife. "I thought it was my imagination. Birds on the fence posts outside Lowmark. A dog in the yard of the tollhouse. All of them saying bits of — of the same thing. I thought I was tired."

"It's not you." Jonah crouched by the fire, hands out to the flames, and hated how badly they shook. "It's me. Or — it's near me. It follows me." She looked up at him, and the truth came out of her before she could dress it in anything safer. "My gift. Animals talk to me. Have done since I was a child. I've spent my whole life hiding it, because in Thornfell a beast-speaker is one bad rumor away from a rope, and now—" she gestured furiously at the darkness and its choir, "—now the whole mountain is shouting your cursed prophecy through their mouths and I can't make them stop."

Edmund sheathed the knife slowly. He sat down across the fire from her, and for a long moment he just looked at her, snow gathering on his shoulders, and she braced herself for fear, or worse, for the particular pity people offered when they decided you were a marvel instead of a person.

"You never told me," he said.

"You never told me why your family let a whole country disappear."

Something flickered across his face — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Fair," he said.

Above them, faintly, the fox said again, almost gently: "*One to open. One to remain.*"

Neither of them spoke after that for a long time.

* * *

They climbed for four days.

The Frozen Stair was not a name the maps used; it was what the smugglers called the series of cut ledges and natural shelves that switchbacked up the eastern face of the Thornfells, and it earned its name honestly, because by the second day the meltwater that seamed the rock had turned to ridges of blue ice and every step had to be tested, trusted, committed to. Jonah went first and Edmund learned to go exactly where she went, no deviations, because she read the mountain the way he read maps — except her reading was alive, and updated itself hourly, and occasionally growled.

On the third morning the beasts began following them.

Not attacking. Following. A marten kept pace along a fallen trunk for half a mile, watching them with bright button eyes. Two ravens rode the wind above the col without beating their wings. Once, crossing a scree field, Jonah stopped dead and held up a hand, and Edmund froze, and from behind a boulder the size of a cottage came the slow, grinding sound of something enormous deciding not to be curious. A bear, she mouthed. Big one. It's just — listening.

Listening. That was the word she used, and the word haunted him. Because the animals weren't threatening them and weren't begging. They were gathered along the route like an audience along a processional road, and they repeated their fragments — *two come up*, *once and only once*, *the door remembers* — in voices not their own, and Edmund began to understand that they were not speaking to Jonah and him at all.

They were reciting. The way a congregation recites. The way a household repeats the words of some old grace whose meaning has worn smooth with use.

"They know it," he said that night, huddled with her under the overhang, sharing a blanket because the alternative was freezing separately and neither of them was stupid. "The animals. They've always known it. The prophecy isn't written in your grandfather's book — the book just copied it down. It's been living in this valley for a hundred years. In the foxes. In the ravens." He laughed, and it came out unsteady. "Your gift isn't a liability, Jonah. You're the only person alive who can hear the country remembering itself."

"Don't make it pretty," she said. But she didn't move away from where her shoulder rested against his, and in the wind-scoured dark that small point of contact was worth more than the fire.

* * *

On the fourth day the Stair turned cruel.

A cornice had let go somewhere above during the night, and the ledge they needed was buried under a fan of avalanche debris — great slabs of ice-glued rock, unstable, groaning faintly when the wind gusted. Jonah stood at the edge of it for a long time, ears pricked in that way she had, listening to the mountain the way other people listened to a room.

"There's a way through," she said finally. "Left side, along the bedrock. But it's narrow, and if we knock anything loose—"

"Then we don't knock anything loose."

They crossed in single file, thirty feet of ledge with a thousand feet of air below and a hanging field of death above, and it was going well, it was actually going well, right up until the ptarmigan burst out of the rocks at Jonah's feet.

It exploded upward in a whirr of white wings, screaming — and the scream had words in it, of course it did, everything had words in it now: "*THE DOOR TAKES ITS OWN*" — and Edmund flinched, and his boot dislodged a slab the size of a door, and the whole slope below them woke up.

He heard Jonah shout. He felt the ledge tilt. And then her hand closed on his wrist — hard, certain, a grip like iron forged specifically for the purpose of holding onto him — and she threw them both backward against the cliff face as the slab went over the edge and fell, and fell, and the avalanche it should have triggered stuttered, hesitated, and settled into a long rumbling grumble rather than a roar.

For a moment they stood pressed together against the rock, chest to chest, her fist still locked around his wrist, both of them breathing like bellows.

"You flinched," she said, "because of a *bird*."

"It *talked*."

"It's been talking for four days, Edmund, you get used to it—"

"I assure you I do not—"

And then, absurdly, horribly, they were both laughing — the ragged, gasping laughter of people who have just watched themselves die and found it embarrassing. She didn't let go of his wrist. He didn't ask her to. They stood there in the cold sun with the abyss breathing below them and laughed until it turned into something quieter, and even then her hand stayed where it was a moment longer than it needed to.

"We should keep moving," she said at last, not letting go.

"In a minute," he said.

In a minute, neither of them moved.

* * *

They made the high col at dusk on the fifth day, and that was where the voices changed.

Jonah noticed it first — the quality of the repetitions shifting, the way a song changes key. All day the fragments had been scattered, random, the mountain muttering in its sleep. Now, as they crested the final rise and the vast white bowl of the upper pass opened before them, every voice within earshot spoke the same line at the same moment. Ravens on the wing. A fox somewhere below. Something huge and slow in the shadowed timber, and the wind itself seeming to carry it:

"*Before the long winter seals it forever.*"

Then silence — total, enormous, the silence of a world holding its breath.

Below them, in the floor of the bowl, half-buried in new snow, stood the ruins of a waystation: a stone chimney, a collapsed roof, a hitching post. And beyond it, half-hidden by the falling dark, a road. An actual road, graded and walled, running straight up the throat of the pass toward the heart of the mountains — a road that appeared on no map of Thornfell that Jonah had ever seen, and that Edmund was already unrolling his map to find.

His breath caught audibly.

"It's there," he whispered. "The road. It's — Jonah, the map is drawing it *right now*. Look."

She leaned over his shoulder. On the old oilcloth, in ink that had been blank an hour ago, a line was spreading up the page like frost across a window, tracing the exact course of the impossible road below them.

And from the ruined waystation, from the dark doorway of it, something that was not a fox and not a raven and not any beast she had ever spoken to said, in a voice like a door opening after a hundred years:

"*Welcome home, cartographer. Bring the speaker.*"

Jonah's hand found Edmund's in the dark, and neither of them pretended it was an accident.
