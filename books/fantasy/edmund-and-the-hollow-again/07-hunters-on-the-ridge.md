## 7. Hunters on the Ridge

Edmund had learned to read snow the way other men read ledgers, and by the third morning on the Frozen Stair the ledger was telling him they were not alone.

It began with the crows. Not their absence — Jonah would have caught that long before he did — but their *behavior*. They flew low and in a line, strung out along the ridge above them like beads on a wire, and every so often one would drop and rise again, drop and rise, the way birds do when something below them is walking and they are keeping count.

"They're following someone," Jonah said, when he pointed it out. She said it lightly, the way she said most things, but her hand had gone to the strap of her pack and stayed there.

"Or leading them," Edmund said.

She looked at him then, a long look, wind-reddened and unamused. "You brought hunters with you."

"I brought a map that three separate people in Thornfell offered to buy before I'd finished my tea. I'd have been more surprised if no one had followed." He kept his voice level because hers was level, and because the truth beneath it was uglier than either of them wanted to say aloud: he had known since Lowmark's name first came up in the tollgate inn that this journey would end with someone trying to take the map from him. He had simply hoped it would happen after the door.

Jonah turned back up the trail. "Then we move faster."

They moved faster. It did not help.

* * *

The storm came down at dusk like a curtain being drawn across the world, and they made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder that the old shepherds called the Wether's Tooth. Edmund built the fire small — small enough to cup in his palms if he had to kill it in a breath — and while Jonah fed strips of dried mutton to the marmot that had adopted her somewhere below the treeline, he took out the map and studied it by firelight for the hundredth time.

The map of Vellharrow. His grandfather's ink, his great-grandfather's survey lines, his family's whole inheritance folded into a square of oiled linen no bigger than a hymnal. The roads were drawn in a brown that had gone almost black with age. The borders were drawn twice: once in confident strokes, once — underneath, faint as a scar — in a trembling hand that had traced the same country and gotten it wrong, or gotten it *changed*.

"You keep looking at it like it owes you money," Jonah said.

"It might." He turned it toward her. "See these two sets of lines? My grandfather drew the top ones. The bottom ones are older. Nobody in my family ever explained why the roads don't match."

"Roads move."

"Not like this. Look — here." His finger found the pass they'd climbed that morning. On the older tracing, it sat half a mile east. "The country is shifting under its own map. Or the map is lying about where things are. I've been telling myself it's age. Ink shrinks. Linen warps."

"And what are you telling yourself tonight?"

He didn't answer, because that was the moment the marmot stopped chewing.

It didn't run. That was the wrongness of it. It went still in Jonah's hands, utterly still, the way a clock goes still, and then it opened its mouth and said, in a voice like dry leaves dragged over stone:

"*Two come to the door. One goes through.*"

Jonah's hands closed around it too fast; the animal squeaked and twisted free and vanished into the rocks, and she sat there staring at the space where it had been, her face gone the color of the snow.

"That's not —" she started. Stopped. Started again. "Marmots don't talk. Nothing talks. They show me things, they feel things, they've never —"

"I know," Edmund said quietly.

"How could you possibly know?"

Because his grandmother had told him, the night before she died, holding his wrist hard enough to bruise. *When the beasts start speaking the words, you'll be close, and you'll wish you weren't.* But he couldn't say that, not yet, not with her looking like that, so instead he said: "Because the prophecy has been waiting a hundred years for someone who could hear it coming. And now it's using whatever mouth is nearest."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, along the ridge, a crow called once and was answered by another, farther off, and then another beyond that — a chain of voices passing something down the dark, and Edmund understood with cold certainty that they were not counting walkers anymore.

They were reporting.

* * *

He should have told her everything that night. He has thought about it since, in all the winters since, and he has never once managed to make the telling feel possible — not because the truth was terrible, though it was, but because the lie came so easily, and the lie was kind, and kindness is how cowards forgive themselves.

Here is what happened instead.

Near midnight, with Jonah asleep sitting up against the granite, her chin dropped to her chest and her fingers still curled loosely around the hilt of her skinning knife, Edmund heard boots on scree.

Not close. A quarter mile off, maybe, along the ridge — but the sound carried in frozen air, and there was more than one set, moving with the deliberate spacing of people who had done this before. He killed the fire with a sweep of his glove and stood in the sudden black, listening.

A voice drifted down. Low, clipped, the accent of Lowmark's merchant quarter — vowels flattened by counting-houses. "...cold trail past the Tooth, sir. But the birds keep marking them. Whatever the woman does to quiet them, it doesn't hold."

Another voice, older, drier: "It holds long enough. We only need it to fail once."

Edmund stood in the dark and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since Thornfell. Four men, maybe five, professional, patient, provisioned for winter. Two of them, half-provisioned, one of them a smuggler with quick hands and no experience of being hunted, the other a cartographer whose only weapon was a very good memory. The math did not favor them. The math had never favored them.

And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and colder, the other thing. The clause. The part of the prophecy he had read forty times and recited zero, the part his grandmother had made him swear to understand before she let him inherit anything at all.

*The door opens for two,* the old book said, *and keeps one.*

He had asked her, once, when he was fourteen and still believed questions were free: *Keeps one how? Kept for how long?*

His grandmother had looked at him for a long time. She had the family eyes, gray as survey ink, and she used them on him now the way she used them on difficult terrain.

"The text doesn't say," she said finally. "But the margins do. Marginalia, in my grandfather's hand. *The door takes its keeper as the toll, and the keeper does not walk back down the mountain.*"

"Dead?"

"Worse than dead, probably. Kept." She had closed the book then, gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "Which is why, Edmund, you will go to Vellharrow alone or not at all, and you will find some poor fool of a guide to stand beside you at the threshold, and you will let the door take the guide."

He had been fourteen. He had nodded. He had hated her a little, and loved her completely, and for eleven years he had carried the instruction like a stone sewn into his coat, and he had told himself it was duty, and he had told himself it was grief for a country he'd never seen, and he had told himself so many things.

Then he had met Jonah Eastwick in a locked bookshop that smelled of dust and cat, watched her argue with a raven and win, and discovered within four days that he would rather burn the map than feed her to a door.

So when the boots crunched closer along the ridge, Edmund Vell did what liars do. He chose the smaller lie to protect the larger one, and he woke her.

* * *

Her eyes opened the way an animal's do — all at once, no drift, no confusion. He put a finger to his lips and pointed up, and she listened, and he watched her hear what he had heard: the boots, the voices, the soft metallic whisper of something long being drawn from something else.

"Hunters," she breathed.

"Prophecy-hunters. Lowmark guild, by the accent. They're setting a watch on the ridge — they'll come down at first light."

"Then we go now. In the dark. In the—" she gestured at the storm, which had eased to fat slow flakes, "—in *that*."

"In that," he agreed. "There's a route. My grandfather marked it — a shepherd's cut below the ridge, steep but sheltered. If we're quiet and lucky, we're through before they realize the birds have nothing left to count."

She was already moving, cinching straps, kicking snow over the last ash. But at the edge of the fire-ring she paused and looked back at him, and the moonlight through the breaking cloud caught her face, and she asked the question he had been dreading since the marmot spoke.

"Edmund. What the beast said. *Two come to the door. One goes through.* What does the rest of it say? You've read it. Your family kept it. Don't tell me you crossed a country's ghost carrying a prophecy you haven't memorized."

The flakes fell between them. Up on the ridge, a boot scraped, unhurried. He had perhaps ten seconds, and he spent nine of them wishing he were braver and the tenth lying.

"It says the door opens for two," he said. "And keeps one."

"Keeps one *how*?"

"No one knows. The text is damaged there. Burned, maybe, or worn away. My family's copy breaks off mid-line." All true, every word, and none of it the truth. "Whoever wrote it didn't record what happens to the one who stays."

Something moved behind her eyes — suspicion, he thought, real and sharp, because Jonah Eastwick missed nothing — and he braced for her to press, and she didn't, and later he would understand that this was its own kind of wound: she had chosen, even then, to trust him past the evidence, and trust given past evidence is a debt that collects interest.

"All right," she said at last. "Then we make sure it isn't either of us. We find another way through. There's always another way."

"There's always another way," he agreed, and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost clean.

* * *

They went down through the shepherd's cut in the blue hour before dawn, single file, silent, the storm covering their sound the way it had covered the hunters'. The path was exactly where his grandfather's memory of the map said it would be — which troubled him more than he let himself notice, because the map's roads had been drifting for a century, and if this one hadn't drifted, then perhaps the road had decided to be found.

Halfway down, Jonah stopped so abruptly he nearly walked into her.

Ahead, wedged in a cleft of rock, a mountain hare sat in the snow. White on white, invisible until it wasn't. It did not flee. It turned its head — slowly, mechanically, the way the marmot had gone still — and looked at Edmund, not Jonah, and spoke with a voice like a hinge in an empty house:

"*One goes through. One remains. The door remembers which.*"

Then it loped away, and the ordinary dark rushed back in behind it like water filling a footprint.

Jonah stood very still. When she turned to him, her expression had changed — not fear now, or not only fear. Calculation. The look of a smuggler pricing goods.

"'Remembers which,'" she repeated. "That's new. The marmot didn't say that part."

"No."

"So the prophecy is... finishing itself. As we get closer." She hugged her arms against the cold, and he saw her arrive, visibly, at the same conclusion he had been living inside for days: the words were not a record. They were a countdown. "Edmund. When we reach this door. How do we know which one it keeps?"

"We don't let it choose," he said. "We find a way to open it without paying. There's always —"

"—another way. Yes." She smiled at him then, tired and crooked and, God help him, trusting. "You say that a lot, cartographer. Start being right about it."

Above them, on the ridge they had abandoned, a flare of orange light bloomed against the snow — the hunters' camp, waking, and a horn sounded, one long note that the mountains took and passed from peak to peak until it seemed the whole range was announcing them.

Jonah grabbed his sleeve. "Run now. Talk later."

They ran. And Edmund ran fastest of all from the thought he could not outrun, which was this: the door would open for two, and keep one, and he had already chosen which one it would keep, and the choosing had been the easy part.

The hard part was that Jonah Eastwick, he was beginning to suspect, was planning exactly the same thing — and only one of them could be the liar who won.
