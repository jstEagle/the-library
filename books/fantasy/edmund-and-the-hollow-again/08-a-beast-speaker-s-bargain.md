## 8. A Beast-Speaker's Bargain

The storm found them an hour after the ravens stopped talking.

Jonah heard it coming before she saw it — a low white roar rolling down the western face of the Thornfell passes like a wave deciding where to break. The trail they'd been following since dawn narrowed to a shelf of ice barely wide enough for one boot, and above them the cliffs went up and up into cloud, and below them the drop went down into a whiteness that had no bottom she cared to imagine.

"We can't cross this," Edmund said. He had the map wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, both arms clamped around it like a man holding a child in floodwater. Snow had built up on his shoulders and in his hair until he looked older, grayer, carved out of the same stone as the mountain.

"Then we don't cross it." Jonah turned her back to the wind and squinted along the cliff line. "There was a cave. Two miles back, maybe less. The marmots den there — I heard them yesterday when we passed."

"You heard *marmots*."

"I hear everything. That's rather the whole problem with me." She caught his arm before he could argue. "Come on. Whatever's behind us is worse than whatever's ahead, and right now ahead is a hole in a rock. Let's go be grateful for a hole in a rock."

They went. The storm ate the trail behind them within minutes, and by the time Jonah smelled the cave — wet stone and old fur and the particular sour-sweet stink of a winter den — she couldn't have found their backtrail with both hands and a prayer. Which meant the hunters behind them couldn't either. Small mercies. She hoarded them like coins.

The cave mouth was half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall, and it was occupied.

Jonah knew it the way she knew most things before she knew them: through her skin, through the fine hairs on her arms lifting, through the sudden animal certainty at the base of her skull that said *something large is breathing in the dark.* She put out an arm and stopped Edmund so hard he stumbled.

"What—"

"Shh." She listened. Not with her ears first — ears were the last instrument, the crudest. She listened the way her grandfather had taught her without ever meaning to, kneeling in the shop among the caged finches: with the part of her that beasts answered to.

Something in the dark shifted its weight. Stone grated. A breath came out of the cave mouth, slow and enormous, and it steamed past them smelling of meat and pine and sleep.

"Bear," Jonah whispered.

"Is that bad?"

"It's a bear. It's not good or bad. It's a bear having opinions about its front door, and we're standing in them."

*Go away,* said the dark, in a voice that wasn't words but arrived in her chest all the same, heavy as a fist on a door. *My den. My winter. Go away, small loud things.*

Jonah closed her eyes. This was the moment — she knew it even then, standing in the snow with the storm screaming and the hunters somewhere behind and Edmund watching her with those careful cartographer's eyes — this was the moment her whole life split into before and after. All her life she had used her gift the way you use a stolen key: quietly, guiltily, only to slip through doors. She spoke to beasts to soothe them, to calm them, to keep herself safe and hidden. She had never once asked anything of one. Asking was leverage, and leverage left fingerprints.

But the storm was closing the passes, and the hunters were on the ridge, and the prophecy was ticking under her ribs like a second heart, and she was so tired of hiding.

She stepped forward, out from behind Edmund, and let the gift rise in her the way heat rises off a stone in summer.

*Great-mother,* she said, in the way that isn't sound. *Forgive the intrusion. We are small and cold and hunted, and we ask for the corner of your den, nothing more.*

The silence stretched. Snow hissed against stone. Somewhere far below, wind worried at the pines like a dog at a bone.

*You speak,* the bear said at last, and there was something new in the weight of it — not anger. Wonder, maybe, or the memory of wonder, worn smooth by a hundred winters. *The two-legs who speaks. My mother's mother told of such. They were lies to make cubs behave.*

"They weren't lies. I'm here."

*Here.* A vast head emerged from the dark, gray-muzzled, scarred across one eye, eyes themselves the color of wet river stones. It regarded her the way a judge regards a plea. *Why should a speaker need shelter? Speakers command. Command the storm to stop. Command the mountain to open.*

"The storm doesn't listen to me. Neither does the mountain. If they did—" Jonah laughed, and it came out cracked. "If they did, I'd be someone else entirely."

*And him?* The great head swung toward Edmund, who had gone very still, one hand pressed flat over the oilcloth bundle at his chest. *He smells of paper and fear and old promises. Why does the speaker travel with a man made of maps?*

"That," Jonah said carefully, "is a long story."

*I am going to sleep until the thaw,* the bear said. *I do not have a long story in me. I have one question and one price.*

Jonah felt Edmund shift behind her. "Whatever it wants," he murmured, "we don't have food enough to—"

"It doesn't want food."

*No,* the bear agreed, with something horribly like amusement rumbling up out of its chest. *Meat is cheap. Meat is everywhere, if your nose is honest. I want what speakers always pay, when they finally pay true. I want a truth. Freely given. One you have not spoken aloud, that sits in you like a swallowed stone. I smell it on you, little speaker. You carry two hearts' worth of weight and only beat one of them.*

The storm howled. Jonah stood very still.

Behind her, Edmund said, "What's it saying?" because of course he couldn't hear; of course the conversation happened on her side of a wall he'd spend the rest of his life pressing his ear against.

"It wants a truth," she said. "Freely given."

"Then give it one. Any one. Tell it your favorite color. Tell it you stole bread when you were nine."

"It has to be real. It has to be the one I'm hiding." Her mouth had gone dry. "It already knows which one. That's how bears are. They don't guess. They just know where the soft places are."

Edmund frowned. "Jonah. What are you hiding?"

And there it was — the question she'd been walking around for six days the way you walk around a hole in the floor in the dark. Six days of shared fire and shared watch and his coat around her shoulders in the high pass, six days of learning the way his laugh started in his chest before it reached his mouth, six days of lying by omission so constant it had begun to feel like breathing.

Because she had read the ending. Back in the shop, that first night, with the cat's warning still ringing in her ears and the candle burning down — she had read every word of the prophecy, including the last lines, the ones about the door that opens for two and keeps one, the ones about the keeper. And she had folded the page closed and said nothing, and let Edmund plan their route, and let him hold her hand on the frozen stair, and let him believe they were walking toward a future instead of toward a ledger with one entry left in it.

*Well?* the bear rumbled. *The storm will not wait for your courage, little speaker. Storms never do. That is why we pay them no rent.*

Jonah turned to face Edmund. Snow gathered on her eyelashes. She realized distantly that her hands were shaking, and that she was cold clear through, and that none of it mattered even slightly compared to what she was about to do.

"There's a truth I owe," she said. "Not to the bear. To you. The bear's just the excuse my cowardice has been waiting for."

"Jonah—"

"I read the whole prophecy. That first night. All of it." The words came out fast now, tumbling, a dam going. "Including the end. The door opens for two and keeps one — I didn't learn that on the ridge from you, I've known since Thornfell, since before I ever met you, since the night your coach rolled in. The keeper stays, Edmund. Whoever goes through becomes the keeper of Vellharrow, and the keeper cannot leave. Not for a year. Not for ten. Maybe not ever — the book doesn't say, it just says *keeps*, and I sat there with the candle going out and read that word until I'd read it so many times it stopped meaning anything, and then I closed the book and I hid it from you."

The wind filled the silence. Edmund's face did something complicated and terrible — hurt moving through surprise moving through something colder underneath, arithmetic happening behind his eyes.

"You've known," he said quietly, "the entire time."

"Yes."

"And you let me tell you about my family's debt. You let me explain the clause on the ridge like it was news to you. You let me—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Every mile of this, you've known the price and said nothing."

"I was afraid that if I told you, you'd stop walking. Or worse — I was afraid you wouldn't. I was afraid you'd decide right there in the shop that it would be you, and I'd spend the whole journey watching you arrange yourself to die, and I—" Her voice broke and she hated it. "So I said nothing. I told myself I'd find a loophole. I told myself the translation might be wrong. I told myself every lie a person tells when they're trading someone else's life for their own comfort. That's the truth. That's the truth freely given, and it's ugly, and I'm giving it anyway."

The bear exhaled, long and slow, a wind out of the deep earth. *There,* it said. *That is a payment worth a den. Come in, small loud things. Warm yourselves on my winter and trouble me no more with questions.*

Jonah turned back to the cave. But Edmund caught her wrist — gently, but he caught it, and she stopped.

"Why tell me now?" he asked. "We could have reached the Hollow with you still carrying it. What changed?"

She looked at him. At the snow in his hair and the map against his chest and the hurt he hadn't finished feeling yet and wouldn't for a long time, and she thought: *because the bear was right, and because a truth carried too long stops being a secret and starts being a stone, and stones sink, and I refuse to drown you with me.*

"Because I watched you lie to me on the ridge," she said. "About which of us the door would take. And I recognized the look on your face while you did it, because I'd been wearing it for six days. And I thought — two people can't walk to a door together each planning to shove the other through it. Someone has to put the truth down first. So." She spread her empty hands. "Consider it put down."

For a long moment Edmund said nothing at all. Then he nodded once, slowly, like a man signing something, and followed her into the dark.

* * *

The den was warm the way dens are warm — with the accumulated heat of a body the size of a haywain and the thick reek of fur and the close mineral breath of stone. The bear curled itself against the far wall with the indifference of a landlord and closed its one good eye, and Jonah and Edmund sat near the entrance where the frozen waterfall threw pale shifting light, and for a while neither of them spoke.

The storm outside rose to a pitch that made conversation useless anyway. Jonah got out the last of the flatbread and the honey she'd traded for in the last village, and they ate in silence, shoulder to shoulder, and the strangeness of it struck her: that she had just confessed the worst thing she'd ever done to the person it was done to, and the world had not ended. The storm still stormed. The bear still slept. The bread still tasted of dust and honey.

"I'm not forgiven yet," Edmund said eventually. It wasn't a question.

"No."

"But you will be." He turned the honey jar in his hands. "That's the thing I can't decide whether to be angry about. You gave me the truth like a coin you expect change from."

"That's fair."

"Is it?" He looked at her then, really looked, and his voice dropped low enough that even the bear's ear twitched. "Because here's mine, freely given, since we're apparently doing this now: I lied to you on the ridge. About the clause. I told you the door takes the one who *opens* it, not the one who walks through. I told you that so you'd think you were safe, so you'd let me be the one to stay. I've known since Lowmark was a rumor on my father's maps. I've known since I was twelve years old and found my grandfather's journal with the last page torn out, and I tore my own copy the same way before I ever showed anyone. So you're not the only liar on this mountain, Jonah Eastwick. You're just the one who confessed first."

Jonah stared at him. The light from the falls moved across his face, blue and silver and blue again.

"So we're both liars," she said.

"We're both liars."

"Both willing to stay."

"Both willing to stay." He almost smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen in weeks. "Which means neither of us can trust the other anywhere near that door."

Outside, the storm screamed itself hoarse and began, very slowly, to die. And inside the den, in the space between one gust and the next, Jonah heard something she had never heard in all her years of listening — a sound with no beast behind it, no bird, no wind, no bear. A sound like pages turning. Like ink moving on vellum. Like a country, very far away and very patient, picking up a pen.

Beside her, Edmund went rigid. He pulled the oilcloth bundle from his chest and unwrapped it with shaking hands, and the map of Vellharrow lay in the blue light — and the roads were moving.

"Jonah," he whispered. "The map's redrawing itself. And I don't think we're choosing the route anymore."
