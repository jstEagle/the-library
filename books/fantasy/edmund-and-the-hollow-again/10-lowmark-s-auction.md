## 10. Lowmark's Auction

They took the map from Edmund at the ridge, and they took his hands after that — bound with waxed cord so tight his fingers went grey by the time the valley opened below them. Jonah walked because a hunter named Corvane walked behind her with a hooked pole pressed between her shoulder blades, and because the bear she'd bargained with on the mountain had gone silent in her mind like a door shut from the other side.

*You gave me a truth,* the bear had said, when the hunters' nets came down over them both in the whiteout. *I keep truths. I do not keep people.*

She had hated it a little for that. She understood now that hatred was a luxury of the free.

Lowmark announced itself first by smell — coal smoke and tallow and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a cellar full of money. Then by sound: hammers, a sawmill, dogs that did not speak to her even when she reached for them. That was the worst of it. In Lowmark every dog she touched with her gift was deaf to her, or pretending to be, muzzled in some way she couldn't see. A town where the animals had learned not to answer.

"Charming place," Edmund said beside her. His voice was steady but his hands were shaking, and he held them up slightly as if showing her, deliberately, so she would know he wasn't hiding it. "I can see why the prophecy-hunters winter here."

"Walk," said Corvane.

The hunters were six, and they were not what Jonah had expected. She had imagined prophecy-hunters as gaunt fanatics, ash-marked, whispering. These were accountants. They wore good boots and carried ledgers, and one of them — a round-faced woman called Mistress Pell who seemed to be in charge — kept making small notes about Edmund and Jonah as they walked, notes like *subject cooperative* and *map intact, minor water damage*, as though they were inventory rather than people.

That was the thing Jonah would remember afterward, when everything else had burned: the ledgers. The way Pell wrote *one (1) map, Vellharrow provenance* in the same column where she'd written *two (2) guides, local, expendable.*

The town proper was built into the side of a dead quarry, terraces of stone houses stacked like crates, all of them facing inward toward a great pit at the center where a pavilion had been raised for the season — white canvas, gilt poles, lanterns already lit against the early dark. Winter was coming down off the mountains behind them like a slow blue tide, and Lowmark had decided to meet it with an auction.

They were locked in a cellar under the easternmost terrace house, which was less a prison than a waiting room. There was bread and there was water and there was a bolted door with a grille in it, and through the grille Jonah could hear the town preparing itself the way a body prepares for fever.

Edmund sat with his back against the wall and worked at the cords on his wrists without appearing to work at them — a slow flexing, a turning, patient as erosion. He'd been doing it since the ridge. His fingers were raw but they were pink again.

"They'll sell the map tonight," he said. Not a question.

"Tonight or tomorrow. Pell wants a crowd. You heard her on the road — first frost brings the collectors up from the coast." Jonah sat across from him, knees drawn up. "There are people who've spent their whole lives looking for Vellharrow, Edmund. Rich ones. Old ones. Ones who want to die inside a country nobody else can find."

"And the rest?"

"The rest want to own the fact of it. A door is worth more shut than open, if you're the only one holding the key."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw him doing the arithmetic he'd been doing since the inn — the arithmetic of the clause, the door that opens for two and keeps one. He thought she didn't know the ending. She let him think it. It was the last kind thing left between them, and she was hoarding it.

"Jonah." His voice dropped. "Whatever happens tonight—"

"Don't." She stood, went to the grille, put her eye to it. "Don't make promises in cellars. They curdle."

Through the grille she could see a slice of the quarry road, and along it, being carried on poles like a relic or a corpse, came things wrapped in oilcloth. Fragments. She knew what they were before anyone told her — pages, shards, half-burnt scraps of other prophecies, other doors, other countries that had once existed and been hunted down to their last syllable. Lowmark wasn't a town that happened to host an auction. Lowmark was a market built around a hunger, and the hunger was older than any single missing country.

A rat came along the cellar wall, quick and grey, and paused near her boot.

Jonah reached out the way she always did, the way she'd been reaching since she was six years old and her grandfather's horse told her its name. *Little cousin. What's above us?*

The rat stopped. Turned. And spoke in a voice that was not a rat's voice at all — dry, papery, old:

*"Two come to the door. One remains."*

Then it was only a rat again, frightened, fleeing into the dark, and Jonah stood very still with her hand pressed flat against the cold stone while her heart tried to climb out through her ribs.

It had been happening since the Frozen Stair — beasts speaking fragments in voices not their own, the prophecy wearing them like masks. But this was the first time it had spoken the ending. The whole ending. In front of her, in a cellar, where Edmund might have—

He hadn't been listening. He was still working at his wrists, eyes closed, lips moving with some cartographer's litany of rivers and passes. She watched him and felt the secret sit in her chest like a swallowed stone: *the keeper cannot leave.* She had read it a hundred years ago in her grandfather's shop, in a book dated the night she'd find it, and she had known since then that one of them would stay forever, and she had decided — quietly, stubbornly, without asking anyone's permission — that it would be her.

Not because she loved him less. Because she was the one who could hear what waited on the other side of a sealed door, and he was the one who could draw maps of everywhere else.

* * *

They came for them at dusk.

Mistress Pell herself unlocked the cellar, two hunters flanking her, and she was almost gentle about it, the way you're gentle with merchandise. "Wash your faces," she said. "There's a basin. The buyers like them presentable. Provenance matters."

"Provenance," Edmund repeated.

"You're the Eastwick cartographer line," Pell said to him, matter-of-fact, checking something in her ledger. "Last of it, we believe. And she's the beast-speaker of Thornfell, which the Thornfell records deny, which makes it worth more, oddly. Fear inflates value." She smiled at Jonah with real warmth, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "You'll stand on the platform. You won't speak unless spoken to. If either of you attempts escape, my colleagues have instructions regarding the map that I trust will make escape unattractive."

"What instructions?" Jonah asked.

Pell's smile didn't move. "Fire is very fast," she said, "and paper is very dry."

So they washed their faces, and they walked up out of the cellar into the lantern-light of the quarry pit, and Jonah saw the auction.

The pavilion was full. Not crowded — curated. Perhaps two hundred people seated in rising tiers around a central stage, and every one of them rich in the specific way of people who collect things that should be free: the sky, the wind, the names of vanished places. Furs, jeweled spectacles, hands heavy with rings. Servants moved among them with wine. At the front, nearest the stage, sat a cluster of perhaps a dozen buyers with empty chairs beside them — space reserved, Jonah realized, for whatever they purchased tonight.

On the stage, under a glass case, lay the map of Vellharrow.

Even from thirty feet away Jonah felt it pull at her, the way it had pulled on the tollgate road — that sense of a country leaning toward her through the parchment. Under the glass it seemed dimmer, muted, a bird in a cage too small for its wings. And around the case, arranged on velvet like the relics of saints, were the fragments: burnt page-corners, a scrap of vellum with a single word in a language no one living spoke, half a compass rose, a child's drawing of a gate in an orchard that made Jonah's stomach turn over because it was *right*, it was exactly right, someone had drawn the Hollow once and Lowmark had taken the drawing.

An auctioneer with a beautiful voice was finishing a lot. "—and we remind our guests that authentication papers accompany all fragments. Lot forty-one: a page-corner, recovered 1841, bearing three words of the Vellharrow closing-verse. Shall we begin at four hundred?"

"Six hundred," said a woman in silver fox.

"Seven," said a man whose voice Jonah knew — Master Quillon of the Lowmark Historical Society, who had once offered her grandfather money for the locked bookshop's back room and been refused. He was leaning forward now with his chin on his laced fingers, and he was looking not at the fragment but at the glass case, at the map, with an expression Jonah recognized from church: devotion. The ugly kind.

Lot forty-one sold for nine hundred. Lot forty-two, the compass rose, for twelve. The numbers climbed all evening, calm and obscene, and Jonah stood at the edge of the stage where Pell had placed her — *display the speakers, it primes the room* — and listened to a country being sold piece by piece to people who would never hear its birds sing.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing she couldn't stop seeing, standing there in the lantern-light while men bid fortunes on burnt paper.

Vellharrow wasn't treasure. She'd felt it through the map, through the beasts on the mountain who spoke its name in borrowed voices, through the mad old guest at the inn who wept at Edmund's surname. Vellharrow was alive. It was a country that had closed its own door to survive, a country of people and beasts waiting a hundred years in the dark between winters — and these people in their furs wanted to buy the door the way you'd buy a skull: interesting, rare, proof of your taste. They would never open it. Opening it would end its value. The moment Vellharrow became findable it became ordinary, and ordinary things don't sell for nine hundred crowns a corner.

They didn't want the country back. They wanted the wanting.

"Lot fifty," the auctioneer sang, and a hunter in formal black carried the glass case to the center of the stage and set it on a pedestal, and the room went quiet in a way that had weight, like snowfall.

The map lay under the glass, and Jonah watched it, and the map — she was almost sure, though no one else seemed to see it — the map shifted. A coastline that had been at the western edge crept a finger-width eastward. A river bent. Somewhere under the glass, patient and furious, Vellharrow was redrawing itself away from them.

"Lot fifty," the auctioneer repeated softly. "The last complete map of Vellharrow. Cartographer's provenance: the family Vane, hereditary wardens of the northern approach." He glanced at Edmund with something like appetite. "Our seller has provided a living witness to its authenticity. Come forward, young man."

Edmund came forward. He stood beside the case with his ruined hands folded, and he looked out at two hundred people who wanted to own his inheritance, and Jonah watched his face do something complicated and settle into calm.

"Tell them what you know of it," the auctioneer prompted.

"I know it's not for sale," Edmund said.

Laughter, scattered, indulgent. The auctioneer smiled thinly. "Everything in this room is for sale. That is the purpose of rooms."

"Then I'll tell them what I know of it," Edmund said again, louder, and his voice changed — took on the cadence of a lecture, of a boy reciting at his father's knee. "I know the ink is mixed with frost-melt from the Hollow itself, and it moves. I know the roads redraw when winter deepens, because the country chooses who may approach. I know my family weren't cartographers the way you mean it. We were *wardens*. We kept the door findable to those meant to find it, and invisible to everyone else, and every one of us died poor and obscure and glad of it." He turned slowly, addressing the tiers. "You're bidding on a lock. None of you owns the key. None of you ever will. And the country on the other side of that lock is watching you right now, and it finds you wanting."

Silence. The particular silence of rich people deciding whether to be offended or amused.

Then Master Quillon began, softly, to clap.

"Beautifully done," Quillon said, rising. "Sentiment. Provenance indeed. Four thousand."

"Four five," said the woman in silver fox.

"Five," said a new voice, from the top tier, and the room turned.

The man who descended the steps was old and straight-backed and dressed plainly, which among all that fur made him the most conspicuous person in the pavilion. He wore a signet ring with a crest Jonah didn't recognize — a gate, closed, above a single star. He walked to the stage unhurried, and when he reached it he did not look at the map.

He looked at Jonah.

"Five thousand," he said again, to the auctioneer, without raising his voice. "And I'll take the speakers as well. Both of them. Thrown in."

"That's not—" the auctioneer began.

"Six thousand," said Quillon.

"Ten," said the plain-dressed man, and took out a purse, and set it on the pedestal next to the glass case, and the sound it made ended the bidding the way a slammed door ends a conversation.

Pell appeared at the stage's edge, ledger open, calculating, and Jonah saw her glance at the plain man's ring and go carefully, professionally blank. "Sold," Pell said. "To the gentleman. Lot fifty, and — contingent on his insistence — the two guides."

"No," said Edmund.

"Yes," said the plain man, and finally, at last, he looked at the map. And Jonah, watching him look, felt the hair rise on her arms — because he didn't look at it the way the others did, with greed or wonder. He looked at it the way you look at a grave you dug yourself. Grief, and guilt, and underneath both, fear.

"You," Jonah said. Her voice came out strange. "You've seen it. The door. You've *been* there."

The old man turned to her. Up close his eyes were pale as weather.

"I closed it," he said.

And somewhere beneath the glass case, faint as breath, the map finished redrawing itself — and every road on it now ran to a single point, a gate in an orchard, marked in fresh ink with two small words that no one else in the pavilion could read but that Jonah read as easily as her own name:

*Come now.*
