## 11. The Escape Through the Bell-Tower

The cell in Lowmark's auction house had one window, and it was the size of a hymn book.

Edmund had spent three days measuring it with his eyes, because that was all he had — eyes, memory, and a woman on the other side of the wall who hadn't spoken to him since the hunters dragged them off the ridge. He could hear her breathing through the stone when the wind dropped. He'd begun to count her breaths the way he counted paces, out of habit, out of something he refused to name.

Three days of auctions below them. He knew the building now the way he knew every map his family had ever drawn: by its bones. The auction house had been a granary once, then a chapel, then a chapel that fell out of love with God and was sold to collectors. The bell-tower still stood at its north corner, stripped of its bell a century ago — sold, no doubt, to some Lowmark magnate who liked the idea of owning a country's voice. The tower's stair was walled off from the corridor by brickwork laid in a hurry, mortar still pale after all these years. Bad mortar. Eager mortar.

And above their heads, through two floors of rot and pigeon dung, an open belfry.

"You're doing the thing," Jonah said through the wall.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and your eyes move like you're reading something. You did it at the inn. You did it on the ridge before you told me about the door." A pause. Her breath fogged the gap beneath the door; he could see it in the lamplight from the corridor. "You've found us a way out."

"I've found *a* way," Edmund said. "Whether it's ours depends on the rats."

* * *

The rats of Lowmark were fat, cynical creatures who lived rich on the auction house's leavings — wax drippings, biscuit crumbs, the occasional dead canary from the collectors' cages. They had no interest in prophecy, in doors, in countries that didn't exist. This made them, in Edmund's growing opinion, the most honest residents of the building.

Jonah knelt in the corner of her cell with her palm flat against the floor, and Edmund watched through the gap under his own door as the shadows along the wall went strange — a ripple, a gathering, as if the dark itself had grown whiskers.

"They'll do it," she said at last, and her voice had that flat carefulness she used when she was frightened and refusing to be. "But they want to know why."

"Why?"

"That's what they asked. *Why.*" She laughed, short and disbelieving. "A hundred years of smugglers and liars in this town, and the first creature to ask me why is a rat."

Edmund sat down against the door, so they were talking through six inches of oak like conspirators through a confessional. "Tell them the truth."

"The truth is long."

"We have until the frost ends and the collectors stop bidding. Tell it anyway."

So she told it. He heard her voice drop into the low even register she used for beasts, and though the words were meant for rats, he listened the way you listen to someone praying — not understanding, but unwilling to interrupt. She told them about Vellharrow. About a door. About a country full of animals that had closed itself away to survive, and about the men downstairs who would sell pieces of that country to anyone with coin, and burn what couldn't be sold.

When she finished, the silence lasted so long Edmund thought the bargain had failed.

Then the rat came. It walked out of the shadow beneath Jonah's door, across the corridor, bold as a tax collector, and sat up in front of Edmund's gap and looked at him with small black eyes that held, unmistakably, an appraisal.

"It wants to know if you're worth the trouble," Jonah translated. "It says the pigeons asked too. And—" her voice caught, then steadied, "—there's a goose. In the yard. She says she's been waiting a hundred years for this and we are not to waste her time."

"A goose."

"Don't repeat it like that. She can hear tone."

* * *

They moved at the change of watch, in the grey hour when the guards' tea went cold and their attention went colder.

The plan was Edmund's and it was simple, which is to say it was terrible, which is to say it was the only plan there was. The walled-off tower stair shared a chimney flue with the old chapel kitchen directly below Jonah's cell. The mortar in the wall was bad. The rats had confirmed it — had, in fact, spent generations widening it, because rats are the original cartographers of any building, mapping the spaces between the walls that no human hand ever draws. Their map ran where his family's maps never reached.

Jonah's job was the wall. His job was everything else.

He heard her start — a soft rhythmic knocking, then the scrape of the iron bedframe levered loose, worked into the seam of pale mortar. Dust sifted down through the flue. Somewhere above, pigeons shifted and muttered. Below, a guard coughed, and Edmund pressed himself against his door and rehearsed the building in his head one more time, the way his father had taught him: *a map is not paper, boy, a map is the country remembered correctly.*

Corridor, forty feet, two turns. The walled arch to the tower stair behind the third door on the left — the door marked STORES, locked, but the lock was older than the wall and the rats had already eaten the wooden frame soft around the hasp. Up the spiral, ninety-one steps — he'd counted them by sound, by the echo of the watchman's boots these three nights, ninety-one steps to a belfry open to the sky. From the belfry, the roof of the north range sloped down to the yard wall, and beyond the yard wall, the frozen canal, and beyond the canal, the fens, and beyond the fens—

Beyond the fens, the Hollow. If the map's last red thread still pointed true. If winter held. If they lived.

The wall came down with a sound like a swallowed cough. Then Jonah was in his doorway, gray with dust, a smear of blood on her knuckles she didn't seem to have noticed, holding half a bedrail like a sword.

"Ninety-one steps," she said.

"You heard me counting."

"I hear everything. It's exhausting." But she was almost smiling, and in the lamplight, dust turning in the air between them, Edmund thought: *oh.* Not for the first time. For the first time without fear of it.

"Stores door," he whispered. "I go first. The frame is rotten on the left side — lift, don't pull. If anything goes wrong—"

"Nothing goes wrong. We're a unit now." She said it lightly. She said it like a vow.

* * *

The stores door gave with a crack like green wood splitting, and the night took them.

Everything after that happened fast and also slowly, the way danger does — each second stretched and detailed and utterly unrepeatable. The corridor. The turn. A guard's chair scraping somewhere ahead, and Jonah freezing against the wall while Edmund counted the man's breathing through the plaster — four breaths, five, the creak of the chair, the man settling back into his doze. Her hand found his sleeve in the dark and gripped it, and he understood that she wasn't asking him to lead. She was telling him she was there.

The tower stair smelled of a hundred years of pigeons and rain. They climbed in the dark, hands on cold stone, ninety-one steps that felt like nine hundred, and halfway up Jonah stopped, pressed her ear to the wall, and swore softly.

"Hunters. Two flights below. They came back from the auction early — they're climbing."

"How far?"

"Far enough to lose. Barely." She looked up the spiral into blackness, and Edmund saw her make the decision before she spoke it. "We don't run quiet anymore. We run loud. Call them."

"*Call*—"

"Not here. Above." Her teeth were white in the gloom. "Trust me. Trust *them*."

They ran. Nineteen steps left, and the belfry opened around them like a held breath finally let go — a square of sky pricked with stars, the stripped bell-frame standing empty overhead, the roof slope falling away toward the yard wall. Behind and below, boot-heels on stone, closing.

Jonah went to the parapet, spread her arms, and did the thing Edmund had only seen once before, on the ridge, when the beasts spoke prophecy in voices not their own. She opened herself. He felt it pass over him like weather.

The yard erupted.

Pigeons came off the granary roofs in a thunderclap of wings — dozens, hundreds, a storm of them funneling up through the belfry opening and straight into the faces of the hunters surging up the stair. The men shouted, flailed, blinded. Rats poured from the walls in rivers, over boots, up trouser legs, and the screaming started. And rising over all of it, magnificent, apocalyptic, came the goose.

She came up the stair last, wings wide, neck extended like a drawn bow, and she did not honk so much as *pronounce* — a sound of such ancient fury that one hunter simply sat down on the steps and began, hysterically, to laugh.

"The roof!" Edmund pulled Jonah toward the south edge. "Slope, then the yard wall, then the canal ice — don't stop, whatever you hear behind us, don't—"

"I'm not going to hear anything behind us," Jonah said, "because I've arranged for everything behind us to be extremely busy."

They went down the roof together, boots skidding on frost-slick slate, and this was the moment Edmund would carry afterward, the moment he returned to in the years of watching and waiting that followed: the two of them moving as one body, her hand catching his wrist exactly when he slipped, his arm going around her waist exactly when the slate gave way beneath her heel, neither leading, neither following, a cartographer and a beast-speaker running a line across a roofline like a route neither had drawn alone.

The yard wall. The drop. The canal ice groaning but holding. Behind them the auction house blazed with lanterns and fury and the ongoing war crime of one very large goose, and Jonah was laughing — actually laughing, breathless and wild, running beside him across the frozen water with dust in her hair and blood on her knuckles and joy all over her face like sunrise.

"We're alive," she shouted.

"We're *lost*," he corrected, because the fens ahead were trackless in the dark, and the wind was rising, and the snow had begun again.

"No," Jonah said, and slowed, and turned to face him on the ice with the whole black sky wheeling overhead. "That's the point, Edmund. Lost is the only place your map can't follow us. So we make a new one."

* * *

They walked the fens until dawn, following the goose.

This was not a sentence Edmund had ever expected to think, let alone live. But the goose — who had, it transpired, no intention of being left behind, having waited a century and developed opinions accordingly — flew a slow line ahead of them through the reeds, honking at intervals like a living compass needle, and Jonah followed her with the easy certainty of someone reading a language everyone else had forgotten.

"She says the fen remembers a road," Jonah murmured as the sky went gray, then rose-colored, then briefly, impossibly gold. "Under the water. Under the ice. An old drove-road from before the vanishing. It runs toward the high passes."

"Vellharrow's road?"

"Vellharrow's road." She glanced at him sideways, and her expression was complicated — wonder braided with dread. "Edmund. Everything points there now. The rats, the pigeons, the goose, the road under the ice. It's like the whole country is—" She stopped.

"*Choosing*," Edmund finished quietly.

Because he had felt it too, these past days, and had not said it: the sense of being not pursued but *summoned*. The map in his coat, wrapped in oilcloth against his heart, had grown warm during the escape — warm as skin, warm as a hand — and he had not dared look at it yet, because he was afraid of what the roads would show. His family's debt. His family's lie. Whatever the ink had become while he slept.

The goose landed on the ice ahead of them and stood there, wings folded, regarding them both with the flat patience of a creature that has outlasted empires.

"She says we should rest," Jonah translated. "And eat. And that we argue better when we've slept."

"We don't argue."

"We argue beautifully. You just haven't noticed because you're always losing."

Edmund laughed — a real laugh, torn out of him, steaming in the frozen air — and for a moment, on the white fen with the sun coming up and a goose for a guide, he let himself believe they might outrun everything: the hunters regrouping behind them, the winter closing ahead, the prophecy coiled between them like a wire.

Then he unwrapped the map to check their bearing, and the laughter died in his throat.

The roads had redrawn themselves in the night. Every route now converged on the Hollow — and at the center, where the door should be marked, the ink had changed. New letters, fine and deliberate, in a hand that was not his father's, nor his grandfather's, nor any cartographer's hand at all:

*One door. Two names. Bring both.*

And beneath them, smaller, as if the map were embarrassed to say it:

*Only one comes home.*

Jonah read it over his shoulder. He felt her go still against his back, felt the fen go silent around them — every bird, every hidden thing, holding its breath.

"Well," she said at last, in a voice carefully light as frost. "Looks like we'd better get there first, and decide later whose name we cross out."

She smiled at him. He smiled back. And neither of them believed the other, and both of them knew it, and the goose honked once — sharp, reproachful, ancient — as if to say she had waited a hundred years and would wait no longer for fools to start telling the truth.

They walked on, toward the mountains, toward the Hollow, toward the door that kept one.
