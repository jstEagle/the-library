## 12. What Jonah Hid

The cave was old snowmelt, hollowed under the shoulder of the mountain where the wind couldn't follow, and by the time they'd dragged themselves into it, both of them were wearing ice in their eyelashes and neither of them could feel their hands. Jonah built a fire from what little dry scrub she'd hoarded in her coat lining — a smuggler's habit, hoarding fire against bad nights — and Edmund knelt across it from her, feeding it splinters of their last crate-wood, and for a long while they simply breathed and steamed and let the mountain be outside.

It should have been peace. It wasn't. She had known, since the night she'd read the book in her grandfather's shop, that this moment would come. She'd just imagined it arriving with more courage on her part.

"Jonah." Edmund didn't look up from the fire. His voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a man walking a rope bridge he already knew was rotten. "On the ridge. When the hunters had us. I told you what the prophecy says about the door. That it opens for two, and keeps one."

"You told me."

"And you said—" He finally looked at her. Firelight moved in his eyes like something drowning. "You said we'd face whatever it said together. You said that."

"I did say that."

"So then tell me why a bear made you scream out a truth in the dark that you've never once screamed at me." He turned a splinter over in his fingers without burning himself, which took a kind of deliberate care. "The bear asked you for a truth freely given. And you gave it one. *I read the ending.* Those were your words, Jonah. I've been turning them over for two days. A truth freely given to a bear before it's given to me isn't a small thing. It's the shape of everything you haven't said."

She fed the fire a twig she didn't need to feed it. The flames took it anyway, greedy, indifferent.

"The ending," she said.

"Yes," he said quietly. "The ending."

Outside, the wind found its voice again in the pass, a long low note like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Somewhere above them, along the ridge, the ravens were still repeating fragments of the prophecy in voices that weren't theirs — she could hear them faintly even here, threaded through the wind, as if the mountain itself had learned the words and couldn't stop reciting them. *Two come to the door. One goes through.* Over and over. It had been days now. She wondered if it would ever stop, or if this range would simply carry those words forever, long after all of them were dead.

"I'm going to tell you," Jonah said. "But I want you to understand when I started knowing. Because it matters, and because you deserve to hate me for exactly the right amount of time and not a moment more."

Edmund set down the splinter. He folded his hands. He waited, and there was something terrible in how patient he could make himself, this man who had crossed half the world on a dead country's promise.

"The night you arrived in Thornfell," she said, "before you ever walked through my grandfather's door. The coach comes in at dusk, but I'd been in the shop since morning. I found the book that afternoon. My grandfather's locked case — the one he never opened, the one I picked the lock on because picking locks is also a smuggler's habit and apparently I have nothing else." She laughed, and it came out wrong, a cracked and ugly sound. "A hundred years old, the book. Prophecies of the Northern Reach, some collector's transcription. And there it was, Edmund. Tonight's date. Written a century before tonight existed. And beneath the date, my name. Not a name like mine. *My* name. Jonah Eastwick, of Thornfell, beast-speaker, daughter of no one, granddaughter of a man who sold tobacco and secrets behind a counter."

"The book named you," Edmund said. Not a question. He'd heard enough of the prophecy by now to believe almost anything.

"It named me. And it named the door. And I read the whole passage, front to back, twice, sitting on the floor of that shop with dust in my mouth, while the cat — Bramble, you met him, the grey devil — sat on the case and watched me with those yellow eyes and wouldn't stop staring." Her hands had gone cold again, and not from the cave. "I told myself it was a trick. Smugglers plant things. Collectors forge things. There are people who'd pay a fortune for a girl who believed she was written into a prophecy — I know, because I've helped move merchandise for those people. I told myself every lie there is, and then I went to the tollgate that evening, because the coach was coming and my crew needed eyes on it, and I stood there in the cold telling myself I'd imagined the whole afternoon."

"And then I stepped off the coach," Edmund said.

"And then you stepped off the coach," she agreed, "carrying a map of a country that doesn't exist, and every animal within a mile of that gate went silent at once, and I thought: well. That settles which kind of liar I am. The kind who lies badly."

* * *

The fire popped. A knot of resin split and flared. Edmund hadn't moved. Jonah realized she was gripping her own wrists behind her back, hard enough to bruise, an old habit for keeping her hands honest when her mouth wanted to run.

"Here is the ending," she said. "All of it. No more hiding. The door opens for two. One passes through, and one stays — that much I told you, that much you told me, we've been carrying that between us like a stone we're both pretending weighs nothing. But that's not the clause that matters. The clause that matters is the next line. Listen." She closed her eyes, and the words came up out of her memory whole, worn smooth from a hundred silent repetitions. "*The keeper shall keep. What passes through does not pass back. The door opens once for each winter, and the keeper remains through all of them.*"

Silence. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Say it plainly," Edmund said.

"The keeper can't leave." She opened her eyes and looked straight at him, because he deserved that at least, the plainness of her face while she gutted herself. "Not for a season. Not for a visit. Not ever. Whoever stays behind doesn't stay behind for a night or a year. They become the door's keeper, inside, and the door only opens outward for others. For everyone else it's a road. For the keeper it's a wall." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "That's the price. Not death. Worse than death, maybe — a life, spent in the country we're trying to reach, alone, forever, watching the gate open every winter and close every spring and never once walking out of it. That's what I read in my grandfather's shop while the cat stared at me. That's what I've known since before I ever saw your face."

Edmund was very still. Stillness, she was learning, was his fury's favorite costume. When other men raged, Edmund went quiet as a held breath, and his eyes did arithmetic.

"A hundred years ago," he said slowly, "when Vellharrow closed—"

"If anyone stayed behind then, they're still there."

"My family's debt," he said. "Sworn cartographers. Keep the door findable." He laughed once, short and bitter, and rubbed his mouth with the back of his hand. "My grandmother used to say the oath differently. *Keep the door.* I always thought it was sloppy phrasing. Old words wearing down." He stared into the fire. "It wasn't wearing down. It was the whole oath. Keep the door. Someone has to be kept with it."

"Edmund—"

"How long?" He looked up, and now the stillness broke, just barely, a crack of heat through ice. "How long did you plan to let me walk to that orchard believing we'd choose together? Believing we'd argue about it, fight about it, that it would be some terrible fair choice between us? Did you mean to decide for me? At the threshold? While I was—" He stopped. Jaw working. "Were you going to shove me through, Jonah? Is that the plan I've been sleeping next to?"

"No." It came out fast, too fast. "No, I was never going to shove you anywhere. I was going to go myself."

He blinked. Whatever he'd braced for, it wasn't that.

"You were going to—" He sat back. "You were going to take it yourself. The keeping. The forever."

"I'm the one the prophecy names," she said. "Not you. Your family kept maps; mine apparently kept appointments. It was always supposed to be me. From the first page, Edmund. Before you, before any of this. So yes — I read the ending and I hid it, because if I told you the true shape of the price, you'd do what you're doing right now. You'd start doing sums. You'd start deciding that it should be you, because you're noble in that quiet mule-headed way of yours, and then we'd be two people racing each other to a door, each trying to give away our life before the other can grab it, and—" Her voice gave out. She pressed her palm flat against the stone floor until the cold bit. "And I couldn't watch you do that. I couldn't be the reason you threw yourself into a cage. So I lied. To protect you. Which is what liars always say, isn't it. I've heard that sentence from a hundred mouths in a hundred back rooms and I swore I'd never be the one saying it."

"You lied to protect yourself, too," Edmund said. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it — he said it the way he'd read a contour line off a map, flatly, factually, marking the elevation of the ground between them.

"Yes," Jonah said. "I lied to protect myself. Because I love you, and I knew if I told you the truth, you'd try to die for me, and I couldn't bear it, and that's selfish, and it's true, and both things are true at once and I don't know how to hold them except badly."

* * *

For a while, neither of them spoke. The fire burned down to a red eye. Jonah got out the hard bread and the last of the dried plums and set them between them without ceremony, because hunger didn't pause for heartbreak, and after a moment Edmund ate — mechanically, absently, the way men eat during sieges. She counted it a small victory that he ate at all.

When he finished, he wiped his hands on his knees and said, "At the inn. The one that remembers."

"The Wetherby."

"When the old man recognized my name and wept, and told us Vellharrow wasn't destroyed, it was closed — saved on purpose — you looked at me afterward and you said, *then someone chose to stay.* Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"You already knew what staying meant. You'd known for weeks. And you said it anyway, gently, like it was a kindness." He shook his head slowly. "Every step of this road, you've been carrying that line in your pocket. Every campfire. Every time I talked about seeing Vellharrow — the orchards, the libraries, my great-grandfather's workshop — you listened, and nodded, and somewhere behind your eyes you were reading the last line of the prophecy over and over like a woman counting money she's already spent."

"Yes," Jonah said. There was nothing left to deny. She'd emptied herself out onto the cave floor, and strange as it was, she felt lighter for the bleeding. "That was every day of this journey. You want to know what loving someone is like when you're hiding the worst thing you know? It's like carrying water in your hands. All day. Uphill. Smiling."

Something moved across Edmund's face — pain, yes, but under it, unwillingly, recognition. "I know," he said.

"You don't—"

"I do." He reached into his coat, into the inner pocket where he kept the map wrapped in oilcloth, and instead drew out a folded scrap of paper, soft as cloth from handling, creased into eighths and eighths again. "Because I have to confess something too, Jonah, and you're going to hate the timing of it."

Her stomach dropped. "Edmund."

"The night before Thornfell. In Lowmark. Before I ever met you." He unfolded the paper with hands that weren't quite steady. "I inherited more than the map. My grandmother left letters. Forty years of letters to a cousin who'd emigrated, full of things she couldn't say aloud — including the full text of the prophecy as our family received it. Including the last lines." He smoothed the paper against his thigh. "'*What passes through does not pass back. The keeper remains through all winters.*'"

The wind outside rose and fell. Jonah heard her own heartbeat in it.

"You knew," she said. "Before you ever got on the coach. Before the tollgate. Before—" She gestured at everything: the map, the road, the frozen stair, the inn, the ridge, the bear, her. "You knew the whole time."

"I knew the whole time," Edmund said.

"And you didn't tell me."

"And you didn't tell me," he said, and for the first time since the ridge, something that was almost a smile touched his mouth, crooked and grieving. "So here we are. Two liars on a mountain, each of us certain the other deserved better, each of us planning to throw ourselves into the same cage so the other could walk free."

"That's why you lied about which one the door keeps." It landed on her suddenly, the whole architecture of it. "On the ridge. You said the prophecy names you — you said the keeper's blood matters, the cartographers' line, that it had to be you. You lied first, Edmund. Weeks before I ever lied to the bear. You've been steering me toward that door this entire time, feeding me reasons it must be you, because you decided before you ever saw my face that you'd be the one to stay."

"I decided before I ever saw your face," he agreed softly, "that if the cost of opening that door was a life, it would be mine. It's the only thing my family ever owed anyone. A hundred years of maps, Jonah. A hundred years of my people drawing roads to a country they'd sealed shut, and someone has to settle that account. It was never going to be you."

"It was always going to be me. The prophecy has my name in it."

"The prophecy has my family's oath in it."

They stared at each other across the small dying fire, and the absurdity of it broke over Jonah like cold water — weeks on the road, whiteouts and hunters and a talking bear, all of it two people maneuvering toward the same sacrifice, each convinced they were protecting the other, each lying beautifully and constantly, and the door at the end of it all waiting to accept whichever of them lost the argument.

"You absolute cartographer," she said, and her voice wobbled between fury and laughter and landed somewhere ruinous in between. "You planned to martyr yourself with footnotes."

"You planned to martyr yourself with a smuggling run," he shot back, and now he was laughing too, or something adjacent to laughing, wet-eyed and helpless. "We could have just—"

"We could have just told each other. On the first night. At the tollgate."

"At the tollgate," he said, "you pointed a knife at me."

"You were a stranger carrying contraband geography!"

"And you were beautiful and suspicious and I was already—" He stopped. The laughter drained out of him, leaving something bare underneath. He looked at the fire. "Already halfway gone for you. Which is why the lying got so easy and so awful. Every day I didn't tell you, the truth got heavier, and every day I loved you more, the truth mattered less. Isn't that the trap? You think you'll tell them tomorrow. And then tomorrow, and then it's a month, and then you're on a frozen mountainside having a confession forced out of you by a bear."

"A bear," Jonah said. "Of course it was a bear. My whole life, and the thing that finally makes me honest is a bear."

Outside, high on the ridge, the ravens had gone quiet for the first time in days. Jonah noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping — the silence had a shape. She tilted her head, listening with the other sense, the one she'd spent her whole life hiding. Far below them in the valley, something vast was moving through the dark. Not a beast. Weather. The air pressure had changed; she could taste it on her back teeth, metallic and enormous.

"Snow," she said. "Real snow. Not squalls. The front. Winter's coming down off the peak tonight, all of it at once."

Edmund was already moving, checking their stores with a soldier's economy. "How long do we have?"

"Hours. Maybe fewer. And Edmund—" she caught his sleeve, and he stilled, and for a moment they were just two people holding onto each other in a cave at the top of the world, stripped clean of every secret, poorer and richer than they'd ever been. "No more lies between us. Whatever happens at that door. Promise me. If you try to take it from me, I'll fight you. If I try to take it from you, you fight me. But we lie to the hunters, we lie to the collectors, we lie to the whole cursed world if we have to — just never to each other again. Deal?"

"Deal," Edmund said. Then his eyes went past her shoulder, to the cave mouth, and the color left his face. "Jonah."

She turned.

Down in the black valley, tiny with distance, fires were moving. Lanterns in a chain, descending the far slope toward the Hollow — a dozen of them, maybe more, spaced like beads on a wire. The hunters from Lowmark. They'd lost the trail on the ridge, and so they'd done the one thing she'd have done in their place: given up chasing and started waiting, heading for the destination instead of the travelers, betting that every road on this mountain ended at the same door.

"They're going for the orchard," Jonah said.

"They're going for the door." Edmund folded the letter, tucked it away, and pulled the map from its oilcloth — and Jonah saw it even from across the fire, saw the ink shift and crawl like something alive under the paper, the roads rearranging themselves as the storm gathered overhead. The map was choosing too. Everything on this mountain was choosing, tonight, all at once.

The first flakes came down through the cave mouth, fat and slow, unhurried, as if the sky had all the time in the world and meant to use it.

"Then we'd better get ahead of them," Jonah said, and kicked dirt over the fire.
