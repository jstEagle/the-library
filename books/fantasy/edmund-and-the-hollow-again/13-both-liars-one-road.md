## 13. Both Liars, One Road

### Edmund

He was halfway down the eastern trail before the mountain decided he was a fool.

The wind came first — a low moan off the ridge that Edmund mistook for wind, though later he would learn it had been a warning, carried in the throat of some animal Jonah had spoken to and never told him about. Then the light went strange. The sky stopped being a color and became a pressure, grey-white and total, and the snow came down not as snow but as the world itself dissolving.

Edmund stood in it with his collar up and his hands empty and understood, with the particular clarity of men who have made an unforgivable choice, that he could not see three feet in any direction. The path was gone. The cairns were gone. Somewhere behind him — a hundred yards, perhaps less — Jonah had turned her back on him and walked the other way, because he had lied to her, and she had caught him at it, and there was no road back from a thing like that except the one you walked yourself, on your knees if necessary.

*The door opens for two,* he had told her, weeks ago, by a fire whose ashes were now somewhere under this whiteout. *And keeps one.* All of that was true. What he had lied about was which one it kept. He had read the ending of the prophecy alone, at night, while Jonah slept with her back against her pack and her hand curled near her knife, and he had known from that moment what he intended to do: stand in the doorway, let her pass through, and let the door take him instead. He had built the whole journey around that intention the way a cathedral is built around a relic. And then she had confessed last night, weeping with shame in the lee of a dead pine, that she'd known too — that she'd hidden it since Thornfell, since the very first night, since before he ever spoke to her — and every stone in his careful architecture had come loose at once.

They had fought. Not loudly; they were too tired and too high up for loud. But the words had been knives laid flat against skin, and when she said *you don't get to die for me out of guilt, Edmund Vell*, he had said something unforgivable back — that her gift made her the obvious keeper, that a country of beasts needed a woman who could hear them more than it needed a man who drew lines on paper — and she had looked at him the way you look at a door closing, and she had shouldered her pack, and she had walked east while he walked west.

Now the mountain had folded them both into its fist.

"Jonah!" His voice went nowhere. It left his mouth and was eaten. He shouted again anyway, turning a slow circle, arms out, feeling snow pack into his cuffs and melt down his wrists. "JONAH!"

Nothing. White on white on white. The kind of blindness where a man can walk confidently off a cliff or confidently in a circle and never know which sin he's committing.

His grandmother's voice, dead thirty years, surfaced in his memory: *A cartographer lost in fog does not move. A moving man is drawing a line, and a line drawn blind is a lie.* So Edmund stopped. He dug into the drift at his feet until he found earth, stamped a marker of stones, and stood over it like a lighthouse with no lamp, and hated himself efficiently and thoroughly, the way he did everything.

It was the ravens who found him. Three of them, dropping out of the murk like thrown black stones, landing on the cairn and screaming at him — and it was not raven-speech, he knew that much now, he had learned enough from Jonah to know the difference between a bird being a bird and a bird being used. These were used. Their voices had a second voice underneath them, older, patient, almost amused.

*Fool cartographer,* said the ravens, or the thing through the ravens. *She is west. You are east. The mountain will close the passes tonight and keep them closed till spring.*

"And if I go west?"

*You will freeze before you reach her. Unless you are carried.*

"Carried by what?"

The ravens tilted their heads in unison, three small mirrors, and said nothing more, and Edmund felt the answer arrive in his body rather than his mind: by me. By the mountain. By whatever old attention lived under the snow and had been watching them since the Hollow, since the inn, perhaps since Thornfell — since a girl touched a map and every living thing went quiet around her. Vellharrow had chosen them. He had written that in his own journal, in his own hand, days ago, believing it abstractly. He had not expected to be *handled*.

"Then carry me," he said to the storm, and felt immediately ridiculous, and immediately after that felt the snow shift beneath his boots — not an avalanche, just a settling, a suggestion, the ground equivalent of someone clearing their throat — and a shape appeared at the edge of visibility: low, four-legged, broad-shouldered, patient. A mountain pony gone feral generations back, gray as weather, standing exactly where a sensible traveler would want a pony to stand.

Edmund looked at it for a long moment.

"I can't pay you," he said.

The pony blew out a breath that steamed, and turned its head west, and waited, and Edmund — liar, heir, fool, lover — took hold of its mane and let himself be led.

### Jonah

Jonah had gotten perhaps half a mile before the whiteout erased the world, and she had done the math quickly and without sentiment: east trail, lower elevation, shelter at the split-rock overhang by dark if she moved fast. She was good at surviving alone. She had been good at it her whole life, long before any prophecy put its cold finger on her name. Alone was a country she knew how to live in.

So it infuriated her that she kept stopping.

Every fifty paces, something made her turn — a sound behind her, a shift in the wind, the irrational certainty that she'd dropped something. She checked her pack twice. Knife, flint, bread, the burned half of the map she'd rescued from the orchard fire's edge, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs. Nothing missing. Nothing missing except, said a small vicious voice, the entire rest of your life, which you left walking the other way in the snow.

"You hid it from him," she muttered to herself, to the storm, to nobody. "A year of hiding it. And then you're angry he hid it back."

The truth was worse than anger and she knew it. The truth was that when Edmund confessed last night — when he said, flat and terrible, *I read the ending in Lowmark, I've always meant to be the one who stays* — she had felt relief like a blade sliding out of a wound, and underneath the relief, shame upon shame: because his lie excused hers, and hers excused his, and two people who loved each other had spent the whole journey building separate sacrificial altars and calling it devotion. Two liars, one road. They had been so busy dying nobly for each other that neither of them had once asked the other to *live*.

The storm thickened. Jonah found the split-rock by luck or by the mountain's mercy and wedged herself into the overhang, and there she sat in the blue-grey failing light, shivering, listening to the wind say her name in voices that were not wind. That had been happening since the Hollow. Beasts along the trail repeating fragments of the prophecy in voices not their own — marmots reciting dates, an eagle screaming the word *keeper* over a frozen lake — and she had stopped asking whether it was the country bleeding through or her own guilt wearing feathers.

Then the ravens came for her too. Three of them, landing on the rock above her head, and the voice beneath their voices was the same one she'd heard in dreams since she was nine years old, the one her grandfather's cat had used that first terrible night in the bookshop: old, unhurried, faintly fond.

*He is coming to you,* they said. *West trail, on the gray mare. Do not make him knock twice.*

"He hates me," Jonah said. Her breath cracked in the cold. "I said things—"

*Yes,* said the ravens, agreeably. *And he said things. The mountain has heard worse. The mountain has heard vows, in its time.* They shuffled their feathers. *Keeper. A door held shut by resentment becomes a prison. You were warned. But a door opened by love —*

"—becomes a door," Jonah finished. "I know. I've known since I was nine years old. That's the whole problem. Knowing things doesn't make you brave enough to say them."

The ravens considered this with three identical tilts of the head.

*No,* one admitted. *But saying them makes the next part possible.*

They flew up into the whiteness and were gone, and Jonah sat with her knees pulled up and her heart going hard, and thought about what it would mean to stop lying. Not the big lies — those were already paid for, confessed, ash. The small ones. The ones still warm in her mouth. *I'm not afraid of staying,* she had told him. Lie. *I chose the door because it was duty.* Lie, and a cowardly one, because the truth was that she had chosen the door the way you choose anything you love: greedily, completely, with her whole ridiculous heart. Vellharrow was a country of beasts with no one to hear them, and she was a beast-speaker, and wanting it wasn't sacrifice. Wanting it was the most selfish thing she had ever done, and she had dressed it in duty so no one — least of all Edmund — would see her reaching out her hands.

Hoofbeats. Faint, muffled, wrong for the storm — and then the grey mare emerged from the white like a fish surfacing, snow crusted in her mane, and on her back, half-frozen, lips cracked, eyes red-rimmed and furious and terrified and hopeful all at once, was Edmund.

They stared at each other across six feet of swirling snow.

"The ravens," he said.

"Yeah."

"They threatened me with a horse."

"With a pony, probably. Ravens are precise." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. He slid off the mare's back and nearly fell, and she caught his arm without deciding to, and then neither of them moved, holding each other upright under the rock, breathing white.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

"You owe me several. Start anywhere."

"I said your gift made you the obvious keeper. That was—" His jaw worked. "That was me trying to make my dying feel like arithmetic. Like there was a correct answer and I'd solved it. There isn't. There's no correct answer, Jonah. There's only who walks through and who stays and both of us bleeding either way, and I thought if I made it math I wouldn't have to admit I was choosing to leave you."

Her throat closed. "And I told you duty chose me. That was also arithmetic. Cheaper math." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods, Edmund. We've been lying with true sentences. That's the worst kind. Every word accurate and the whole thing false."

The mare snorted and stamped, and Jonah reached out — old habit, forbidden habit, the thing she'd spent her life hiding — and let her gift rise. The mare's mind opened to her the way a hand opens: tired, loyal, carrying the flavor of a long walk and a stubborn passenger. *He talked to you the whole way,* Jonah said silently. *What did he say?*

The mare sent back an impression, wordless and devastating: *her name, her name, her name.*

Jonah let go of the mare's nose and turned back to Edmund, who was watching her with the expression of a man watching a lock he'd given up on pick itself.

"What did she tell you?" he asked.

"That you talk too much."

"That sounds accurate." He smiled, and it wobbled, and then he stopped smiling and took both her hands in his frozen ones, right there in the snow, under the rock, with the storm howling the mountain's oldest secrets around them. "No more arithmetic. No more noble lies. So here's the plain version, and it's the only one I've got: I love you. I've loved you since the tollgate, when you touched my map and every bird in Thornfell went silent, and I thought — *there she is, the person the world goes quiet for.* I planned to stay behind the door because I couldn't bear a country that had you in it and me outside it. Not duty. Not debt. Jealousy of a door. That's the whole shameful truth."

"And mine," Jonah said, "is that I wanted it. Vellharrow. The beasts singing. I've heard lonely animals my whole life and pretended I didn't, because hearing them was the crime my family hid, and I am so tired of pretending. When I stepped toward the door it wasn't sacrifice. It was hunger. I lied and called it duty because wanting felt too big to survive being seen." Her eyes were hot and freezing at once. "Two liars, one road."

"Both liars," he agreed softly. "One road."

Above them, impossibly, the wind eased — not stopped, but gentled, like a great animal lowering its head — and through the thinning white Jonah saw the split-rock, and beyond it the western ridge, and far below, a single point of light where no light should be: warm gold, steady, patient. The Hollow, showing them the way even now. Or showing them the door. Or simply refusing to let them lose each other in the dark.

Edmund followed her gaze. "Is that—"

"Tomorrow's problem." She pulled him deeper under the overhang, and the grey mare crowded in beside them, warm as a hearth. "Tonight we're just two liars who ran out of lies, sharing a rock on a mountain that apparently ships ponies."

He laughed — really laughed, the first time since the orchard burned — and wrapped his coat around them both, and the storm sealed the world away outside their little circle of breath and warmth.

But Jonah lay awake a long time, listening. Because under the wind, under the mountain, faint and unmistakable, something on the far side of the ridge had begun to sing — many voices, beast-voices, weaving together — and they were singing, clear as a bell rung in another room:

*her name, his name, and the word* tomorrow.
