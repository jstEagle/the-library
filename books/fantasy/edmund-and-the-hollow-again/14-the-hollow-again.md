## 14. The Hollow Again

The map had been wrong for three days.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the whole of my inheritance — the Vellharrow cartographers were sworn to it, ink and oath and blood. A map is a promise made to strangers. And the map in my coat had broken every promise it had left: the pass we'd climbed on Tuesday was gone by Wednesday, redrawn as a lake; the lake became a ridge; the ridge became a road I did not remember drawing and could not have drawn, because no one alive had ever seen it. The roads were moving like animals under a blanket, settling into positions they preferred.

Jonah noticed before I said anything. She always did. She'd taken to watching me watch the map the way she watched weather come over the Thornfell peaks — not with fear, exactly, but with the particular attention of someone who has learned that the world keeps its own counsel and only sometimes lets you overhear it.

"It's leading us," she said that morning, breath steaming, standing at the lip of a descent that had not existed when we slept. "Or herding us. There's a difference, and I don't love either option."

"Cartographers have a word for a map that changes," I told her.

"Do they?"

"No. We mostly just burn them and start again." I folded the thing away before it could rearrange itself further. "My grandfather would have called this blasphemy. My father would have called it proof. I'm calling it a road, because my feet are cold and it's the only one going anywhere."

She almost smiled. Three weeks ago she wouldn't have. Three weeks ago she'd looked at me like a debt she hadn't agreed to incur, all suspicion and crossed arms and that voice like flint dragged over flint. Now there was something else underneath, worn smooth by whiteouts and shared bread and the night in the bell-tower when she'd screamed *left, Edmund, LEFT* at a flock of pigeons and I had obeyed without thinking, because her voice had become the truest map I owned.

We went down.

* * *

The change came first as warmth.

You have to understand what cold does to a person after three weeks in the high passes. It stops being a sensation and becomes an occupation. Every thought you have is a thought about cold. Your body is a committee meeting about cold, adjourning briefly for hunger. So when the air turned — gently, gradually, the way bathwater turns — I didn't trust it. I stopped walking and put out my hand like a man testing rain.

"Edmund?" Jonah had gone still ahead of me, and I saw her nostrils flare, the way she did when she was listening with parts of herself that weren't ears.

"Smell it," I said.

Grass. Not the dry, dead, frost-bitten stubble of the passes — green grass, cut grass, grass somewhere being mown or grazed or simply growing, which after three weeks of stone and snow was the most obscene smell imaginable. And under it, apples. Ripe ones. Windfalls rotting sweet in orchard rows.

"It's the wrong season," Jonah whispered. "It's the wrong *everything*. There's nothing below us but the Hollow, and the Hollow's been empty since—"

"Since Vellharrow closed." I heard my own voice go strange. "Since it vanished."

We descended through pine, then birch, then trees I couldn't name, their bark pale and smooth as candle-wax, and the snow thinned underfoot until it was patchy, then absent, then replaced by actual soil, dark and soft, giving beneath our boots like something living. The wind at our backs stayed winter. The wind in our faces was October in a country I'd only ever known as ink.

Then the trees opened, and I saw the orchard.

I have tried, since, to describe it to myself, and I keep failing, so let me fail honestly. It was an orchard in full fruit under a sky still grey with the year's dying. Apples hung heavy on branches that should have been bare a month past. Some of the trees were solid enough to cast shadows. Others were — less. Translucent, like breath on glass, their fruit glowing faintly from within, and when the warm wind moved through them the ghost-trees bent and swayed a half-second behind the real ones, as if remembering how. Between the rows ran a road. Not a track, not a suggestion — a proper road, graded and gravelled, with wheel-ruts worn deep by a century of carts that had never come.

And the road went somewhere. That was the part that undid me. Every road in the passes had ended in rockfall or whiteout or the simple honest nothing of high country. This one curved away between the trees, down and onward, confident as a sentence, going where it had always gone.

Jonah walked to the nearest ghost-tree and put her hand flat against its trunk. Her hand didn't pass through. It sank, slightly, the way a foot sinks into moss, and the tree — I swear this, and I know maps, I know what is real — the tree leaned toward her.

"They're not dead," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle. "Edmund. They're not dead, they're *waiting*. They've been waiting so long they've forgotten they're waiting." She looked up at me and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "This is what it's like for them. All of them. Every beast I've ever spoken to that went quiet and strange near the border — they weren't afraid of something here. They were *homesick*."

I took out the map.

It had changed again. Of course it had. But now the changes made a kind of terrible sense: the moving roads, the vanishing passes, the route that had herded us east and up and over — they all converged here, on this valley, on this orchard, and at the center of the convergence the old vellum had gone soft and dark, and lines were surfacing in it that I had never drawn, fine and sure as spider-silk, sketching streets. A town square. A bell-tower. A river running silver where the paper should have been blank.

Vellharrow wasn't bleeding through. Vellharrow was *surfacing*, the way a drowned thing surfaces, and our map was its face breaking water.

"My family kept this door findable," I said slowly, and hated the words even as I said them, because they had been my creed since I was nine years old and my father first put a compass in my hand. "That was the debt. That was the honor. Keep the way open. Keep the memory alive. One day the door opens and we walk our country home."

"And now?"

"Now I think we were never mapmakers." I knelt in the warm grass, in the impossible October light, and pressed my palm to the page. "I think we were jailers who forgot which side of the bars we stood on."

* * *

We followed the road because there was nothing else to do and nowhere else to go, and because — I'll admit it — because it was beautiful, and we were starving for beauty the way we'd been starving for everything else. The orchards gave way to fields, some real, some remembered, wheat standing gold in rows beside wheat that shimmered like heat-haze. A well stood at a crossroads, its bucket rope new-looking, its stones warm to the touch. Somewhere ahead, faintly, a bell rang once — though when we stopped to listen, it did not ring again, and Jonah said, very quietly, "There are no birds here," and I realized she was right, and that the silence was not empty but *held*, like a room full of people holding their breath.

She stopped at the well and laid both hands on its rim and closed her eyes, and I watched her do the thing she did — the forbidden thing, the gift she'd spent her whole life hiding in a border town that would have burned her for it — and after a long moment her shoulders began to shake.

"What do you hear?" I asked.

"Not voices. Not yet. It's more like—" She struggled. "You know how a house sounds when everyone's asleep? The floor settling, the fire ticking? It's like that. A whole country, asleep. And underneath it—" Her eyes opened, and what was in them frightened me more than the hunters had. "Underneath it, something knows we're here. Something's waking up. And it's saying my name, Edmund. It's been saying my name since the bookshop. Since before the bookshop. Since a hundred years before I was born."

"The prophecy."

"The prophecy's not a prediction." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, rough, angry at her own tears. "It's an *address*. It was never telling the future. It was leaving directions."

I wanted to hold her. I had wanted to hold her for two hundred miles, through whiteouts and ambushes and the long terrible honesty of the mountain, and I had not, because there was a clause in the prophecy sitting between us like a knife on a table — *the door opens for two and keeps one* — and because I had already decided, alone, in the dark, weeks ago, which one of us I intended it to keep, and I had lied to her about it, and every kindness I offered her now was built on top of that lie like a bridge over a gorge.

So instead of holding her, I checked the map, and that was when I saw the hunters.

Not on the road behind us — the road behind us had erased itself, the way everything here seemed to erase and restore itself at will. But at the rim of the valley, along the ridgeline where the pines went black against the sky, torches. Seven of them. Eight. Moving with the unhurried confidence of men who have finally stopped chasing and started herding.

They shouldn't have found us. The whiteout had buried our trail for two days; the shifting passes had confused even Lowmark's trackers; I'd staked my life on geography shaking them loose. But the same force that was pulling us down into the Hollow had evidently pulled them too, because the prophecy-hunters had come to the same conclusion the map had: everything converged here. Everything always had.

"They're not hurrying," Jonah said. She'd seen them too — of course she had; the ravens had probably told her before my eyes did. "Why aren't they hurrying?"

"Because they don't need to catch us." I rolled up the map and my hands were steady, which surprised me. "They need to follow us. We're the key. We're the address. Let the key turn the lock, and then take what's inside." I looked down the road, toward the bell that had rung once, toward whatever sleeping country lay at the end of it. "They're not hunting us anymore, Jonah. They're escorting us."

"Then we run."

"Where?" I gestured at the valley, the orchards, the patient golden fields, the road that went somewhere. "Look around. Everything here goes where it wants. We're not choosing the direction anymore. We haven't been since the pass."

For a moment neither of us moved. The warm wind came through the ghost-orchard behind us and the translucent trees bowed, half a beat late, and the apples glowed like lanterns, and eight torches burned along the ridgeline like a crown of embers, and far down the road — close enough now to see, though I could not have said how it had gotten so close — stood a gate.

It was small. That was the worst of it. After everything — the prophecies, the smugglers, the burning map, the bear's bargain, the bell-tower, the whole mad geography of the last three weeks — the door of Vellharrow was a garden gate, iron and ivy, the kind that might guard a vicar's roses. It stood alone in the middle of the road with no wall attached to either side, and beyond its bars I could see not the road continuing but *light* — a warm, domestic, lamplit light, the light of windows seen across a square at dusk.

And the gate was opening. Slowly. By itself. With a sound like a held breath finally let go.

"Jonah," I said.

"I see it."

"It only opens once." I didn't know how I knew. I knew the way I knew north. "Tonight. Whatever happens tonight — after tonight it's shut for another hundred years, or forever, or until the world ends. The map says so. The map has always said so. I just couldn't read it until now."

Behind us, on the ridge, the torches began to move downhill. No longer unhurried.

Jonah looked at the gate. Then at me. And I saw her arrive, all at once, at the same arithmetic I'd been carrying for weeks — two travelers, one threshold, one price — and I watched her decide, in the space of a heartbeat, to lie to me about it.

I recognized the look, God help me, because I'd been wearing it since the mountain.

"Edmund," she said softly, taking my hand, her fingers cold despite the warm wind, "whatever happens at that gate — promise me you'll stay behind me."

"I promise," I lied.

And together, with the hunters' torches streaming down the slope behind us like a comet's tail, we walked toward the door in the orchard.
