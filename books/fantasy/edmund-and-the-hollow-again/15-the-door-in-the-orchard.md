## 15. The Door in the Orchard

The snow came down soft as ash, and the Hollow breathed warm air against it.

Jonah felt it before she saw it — a current of heat rising from the valley floor like breath from an open mouth, carrying the smell of ripened apples and cut grass and something older underneath, something like the inside of a church. She stopped on the ridge with Edmund a step behind her and let it wash over her face. Above them the sky was iron-grey and shedding its first real snow of winter. Below them, impossibly, the air shimmered.

"There," she said.

Edmund came up beside her, his breath ragged from the descent, his hands blue at the fingertips despite the gloves she'd made him wear since the whiteout. He followed her gaze down into the valley and went very still — the stillness of a man trying not to hope too loudly.

The Hollow was a bowl of land perhaps half a mile across, ringed by cliffs that no map had ever troubled to name. Snow fell into it and did not land. It fell and fell and thinned as it dropped, until near the valley floor it simply stopped, dissolving into that warm updraft like sugar into tea. And in the center of the valley stood an orchard.

Apple trees. Hundreds of them, heavy with fruit, their branches bowed under blossoms *and* apples at once — spring and autumn sharing the same boughs, petals and ripe fruit falling together into grass so green it hurt to look at after days of grey stone. The trees were arranged in rows too straight to be wild and too old to be young, their trunks gnarled like the knuckles of sleeping giants.

"It's real," Edmund whispered. "God help me, it's real."

"Course it's real," Jonah said, though her voice shook. "I've been talking to its bees for two days."

That much was true, and it was the only reason they'd found the place at all. Since the map had rewritten itself — since Edmund had gone grey-faced over it and told her what he'd finally understood about his family's debt — the beasts of the high country had been pulling them along like a leash. Foxes appearing on ridgelines just long enough to turn their heads. A hawk circling twice and flying on toward a pass Jonah would never have chosen. And bees, in the dead of late autumn, when no bee should live — a low warm hum threading through the frozen air whenever they took the wrong turning, fading sweetly when they took the right one.

*This way,* the mountain said, in a hundred small voices. *This way, this way.*

She hadn't told Edmund everything the voices said. That was one secret left, and she meant to keep it until she couldn't.

They went down into the valley single file, along a path of dark earth that appeared out of the snow like a seam unzipping. Where the path crossed the invisible boundary between winter and warmth, Jonah felt it pass through her body — a shiver running backward, cold leaving her bones the way water leaves a wrung cloth. Behind her she heard Edmund gasp.

The grass was soft underfoot. Somewhere ahead, water moved — a stream, unhurried, unfrozen. And the light. The light in the Hollow was the light of a late September afternoon that refused to end, gold and slanting and gentle, though the sky above the cliffs stayed iron and the snow kept falling and never arrived.

"The door will be at the heart of it," Edmund said quietly. "It always is. In every account my family kept — the orchard's heart. There'll be a tree older than the rest." He glanced at her. "Jonah. Once we find it—"

"I know what happens once we find it."

"You don't. You can't know until you're standing in front of it." His voice was careful, the way it had been careful for three days now, ever since the bear, ever since she'd given her truth freely and watched him carry it away like a wound he didn't know how to dress. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything," she lied, and walked faster.

* * *

The orchard swallowed them.

Up close the trees were stranger than they'd looked from the ridge. Their bark was warm to the touch — actually warm, like the flank of a horse — and when Jonah laid her palm against the nearest trunk she heard, faint and layered beneath the wind, a sound like a choir very far away. Not birdsong. Not quite singing. Something between the two, the way a word remembered is between speech and silence.

*Vellharrow,* the trees murmured. *Vellharrow, Vellharrow.* Her own name threaded through it, once, twice, so softly she might have imagined it.

She pulled her hand back. Edmund was watching her with that expression he wore when he was trying not to ask about her gift — respect and fear and longing all pressed flat together.

"They know us," he said.

"They know *of* us. There's a difference." She wiped her palm on her coat. "Come on. Heart of the orchard. You're the cartographer — navigate."

He navigated. It was strange to watch him work: he'd lost the map to the fire in the escape from Lowmark — most of it, anyway; a scorched corner had survived in his breast pocket, and she knew he slept with it against his heart like a relic — but he moved through the orchard as if the map lived in his feet. Left at the third row. Straight past the well that shouldn't have held water and did, black and mirror-still. Right where the rows bent, following a curve no eye could see but his family's blood apparently remembered.

And the whole time, the snow fell above them and never touched them, and the apples fell around them with soft knocks on the grass, and the far-off choir grew louder, and Jonah counted.

She couldn't help counting. Every step toward the heart of the orchard was a step toward the thing she'd known about since the night in her grandfather's bookshop, since the prophecy's last lines she'd read by candlelight while the cat spoke its warning and she'd told herself it was a smuggler's trick. *The door opens for two and keeps one. The keeper cannot leave.* She'd hidden those words from Edmund for weeks. Then she'd confessed them on the mountainside with a bear's hot breath on her neck, and learned that he had hidden something worse: that he'd decided, before they'd ever kissed, before they'd ever trusted each other with anything larger than a fire and a shared blanket, that *he* would be the one who stayed.

Both liars. One road. They'd said it to each other in the whiteout like a vow, and then they'd laughed, badly, and then they'd held each other in the freezing dark and neither of them had slept.

Now the road was ending, and the lying was over, and there was nothing left between them except the argument they'd been having silently for a hundred miles: which of them the door would take.

"There." Edmund's voice cracked on the word.

At the orchard's heart stood a tree unlike the others. It was vast — vast as a tower, vast as a lie — with silver-grey bark and roots that rose from the ground like the shoulders of something surfacing. Its branches spread wider than any apple tree had a right to spread, and they were bare. No blossoms. No fruit. Every other tree in the Hollow bloomed and burdened at once, but this one waited, naked against the warm gold light, and at its base —

"A gate," Jonah breathed.

It stood in the tree itself, or the tree had grown around it; there was no telling which. An archway of dark wood bound in iron, tall enough for a rider on horseback, set into the trunk as if the tree had opened a mouth. Through the archway there was no bark, no hollow interior, no far side of the tree. There was a road. A cobbled road running away through evening light into a country Jonah had never seen and somehow recognized: rooftops of pale slate, a bell-tower, terraced fields climbing hills that glowed with their own internal dusk. Wind moved the grass there. Smoke rose from chimneys.

A country. Alive. Waiting.

"It's open," Edmund whispered. "It's already open."

"No." Jonah caught his arm. "Look at the edges."

The iron binding the arch was glowing — faintly, unevenly, like coals breathing. And along the gate's threshold, where the cobbles of Vellharrow met the grass of the Hollow, the boundary flickered. For a heartbeat the road would be solid and certain; the next, it shimmered like heat-haze, and through it they could see the far country dimming, guttering, like a lamp running low on oil.

"It's closing," Jonah said. "Or it's deciding. I don't know which."

Edmund stepped closer, and the choir in the trees swelled, and every hair on Jonah's arms stood up. From somewhere deep beyond the gate — deep in Vellharrow — came a sound she felt in her sternum before she heard it with her ears. A bell. One slow toll, bronze and enormous, rolling out across the hidden country and spilling through the archway into the orchard like water over a sill.

Then another. Then another. Bells, dozens of them, all across the unseen valleys, ringing without hurry, ringing the way a country rings when it has waited a hundred years for someone to come home.

"They know," Edmund said. His eyes were wet. "Jonah — they *know*. Someone's ringing the bells."

* * *

They stood before the gate a long time, and neither of them crossed the threshold.

Later, Jonah would try to remember what they said there, and find that memory had taken most of it — folded it away somewhere private, the way you fold away the best hour of your life so the world can't wear it smooth. She remembered fragments. Edmund saying, *My family spent four generations keeping this shut, and I am going to be the Eastwick who opens it, whatever it costs.* Herself saying, *It won't cost you. That's the whole point of what I—* and stopping, because the argument was old and circular and the gate was flickering and there wasn't time.

She remembered him taking both her hands. She remembered the warmth of them, and how absurdly ordinary it felt, standing at the door of a vanished country holding the hand of a man she loved, arguing about who got to be sacrificed.

And she remembered the smell.

It came on the warm wind out of Vellharrow — smoke, but not hearth-smoke. Pitch-smoke. Torch-smoke. Thin and wrong and getting thicker, and beneath it another smell that turned her stomach: burning feathers.

Jonah turned toward the ridge they'd descended an hour ago, and saw the hunters.

They came down into the Hollow in a line, torches blazing against the falling snow — a dozen of them, maybe fifteen, with the Lowmark collectors' grey-and-gold livery burned off their coats and fire-pots swinging from chains. At their head walked a broad figure Jonah knew by silhouette alone: Master Corvane, the auctioneer whose voice had called bids on pieces of countries, who had looked at her across an auction block and priced her gift like a bolt of cloth. He'd escaped the bell-tower with his men and his pride and apparently an inexhaustible supply of fire.

"How?" Edmund's hand closed hard on her shoulder. "The Hollow doesn't show on any map. It doesn't show on *any* map."

"We showed them," Jonah said, and hated herself for the clarity of it. "We led them here. All that way through the passes, all those trails we thought we were covering — we weren't covering anything. We were cutting a road." She watched the torches descend. "The Hollow wanted us. Maybe it doesn't care what else follows."

The first fire-pot arced out from the lead climbers and burst against an apple tree at the orchard's edge. Flames roared up the warm dry bark with a hunger that made no sense — the Hollow was damp with otherworldly spring, but the fire climbed anyway, greedy and fast, and the tree's far-off choir rose into a shriek.

The shriek hit Jonah like a physical blow.

Every beast-speaker knows the sound of animal pain, and she had spent her whole life hearing it and hating it and hiding from it. But this was worse, because these were not animals exactly — they were the trees themselves, and the bees, and the stream, and the grass, the whole living warmth of the Hollow crying out at once, a hundred voices braided into one scream of betrayal. She staggered. Edmund caught her.

"Jonah. Jonah, look at me. We have minutes. Maybe less." His face was streaked with soot and tears and terrible resolve. "The gate's already thinning. When the fire reaches the heart—"

"Then we stop wasting them." She pulled free of his grip and turned to face the orchard, the burning edge of it, the snow falling into flames, and she did the thing she had hidden her entire life, the thing that had made her a smuggler instead of a wonder, the thing her grandfather had locked behind a shop door and a century of silence.

She opened her gift all the way.

It was like opening a door in a flood. The Hollow poured into her — every bee and beetle, every fox denned in the cliffs above, every crow riding the storm-wind, the fish in the unfrozen stream, the worms in the warm dark soil, and beneath them all the great slow voice of the orchard itself, ancient and wounded and *listening*. She had never spoken to so many at once. She had never dared.

"*The door,*" she said, in the voice that isn't a voice, the voice that lives below language. "*Defend the door.*"

The Hollow answered.

It began with the bees — a golden-black river pouring out of the blooming rows, streaming toward the fire-line in a humming arrowhead. Then the crows came down off the cliffs in their hundreds, then the foxes flowing silent between the trunks, then things Jonah had no names for, shapes moving in the deep grass that the Hollow had kept hidden even from her. The hunters' line faltered. A man screamed. Torches scattered.

But fire-pots kept arcing over the treetops, bursting among the blossoms, and the gate — the gate was flickering faster now, the road beyond it strobing between solid and ghost, the bells of Vellharrow ringing quicker, urgent, a country calling across a narrowing gap.

Edmund grabbed her arm. "It has to be tonight. It has to be *now*, Jonah — do you understand? If we don't go through before the fire—"

"I understand."

"And you know what I've decided. You've known since the whiteout. So let me—" He stepped toward the archway, toward the flickering road, and Jonah seized his collar with both fists and hauled him back with all the fury of a woman who had carried one secret too long and would not carry this moment wrong.

"No," she said. "Not you. Not like this. Not while I'm standing here able to stop you."

"Jonah—"

"*Not you.*"

Beyond them the orchard burned and bloomed at once, and the hunters regrouped at the tree-line with fresh fire, and the bells of Vellharrow rang on and on, and the gate narrowed by another finger's width, and Jonah Eastwick turned to face the door she had been walking toward since the night her own name found her in a dead man's book — and stepped forward.
