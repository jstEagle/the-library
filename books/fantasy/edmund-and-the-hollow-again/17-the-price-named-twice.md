## 17. The Price Named Twice

The door was a gate of white wood in a wall of blooming apple trees, and it was closing.

I could see it happening — not the way you see a door swing shut, but the way you watch frost crawl across a window. The gap between the gate and its frame had been wide enough to drive a cart through when we found it. Now it was the width of a wagon. Then the width of a man standing sideways. The warm wind that poured out of Vellharrow — wind that smelled of cut grass and woodsmoke and something older, something like bread baking in a house that hadn't existed for a hundred years — came through in shorter and shorter breaths.

Behind us, the ghost orchard burned.

Not all of it. Not yet. The hunters had come up the valley with torches and pitch and the particular patience of people who believe they are owed something, and they had fired the eastern rows of trees. But these were not ordinary trees, and fire did not move through them the way fire moves through ordinary things. The flames burned slow and strange, like they were being argued with. Blossoms kept opening on branches that were already black. The snow fell into the fire and did not hiss; it simply vanished, as if the orchard were drinking it.

Edmund had the map's ashes in his breast pocket. Half of it, anyway. The half that had fallen through with me when I'd stumbled back from the threshold during the first rush of fighting. He'd told me once that a map was never paper — it was the shape of knowing a place, and paper was only where the shape happened to live. I hadn't understood him then. Watching him press his hand flat against his chest, over that pocket of char, I understood him now.

"Jonah." His voice was raw from smoke and shouting. "It's narrowing. We have to go. *Now.*"

"I know." I was looking at the gap. It was the width of a door now. A proper door, the kind you'd find in any house in Thornfell. "I'm going."

"No," Edmund said. "You're not."

And he shoved me.

He wasn't strong — he was a cartographer, a man who'd spent his life bent over tables and trailing his family's debts across frozen passes — but he caught me off guard, both hands flat against my shoulders, and I went down hard into the snow between the apple rows. By the time I'd rolled onto my knees he was three strides away, walking toward the gate with his coat singed and his hands bleeding from where he'd pulled burning branches off the path, and he was moving with the terrible calm of a man who has already decided everything.

"Edmund—" I got up. My palms stung. Snow had gone down my collar. None of it mattered. "Edmund, *stop.*"

"The prophecy says one stays," he said, without turning around. "One goes free. That was always the shape of it. You told me yourself — you read the ending before I ever knew there was an ending to read." He stopped at the threshold and turned, and his face was so calm it made me furious. "You've carried that alone since Thornfell. Since the night we met. You've been planning this since before you loved me, and I have been planning it since after, and *after* beats *before*, Jonah. That's just arithmetic."

"That is not arithmetic, that is *insanity*—"

"It's the only sum that works." He put his hand against the gate. The white wood glowed faintly under his palm, the way ice glows when a lantern is behind it. "Vellharrow needs a keeper. Someone who knows its roads. Someone whose whole family was sworn to it — badly, wrongly, but sworn. My grandfather helped close this country. Let me be the one who holds it open." He smiled, and it broke my heart, because it was the smile he gave maps. Proud. Finished. "You go back. You take the beasts. You tell their stories. That's your country too — every animal between here and the sea. Don't waste it standing at a closed door grieving me."

"You don't get to grieve *me* by dying at me!" I was shouting now, and somewhere in the burning rows a crow took up the shout after me, relaying it down the valley in my own voice: *don't-grieve-me-dying-at-me, don't-grieve-me-dying-at-me.* My gift had gone strange since we entered the Hollow — every creature within miles hearing me, repeating me, the whole valley a throat. "Edmund, the door doesn't care who walks through! There's no rule that says it has to be you!"

"There's no rule that says it has to be you either," he said, "which is why I'm going first."

And he stepped forward—

—and I threw myself at him.

I hit him at the waist and we went through the gap together, both of us, tumbling out of the snow and into grass. Real grass. Warm, deep, June-green grass, under a sky the color of late afternoon in a summer that had been waiting a hundred years to happen. For one heartbeat we lay there in Vellharrow, both of us, in a meadow full of flowers I had no names for, and I heard — distantly, sweetly, unbearably — singing. Beasts singing. Hundreds of voices, some of them animals I knew and some of them animals that existed nowhere else, all of them singing a name, and the name was mine.

Then Edmund shoved me back toward the gate, and I dug my heels into soil soft as bread and shoved him back, and the doorway swung above us like a mouth deciding.

"*Get out,*" he said.

"*No.*"

"Jonah—"

"You lied to me." I had him by the collar now, both fists, and I was stronger than he was and we both knew it, and I hated that it mattered. "On the ridge. You told me the prophecy said the one who *entered* was freed. You said it plain as rain. And then later I found out you'd misquoted it — deliberately, Edmund, you changed a *word* — because you'd decided to stay before I ever confessed what I'd read. So don't stand there talking about arithmetic. You're a liar. I'm a liar. We are two liars who love each other, and neither of us gets to win this by being noble, because nobility is just lying with better posture!"

He laughed. Actually laughed, breathless, pinned in the grass with a burning country at his back and a closing door over his head, and for a moment he looked so much like the man who'd stepped off the last coach at Thornfell — mud-spattered, map-sick, certain — that I nearly lost my grip entirely.

"You're right," he said. "I lied first. You lied longer. We'll call it even and settle it the fair way." His eyes flicked past my shoulder, to the gate. "Whoever's closer to the door when it shuts—"

"There is no fair way—"

"—*Jonah*—"

We were both scrambling now, graceless, absurd, two people wrestling in a meadow while an orchard burned on the far side of a shrinking miracle. The gap was down to the width of a window. The warm wind came through in gasps. Behind us, in the meadow, the singing had changed key — lower, sadder, the way beasts sing when they understand more than you want them to.

And then a voice came through the gate. A human voice. Ragged and wet and angry.

"Stop. Both of you. Stop, damn you—"

Merrick. The hunters' leader. He had come through the fire — I could see him now through the narrowing gap, half his coat ablaze, one arm hanging wrong where a branch or a beast or the orchard itself had broken it. He dragged himself toward the threshold across ground that smoked, and behind him the rest of his crew had scattered or fallen or fled, and the fire was winning its slow argument with the trees.

"Merrick," Edmund breathed. "Get away from there—"

"You think I want your door?" Merrick coughed, and the cough had blood in it. He propped himself against the gatepost, inside the burn, outside the bloom, a man exactly halfway between countries. "I've spent thirty years hunting prophecies. Thirty years. Do you know what I learned? Every one of them is a debt someone else didn't want to pay." He looked at us — at me still gripping Edmund's collar, at Edmund still braced to shove me through — and something moved across his ruined face that was almost tenderness. "Look at you. Two children fighting over which one gets to die. You think that's love? That's just pride wearing love's coat."

"Stay out of it," I said, but my voice cracked in the middle, because he was dying, plainly dying, and even hunters deserve better than to die mid-argument.

"One thing," Merrick said. "One thing, and then you can go back to shoving each other." He gripped the gatepost with his good hand. The white wood flared under his touch and dimmed, rejecting him gently, the way a sleeping dog shrugs off a stranger's hand. "I knew a keeper once. Long ago. An old woman in Lowmark, mad as March, everyone said. She used to sit by her window and curse the frost every winter. Curse it. Because she'd gone through her door hating it — hating the ones who made her, hating the one who didn't stop her, hating the door itself. And the door took her anyway, because a door doesn't judge. It just keeps." His breath rattled. "A keeper who resents her door becomes its prisoner. The country can't help her then. Nothing can. She'll live forever in a beautiful place and hate every minute, and the door will stay shut to kindness until she's done resenting, and she never, ever finishes." He sagged. "So whatever you two do — walk through willing, or don't walk through at all. The door can tell the difference. Believe me. It can *tell.*"

"Merrick—" Edmund started forward, but the fire surged between them, and Merrick waved him off with the last of his strength.

"Thirty years I thought the prophecy was a treasure," he said. "It's a warning. It was always a warning." He looked straight at me, and his eyes were clearing, going somewhere past the pain. "Beast-speaker. You heard them singing your name. Ask yourself what kind of country sings for a prisoner." His mouth twisted — half a smile, half a wound. "Go on. Decide. But decide *clean*. No debts. No trades. Nobody paying for anybody." He slumped against the post. "That's how she went in. Paying. Don't... don't be her..."

The fire took the rest.

I won't write down what it looked like. Some things belong to the orchard now, and the orchard keeps its own records.

* * *

For a long moment neither of us moved.

The gap was the width of a shutter now. Narrow enough that the light coming through it laid a single bright bar across the meadow grass, and beyond the bar the Hollow burned, and the snow fell into the flames and was drunk, and the blossoms went on opening on the black branches because the orchard did not know how to do anything but bloom.

Edmund sat down in the grass. Just sat, abruptly, like a puppet with cut strings. His hands were shaking. I realized mine were too.

"He's right," Edmund said quietly.

"Don't."

"No — listen to me. He's right about all of it. About the pride." He looked at his hands. "I've been telling myself for weeks that staying was duty. My family's debt. The honorable thing. But you know what it actually was? It was a way to keep you. If I stayed, you'd carry me forever. You'd build your whole life around the shape of my absence, and I'd never have to risk the ordinary, daily, unglamorous business of loving you and possibly losing you anyway. Dying for someone is easy, Jonah. It's *living* for them that costs."

"So what are you saying?" My voice came out small. The singing in the meadow had gone very quiet, the way a room goes quiet when it's listening.

"I'm saying I don't know how to do this." He looked up at me, and there was no map-face anymore, no calm, no finished smile. Just Edmund, frightened and honest. "I don't know how to walk away from you. I don't know how to let you walk away from me. I've been trying to solve it like a route on a chart — pick the line, commit to the line — and there isn't a line. There's just..." He gestured helplessly at the bright bar of light, the burning orchard, the meadow full of listening flowers. "There's just this."

I knelt in front of him. Took his shaking hands in my shaking ones.

"Then we don't decide tonight," I said. "We decide *right now*, and we decide clean, like he said. No trades. Nobody paying for anybody." I swallowed. My heart was going so hard I could hear it in my ears, or maybe that was the meadow, maybe the whole country had one heart and I could hear it. "Edmund. Tell me the truth. Not the noble truth. The real one. What do you want?"

He was quiet for a long time. The bar of light narrowed. Somewhere behind us, in the deep meadow, something large and gentle shifted its weight, waiting.

"I want to go with you," he said finally. "Into Vellharrow. Tonight. Both of us. Even if it's impossible. Even if the door only keeps one. I want to walk through holding your hand and let the door do what doors do, because I am done choosing for both of us, and I am done letting you choose for me." His grip tightened. "And if the door takes me and frees you — then you live, and you come back every first frost, and you tell the beasts I held the line. And if it takes you—" his voice broke, and he let it, "—then I will spend the rest of my life mapping the way back to you. Every year. First frost. Until one of those years the door remembers me kindly."

"And if it takes neither?" I whispered. "If it shuts on both of us?"

"Then we're together in a country that doesn't exist," he said, "and honestly, Jonah, I've had worse winters."

I laughed — a wet, cracked, ridiculous laugh — and pulled him up by the collar, and we stood facing the gate together as the light thinned to a blade, to a wire, to a thread of gold in a wall of blooming dark.

"Not paying," I said. "*Answering.*"

"Not paying," he agreed. "Answering."

Hand in hand, we stepped toward the closing door — and the moment our joined hands crossed the thread of light, the whole meadow began to sing.
