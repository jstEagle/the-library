## 18. Keeper

The orchard was burning, and the door was closing, and Jonah had never heard so many voices at once.

They came from everywhere — the ghost trees groaning in their old wood, the snow hissing on embers, the horses screaming in the hunters' camp below the ridge, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse under ice, the country itself. Vellharrow. Not a voice exactly. More like a listening that had gone on so long it had begun to make its own sound, the way an empty house creaks just to prove it's still standing.

*Hear us,* said everything, all at once. *Hear us. Hear us.*

"I'm trying," Jonah whispered.

Edmund had her by the wrist. His grip was iron and desperation both, and there was blood on his face — someone's, maybe his own, she couldn't tell anymore — and behind him the gate stood open in the last unburned row of apple trees, a rectangle of warm gold light with snow falling through it and *not melting*, snow that belonged to Thornfell passing into air that smelled of June.

"Jonah." His voice cracked down the middle. "It has to be me. It was always going to be me. My family built the lock — let me be the one who finally—"

"No."

"You don't get to say no to this for me!"

"You don't get to say yes for me either!" She wrenched against his grip, not to free herself but to hold on harder, and they stood there swaying like two people dancing badly, shouting over the fire. "You think I haven't done the sums? You've got maps to finish, a name to clear, a whole life of cartography waiting — I've got a gift nobody in Thornfell ever wanted, a grandfather's shop full of books nobody buys, and a cat who'll barely miss me—"

"That's not true," Edmund said, "and you know it's not true," and his voice broke again, because they both knew what was true: that he would miss her until the day he died, that she would miss him, that love had walked up the Frozen Stair beside them for weeks now wearing ordinary clothes and neither of them had been brave enough to name it until it was almost too late to matter.

The door narrowed. She could see it happening, the gold going thin at the edges like candle wax.

And then the dying man spoke.

The hunters' leader lay ten feet away where the bear had left him, half-buried in snow that steamed around him, his coat scorched, his breath coming in wet rags. He'd crawled toward them during the fight — or maybe just toward the fire; Jonah didn't know and wouldn't learn. But his eyes were fixed on the gate, and there was something in his face worse than hatred. It was longing. The pure, bottomless longing of a man who had spent his whole life hunting doors and never once been invited through one.

"Listen," he rasped. "Listen to me, girl, before it shuts."

Edmund moved to stand between him and Jonah. Jonah put a hand on Edmund's arm.

"He's dying," she said. "Let him talk."

The hunter laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing cost him something he didn't have much left of. "A century," he managed. "My family hunted Vellharrow for a hundred years. My grandmother died with a prophecy fragment in her fist. We thought — we always thought — if we could just get *in*. All that gold. All that country. And now I see it—" he lifted one shaking hand toward the golden light, "—and I understand why it closed itself."

"It didn't close itself," Edmund said quietly. "People closed it. To save it."

"Yes." The hunter smiled, and it was the saddest thing Jonah had ever seen. "Yes, exactly. So hear the last thing my line ever learned, cartographer's boy, and carry it if you can." He dragged himself up onto one elbow, and his eyes found Jonah's and held them. "The door takes a keeper. That part of your prophecy is true. But hear the rest, the part that isn't written anywhere: *a keeper who resents her door becomes its prisoner.* Do you understand? Go through angry and you'll spend eternity rattling the bars of a cage you built yourself. Go through as payment and you'll count every day like a debt. The door doesn't want a sacrifice." His voice was failing now, fraying out like smoke. "It wants an answer. There's a difference. There is *all the difference in the world*—"

He fell back. The snow took his breath the way snow does, gently, without ceremony, and Jonah felt the moment his animals went quiet — the horse far down the ridge, the dogs in the camp, even the lice in his coat, every small life that had known him known and then unknowing. She closed her eyes for one heartbeat and gave him the only rite she had: she listened while he went.

Then she turned to Edmund.

"I know what my answer is," she said.

"Jonah—"

"Edmund." She took his face in both hands, and his skin was cold and singed-smelling and beautiful, and she made herself look at him, really look, the way she looked at beasts when she needed the truth underneath the noise. "I've been doing the sums wrong. Both of us have. We kept asking who could *afford* to stay, who owed more, whose life was worth less — and that's why we nearly tore each other apart at this threshold, because you can't trade a person like coin. It was never a price. Don't you feel it?"

The country was singing. Faintly, under the fire and the wind, under everything — a sound like a hundred years of held breath about to come out. And threaded through it, voices she knew: foxes she'd spoken to on the Stair, ravens who'd carried fragments of prophecy in borrowed words, the great bear who had taken her truth as toll. They were all on the other side of that golden light. She could feel them the way you feel sunlight through a wall.

"Vellharrow is full of beasts," she said. "A whole country of them. People who shut themselves away with their cattle and their hawks and their hounds and their wild things, a hundred years ago, and no one to speak for any of them since. No one to hear them." Her throat tightened. "Do you know what that's like? To have a voice and no listener? I do. I've had it my whole life. I hid the best thing about me in a locked bookshop because the world made it dangerous, and every beast I ever loved learned to whisper." She was crying now, and she let herself, because some truths shouldn't be said dry. "I'm not going through as payment, Edmund. I'm not going through for you, or instead of you, or to win some argument we've been having since Lowmark. I'm going through because somewhere in there is a wolf who hasn't been asked how she is in a hundred years, and I am *tired*, I am so tired, of hearing things nobody else can hear and pretending I can't."

Edmund stared at her. Snow gathered on his lashes. Behind him the door thinned to a ribbon of gold.

"And me?" he said, very quietly. "What am I supposed to do with the rest of my life?"

"Map it," Jonah said, and laughed through her tears. "You're a cartographer. Map the Hollow. Find out when the frost opens the way — because it will, Edmund, a country that exists between winters has to open between winters, that's just arithmetic — and then find me. Every year. First frost. You knock, and I'll answer, and that's not a goodbye, that's a *schedule*."

"A schedule," he repeated, and something wild and hopeful and terrified moved across his face like weather.

"A very long-term commission. Your family kept a door findable for a hundred years. Can you keep a promise for one?"

He caught her hands. He pressed his forehead to hers. For a moment neither of them said anything, and the fire roared, and the snow fell, and the door sang, and Jonah understood that grief and rightness are not opposites — they travel together, twin lamplighters, and you let them both in or you let neither.

"If you resent it," Edmund whispered, "if it becomes a cage — you tell the door. You tell the wolves. You tell the first frost. And I will tear the hinges off myself."

"I know," she said. "I know you will. Now kiss me before I lose my nerve, because I'm a smuggler and I will absolutely steal this moment back later if you waste it."

He kissed her, and it tasted like smoke and snow and the inside of a word she'd been carrying since the Frozen Stair, and when they broke apart the door was a thread of gold no wider than her shoulders.

She stepped back. One step. Two. She pulled her grandfather's knife from her belt — the one thing she'd keep, the one thing from Thornfell — and pressed it into Edmund's palm and folded his fingers over it.

"For the map's edge," she said. "When you redraw it. Put Vellharrow back on. Right there. Promise me."

"On my name," he said. "On my family's whole stubborn name."

Jonah turned to the door.

It did not look like a door anymore, up close. It looked like a decision. Warm air came through it smelling of cut grass and apple blossom and animal warmth, hay and hide and living things, and the singing was louder now, and she could pick out individual voices — a dog, deep-voiced and glad; a hawk, sharp and imperious; something enormous and slow and patient that might have been the mountain itself.

*Who comes?* said the door, or the country, or both.

"Not a payment," Jonah said aloud, and her voice didn't shake. "An answer. You called for someone to hear you. I'm someone who hears. I'm Jonah Eastwick, and I'm a beast-speaker, and I have been hiding my whole life, and I am done hiding."

She stepped through.

Warmth closed over her like water. For one instant she stood in two countries at once — snow on her shoulders, blossom-light on her face, Edmund's voice saying her name behind her in the tone people use for prayers — and then the door sealed at her back with a sound like a book closing, soft and final and kind.

Silence. Then not silence.

Grass to her knees. An orchard stretching away under a sky the color of late afternoon that had been late afternoon for a hundred years. And beasts — beasts everywhere, coming out of the treeline and the long grass and the shadows of the ghost houses beyond, wolves and deer and shaggy hill-cattle and birds in numbers that darkened the air, and every single one of them looking at her.

Waiting. A hundred years of waiting, gathered in one field.

Jonah Eastwick knelt down in the grass of Vellharrow, in the country between winters, and opened her arms, and began — at last, at the top of her voice, with no one left to hide from —

to listen.

* * *

The first voice to reach her was small. A hedgehog, ancient and prickly, shuffling out of the orchard grass with the unhurried dignity of the very old.

"You're late," it said.

"I know," Jonah said, laughing and weeping and meaning every syllable. "I'm sorry. I'm here now. Tell me everything."

And the hedgehog opened its mouth, and behind it the whole country drew breath to speak—

—and far away, on the other side of a door only frost could open, a man knelt in the snow holding half a burned map, already counting the days until winter came again.
