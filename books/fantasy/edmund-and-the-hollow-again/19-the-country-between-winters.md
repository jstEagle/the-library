## 19. The Country Between Winters

The first thing Vellharrow did was take her weight.

One moment she was falling through the narrowing door — through cold and fire-smoke and the sound of Edmund shouting her name from the wrong side of everything — and the next, the ground rose up to meet her like a hand under a stumbling child. Not a catch. A courtesy. The grass bent beneath her boots and then held, gently, as though it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to land on it and had practiced.

Jonah lay still.

Above her there was sky, but it was not Thornfell's sky. It was deeper blue than any winter had a right to make, and along its lower edge hung clouds that glowed faintly gold at their undersides, lit by a sun she could feel on her face — warm, patient, entirely wrong for the season she'd left behind. Snow should have been falling here too. She could smell the memory of snow somewhere far off, at the rim of things, like a scent kept in a sealed jar.

She sat up slowly. Her ribs ached where the hunters' leader had struck her with a torch-stave. Her palms were blistered from the burning orchard. She was, she realized distantly, alive in every particular, which felt like an accusation.

"Edmund," she said aloud, and her voice came back to her from three directions at once, soft as an echo in a small stone room, though there was no room and no stone. "Edmund."

Nothing answered. Nothing except —

A bird sang. It was a song she knew, because every thrush in the Thornfell passes sang some version of it, four notes rising and a fifth falling away. But this thrush — and when she turned she saw it, a small brown bird on a low branch of an apple tree in full white blossom — this thrush was singing her name.

Not the word *Jonah*. Beasts didn't use words; that wasn't how the gift worked. It was the shape of her name rendered in birdsong, the *Jo* rising, the *nah* falling away, and underneath it something that no Thornfell bird had ever put into its music: welcome. Plain, unguarded, offered-before-you-asked welcome.

Jonah's gift woke all at once, the way it always did, a warmth behind her sternum like a second heart. And then it kept waking. It went on past the place where it had ever gone before, past the little flickers of understanding she'd spent her whole life hiding, out into something vast and open, and she gasped because suddenly she could hear *everything*.

The orchard was speaking. Not the trees themselves — or not only the trees — but the roots conversing underground in slow creaking syllables, the bees humming in registers below hearing, the worms turning the soil in long contented sentences, the thrush, two wrens, a fox asleep in a hollow log dreaming in pictures, and farther off, much farther, a chorus of voices so numerous they blurred into weather. Every one of them said her name. Every one of them said: *you came, you came, you finally came.*

"We've been singing for you," said a voice behind her, in words, in the common tongue, "for longer than I've been alive, which is saying something, since I'm ninety-one."

* * *

The woman who had spoken stood between two apple trees with a basket on her hip and flour to her elbows. She was small and round and brown as bread, with white hair braided in a crown and eyes the exact grey-green of the sea before weather. Behind her, down a gentle slope, lay a village.

That was the word Jonah's mind supplied, and then immediately objected to. It was a village the way a dream is a room. Stone houses with slate roofs and smoke rising straight from chimneys into windless air. Gardens heavy with late vegetables alongside beds of early spring flowers. Laundry drying on lines. A church tower without a church, just the tower, standing alone in a field like a tooth pulled from a jaw. And beyond it all, fields going gold and green into a distance that seemed, when she tried to follow it with her eyes, to curve slightly upward at the edges, the way the inside of a bowl curves.

"You're dead," Jonah said. It was the first coherent thought she managed, and she said it rudely, the way one says things to family. "All of you. You died a hundred years ago. There's nothing here. There's *nothing here*, it's not on any map—"

"No," the woman agreed comfortably. "It isn't." She set down her basket, took out a loaf wrapped in cloth, and held it toward Jonah with the uncomplicated authority of someone offering food to someone who clearly needed feeding. "Maren Tuller. I baked for the last harvest festival before the Closing, and I've baked every day since, because what else would I do? You look like you haven't eaten since Lowmark. Sit down before your legs decide the matter for you."

Jonah sat. Her legs had indeed been considering it.

The bread was warm. That undid her more than anything else so far — more than the impossible sun, more than the singing. Warm bread meant ovens, meant mornings, meant someone's ordinary life continuing in defiance of every fact Jonah knew about this country. She ate and found herself crying without drama, tears simply present, the way salt is present in bread.

"There was a door," Maren said, sitting beside her on the grass with a sigh of settled joints. "In an orchard. Under snow. My grandmother walked through it the night they closed the country, and she told me about it until the day she died, which was also the day she stopped aging, but that's another matter. She said whoever came through would come through *choosing*. That the door wouldn't open for a thief or a conqueror. Only for someone who gave themselves to the country freely." She looked at Jonah sideways. "You're bleeding, child. And burned. And you came anyway."

"The prophecy said one stays," Jonah said. Her voice came out flat. "I stayed. That's all. It's arithmetic."

"Mm," said Maren Tuller, in the tone of a woman who had raised children and grandchildren and great-grandchildren and had heard every lie arithmetic ever told. "And the one who didn't stay?"

Jonah looked at her hands. "He'll live. That was the point."

"And the rest of the point? The part you're not saying out loud?" Maren picked up the basket again and stood, brisk as morning. "You can keep it unspoken if you like. We're good at keeping things here. Come and see the village. And mind the goose."

"What goose?"

"The goose," said Maren, "has opinions about strangers. Most of ours do. Yours will be along shortly, I expect, once it's finished being furious on your behalf."

* * *

The village was called Harrowfield, Maren said, though names were loose things here. The country between winters didn't hold its borders the way solid countries did. Roads wandered. Fields migrated politely, giving way to one another like dancers changing partners. The church tower had lost its church in the first decade of the Closing, when the people inside had decided that if they were going to be a secret, they may as well be a thorough one, and had dismantled it stone by stone to build a schoolhouse instead.

There were perhaps three hundred souls in Harrowfield, and beyond it other villages, other valleys, a whole country folded up inside the seam of the world. They had closed the doors themselves — that was the truth the half-mad guest at the inn had wept about, and it was true, all of it. A century ago, Vellharrow had been dying. Not by war, though war had circled it like wolves around a fire. By forgetting. Maps had begun to lose it, then songs, then trade, then the interest of kings, and the cartographers of the Tull-and-Vane line — Edmund's line, and the knowledge of that landed in Jonah's chest like a swallowed stone — had understood what was happening and made a choice. Better a country hidden than a country devoured. Better a door shut from the inside than a nation bled white on the altars of larger neighbors.

So they had closed it. All of them. Everyone who remained chose to remain, knowing the price: that they would live outside time's harsh arithmetic, that seasons would soften into suggestion, that no one would ever leave and no one would ever arrive.

No one had ever arrived.

Until now.

"They sent the map out," Maren explained as they walked the village lane, past a smithy where a boy of maybe twelve was hammering iron while a badger supervised from a woodpile with the gravity of a master craftsman. "One copy, to the cartographer's family outside. A thread. In case the country ever needed finding again — or in case someone out there needed finding *in*. Doors have to be tended from both sides, my grandmother said. A door tended from only one side stops being a door and becomes a wall." She glanced at Jonah. "Your young man's family kept the thread a hundred years. That's a kind of faithfulness most people never manage in a lifetime."

"He's not my—" Jonah began, and stopped, because the sentence had nowhere honest to go. *He's not my anything* was a lie. *He's mine* was a different lie, sadder. She said instead: "He thinks I'm dead. Or trapped. He thinks the door took me."

"The door doesn't take," Maren said. "It receives. There's a difference, and he'll work it out. Cartographers always do. It's the whole of their trade — learning the difference between what the land takes and what it gives."

They had reached the village square, if a space between a well, a bakehouse, and an enormous leaning elm could be called a square. And there Jonah stopped walking, because the square was full of beasts.

Not menacingly. Not the way the animals of the high passes had gathered when the prophecy rode their voices. This was — she searched for the word and could only land on the true one — this was *festive*. Goats and geese and dogs and a pair of enormous shaggy cattle, sparrows in the elm, a fox sitting upright like a gentleman at a concert, cats on walls, a raven on the well-sweep, and threading among them all, a hum of attention directed at her with an intensity that made her skin prickle.

"They know," Maren said quietly. "We told them you were coming. Well — we *hoped* loudly, and hope travels fast among beasts here. This is a country of beasts, child, as much as of people. When the Closing came, the wild things came too. They couldn't be mapped either. And for a hundred years they've had no one who could hear them."

Jonah's throat closed.

Her whole life she had hidden. Had flinched from every friendly dog, had kept her eyes down in marketyards, had let her grandfather believe the gift had skipped her as it had skipped him, had smuggled goods through mountain passes rather than risk a single soul learning what lived behind her sternum. A curse, everyone called it, in the old stories. A witch-mark. Something to drown or burn or bury.

And here was a country that had saved a place for it. Had waited a hundred years, singing her name into the dark, the way you sing for someone you love who is coming home along a road you cannot see.

The fox approached first. It sat down in front of her, tail curled over its feet, and looked up, and what it said — not in words, in the deep grammar of beasts, the language beneath language — was: *Say something. Say anything. We have been silent so long.*

"I hear you," Jonah whispered, and her voice broke on it, and the square erupted.

Not in alarm. In joy. The dogs bayed, the geese trumpeted, the cattle lowed a note so low she felt it in her boot-soles, the sparrows burst upward in a wheel of song, and the raven on the well-sweep threw back its head and made a sound no raven should be able to make — clear, bell-like, almost human laughter. The sound rolled out across the valley and came back from the hills multiplied, and Jonah stood in the middle of it with tears running freely down her face and her gift wide open for the first time in her life, hearing all of it, every voice, every name, every long-held question — *what is it like out there, has the world gotten kinder, do they remember us, does anyone remember us* — and she answered them, one by one, as fast as her heart could carry the words.

It was nightfall — or what passed for nightfall here, a slow deepening of the gold-lit blue into violet — before she remembered the charred rectangle pressed against her ribs.

* * *

She'd carried it through the door without deciding to. In the last moment, as the orchard burned and the gate narrowed to a slit of light, Edmund had thrust the map at her — half of it, the surviving half, the western third with its inked mountains and its careful marginal notes in his grandfather's hand — and shouted something she hadn't heard over the fire, and shoved her through, and the door had taken her and the map together, and closed.

Now she sat on the bakehouse step with the burnt fragment across her knees. Half of it was gone to ash and curl. But the surviving portion had changed. She was nearly sure it had changed. Where the roads had run to the edges of the paper and stopped — cut off by the Closing, by the limits of what a cartographer outside could know — new lines had appeared, fine and faint, drawn in no ink she recognized, extending inward. Into Vellharrow. Mapping the unmappable. And at the center of the fragment, where the Hollow should have been, where the door itself should have been, a small mark had appeared that she knew from a hundred nights of studying Edmund's work: his personal sign, the little compass-star he signed his maps with, the one he drew absent-mindedly in frost and spilled flour and the fog of windows.

Except this one was drawn differently. This one had a second star beside it. Two points of light joined by a single line.

"A thread," Maren said, settling beside her with two cups of something hot and honeyed. "That's what it was always meant to be. Not a key. Not a weapon. A thread — so the ones on either side of the door would never lose each other entirely." She nodded at the twin stars. "He's marked himself on it. Look at the line. It's fresh."

Jonah looked. The line between the stars was brighter than the rest of the new work, and as she watched, breath held, it seemed to pulse — very faintly, very slowly, like something breathing on the far side of a wall.

"He's alive," she said. "He's out there. He's—" She stopped, because the next word was *waiting*, and saying it aloud made it real, and real meant a year, a whole year of frost and distance and a door that would not open until first frost came again.

"He's mapping," Maren corrected, with a small dry smile. "Grief is unbearable with idle hands. He'll walk the Hollow until he knows its moods better than the Hollow does. And when the frost comes—" she touched the twin stars with one flour-dusted finger, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal, "—the door will know exactly whom to open for. Both halves of the thread are accounted for. That's more than the door usually gets."

Jonah drew the fragment against her chest and held it there, over her heart, where the gift lived.

Outside, the violet night filled with stars she didn't recognize, and the beasts of Harrowfield sang her name from the fields and the rooftops, softly now, a lullaby instead of a welcome. Somewhere beyond the sealed door, beyond the winter, a man with charcoal-stained fingers was drawing a valley that existed between seasons, drawing it over and over, getting it wrong and getting it wrong and getting it right.

And in the morning — Jonah was sure of it, sure the way she was sure of rain coming and foaling dates and the turn of migratory birds — in the morning she would ask Maren Tuller to teach her the way-keeping, the tending of the door from the inside, everything a keeper needed to know.

Because first frost was only a year away.

And she had half a map to keep warm.
