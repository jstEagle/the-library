## 22. Edmund and the Hollow Again

The frost came early that year, and Jonah felt it before she saw it — a thin bright ache in the orchard's oldest tree, the one with roots on both sides of the door.

She was up before dawn, as she always was now, standing barefoot in the ghost grass while Vellharrow's second winter gathered itself around her like a coat being handed over by someone who loved her. The sky over the Hollow had gone the color of pewter. Somewhere beyond the ridge, in the world she had left, snow would be falling on Thornfell, on her grandfather's locked bookshop, on the tollgate where a stranger once stepped off the last coach of the season carrying a country folded under his arm.

Two years. It had been two years since she stepped through.

"Talking to the trees again?" Edmund's voice came down the slope, warm and rough with sleep. He came through the mist with his collar up and a roll of fresh paper under one arm, ink already staining his fingers because he never could wait for breakfast to start working. "You know they can't answer."

"They can," Jonah said. "They just don't say anything worth hearing before sunrise."

The apple tree at her shoulder creaked, and she laughed. "All right. That one disagrees with you."

Edmund reached her and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and together they looked down into the valley where the door waited — a low stone gate in a wall that led nowhere except everywhere, half-drowned in blooming branches, breathing out warm air into the cold morning like a sleeping animal. Every year it was the same. Every year, when the first true frost crossed the Hollow, the door woke, and the country between winters opened its one eye.

"You're nervous," he said.

"I'm always nervous."

"You're more nervous than always." He turned to look at her, and there it was still — that look he'd had at the tollgate, wary and wondering, a cartographer measuring something he wasn't sure he wanted to find. Two years had worn the wariness away and left only the wonder. "How many this time?"

Jonah breathed out. "Nine letters came through the crack last spring. Nine people asking." She ticked them off on her fingers, an old smuggler's habit. "A woman from Lowmark whose daughter went missing in the passes three winters ago. A boy from Thornfell — you remember him, the cooper's son, the one who talks to bees. An old man who claims he remembers Vellharrow from before, though he'd have to be a hundred and ten, so either he's lying or he's exactly who we've been waiting for. And six others. Six people who wrote 'I need a country that doesn't exist' on paper and pushed it through a door in a wall, which is either madness or faith, and I've stopped trying to tell those apart."

"And we take all nine?"

"We take whoever the beasts vouch for." She said it lightly, but it wasn't light. It had never been light. That was the whole of it, really — the thing she had understood in her first hour inside, standing in a meadow full of creatures singing her name: Vellharrow was not empty. It had never been empty. It was a country of beasts and of the people who chose to stay, and what it had needed for a hundred years was not a keeper with a sword or a king with a bloodline. It needed someone to listen.

She had spent her whole life hiding that she could listen. Now it was her office, her title, her daily work. Keeper of the Door. Speaker for the Hollow.

* * *

They walked down together through the orchard, past rows of trees that fruited out of season and blossomed in defiance of every calendar Edmund had ever drawn. The half-burned map hung framed in the gatehouse — what was left of it, the corner Edmund had saved from the fire, its roads still slowly redrawing themselves whenever the country changed its mind about where it lived. He checked it every morning the way other men check weather. Some mornings the road to the coast had moved a mile east overnight. Some mornings there was a new village where no village had been, and they had to walk out and introduce themselves.

That was the strange arithmetic of the place, the one Jonah had stopped questioning around her second month: Vellharrow existed only between winters, and so it had learned to hold everything loosely. Its people — the ones who had closed the doors a century ago to keep the hunters and collectors and fire-bringers out — had made a virtue of impermanence. Houses were built to be rebuilt. Names were given twice, once in spring and once in autumn, in case one of them didn't survive the closing. Nothing here pretended to be forever, and somehow that made everything kinder.

"They'll ask again," Edmund said quietly as they neared the gate. "The council. They asked last year and I told them I'd put it to you."

"I know what they'll ask."

"Do you?" He stopped and faced her fully, and she saw he'd been carrying this longer than a morning. "They want to know if the door should open wider. Not just the nine. Not just the ones who write letters. They want to know if Vellharrow should go looking for the world again — send word, send maps, tell people it's here."

Jonah looked past him, through the archway, to the far side — the thin place where the air shimmered like heat over a forge, where the other side waited: Thornfell, the passes, the whole cold ordinary world with its smugglers and prophecy-hunters and locked bookshops.

"The last time this country let the world know where it was," she said, "people came with fire."

"Yes."

"But the last time this country shut the world out entirely," she said, "it nearly starved, and it forgot its own songs, and a cartographer's family had to carry a debt for four generations just to keep one door findable." She took his ink-stained hand. "I think the answer is the one we've been living. Open it a crack. Once a year. Let in the ones who need it, and turn away the ones who want to own it. And make them leave in spring."

Edmund exhaled, and some tension went out of him that she suspected had been there since chapter one of everything. "I'll tell them the keepers say a crack."

"The keepers say a crack," she agreed. "And the keepers say the cooper's son gets first pick of the beehives."

* * *

The opening itself never got old, and Jonah hoped it never would.

It happened at dusk, as it always did, when the frost had gone silver on every branch and the first star found the notch in the western ridge. The beasts gathered — that was her favorite part, the part no prophecy had ever mentioned. Wolves from the high forest, patient as monks. The bear from the mountain pass, gray-muzzled now, who had once held her life in the balance of a bargain and had never once mentioned it since, though his eyes said he remembered everything. Birds beyond counting. The cat — her grandfather's cat, who had crossed through with her that first winter and refused ever after to be called anything but Warden of the Threshold, a title Jonah had invented for him and he had accepted with the gravity of a coronation.

They sang the door open. Not words. Something older than words, a chord held by a hundred throats until the air in the archway thinned and warmed and became, simply, elsewhere.

And through it, one by one, hesitant as deer at a forest edge, came the nine.

The old man came first. He was a hundred and twelve, and he was not lying. He stood in the archway and looked at the ghost orchard and the pewter sky and the mountains he had seen last as a boy of two, carried on his mother's hip on the last night before the closing, and he went down on his knees in the frost and wept without any shame at all. Jonah knelt with him. She did not say *welcome home*, because he hadn't been home in a century and the word might break him. She said, "Take your time. The country's not going anywhere until spring."

The cooper's son came through grinning, bees already riding his shoulders like small golden epaulettes, and Warden of the Threshold inspected him at length and pronounced him acceptable by headbutting his shin.

The woman from Lowmark did not find her daughter — the girl had never come to Vellharrow; she had gone south, married, forgotten — but she found something else, in the weeks that followed: a country where grief was given a season and then given rest, where nothing was expected to last forever and so nothing was expected to be perfect either. By midwinter she was teaching in the village school, and she had stopped flinching at the word *home*.

This was the work. This was what the door was for. Not escape — Jonah had learned the difference the hard way, at a threshold with fire coming, with a man she loved shoving her toward safety and herself shoving back. The door was not for running. It was for the ones who needed a country that didn't exist, which, Jonah had come to believe, was eventually everyone.

* * *

Spring closing was harder, every year, and this year hardest of all, because the old man chose to stay.

He had a right to — the stayers' law was plain, written by the founders a century ago: anyone who crosses may remain, if the country consents and the keeper speaks for them. But staying meant staying through the closing, alone among the winter-quiet hills, sealed in with the beasts and the few hardy souls who had chosen permanence-in-impermanence, waiting for the next frost with nothing but snow and memory.

"You could come out," Jonah told him, the night before the gate shut. "See the world again. It's changed. There are trains now. You'd hate them, probably, but you should hate them yourself."

The old man smiled with most of his teeth. "Girl, I spent a hundred and ten years on the outside. I know exactly how much I hate it." He patted her hand, papery and strong. "Besides. Someone has to mind the house while you're away."

So on the last morning, when the frost began to fail and the orchard's blossoms started their long slow surrender, the nine who had come went back through — eight of them, lighter than they'd arrived, carrying apples that would never rot quite right in any other country and letters for the families they'd left, and one of them, the cooper's son, carrying a signed petition from the beekeepers' guild of Vellharrow requesting, formally, an annual trade delegation. Jonah promised to present it to the authorities. The authorities, as far as she could determine, were her, Edmund, a very opinionated goose, and the bear.

Then the beasts sang the chord again, descending this time, a tide going out, and the air in the archway thickened and cooled and became, simply, stone.

The gate closed. The orchard sighed. Somewhere under the snow that would soon bury the pass, a country folded its hands and slept.

Jonah stood before the blank wall with Edmund's arm around her shoulders, and she did not cry, because she had learned that the closing was not an ending. It was a held breath. In seven months — or eight, or however long the world took to get cold enough — the frost would come down off the ridge, and the door would wake, and she would open it again.

But they would not be inside when it opened. That was the arrangement, the strange shape their lives had taken, the shape no prophecy had predicted and no map had ever shown: they kept the door from both sides. Winter in Vellharrow, when the country slept and needed only its stayers and its beasts. Spring, summer, autumn in the world — in Thornfell, where Edmund had reopened the old Eastwick bookshop under a new sign, MAPS & BOOKS, and where Jonah had finally, finally unlocked her grandfather's back room and put the prophecy book on an open shelf, because secrets kept in the dark festered, and truths kept in the light became gifts.

People came to the shop now. Not many. The right ones. A girl who heard her dog thinking. A shepherd whose sheep had started singing fragments of something old. Each autumn, Jonah gave them the same speech she had once needed someone to give her: *the gift is not a curse unless you're alone with it, and you don't have to be alone with it.* Then, at first frost, she walked them up the frozen stair to the Hollow, and knocked on a wall that wasn't a wall, and the door remembered them kindly.

* * *

That phrase had appeared in the book the second winter. Jonah had checked — she checked every year, half expecting the prophecy to keep writing itself, and mostly it didn't. But that winter, the last page, the page that had been scorched almost blank since the fire in the ghost orchard, had grown a single line of ink in a hand none of them recognized, old-fashioned and unhurried:

*and the door remembered them kindly.*

No dates. No names. No price named twice, no keeper condemned, no cruel clause. Just that. Edmund theorized the prophecy had always ended there and the burning had merely hidden it. Jonah had her own theory, which she shared with no one but the cat: prophecies were just doors too, and this one had finally been opened from the correct side.

On the evening of the closing, back in the warm gatehouse with the wind rising outside and the kettle on, Edmund spread his winter's work across the table — the new maps, the ones he'd draw all summer in the shop's back room from memory and from the letters that slipped through the crack each spring. The Hollow rendered in fine ink. The shifting coast. The villages with their double names. And in the corner, where cartographers traditionally put their crest, a small drawing: an apple tree, a stone gate, and two figures standing on either side of it, one in frost and one in blossom.

"What will you put on next year's version?" Jonah asked, chin on his shoulder.

"Hm?"

"The map changes every year. The country moves. What goes in the corner next year?"

Edmund considered this with the seriousness he brought to all questions of cartography, which was to say, the seriousness other men brought to war. "Same drawing," he decided. "Different season. We've earned all four."

Outside, the first real snow of the outer winter began to fall — the world's snow, not Vellharrow's, fat and slow and indifferent. Inside, the kettle sang. On the shelf, the prophecy book sat closed and quiet, its last line legible at last, asking nothing of anyone.

Jonah poured the tea, and thought about the girl who would come to the shop in March, the one who heard her dog thinking, and how frightened she'd been, and how she'd stand at this same table a year from now with frost in her hair and apples in her pockets and a country in her heart that didn't exist — except that it did, and it knew her name, and it was sleeping soundly until they called.

"Warden of the Threshold," she said to the cat, "your round."

The cat rose, stretched, and went to inspect the sealed door, tail high, satisfied that the kingdom was in order.

And far beneath the snow, in the dark and the quiet, something vast and patient and kind turned over in its sleep — and dreamed, Jonah was certain, of frost.
