## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's breakfast arrived at half past seven, as it always did, on a lacquered tray carried by a boy who never met my eyes. I stood at my station in the shadowed alcove beside the serving door, hands folded, face arranged into the particular blankness that had taken me nine years to perfect. The tray held soft-boiled eggs in a silver cup, brown bread, butter stamped with the royal crest, and a pot of honey so pale it looked like light that had given up.

I tasted all of it.

The eggs first—spooning a measure onto my tongue, holding it there while I counted, the way Master Corvus had taught me in my first year. *Count to thirty. Poison is impatient; it will announce itself before thirty if it means to kill you. If it waits longer than that, it wanted to be found, and you should worry about the cook, not the dish.* The egg was clean. Butter, bread, honey. Clean, clean, clean. I touched nothing to my lips that I hadn't first taken into my mouth, and I took nothing into my mouth without weighing it against every death it might contain.

This was my trade. I was very good at it.

"Yarrow." The steward said my name the way he said everything: flatly, already forgetting it. "His Majesty wants the pears at midday again. Tell the kitchen."

"Of course," I said, and he moved on, and I watched him fail to remember me even as he turned away. This was not an accident. Nine years of practice had taught me how to stand so that eyes slid off me like rain off oiled cloth. I kept my hair a color that matched no one's memory of anyone—brown, but not quite brown, a color like dust or like nothing. I wore gray because gray is the color of furniture and furniture is not remembered. When people asked where I was from—and they rarely bothered—I told them *the provinces*, which is a country with no borders, no capital, no name.

I was Ada Yarrow. A yarrow is a weed that grows in cracks. It has a hundred small uses and no one writes poems about it. I had chosen the name myself at fourteen, when I needed one, and I had chosen it the way I chose everything: to leave no mark.

* * *

By night I kept other accounts.

The Hollow Guild met in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, down a stair so narrow you had to turn sideways, past shelves of tallow that smelled of old fat and extinguished things. The guild traded in impossible goods—that was its whole business, and its whole secret. Not stolen crowns or smuggled wine, though we handled those too when the margins pleased Mistress Vetch. No: we dealt in the trades no honest ledger would survive recording. A widow's grief, sold by the ounce to a playwright who couldn't cry on cue. Twenty years of someone's courage, exchanged for a house. Sleep. Shadows. Names.

It was my job to write it all down.

I sat at my desk in the back room—the same room whose outer door opened onto the salt alley, which is why we called it the salt door—with the ledger open before me and the good lamp trimmed low. The ledger itself was impossible, which seemed only fitting. Its pages were grey-green and faintly warm, like skin, and ink sank into them rather than drying, so that an entry became less something written than something swallowed. I had learned early never to lick my fingers when turning pages.

On this particular night the entries were ordinary by our standards. A dream of falling, purchased from a sleepless cobbler, resold to a general who wanted to know what his soldiers feared. Six hours of silence, bottled, from a hermit outside the walls—Mistress Vetch had three buyers waiting. And one line that made me pause with my pen above the page:

*One (1) memory of snow, unseasonable, from the girl on Cooper's Row. Price: two silver and a cure for her father's cough.*

I wrote it out in full, dated it, initialed it. My hand did not shake. It never shook anymore. That was the first thing the guild had bought from me, years ago—a portion of my trembling, if such a thing can be parcelled out—and what remained had been trained out of me like a bad habit. I had been fifteen and starving, and Mistress Vetch had looked at my empty hands and seen not a beggar but a bookkeeper who would never steal, because I understood viscerally, in my bones, what it meant to have nothing left to lose.

She had been almost right about me.

"You're frowning at the page," said a voice from the doorway, "and frowning wrinkles the forehead, and foreheads are how people get remembered."

I didn't startle. Startling is also memorable. But my pen stopped moving for exactly one heartbeat before continuing, and I knew she'd counted it.

Mistress Vetch came into the lamplight carrying two cups of chicory. She was a small woman built like a knot in wood—all grain and hardness, nothing wasted—and she wore her sixty years the way a blade wears its edge. She set one cup at my elbow without asking whether I wanted it. She never asked. Asking implies uncertainty, and Vetch was uncertain of nothing except, perhaps, me.

"The Cooper's Row girl," she said. "You think I paid too little."

"I think," I said carefully, "that memories of snow are about to become scarce."

Something crossed her face then—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "And why would you think that?"

"It's been snowing in the high country since harvest," I said. "The caravans are late. The wool factors are complaining. Snow in autumn isn't remarkable. Snow that doesn't stop is." I dipped my pen. "If half the province is buried by midwinter, everyone will want their childhood winters back, and we'll be selling memories of snow at a crown the flake."

Vetch studied me for a long moment, and I felt the look the way you feel weather changing—pressure behind the ears, a taste of iron. Then she laughed, short and dry as a struck match.

"There's the tongue that tastes for kings," she said. "You can find poison in anything." She drained her chicory and stood. "Finish the ledger. Lock it in the box. And Ada—" she paused at the door, not turning, "—go home by the long way tonight. The short ways have been getting watched."

She left. I sat with my cooling drink and my warm-skinned book and I did not let myself wonder what she'd meant, because wondering leads to worrying and worrying leads to a face that people remember.

But I wrote the entry. And when I closed the ledger I rested my palm on its cover, briefly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, and I felt—or imagined I felt—the faintest pulse beneath the grey-green skin of it, like a second heart keeping time somewhere below the city, below the cellar, below the floor of the world.

* * *

My rooms were three flights up over a dye-shop on Tanner's Row, one room and a closet, furnished in the style of a guest who might leave any day. I owned a bed, a chair, a kettle, a mirror I rarely consulted, and a locked box under a floorboard that held, besides my savings, a bundle of letters I had never sent and could no longer read without pain, addressed to no one, signed with a name I had decided at fourteen to stop using.

That was the whole inventory. I sometimes performed the count at night, lying awake, the way other people say prayers. *A bed. A chair. A kettle. A mirror. A box.* If you own little enough, you cannot be robbed of much, and if you cannot be robbed, you cannot be made desperate, and desperate people do memorable things. This was my philosophy entire, and I had built it brick by careful brick over nine years, and I believed in it the way some people believe in gods: completely, and without comfort.

Because here is the truth of it, the truth I kept folded up small and hidden even from myself, in the place where I hid everything: forgettability is a kind of hunger. To pass unseen through the world, day after day, year after year—to pour your competence out like water into sand and receive in return nothing but a flat nod from a steward, a curt word from a mistress, a king's life preserved by your tongue while the king himself could not have picked you from a crowd of clerks—it wears a groove in you. Loneliness, I mean. I had made loneliness my craft the way other women made lace, stitch by patient stitch, and I told myself the finished work was freedom.

Some nights I almost managed to believe it.

That last night—the night everything cracked—I lay awake doing the inventory. *A bed. A chair. A kettle. A mirror. A box.* Outside, the wind had come around to the north, and it carried a smell I could not place, a smell like a room long shut, like paper gone soft, like cold stone and old snow. Below my window the city went on breathing, ten thousand chimneys, ten thousand small fires, none of them mine.

I was just beginning to drift toward a shallow, competent sleep when I heard footsteps in the alley beneath my window. Slow ones. Dragging ones. The kind of steps a body makes when it is spending its last strength on forward motion and losing the argument.

They stopped directly below me, at the mouth of the salt alley, and a voice—thin, cracked, familiar in a way that made no sense, the way a song is familiar when you've never heard it but dreamed it—said words I would carry the rest of my life:

"Ada. *Ada Yarrow.* You're the one. Please. I haven't much time, and the door won't wait, and neither will I."

No one knew that name. I had invented it myself, alone, at fourteen, and spoken it aloud perhaps twice in nine years. No one living could have known it.

Whoever he was, he was dying on the cobblestones below my window, calling me by the only name I had ever chosen for myself—and he was right.
