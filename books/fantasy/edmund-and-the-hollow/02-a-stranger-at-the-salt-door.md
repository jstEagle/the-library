## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Door

The salt door had a sound, and Ada knew it the way she knew her own pulse.

It was not a lock turning. Locks were for things worth stealing, and the Hollow Guild's back room held nothing but ledgers, lamp oil, and the particular silence of a place that did not officially exist. The sound was softer than that—a wet scrape of iron on stone, like a knife being wiped clean—and it came only when someone who owed the guild, or was owed by it, came in from the alley behind the tannery.

Ada heard it at half past midnight, while she was balancing the week's column of trades: two years of someone's shadow against a jar of stormlight; a first kiss, still warm, sold twice by a broker who should have known better; eleven pounds of sleep, decanted from a feverish child whose mother had signed in a shaking hand. Her pen paused above the page.

She listened. The alley stayed quiet. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere below, the river Vane moved through the capital like a rumor no one repeated twice.

She went back to her figures.

That was the discipline of her life, and she trusted it the way other people trusted God: do the work, keep the columns true, be no one. For nine years she had been Ada Yarrow—no, not even that, not truly, because *Yarrow* was a name she had chosen off a herb-seller's cart precisely because it clung to nothing. She was the woman who tasted the king's supper and was forgotten before she swallowed. She was the guild's ledger-keeper, whose face the brokers' clerks could never quite describe to one another. She had practiced unmemorability the way musicians practice scales: daily, without mercy, until it stopped being an act and became a kind of skin.

So when the footsteps came up the stairs—uneven, dragging, wrong—she did what the skin demanded. She closed the ledger. She doused the lamp to a coal-glow. She picked up the little knife she kept for trimming quills and stood behind the door's hinge-side, where a body entering would have to turn its back to find her.

*Be no one,* she told herself. *No one is safe.*

The door opened without a knock.

He came in sideways, which was the first strange thing, as though he were trying to take up less of the world than a man is allotted. The second strange thing was the blood. It soaked his left side from collar to hip, black in the coal-light, and it left a print on the floorboards like a hand with too many fingers. He was older than she'd guessed from the steps—fifty, sixty, weathered to the color of old rope—and he wore a traveler's oilskin gone stiff with dried spray, though the nearest coast was four days' ride.

"Shut it," he said. "Please. Shut it behind me."

She didn't move. "You have the wrong room."

"No." He turned his head, and even in the dark she felt the weight of his gaze find her exactly, the way a compass finds north—no searching, no doubt. "I don't think I ever have."

Her mouth went dry. There are moments when the mind offers you a small, reasonable explanation—the man is mad, the man is drunk, the man has mistaken this floor for another—and Ada reached for all three and found them flimsy as wet paper. Because he looked at her the way no one had ever looked at her. Not past her. Not through her. *At* her, with recognition so complete it felt like being undressed in a market square.

"You're mistaken," she said, and her voice came out steady, which she counted as a small victory.

"I've been walking since the spring thaw," he said. "I can afford to be a great many things, but not mistaken." He took another step and his knee buckled; he caught himself on the edge of her desk, and the ledger there, and left a smear of red across the green baize like a wound in the cloth. "They're coming behind me. Inquisitors. Maybe worse. I need—" He stopped, breathed, and she heard the wet rattle in it and understood, with a cold settling in her stomach, that this man was not going to survive the hour. He knew it too. It made him strangely calm. "I need you to hold something."

"I'm a food-taster," she said. "I hold nothing."

Something moved through his ruined face—not a smile, but the ghost of where a smile had once lived. "Yes," he said softly. "That's why."

Then he said the name.

Not *Ada*. Not *Yarrow*. Not anything she had ever been called by the matron at the foundling house, or the guild mistress, or the king's chamberlain who signed her wages. He said a name that was short and strange and old, two syllables that landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well, and she heard them fall and fall and never touch bottom.

She had never heard it before. That was the horror of it. And some locked room deep under her ribs answered anyway—some door she hadn't known she carried swung open at the sound, and a draft came out of it that smelled of pine smoke and snow and something green growing under white.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't call me that."

"It's yours." He fumbled at his breast with both hands, and she saw then how bad the wound was, how much of him had already leaked out onto the boards. "It's been yours since the night you were born. I only kept it. All these years, I only kept it for you."

"The night I was born," she said, and hated how her voice shook, "was twenty-two years ago in a charity ward in Corvess. There's nothing to keep."

"There's a whole country to keep," he said.

And then his legs went, and she caught him—or caught enough of him—and they went down together onto the cold floor among the smell of lamp oil and blood, and his hands were pressing something into hers, folding her fingers over it with a strength that surprised her, a dying man's last economy of force.

A map. Folded into eighths, soft as cloth from handling, and warm—warm as if it had been carried against a living heart for a very long time, which perhaps it had.

"Listen." His breath was failing between words now, each one costing him. "The door out of Vell. You'll know it when—the map knows you better than I do. Don't let them burn it. They can't read it, but they'll burn it, burning is all they—" A cough took him, and brought red with it. "The guild will want it. Your mistress. She's sold pieces already, do you understand? Pieces of—she doesn't even know what she's been selling. Don't let them weigh it. Nothing about you weighs right anymore, that's how they'll find you, that's how they found—"

"Who are you?" she asked. "What is Vell?"

But he was looking past her now, or through her, at something on the ceiling or beyond it, and his eyes had gone wide and quiet the way eyes go when the pain stops mattering.

"Snow," he said. "It's starting early this year."

There was no snow. It was the ninth of Harvestmonth, and outside the rain was doing what rain does. But he said it with such relief, such plain homecoming joy, that Ada felt her throat close.

"Take it," he said, and pressed her hands closed over the map one final time. "And forget my face. You're good at forgetting. Better than anyone I ever—" The sentence lost its way. His grip loosened. "Edmund," he said, as if answering a question she'd asked years ago. "My name was Edmund. Write it somewhere. Someone should—"

And then the room was very quiet, and the man called Edmund was dead on her floor with his blood still moving in slow fingers toward the drain in the corner, and Ada knelt over him holding a folded piece of warmth and could not make herself let go.

* * *

She did not remember deciding to hide it. Her hands decided, the way hands decide things—slipping the map inside the false spine of the ledger, into the gap where the guild's oldest accounts slept, before her mind caught up. Then she sat back on her heels in the coal-dark and shook, silently, thoroughly, the way she had not shaken since she was a child in the foundling ward learning that crying aloud got you less, never more.

Footsteps on the stairs. Two sets, heavy, unhurried. The tread of people who had never once needed to hurry.

Ada rose. By the time the enforcers filled the doorway—Brakka with her shaved head and her scale-of-iron stare, and little Ferren behind her with his hooked crowbar and his pleasant, terrible smile—the room was only a room, and Ada was only a clerk standing beside a dead stranger, wearing the expression of mild, forgettable distress that had served her for nine years.

"Well," said Brakka, looking at the blood. "Messy night for deliveries."

"He came in off the street," Ada said. "I couldn't stop the bleeding. I don't even know him."

"Mm." Brakka crossed the room in three strides and crouched over the body. She was not gentle. She turned Edmund's face to the light, then his hands, then—slowly—she opened his oilskin coat and looked at the wound beneath, and whatever she saw there made her go very still.

"Ferren," she said. "Fetch the mistress."

Ferren didn't move. He was looking at Ada. Looking at her the way men look at a column of figures that won't balance.

"She's lying," he said pleasantly.

"Everyone lies," said Brakka. "Fetch the mistress."

When he had gone, Brakka remained crouched by the body, and Ada stood where she was, hands loose at her sides, heart hammering so hard she feared the woman could hear it. The map lay six feet away inside the ledger's false spine, and Ada fixed her eyes on a point just past Brakka's shoulder and thought, with the whole force of her trained mind: *nothing here, nothing here, I am no one, this room contains nothing.*

Brakka straightened. She looked around the room slowly—the desk, the lamp, the shelves of ledgers—and Ada watched her look, and watched her find nothing, because there was nothing to find, because Ada had spent nine years becoming a person the world slid off of.

Except.

Brakka's gaze snagged, just for a heartbeat, on the green baize of the desk. On the smear of blood drying there. And her nostrils flared, once, like a hound's.

"Strange thing," Brakka said, not looking at Ada. "Dead man walks past three doors on this floor to get to yours. Dead man bleeds all over your desk. Dead man dies looking at you." She turned her iron stare upon Ada at last. "You want to tell me what he gave you?"

"Nothing," said Ada. "He was dying. Dying men grab at whoever's near."

"Dying men grab," Brakka agreed. "This one *gave*." She took a step closer, and the floorboard under her weight creaked like a ship. "I've seen a hundred deaths, girl. This one was a delivery."

Ada held her ground, and held her face, and said nothing, and the silence stretched until it sang.

Then the stairs again—Ferren returning, and behind him a third set of footsteps, soft ones, ones Ada knew the way she knew the salt door's scrape. The guild mistress did not hurry either, but her unhurrying was a different animal entirely: silk over wire.

She filled the doorway in her grey dress, grey hair coiled like a sleeping adder, and her eyes—pale, patient, appallingly kind—went first to the body, then to the blood, then to Ada, and rested there.

"Oh, my dear," said the mistress gently. "Whatever have you gotten?"

And Ada, keeper of perfect columns, holder of no one's secrets but the guild's, opened her mouth to lie—and found, to her bottomless terror, that she did not know yet what the truth was going to be.
