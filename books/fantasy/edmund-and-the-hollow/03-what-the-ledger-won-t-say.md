## 3. What the Ledger Won't Say

The Hollow Guild kept its ledgers in a room with no windows, which was sensible, because the things recorded in them had no business being seen by daylight.

Ada descended the spiral stair just past midnight, candle in one hand, the dead man's map folded against her ribs where her heart could feel it like a second heartbeat. She had not opened it again since the salt door. She had not needed to. The shape of it pressed through her shift like a coal that refused to cool, and every time she shifted in her chair at the palace that morning—tasting the king's quail, tasting the king's wine, tasting nothing worth dying for—she felt it there, patient as a debt.

The ledger room smelled of tallow and cold iron and, faintly, of rain that hadn't fallen anywhere yet. Nine shelves of ledgers stood in ranks, bound in leather gone soft as old skin, their spines unlabeled because a labeled spine was an accusation waiting to be read. Only the keeper knew the order. Only the keeper knew which book held trades in years, which held trades in names, which held the single entry, inked in rust-brown, recording the sale of a Tuesday that had never been returned to its owner.

Ada was the keeper. That was the whole of her, if anyone had ever thought to sum her up: Ada Yarrow, who tasted for the Crown by day and remembered for the guild by night. No family. No history before her seventeenth year, when she'd walked into the guild mistress's kitchen half-frozen and been fed, examined, and entered into service in the space of an hour. She had built herself the way masons build with river stone—whatever fit, fitted tight—and she had called the result a life.

She set her candle on the reading table and took down the current ledger, the one bound in grey calfskin with a water stain shaped like a closed eye on its cover. Her own ledger. Her hand had filled every page of it for six years: dates, goods, parties, prices. *One memory of a first snow, sold by a cooper's widow, bought by a man who wished to have never loved his brother. One shadow, pawned against winter. One promise, redeemed early, at a loss.*

She turned to the fresh page near the end, where tonight's entries would go—the dead stranger among them, though what column did a corpse belong in? Goods received? Debts inherited?—and slid the map flat between the pages, deep in the book's body, where paper hid paper the way a knife hides in a loaf.

Then she saw the entry.

It sat three lines above where she'd meant to write, in ink already dry, in handwriting she knew better than her own face, because it *was* her own face—the same forward-leaning slant, the same cramped ascenders, the same little hook she made on the letter *d* that no writing-master had taught her and so no one else could copy.

*Owed to the door out of Vell: one name, freely given. Interest accruing.*

Ada did not move for a long time. The candle flame leaned and righted itself, leaned and righted, as if bowing to something in the room.

She read it again. And again. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense. They simply stayed, black and small and certain, in her own hand, dated four days ago—a day on which she had been nowhere near this room, a day she could account for hour by hour, because accounting for hours was the one thing she had never once failed to do.

Four days ago she had been tasting the king's breakfast, lunch, and supper, and sleeping eight hours in her narrow bed above the tannery, and she had written nothing anywhere, because she wrote only here, only at night, only in this book.

And Vell. The word sat there like a stone in a shoe. She knew it the way you know a word from a dream—familiar, weightless, impossible to hold onto. She tried to say it aloud and her mouth made the shape and produced nothing, a silence shaped exactly like the word, and the silence hurt somewhere behind her sternum, in a place that had no business hurting.

Someone had written in her ledger. In her hand. In her book, on her shelf, behind her locked door, in her windowless room beneath a guildhouse that had taken her in when the world had nothing else to say to her.

Nothing she owned was fully hers. She had known that the way you know the floor is there—by standing on it. Now the floor had spoken.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the ledger room until the candle drowned in its own wax, then lit another, then another, and read backward through six years of her own entries looking for the seam—for the place where someone else's hand had slipped into hers, some page where the slant went wrong or the ink was a shade too brown. She found nothing. Every line was hers. Every line was perfect. That was the worst of it: either the forgery was beyond her skill to detect, or the hand had always been there, and she was the forgery, a copy of a girl walking around in a life that had been written for her.

Near dawn she heard the stair creak, once, twice—the particular rhythm of the guild mistress descending, unhurried, each step placed like a coin on a counter.

Mistress Corvane came into the ledger room carrying two cups of chicory and no expression at all, which from her was a kind of courtesy. She was a broad, grey woman, built like a doorframe, with hands that had strangled chickens in a former life and possibly in her current one. She set both cups on the table, sat across from Ada, and pushed one cup toward her.

"You look," Corvane said, "like a woman who has found something in her books."

Ada wrapped her hands around the cup. The warmth felt borrowed. "There was a man at the salt door last night."

"I know. I sent Bram and Osket to him." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "They were eleven minutes late. I am told he died eleven minutes and some seconds after arriving. I am told—" Corvane's eyes were pale as dishwater and missed nothing, "—that he spoke to you before the end. That he used a word we do not use in this house."

"He said my name." Ada kept her voice level, the way she kept her face level when the king's broth smelled of bitter almond and she had to decide, in the space of a swallow, whether today was the day she died for her wages. "A name I've never used. He called me—"

She stopped. Not because she feared the answer, but because she realized, with a cold sliding feeling, that she had almost said it. The name. His mouth had given it to her and it had lodged under her tongue like a fishbone, and all night, alone with her ledgers, she had been careful not to swallow.

Corvane waited. The guild mistress was very good at waiting; it was most of her trade. People brought her their secrets the way rivers brought silt, gradually and then all at once.

"There's an entry in my ledger," Ada said instead. "In my handwriting. Dated four days ago. I didn't write it."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—not surprise. Recognition, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"May I see?"

Ada turned the book around. She watched the other woman read, watched the stillness settle over her like frost forming on glass, and understood that Corvane had seen this before. Not this entry. This *kind*. The guild traded in impossible things; it stood to reason that impossibility occasionally came home to roost.

"Vell," Corvane said at last, quietly, and the word came out of her whole and unbroken, and Ada's chest seized with a jealousy so sudden and childish it shamed her—that the old woman could say it and she could not.

"What is it?" Ada asked. "What is Vell?"

"A country," Corvane said. "Or the shape where one used to be. Twenty years ago, more or less—it's difficult to be precise about a thing everyone agreed to stop remembering." She turned the ledger back toward Ada with one blunt finger resting beside the entry, not touching the words. "It isn't in any atlas. It isn't in any archive. Ask a scholar and he'll tell you there's a gap in the histories about so long ago, a famine of records, and he'll invent reasons for it, because scholars would rather be wrong than unfinished. But those of us who deal in what can't be owned—" her mouth did something that was not quite a smile, "—we hear the word sometimes. From sellers. From the desperate. It comes up the way water comes up through a cellar floor. You don't ask where from. You mop."

"And the entry? In my hand?"

"I don't know," Corvane said, and Ada believed her, and believing her frightened Ada more than any lie would have. "But I know this, girl. Ledgers don't lie, but they do *wait*. An entry like that isn't a record of a debt. It's a summons. Somewhere, something has decided you owe it, and it has used the only language it could reach you in." She lifted her chicory, drank, set it down. "Your own hand. Think about what that means. Whatever calls you, it doesn't need your voice. It *has* your voice. It has been practicing."

The candle guttered. In the silence Ada heard the sound the guildhouse made at dawn, the settling of beams, the far-off clatter of Bram lighting the kitchen stove, ordinary sounds, and they sounded thin, like paint over a wall that was hollow all the way down.

"The man at the salt door," Corvane said. "What did he give you?"

"Nothing."

"Ada." Gently. Dangerously gently. The guild mistress had never once raised her voice at her in nine years; she had never needed to. "You are the best keeper I have trained and the best liar I have failed to. You are sitting with your shoulders curled around your own ribs like a wall around a city. Something is under your coat or under your tongue, and I will have it, now or later, and the difference between now and later is whether this house stands with you when the Crown comes asking. Because it will come asking. Dead men who speak erased names draw inquisitors the way blood draws sharks."

Ada looked at the ledger. At her own handwriting holding a debt she couldn't remember incurring, to a country she couldn't remember losing, and felt the truth of Corvane's words settle over her like snowfall: everything she had built—the forgettable name, the unremarkable face, the careful, competent, empty life—had been a shelter, and shelters only work until something wants inside badly enough.

"It's a map," she said.

Corvane went very still.

"A map," Ada repeated, and reached into her shift, and drew out the folded paper that had warmed against her heart all night, and laid it on the table between them like a card she didn't know how to play.

The guild mistress looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at Ada, and for the first time in nine years, Yara—Ada—saw fear move plainly across Mistress Corvane's face, quick as a fish under ice.

"Where did he touch you," Corvane said, "when he gave it to you?"

"My hands. Why?"

"Because," the old woman said slowly, "that entry is dated four days ago. And the man died last night. Which means whatever wrote in your book knew he was coming before he did." She rose, joints cracking, and stood over the map without unfolding it, as if it might unfold itself. "Girl, put that away, and bar your door tonight, and sleep with your shoes on. Because if the debt is already in your hand—"

From upstairs, faintly, came the sound of the kitchen bell ringing once—the signal for a visitor at the salt door—and then, after a pause, ringing again, though no one had knocked.
