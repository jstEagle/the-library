## 4. Snow That Falls Upward

She left the capital by the milk road, before dawn, before the bells, wearing a coat that belonged to no one in particular and a face she had spent eleven years sanding smooth. The map rode against her ribs, folded into the false spine of her ledger, and every step of the way out of Vellamere she felt it the way you feel a swallowed coal—small, hot, patient.

The city did not try to stop her. That was the worst part. She had braced for gates and halberds, for the whisper-network of the Hollow Guild to close around her like a fist. Instead the sentries at the north post looked through her as they always had, because being unmemorable was not a trick she could switch off; it was load-bearing. She had built her whole life on the architecture of being overlooked, and now she walked out through its doors untouched, carrying the one thing in the kingdom worth killing for, and no one so much as asked her name.

*No one knows my name,* she thought, on the far side of the wall, where the milk road turned to mud and the hedgerows went silver with frost. *That was supposed to be safety.*

The frost was wrong. She noticed it a mile out, in the gray half-light: frost in July, lying thick on the south-facing slopes where nothing should hold cold past midmorning. She told herself it was weather. Weather happened. Kings died, strangers bled out against salt-cellars, ledgers wrote themselves in your own hand—those were the impossible things, and weather was just weather.

Then, three days later, in the market town of Bracken Hollow, the snow began to fall upward.

* * *

It started small, the way catastrophes do. A single flake rising past the bakery window while Ada haggled over bread, drifting from the cobbles toward the clouds with the unhurried confidence of something that had never been told any different. She watched it go. Then another. Then dozens, then hundreds, lifting off the rooftops and gutters and the shoulders of passersby, spiraling up into a sky gone the color of old pewter, until the whole town seemed to exhale winter into the air above it.

The market did not stop. That frightened her more than the snow itself. A woman selling plums brushed flakes from her awning without comment. Two boys caught them on their tongues and declared, with the solemnity of experts, that they tasted of nothing at all. Only Ada stood in the square with her heart hammering, understanding what the others could not: that snow which forgot which way was down was not weather. It was a symptom. It was Vell, bleeding through the seams of the world, trying to remember gravity, direction, itself.

Edmund's voice came back to her across the days since the salt door: *The country isn't gone. It's forgotten. There's a difference, and the difference is a door.*

She bought the bread. She bought dried apples, a skin of watered wine, a second cloak. She kept her eyes down and her hands still, and she told herself the flake that landed on her sleeve and refused to melt—refused, though her skin was warm, though she pressed her thumb to it until it should have been a drop of water—was only a curiosity. It sat on the wool like a small pale seed. When she finally flicked it away, it rose, obedient to some other law, and joined the slow white river climbing past the eaves.

She meant to leave Bracken Hollow within the hour. She might have, too, if the inquisitor had come an hour earlier or an hour later. But he came exactly when she was halfway across the square, and the crowd parted around him the way crowds part around anything that carries the Crown's paper.

He was younger than she expected, with a scholar's stoop and a soldier's boots, and he held a warrant unfolded in both hands like a man holding a bird he wasn't sure was tame. Even at forty paces Ada could see the paper was wrong. It didn't catch the light; it drank it. The edges smoked faintly, but the smoke fell downward, and where it touched the air, the air frosted.

"Ada Yarrow," the inquisitor said, and her name went through the square like a nail through cloth.

Eleven years of being no one, undone by one sentence. She learned later—he would tell her, in another life, in another chapter of this—that the warrant had whispered her name to him, that Crown paper made from rendered memory does that, that it burns cold and speaks to whoever holds it. But standing in the market square with the snow falling up around her, all she knew was that a stranger had spoken her name aloud for the first time since her mother died, and that the sound of it was unbearable, like hearing a song you'd sworn never to sing again.

"Run," someone advised mildly, and the whole square took the advice at once, because townsfolk will scatter from inquisitors on general principle even when they have nothing to hide.

Ada ran with them. She knew Bracken Hollow now only as a blur of doorways and awnings, the plum-seller screaming about her abandoned cart, the upward snow thickening until it filled the street like a river running the wrong way. Behind her, boots on cobbles—not hurrying. Inquisitors never hurry. They carry paper that cannot be outrun.

She cut through a tannery yard, over a fence slick with lime, down an alley that smelled of ash and old blood, and came out at the town's back gate, which was barred for the night. Beyond it, fields. Beyond the fields, the hills, and somewhere in the hills, according to a dead man's map, the first mark on the way to a country that did not exist.

The warrant found her there. It came around the corner ahead of the inquisitor, unfolding itself as it flew, a white rectangle skating through the rising snow, and it pinned itself to the gatepost an inch from her cheek. The wood beneath it frosted instantly, crackling. Words surfaced on the page in ink that moved like something alive:

*ADA YARROW. WANTED FOR THEFT OF CROWN PROPERTY AND CONCEALMENT OF ERASED GEOGRAPHY. TO BE TAKEN ALIVE. THE PAPER WILL KNOW HER.*

"The paper knows me," the inquisitor repeated, arriving at the mouth of the alley, breathing easily. He had his hands open, empty, non-threatening, which was somehow worse than a drawn blade. "It's been knowing you since Vellamere, actually. I let it lead. It has better instincts than I do." He tilted his head at the rising snow. "You've seen this before, haven't you? You're not afraid of it the way the others are."

"I'm afraid of it exactly the way the others are," Ada said. "I'm just better at standing still."

"That's a taster's answer." He stepped closer. Up close he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the chase—a tiredness behind the eyes, old and sedimentary. "Poisoners are always calm. You learn to be calm or you die at the table." His gaze dropped to the satchel at her side, to the ledger-shaped bulge in it, and something hungry moved through his face and was suppressed. "Give me the map, Yarrow, and the warrant will settle. It doesn't want you dead. Honestly, I don't think it wants anything. It just remembers wanting."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I take it off your body, and the taking is regrettable, and I write 'regrettable' in my report, and we're both very professional about it." He sighed. "I'd rather not. You remind me of—" He stopped himself. The falling-upward snow drifted between them, and one flake settled on his shoulder and stayed, unmelted, and he stared at it with an expression she couldn't read and didn't have time to read.

Because Ada had already understood the shape of the trade.

She had learned it in the guild's back rooms, over the ledgers of impossible things: everything costs, and the coin is never money. The Hollow Guild traded in years, in shadows, in the names of unborn children. And here, under a sky full of ascending snow, at the edge of a country trying to claw its way back into existence, she understood that the currency nearest to hand—the only thing she carried in sufficient quantity—was memory.

The map wanted to be followed. The door wanted to be opened. And the world, she suspected, would exact its tolls along the way, in whatever denominations she had.

"You want the map," she said quietly. "I can't give it to you. But I'll give you something else. Something fair."

"There's nothing fair about warrants."

"There's this." She closed her eyes before she could lose her nerve, reached into herself the way Edmund must once have reached into the country he loved, and found the memory she had guarded hardest: her mother, in the kitchen of a house she could barely picture anymore, flour to the wrists, singing a song in a language Ada had never been able to name. *Vellish,* she realized, in the last instant before she let it go. *It was Vellish. I've known it all along.*

She gave it away.

There was no pain. That was the obscenity of it. The memory simply lifted out of her like heat off a stone, and she felt it pass through the space between them, saw the inquisitor stiffen as it struck the warrant-paper, saw the page drink it down—the kitchen, the flour, the song, the mother's face, all of it dissolving into ink—and the warrant shuddered once, satisfied, and folded itself closed, and drifted back to its master like a fed animal.

And Ada stood at the barred gate of Bracken Hollow holding a hole where her childhood used to be.

She knew facts still. Mother: deceased, twenty years ago, same night as everyone else's missing year. Kitchen: existed, presumably. Song: there had been one. But the texture was gone, the warmth, the flour-dust light of late afternoon, the voice. She reached for her mother's face and found a smooth blankness, like a portrait left out in rain.

"That's what it eats," the inquisitor said softly. He sounded shaken. He looked at the closed warrant in his hand as if it had bitten him. "Memories. I didn't—I didn't know it fed directly. I thought it was just paper." He looked up at her, and for a moment he was not a Crown man at all, just a tired young person standing in impossible snow. "Why would you pay that? For a map?"

"Because a map goes somewhere," Ada said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; grief, it turned out, could be spent calmly, like a merchant counting coins. "A memory doesn't. It just sits in you getting older." She put her hand on the gate bar. "You should look at your warrant's new entry, inquisitor. It ate something of mine. Ask it what it tasted like. Ask yourself why a song in a dead language feels familiar to you."

His face went white. Whether from the cold or the question, she didn't stay to learn.

She climbed the gate, dropped into the field beyond, and ran—not because the paper would not know her still, but because the hills were dark and welcoming and somewhere in them, the first mark waited. Behind her, Bracken Hollow glowed with its ordinary lamps, and above it the snow went on rising, thousands of small white things refusing to fall, refusing to melt, refusing to forget which country they belonged to.

Her cheeks were wet. She wiped them and found she could not remember whether she was crying for her mother or for the loss of the reason to cry.

The map, against her ribs, pulsed once, warm as a swallowed coal, and pointed her on toward the ruins where a dead cartographer was waiting to tell her whose daughter she was.
