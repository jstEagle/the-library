## 6. The Guild's Price

They found me in a chapel that no longer had a god.

The village of Marrowfen had lost its bell, its priest, and its faith sometime in the last century, and what remained was a stone shell with a leaking roof and a smell like wet ash. I'd chosen it because nothing chooses a place like that. I'd learned to sleep in the hollows of the world, the spots where even memory skimmed past without stopping. It was the one skill my old life had given me that I still trusted.

I woke because the snow had stopped.

Not ended—stopped. The upward-falling flakes hung in the air above the broken altar like a held breath, thousands of them, unmoving, each one catching the grey light and giving it back wrong. I sat up with my heart already climbing into my throat, because snow did not pause for anything, not wind, not prayer, not the slow forgetting of names.

"You always did notice the important things," said a voice from the doorway.

Mistress Corvane stood in the ruined arch with her hands folded at her waist, as neat as if she'd stepped out of the guildhouse on Copperlane and not across two hundred miles of unraveling country. She was small and silver-haired and she moved like poured oil, and for eleven years she had been the closest thing I had to a mother, which is a sentence I can only write now, after everything, when it can do no more harm.

Behind her, filling the doorway and spilling into the nave, were six of the guild's enforcers in their grey travel cloaks. I knew three of them by name. One of them, Brannic, had taught me to hold a knife so it looked like an accident waiting elsewhere. He wouldn't meet my eyes.

"Mistress," I said. My voice came out steady. Eleven years of tasting poison will teach you to keep your voice steady while everything inside you curdles. "You're a long way from Vell."

"Vell." She tasted the word like I once tasted wine for the king—carefully, professionally, hunting for what was hidden in it. "There's a name I haven't heard spoken aloud in twenty years."

* * *

She walked the length of the nave unhurried, her boots quiet on the frost-slick flags, and the hanging snow turned slowly above her as though it were watching too. She stopped an arm's length away—the exact distance a taster learns to keep from a dish she hasn't approved—and studied me with those pale eyes that had counted every coin of my life since I was fourteen and starving on the Copperlane steps.

"You look terrible, Ada."

"I've been walking."

"You've been running." She reached out and touched my jaw, turning my face toward the light the way she used to when I came back from the palace with something bitter under my tongue. Her fingers were cold. Her fingers had always been cold; I had told myself for years it was the ledgers' cellar where she kept them. "There are shadows under your eyes like bruises. You've been trading."

I didn't answer. She smiled, and it was the smile she gave new apprentices on their first day, warm as a banked fire, and I hated how much of me leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

"The guild knows about the memories," she said gently. "We know what you paid at Harrowmarket. We know what you paid before that. A girl cannot spend herself forever, child. Eventually there's nothing left in the purse but the name."

"Then you know why I'm here," I said. "Alone."

"I know why you *think* you're here alone." She let go of my chin and folded her hands again. "I'm offering you sanctuary. Real sanctuary—not these ruins, not stolen nights in haylofts. The guild has places the Crown's paper can't burn. Come home. Bring the map. Keep your ledgers. Nothing needs to change except that you stop being hunted."

"And the map?"

"Goes in the vault," she said smoothly. "With the other impossible things. Where it belongs."

The word *other* hung in the cold air between us, and I caught it the way I'd been trained to catch things—a fraction of a second too late to pretend I hadn't.

"What other things, Mistress?"

* * *

She told me. That was the worst part, afterward—that she told me plainly, standing in that dead chapel with the snow suspended overhead, because she had never once lied to me in eleven years and saw no reason to begin now. Lies were for people who feared each other. We were family. Families only ever tell each other the truth when they want something.

"There was never just one door," she said. "Edmund's map shows the great one, the throat of the country, but a country is made of small things. A well that remembered sweet water. A bridge that hummed when you crossed it honest. A hearthstone that never went cold. When Vell was erased, all those little pieces didn't vanish—they fell loose. Like coins from a torn pocket. And coins, Ada, are our trade."

My stomach went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the chapel.

"You sold them."

"We *placed* them," she corrected, with the mild precision she used correcting an apprentice's ledger entries. "A duke wanted a hall his enemies could never find. We found him a doorframe from Vell. A queen wanted a winter that would end her wars. We sold her a handful of Vellish snow—it falls upward, it never melts, and it makes men forget why they marched. The Crown has been buying pieces of a dead country for twenty years, and paying us in gold and in blindness. Why do you think the guild has survived every purge, every inquisition? We are useful to the erasure, child. We are its pawnbrokers."

I thought of the ledger cellar beneath Copperlane. Rows of iron-bound books. Entries in my own careful hand recording trades I'd never understood—a lock of hair that weighed more than it should, a key that opened houses instead of doors, a jar of silence sold to a magistrate. I had catalogued the corpse of a country piece by piece and called it bookkeeping.

"You had me writing the sales," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, and I hated that too. "Eleven years. You put the pen in my hand yourself."

"Of course I did." No shame in her at all. That was the thing I could not look at directly, like the sun. "Who better than a girl born on the night of the erasure? Half in the world, half out of it. Erased things don't hurt you, Ada. They slide right past. You could handle Vell's leavings the way no one else could. It was a gift."

"It was training."

The words came out of me flat and true, and I watched them land. For the first time, Mistress Corvane's composure shifted—not much, a hairline crack, a taster's flicker—but enough.

"Yes," she admitted. "It was training."

* * *

She laid it out for me then, because she believed—I could see it in her face, in the terrible patience of her—that knowing would bring me home. That I would prefer any truth to being unclaimed.

"The door out of Vell doesn't open for maps," she said. "Maps are just paper. It opens for bodies that have been made ready. Twenty years of swallowing erased things, child. Twenty years of tasting what the Crown's cooks laced with Vellish dust—you thought we only tested for arsenic? Every meal, every drop, every impossible trade run through your hands was tempering you. Firing you like clay. There may be four people alive who could walk through that door and live. You are one of them. Possibly the only one left."

"Why?" I whispered. "Why would you build me for a door you wanted locked in a vault?"

"Because the door was going to open anyway." Her voice dropped, and for one moment the oil-smoothness failed and I heard underneath it something old and tired and afraid. "The country is dying again, Ada. You've seen the snow. The long winter isn't punishment and it isn't accident. It's Vell trying to remember itself, and when it finishes remembering—if it finishes—it comes back *all at once*, everywhere, and everything the Crown built on the erasure burns with it. Wars. Treaties. Two thrones. The guild wanted the door found first, opened *first*, controlled. On our terms. With someone we trusted holding the hinge."

"Someone forgettable," I said. "Someone with no family name. Someone whose whole life you designed so she'd have nothing else to be."

The chapel was very quiet. Above the altar, the hanging snow began, very slowly, to move—not falling upward anymore, not falling at all, but drifting sideways, all together, like a flock changing its mind.

Brannic shifted behind her. His hand wasn't on his knife, but it was near it, and he still wouldn't look at me, and I understood finally that his shame was the shape of the order he'd been given.

"Come home," Mistress Corvane said softly. "Give me the map. You'll want for nothing. I swear it on the ledger."

"On the ledger," I repeated. "The ledger I wrote. The ledger full of my country, sold off coin by coin."

"Your country?" Something flickered across her face—pity, or its well-made counterfeit. "Child, you were born in Vell for one night. You've been ours ever since."

And that was the price, laid bare at last. Not the map. The map was paper. The price was this: that I choose which lie to live inside. The one where I was nobody, safe and owned. Or the one where I was Vell's—and hunted by everyone, including the only family I had ever had.

I looked at Brannic. At last, at the very end, he met my eyes. He didn't say *run*. He didn't have to. Eleven years of guild signs passed between us in a single glance, the small grammar of people who survive by noticing: *the east window, the loose stone, the moment before the moment.*

Mistress Corvane opened her hands to me, warm as a hearth, cold as a vault.

"Ada," she said. "Come home."

Above us, all the hanging snow turned at once—and began to fall downward, straight down, like it had somewhere to be.

Like it was answering a summons.

Like the door had already started to open.
