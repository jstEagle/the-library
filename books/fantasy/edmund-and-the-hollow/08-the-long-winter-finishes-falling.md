## 8. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The snow reached the lowlands on the third day after the market town, and it fell wrong everywhere now.

Ada saw it from the ridge above Harrow Fen: flakes rising out of the frozen marsh like sparks off a struck flint, climbing past her face, past the bare black alders, up into a sky the color of old pewter. They did not melt. They did not land. The ground beneath her boots was dry as August dust, though her breath came out in white ribbons and the reeds were stiff with frost. Cold without snow. Snow without weight. A winter that had forgotten which way down was.

Harrow Fen was still there. That was the first thing she noticed—the village existed, chimneys smoking, dogs barking at nothing, a woman beating a rug on a line. But the name of it had gone strange in Ada's mouth when she tried to say it. *Harrow*—she knew the word meant a tool that broke clods of earth, and she could not remember why knowing that mattered, or whether she had known it an hour ago.

That was how it worked. Not all at once. It took the small things first, the way frost takes a window: the edges, then the middle, then everything.

She went down into the village because Edmund's ghost tugged at her like a fisherman's line. He had been growing more solid since the ruins—or perhaps she had been growing less solid, half outside the world as she was, until the space between them hardly mattered. He walked beside her now where before he had only flickered, his cartographer's coat patched at the elbows, his face carrying the particular exhaustion of a man who has been awake for twenty years.

"You shouldn't stop here," he said. His voice sounded like paper being unfolded. "Villages are going fast."

"I need food." She heard how thin her own voice had become. Since trading her mother's memory to Hale's warrant-paper, something in her had gone quiet—not hollow exactly, but *shelved*, like a book put away in a room she could no longer find. Grief, she was learning, was not a thing you felt once. It was a thing you spent, and spent, and spent, until you learned to count what remained.

"There's no food left in a week," Edmund said. "There may be no week."

The inn's common room was warm, which seemed obscene. A dozen people sat around the fire, and Ada watched them with a taster's attention—the same attention she had once given the king's soup, hunting for poison in the almond paste, reading faces for the flicker that meant *something is wrong*. Every face at that fire carried the flicker.

An old man was telling a story to his grandson. "—and so your grandmother says to me, says, *Tam*, she says—" He stopped. His mouth stayed open. He looked at his hands as if they belonged to someone else. "What did I just say?"

"You were talking about Gran," the boy said.

"Who?" said the old man pleasantly. And then, seeing the boy's face fall, he frowned, and reached for the memory the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark that isn't there. "Your—ah. The woman. In the—there was a photograph of her over the—" He gave up. He smiled instead, because smiling was easier than grieving something he could no longer name. "Never mind, lad. Never mind. More tea?"

The boy looked at Ada. Children always noticed her; forgettable adults slid past children's notice the way water slides past stone. His eyes were wet and furious. "It's been happening since yesterday," he whispered, coming to sit across from her uninvited. "First the miller forgot his dog. Then my teacher wrote her own name on the board and stood there staring at it like it was written by a stranger. Then Mrs. Cotter stopped halfway through grace and couldn't finish, because she'd forgotten who she was thanking." He lowered his voice further. "My mum says it's miasma. My dad says it's the Crown testing something. I think—" He hesitated, then said, very quietly, "I think the country's dying."

Ada set down her cup. "Why do you think that?"

"Because of the snow." He nodded toward the window, where flakes rose past the glass like inverted rain. "Snow falls *down*. Things that are dying don't get to choose which way they fall. Unless—" and here the boy's voice cracked with a certainty far too large for him, "—unless they're trying to get back up."

Edmund made a sound beside her that might have been a laugh or a wound. When she glanced over, he was looking at the boy with an expression she hadn't seen on him before: recognition. *This one would have made a fine mapmaker,* the ghost's face said. *In another country. In a country that lasted.*

* * *

They left before dusk, and the village did not see them go. By then three more people had paused mid-sentence, mouths open, reaching for words that had been taken out from under them like a tablecloth pulled clean away.

Ada climbed the eastern road with the map against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat. It had changed again overnight. She had stopped being surprised by this. New lines inked themselves along its margins in handwriting that was hers and older than her, and the route it drew had shortened—no longer the long spiral approach it had demanded in the ruins' shadow, but a straight line now, urgent, like a vein running toward the heart. The door out of Vell wanted her closer. Or Vell did. She was beginning to suspect those were the same want.

At the top of the pass she stopped and looked back, and understood, finally, what she had only been circling for days.

The winter was not falling *on* the country. It was falling *out* of it. Every upward snowflake was a piece of Vell trying to rise back into the world—a chimney smoke, a grandmother's name, a dog's bark, a grace before tea—climbing toward somewhere it remembered being, and failing, and falling up forever because there was no longer any *there* for it to arrive at. Twenty years ago someone had erased a country, and everyone in it, and every record and road and grave. But erasure, Ada saw now, was not a wall. It was a wound. And wounds do not close cleanly just because the surgeons go home. The country was bleeding backward into the sky.

"It's dying," she said aloud. Her breath tore away sideways in the wind. "Not punishing us. Not hunting me. Dying. Again."

"Yes." Edmund stood at the edge of the overlook, looking down at the valley where Harrow Fen's lights were going out one window at a time—and not because anyone had gone to bed. "The first time, they cut it out whole. Names, borders, debts, dead. A country isn't a place, Ada. It's an agreement. Enough people agreeing to remember the same things in the same shape. They broke the agreement, and Vell has been holding itself together by sheer stubbornness ever since—me, mostly, and the others like me, and the map, and the door." He turned to her, and his tiredness was total now, a tiredness with bedrock under it. "But stubbornness spends down. The door is its last heartbeat. When it stops—"

"The snow stops rising," Ada finished. "And everything it was carrying falls nowhere. Forever."

"And everyone the erasure swallowed stays swallowed. Including," Edmund added gently, "a certain inquisitor who has been following you for nine days and is currently standing behind you."

* * *

She didn't reach for the knife. There was little point; she had seen what Hale's warrant-paper could do, and besides, some part of her had known he was there since the second bend in the road—he walked like a man raised in a country where you stepped softly, even if he didn't know anymore why.

"Inquisitor," she said, without turning.

"Lady Yarrow." His voice was rougher than it had been in the market town. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." Now she turned. "Don't say it. You don't know what it costs to be called by a name the world has already taken back."

Hale stood six paces off, snow rising past him, his gray coat furred with frost that never melted and never accumulated either—frost in a state of argument with itself. The warrant was in his hand. She could hear it whispering even from here, that dry cold rustle, like pages turning themselves. But he wasn't reading it. He was looking past her, down into the darkening valley, and his face was doing something warrants are not designed to survive.

"I grew up somewhere," he said. "I've told myself that my whole life. Somewhere with a river you could walk across in February. Somewhere with a bell that rang wrong on purpose, so people would know the hour without asking." He swallowed. "I filed it under childhood fancy. Twenty years of reports and interrogations and burnings-at-the-stake-of-record, and I filed my own childhood under *fancy*." He lifted the warrant. It strained toward her like a dog on a lead. "This paper wants your name. It's been wanting it louder every day. And yesterday morning I woke up and realized I couldn't remember my mother's voice, but I could remember a bell ringing wrong on purpose, and I understood that I am not hunting you, Lady Yarrow. I'm hunting the only person alive who knows the way back to the place I was stolen from."

"You'd arrest me anyway," Ada said. It wasn't a question. "Obedience is the only country you have left."

Something moved behind his eyes—hurt, or its respectable cousin. "You traded your mother's memory to escape me," he said. "Do you imagine I don't know what that cost? I read the residue off the warrant-paper. I've carried it for days." He closed his fist around the whispering document. "So no. I won't arrest you. Because arresting you serves a Crown that is currently presiding over villages full of people forgetting their own children, and calling it weather. And because—" his jaw worked, "—because I am done filing myself under *fancy*."

Edmund drifted forward, and Hale flinched—the living always flinched, even inquisitors—but held his ground as the ghost regarded him with something like appraisal.

"He's telling the truth," Edmund said to Ada. "Mostly. Men like him are mostly true the way rivers are mostly water. But the direction matters."

"What do you propose, then?" Ada asked Hale. "A pact? You've seen what happens to pacts between the Crown and everything else."

"I propose nothing so grand as a pact." Hale's voice dropped, and for a moment he was not an inquisitor at all but a boy standing in a February river-country, listening to a wrong bell. "I propose we walk together to wherever that map ends, and when we arrive, I will stand between you and whatever comes after us—the guild, the Crown, my own colleagues—and you will open whatever needs opening. And in exchange—" Here he faltered, and the falter cost him visibly. "In exchange, if there's anything on the other side that remembers a bell, you let me go to it. Even if it kills me. *Especially* if it kills me."

The wind came up the pass, and with it the snow, rising, always rising, thousands of small impossible things climbing toward a country that no longer agreed to exist. Ada looked at the map's new straight line pointing east into the mountains, at the ghost who had chosen her, at the inquisitor who had unchosen his Crown in the space of a single sentence.

Somewhere below, another window went dark. Another name went home to no one.

"Then we walk tonight," Ada said. "Because the door is a heartbeat, Master Hale—and I intend to reach it while Vell is still beating."

Behind them, far to the west, the sky over the capital lit briefly gold—as if something vast had turned over in its sleep—and every upward snowflake for fifty miles shuddered, and began, very slowly, to fall.
