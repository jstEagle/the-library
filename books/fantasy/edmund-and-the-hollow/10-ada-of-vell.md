## 10. Ada of Vell

The door stood in the ruin of a threshing floor, and it was not a door at all until you looked at it too long.

That was the trick of it—the same trick that had hidden a country for twenty years. From the corner of her eye Ada saw oak planks, iron hinges gone to lace with rust, a latch worn smooth by ten thousand hands. Look directly and there was only winter: snow hanging in the air like held breath, the pale seam where the world had been stitched shut. Edmund's map had burned itself away in her hands on the last mile of road, its ink rising off the paper in threads of gold, and now she understood why. The map had not been leading her here. It had been *becoming* this.

Around the threshing floor, everyone who wanted the door was arriving at once.

The Hollow Guild came first, out of the treeline, Mistress Corvane walking at their head with her hands folded as if she were entering a chapel rather than a battlefield. Behind her, the Crown's soldiers in their grey-and-scarlet, pikes grounded, breath steaming. And between them, walking alone with his warrant-paper fluttering against his chest like a moth that had given up flying, Inquisitor Hale.

Ada stood at the center of it all with an open ledger under her arm and snow falling upward around her shoulders, and she found that she was not afraid. That surprised her more than anything else that day. She had spent her whole life being afraid in a very disciplined way—afraid quietly, afraid efficiently, afraid the way a taster sips poison: carefully, always, expecting it. But fear required a self to protect, and hers had grown strangely thin over the last weeks, worn away like a coin passed through too many hands, until what remained was not nothing but something simpler. Something that could be spent.

"Ada Yarrow." Mistress Corvane's voice carried across the frozen ground, warm as a hearth and just as contained. "Child. You look half-dead. Come home."

"I don't have a home," Ada said. "You made sure of that. You built me without one, stitch by stitch, so I'd fit through a keyhole." She turned the ledger so its pages caught the strange light. "I read the old entries, mistress. The ones in my handwriting that I never wrote. *One taster, trained to be forgotten, owed to the door.* You weren't raising a daughter. You were minting currency."

Corvane's face did not change, which was answer enough. "Every guild keeps ledgers," she said gently. "Every kingdom keeps borders. We only ever traded in what was already lost."

"You sold pieces of Vell," Ada said. "A well here. A wedding song there. A whole street of names to the Crown's archive. And when the country started dying again—when the winter started falling—you sent me to close the door from this side so no one would ever know what you'd done."

Behind Corvane, one of the enforcers shifted his weight, and the snow between him and Ada bent toward her like grass toward sun. Even the impossible weather, it seemed, knew whose side it was on.

Hale stepped forward then, past his own soldiers, and they let him, because a chief inquisitor's authority is a strange currency that spends even when the man carrying it is breaking. Up close she could see what the warrant-paper was doing to him—it had stopped whispering and begun to *weep*, thin lines of cold running down beneath his coat like tears he refused to acknowledge.

"The king's order is to seal it," Hale said. His voice was steady. His eyes were not. "Burn whatever's behind it. Erase the erasure, as it were." A ghost of a smile. "But I've been reading my own warrant for three hundred miles, Ada Yarrow, and this morning it started writing back. Do you know what it says?"

"No."

"It says *Vell*." He held up his hand, and the paper flared—not with heat but with memory, images rising off it like steam: a kitchen with blue tiles, a woman singing off-key, a boy chasing a dog through upward-falling snow. "It says I had a grandmother who came from somewhere that doesn't exist, and that somewhere has been waiting twenty years for me to remember her name. I can't." His jaw tightened. "I have tried every art the Crown taught me, and I cannot remember her name. But I can feel the shape of the hole where it was."

"That's enough," Ada said softly. "For a door, that's enough. Doors don't need certainty. They need want."

* * *

She opened the ledger across her forearm the way she had opened it a thousand nights in the guild's back room, by candlelight, keeping accounts of impossible things. The pages were blank now—all of them; the book had emptied itself as they walked, every debt and trade and name dissolving into the winter, because the winter was not a punishment. She understood that at last, standing in the threshing floor with two armies breathing frost at each other. The long winter was Vell itself, dying again the way it had died before, trying to gather enough of its scattered pieces—its wells, its songs, its half-remembered grandchildren—to make one last attempt at existing. The erasure hadn't been a weapon aimed at Vell. It had been a wound, and wounds close, and closing is another word for forgetting.

Unless someone held the door.

Edmund was there. She couldn't see him, but the air near the threshold had gone the particular shade of tired that meant him, and when she spoke she spoke to that shade.

"You said the door opens from either side," she said. "And that it needs a name freely given. Not taken, not traded, not forged. Given." Her thumb found the last page of the ledger, the one that had stayed stubbornly unblank, the way a scar stays. "You chose me because I was born the night Vell vanished. Half in, half out. A child of both countries, with a foot in each door. And you chose me because—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—because I'd already spent my whole life learning how to give myself away. The guild trained me for it. My mother's memory, sold in a market town. Every year of being nobody. You didn't choose me despite all that, Edmund. You chose me *because* of it. I'm the only person alive who knows exactly what her name weighs."

The tired air stirred. *Yes,* said the snow, or something moving through the snow. *And are you willing?*

Ada looked out at them all one more time. Corvane, whose face had finally, finally cracked—not with remorse but with calculation collapsing, the arithmetic of twenty years failing all at once. The soldiers, young and cold and confused, holding orders they no longer believed in. Hale, standing with his hand pressed flat over the weeping warrant, over the hole shaped like a grandmother's name.

And beyond them, faint as breath on glass, others: figures gathering at the edge of the winter the way mourners gather at the edge of a grave, the erased ones, drawn home by the door's slow heartbeat. A woman with flour on her hands. A boy with a dog. People mid-sentence in villages swallowed whole, still holding the unfinished word on their tongues.

They were waiting for her. All of them, on both sides of everything, waiting for one small woman with a ledger to do the one thing she had been made—and had chosen—to do.

"Yes," Ada said.

She uncapped the little inkhorn at her belt. She dipped the pen. And on the last page of the ledger of impossible things, in her own true handwriting—the handwriting no one had ever seen, because no one had ever known her—she wrote:

*Ada Yarrow. Taster. Ledger-keeper. Daughter of Vell.*

Then, beneath it, smaller, steadier:

*Given freely. Hold the door.*

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the strangest part. She had braced for pain, for tearing, for the cold bite of the inquisitors' warrant-paper—but giving her name felt like setting down a pack she had carried so long she'd forgotten its weight. The letters lifted off the page one at a time, glowing soft as coals, and drifted toward the threshold, and where each one touched the door the door became more door: plank and hinge and latch assembling themselves out of snowfall, out of memory, out of her.

Her name went last. It went the way her mother's memory had gone in the market town—willingly, warmly, with a sensation like being *known*. For one moment she was entirely, unmistakably Ada Yarrow, royal food-taster, keeper of debts, daughter of a country that had never existed and was about to again. She tasted herself the way she tasted the king's food: fully, attentively, saying goodbye.

Then she gave it, and the door opened wide.

Snow fell downward.

All across the horizon, all across the drowned villages and the empty roads, the impossible winter reversed itself—flakes turning in the air like birds deciding, drifting down at last after twenty years of climbing, settling on rooftops and fields and shoulders, melting, ordinary, blessed. Where each flake landed, something returned. A well filled with water that sang. A street assembled itself out of mist, lamplit, smelling of bread. The erased came stepping through the door in ones and twos and dozens, blinking, weeping, calling names that suddenly had owners again—and among them a boy of perhaps ten ran straight past Ada into the arms of a tall grey man who dropped his warrant-paper in the snow and knelt and held on, and the paper dissolved into nothing, its work done, its whisper answered.

Hale looked up at her over the boy's head. He didn't say thank you. He said, "Her name was Maren. My grandmother. I remember." And that was better than thanks.

Mistress Corvane stood alone at the treeline, her guild scattering around her like a spilled purse of coins, and Ada—who was no longer anyone—met her eyes and felt, instead of hatred, something almost like pity. *Someone has to keep the new ledger,* she thought at her, and watched the understanding land, and watched the old woman turn and walk into the trees with her hands still folded, poorer than she had ever been.

* * *

The door remains. Smaller now, humbler—a green door in a stone wall in the middle of a flowering field, unlocked, unlatched, kept open by a name that is no longer attached to anyone.

Ada lives in the cottage beside it. She has a garden, and a kettle, and a great blank book that fills slowly with the returning country's accounts: births, harvests, songs recovered, debts forgiven. The people of Vell bring her entries the way pilgrims bring offerings, though none of them can quite say who she is. They call her the Keeper. Some call her the Taster, because she samples everything first—the first honey of spring, the first snowmelt—and nods, and says, *yes, this is real,* and somehow that makes it truer.

She does not miss her name. A name, she has learned, is only a door you carry with you, and hers is holding open the most important one in the world.

Yesterday a traveler came up the road out of nowhere—a stranger, bleeding a little, tired the way Edmund used to be tired—and asked her, in a language only she could parse, whether the door still worked both ways.

Ada smiled, poured him tea, and opened the ledger to a fresh page.

"It works," she said, "for anyone willing to write themselves down honestly. Now. Tell me your name—and tell me where you've come from. We have room for one more entry."
