## 1. Charms for a City Without Magic

The bells of Vell rang six times before the fog lifted, and Lena Blackwood was already at her stall, arranging her wares with the precision of a woman defusing something dangerous.

Which, in a way, she was.

Copper charms shaped like sleeping birds. Glass beads that caught the morning grey and gave back a pale imitation of blue. Knotted cords dyed the color of river water. She laid them out in rows on faded velvet, each one blessedly, provably empty—dead things, trinkets, the sort of magic a person could hold in their palm and feel nothing but cold metal and hope. In Vell, that was all magic was permitted to be: a keepsake, a superstition, a small lie sold to people who needed it.

She had learned to make them well. That had been the first lesson of exile, though no one had spoken it aloud: *be useful in ways that cannot be punished.* The second lesson she kept stitched inside her left boot, where her real name used to live. Now the boot held a scrap of paper reading *Lena Marsh, charm-seller, born in Lowmarsh parish*, and the name Blackwood lived nowhere at all except in the hollow behind her ribs where storms went to wait.

"Morning, Marsh." Old Pell from the fishmonger's row shuffled past, his cart wheels groaning against the cobblestones. "Cold one coming. My knee says so."

"Your knee's smarter than the criers," Lena said, and smiled the smile she practiced in her cracked mirror each dawn—the one that said *I am ordinary, I am dull, I am no one worth remembering.*

It was true about the cold. She could feel it herself, deeper than weather—a pressure building far north beyond the mountains, like a held breath. Storms spoke to her even here, even muffled beneath five years of careful silence. They tapped at the walls she'd built, patient as water working stone. *Come out,* they seemed to say. *You are lightning wearing a wool coat. Come out and remember what you are.*

Lena adjusted a glass bead a half-inch to the left and did not listen.

* * *

By midmorning the market had filled with the particular noise of Vell: haggling, cart-rattle, the wet slap of fish on scales, and underneath it all the constant low hum of fear that every citizen carried like a second heartbeat. Magic had been outlawed in Vell for eleven years—not just worked magic, but the capacity for it, the blood-deep aptitude that could be tested with a needle and a drop of blood at any checkpoint. People had learned to walk small. To speak softly. To report their neighbors quickly and weep at the funerals afterward, if funerals were permitted.

Lena had made herself a master of walking small.

She bought her bread from the baker on Tanners' Row who never met her eyes. She drew water at the third pump because the first two sat under the watchman's window. She went to bed at the same hour each night and rose at the same hour each morning, because routine was armor, because a woman with no surprises gave the world nothing to grab onto. Five years she had been Lena Marsh, and in five years she had called no storm, lit no candle with a wish, done nothing—*nothing*—that the needle would find.

Except once, in her first winter, when a cart horse had slipped on ice with a child beneath its hooves, and Lena had stopped the rain above the street for exactly four seconds. Four seconds. Long enough. The child lived; the watchman saw nothing; and Lena had gone home and been sick into her washbasin and not slept for three days.

Never again. She had meant it, then.

"Marsh! You've got the pretty ones today." A girl of perhaps nine leaned over the stall's edge, all elbows and chapped cheeks, pointing at the copper birds. "Mam says your charms are the honest kind."

"The honest kind?"

"Ones that don't pretend to do anything." The girl shrugged with the fatalism of the very young. "Most charm-sellers lie. You don't."

Lena laughed despite herself and let the girl hold a bead up to the light without buying it. This was the whole of her life now, she thought. Small kindnesses traded across a market stall. An honest lie, sold by the piece. And some days—most days—it almost felt like living instead of hiding.

Almost.

* * *

The criers came at noon.

She heard them before she understood them: three sets of boots striking cobblestone in unison, then the flat brass blare of a herald's horn cutting through the market noise. Conversations died in patches, spreading outward like frost. Lena's hands went still over her beads.

A crier mounted the fountain steps in Candlemaker's Square, unrolled a scroll sealed with the crowned stag of King Aldric's house, and read in a voice trained to carry:

*"By order of His Majesty, a general hunt is declared throughout the city of Vell and all its territories. All persons harboring the tainted gift, or knowledge of persons so harboring, must present themselves for testing within seven days. Those found guilty of concealment will be tried as accomplices. Reward offered for informants. The crown protects the faithful."*

*The crown protects the faithful.* The words hadn't changed since the fall. Since the night soldiers came over the passes into a country whose name Lena still refused to forget, carrying fire and iron and the king's new maps—maps already printed with her homeland scraped away, replaced by blank space labeled *unincorporated territory.* She had been nineteen. Edmund had been—

No. Not him. Not today.

The market did not erupt. That was the terrible thing about Vell; nothing ever erupted. The market simply *absorbed* the proclamation the way sand absorbed water, heads bowing, voices resuming at lower volume, everyone performing the arithmetic of their own survival. Lena watched a woman three stalls down quietly begin packing her unsold candles. Watched a man in a butcher's apron stare at his own hands as though they might betray him.

Seven days.

Her fingers had gone cold. Not with fear—she knew fear intimately, kept it like a pet—but with something older, rising from the same place the pressure of the coming storm rose from. Her skin prickled. Above the square, the grey clouds had begun, very faintly, to turn.

*Lena Marsh,* she told herself firmly. *Charm-seller. Born in Lowmarsh parish. Nothing to see.*

* * *

It happened at dusk.

She was closing the stall, wrapping her beads in cloth, when the shouting started on Cordwainer Street—two streets over, close enough that the sound carried clearly through the evening air. Then boots, many boots, and the high metallic voice of a watch-sergeant giving orders he clearly enjoyed.

Lena froze with a knot of cord in her hand.

She knew that street. She knew the houses on it, and the people, the way you come to know people after five years of shared pumps and shared walls and shared silences. The widow Osla, who left turnips on Lena's doorstep every harvest-tide without a note. Tam's mother—no, that family was gone, moved south. The tanner Brannic, who whistled off-key. And directly beside her own tenement, old Wren.

Wren, who mended nets on his stoop and sang songs in a language the maps had forgotten. Wren, who had never asked Lena Marsh a single question about where she came from, because he recognized the stitch inside another exile's boot when he saw one.

The dragging sound reached the corner before they did. Lena stood very still among her dead trinkets and watched them bring him around the bend—four watchmen and the sergeant, Wren's arms bound behind him in chains that were unnecessary, because Wren was seventy years old and had spent those years being precisely as harmless as the law required. His net-mending knife lay in the gutter where they'd dropped it. His face, when it turned toward the market, toward her stall, toward *her*, was not afraid.

That was the worst part. He looked at her the way the dying look at the living—with apology, almost, for making them witness it.

"Healer," the sergeant announced loudly, to the gathering crowd, to the record, to the darkening sky. "Unregistered gift. Concealed for years, by his own admission. The crown is merciful—he'll be tested fairly before judgment." A pause, and then the smile she could hear rather than see: "Anyone else wish to confess tonight? It goes easier."

Nobody moved. Nobody breathed. Lena stood behind her stall of honest charms and felt the storm behind her ribs press hard against the wall she'd built, furious, wanting out, wanting *thunder*, wanting to split the cobbles open under the sergeant's righteous boots—and she pressed it down, down, down, the way she had for five years, until her vision spotted and her teeth ached from clenching.

Wren passed within arm's reach of her. Close enough that only she could hear, his ruined voice barely a breath:

"Don't you dare be here when they come back, girl."

Then he was past, and the chains dragged, and the corner swallowed him, and Cordwainer Street was quiet again except for someone's shutters closing, one after another, like eyes refusing to see.

* * *

Lena did not light a lamp that night.

She sat on the floor of her single room with the forged papers spread before her in the moonlight—*Lena Marsh, charm-seller, born in Lowmarsh parish*—and she read them until the words lost meaning, which took less time than usual. Seven days. The needle would come to every door. It would come to hers. And the paper would not save her, because papers never had; the king's men had burned an entire country's worth of papers, along with everything the papers named.

She thought about running. She had always known this day would come; she had rehearsed the routes in her head a hundred times—the south road to the river barges, the pilgrim track over the shoulder of the hills, and, far north, the rumor she'd paid a drunk carter three silver pieces to confirm. The Grey Door. A passage out. A seam in the world the crown's maps couldn't scrape away because it had never been on any map at all.

The Grey Door was a week's hard travel through country crawling with hunters, alone, in the first teeth of winter.

She looked at her hands in the moonlight. Ordinary hands. Charm-seller's hands. Hands that had stopped the rain for four seconds once and had been punishing her ever since.

Outside, far to the north beyond the mountains, the storm she'd felt all day finally broke—too distant to see, but she felt it the way she felt her own pulse: a long rolling murmur of thunder passing over the world, saying her name in the only language that had never lied to her. *Lena. Lena. Come out. Come out.*

She began, slowly, to pack.

Not the trinkets. The trinkets stayed—let the watchmen find an abandoned stall full of honest charms and draw their own conclusions. She packed the forged papers she'd no longer need, a knife, bread, the small hoard of silver sewn into her cloak hem five years ago for exactly this night. And at the bottom of her pack, wrapped in oilcloth she'd never once unwrapped, she placed the thing she'd carried out of a burning country: a strip of her mother's storm-blue scarf, stiff with old smoke, that still smelled, if you believed hard enough, like rain on hot stone.

At the door she paused, hand on the latch, and allowed herself one last look at the life she'd built from invisibility—the cold hearth, the narrow bed, the cracked mirror that had taught her the ordinary smile.

"Foolish, stubborn woman," she said aloud to her reflection. "You waited five years for permission."

There wasn't going to be any permission. There was only the Door, and the road, and whatever was left of her name.

Lena Blackwood pulled her hood up, slipped into the sleeping street, and turned her face north—toward the mountains, the storm, and the first grey light of a country that had forgotten how to find her.

Behind her, unseen in the shadow of the tannery wall, a man in a travel-stained oath-keeper's coat straightened from where he'd been waiting, and followed.
