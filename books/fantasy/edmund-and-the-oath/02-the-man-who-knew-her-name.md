## 2. The Man Who Knew Her Name

Lena packed the way she did everything: quietly, and in the order that would let her leave fastest.

The trinkets went first—three trays of painted charms, the little tin birds that were supposed to bring sweet dreams, the knotted cords sold as protection against fever. All of it harmless. All of it lies, in a sense, though not the kind anyone had ever been hanged for. She wrapped each tray in oilcloth and tucked them into the bottom of her satchel, because even now, even with the criers still ringing through Vell's grey morning, she could not quite make herself throw away the only honest work she'd had in five years.

Then the rest. The forged papers with her false name—Mira Voss, charm-seller, born nowhere worth recording. A knife with a plain wooden handle. Dried apples. The flint. And last, from beneath a loose floorboard, wrapped in three layers of wool as if it might bruise: a smooth grey stone, no bigger than an egg, that hummed faintly when a storm was coming.

It was humming now.

She sat back on her heels in the dark of her rented room and listened to it sing against her palm, that low tremor like a held breath, and hated it, and could not put it down. Outside, the city went about its business. Cart wheels in the mire. A fishwife arguing over eels. Somewhere a child laughing, bright and oblivious, and Lena thought: *enjoy it while you can, little one.* The king's men had come to Vell before for debtors and deserters, and the city had swallowed them and moved on. But they had never come for the gifted. Not here. Not since the Edict.

Until yesterday, when the criers had read out the new decree in voices polished by practice: *all persons harboring or concealing unlawful magic to be surrendered to crown authority. Reward for information. Mercy for the cooperative.*

Mercy. She'd heard what mercy looked like on the face of the woman two doors down, old Sella, who told fortunes with tea leaves and had never once been right. They had dragged Sella into the square at dawn with her wrists chained behind her, and Sella had been screaming—not words, just sound, the sound of someone whose small safe life had been torn open—and the neighbors had watched from their windows with their lamps unlit, and no one had said anything, and neither had Lena.

*You could have done something,* said a voice in her chest that sounded like her mother.

*I would have died,* said the other voice, the one that had kept her alive five years, the one that sounded like her own and always won.

But Sella's screaming had followed her into sleep, and woken her before light, and now here she was, packing in the dark, listening to a stone hum.

She blew out the candle and stood a moment in the blackness, letting her eyes adjust, letting the room become shapes. The bed. The table where she painted the tin birds. The window with its view of the alley. She had chosen this room for the window; she had chosen everything in her life for escape routes, and this was the first time in five years she'd actually needed one.

The Grey Door. Everyone whispered about it eventually—the smugglers, the exiles, the ones who dealt in secrets along the river quays. A passage out of the kingdom, hidden somewhere in the mountains past the frozen pass, older than any border, older maybe than the kingdom itself. Half of them said it didn't exist. The other half said it existed but cost more than a life to reach. Either way, it was north, and north was away, and away was all she wanted.

She slung the satchel across her chest, pulled her hood low, and opened the door onto the stair.

He was sitting on the third step up, in the shadow beneath the broken lamp, and he said her name before she could move.

Not Mira. Not Voss. Her name. Her true name, spoken soft and low, in the accent of a country that no longer appeared on maps—a country scraped off signposts and burned out of records, a country that lived now only in the mouths of people who should have been dead.

Lena's hand found the knife before her mind caught up. "Who are you?"

"You know who I am." He didn't rise. He kept his hands visible, open, empty, resting on his knees—an old gesture, a soldier's gesture, *I am not a threat unless I choose to be*. He was big the way she remembered, broad through the shoulders, but thinner than the man in her memory, worn down like stone in running water. His beard was cropped short and going grey at the jaw. And his eyes—his eyes were the same, dark and steady and unbearably sad, and they had looked at her exactly this way once before, through smoke, through firelight, through the worst night of her life.

"Edmund," she said. The name came out flat, dead, a coin dropped on stone.

"Hello, Lena."

"No." She took a step back toward her room, toward the window, toward anywhere. "No. You don't get to say my name. You don't get to sit on my stairs."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked upward, louder than she meant, and she strangled it down to a whisper. "Do you actually know? Because five years ago you stood in my father's hall and swore—on your life, on your blood, on the old words—you swore to guard us. And then the king's riders came, and you—" She stopped. Her throat had closed.

"I let you go," Edmund said quietly.

"You let me go." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "That's what we're calling it. You put me on a cart with a merchant's wife and you watched it roll south, and then you went back inside and you—" *and then the hall burned*, her mind finished, and showed her the memory whether she asked or not: the orange glow against the winter sky seen from the cart's rear window, growing smaller, smaller, gone. "Everyone else stayed. Everyone else *died*. And you walked away too, didn't you? You just walked in the other direction."

"Yes."

"Don't say yes like it's simple!"

"It wasn't simple." For the first time something moved behind his stillness, a fault line under ice. "It was the hardest thing I've ever done, and I have spent every day since wishing I'd chosen differently, and I will spend every day until I die the same way. But I chose it, Lena. I'm not here to ask you to forgive it. I'm here because there are soldiers two streets from this house, and by noon they'll be knocking on doors in this quarter, and you need to be gone."

"How do you know where I live? How did you find me?"

"The same way they will." He rose slowly, unfolding to his full height, and she forgot how tall he was, how he used to seem like a wall between her family and the world. "I've spent five years learning to hunt people who hide. It seemed only fair to learn it well enough to find the ones I—" He stopped himself. "There's a man named Corvin who sells passage papers near the tannery. He sold yours. He'll sell theirs."

The floor tilted under her, or felt like it. Five years of careful routines, of the false name and the harmless trinkets and the storm locked down so deep she sometimes dreamed she was ordinary—all of it, a paper wall, and this man had walked through it in a week.

"Why?" she whispered. "Why warn me? Why now?"

"Because the hunt is different this time. It isn't collection, it isn't taxes on the gifted like it was in the provinces. The king wants you *found*, Lena. All of you. There's a captain—Ives, they call him the smiling one—and he doesn't chain people and ship them to labor camps. People he takes don't come back. And he has a list." Edmund's jaw tightened. "Your name is on it. Your real one. Which means someone talked, somewhere, sometime, and it means hiding in cities is finished. For you, for me, for every soul like us."

"Like us," she repeated. "You're marked too?"

"I broke my oath to the crown when I let you live. That's enough." He almost smiled, and it was terrible, that almost-smile. "Though between us, I think I broke it long before."

She should go. Every instinct she'd honed for five years said *go, alone, now, trust no one*—and the instincts had kept her breathing through things that would have killed a braver woman. But her feet wouldn't move. Because underneath the resentment, banked like coals under ash, was something she hadn't let herself feel since the night the sky turned orange: the hunger for one familiar thing. One voice from home. One person alive who knew her mother's laugh, who knew the smell of the cedar halls, who knew that when Lena was frightened she got quiet instead of loud.

One person who knew her name.

"There's a pass," she heard herself say. "North. They call it—"

"The Grey Door. Yes." He nodded once. "I know the road. I've walked most of it already, checking it, clearing it. There's a place past the hill country where others like us are sheltering—healers, hedge-witches, a child or two. We could join them, or go around. Your choice. Everything is your choice, Lena. I'm not leading you anywhere. I'm offering to walk beside you as far as you'll allow."

"As far as I'll allow," she echoed. "And after the Door?"

His eyes held hers, and he didn't flinch, and that was its own kind of answer.

"After the Door," he said, "that's yours too."

She looked at him for a long moment—at the man who had carried her on his shoulders through orchards when she was six, who had taught her to whistle through a blade of grass, who had stood in her father's hall with his hand on his sword and made a choice that saved her life and damned him in her heart. She hated him. She hated him cleanly and completely, the way you can only hate someone you once loved.

And she was so tired of being alone.

"If you betray me," she said, "if you so much as look at me wrong, I will show you what I can do. Do you understand? You've seen it once. You haven't seen it angry."

Something flickered across his face—not fear. Grief, maybe, or its cousin. "I understand."

She stepped past him on the narrow stair, close enough to smell woodsmoke and cold iron, and pushed open the street door onto the grey morning. The humming stone pressed against her ribs through the wool like a second heartbeat. Far off, at the edge of hearing, she could swear she heard thunder, though the sky was clear.

Edmund fell into step behind her, half a pace back, the old guard's position, and she didn't tell him to move.

They reached the mouth of the alley together, and that was where she saw them: four riders in the king's black and silver, moving unhurried up the far side of the street, stopping at doors, knocking. At the next house, a woman began to scream.

Edmund's hand closed gently on her shoulder. "Back way," he murmured. "I know a road out of Vell that isn't on their maps. None of ours ever were."

* * *

They left the city through the tannery ditches, wading stinking water in the dark under walls that smelled of lime and rot, and came out into open country an hour before dawn. Behind them Vell lay like a sleeping animal, chimney smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Ahead, far to the north, past hills still black with pine, the mountains waited—white-crowned, remote, holding their secret.

Lena stopped once, on the crest of the first field, and looked back. Somewhere in that city was Sella's empty house. Somewhere was the room she'd slept in for three years, the table where she'd painted tin birds, the whole small careful life she'd built out of silence. She had left it without looking back, and now she looked, and felt nothing she could name.

Beside her, Edmund said nothing. He simply waited, patient as stone, the way he'd always been.

Then the stone at her breast hummed—louder now, insistent, a wire drawn tight—and far to the north, above the mountains, lightning flickered once inside a cloudless sky, silent as a signal.

Edmund went very still. "Lena," he said carefully. "Was that you?"

She hadn't touched it. She hadn't called anything. But the power under her skin had risen like a dog hearing its name, answering something in the distance, something old, and for the first time in five years she wasn't afraid of it.

She was afraid of how much she'd missed it.

"No," she said, and started walking north. "But I think it knows I'm coming."
