## 3. What the Maps Forgot

The signpost at the crossroads outside Vell had been scraped raw.

Lena stopped in front of it, her pack straps biting into her shoulders, and ran her thumb along the gouged wood. Someone had done the work thoroughly—chisel and fire both, by the look of it, until no letter remained, only a pale scar where a name had lived. Below the scar, someone had painted in careful official black: **THE KING'S ROAD SOUTH**.

She knew what the sign had said. Everyone from home knew. *Ashvane Crossing, three leagues.* The place where her mother had once bought honey cakes on market days. The place whose name now existed nowhere in this kingdom except inside the heads of people like her—the ones the king was hunting.

"They did the same to every marker between here and the mountains," Edmund said behind her. He stood with his hood up, hands loose at his sides, watching the road in both directions with the patience of a man who had spent five years learning to wait. "I walked it last spring. A hundred miles of blank wood."

"A hundred miles of blank wood," Lena repeated. "And they wonder why we don't forget."

"I didn't say I approved of it."

"You didn't say anything about it. That's rather your specialty." She pulled her hand back from the scarred post before she could do something foolish—something that would leave frost blooming across the grain, or worse. "Which way?"

"South through the barley fields until dark, then east along the river. There's a patrol schedule on the King's Road—Ives's men run it at dusk and dawn." He said the name *Ives* without flinching, which told her he'd met the man, or at least studied him. "We move when the fields are empty."

"And if we're not empty-handed when they come?"

Edmund looked at her then, and there was something in his grey eyes she couldn't read—or could read too well. "Then we run," he said. "That's all we do for now, Lena. We run."

* * *

Edmund counted things.

He counted the crows lifting off the hedgerow—four, meaning nothing larger had spooked them. He counted the seconds between distant hoofbeats and their fading. He counted the ways he might fail her again, though that count never ended and never helped.

She walked ten paces ahead of him through the stubble fields, and every one of those paces was a small punishment. She hadn't asked him to walk behind her. She simply arranged it, each morning, by setting out first and choosing her pace, and he let her, because he understood what the arrangement meant: *you are not my protector anymore. You are my shadow.*

Fair enough. He had earned shadowhood.

"Lena." He quickened his stride as the river came into view below, silver-braided through the willows. "There's a ford half a mile down. If we take the open road instead—"

"The open road is where patrols are."

"The open road is where we can see them coming. In the willows we'll hear them when they're already on us."

She stopped. Turned. The wind coming off the water pushed a strand of dark hair across her face, and he watched her push it back with fingers that were, he knew, holding themselves very carefully still. He'd seen what happened when her hands weren't still. Once. From a distance. Through smoke.

"You want to argue tactics," she said, "after standing down at Callowmere? After following orders while—" Her voice caught, and she killed the catch the way she killed everything, cleanly, before it could live. "You don't get to have opinions about keeping me safe, Edmund. You forfeited those."

"Yes," he said quietly. "I know."

"That's it? *You know?*"

"What would you have me say?" He spread his hands. "That I've regretted it every day for five years? You already know that. That I'd die before I stood down again? Words. You've heard my words before, and they weighed nothing when it mattered." He nodded toward the willows. "So we go the way you choose. I'll be wrong or I'll be right, but either way I'll be behind you."

Something flickered across her face—not softness, exactly. Something more tired than softness. "You make it very hard to hate you," she said, "when you agree with everything."

"I'm not agreeing with everything. I think the willows are a mistake."

"Then why—"

"Because you need to lead something," Edmund said, "and I need to learn how to follow. One of us has to start somewhere."

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she turned and walked down into the willows, and he followed his own words into the green shade, counting again: her footsteps, thirty-one; his heartbeat, too fast; the distance between them, slowly, grudgingly, beginning to close.

* * *

They heard the child before they saw him.

It was nearly dusk, and they had made the reed-beds east of the river, and Lena was beginning to believe they might actually get through a whole day without disaster when the sound came—a thin, hitching sobbing, somewhere ahead in the rushes, and beneath it something else. A hum. A crackle, like the air before lightning.

Lena froze. Edmund's hand went to his belt, to the short sword he carried openly because oath-keepers' swords were the one weapon the crown still tolerated.

"No," Lena breathed. "Listen."

The crackle rose with the crying. In the reeds twenty yards ahead, a small shape sat hunched against a fallen log—a boy, seven or eight perhaps, mud to his knees, hair pale as river-foam. And around him, hovering in the air like startled birds, hung dozens of tiny lights. Not fireflies. Sparks. Blue-white motes that flickered and spun in time with his sobs, shedding little stings of static that made the reeds around him twitch and curl.

Every time he cried harder, the sparks brightened.

"Oh, sweetheart," Lena whispered, and forgot, entirely, to be afraid.

She moved toward him slowly, the way she'd approach a spooked animal, kneeling in the mud. "Hey. Hey, little one. It's all right. My name's Lena. Can you tell me yours?"

The boy's head snapped up. His eyes were red-rimmed and terrified, and the sparks whirled faster, and one of them struck the log beside his hand and left a scorch mark the size of a coin.

"Don't," he gasped. "Don't come close, I'll—I can't stop it, when I cry it comes out, I can't—"

"I know," Lena said. "I know exactly. Look at me. See my hands?" She held them out, palms up, empty. "I've got the same trouble. Mine's bigger and louder, but it's the same trouble. It comes out when I'm scared. So we're going to be scared together, and that makes it easier. All right?"

The boy stared at her. The sparks slowed, just slightly, like snow deciding whether to fall.

"Tam," he said finally. "My gran said to run to the reeds if the soldiers came, and I did, but she didn't come after, and I waited, and—" The sobs started again, and the air filled with light.

Lena reached out and took his small, shaking hand in hers.

For a heartbeat, her gift stirred against her ribs—the old storm answering the boy's small static like recognizing like—and she felt the two magics touch, and settle, and quiet. The sparks dimmed to embers. Tam's breathing steadied.

"There," she murmured. "See? Easier together."

Behind her, Edmund had appeared at the edge of the reeds, silent as he always was, and Tam shrank back at the sight of him—until Edmund did something she had never seen him do. He knelt in the mud, sword and all, and lowered his head.

"Tam," he said gravely. "My name is Edmund. I keep oaths. And I'm giving you one right now, free of charge: nobody touches you while I'm breathing. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked."

Tam blinked at him. "Is that true? Oaths work like that?"

"It's the truest thing I know," Edmund said, and Lena, watching the boy's frightened face begin, cautiously, to believe him, felt something shift in her chest—a stitch pulling tight, the first one, in a tear she hadn't known was there.

* * *

They moved again within the hour, because Tam's gran had not come, and because the glow of burning farmsteads was visible to the south, and because Edmund found a soldier's bootprint in the mud near the reed-bank that was fresher than any of them wanted to discuss.

Tam rode on Edmund's shoulders, too exhausted to walk, his chin tucked into the man's collar. Lena watched them from a few steps away—watched the boy's grip tighten trustingly in Edmund's hair, watched Edmund carry him as carefully as a man carries something he believes he doesn't deserve to hold.

"He asked me if you were my family," Edmund said quietly, not turning his head.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him families aren't only born. Sometimes they're built. Out of whatever's left."

Lena looked south, at the fires she couldn't unsee, at the blank signposts and the scraped maps and the kingdom erasing itself clean. "And what's left?" she asked. "After everything? What do we build with?"

Ahead of them, on the darkening road, torchlight swung into view. Three riders. Then six. Then the low, carrying voice of a patrol horn, and beneath it—faint, but unmistakable, riding the wind toward them like a promise—

the sound of a man laughing.

Edmund shifted Tam higher on his shoulders. "Run first," he said. "Build later."
