## 4. The Hearth Beneath the Hill

Tam knew the hill was alive before anyone told him.

He felt it the way he felt other things nobody else could feel — a low, warm thrumming under his boots as they climbed the sheep path behind Edmund, like a heartbeat too big and slow to belong to anything with a face. The snow had started an hour ago, fine and dry as flour, and Lena walked ahead of him with her hood up and her shoulders hunched against it, though Tam had noticed that the flakes never seemed to land on her. They swerved. Little invisible hands brushing them aside.

His own gift did that when he cried, except backwards. Things broke.

"Almost there," Edmund said over his shoulder. He was favoring his left leg — he'd twisted it two days ago on the river ice — but he still carried Tam's pack along with his own, and Tam hadn't been able to make him stop. Oath-keepers were stubborn like that. Tam's mother had told him stories about them, back before stories were dangerous.

The hillside opened into a hollow where an old shepherd's cottage sat sunk to its windows in green, even in winter, grass around it soft and unseasonable. A wisp of smoke rose from a chimney built of stones that didn't match, as if they'd been gathered from ten different ruined houses. Tam counted seven chimneys' worth of smoke rising from what should have been one small fire, curling together into a single grey ribbon that bent eastward no matter which way the wind blew.

"That's a misdirection charm," Lena murmured. "A good one." There was something in her voice Tam couldn't name. Not quite fear. More like a person hearing music from a house they'd been told was empty.

Edmund knocked on the door in a pattern — three, pause, two, pause, one — and Tam felt the hill's heartbeat skip, then quicken.

The door opened on a woman with silver-shot braids and forearms brown as bread crust. She looked at Edmund first, then Lena, and last and longest at Tam, and something in her face went soft and terrible all at once, the way grown-ups looked when they saw something in you that you wished they wouldn't.

"Well," she said. "Come in before the hill decides it doesn't know you."

* * *

Inside, the cottage was bigger than outside. Tam had heard of houses like this in songs — crooked houses, folded houses — but he'd never stood in one. The main room went back farther than any cottage had a right to, with a hearth at its heart so large you could have roasted an ox sideways, and doors leading off into warm-lit rooms where he could hear voices, a loom clacking, someone playing a reed flute badly and laughing at themselves for it. Maybe twenty people lived here. Maybe more. Children's shoes by the mat, three pairs, each pair patched differently.

"Maren," Edmund said, and bowed — actually bowed, hand to chest, which made Tam's ears go hot on the man's behalf, because Edmund was old enough to be somebody's grandfather and here he was bowing like a squire.

"None of that," Maren said briskly. "Sit. Both of you sit. The boy sits closest to the fire, he's half-frozen, I can see it in his fingertips." She crouched in front of Tam without asking permission and took his hands in hers, and Tam flinched, because the last time a stranger had grabbed his hands—

But nothing happened. Nothing broke. Her grip was warm and dry and utterly unafraid, and when she turned his palms over and studied the faint blue shimmer that lived in the lines of them — the shimmer his mother used to kiss goodnight, back when kissing it was safe — she only nodded, as if confirming a recipe.

"Cry-burst gift," she said. "Strong. Untutored." She glanced up at Lena. "How long has he been holding it in?"

"All day, every day," Lena said quietly. "Since his village."

"And how many things has he broken since?"

Tam's throat closed. He held up four fingers, then thought about the well bucket, and made it five. He was not going to cry about it. He was absolutely not going to cry, because the last time he'd cried indoors, the beams above the stove had cracked like gunshots and everyone had screamed, and that had been the night the soldiers came, following the noise, following *him* —

Maren pressed his hands closed around hers. "Nothing here breaks, little one," she said. "That's rather the point of the place."

And Tam, who had not believed an adult's voice since autumn, believed hers. The tears came anyway — hot, humiliating, five days overdue — and the whole room braced, he could feel them bracing, Edmund shifting forward, Lena going rigid —

— and nothing happened. The fire didn't gutter. The window glass didn't spiderweb. The warmth simply absorbed it, the way sand absorbs rain, and Tam realized the hearthstones were humming louder now, tuned to him somehow, drinking down the wildness and giving back only heat.

He cried harder after that, and it was the easiest crying of his whole life.

* * *

They ate stew with barley and dark greens Tam didn't recognize, and bread with honey, real honey, and there was a boy called Wick — eleven, gap-toothed, gifted with small fires that lived in his cupped palms like pets — who showed Tam the loft where the children slept and pointed out which floorboards squeaked and which were liars. Wick talked the way water runs downhill, constant and cheerful, and Tam found himself answering questions he would normally swallow. What gift do you have? Does it hurt? Did your people run or hide?

"Hide," Tam said. "Then run. Then—" He stopped.

Wick waited, and didn't push, and after a while said, "Maren found me in a charcoal burner's camp. I'd set fire to my bed twice. They thought I was cursed. She paid them in silver and told them God had made a mistake." He grinned. "She says that about everything. Mistake, she says. As if the world makes mistakes instead of people."

From below, Maren's voice floated up, low and level, and both boys went quiet to listen, the way children do when their names are anywhere near a conversation.

"—can't stay on the roads, Edmund. Ives has doubled the patrols since Vell. And the passes will close within the fortnight. You're asking the girl to cross the Teeth in deep winter with a child and no escort worth the name."

"I'm asking her to decide," Edmund said. "I've done enough deciding for other people to last me two lifetimes."

A silence. Tam crept to the top of the ladder, careful of the honest boards, and lay flat with his chin on his hands.

"You know what I'll offer her," Maren said. "I always offer. It's why the Door stays shut and my kitchen stays full."

"She won't take it," Edmund said.

"Most don't. That's the tragedy of us, oath-keeper. We'd rather be hunted than hobbled." A log settled; sparks whispered. "But she's different from most, isn't she. She's not running *from*. She's carrying something that gets heavier every mile. I can smell a storm coming off that girl the way you smell rain on stone. If she buries it here, she lives. If she frees it out there—" the spoon tapped the pot rim, once, "—then we'll all learn what her grandmother's weather was really like."

"What was it like?" Edmund asked.

Maren laughed, short and without much humor. "There's a reason the kingdom scraped her country off the maps, man. They didn't fear the people. They feared the inheritance."

Tam looked over at Lena, sitting by the hearth with her bowl forgotten in her lap, staring into the coals. She had heard every word; he was sure of it. Her jaw was set the way Edmund's got set. And her hands — her hands were doing the thing again, the thing he'd noticed on the road, fingers spread slightly apart, hovering just above her knees, holding something invisible and enormous very, very still.

Like a woman keeping a lid on a boiling pot with her bare palms.

* * *

That night Maren came to the loft and sat on Wick's abandoned mattress across from Tam, lamplight making her braids glow like straw.

"I'm going to tell you what I told your friend," she said, "because you have a right to hear it too. This place is sanctuary. No soldier has found it in thirty years. The children here learn to read, to mend, to cook, and — once, carefully, only ever a little — to hold their gifts quiet. I buried mine nineteen years ago. Under the hearthstone, if you like poetry. It still hurts some mornings, the way an old break hurts before rain. But I am alive, and everyone under this roof is alive, and I have buried eleven of my people and dug none of their graves." She let that sit between them. "That is what I can give. Safety that costs you the loudest part of yourself. It is not nothing, Tam. For some, it is everything."

Tam pulled his blanket up to his chin. "What happens if you say no?"

"Then you keep walking." She smiled, tired and true. "And I'll pack you food for the road either way. Hospitality isn't a bribe."

After she'd gone, Tam lay listening to the house breathe — the loom, the flute, Wick's small snores, the hill's great slow heartbeat underneath it all. It was the safest he had felt since autumn. It was warm, and fed, and hidden, and kind.

It also felt, he realized, the way the cage in the poultry yard back home had felt: clean straw, fresh water, a latch that clicked.

Downstairs, through the floorboards, he heard Lena's footsteps cross to the hearth and stop. Heard her kneel. Heard her press one palm flat against the stone above where Maren's gift lay buried — and heard the storm inside the stone answer her, a far-off rolling grumble, like thunder remembering its name.

Lena stayed there a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was so quiet Tam almost lost it under the fire.

"Nineteen years," she said, to Maren or to the stone or to herself. "You put it down and it stayed down. Mine won't. It's getting louder every day we walk toward the mountains, and I think—" a breath, ragged at the edges, "—I think it knows where it's going. I think it's going home."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the walls the house groaned and resettled, and Tam had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that the hill itself had heard her — and agreed.

In the morning, he knew, before anyone said a word, there would be packed bags by the door.
