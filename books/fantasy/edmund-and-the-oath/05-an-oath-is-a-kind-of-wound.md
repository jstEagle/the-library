## 5. An Oath Is a Kind of Wound

Edmund had kept watch over a hundred fires in his years of atonement, and he knew the way firelight lied. It softened faces, gilded the edges of things, made even a fugitive camp in the hollow beneath the hill look like something close to home. Tam slept against Maren's side with his mouth open and one hand curled around a river stone he refused to surrender, and for a moment—only a moment—the boy's small gift lay quiet, no blue flicker bleeding from between his fingers.

Lena sat across the flames, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen.

Edmund watched her hands move along the whetstone, slow and rhythmic, and understood that she was not tending the blade. She was tending herself. He had seen soldiers do it on the eve of battles they expected to lose. The rhythm gave the body something to do while the mind walked roads it did not want to walk.

He should let her walk them alone. That would be the kind thing.

But an oath was a kind of wound, and wounds left untended went bad.

"Maren says the pass will hold another three days," he said. "After that, the snow closes it until spring."

"I heard her." The knife whispered against the stone. "I can hear everything you all say, Edmund. I've been doing it my whole life."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Then you know I've been circling this conversation since we left Vell."

The whetstone stopped.

Tam stirred, murmured something about doors, and settled again. Maren looked up from her herb-sorting, read the air between them the way healers could, and rose without a word to check the wards at the cave mouth—a courtesy Edmund noted and would remember. Some kindnesses were loudest when silent.

Lena set the knife down. "Say it, then."

"There are things you don't know about that night," Edmund said. "About what happened before the fall of Ashvale. You've carried five years of questions, and you've earned every answer, even the ones that will make you hate me more than you already do."

"I don't hate you." Her voice was flat, careful. "Hating requires thinking about someone. Mostly I just try not to think about you at all."

"That's worse," he said quietly. "I know. I've tried it."

* * *

He told it the way he had rehearsed it a hundred times in a hundred lonely camps, and the way it always failed to come out as rehearsed.

"You remember Captain Drevan. Your father's shield-brother, before the crown bought him." Lena's jaw tightened; she remembered. "Three days before the massacre, Drevan came to me privately. He'd been given new orders from the capital—not protection orders. Containment orders. Every gifted soul in Ashvale was to be registered, then gathered, then—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "Then handled."

"Handled," Lena repeated. The word came out of her like a stone dropped into deep water.

"The oath-keepers were sworn to your family. All of us. Forty-two sworn shields, and every one of us bound by word and sigil to stand between the Blackwoods and harm." Edmund turned his hands palm-up toward the fire, as if the light might show him the moment again, might let him see some path through it he had missed. "Drevan told us the king had declared the old oaths void. That any keeper who raised steel against royal soldiers would be executed as a traitor, and their families with them. He gave us until dawn to choose."

"And you chose."

"I chose wrong." The words scraped coming out. "No. That's not true either. I chose the only way I could live with myself, which is its own kind of cowardice. I couldn't fight—I wouldn't lead forty-two men to slaughter and orphan half the kingdom's children for a battle we'd lose before it began. But I couldn't stand down either. So I did neither. I went to your mother in the night and I told her to run. I told her to take you and go north, past the maps' edge, and I gave her every coin I had and my fastest horse, and I stayed behind to buy time."

Lena had gone very still. "You never said any of this."

"You never asked. And I never offered, because the truth is uglier than the failure." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and made himself meet her eyes across the fire. "Here is the ugliest part, Lena. When the soldiers came at dawn, I stood aside. I stood aside while they burned the hall where your aunts slept. I stood aside while they put chains on children younger than Tam. My oath said *between her and the world*, and I stepped out of the way. I told myself I was saving who I could. But an oath doesn't have clauses. It doesn't have *except when it's hard*. I broke it, and then I spent five years telling myself the breaking was mercy."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines like a long exhale.

Lena's voice, when it came, was strangely calm. "My mother sent me ahead with the horse. Did you know that? She put me on it herself and told me to ride north and not look back. So whatever time you bought her—it wasn't enough, but it was something. She used it to save me." Her hands were trembling now; she pressed them flat against her knees. "I've hated you for five years because I thought you simply ran. That's easier to hate. This—" She shook her head slowly. "This is worse. This is a man who tried, and whose trying wasn't enough, and who has been flaying himself alive ever since. I don't know what to do with that, Edmund. I don't know how to forgive someone who punishes himself harder than I ever could."

"Don't forgive me yet," he said. "Forgiveness given too early is just another lie. Just—" He faltered. "Just don't send me away. Let me carry what I carry beside you instead of behind you. That's all I'm asking. Not absolution. Proximity."

She looked at him for a long time. The storm-gift flickered somewhere behind her eyes—he could always tell, a gray restlessness like weather deciding—and then she banked it, the way she banked everything.

"Proximity," she said at last. "That I can give. For now."

It was not forgiveness. It was a door left unlatched, and Edmund took it as the treasure it was.

* * *

They came at dusk two days later, three figures stumbling down the frozen streambed with the wards' warning-chime still fading: a broad woman with frostbitten ears named Odile, whose gift was to speak with birds and who had not stopped talking to a crow perched on her shoulder since Vell; a gaunt former bridge-warden called Hesk, who could mend broken things but not living ones, and whose hands shook constantly; and last, a slight man in a coat too fine for a refugee, dark-haired and quick-eyed, who bowed over Maren's hand as though they'd met at court rather than in a smuggler's hollow.

"Sef," he announced, to no one in particular and everyone at once. "Illusionist, formerly of the Amber Theater in Vell, currently of nowhere at all. Before anyone asks—yes, the illusions are real magic, no, they cannot hurt anyone, and yes, I am extremely charming. Two of those statements are boasts. I'll let you sort out which."

"He talked the whole way here," Odile said wearily, to her crow. "Whole way."

Edmund hung back while Maren fed them and Lena, cautiously, drew them into the circle of the fire. He had learned to read arrivals the way he once read battlefields, and Sef bothered him—not for anything he said, but for the ease of him. Fear wore many disguises, and charm was the most comfortable one. Still, the man had crossed forty miles of hunted country to reach them, and Edmund had no evidence against him beyond his own scarred instincts.

It was Sef who solved their problem for them, leaning forward with firelight dancing on his quick face.

"You need eyes ahead of you," he said. "The pass, the Door, whatever waits on the far side of it. I can go where none of you can—make myself look like a tax collector, a shepherd, a corpse if necessary. Give me a day's lead and I'll bring you back the road, clear or crowded." He spread his hands, all innocence and theater. "Consider it my audition. Every caravan needs a scout. Every scout needs a caravan worth scouting for."

Lena glanced at Edmund. *Well?* her look said.

Edmund studied Sef a moment longer—at the too-fine coat, the smile that reached everything except his eyes—and found nothing he could name, only a cold thread of doubt he could not justify aloud.

"We leave at first light," he said. "Scout well, Sef. We'll be following your word with our lives."

"Then I'll choose my words carefully," Sef said, and smiled, and the firelight gilded him gold, and Edmund told himself that a man's coat meant nothing, that suspicion without proof was just old fear wearing armor.

Above the hill, far to the north, the sky had begun to bruise with early snow.

And in the morning, when Sef slipped away alone down the pass, he did not look back at the camp—

but he counted the fires behind him as he went, and remembered exactly how many there were.
