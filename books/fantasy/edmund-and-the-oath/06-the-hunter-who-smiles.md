## 6. The Hunter Who Smiles

Sef had always believed that a lie was a kind of architecture. You built it the way a mason built an arch—each stone cut to bear the weight of the others, nothing wasted, nothing that could be pulled free without the whole thing coming down around your ears. The caravan's trust was his keystone now, and he had learned to love it the way a thief loves a lock: intimately, professionally, without tenderness.

He told them he was going ahead to buy salt pork in Harrow's Ford. He even brought back salt pork.

That was the trick of it, he'd found. Lies survived on details. A man who returned empty-handed was a man worth watching; a man who returned complaining about prices was furniture. So Sef stood in the market at Harrow's Ford for half an hour, haggling over smoked meat with a woman who had a mole like a spilled inkwell on her chin, and he let her cheat him by two coppers because letting yourself be cheated was the cheapest insurance in the world.

Then he walked out past the last sheep pen, past the shrine with its faceless saint—the king's masons had chiseled the features off every holy image in three counties, and no one could tell him why that was supposed to make people feel safer—and into the birch wood where the road bent north toward the mountains.

Captain Ives was waiting under the trees, exactly where the message had said, and he was smiling.

He was always smiling. That was the thing about Ives that the stories never quite captured. The wanted-broadsheets drew him as a wolf, all angles and hunger, but the truth was worse and softer: he looked like a man greeting old friends at a wedding. Round-faced, ruddy, with laugh lines carved deep as furrows. He smiled the way other men breathed.

"Sef." He spread his hands, delighted. "You've gotten thinner. Are they feeding you, or do they make you earn your supper with card tricks?"

"They feed me." Sef kept his voice bored. Boredom was armor. "You got my letter."

"I did." Ives produced it from inside his coat—a cheap scrap of paper covered in Sef's left-handed scrawl—and read it again, though Sef knew he'd already memorized it. He liked to be seen reading it. It made the transaction feel official. "'The storm-caller grows restless near the peaks. Her power comes back to her. They mean to reach the Grey Door before first snow.'" He folded the letter away. "And the oath-keeper?"

"Edmund Vael walks point every morning and takes last watch every night. He sleeps four hours and prays through the rest of them. If you want him, you'll have to kill him, and he'll make it expensive."

"And the boy?"

Sef felt something shift behind his ribs, small and unwelcome, like a splinter working loose. He kept his face smooth. Illusionists learned early that the face was just another prop, and props didn't bleed.

"The boy is nothing. A child who makes lights when he cries. The healer keeps him dosed with willow tea so he sleeps through the cold marches."

"Mm." Ives studied him with those wedding-guest eyes, and for one long moment Sef felt the peculiar vertigo of being seen—not the way the caravan saw him, charming Sef, useful Sef, but the way a buyer appraises a horse, checking teeth and wind. "You know what I can't decide," Ives said pleasantly, "is whether you're the best agent I've ever had or the worst. Five years I've paid you. Five years of routes and names and little maps drawn in your pretty hand. And still no storm-caller in chains."

"You want her delivered breathing," Sef said. "Breathing is slow work."

"I want her delivered *certain*." Ives stepped closer, and beneath the smile there was something else, something patient and bottomless, the way a well is patient. "The king has hanged forty-one gifted souls since midsummer. Do you know what they all had in common? Not power—I've hanged farmers who could only make cream sour. Certainty. Every one of them, at the end, was certain of who they were. That's the disease, Sef. Not magic. Magic is just the symptom. The sickness is knowing what you are and refusing to be ashamed of it." He clapped Sef on the shoulder, warm as a hearth. "Your storm-caller spent five years ashamed. Now she's walking toward her own mountains and remembering. You understand why she has to be stopped before she arrives at whatever she's becoming."

"I understand the price goes up," Sef said. "Storm-caller, oath-keeper, healer, child. Four heads instead of one. Double."

Ives laughed—a real laugh, full-bodied, echoing off the birches until a jay went up shrieking. "Double! Gods, I've missed you." He wiped his eyes. "Triple, if the Door closes behind them. The king wants this passage sealed with examples. Give me the canyon pass at Wolfjaw—you know the one, where the broken road narrows—and I'll give you enough gold to buy a name in some southern city where nobody's ever heard of Vell."

"Wolfjaw." Sef turned the name over. He had walked that stretch of road with them just yesterday, had noted how the cliffs leaned in like conspirators, how the snow already dusted the high shoulders of the pass. It would be easy. It would be so easy. "Snow's coming early. If the pass closes—"

"Then everyone freezes comfortably and I collect the survivors. Everyone wins." Ives was already turning away, pulling his gloves back on, becoming once more the smiling officer who bought ale for tavern-keepers and patted children on the head. Over his shoulder he added, lightly, as though discussing weather: "Oh—and Sef? The little one. The boy who cries light." He paused. "Bring him out clear, if you can manage it. The king has a use for children. Even the ones who aren't certain yet."

He whistled as he walked away. Sef stood among the birches until the sound faded, then longer, watching his breath cloud and vanish, cloud and vanish, like something being said and unsaid, said and unsaid.

Then he went back to camp, carrying salt pork and a route report he had written himself, and smiled at them all.

* * *

Lena felt the mountains before she saw them properly.

It began as a hum in her back teeth, faint as a fly against glass, on the morning they climbed out of the river valley. By noon it had become a pressure behind her breastbone, and by the time the sun dropped behind the first white shoulders of the range, she could feel the storm systems moving up from the south the way a sailor feels swell through a hull—vast, patient, and speaking a language she had spent five years pretending not to know.

She stood apart from the fire that night, ostensibly checking the horses, actually holding her palm flat toward the sky. Clouds were stacking above the peaks in great grey terraces, and when she opened the smallest door inside herself—the one she kept barred and padlocked and buried under routine, under forged papers, under the careful dullness of a charm-seller named Marta Kess—she felt the clouds answer.

Not lightning. Not yet. Just a recognition, electric and intimate, like hearing your own name spoken in a crowd after years of silence.

*There you are,* the sky seemed to say. *There you are.*

"Lena."

Tam had come up beside her without a sound, wrapped in Maren's good blanket, his breath steaming. He followed her gaze upward. Above the highest peak, auroral light was beginning to move—faint green curtains, too early in the year, too far south, shimmering like something alive behind the stars.

"Is that you?" he whispered.

"No." But her hand trembled, and the curtains brightened, just slightly, just for a heartbeat, and Tam looked at her with wide dark eyes and said nothing, because children who had been hunted for their gifts learned very young which questions not to ask twice.

"It's beautiful," he said instead.

It was. That was the trap of it, Lena thought. Everyone warned you about the danger, the burning villages, the drowned fields, the reason kings put your likeness on broadsheets. No one warned you about how *good* it felt. The storm rising in her chest was not rage. It was relief—five years of held breath finally leaving her body, five years of being a candle flame cupped in wet palms, and now the wind was here and it wanted to carry her.

She closed the door inside herself. Barred it. Padlocked it. Her hands were shaking.

"Come by the fire," she said. "Maren's making the willow tea."

They walked back together, and Edmund looked up as they came, and Lena saw him see her trembling hands, and saw him decide—deliberately, kindly—not to ask. He moved aside to make room for Tam at the warmth. He always did that. Five years of guilt had taught him to speak in gestures.

Sef came back from Harrow's Ford an hour later, stamping snow off his boots, complaining cheerfully about the price of pork, and the caravan gathered around him like planets around a warm sun. He dealt out the meat. He told a story about a merchant with an inkwell mole. He made a coin vanish behind Tam's ear and produce a dried apple from nowhere, and the boy laughed his rare, startled laugh, and Maren smiled, and Edmund almost smiled, and Lena sat with the storm locked behind her teeth and thought: *this. This is what I ran toward without admitting it.*

Sef caught her looking at him and winked.

Later, when the others slept, he sat by the coals sketching in his little book—the route, the landmarks, the narrowing walls of Wolfjaw canyon where the broken road bent north—and his pencil did not hesitate even once.

Above the peaks, the green lights burned brighter, and brighter, and no one awake saw them but the man who was counting them like coins.
