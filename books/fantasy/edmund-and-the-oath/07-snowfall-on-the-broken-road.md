## 7. Snowfall on the Broken Road

The snow came three weeks early, and it came like a verdict.

Lena felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her teeth, a hum in the marrow of her arms, the old familiar ache that meant weather was gathering somewhere above the clouds and looking for a way down. For five years she had learned to read that ache the way a sailor reads the sea: as warning, as enemy, as something to be endured with her hands clenched and her gift locked behind walls of habit so thick they had begun to feel like bone.

But here, in the high country below the mountains, the walls were thinner. She could feel the storm circling the peaks like a wolf around a fire, patient and enormous, and every hour it grew harder to pretend she was only a woman named Wren Voss who sold charms and had never once in her life called lightning out of a clear sky.

"Snow by nightfall," Tam said beside her, squinting at the sky with the grave authority of a nine-year-old who had recently discovered his gift could make frost bloom on windows when he cried. "I can feel it. Like when Maren's kettle is about to sing."

"You feel kettles?"

"I feel things *before* they happen sometimes." He said it defensively, hunching into his too-large coat—one of Edmund's, sleeves rolled four times. "Is that bad? Sef says it's not bad."

"Sef says a lot of things." Lena heard the edge in her own voice and softened it. It wasn't the boy's fault. It wasn't even Sef's fault, she reminded herself—she had no reason yet, only a splinter of unease lodged where trust should be, working deeper every time the illusionist smiled. "It's not bad, Tam. It just means you'll want to keep it quiet until we're somewhere it can't be used against you."

"Like your storms?"

She looked down at him. His eyes were wide and guileless and terrible in their honesty.

"Exactly like my storms," she said.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of a broken waystation, one of the old relay houses that had dotted this road back when the kingdom had a different name for it—back when the maps showed a country beyond the pass instead of blank parchment and the words *Here there is nothing*. The stone walls still stood to shoulder height. Someone had carved a lintel once; the letters had been chiseled away, but the shape of them remained, ghosts of an alphabet, and Lena ran her thumb along the empty grooves while Edmund built the fire.

He built fires the way he did everything now—carefully, thoroughly, with the air of a man paying off a debt installment by installment. Five years of atonement had worn grooves into him the way the snow wore grooves into stone. He fed the flame small sticks first, then larger ones, and only when it was certain did he look up and catch her watching.

"The pass closes tomorrow," he said. "Maybe today, if the snow's heavy. We need to be through it or under shelter when it does."

"Sef said the eastern fork is open. He scouted it yesterday—he said he could see the Door valley from the ridge." Lena crouched across the fire from him, warming her hands. The words tasted strange on the way out, like fruit with a bruise hidden underneath. "He was very convincing."

Edmund's jaw tightened. She watched him decide whether to say what he was thinking, and watched him lose the argument with himself, as he always did.

"You don't trust him," he said.

"I don't distrust him. There's a difference."

"There isn't, though. Not out here." Edmund poked the fire, sending up a spiral of sparks. "I've been thinking about it. The timing of his scouting trips. How he always volunteers. How he comes back with exactly the news we need, wrapped up neat."

"And?" She kept her voice light. "What have you concluded, oath-keeper? That the charming man who sings to Tam and mends our boots with illusions is selling us to Ives?"

The joke fell between them and lay there, cold.

"That's not funny," Edmund said quietly.

"No," Lena admitted. "It isn't."

Because the truth was she had been having the same thought for days, and she hated herself for it. Sef had found them at Maren's hearth, all laughter and clever hands, and he had been nothing but kind—to her, to Tam, to the two other refugees they'd gathered on the road, a hedge-witch named Odile who spoke to crows and a silent man called Brannic whose gift no one had asked about twice. Kindness was easy to fake. But so was suspicion, and Lena had spent five years learning that the second was more dangerous than the first. Suspicion was how you ended up alone. Alone was how you survived, but survival wasn't the same as living, and she was so tired of merely surviving.

"He'll be back before dark," she said. "We'll ask him to walk us through his route again. Every step. If he hesitates anywhere—"

"If he hesitates anywhere, we'll know nothing," Edmund said. "A liar who's good enough to fool scouts is good enough to fool us." He looked at her across the flames, and for a moment the firelight caught the old scar along his jaw, the one he'd earned the day her family died, the day he obeyed his orders and stood down. "I let my orders kill them once, Lena. I won't let my suspicions do it again. If Sef is false, we find out from evidence, not from fear."

"And if the evidence comes too late?"

Edmund didn't answer. Above the broken walls, the first flakes began to fall—fat, slow, almost gentle, like the sky hadn't yet decided to be cruel.

* * *

Sef returned after dark, snow in his dark curls, cheeks flushed with cold and triumph, and his story was perfect.

Perfect the way forged coins are perfect, Lena thought later. Perfect the way her own identity papers were perfect.

"The eastern fork is clear to within a mile of the glacier," he reported, crouching at the fire, rubbing his hands. "I climbed the ridge myself—I could see the ice wall, the whole valley. There's a canyon road, narrow but passable, and it cuts a full day off the northern route. By tomorrow evening we're standing at the Door." He grinned, and the grin was warm as brandy. "We might even sleep tomorrow night in a country with no king."

Tam cheered. Odile's crows muttered approvingly from the woodpile. Brannic nodded his slow nod. And Edmund looked at Lena across the fire, and she saw in his face the same arithmetic she was doing—the same hope wrestling the same splinter of doubt—and neither of them spoke, because what would they say? *Show us your soul, illusionist. Let us check it for cracks.*

"We leave at first light," Edmund said finally. "Single file in the narrows. Tam walks with me."

"With me," Lena said, and it wasn't a negotiation, and everyone at the fire understood why without her having to say it aloud. The boy with the flaring gift walked beside the woman who could hide it best.

Sef agreed easily. Too easily, some cold voice whispered. But the fire was warm and the boy was laughing and the snow was falling soft as forgiveness, and Lena let the whisper drown.

* * *

The canyon swallowed them by midmorning.

It was beautiful, in the way traps are beautiful. Walls of grey stone rose on either side, streaked with ice, narrowing until the sky was a ribbon of white overhead. The road beneath their boots was old—older than the kingdom, older than the erasure, paved with stones fitted by hands that had believed the world would always have room for another country in it. Lena trailed her fingers along the wall as she walked and felt, faintly, the hum of old wards, dead or sleeping, and wondered if anyone had ever stood in this exact place and felt safe.

Tam walked beside her, chattering about the Door. Would it hurt? Would it look like a door, or like the seam Maren had described, a line of light in the ice? Did countries on the other side have kings, and if they didn't, who decided things? Lena answered what she could and let the rest hang, and the sound of his voice filled the canyon the way birdsong fills a forest, making the silence afterward seem intentional rather than empty.

That was the first wrongness, and she almost missed it. The silence *after*.

No wind. No birds answering. The snow had stopped an hour ago, and the air hung still as held breath, and the storm-pressure behind her teeth had gone strangely quiet—not gone, but *waiting*, the way her gift went quiet when danger was near, an animal flattening itself in tall grass.

Lena stopped walking.

"What is it?" Edmund murmured, half a pace behind.

"I don't know." Her hand found Tam's shoulder. "Everyone stop. Quietly."

Ahead, Sef turned, and for one unguarded instant—half a heartbeat, no more—his face did something complicated. Then the smile arrived, right on time, warm as ever. "What's wrong? Cold feet? The valley's just past this bend—"

The bend. A box canyon needed a back wall, and a back wall needed a reason, and Lena was already turning, already looking up at the canyon rim behind them, when the sun glinted off steel.

Crossbows. A dozen of them, ranged along the southern rim, kneeling in the fresh snow. And on the boulder at their center, unhurried, a man in a captain's cloak raised one hand—not a command, not yet, just a greeting, courteous as a host at his own table.

Captain Ives smiled down at them the way Sef smiled. It occurred to Lena, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had seen that smile twice now, and both times it had meant the same thing.

"*Sef,*" Edmund breathed.

"Don't," Sef said, and his voice cracked in the middle, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "Don't make it—look, Ives has orders to take the gifted alive. *Alive.* Nobody has to die today. I checked. I made sure. I told him I'd walk you out myself if—"

"You sold the route," Lena said. Flat. Cold. The storm behind her teeth waking, pressing outward, tasting the open sky. "You sold *Tam*."

"I sold a *path*—" 

"DOWN ON YOUR KNEES!" Ives's voice rolled down the canyon walls, pleasant and carrying. "By order of the crown! Resist, and my men are authorized to simplify matters!"

And Tam broke.

Not toward the soldiers—away from them, backward, into Lena, and his terror tore the lid off his small gift the way it always did. Frost exploded across the canyon floor in a white starburst. Ice sheathed the stones. And on the rim, a crossbowman flinched, and loosed, wild—

—at the child.

Lena saw it all in the stretched silver moment that her gift gave her when the world went wrong: the bolt's arc, the snow, Tam's upturned face. She saw Edmund move before the thought finished forming, saw him understand the geometry of it faster than grief could arrive, saw him step—not away, never away, *into* it—with his arms closing around the boy.

The bolt took him high in the back, below the left shoulder, and punched through.

Edmund made no sound. That was the worst of it. He folded around Tam like a wall coming down gently, deliberately, the way he did everything, paying debts installment by installment, and then his knees hit the ice and Lena was screaming his name and the storm behind her teeth stopped waiting.

Lightning crawled along the canyon rim. Somewhere very far away, thunder cleared its throat.

"Lena." Edmund's voice, wet and ragged, his hand fumbling for hers against the ice. Blood spread beneath him, shockingly red, shockingly bright against the white. "Lena—the Door—you take them and *go*—"

"Shut up." She pressed her palms over the wound and felt the warmth leaving him, felt the arrow's shaft shift under her fingers, and five years of suppression rose in her throat like a scream finally given permission. "Shut up, Edmund. You don't get to die paying. You don't get *off* that easy."

Above them, Ives raised his hand at last, and the crossbows leveled, and the snow began to fall again—harder now, faster, as if the sky had finally chosen its side.

Lena stood up, and let the storm see her face.
