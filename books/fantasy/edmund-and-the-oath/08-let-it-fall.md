## 8. Let It Fall

The snow had stopped falling, which was the first wrong thing.

Lena noticed it the way she noticed everything now—the world had sharpened since the bolt went into Edmund's shoulder, since Tam's screaming had gone quiet and hoarse against Maren's absent hands. The flakes hung in the air above the canyon as if the sky itself were holding its breath. Below them, where the broken road narrowed to a throat of ice between two walls of granite, Captain Ives's soldiers stood in their neat grey lines, torches hissing in the wet, waiting with the patience of men who knew exactly how small this box was and exactly who they had caught inside it.

Forty soldiers. Two crossbowmen on the ridge behind them—she could hear the creak of wound strings when the wind shifted. One captain with a smile like a drawn blade, standing forward of his line with his hands clasped behind his back, unhurried, almost courteous.

And up here, crammed into the rocks above the pass: Edmund, white-lipped, his shoulder bound in strips torn from Sef's fine coat, propped against a boulder because he could not stand without help. Tam, curled small against Lena's side, his tears frozen to salt tracks on his cheeks. The others—old Yeva whose gift was knowing when a lie was told, the twins Pell and Ansa who could warm stone with their palms, quiet Brann who spoke to ravens—huddled behind her like a congregation at the edge of a cliff.

Sef was down there. She could see him standing beside Ives, his handsome face turned up toward her, wearing an expression that was not quite apology. It never would be. He had sold every mile of their road for coin or safety or whatever it was that men like Sef traded instead of souls.

"Lena Blackwood," Ives called up, and his voice carried strangely well in the dead air, "or should I say the name on your papers? It hardly matters. The king wants you alive. The rest—" he gestured gently, the way a man gestures at weather he cannot control, "—the rest are negotiable."

Tam's small hand found hers. His fingers were ice.

"I'm scared," he whispered. Not of the soldiers. She understood that in one terrible flash. He was scared of her—or of what lived under her skin, the thing he'd felt stirring all week as they climbed higher into the mountains, the thing that made the hair on his arms rise and his gift flutter in his chest like a trapped bird.

*So am I,* she wanted to tell him. *Every day of my life.*

Instead she said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that wasn't a lie, and she had spent five years telling those lies until her throat was full of them.

*Careful routines. Small charms. Be smaller. Be less. Be nothing.*

Her mother had taught her otherwise, once, in a country that no longer appeared on maps. *The storm is not your shame, little lightning. It is your inheritance.* Her mother had died with that inheritance unspent, standing in a doorway while the king's men burned what was left of their name off the earth. And Lena had run, and hidden, and shrunk herself down year after year into something so small and careful and safe that sometimes she forgot there had ever been thunder in her blood.

Edmund shifted against the boulder. His breath came ragged. "Lena." Just her name. He didn't have the strength for more, and he didn't need it. She heard everything underneath it anyway—the same words he'd been carrying since the firelight confession, since the massacre he'd been ordered to stand down from, five years of guilt worn smooth as riverstone. *I will not fail you twice.*

"You can't fight them," Brann whispered. "There are too many."

He was right. That was the whole arithmetic of the moment, simple and final. Forty against nine. Bolts against bodies. A child, a wounded man, an old woman. Every road out of this canyon ended in chains or in graves.

Except one road.

It had always been the only road. She'd just spent half a lifetime refusing to look at it.

Lena closed her eyes.

She had built the cage around herself so carefully that opening it required remembering every bar. The forged papers. The trinkets with no true charm in them, sold with a smile while real magic rotted behind her teeth. The nights in Vell when storms rolled over the city and she lay awake counting the lightning flashes, wanting them the way the drowning want air, and hating herself for wanting it. The day her neighbor Marta was dragged away in chains, and Lena had watched from her doorway and done nothing, because doing nothing was survival, because being invisible was the only country she was allowed.

She was so tired of that country.

"Get behind me," she said. Her voice came out strange—not loud, but carrying, the way thunder carries before you see the cloud that made it. "All of you. Behind me, against the rock. Now."

They moved. Even Edmund, dragging himself with his good arm, refusing the help Yeva offered. Tam pressed his face into Lena's coat, and she felt him trembling, and she put her free hand flat against the granite wall of the canyon.

Stone first. Stone was patient. Stone remembered.

Beneath her palm she felt it—the deep cold pulse of the mountain, the slow water locked in the glacier above, the iron veins threading the rock like dark roots. And threaded through all of it, thin and wild and hungry, the storm that had followed them for three days, circling overhead, waiting. It had been waiting all along. Storms always knew her. They had known her since she was six years old and called her first thunder by accident, crying over a dead sparrow, and split the sky open in answer.

*I am not ashamed of you,* she told it, silently, the way you speak to something you've starved and feared and loved. *I am done being ashamed.*

She opened the cage.

It did not feel like power. That was the last lie the hiding had taught her—that the gift was a weapon she could pick up or set down. It felt like *remembering*. Like a limb waking after years asleep, all pins and needles and then agony and then something beyond agony, a fullness so vast her body could barely contain the shape of it. The storm poured into her through the stone and the air and the snow-hung sky, and she poured back, and the boundary between Lena Blackwood, charm-seller, liar, exile—and the thing her mother had named her—dissolved like frost on warm glass.

She opened her eyes, and they were not entirely her own color anymore. Silver-white, shot through, lit from inside.

Captain Ives stopped smiling.

"Lena," Edmund breathed, and there was awe in it, and fear, and neither of those frightened her. What frightened her was how much she loved this. How natural it was. How the storm fit her the way her mother had promised it would, seamless, inevitable, like a truth finally spoken aloud.

"Run," she said to her people, and did not wait to see if they obeyed.

She lifted her hand, and the sky answered.

The first strike hit the canyon mouth and turned three feet of packed snow to steam. The crack of it was not sound so much as a fact—a declaration written in white fire across the walls of the pass, echoing and re-echoing until the whole gorge rang like a struck bell. Horses screamed. Soldiers broke line, broke formation, broke *rank*, forty men discovering all at once that discipline is a small thing to carry into the path of a god's bad temper. Ives was shouting orders and no one was listening; even his voice, that beautiful composed instrument, was swallowed.

Lightning walked the canyon walls. She guided it—not commanded, never commanded; the storm was no soldier to be ordered, it was kin, and kin must be asked—she asked, and it gave, and each strike was a door slamming shut on the road below. The second crossbowman's bolt never flew; the tower of ice he'd braced against shed its top half in a groaning slide that sent him scrambling down into the rout. Snow came loose everywhere, great sighing sheets of it, and the temperature dropped so fast that torches guttered blue and died.

Through it all Lena stood with her hand raised and her heart wide open, and she wept, though she couldn't have said whether from grief or joy, and suspected it made no difference. This was who she was. This had always been who she was. The cost of knowing it arrived with the knowledge, indivisible: she could feel the storm's hunger now, its indifference, its vastness. It did not care about Tam's frozen fingers or Edmund's wound or the green valley she dreamed of. It cared about falling. And she was the only levee between its falling and everyone she loved.

That was when she felt it slip.

Power like this did not flow; it *flooded*. And somewhere in the flood, in the gorgeous terrible communion of it, she lost the thread of herself—lost track of where the storm ended and the small mortal girl began. Her next strike came down too close. Too wild. It hit the slope above the ledge where her people huddled, and the granite shrieked, and a shelf of ancient ice let go.

She saw it all in the stretched time of catastrophe: the avalanche's white tongue reaching for the rocks, and beneath it—too slow, still too weak to run far—Tam, stumbling away from Edmund's side, looking up at the falling sky with his mouth open.

"No—" Her voice. Or the storm's. She honestly could not have said which.

She wrenched at it, hauled back on the flood with every scrap of self she could find, and the lightning died mid-air—but the ice was already moving, physics and gravity owing nothing to her gift, tons of white death sliding down the mountainside toward the smallest person she had ever loved.

And Edmund moved.

She would replay it forever after, in the long nights of the country they would someday build: Edmund, one-armed, half-dead, a crossbow bolt still lodged near his shoulder, pushing off the boulder and crossing the distance in four staggering strides that no one who saw him ever believed possible—and wrapping himself around Tam like a shield wall of one man, turning his back to the avalanche, folding the boy into the hollow of his body against the rock.

The ice broke over them like a wave.

When it settled, there was a mound of snow and shattered shelf-stone, and Lena could not see either of them, and the storm inside her went silent as a held breath.

She ran. She tore at the snow with bare hands, silver-eyed, lightning still crawling faintly along her knuckles and leaping harmlessly into the drifts, screaming names—*Edmund, Tam, please, please*—and Brann and Yeva dug beside her, and the twins melted the packed ice with their palms until their noses bled, and none of it mattered, none of it was fast enough—

Then the snow shifted. Heaved. And a black-gloved fist punched up through the white like something rising from a grave, and Edmund dragged himself out with Tam bundled against his chest, coughing, bleeding freely from his reopened wound, his lips grey as the glacier.

But breathing. Both of them. Breathing.

Tam was sobbing into his coat. Edmund looked up at Lena—at what she was now, storm-eyed and terrible and haloed in drifting snow-light—and he did not flinch. He smiled, bloody and exhausted, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"There she is."

Below them, the canyon was empty. What remained of Ives's company had fled down the pass, and the snow was already filling their tracks. But Lena could feel him still—somewhere in the white murk, retreating but not broken, the smiling man already rebuilding his composure piece by piece, already thinking three moves ahead.

The Grey Door lay a half-day's climb above them, in the glacier wall. And the storm she had unleashed was not finished with these mountains. She could feel it circling, vast and delighted, wanting more.

She had opened the cage. Now she had to learn—fast, with a child in her arms and a dying man at her side—how to close it again without closing herself.
