## 9. The Grey Door

**Edmund**

The Door was not what Edmund had expected. He had imagined something grand—a gate, an arch, some monument of the old kingdom that had erased itself. Instead it was a seam, a hairline fracture of light running up the glacier wall like a crack in blue glass, thin as a knife's edge and humming with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard. Snow hissed against it and did not land. It simply ceased, as if the Door were a wound in the world that swallowed everything that touched its lips.

Behind them, down the broken road, the storm Lena had called still raged—but it was not her storm anymore. It had gone feral, wheeling above the pass like a living thing, and Edmund knew, watching her stand before that seam of light with snow in her lashes and exhaustion carved into every line of her, that she was running on something beyond strength now. Will, maybe. Or grief wearing will's clothing.

"Edmund." Her voice was hoarse from shouting into the wind. "How long can you hold them?"

He turned. Below, at the mouth of the pass, torches. Dozens of them, snaking upward through the snow like a lit fuse burning toward them. Ives had not stopped. Ives had never once stopped.

"Long enough," Edmund said, and drew his sword.

It was an old sword, oath-keeper's steel, and it had failed him once already. He had stood in a courtyard five years ago with this blade at his hip while orders were read aloud—*stand down, let the storm-callers be taken*—and he had obeyed, because an oath-keeper's first duty was obedience, and he had told himself that for years, right up until the night by the fire when he'd finally said the truth aloud: *I broke it. I let them die. I let her go because dying beside her would have been easier than living after.*

Now the sword felt lighter than it ever had. Perhaps because he no longer asked it to carry the weight of atonement.

"Edmund." Lena caught his sleeve. Her fingers were shaking—from cold, from the storm still leaking through her skin in small blue sparks. "There's something I need you to do first."

"There's no time—"

"There's always time for this." Her eyes were fierce and wet. "You said you'd hold them. Fine. But you don't hold them for me as my protector. I'm done being protected. Do you understand? I'm done with men standing between me and the world."

The wind screamed down the pass. He looked at her—this woman he had carried like a wound for five years, this woman he had followed across a kingdom that had forgotten her country's name—and he understood what she was asking. Not forgiveness. Not yet. Something harder. Something that had to be built by hand, stone by stone, in front of witnesses.

He knelt in the snow.

Not before her—beside her, one knee down, the way oath-keepers swore to equals, to partners, to those who walked the same road. Tam gasped somewhere behind him. Maren, who had arrived two days past, half-frozen and furious at herself for coming, put a hand on the boy's shoulder and went still.

"Edmund of the Vell garrison," he said, and his voice carried strangely in the wind, as if the Door itself were listening. "I, Edmund Hale, do release the oath I swore to House Blackwood. The oath is broken. It has been broken five years. I name it broken now, and I will not carry its ghost any longer." His throat tightened. He forced the words through. "I swear no new oath of protection. Protection is a wall, and walls have failed her before."

He looked up at Lena. Snow was melting on her cheeks, and he could not tell if it was only snow.

"I swear instead this: to walk beside Lena Blackwood. Not before her. Not behind her. Not between her and the world. Beside her. Into whatever country we build. Into whatever storm she calls. Until the road ends or I do. This I swear on my name, which is the only thing I have left that was never anyone else's."

The wind dropped. Just for a heartbeat—the whole pass went silent, snow hanging in the air like held breath—and Edmund would swear until his dying day that the Door hummed a note lower, deeper, acknowledging what had been unmade and what had been made.

Lena reached down and took his hand. Not pulled him up. Held.

"That's the first true thing anyone's sworn to me since the fall," she said. "Get up. They're coming."

* * *

**Lena**

The soldiers came up the pass like a dark tide, and Lena turned to face them with the Door at her back and her people moving through it behind her.

She had thought, in the canyon, that calling the storm would feel like dying. It had not. It had felt like *remembering*—like a limb waking after five years of sleep, all pins and needles and terrible, gorgeous ache. Now she stood with that power humming in her chest, and she made herself a promise: she would not suppress it, and she would not let it use her. Both of those things had been cages. She was done with cages.

"Through," she said over her shoulder. "Tam first. Then the others. Maren, you're last after Edmund."

"I'm not going," Tam said, small and stubborn at her elbow, his little gift flickering gold around his fingers the way it did when he was scared. "I'm not leaving you."

"You're not leaving me. I'm right behind you." She crouched, took his face in her cold hands. "Tam. Look at me. The Door stays open as long as I say it stays open. And I say it stays open until everyone I love is on the other side. That's how this works. That's the whole trick."

His lip trembled. Then he nodded and let Maren pull him toward the light.

Captain Ives reached the head of his soldiers and stopped. That was the thing about Ives that had always unsettled her, in the two glimpses she'd had of him across the canyon and the pass—he stopped when other men would have charged. He stood in the driving snow with his sword sheathed, of all things, and he looked past her, at the seam of light in the glacier, and his face did something complicated.

"So it's real," he said. His voice was almost gentle. "The Door. We were told it was a smuggler's fable. A story to keep the gifted running in circles." He tilted his head. "You could come back, you know. The king would pardon a storm-caller of your strength. Pardon, and a title. They always want the strong ones on their side."

"I had a homeland once," Lena said. "It had a name. Your king scraped it off every map and every signpost from here to the coast. Why would I want anything else of his?"

Ives studied her for a long moment. And then—she would think about this for years, on the other side, in the green valley, trying to understand it—he stepped aside. Not far. Just enough. His soldiers looked at him, and he looked at the storm still wheeling overhead, the impossible storm that had scattered forty armed men in the canyon, and something in his face was a man doing arithmetic he did not like the answer to.

"Form a line," he said quietly. "Hold the pass. Let no one *return*." And to her, barely audible under the wind: "Go, then. See what's out there. See if the stories are kinder than the king."

It was not mercy. She knew that. It was perhaps the first crack of doubt in a man built entirely of certainty, and she would take it, because she had no use for hating him and every use for the road behind her staying quiet.

Then the line wavered—one soldier, younger than the rest, whose nerve broke at the sight of the Door—and Ives's discipline snapped back like a whip, and he gave the order after all, and the tide came on.

Lena turned to the storm.

She did not fight it this time. She *invited* it. Lightning walked down the pass in slow, branching steps, not to kill—she was done with nearly killing children, done with power that cost more than it bought—but to blind, to deafen, to make the ice groan and the snow slide and the soldiers' courage fail. Thunder cracked the sky open like a struck bell. The glacier shuddered. And through all of it she held the Door, one hand pressed to the seam of light, pouring everything she was into keeping that wound in the world open while her family passed through it one by one.

Sef went through without a backward glance, and she was glad, in a cold way, that she'd stopped expecting anything else from him. Maren went with Tam's hand in hers, the healer's face wet, whispering something that might have been a prayer or an apology to the life she'd buried in the hill beneath the hearth. The last of the refugees—old Corrin, the twins, the mute woman whose gift was in her hands—went through like water finding its level.

Then it was Edmund, and he was bleeding from a graze across the brow where a crossbow bolt had kissed him in the canyon, and he stood at the threshold and did not step through.

"Together," he said. "That was the oath."

"The Door closes when I let go."

"Then let go, and we find out what's on the other side together. Or we hold it together until it takes us both. But I don't walk through anything without you again, Lena. Not ever again. That's the whole oath. That's all of it."

The storm above her was dying—storms spent, like everything—but the Door's pull was growing, hungry, the light narrowing around her fingers like a closing eye. Her arm ached to the shoulder. Her knees were water.

She looked at him—this disgraced, stubborn, faithful man—and forgiveness did not come, not the way she'd once imagined it would, like a warm tide. It came the way a wall comes down: stone by stone, by decision, by the raw act of choosing it with no feeling in it at all except the choosing.

"I forgive you," she said. "Not because it's finished. Because I'm tired of carrying it, and you're tired of owing it, and neither of us can build a country out of that." She grabbed his wrist. "Come on, oath-keeper. Walk beside me."

They stepped through together.

The world turned inside out—a soundless, lightless instant, the sensation of a held breath five years long finally released—and then snow was falling on them again, but it was *warm* snow, and the air smelled of pine and green growing things, and below them a valley opened in the blue evening like a cupped hand, empty, unmapped, belonging to no king and no history.

Lena stood at the top of the slope, Edmund beside her, and looked down at the campfires already being lit by the small figures of her people, and at the wide dark land beyond them, waiting.

"We'll need a name for it," Edmund said.

"Not tonight." She smiled, and sparks of storm-light danced in her hair like a crown she had finally stopped being afraid to wear. "Tonight we sleep. Tomorrow—" She looked at the valley, and at him, and at the Door sealing itself into ordinary ice behind them, its hum fading like a song ending. "Tomorrow we decide who we are when no one is hunting us."

She took his hand, and they walked down together into the country not yet named.
