## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

There were three hundred and eleven ways to misfile a book, and Coral Iversen had personally invented at least forty of them.

This was not a confession they made lightly. It was, in fact, the sort of thing one only admitted after eleven years in the vaults of the Guild of Impossible Things, where the shelving system had been designed by a man who believed alphabetical order was a form of tyranny and had replaced it with something he called *emotional adjacency*. Books about grief sat next to books about bread. A treatise on the migration patterns of extinct birds shared a shelf with a cookbook annotated by someone who had clearly been weeping into the margins. Coral had spent their first year at the guild weeping quietly over the cataloguing system and their second decade defending it to visiting archivists with the serene confidence of a convert.

So it was that on an ordinary Tuesday — the twenty-third of Marrowmonth, which was not a real month but had been invented by the guild's founders because they felt twelve was too few and thirteen invited trouble — Coral stood on the rolling ladder in Vault Nine, doing inventory, humming.

The humming was important. The vaults responded poorly to silence. Coral had learned this early: leave a vault quiet too long and the books began to whisper to each other, trading marginalia like gossiping aunts. Humming kept them docile. Tonight's selection was an old ferry song Oddrun used to sing while she scalded the milk for tea, back when Coral was small enough to sit on her counter and steal sultanas.

"Item," Coral said, in the flat recitation required by inventory protocol. "*Consolations of Botany*, water-damaged, owner deceased, marginalia sentimental. Shelf position confirmed." They made the entry in their ledger — an actual paper ledger, because the guild did not trust anything that could be edited without leaving fingerprints — and reached for the next volume.

It was bound in grey leather gone soft as moleskin, unmarked except for a date stamped on the spine in faded gilt: **1899**.

Coral paused.

They knew every book in Vault Nine. That was not a boast; it was a job requirement, and also the natural consequence of having no social life whatsoever. There were 4,206 volumes in this vault and Coral could have told you where each one sulked. This book was not sulking anywhere Coral remembered. It was sitting on the *Consolations of Botany* shelf, which was emotional-adjacent nonsense even by the founder's standards, unless you counted prophecy as a consolation, which Coral absolutely did not.

"Item," Coral said, more slowly now. "Untitled. Grey binding. Date of... publication?" They turned it over. No publisher's mark. No author. Just the date, and inside, when they opened it, handwriting so fine and even it looked typeset.

The first pages were prophecies. Of course they were. The guild collected impossible things, and prophecy was the most impossible genre there was — all of it wrong until suddenly, catastrophically, some of it wasn't. Coral had catalogued hundreds of these. A book predicting the Great Fire of Harrow Quay three days late. A woman who dreamed shipping forecasts accurate to the wave. A child's drawing of a machine two centuries before anyone built it, complete with a note in crayon: *this is probably nothing.*

Prophecy, properly filed, was harmless. It was history wearing a costume.

Coral turned the pages with the detached efficiency of long practice. Famine, flood, a comet described as "disappointingly small." The handwriting never wavered. Whoever wrote this had the composure of a bank clerk recording other people's disasters.

Then, near the end, a page headed with a date.

*The twenty-third of Marrowmonth.*

Coral's hand went still on the page.

That was tonight. That was *this* Tuesday, this inventory shift, this exact hour — Coral knew because the guild clock in the corner had chimed the half-hour four minutes ago and Coral had resented every chime as usual.

They read on, and the professional calm that Coral wore like a well-tailored coat — the calm that had survived leaking roofs, haunted folios, and one memorable incident involving a book that bit — began, very quietly, to come apart at the seams.

*A keeper of ledgers shall find this page,* the handwriting said. *They will be doing inventory. They will have been humming.*

Coral stopped humming.

The silence lasted perhaps three seconds before Coral, out of pure spite and deeper instinct, resumed it — off-key, defiantly, like whistling past a graveyard that had started whistling back. But their eyes kept moving down the page, because that was the terrible thing about reading: you couldn't unread. Coral had tried once, with a particularly graphic account of a sea monster's digestion, and failed utterly.

*Their name is Coral Iversen.*

The ladder swayed. Coral gripped the rail and did not look down, because looking down would mean acknowledging that their knees had opinions, and Coral's knees were strictly ornamental as far as Coral was concerned.

Names in prophecy books were common enough. Names were cheap. Half the population of the city appeared in some crank's fevered margins, destined to marry a stranger or drown in a canal or inherit a haunted spoon. Coral had once catalogued a book naming forty-seven different people as the true heir to a kingdom that didn't exist. Names meant nothing.

But the handwriting didn't stop at the name.

*They will hold this book at arm's length first, then close to their face, because they are vain about their eyes and will not admit they need spectacles.*

Coral lowered the book from arm's length, where they had indeed been holding it, and brought it closer to their face, where they could actually read it, and hated everything.

"Right," Coral said aloud, to the vault, in the tone of a professional addressing a colleague who has taken a joke too far. "Very funny. Who put you here?"

The books of Vault Nine did not answer, which was unusual, because normally they couldn't help themselves.

*On this night,* the page continued, *the salt gate beneath the guildhouse will begin to weep. And the keeper of the Vessengard door will learn what they have been keeping. On this night, the ledger becomes the prophecy, and the keeper becomes the key.*

Coral read it twice. Then a third time, mouthing the words, because somewhere between the second and third reading their brain had begun insisting the text was in a foreign language, and Coral's brain was a coward.

Vessengard.

Everyone knew the word the way everyone knew a scar — you stopped seeing it, until someone asked about it. Vessengard had ceased to exist forty years ago. Not conquered, not dissolved: *ceased*. One day it was on the maps; the next day the cartographers' guild issued corrections, the treaties were reprinted without it, and anyone who claimed to remember it was gently encouraged to take up a hobby. Officially, Vessengard was a printing error that had gotten out of hand.

Unofficially — and Coral worked for the unofficial capital of the world — there was a gate beneath the guildhouse. Salt-sealed. Off-limits. Coral had walked past its corridor a thousand times and had been told, since their first week, exactly one thing about it: *not your department.* Coral had accepted this the way they accepted the emotional shelving and the biting books — as one of the guild's eccentricities, no more worth questioning than why the tea always brewed itself stronger when Oddrun was worried.

Oddrun. Coral thought of her suddenly, sharply, the way you think of home when the floor tilts. Oddrun, who had found them abandoned on the guild steps as an infant and raised them on tea and stubbornness. Oddrun, who hummed the same ferry song. Oddrun, who had a faint accent she'd never explained and a way of saying the word *salt* that sounded less like seasoning and more like grief.

Coral looked at the page again. *The keeper becomes the key.*

"I'm not a keeper," Coral informed the book. "I'm an archivist. I keep *ledgers*. There's a difference, and it's the entire difference, and whoever wrote this should be ashamed."

The book, being a book, declined to defend itself.

Coral did what any reasonable professional would do. They climbed down the ladder with exaggerated care, carried the volume to the inventory desk, and prepared to file it.

This was the plan: catalogue it under *Errata, Prophetic, Discredited*, cross-reference nothing, shelve it in the farthest recess of Vault Twelve behind the tax records of a nation that had drowned, and never think of it again. Prophecy was harmless when properly filed. Coral had built a whole life on that principle — a good life, a warm life, a life measured in tea and tidy entries and Oddrun's cooking — and the fastest way to protect it was to make this book somebody else's problem, preferably a problem for a future archivist with better nerves.

They opened the front cover to write the accession number.

Inside, in the same immaculate hand, was a single line Coral had somehow missed:

*Filed by C. Iversen.*

And beneath it, in ink that was still, unmistakably, wet:

*Tonight.*

* * *

Coral set the book down very gently, the way you set down something that might be asleep.

Then they picked up their own ledger, checked the date, checked the time, and performed the small ritual that had steadied them through every crisis of their working life: they made an entry.

*Item: one (1) volume, prophetic, disreputable. Found misplaced in Vault Nine. Contents: alarmist. Recommendation: file under Errata and review never.*

The pen scratched. The vault listened. Somewhere below the floor — below the archives, below the cellars, below whatever the guild actually kept down there — Coral heard, or imagined they heard, a sound like water finding a crack in stone.

Salt, said a smell that hadn't been there a moment ago. Old sea, old stone, old sorrow.

Coral capped the ink, squared the ledger, and told themselves — firmly, professionally, in the voice they used on haunted folios — that the sensible course of action was to finish the inventory, go upstairs, drink tea, and mention none of this to anyone, ever.

They got as far as the vault door.

Then, from the direction of the sealed corridor, came a sound Coral had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own name called in a crowd.

Dripping.

Slow. Rhythmic. Patient as a tide.

Something beneath the guildhouse had begun, very softly, to weep.
