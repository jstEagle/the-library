## 2. Salt on the Sill

Coral stood in the dark of the under-corridor with a lantern in one hand and a very strong desire to be elsewhere in the other.

The dripping came again. *Drip.* A pause, long enough to be polite. *Drip.*

"That's not ominous at all," Coral told the corridor, because the corridor was listening, and because saying things out loud had always been how they kept the fear at a manageable filing size. "That's just plumbing. Old buildings have plumbing. Old buildings have *moods.*"

The Guild of Impossible Things had many moods, in Coral's long professional experience. It creaked in winter like an old man complaining about his knees. It hummed on Tuesdays for reasons the Survey Committee had never successfully documented. It had once, for eleven days, smelled of cinnamon, and nobody had ever found out why, and eventually everyone had simply accepted it, the way you accept an aunt who laughs at the wrong moments.

But the guild had never *wept* before.

Coral followed the sound. This was, they would later reflect, a decision made by the part of them that had spent fifteen years as an archivist and had therefore lost the healthy instinct to leave strange things alone. The corridor sloped downward past the wine cellar that held no wine — past the door labeled FUTURE ACQUISITIONS, which was bricked over and had been since before Coral's time — and past the shelf where the guild kept its collection of apologies, bottled and labeled by decade. The air grew cold and then colder, and then it grew *briny*, thick as a held breath, and Coral's lantern flame leaned sideways as though trying to look over their shoulder.

The corridor ended at the gate.

Everyone at the guild knew about the gate, in the way that everyone knows about the load-bearing wall in the middle of the house: you don't ask about it, you don't lean on it, and you absolutely do not, under any circumstances, knock. It was a door of grey stone set into the living rock, taller than two men, carved over with script that the guild's linguists had spent a century failing to read. It had no handle. It had no keyhole. It had, according to the inventory Coral had personally catalogued on their first day, no function whatsoever.

*Sealed portal, origin unknown, status: decorative,* the ledger said. Coral had written *decorative* themselves, at twenty-two, with the confidence of the young.

It was weeping now. Brine ran down the stone in slow, glistening threads, pooling at the threshold, creeping toward Coral's boots with the unhurried confidence of something that had all the time in the world. Where it touched the floor, the dust *curled away from it*, as though the floor were flinching.

Coral crouched — because you should always greet a crisis at its own level, Oddrun said; it disarms them — and dipped one finger into the pool.

Salt. Cold as grief. And underneath the salt, faint as a name called from a far room, something that was almost a voice, almost a word, almost—

"*Don't,*" said Coral, to the gate, to themselves, to the universe in general. "I've had a very long day. I found a prophecy with my name in it. My name. In a book older than the guild. And I have decided, as a professional, to file that under *pending*, which means the universe is going to have to *wait its turn*."

The dripping paused.

Then it resumed, and Coral would have sworn — would have sworn on Oddrun's recipe book, which was the holiest object they knew — that it sounded *amused*.

* * *

Coral did what any sensible archivist would do. They went upstairs, they fetched a mop, a bucket, and the guild's Manual of Structural Anomalies (Revised), and they came back down and began, with tremendous dignity, to mop.

This was the correct response. The Manual was very clear on this point. *Anomaly Class III (Ambient Distress): Do not engage. Document. Mop if necessary.* Somebody, sometime in the guild's long history, had clearly dealt with a weeping wall before, and Coral found enormous comfort in the thought that there was precedent, that somewhere in the archives there was a form for this, a *procedure*, a nice tidy box to put the end of the world in.

They were on their second bucket when the bell rang.

Not the front bell. Not the tradesman's bell, not the members' bell, not any of the seven bells the guild rang for its seven kinds of visitor. This was the eighth bell — the one above the door that officially did not exist, the one Coral had catalogued as *decorative* on the same confident day as the gate.

The bell that meant: *someone is here who was never invited, and the house knows it.*

Coral set down the mop with the care of someone laying down a sleeping child, and went up.

* * *

The entrance hall of the Guild of Impossible Things was designed to intimidate visitors, though most of its intimidation was accidental. The chandelier was made of antlers. The carpet was the color of a bruise and had, on at least one occasion, been the site of a duel. The portrait of the Founding Twelve hung above the hearth, and their painted eyes followed you, which the guild insisted was a trick of varnish and which Coral had long ago stopped believing.

The visitor stood in the middle of the carpet with the stillness of someone who had never once had to hurry.

They wore a mask. Of course they wore a mask; that was what patrons *did*. It was porcelain, white as fresh paper, featureless except for two slits, and it caught the firelight in a way that made Coral's eyes water if they looked too long. The visitor's coat was grey. Their gloves were grey. Their voice, when it came, was grey too — not unkind, exactly. Worse than unkind. *Administrative.*

"Good evening," the visitor said. "I am here for the Vessengard asset."

Coral, who had spent fifteen years perfecting the guild's front-of-house manner — warm, brisk, faintly amused, the manner of a person who has heard every strange request and found a drawer for it — heard themselves say:

"I'm terribly sorry, we don't have one of those."

It was, in hindsight, the worst possible answer. It was the answer of a person who knew exactly what the words meant.

The mask tilted, very slightly. "No?"

"No," said Coral, doubling down with the desperate energy of a cart going downhill. "We have ledgers. We have a rather good collection of bottled apologies, if you're interested — the 1870s are very strong. We have a door in the cellar, but it's decorative."

"Decorative," the visitor repeated.

"Entirely."

"And yet," said the visitor, and Coral watched, with the detached horror of a person watching their own lie walk off a cliff, "it is weeping."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked in the way it did when the house was listening in, which it always was, the busybody.

"Condensation," said Coral.

"From a sealed stone door."

"Old stone is *very* humid."

The visitor regarded Coral for a long moment. Coral had the sudden, vertiginous sense of standing on a ledge they had been standing on their whole life without ever noticing it was a ledge — that the floor beneath them, the warm familiar floor of ledgers and tea and Oddrun's cinnamon buns, was in fact a *cover story*, a beautifully maintained fiction, and that they had just now looked down.

Because here was the thing, the thing that arrived in Coral's chest like a stone dropped down a well: the visitor had not asked *what is behind the gate.* They had not asked *where is the gate.* They had said *the Vessengard asset*, the way you say *the east wing* or *the good scissors*, the way you say a thing that everyone in the room already knows.

And Coral had known. Some buried, unexamined, filing-clerk corner of them had *always* known — had known why the guild's charter, written in 1743, included a clause about "the safekeeping of that which was lost"; had known why the gate corridor was swept daily by rota though no one had ever assigned it; had known why the Founding Twelve's portrait showed only eleven faces, the twelfth space painted over with a curtain, and why no one had ever, in Coral's hearing, once asked why.

"You're new here," the visitor said. It was not a question.

"Fifteen years."

"A long time to stand in front of a door without opening it." The visitor's gloved hand rose, and for one wild moment Coral thought they were about to be touched, seized, *taken* — but the hand only gestured, gently, at the hall around them. "Do you know what the Guild of Impossible Things was founded to do, archivist?"

"Collect," said Coral. "Catalogue. Preserve."

"Mm." The mask's blank face managed, somehow, to convey pity. "That is what it was founded to *say*. What it was founded to *do* was stand between a dying country and the people who want to make certain it finishes dying. You have been a guard your whole career and no one thought to tell you. How careless of them."

The word *Vessengard* sat in Coral's mind like a coin on a rail. They knew it, of course — everyone knew it, the way everyone knew the shape of a country on a map that had been redrawn forty years ago, a country that had *stopped existing*, officially, diplomatically, cartographically, the treaties signed and the ink long dry. A country that was now a footnote, a trivia question, a melancholy song.

A country with a gate under the guildhouse that was weeping salt into a bucket.

"The asset," the visitor said, "is not the door. The asset is the *keeper*. And the keeper, I am told, is on duty tonight."

Coral's mouth was dry as old vellum. "I think," they said, with the last shreds of their front-of-house voice, "that you should make an appointment."

"Delightful," said the visitor, and Coral could *hear* the smile behind the porcelain. "I shall return. Do give my regards to the tea-brewer. She'll remember me. She was there when the gate was sealed — though I imagine she's spent forty years hoping you'd never be old enough to ask her why."

And the visitor walked out the front door, which opened for them without being touched, and the night swallowed them, and the eighth bell did not ring again.

* * *

Coral stood in the entrance hall for a long time. Long enough that the fire burned down a log. Long enough that their own reflection in the darkened window became a stranger wearing their face, and Coral thought, with a laugh that came out wrong, *well, that's the theme of the evening, isn't it.*

Their whole life. Their whole *life* — the orphan found on the guild steps, the child raised in the kitchens among tea steam and gossip, the archivist with the tidy hand and the tidy drawers — had been a *cover story*. Not even their own. Someone else's cover story, and they had lived inside it like a snail in a borrowed shell, and the shell had a door in the cellar, and the door had a name, and the name was a country that wasn't there.

And Oddrun.

*She was there when the gate was sealed.*

Coral's hands were shaking. They looked down at them, at the ink stains and the mop-water wrinkles, at the hands of a person whose entire biography had just been revealed as a forgery — and found, underneath the vertigo, underneath the dread, something small and stubborn and warm.

*She'll remember me,* the visitor had said. *She was there.*

Oddrun had lied to them for thirty years. Oddrun had folded them into a life like a letter into an envelope. Oddrun had, possibly, been part of the thing that had orphaned them in the first place — or part of the thing that had *saved* them, which might be the same thing, which might be the whole point of the guild, which might be the answer to every question Coral had never had the nerve to ask.

There was only one way to find out.

Coral took the lantern back down the corridor — past the apologies, past the bricked-over future — and stood before the gate, which was still weeping, patient as a tide, and said, in a voice that only shook a little:

"I'm going to need the whole story. And Oddrun is going to tell it to me."

Behind the stone, soft as a wave drawing back, something that was almost a voice said a word.

Coral did not understand it.

But they knew, with the certainty of a person who has spent their life among old books, that it was a name.

Their mother's.
