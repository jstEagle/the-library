## 3. A Country That Isn't There

Coral did not sleep. This was not unusual — archivists are a nocturnal genus, like moths or mathematicians — but tonight the sleeplessness had teeth. The prophecy page sat in front of them on the reading table, weighted down at the corners with four brass inkwells as though it might otherwise fly away and take Coral's name with it.

*The keeper becomes the key.*

They had considered, briefly, filing it under M for Mistakes, which was a real drawer in the guild catalogue and contained, among other things, a taxidermied pigeon that had once been a duke. But the handwriting was their mother's. That was the thing Coral kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. The slanted capitals. The way she crossed her sevens. Coral had spent thirty years cataloguing dead people's hands, and this one they would know anywhere, because it was half of where their own came from.

So: no drawer. No pretending. Research instead, because grief in Coral's experience behaved best when given a task.

Vessengard, then.

* * *

The guild's map room occupied the third floor of the vault wing, a long chamber with skylights of smoked glass and a smell of ozone and old varnish. Maps were difficult things at the Guild of Impossible Things. A map that showed only what was true was nearly useless here; a map that showed what *believed itself* true was worse. The guild kept both kinds, clearly labeled, and a third kind that nobody labeled because nobody could decide what to call them.

Coral pulled the atlas marked **VESSENGARD — DISPUTED / DISSOLVED / SEE ALSO: NO** from its shelf and opened it on the great oak table.

The map inside was wrong.

Not badly wrong — not the charming wrongness of a cartographer who'd never seen the coastline he drew — but wrong the way a face is wrong in a dream. The mountains were in plausible places. The rivers ran downhill. And yet every time Coral looked away and looked back, something had shifted by a finger's width. The port city of Halmvik drifted northward along the coast like a man edging toward an exit. The border with Aldemar breathed.

Coral pinned the corners flat and fetched the guild's oldest gazetteer, printed in 1887, when Vessengard still officially troubled the world by existing.

> **VESSENGARD** (Old Norse *vess-*, "wet," + *gardr*, "enclosure"): constitutional kingdom, pop. 2,100,000 (est.). Chief exports: salt, amber, lullabies (see FOLK TRADES). Capital: Vesselund. Notable features: the Brine Gate at Solvfjord, one of three known doors between the world and the Undervold.
>
> *Note appended 1949:* Kingdom dissolved by mutual accord. See ALDEMAR ANNEXATION. See also NOTHING HERE. See also PLEASE STOP ASKING.

Coral read that last line four times. Somebody — some archivist forty years ago — had been frightened enough to write *please stop asking* into a reference book, which violated about six of the guild's standing rules and suggested the seventh rule had been added specifically because of them.

And underneath the entry, in the margin, in a hand Coral knew better than their own pulse:

*Coral, love — if you're reading this, you found the atlas before I found you. I'm sorry. Ask Oddrun about the gate. Don't ask the patrons anything. — M*

Their mother had known. Their mother had stood at this same table, touched this same page, and written a note to a child she must have known would one day be tall enough to reach the top shelf, and then she had gone into Vessengard anyway.

* * *

The letters lived in a hatbox, because their mother had believed important documents should live somewhere slightly ridiculous so no one would look in them seriously. Coral had read them all before, dozens of times across the years — but that was before the prophecy, before *the keeper becomes the key*, before they knew there was a door beneath the guildhouse that wept salt. Letters, Coral was learning, are documents that change their minds about what they mean.

The earliest ones were travel notes, cheerful and specific:

*Dearest sprout — Halmvik smells of fish and church candles, and everyone apologizes in two languages at once. The salt here isn't for preserving food. It's for preserving everything else. They pack the doorways with it like plum jam. I asked a woman why and she said, "So the country keeps," and laughed, but not like it was funny. Buy me postcards. — M*

Then the tone shifted. Coral could pinpoint the letter. It was the one dated the autumn before their birth, the one with a pressed sprig of sea-lavender between the pages that had long since crumbled to violet dust:

*Sprout — I've made a trade. Don't be angry. You weren't using them yet, that's what the broker said, and he wasn't wrong, though I hit him anyway. Three memories, yours not mine, held in trust until you'd want them. It bought us passage out, and passage out means you get to exist somewhere with weather and bread and more than one sky. I'd do it again. I'll always have done it again. That's the grammar of mothers. — M*

Three memories. Held in trust. Coral set the letter down very carefully, as if it were sleeping, and noticed for the first time the hollow feeling behind their sternum — the ache of missing something they couldn't name because the naming had been sold off too. There were gaps in them shaped like a woman's face. Coral had assumed all their life that the gaps were just how people were built.

The last letter was undated. The ink was different — rain-blotted, or brine-blotted, or blotted by something that had never learned to be water.

*If I don't come back, I went through. Understand that I didn't fall, sprout, and I didn't fail, and please don't come looking until you're old enough to argue with a door. Vessengard isn't gone. It's kept. Somebody has to keep the keeping. I thought it would be me, but the door has opinions, and lately its opinions sound a lot like your name.*

*I love you past the edge of the map.*

*— M*

*Its opinions sound a lot like your name.*

Coral sat in the map room while the smoked-glass light went from grey to pewter to nothing, and did the arithmetic that archivists do, which is the arithmetic of dates and lies. Forty years ago, Vessengard stopped existing. Thirty-one years ago, their mother walked through a gate and did not come back. Nine years of overlap — nine years during which the guild had hired a young archivist named Coral Iversen, trained them, trusted them with keys, and stationed them, night after night, in the one building in the world with a sealed door beneath it.

Nobody hires a locksmith's child to guard a lock by accident.

* * *

At some point Oddrun appeared with tea, because Oddrun always knew when someone in the building hadn't eaten, in the way that some people know weather in their knees. She was a broad, soft-spoken woman with forearms like a dockworker and a cardigan that had survived three wars, none of them declared. She set the cup down without asking and stayed standing, which she almost never did.

"You found the atlas," she said.

"I found the atlas." Coral turned it toward her. The borders had settled overnight into something almost honest — almost — except for a thin new line of text along the coast that neither of them had written: *she is waiting.*

Oddrun looked at it for a long moment. "Your mother used to sit exactly where you're sitting," she said. "Feet on the chair. Terrible posture. Certain she could out-read any problem."

"Oddrun." Coral's voice came out smaller than intended. "What is Vessengard?"

"A country that made a bargain," Oddrun said. "It agreed to stop being remembered so it wouldn't stop being. Salt seals the door. Memory seals the salt. And the guild—" she paused, choosing words with the care of someone defusing something, "—the guild keeps the ledgers that prove it happened. That's all we are, love. A filing cabinet for a country."

"And my mother?"

"Was the bravest fool I ever brewed tea for." Oddrun's jaw tightened. "She thought she could open the gate from the outside. The gate doesn't open from the outside. That's rather the point of gates."

"Then I'll go in through it."

The words were out before Coral had decided to say them, and once out, they refused to be recalled — which, Coral reflected, was probably why the guild filed decisions under the same heading as pigeons. Oddrun went very still.

"No," she said.

"The prophecy names me, Oddrun. Tonight. *The keeper becomes the key.* I can file that page, I can burn it, I can feed it to the taxidermied duke — it will still be tonight tomorrow, and I will still be the person it's about. If my mother died trying to open that door from the wrong side, then the least I owe her—"

"The least you owe her is to stay *alive*—"

"—is to finish the sentence she started!" Coral stood, and the map rustled on the table as though applauding, which was unsettling. "I've spent my whole life guarding a door I didn't know was a door, cataloguing a country I didn't know was mine, mourning a woman I couldn't remember. I am done being the keeper of other people's secrets. If the key is me, then fine. Keys don't stand in hallways."

Oddrun looked at them for a long time — looked, Coral realized, the way you look at someone you have loved since they were small enough to carry in one arm, and whom you have just watched walk to the edge of something.

"There's a ledger," she said at last, quietly. "Downstairs. The ledger of trades. Your mother's broker kept copies." She pressed her thumb and forefinger briefly to the bridge of her nose. "I was going to tell you when you were older. Everyone always says that, and it never helps anyone. Go. Read it. And Coral — whatever you find in there, remember that she paid it *gladly*. Whatever it cost, she'd tell you it was cheap."

When she had gone, Coral looked down at the atlas. Along the coast of the country that wasn't there, the thin line of text had changed again.

*Come home,* it said. *We kept your seat.*

Coral closed the atlas, picked up their lamp, and went down into the vaults to find the ledger of trades — and the shape of the three holes in their own heart, priced and itemized, waiting to be filled.
