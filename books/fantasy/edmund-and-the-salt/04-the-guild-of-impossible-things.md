## 4. The Guild of Impossible Things

The summons came by teacup.

That was how the guild did things, Coral had learned. No letters — letters could be intercepted. No bells — bells could be heard. Instead, at half past four in the afternoon, the cup in the reading room had filled itself with dark amber tea and a single floating message, written in something that steamed:

*Inner circle convenes at six. Attendance: required. Yours.*

Coral stared at it for a long moment. Tea-messages were usually addressed to guild officers, to archivists of the third rank and above. Coral was an archivist of the first rank, which was a polite way of saying *the person who does the filing*. In eleven years of service, Coral had never once been summoned anywhere.

They drank the tea anyway. It tasted like chamomile and dread.

* * *

The inner circle met in the Round Room, which was not round. This was, Oddrun had once explained, deliberate — "so that no one can ever say the guild thinks in circles" — and Coral had laughed, back when the world still made sense, back before prophecies and weeping gates and patrons with faces like closed books.

Now Coral stood at the edge of the room, beneath the shelf of taxidermied impossible animals (a two-headed owl, a fish that had learned to whistle, a cat mid-pounce that had been pouncing for ninety years), and watched eleven masked figures arrange themselves around the long table.

They had all come. That alone was terrifying. The inner circle rarely managed a quorum; Master Vellum was usually off buying a bridge that didn't exist, and Mistress Quill hadn't left her tower since the incident with the self-writing novel. But tonight every chair was full, every mask on, and the air smelled of ozone and old decisions.

"Coral Iversen." Master Vellum's voice came out flat through his mask, which was painted to look like a ledger page. "Thank you for coming."

"You gave me tea," Coral said. "It seemed rude not to."

A few masks turned toward each other. Somewhere, someone almost smiled.

"You found the page," Mistress Quill said. Her mask was white silk, blank as an unwritten book. "The 1899 prophecy. You found it three days ago, and you filed it under *Miscellaneous, Weather-Adjacent*, and you went home and ate dinner."

Coral's stomach dropped. "How—"

"We are the Guild of Impossible Things," said a third voice, low and gravelly, from behind a mask shaped like a keyhole. "You are our archivist. We know what you file. We know what you *don't* file. The weather-adjacent section has not been used since 1962, when Junior Archivist Pemberton hid his love letters there. We read those too."

"I burned those," said Mistress Quill, with feeling.

"That's not the point," said Keyhole-Mask. "The point is that Coral Iversen found their own name in a century-old prophecy, discovered that the country they've been guarding is real, watched the gate begin to weep salt, lied to a patron — badly, I'm told, you told him the vault was flooded, which he would have known was false because he *built* the drainage — and then came to work this morning and alphabetized the correspondence drawer."

"What else was I supposed to do?" Coral asked.

"*Anything else,*" said three voices at once.

Master Vellum raised a hand, and the room settled. "The guild finds itself," he said, "in a delicate position. For forty years, this institution has maintained a fiction: that Vessengard is a story, a fable, a country that never quite existed. We maintain ledgers proving it never existed. We employ historians whose sole task is to mis-catalogue its history. And all the while—" he gestured downward, toward the foundations, toward the gate, "—we keep it. Behind salt. Behind silence. Behind one archivist who believed they were organizing stationery."

"The asset," Coral said quietly. "The patron called me the keeper of 'the Vessengard asset.' Is that what I am? An asset?"

"You are an archivist," Vellum said. "Who has become, through no fault of your own, load-bearing."

Murmurs around the table. Coral felt the word settle onto their shoulders like wet wool.

"The situation," Mistress Quill continued, "is as follows. The gate is destabilizing. The prophecy is active. And the person named in that prophecy is standing in this room, untrained, unbonded, and — forgive me — *filing things*. The circle must decide what to do with them. Two motions have been prepared."

Keyhole-Mask stood. "Motion one. The liability motion. Coral Iversen's memories of the guild, the gate, and the prophecy are to be removed, cleanly and completely. They will wake tomorrow believing they are a junior clerk at a shipping firm in Harrowgate. They will be happy. They will be safe. The guild will assign a new keeper, one we choose and train from birth, as we should have done forty years ago."

"And motion two," said a mask painted like a storm cloud — Coral thought it might be Mistress Tempest, though she'd always seemed so kind when she came in for her reading materials — "the alternative. If the prophecy names Coral Iversen as either keeper or key, then we do not erase them. We *use* them. Controlled activation. We bind them to the gate on our terms, extract whatever power the prophecy offers, and secure Vessengard as a guild holding. A country behind a door is a country we can open when convenient and close when not. Think of what that's worth. Think of who would pay."

"They're people," Coral said.

"They're *leverage*," said Storm-Mask.

"They're people," Coral repeated, louder, and heard their own voice crack. "I've been carrying their mail. Did you know that? Letters come through the gate sometimes, sealed in salt-paper, and I've been filing them under *Miscellaneous, Correspondence, Unverifiable Origin* for eleven years because I thought they were a prank. There's a woman in there named Sela who writes to her grandson every solstice. He died in 1987 and she doesn't know. She keeps writing. And you want to make her a *holding*?"

Silence. The whistling fish on its shelf seemed, briefly, to stop whistling.

"Motion one," said Vellum. "All in favor."

Five masks rose.

"Motion two."

Five masks rose.

And then, from the end of the table, a voice that Coral knew better than any voice in the world.

"That's ten," said Oddrun. "There are eleven of us. And I haven't voted."

* * *

Oddrun never wore a mask.

This had been a small scandal once, decades ago, and then simply a fact, the way her tea was always exactly the right temperature and her kitchen always smelled of cardamom and woodsmoke. She sat at the end of the table now in her apron, hands folded, gray hair escaping its braid, looking for all the world like someone who had wandered in from a much kinder story.

"Oddrun," Vellum said carefully. "You hold a courtesy seat. You brew for this circle. You do not vote."

"I brewed for this circle when your mother was in swaddling clothes, Tomas Vellum, and I'll thank you to remember it." She looked down the length of the table, at every mask, unhurried. "Ten votes. Five to hollow out the child, five to wear them like a glove. Remarkable. Not one of you asked what Coral wants. Not one of you asked what's *right*. So I'll cast my vote, and then I'll tell you something I should have told them years ago, and if any of you object, you can come to my kitchen and say so, where I keep the knives."

Nobody objected. Nobody ever objected to Oddrun in her kitchen.

"My vote," she said, "is neither. My vote is that Coral Iversen decides for themselves, with full knowledge, and that this guild protects their choice with everything it has. And now the knowledge." She turned to Coral, and her eyes — brown, warm, lined with decades of steam and laughter — were suddenly very old. "You asked me once, when you were nine, why I never spoke of where I was born. You asked me again at fourteen. At nineteen you stopped asking, because I taught you, without meaning to, that some questions close doors instead of opening them. I'm sorry for that. It was cowardice, dressed up as kindness."

Coral's mouth had gone dry. "Oddrun—"

"I was born in Vessengard," she said. "In a town called Hjelle, on the fjord, in a house with blue shutters that I can still draw from memory, though the memory is all that's left of it. I was twenty-six when the sealing came. My mother put me on the last boat out and stayed behind with my grandmother, who was too old to travel and too stubborn to try. I have spent fifty-three years making tea in a guildhouse above the door that keeps them from me. Every letter you filed, Coral — every solstice letter — I read them first. Sela was my neighbor. I held her grandson when he was born."

The room was very quiet. On its shelf, the two-headed owl gazed down with both heads, neither of them wise.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Coral whispered.

"Because telling you made you part of it. And I wanted — God help me, I wanted you *small*. Small and safe and mine, here, in the kitchen, where the prophecies couldn't reach." Her voice frayed. "I traded your bigness for your safety, and I'd do it again, and I've hated myself for it every single day, and that is what love looks like sometimes, and I am done pretending otherwise."

"Oddrun," said Keyhole-Mask gently, "you understand the position this puts us in. If the archivist's guardian is herself a citizen of the vanished country—"

"I'm not finished." Oddrun stood, and even the storm-mask leaned back. "Here is what none of you will say, so I will. The gate isn't failing because of the prophecy. The gate is failing because it's *tired*. Salt seals don't last forever, and the thing behind that door has been holding a whole country in a single evening for forty years, and it is running out of strength, and when it goes, Vessengard doesn't fade peacefully — it *drowns*, all at once, everyone in it, and the flood comes up through our foundations. Your liability motion doesn't save anyone. Your leverage motion just picks which drowning people you profit from. There is one way through this and it's standing right there, frightened out of their mind, and every one of you knows it."

She looked at Coral. Coral looked at her. Fifty-three years of secrets hung between them like steam.

"The vaults," Coral said hoarsely. "The deep ledgers. The ones only the First Archivist can open. Where are the records of the trades?"

Oddrun's face went pale. "Coral—"

"The trades," Coral said again, louder. "If this guild can hide a country, it can trade memories. I've seen the receipts, Oddrun. I've *filed* the receipts. Whose were they? What did my mother sell?"

"Coral, that's not—"

But Coral was already moving, backing toward the door, toward the stairs, toward the cold stone throat of the vaults where the truth had been waiting in the dark for longer than they'd been alive.

"Coral!" Oddrun called after them, and for the first time in Coral's life, the tea-brewer sounded afraid. "Those ledgers cost your mother everything! Some prices, once you see them, you can't unsee!"

Coral took the stairs three at a time, past the reading room, past the shelf of impossible animals, down into the dark where the oldest ledgers slept.

Behind them, faintly, they heard the inner circle rising from their chairs — and beneath their feet, deeper than the vaults, deeper than the gate, something vast and patient shifted in its sleep, and the salt began, softly, to sing.
