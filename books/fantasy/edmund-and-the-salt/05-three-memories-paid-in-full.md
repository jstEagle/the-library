## 5. Three Memories, Paid In Full

The vault beneath the guildhouse had a smell Coral had long ago stopped noticing and now noticed all at once: dust, tallow, old paper, and under it all a faint mineral tang that Coral was beginning to suspect came up through the floor from the gate. Salt. Everything in this building, it seemed, was seasoned with secrets.

Coral had come down to work. That was the whole of the plan, and Coral clung to it with both hands. Work. Inventory. The comforting arithmetic of cataloguing — *one crate of unsong lullabies, water-damaged; one jar of borrowed courage, expiring; one box marked DO NOT OPEN (see other box)* — because arithmetic did not weep brine on windowsills, and arithmetic did not have masked patrons asking after "the Vessengard asset" in voices like velvet drawn over knives.

Arithmetic also did not include the ledger Coral was currently not looking at.

It sat on the reading stand where Coral had set it an hour ago, having fetched it from the deep stacks on pure professional instinct. *Ledger of Trades, 1841–1911.* The guild recorded everything, that was its purpose and its curse. Debts, exchanges, bartered things. Years sold by the month. Names lent out and never returned. A man in 1873 who traded his shadow for a favorable wind and spent the rest of his life walking slightly sideways.

Coral had read ten thousand entries like these. They had never once made Coral's hands shake.

But Coral knew what they were looking for now. They were looking for *Iversen*.

*Don't,* said the sensible part of Coral, the part that filed pages away and pretended prophecies weren't real. *You could shelve this book spine-out and never open it again. You could inventory candle stubs until your eyes bleed. There is always more candle stub.*

"Yes," Coral said aloud to the empty vault, "but there's always been something wrong with me."

They opened the ledger.

* * *

The entries were written in a dozen hands across seventy years, but Coral found the Iversens quickly, because grief has excellent indexing skills. Or perhaps because the book wanted to be found. Coral was no longer certain there was a difference.

*12th of Frostmonth, 1866 — E. Iversen, of Vessengard, trades one (1) memory of her sister's wedding for safe passage across the Weeping Strait. Price assessed as fair. The bride is remembered as wearing blue. She wore green.*

Coral read it twice. The clerk's note at the bottom — *the bride is remembered as wearing blue. She wore green* — sat in Coral's chest like a swallowed stone. That was what a trade cost, then. Not just the thing given, but the truth of it, smeared, approximated, lost even to the people who kept the loss.

There were more. E. Iversen appeared again and again down the years, each entry a small amputation:

*A memory of her father's workshop, sawdust and linseed oil.*

*A memory of the harbor bells at midwinter.*

*A memory of learning to swim, aged six, in water so cold she screamed and laughed in the same breath.*

Coral tracked her through the decades — their mother, though Coral hadn't known it yet, couldn't know it yet, the ledger only saying *E. Iversen*, and Coral telling themselves *E* could be anyone, *E* was a common letter, stop it, stop it — paying out pieces of herself like coins worn thin. Buying passage. Buying time. Buying, entry by entry, the right to exist somewhere the world insisted she didn't.

And then, near the end of the volume, in a hand that shook:

*3rd of Emberfall, 1898 — E. Iversen, heavy with child, requests final passage out of Vessengard. Has nothing left of her own to give. Requests to trade against the unborn. Broker advises against. Broker overruled.*

Below it, three entries in the broker's careful script, each one a door closing on a room Coral had never known existed inside them:

*Trade the first: from the child, one (1) memory of the mother's face. Held in trust until claimed or forfeit.*

*Trade the second: from the child, one (1) memory of the mother's voice singing. Held in trust until claimed or forfeit.*

*Trade the third: from the child, one (1) memory of being loved before language. Held in trust until claimed or forfeit.*

*Paid in full. Passage granted. May the sea be kinder than the price.*

* * *

Coral did not remember sitting down. They were on the floor between the shelves, the ledger open in their lap, and the lamp had burned low, and their face was wet in a way they had no professional explanation for.

It wasn't crying, exactly. It was mourning. Which was worse, because you can blow your nose and be done with crying. Mourning something you never had was like reaching into your own chest and finding a shaped absence — a hollow exactly the size of a face, a voice, a feeling older than words. Someone had taken a spoon to Coral's life before Coral ever lived it, and the strangest part, the part that made Coral laugh once, horribly, alone in the dark among the unsung lullabies, was that it had been done *for* them. Their mother had paid for Coral's existence with Coral's own interior. Passage purchased with the passenger.

"She didn't have anything left," Coral said to the shelf of water-damaged lullabies, which received the information without comment. "She'd already sold everything else. So she sold me to myself."

And the worst of it — Coral pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes — the worst of it was that Coral understood. Completely. Utterly. If Coral had been standing at a border with a country dissolving behind them and a child on the way, Coral would have made the same trade, and hated themselves in exactly the same handwriting.

*Held in trust until claimed or forfeit.*

Held in trust.

Coral's breath caught. They read the line again, then again, hunting for the trick in it, because there was always a trick, the guild was built out of tricks the way coral reefs are built out of bones.

*Held in trust until claimed or forfeit.*

Not destroyed. Not spent. Not dissolved into the broker's general stockpile of secondhand Tuesdays. *Held.* Somewhere in this building — or wherever the brokers kept their stock — three pieces of Coral were sitting in a drawer, waiting, the way Oddrun's tea waited in the pot: patient, warm, certain of eventually.

Nobody had ever claimed them. For forty years, nobody had even known to look.

"Well," Coral said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "I'm looking."

* * *

Getting up took some doing. Grief, Coral discovered, pools in the knees. But they got up, and they closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin, and they stood for a moment with both hands flat on its cover, thinking.

The prophecy said *the keeper becomes the key*. The patrons said *asset*. Oddrun said — well, Oddrun had said a great many things over the years, mostly *eat something*, but last night she had said *born in Vessengard* with her chin up and her hands steady on the teapot, and Coral was still turning that over like a stone with a fossil in it.

Three memories. Three payments. And every recovered piece, Coral suspected, would make them more legible to the prophecy — more readable, more findable, more *key-shaped*. The sensible move was to leave the memories where they lay. Stay illegible. Stay a clerk. Inventory candle stubs until the world ended politely around them.

Coral thought about their mother's face, which they had never seen, which was sitting in a drawer somewhere wearing someone else's memory of it.

"No," Coral told the ledger. "I don't think I'll be sensible. I've tried it. It doesn't suit me."

There was one problem, and it was a large one, and it had teeth. The brokers' stock was not freely browsable. Memory-brokers were a peculiar guild-within-the-guild — pale, punctual people who spoke of feelings in units and considered sentiment a form of leakage. To trade with them you needed collateral, and Coral's collateral consisted of one rented room, a fountain pen with a chewed cap, and a collection of ledgers that technically belonged to the guild.

Coral patted their pockets anyway, on principle, and found the usual: lint, a pencil stub, and a scrap of paper on which they'd copied, years ago and for no reason they could name, the first page of Oddrun's recipe book. *For homesickness: strong black tea, honey, a pinch of salt — yes, salt, stop making that face — and drink it while the kettle still sighs.*

Salt. Even Oddrun's recipes had been seasoning Coral for this.

Coral looked at the ledger of trades one last time. Then, because they were who they were — an archivist to the marrow, a person who believed that records were promises — they took out the pencil stub, turned to the margin beside the third entry, and wrote, small and precise:

*Claimant located. Intent to redeem. — C.I., this night.*

The ink of the original entry seemed, very slightly, to darken. Coral chose not to have noticed.

Then they blew out the lamp, climbed the stairs out of the vault, and went to find the one person in the guild who might know how to make a broker cry uncle — or at least how to brew something strong enough to survive the negotiation.

Oddrun was not in her kitchen. Oddrun was nowhere on the upper floors. In her place, sitting at the scrubbed table with its back to the door and a mask pushed up on its forehead like a pair of spectacles, was a patron Coral had never seen before — young, or young-seeming, with ink-stained fingers and an expression of enormous, apologetic regret.

"You've been reading the wrong ledger," the patron said kindly. "The one you want is in the deep stacks. Third door past the unsung lullabies." They slid something across the table: a brass token stamped with a scale weighing nothing against nothing. "Consider it a professional courtesy. The broker opens at midnight."

"And why," said Coral, not touching it, "would your side want me getting my memories back?"

The patron smiled, and it was the smile of someone about to say something true, which in Coral's experience was far more frightening than a lie.

"We don't," they said. "We want you *legible*. And you'll want to hurry. The other side just sent theirs."
