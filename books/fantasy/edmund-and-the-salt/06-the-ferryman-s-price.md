## 6. The Ferryman's Price

The memory-broker kept office hours the way a cat keeps a schedule: technically, and only if it suited him.

Coral found him at half past midnight in the sub-basement beneath the guild's counting room, in a chamber that smelled of beeswax and old rain. The room was round, which was unusual for the guildhouse, whose architects had apparently held a grudge against curves. Shelves curved with it instead, following the walls like rings in a tree, and every shelf was full of jars.

Not pickle jars. Coral had made that mistake on their first week as an archivist, aged nine, and had spent an embarrassing afternoon wondering why the guild preserved so many jars of fog.

Each jar held something that had once been someone's. A first snowfall. The smell of a grandmother's kitchen. In one, if you tilted it right, you could see the exact color of a sky over a wedding nobody remembered anymore.

The broker sat behind a desk shaped like an upturned boat, doing nothing in particular with great commitment. He was small and silver-haired and wore a waistcoat with too many pockets, each one bulging with the suggestion of something valuable. His name, officially, was Master Quillon. Everyone called him the Ferryman, because he moved things from one side of a life to the other, and because he charged like one.

"Coral Iversen," he said, without looking up from the blank sheet of paper in front of him. "At this hour. Either the vaults are on fire or you've come to trade."

"The vaults are fine."

"Then you've come to trade." He looked up at last, and his eyes were the pale, patient gray of a man who had watched ten thousand people decide to give away the best parts of themselves. "Sit. You have the look of someone who found out what their mother paid for them."

Coral's stomach dropped through three floors of guildhouse. "You know."

"I keep the ledger of trades, dear. You read it four days ago. You think I didn't notice my own ledger reading itself?" He tapped the boat-desk with one finger. "Three memories, sold before you were born. Passage for two out of Vessengard, plus a bribe for the ferrywoman at the salt gate, who — I'll note for the record — was me. I took the payment myself. Your mother cried the whole time and thanked me anyway. It's not my favorite memory of the job."

"You were *there*? At the gate?"

"Someone has to hold the jar." He said it lightly, but his hands went still on the desk. "So. Which one do you want back first? And more importantly—" his eyes flicked up, sharp now, "—what are you prepared to pay?"

* * *

Here is what Coral had learned about prices in four days of furious, sleepless research:

Memory did not trade for coin. Coin was only paper that agreed to remember a number, and the Ferryman dealt in the real thing. Memory traded for memory, or for objects soaked so thoroughly in a person's feeling that the difference stopped mattering. A lock of hair from someone you loved. Letters you'd reread until they fell apart. A ring worn past its gold.

Or a book.

Coral had Oddrun's recipe book at home on their shelf, wedged between a water-damaged atlas and a novel they'd never finished. It was a fat, grease-spotted thing bound in blue leather gone soft as cloth, and it was worth exactly nothing to anyone else in the world, which meant it was worth everything.

Oddrun had started it the year she came to the guildhouse, before Coral existed even as a rumor in the ledgers. Every recipe in her handwriting: the honey bread she made when someone died. The pepper soup she made when someone lied to her and she wanted them to know she knew. The cardamom buns for no reason at all, which were, everyone agreed, the best ones. Margins full of notes in two hands — hers and, later, a child's careful scrawl: *she says stir it widdershins but I think she just likes saying widdershins.*

Thirty years of shared years. That was the price of a face.

Coral set it on the boat-desk and tried very hard not to snatch it back immediately.

Quillon looked at the book for a long moment. Then he put on a pair of spectacles that hadn't been on his face before and would not be there afterward, and opened it with a gentleness that surprised Coral, turning pages with fingertips rather than thumbs.

"Ah," he said softly. "Cardamom buns."

"For no reason at all," Coral said. Their voice came out wrong, thick at the edges.

"There's always a reason. Sometimes the reason is that the world is terrible and we require buns." He closed the book and rested both hands flat upon it, and when he looked up, all the merchant had gone out of his face and left only the old man. "I'll be honest with you, archivist, because honesty is cheaper than you'd think and I'm feeling generous at this hour. This is enough. More than enough. Your mother paid less for all three memories combined, and she haggled, which I respected."

"Then take it."

"And here is where I disappoint you, because taking it is the easy part and I've grown suspicious of easy things in my old age." He leaned forward. "Do you understand what happens when you take these memories back? Not sentimentally. *Mechanically.*"

"They come back to me."

"They come back to you, yes, like water coming back to a dry channel. But water leaves a mark on the banks, Coral. Right now the prophecy can barely see you. You're a smudge in its margins — a name, a date, a role it hasn't decided how to cast. Every memory your mother sold away was a piece of you the book couldn't read. Sold *before* you were born, understand? Those pieces never got the chance to become anything. They're raw. Unwritten." His finger tapped the ledger between them. "Take the first one back, and you stop being a smudge. Take the second, and the prophecy starts to recognize your handwriting. Take the third—"

"—and it knows me by heart," Coral said quietly. "I know. Oddrun told me. Half the inner circle told me, in the tones people use to describe weather disasters."

"And yet here you sit, offering me thirty years of buns." Quillon took off the impossible spectacles. "I've been the Ferryman for sixty-one years, girl-of-no-particular-category, and I will tell you the one true thing about my profession: nobody ever regrets the trade in the moment. Regret is a later flavor. So I ask you plainly, and I want you to answer like an archivist and not like a grieving child. Is a face worth thirty years?"

Coral thought about it honestly, which was the worst way to think about anything.

They thought about the ledger entry in their mother's hand — *three memories of my child, unbought and unmade, for two passages and a lie told well* — and how her handwriting had shaken only on the word *child*, and firmed again after. They thought about the maps that redrew themselves, and the dusk-colored country behind the gate, and the fact that somewhere in that fading place their mother had died without ever seeing her own baby's face either. Fair trades cut both ways; Coral was an archivist; they believed in symmetry.

And they thought about Oddrun, who had raised them on cardamom buns for no reason at all, and who would say — Coral knew this with the certainty of tides — *"Don't you dare trade my book for me, you stubborn scrap of paper"* and then help them carry it downstairs herself, because love argues with its hands while its heart signs the receipt.

"Yes," Coral said. "It's worth thirty years. Because those years don't disappear. They go into a jar on your shelf, and someday maybe someone else buys them back, and meanwhile I'll remember my mother's face, and I'll make the buns from memory instead. Widdershins and all."

Quillon was quiet for a moment. Then he snorted — an undignified, genuine sound, entirely unlike a Ferryman.

"Oddrun taught you to bargain worse than she taught you to bake," he said. "That speech cost you nothing and bought you my respect, which is the only currency I can't mint myself. Fine. Done. Signed." He drew the book toward him and paused. "One condition, and it's not negotiable, so don't waste your fury on it. When this is over — whatever 'over' means for you and that gate — you come back and buy this book off my shelf. Full price. I don't care if you pay in teeth. Some things belong on shelves, and some things belong in kitchens, and I've been in this business long enough to know which is which."

"It's a deal," Coral said, and meant it harder than they'd ever meant anything.

* * *

He gave them the memory in tea, because of course he did. Everything in this guild came in tea eventually.

The cup was small and chipped at the rim, and the liquid inside was the color of late afternoon. "Drink it slow," Quillon said. "Fast memories bruise."

Coral drank.

For a moment there was nothing — the particular nothing of stepping through a doorway into a dark room — and then light, and warmth, and a voice singing badly in a language Coral shouldn't have known but somehow did, and then—

A face.

A woman leaning over a cradle that wasn't a cradle, really, but a drawer lined with a shawl. Dark hair escaping its braid. Laugh lines already starting, though she couldn't have been older than twenty-five. She had ink on her fingers — of course she did, Coral's own fingers had ink on them right now, this very minute, forty years and one world away — and she was talking to the drawer-baby in that bad-singing language, telling it something urgent and tender.

*You'll forgive me,* she was saying. *Someday, in some life, you'll find the receipts, because I know you, I can tell already, you'll be the kind who reads everything, and you'll find them, and you'll hate me a little, and that's all right. Hate is a kind of keeping. But listen — listen — I am leaving you the part of me that matters least. My face is nothing. My face is a door. What I'm buying with it is you.*

She smiled, and the smile was Coral's smile, the one they saw in polished brass and never trusted, and Coral understood suddenly that they had spent thirty years not trusting it because some deep cellar of them knew it wasn't theirs yet. It had been waiting in a jar.

"Oh," Coral said aloud, in the round room, with tears going down their chin and onto their collar, entirely unarchival. "Oh, she had *freckles*."

"She did," said Quillon gently. "I remember. I held the jar."

Coral laughed, which was the same motion as sobbing wearing a different coat, and wiped their face with their sleeve like a person and not a professional. The memory settled into them like heat settling into a stone — not loud, not dramatic, just *present*. Permanent. Their mother's face lived behind their eyes now, and the strange vertigo of it was that it changed nothing and everything: they were the same person, except that now the prophecy could see them clearly.

And Coral felt it happen. Felt the century-old page turn somewhere in the dark of the vaults above, felt their name sit up straighter in its sentence, felt the words *the keeper becomes the key* shift from prophecy into appointment. Like ink drying. Like a signature finishing itself.

"Second one," Coral said, when they could speak steadily. "My mother's voice — the lullabies. What's the price?"

Quillon didn't answer at once. He had gone very still, the way he'd gone still when he mentioned holding the jar at the gate, and his pale eyes had drifted to the round room's door, where the lamplight was trembling in a way lamplight should not.

Because beneath them — down, down, below the counting rooms and the vaults and the oldest shelves — the sealed gate had begun to sing.

Low. Patient. Getting louder.

"That," said the Ferryman, "is going to complicate our negotiations."
