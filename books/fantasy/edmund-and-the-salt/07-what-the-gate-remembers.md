## 7. What the Gate Remembers

The second memory came back to Coral in the vaults, at three in the morning, with the taste of salt and old paper on their tongue.

They had not meant to open the ledger of trades again. They had told themselves — firmly, in the voice Oddrun used for misbehaving kettles — that they would sleep, that grief could keep until morning like any other filing problem. But the vaults had other ideas. The vaults always did. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its impossible things in the deep stacks below the guildhouse, and the deep stacks had a way of rearranging themselves so that whatever you were trying hardest to avoid ended up at eye level, spine out, waiting.

The ledger lay open where Coral had left it, and the entry glowed faintly, the way entries did when a trade was *active*:

**TRADE THE SECOND. One (1) memory: the child at the gate, age six. Paid by Maren Iversen. Price withheld by broker's discretion. Status: held in escrow.**

Below it, in fresher ink that had not been there an hour ago, someone had written: **Buyer approaching.**

Coral's hands were steady. That was the strange part. Their whole life they had been told they were calm — calm Coral, careful Coral, the archivist who never rattled — and only now, standing in a vault that smelled of brine and imminent catastrophe, did they understand that the calm was not theirs. It was the shape of the hole where the memories used to be. A person missing pieces is very quiet, like a room missing furniture.

"Fine," Coral said aloud, to the ledger, to the dark, to nobody. "Show me."

The memory rose out of the page like steam off tea.

* * *

Salt. That was the first thing. Salt on the air, salt underfoot, a white crust blooming along the stone sill of a door that was not a door — a slab of grey-green stone set into the guildhouse's lowest wall, older than the guildhouse, older than the city, carved all over with letters in no alphabet Coral knew except that some traitor part of them knew every one.

And small hands. Their own hands, six years old, chapped from cold, pressed flat against the stone.

"You mustn't touch it," said a voice above them — young Oddrun, her accent thicker then, vowels rolling like the sea she'd come from. "Coral. Little wren. Hands off."

But Coral hadn't been reaching for the gate. Coral had been *listening* to it. Even at six, even before the trades, they had known the difference between touching and listening. The gate was humming. Not sound exactly — more like the feeling you get when you press your ear to a railway line and the world hums back.

"It's sad," little Coral said, and their own adult heart clenched around the words like a fist around a knife. "It's crying but it can't. Somebody should let it."

Oddrun went very still. Coral remembered that stillness now with terrible clarity — the way her hand had hovered over the child's shoulder without landing, the way her eyes had gone to the shadows at the end of the corridor, where two figures stood wearing masks of pale porcelain, featureless as eggs.

"The asset shows affinity," one of them said. Its voice was like pages turning.

"Six years old," Oddrun said, and her voice was iron wrapped in wool. "It's a *child*, you ghouls."

"The prophecy does not concern itself with age." The second mask tilted. "The keeper becomes the key. Or the key becomes the keeper. Either way, Iversen blood opens Vessengard. We only need to decide which side of the door we'd prefer it standing on."

Little Coral, being six, had understood perhaps one word in five. Adult Coral, remembering, understood all of them, and understood also what Oddrun had done next: she had scooped the child up, turned her back on the patrons, and walked away whistling a tune in a language the guild was not supposed to know existed — a lullaby from a country that wasn't there.

*She knew,* Coral thought, surfacing from the memory with tears freezing on their cheeks in the vault-cold. *She knew from the beginning, and she hid me anyway. She raised me inside the lie because the lie was the only wall between me and them.*

The memory settled into place like a bone reset. It hurt the way setting bones hurt — clean, necessary, awful. And with it came something else, something the ledger had not promised: the gate's own attention, turning toward them through forty years of stone, like a lighthouse beam swinging round.

*Coral,* said the gate, in the voice of salt and slow water. *You came back.*

* * *

Down in the lowest corridor, the gate wept.

It had wept for forty years, thin lines of brine tracing down its face like the tracks of a patient grief, and for forty years no one had listened, because listening was not in anyone's job description. But tonight the guildhouse above was full of footsteps and bad intentions, and the gate felt them all through the foundations the way a sleeper feels a storm through a roof.

It had known the child would return. Gates know things; it is most of what gates are. It had felt Maren Iversen press her palm to the stone and say *take this instead, take these, let my baby be ordinary, let my baby be free* — and the gate had taken the memories the way a drowning sailor takes a rope, without pride, without choice, and had carried them folded in its stoneness ever since, alongside everything else the world had thrown away: a country's borders, a language's last speakers, a thousand small Tuesdays that no one outside remembered had happened.

Now the child stood above it, grown, holding its memories like stolen letters finally returned.

*You are afraid,* the gate observed. It did not say it unkindly. Gates have no tone; they have only truth, which is worse.

*I'm furious,* Coral answered, without deciding to answer. The conversation happened somewhere behind their sternum, in the place where the humming lived.

*Good. Fear closes doors. Anger opens them.*

*That's exactly what worries me.*

Above, the footsteps stopped being footsteps and became a delegation. Coral heard the vault door groan, heard porcelain voices conferring in the dry rustle of their page-turning speech, and heard — cutting through it all, unmistakable after twenty years — the clink of Oddrun's tea trolley being pushed much too fast.

"No," Coral breathed. "No, no, *no*—"

They ran.

* * *

The corridor outside the deep stacks was lit by lamps that had decided, in solidarity with the general emergency, to burn green. Oddrun stood between Coral and four masked patrons, and she had put down the tea trolley, which in twenty years of service Coral had never once seen her do. Her hands were empty. Her stance was not.

"—the archivist is guild property," one patron was saying, in the tone of a man reading a receipt aloud. "Property does not get opinions."

"Funny," Oddrun said. "The ledgers say *I'm* property too. Bought out of Vessengard in '61, price of passage, one lullaby and a recipe book. And yet here I stand, having opinions all over the place." She spat, delicately, at the patron's feet. "Come and file a complaint."

What happened next happened fast, the way catastrophes do — no speeches, no dramatic pauses, just the first patron moving with a speed nothing egg-shaped should possess, and the blow meant for Coral's temple, and Oddrun stepping into its path the way she had stepped into every path Coral had ever walked, twenty years of intercepted bruises distilled into one final one.

The sound was small. That was the worst of it. A sound like a book closing.

Oddrun went down against the tea trolley, and the good teapot — the blue one, the one with the chip shaped like Vessengard if you squinted — hit the floor and broke, and the smell of her morning tea spread through the green-lit corridor like a flag going down.

Something in Coral did not break so much as *finish*.

Later, they would not remember choosing. They would remember the gate's voice saying *now, wren, now*, and their own hand already on the stone, and the carved letters lighting up under their palm letter by letter like a city waking at dusk, and the patrons' porcelain faces turning toward them with the first expression they had ever shown, which was alarm.

"You don't understand what you're doing," one said.

"I understand perfectly," Coral said, and their voice came out doubled, theirs and the gate's, salt and ink together. "I've read the ledger. I've read *everything*. You want a key? Here's your key. You want a keeper?" They laughed, and it was not a kind sound, and they were glad of it. "Get in line."

The stone did not open so much as admit it had been a door all along. Beyond it was not darkness but dusk — a gold-and-violet evening hanging motionless over rooftops Coral had never seen and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a photograph you were never shown. Smells came through: bread, woodsmoke, the sea, home, home, *home*.

"Coral!" Oddrun, on the floor, blood in her hair, gripping Coral's ankle with a strength that had outlasted empires. "Wren — listen — whatever's in there, whatever it asks—" She coughed. "Don't you dare pay full price. Haggle. Promise me you'll *haggle*."

Coral knelt, pressed their forehead to hers, and said the truest thing they knew: "I learned haggling from a master."

Then the patrons surged forward, and the gate pulled its keeper through, and the stone closed — but gently, the way a hand closes around something it means to keep.

On the far side, under a sun that had been setting for forty years, Coral Iversen stood in Vessengard, and the country looked at its last visitor, and began — very softly, like an orchestra tuning — to sing.
