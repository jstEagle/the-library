## 1. The Cartographer's Lie

There are three rules to selling magic in a city that has outlawed it: never use your real name, never charm anyone you like, and never—under any circumstances—let them see you draw.

I had broken the second rule exactly once, seven years ago, and I still owed a woman in the Salt Quarter an apology and a refund. The first two I kept the way other people keep faith: quietly, daily, without expecting to be rewarded for it.

My name, as far as Vespergate was concerned, was Mistress Sabine Quill, purveyor of fine maps, charts, and geographical curiosities. My shop smelled of ink and cedar oil and the particular dustiness of paper that has been aged on purpose, because customers trust old things. Behind the counter, in a locked drawer I could open with a thought, lived everything that would get me hanged: three vials of charm-ink, a needle of white ash wood, and a ledger written in my own private shorthand, which looked, to any inquisitor who might someday kick down my door, like nothing more than a very obsessive record of tide tables.

The tide tables were even accurate. I find it helps to build your lies on something true.

The noblewoman arrived at half past four on a grey Tuesday, which is the hour desperate people choose, having spent the morning telling themselves they won't come and the afternoon losing that argument. She was swathed in enough grey wool to be anonymous and enough pearls underneath it to be identifiable to anyone who mattered. Her gloves were new. Her eyes were not; they had the rubbed-raw look of someone who had been crying in a way she considered undignified.

"You're the mapmaker," she said. Not a question. "They say you sell... other things."

"They say wrong," I said pleasantly, unrolling a chart of the harbor approaches across the counter. "Though if you're interested in the dredging depths off Gullspit Shoal, I can offer you the finest survey south of the mountains."

She stared at me with the particular fury of the wealthy confronted by a shopkeeper's discretion. Then her composure cracked, all at once, like river ice in spring.

"It's my husband," she said. "He's stopped—" Her hand pressed flat against her sternum, as if holding a door shut from the inside. "There's another woman. He looks at me now the way you look at a piece of furniture you're deciding whether to sell."

And there it was. The oldest commission in the world. I have made charms against fever, against nightmares, against the king's tax assessors—one of my better pieces of work, that last, though its mechanism was purely administrative—and none of them ever paid as well or sat as heavy as the love charms.

"Madam," I said, "I draw coastlines."

"I was told—" she began, and then, because desperation is honest even when everything else isn't: "I was told by Lady Maren that if anyone in this city could help me, it was you. She said you'd know what she meant." A pause. "She said to tell you the chart of the northern passes was *worth every coin*."

Lady Maren. My oldest client, my most careful one, a widow with a taste for dream-easing tonics and a habit of paying in advance. And apparently a habit of giving out my name to weeping countesses, which I would be speaking to her about, if speaking to her were still going to be possible after today. I didn't know that yet. At half past four on that grey Tuesday, I knew only that a woman with new gloves was about to make my afternoon considerably more complicated.

"The northern passes," I repeated. "Hm. Well. Charts do sometimes have... marginalia."

Her breath went out of her like a sail luffing.

* * *

Here is how you make a love charm when love charms are punishable by the removal of whatever part of you the inquisitor finds most convenient: you don't make a love charm. You make a *remembering* charm. You cannot force a heart—that way lies ruin, both the moral kind and the practical kind, because forced love curdles in about a season and comes back on the maker like smoke through a cracked flue. What you can do is remind. You take a token that carries the shape of what once was—a ring worn thin, a lock of hair kept past all reason—and you fold into it a small, warm insistence: *look again. Look again. Look again.*

It is, I have always felt, a defensible fraud. I am not selling love. I am selling attention, which is the same thing, but legal in no jurisdiction and therefore equally criminal everywhere, which at least simplifies matters.

I took the countess's ring—it had been her grandmother's, and her husband had put it back on her finger at their wedding, a detail she offered up unprompted and then seemed embarrassed by—and I drew for her while she watched the street through the window, pretending not to watch me. That was rule three, observed: never let them see you draw. Not because the drawing looks like anything. It looks like a woman doing very fine penmanship on a small object, which is precisely why it must never be seen. Inquisitors are not scholars. They cannot read a sigil, but they know handwriting when they see it, and they know that handwriting on a ring means a confession is only a thumbscrew away.

The work itself takes perhaps ten minutes and costs me what it always costs: a little warmth, a little color, the sense of standing slightly further away from my own hands than I ought to. I think of it as paying a toll. Every gift has one. Mine happens to be collected in installments.

"There," I said, sliding the ring back across the counter. "Wear it on your right hand. If he asks why you've changed hands, tell him the truth about the ring and nothing else. Truth is the best mortar for a lie; it sets hard."

"And it will work?" Her fingers closed around the gold like a drowning woman around a spar. "You promise it will work?"

"Madam," I said, "I promise you the dredging depths off Gullspit Shoal are accurate to within a handspan. Everything else in this shop is geography of a more speculative kind."

She laughed—wetly, but she laughed—and paid me triple, and left smelling faintly of pearls and panic. I watched her go through the window and told myself, as I always did, that this was the last one. That I'd bank enough by midsummer to close the shop, move to the coast, and spend the rest of my life drawing honest maps of dishonest coastline until my hands forgot their trick entirely.

I believed it for approximately six hours.

* * *

The notices went up at dawn on Thursday. I know because I was awake at dawn, as I always am, and because by the time I reached my own front door with the milk, there were already four of them pasted along Vespergate, their paste still glistening, their letters tall and red and official.

BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY'S CROWN:

Let it be known throughout the city and its liberties that the practice of sorcery, witchcraft, charming, hexing, scrying, or any Art whatsoever is henceforth forbidden upon pain of forfeiture and fire. Any person possessing knowledge of such practices shall present themselves to the Provost's office within nine days, whereupon clemency may be considered. Any person harboring, concealing, or abetting a practitioner shall share their penalty.

REWARD: Fifty crowns for information leading to the taking of any practitioner.

Fifty crowns. I did the arithmetic before I'd finished my tea, because arithmetic is a comfort. Fifty crowns was four years of my rent. It was a dowry. It was a boat ticket and a new life in a country where the tides run different. It was what nearly everyone I had ever sold anything to would be able to buy with a single sentence spoken to a man in a red sash.

I took the notices down. All four. There were eleven more by evening, and I took those down too, and by Friday they'd posted men to watch the boards, so I stopped, and stood across the street with my milk going warm in my hand, and understood that the city had changed shape overnight in a way no map of mine could render.

Nine days of clemency. Which meant, translated from the language of crowns: nine days in which to inform on your neighbors while informing was still rewarded rather than merely expected.

* * *

I want to say I spent those days burning my ledger and packing. What I actually spent them on was business as usual, because fear makes us stupid in the specific direction of our habits, and mine ran toward the counter, the charts, the drawer with the vials. I told myself I was winding down affairs. I sold a headache charm to a cooper's wife. I drew a survey of a vineyard in the hill country and charged fairly and slept badly.

On the sixth day, Lady Maren came to the shop.

She came at dusk, which was unlike her—she was a morning caller, punctual as church—and she came without her usual escort, which was more unlike her, and when she stepped inside I saw why she hadn't wanted witnesses. Her face was the color of candle wax. One eye was swollen mostly shut, and she held her left arm the way you hold a thing that might still be useful later, if you're careful.

"Sabine," she said. We had long ago dropped the fiction of Mistress Quill between us; she was the one client who knew my name, which should have frightened me more than it did. Love makes liars of us all, and I had liked her for eleven years. "They came to the house this morning. Not soldiers. Men in plain coats, with a list." She swallowed. "My name was on it. Third from the top."

"Maren—"

"I gave them nothing. I want you to know that. Whatever they do to me, I gave them nothing." She smiled, and it was the bravest and worst thing I had ever seen. "But they'll come here, child. They'll work backward through everyone I ever spoke to, and I talked too much, I always talked too much, and there's a countess with a ring on her right hand who will remember my name the moment they show her a red seal—"

"Then don't be here when they come," I said. "There are ways out of the city. I have coin. We can—"

"Hush." She said it gently, the way you'd quiet a child at a funeral. "I'm seventy-one years old and my hip is ruined and I would slow you down by a street and a half." She set something on the counter between us, wrapped in oilcloth, and pushed it toward me with her good hand. "For the tide tables."

"Maren, I can't—"

"You can and you will, because if you argue with me I shall start crying and neither of us has time." Her good hand closed over mine, brief and fierce. "You were the best of what I spent my widowhood on, girl. Don't you dare let them make you smaller than you are."

Then she went out through my door into the street, straight-backed as a mast, and I watched her go, and I was already moving to follow—I swear to you I was already moving—when the red-sashed men came around the corner from the opposite end of Vespergate, two of them and a clerk with a list, and there was nowhere in the geometry of that street for me to be that wasn't beside her.

So I stayed behind my glass and watched them take her. She didn't struggle. She walked between them like a woman being escorted to dinner, chin up, and at the corner she turned—not toward them, toward my window, though she couldn't have seen me through the lamplight and the glass—and inclined her head. Just slightly. The way you nod to a passing acquaintance. The way you nod at a grave.

The clerk checked her off his list. They walked her east, toward the Provost's office, and the crowd on Vespergate parted for them, and nobody met her eyes, and neither did I, and I will carry that particular cowardice to whatever fire is waiting for me.

I stood in my shop full of honest maps and dishonest drawers until the lamp burned low. Then I unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside was a chart—the northern passes, the one she'd praised to the countess, creased soft as cloth from eleven years of handling. And folded into it, a second paper, newer, in her shaking hand: a name. Lord Averil. An address in the Old Palace quarter. And beneath it, five words, underlined twice:

*If they take me, find him.*

Outside, somewhere east, a bell began to ring the hour, and the sound of it went through the city like a crack through ice.
