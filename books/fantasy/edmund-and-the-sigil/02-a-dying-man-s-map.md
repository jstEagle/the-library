## 2. A Dying Man's Map

The knock came at midnight, which told me two things before I opened the door: whoever it was had money, and whoever it was was afraid. In my experience those two qualities traveled together like fleas on a dog.

I nearly didn't answer. The bounty notices had gone up that morning—crude woodcuts of an eye weeping ink, the words *BY ORDER OF THE CROWN* beneath, and a sum for each "practitioner of unlicensed arts" that would buy a modest house in the better quarters. I'd stood in the square reading them with my hands in my pockets like any honest citizen, memorizing every face around me, cataloguing who lingered too long, who looked frightened, who looked hungry. A cartographer's habit: always know where you are and what's watching.

So when the knock came again—three soft raps, then one, then three—I should have blown out my lamp and lain still until morning.

Instead I asked, through the door, "What do you want?"

"Maps," said a voice like dry leaves. "And a charm against pain, if you sell such things."

"I sell surveying instruments."

A pause. Then, faintly amused, faintly terrible: "Child, I have bought love charms from you twice. Do you imagine I've forgotten?"

Lord Averil. My oldest client, though he'd never once needed a charm for himself—he bought them for others, courtiers and servants and once, memorably, a guardsman whose wife was dying badly. He paid triple and never haggled, and he'd never given me his true name until tonight, when he said it plainly through four inches of oak as though naming himself were the payment.

I opened the door because some part of me had already understood that the world I'd built—the careful, gray, invisible world—had ended this morning in the square, and there was no sense pretending otherwise to a dying man.

He was dying. That was the first thing I saw. He leaned on a cane in my doorway with blood soaking through his coat at the side, old blood and new, and his face had the waxy patience of someone who has made an arrangement with his own death and is simply waiting out the paperwork.

"You're hurt," I said, brilliantly.

"I'm seventy-one," he said. "The wound is merely decorative." He looked past me into the room, at the maps pinned to my walls—not the ones I sold, the honest ones of coastlines and trade roads, but the others, the ones I'd never been able to explain even to myself. His eyes went to them the way a man looks at a door he's been searching a house for. "May I come in? I'd rather not bleed on your step. It's very clean. You strike me as someone who values that."

I let him in. God help me, I let him in.

* * *

He sat in my only good chair and refused, with great courtesy, everything I offered except water. When I moved toward his wound with cloth and needle he raised one hand—not imperious, just final—and said, "Later. First I must talk, while I still talk well. Pain makes a man circular."

So I sat on my workbench among my brass instruments and listened to a dead man tell me the state of the kingdom.

"The king is not the king," he began, and I confess my first thought was that the blood loss had gotten to him faster than advertised. But he watched me say it, watched my face do whatever it did, and nodded slowly. "Yes. That's the face everyone makes. Good. Hold onto it. Skepticism will serve you where faith would fail."

"My lord—"

"Averil. You may as well use it; I've precious little left to protect." He took another sip of water, precise as a man rationing his last coins. "Seven years ago the king changed. Not his policies—those changed too, but that's politics, kings drift. *Changed.* Overnight. Men who knew him since boyhood swore he was different. Smaller somehow, though he's the same height. Colder. And the ones who noticed most sharply—" he touched his own side, gently, the way you'd touch a bruise you'd stopped expecting to heal, "—were the first to die. Slowly, quietly, over years. Accidents. Fevers. Duels they didn't start."

"And you survived by noticing nothing."

"I survived by being useless," he agreed. "Old men are beneath suspicion. Who plots treason with a man who falls asleep at council?" He smiled, and it was a real smile, which made it worse. "But I kept accounts, child. I'm a bookkeeper of grievances. And seven years of them add up to this: the man on the throne is not Edmund Vane. The true king has not been seen since the night his father died. And the purge of the gifted—" his eyes flicked to my walls again, to the impossible maps, "—is not piety. It is a hunt. He is looking for something. Someone. Power enough to finish a job begun seven years ago."

My mouth had gone dry. "That's sedition."

"That's arithmetic." He set down the glass. "Now. The part you won't believe."

I laughed—a short, ugly sound. "There's more?"

"There is always more. That's what no one tells you about catastrophe; it arrives in installments." He leaned forward, and the effort cost him something—I saw the color leave his lips and return grudgingly. "You draw maps in your sleep."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a floor does when a ship changes course.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, which was a lie so reflexive it embarrassed us both.

"No," he said kindly. "But I know what I mean. Three years ago I commissioned a surveyor's copy of the palace plans—you'll recall the job; you were paid honestly and asked no questions. What you delivered contained rooms that exist on no plan I have ever seen, and I have seen them all. Corridors beneath the east wing. A stair that descends from a chapel that has no crypt. And at the heart of it all, a chamber marked—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it, "—with a circle of nine points and a line through the center. Drawn in a hand steadier than your waking one."

I remembered. Of course I remembered. Those maps came out of me over three nights I could not account for, ink-stained fingers and aching wrist and pages I had no memory of making, and I'd told myself it was exhaustion, or drink, or the ordinary treachery of a mind under strain. I'd almost destroyed them. Some instinct—call it cowardice, call it self-preservation, call it whatever you like—had made me keep them instead, rolled in oilcloth behind the loose brick by the hearth.

"How do you know what's in my head?" I whispered.

"I don't. I know what's in the palace." He drew a breath that whistled. "Those rooms are real. Sealed, hidden, erased from every record—but real. I found the stair myself, eleven years ago, before the king changed, when I was young enough to be curious and foolish enough to follow it. There is a chamber at the bottom, Sebastian Delacroix. A chamber of bindings. And the sigil on its floor is the one you drew."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past, carrying some innocent cargo to some innocent destination, in a city that was still pretending to be itself.

"Why me?" I said. "Why would I—"

"Because the palace remembers," he said, "and something in you listens. I don't pretend to understand it. Perhaps you're descended from the wardens who built it. Perhaps the gift skips generations like a fever. It doesn't matter. What matters is this: the king is in that chamber. Bound beneath the sigil. Alive—God, I pray alive—and the thing wearing his face means to complete the binding permanently, and when it does, Edmund Vane will be unwritten. Not dead. *Unwritten.* Every record, every memory, every name scratched from the world as though he never drew breath. And no one, child, will ever know there was anything to mourn."

* * *

I want to say I argued. I want to report that I called him mad, threw him out, spent the rest of the night convincing myself it was fever-talk. That would be the sensible version, the version where the cartographer keeps her invisibility and her small gray life and dies old and safe and ignorant.

Here is what actually happened: I sat very still, and I thought about the maps. About how my hand had known things I didn't. About the trance-nights, and the steadiness of that other handwriting, and the way I'd sometimes woken with the taste of stone dust in my mouth, as though I'd been somewhere deep and underground and hadn't washed it away properly.

And I thought about the eye weeping ink on the notice boards, and the noblewoman dragged from her sedan chair that morning while the crowd learned, collectively and forever, the exact price of looking away.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"To go where your maps go," he said simply. "Into the sealed heart. To find him. To unbind him, if unbinding can be done—which I believe it can, because the binding was made by men, and everything made by men can be undone by them, given time and spite." He smiled again. "You'll need help. There are others—careful people, patient people, people who remember the true king. I'll give you names before I—"

He stopped. Not dramatically. He just stopped, mid-sentence, the way a clock stops, and I was moving before I understood why, catching the glass before it hit the floor, getting my shoulder under his arm as he sagged sideways out of the chair.

The wound, when I finally got the coat open, was worse than decorative. Something had gone through him below the ribs and he had walked—walked—across half the city to reach me, holding himself together with posture and spite. I stitched. He surfaced twice, both times trying to speak, both times managing only fragments: "*Names... in the lining...*" and "*Don't trust the one who...*" and then, the third time, nothing at all.

I sat back on my heels with bloody hands and looked at the oldest client I had ever had, dead in my only good chair, having paid me, in the end, with the truth.

* * *

Dawn found me doing the least magical thing I know: burning evidence. His coat went into the stove piece by piece, except the lining, which I cut out whole and spread on my worktable under the lamp. Sewn into it, flat and patient, was a packet of papers—names, dates, sums, a network of loyalists rendered in a bookkeeper's beautiful hand. And at the bottom, a single sheet in a different ink, older:

*A map fragment. The stair beneath the chapel. Nine points, a line through the center.*

My own handwriting. Steadier than my waking hand.

I stood there a long time, listening to the city wake up outside, to the criers already calling the news, to the sound of a kingdom lying to itself. Behind the loose brick, the oilcloth roll waited with the rest of the impossible maps. Somewhere beneath the palace, if a dead man could be believed, a living king lay bound beneath a sigil drawn by my own sleeping hand.

And somewhere in the city, whoever had put the hole in Lord Averil was asking questions, and the next name in the lining might be mine.

I rolled up the fragment, tucked it beside the others, and got out my ink.

If I was going to commit treason, I intended to do it accurately.
