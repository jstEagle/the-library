## 3. The Face in the Mirror Room

The forged surveyor's writ cost me eleven silver and a week of nerve, and I regretted every coin of it the moment the palace gates swallowed me whole.

"Delacroix," said the gate sergeant, squinting at my papers. "You're the one measuring the east wing?"

"The subsidence," I said, with the weary authority of a woman who has spent her life being believed about things she'd rather not discuss. "The foundations are settling. His Majesty's floors will crack if no one charts it."

That was a lie built on a truth, which is the only kind of lie that holds weight. The palace *was* settling. Lord Averil had told me so with his last lucid breath—told me that the whole edifice was sinking into the thing buried beneath it, the way a coffin settles into earth. And my maps, the ones I'd drawn in trances I couldn't remember, had charted rooms under the east wing that appeared on no royal plan: a corridor of mirrors, a stair that went down farther than the foundations should allow, and at the bottom of everything, a chamber marked with a symbol I now knew by heart because I'd traced it in my sleep for three years without knowing what it was.

A sigil. A binding. A king.

I carried those trance-maps rolled inside my legitimate ones, hidden in plain sight the way I hid everything—inches from discovery, protected only by the fact that no one bothers to look closely at a cartographer.

*Invisibility is armor*, I reminded myself as a footman led me through corridors thick enough with gilt to choke on. *Invisibility is survival.* I had repeated some version of this to myself since I was nine years old, when my mother pressed her hand over mine on a candle flame and taught me to feel the heat without flinching, and then made me promise never to let anyone see me do it.

I had kept that promise for twenty years. I intended to keep it through this too, whatever *this* turned out to be.

* * *

They gave me a room in the east wing—a narrow chamber with a slanted floor, which vindicated my cover story beautifully—and three days in which to measure, sketch, and be ignored. Being ignored is an art form, and I am its finest living practitioner. I measured baseboards. I plumb-dropped walls. I filled pages with numbers that were mostly true, and beneath them, in a shorthand of my own devising, I recorded the things that were not on any surveyor's brief.

The servants avoided the east wing after dark. That was my first note. My second was that the guards changed twice as often near the sealed door at the corridor's end—the one my trance-map showed leading down. My third was stranger: the mirrors.

There were mirrors everywhere in the east wing. Tall ones, gilded, angled to catch the light from the high windows, and every single one of them draped in gray cloth except in the corridor where I worked. I asked a maid why, and she looked at me the way people look at a woman who has asked an impolite question.

"They're covered for the cleaning," she said, and left quickly, and did not meet my eyes again for the rest of the day.

For the cleaning. All forty of them. At once. Permanently.

I have sold love charms to enough guilty noblemen to know what a lie of convenience sounds like. It sounds exactly like that.

* * *

On the fourth day, the summons came—not for me, but for everyone. The king would process through the east wing at midday, blessing the restoration works, and all workers would stand aside with their heads bowed.

I stood aside. I bowed my head. I am very good at both.

But I had also done something I hadn't planned, something that still makes my stomach clench when I think of it: I had positioned myself beside one of the uncovered mirrors. Not by accident. Averil's dying words had been a map and a warning, and the map had a room on it labeled in his shaking hand: *where he keeps his face.*

I thought he'd meant a mask. Kings keep masks; it's practically the job description.

The procession came down the corridor like weather—guards first, then heralds, then the king himself, and the whole corridor sank to its knees around me, and I sank too, head bowed, eyes down, the picture of a humble tradeswoman.

Except that a surveyor learns to measure things by eye, and I measured him.

He was beautiful. That was the first wrongness. No man is actually beautiful; men are handsome, or striking, or weathered into interest, but beauty—that flawless, symmetrical, painted-icon beauty—belongs to statues and to lies. His face was the face from the coins, the face from the proclamations, rendered in flesh so perfect it looked recently applied. He moved like a man who had studied walking rather than been born knowing how. And when his gaze swept the bowed heads along the corridor, it passed over me the way light passes through glass, and I felt—I swear this—I felt *nothing behind it*. No curiosity. No contempt. Nothing. A king looks at his subjects the way a farmer looks at fields. This man looked at them the way a lamp looks at a wall.

Then he reached the mirror.

And for one moment—one heartbeat, no more—he stopped.

I felt it before I understood it: a pressure in my ears, a taste like copper coins, the fine hairs on my arms lifting. Anyone else would have called it awe. I have spent my life around magic; I know its smell, and this was magic the way a slaughterhouse is meat.

In the mirror, the king's reflection did not bow its head.

Everyone else in that corridor was doubled in the glass—guards, heralds, me, all of us bent and small and honest. But the king's reflection stood straight, and it was *smiling*, and the smile was nothing any human face could hold, because it went too wide and showed too many teeth, and the eyes above it were not eyes at all but two holes burned through the shape of a face, glowing a dull furnace-orange, like coals seen through a grate.

And then the reflection leaned close to the glass—from the inside—and pressed one long, jointless finger against the surface, right where my bowed head was reflected, and though no sound crossed over, I heard it anyway, the way you hear your own name in a dream:

*Mine is the seventh year. Mine is the rite. Look for me at the solstice, little thief of lines.*

The king walked on. The procession flowed past me like a river past a stone. By the time I dared lift my head, he was ten paces gone, radiant and flawless and hollow, and the mirror behind me showed nothing but a draped wall of glass reflecting a corridor full of kneeling subjects, ordinary as bread.

My hands were shaking. I noticed distantly that I had gripped my measuring chain hard enough to bite crescents into my palm, and that I was bleeding a little, and that I could not remember deciding to stand up.

* * *

I got back to my workroom on legs that belonged to someone else and sat down among my instruments and did not move for a long time.

Let me tell you what knowing does to you. Knowing is not a clean thing. It doesn't arrive like a revelation, all trumpets and clarity. It arrives like water finding a crack—it seeps, it spreads, it gets into everything you thought was solid, and then the whole structure groans and shifts and you realize the ground was never ground at all.

I had spent four days telling myself a manageable story. That Averil had been mad with pain. That the "prisoner king" was a metaphor, a loyalist's fever dream, a coded way of saying the true Edmund was dead and this creature wore his memory. Dead kings can't be saved. Dead kings ask nothing of you.

But dead kings don't press their fingers against mirrors. Dead kings don't count their years. *Mine is the seventh year*—seven years of being worn like a coat, seven years of watching from somewhere while that thing smiled at his court and signed his name and ordered the purge of everyone like me. Seven years, and it had known I was there. *Little thief of lines.* It had looked straight through my invisibility, straight through twenty years of careful nothing, and named me in front of its own reflection, casually, the way you'd flick a crumb off a table.

It wasn't afraid of me. That was the worst part. I had walked into the heart of the most dangerous building in the kingdom carrying forbidden maps and a forbidden self, and the thing on the throne had glanced at me the way you glance at weather.

I unrolled my trance-maps with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and looked again at the chamber at the bottom of everything, and the sigil drawn across it in lines I had made without knowing I was making them. And beneath the sigil, in Averil's handwriting, added in his final days:

*He is alive. He is awake. God forgive us, he has been awake the whole time.*

I thought of that—of seven years awake in the dark, aware, while a thing wore your face upstairs—and something in my chest cracked along a fault line I didn't know I had.

Here is the vertigo of knowing too much: every safe thing becomes a lie. My forged writ became evidence. My rented room became a trap. The maid who wouldn't meet my eyes might be an informant; the guard who nodded to me might already carry my description. Somewhere below me, a king was bound in a cell woven from his own name, and somewhere above me, the thing wearing him was counting down to a solstice, and I was standing between them with a measuring chain and a bleeding palm, and there was no version of the world anymore in which I walked out of this palace and back to my small careful life and pretended I hadn't seen.

I had spent twenty years learning how not to be seen. In one morning, a reflection had seen me anyway.

I rolled up the maps. I packed my instruments. And I made myself a promise in the silence of that slant-floored room, a promise that felt less like courage than like a door closing behind me:

I would find the way down. Whatever slept at the bottom of my own maps, whoever wore the crown above me—I would chart it to its end.

Because I had seen the face in the mirror room.

And worse—far worse—it had seen me back.
