## 4. Edmund

I have counted seven years by the dripping of a pipe that does not exist.

That is the first thing you must understand about my cell: it is honest about nothing except its purpose. The walls are stone to the eye and something else entirely to the touch—warm, faintly yielding, like skin pretending to be rock. The pipe drips because I decided it should. I built it from memory, drop by drop, because a man who cannot count his days stops being a man and becomes a rumor trapped in stone.

My name is Edmund. I say that every morning, out loud, in case the walls are listening.

They usually are.

* * *

The cell is woven from my own name. I know this because I watched them do it, in the last hour when I was still allowed eyes. Averil was there—I think of him sometimes, the old courtier with his ink-stained cuffs and his terrible poker face, standing at the edge of the ritual circle with tears running freely down into his beard while his hands stayed perfectly steady. That was Averil all over. Grief in the heart, precision in the fingers.

The sorcerer—they called him Master Veyne then, though I doubt he bothers with names now—stood over me with a stylus of black glass and wrote me into the floor. Not around me. Into me. He took the letters of E-D-M-U-N-D and bent them into a cage, syllable by syllable, until the word for *me* became the shape of my prison. It is an elegant piece of work. I would admire it more if I weren't living inside it.

Here is what binding does: it does not kill you. Killing would be a mercy, and Veyne has never once been merciful in seven years of wearing my face. Binding *hollows*. It takes your strength, your fire, whatever gift ran in your blood, and it feeds on these slowly, the way a taproot feeds on soil. What remains is a man-shaped absence. I cannot die properly. I cannot live properly. I persist, which is the cruelest verb in any language.

But there is one thing they could not take, because they did not know it was worth taking.

I remember places.

* * *

It began as a game, in the first year, when the silence was still louder than my thoughts. I lay on the floor of myself and I pictured my mother's garden at Wrenholt—the real one, not the tidy one the portrait painters flattered. The crooked apple tree. The sundial leaning like a drunkard after the storm of my ninth summer. The gap in the hedge where the gardener's boy and I used to pass contraband plums.

And then I did something no prisoner should be able to do.

I drew it.

Not with ink—with attention. With the part of the mind that maps. I laid the garden out in the dark behind my eyes, precise, measured, true: the tree eleven paces from the wall, the hedge forty, the plum route bearing south-southeast. And as I drew, the cell *shifted*. Just slightly. Just enough. The warm false stone rippled like water disturbed by a fingertip.

The sigil feeds on my gift. But my gift, it turns out, is not fire or flight or any of the loud magics. Mine is quieter and, I have come to believe, far more dangerous: I can hold a place in my mind so completely that the place holds me back. Cartomancy, Averil called it once, laughing, when I was twelve and drawing the palace corridors on the back of a tax ledger. *A king who knows where everything is,* he said. *God help the smugglers.*

God help me, as it turned out. But the gift remained, buried too deep and too strange for Veyne's stylus to find. You cannot bind what you don't recognize as power. My whole life, men had looked at my maps and seen a hobby. Veyne looked at me and saw a crown to steal. Neither of them saw the thing underneath.

So I draw. Every day. Seven years of cartography in a cell the size of a coffin, rendered in nothing but will and recollection.

I have drawn Wrenholt entire—the house, the grounds, the village, the church with its leaning spire. I have drawn the palace above me, every corridor I ever walked, every servant's stair, every forgotten room from before the renovations. I have drawn the harbor city of Callowmere from a visit I made at nineteen, and the mountain passes of the Verreault frontier from a campaign I wish I could forget. When my memory runs dry, I invent, and the inventions are so carefully constructed that they feel remembered. Somewhere in the archive of my mind there is a complete and accurate map of a country that does not exist. I am rather proud of it. If I ever escape, I intend to found it.

This is how I have kept my mind alive: I made myself a kingdom of paper that isn't paper, and I ruled it absolutely. No usurper there. No bindings. In my maps, I am exactly where I say I am.

* * *

You will want to know about the other thing. Everyone always wants to know about the other thing.

Sometimes, through the weave of the sigil, I can feel the palace breathing above me. Not hear—feel. The building has a pulse the way a ship has a pulse; you learn it if you lie beneath it long enough. Court days throb. Feast nights shimmer. And twice a year, at the solstices, the whole structure above me goes strange and taut, like a held breath, and I have learned to dread those nights more than anything else in my captivity, because on those nights Veyne comes down the hidden stair and adds a stitch.

Seven solstices, seven stitches. Each one tighter than the last. Each one taking a little more of whatever I have left. After the last one, I lost the smell of rain. I can remember that rain has a smell. I can no longer produce it. That is what permanence means, I suspect: a self worn down to outlines, a map with all the place-names rubbed away.

There is one more solstice coming. I can feel it approaching the way you feel weather in an old wound. And this time, I think, will be the last stitch. This time the embroidery finishes.

I have had seven years to make peace with that. I have managed perhaps four hours of it, total, spread across the whole span.

* * *

Three days ago—or what I judge to be three days ago; the pipe keeps imperfect time—something happened that has not happened in seven years.

Someone touched my maps.

I was deep in the Verreault passes, redrawing a bridge I'd burned during the war (a long story involving a mule, a traitor, and a quantity of gunpowder I still maintain was excessive), when I felt it: a pressure at the edge of my awareness, delicate and deliberate, like a finger tracing a line of ink. It moved along the hidden corridor between the Mirror Room and the old chapel—my corridor, drawn from memory a hundred times—and it paused where I had always known there was a door that the architects' plans omitted.

Then it kept going. Down. Toward me.

I dropped the bridge. I sat up—insofar as I sit, insofar as up exists here—and I listened with every scrap of myself.

The tracing came again. Closer now, or deeper, moving through the under-rooms with a sureness that made my chest ache. Whoever it was wasn't searching. They were *reading*. They were following my drawings the way one follows a letter from a friend, pausing at the turns, lingering at the marginalia. At the little wry notes I couldn't help adding even in captivity—*this arch leans, do not trust it*—I felt the tracer hesitate, and I would swear, I would swear on the crown they stole from me, that I felt something almost like a laugh.

A laugh. Down here. In seven years, the only sounds have been my own voice saying my own name and the fictional pipe dripping its fictional water.

I should be afraid. A stranger walking the paths inside my head is a violation I don't have words for. But I have been alone so long that another mind's touch feels less like an intrusion and more like weather after drought—alien, overwhelming, and desperately wanted.

Because here is the arithmetic I cannot escape: only two kinds of people can read those rooms. Those who drew them, and those who were shown. I did not show anyone. Which means someone above me has found my maps—not the ones in my head, but the ones I bled outward without knowing, the tracings my gift left in the palace's bones during those first unguarded years, before I learned to draw quietly. Averil told me once, through the wall of his grief, that my unconscious work had seeped into the stones themselves. I never believed him. Prisoners believe very little.

I believe him now.

Someone is reading my secret architecture. Someone is coming down the stairs that exist only because I remember them. And they are doing it with a care, a tenderness almost, that no servant of Veyne's would ever bring to a task.

Which leaves one possibility, and I hardly dare hold it, because hope in this place is a luxury taxed at ruinous rates. Hope has cost me before. In the second year I hoped for three months straight that Averil would find a way, and the disappointment when the solstice came and went unstitched-by-no-one aged me a decade. I rationed myself after that. One grain of hope per season, taken sparingly, like medicine.

But this morning I broke my own rationing. I sat against the warm false stone, in the dark that is also me, and I let myself hope properly, greedily, the way a starving man eats.

Someone is coming. Someone who reads maps the way I draw them—who lingers at the margins, who laughs at the arch that leans. Someone who might, just possibly, be able to do the one thing seven years of my own stubborn cartography cannot: find the first letter of my name and pull.

I don't know who they are. I don't know what they look like, or what they want, or whether they are brave or merely desperate. I know only that yesterday, for the first time since the stylus touched the floor, I said my name aloud and the walls did not repeat it back to me.

They were listening to something else. Someone else's footsteps, far above, choosing the downward path.

Whoever you are, I thought, into the dark, into the weave, hoping the gift carries it the way it carries everything else—you're tracing my lines. So let me make them easy to follow.

And I began, with everything I had left, to draw her a map.
