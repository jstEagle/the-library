## 5. What the Gift Costs

The stair went down farther than the palace had any right to be deep.

I had mapped buildings my whole life—drawn their bones from the outside in—and I had never once encountered a structure that was *more* on the inside than its foundations allowed. Yet here I was, four levels below the lowest cellar Averil's notes admitted existed, descending a spiral cut into living rock, and the palace above me still stood at its full height, smug and intact, as though the earth beneath it were merely a rumor.

My candle didn't like it either. The flame leaned sideways, toward the dark below, like a dog straining at a leash.

"Of course," I murmured. "Why would anything down here be sensible."

Averil's map rode against my ribs, folded into eighths inside my coat, and every few steps I felt it—not with my hands but with the gift, that low hum behind my sternum that had been getting louder since I entered the sealed heart. The old courtier had died with half his instructions delivered, blood on his lips and urgency in his grip, and left me the rest as a puzzle of ink: a chamber drawn in a hand that wasn't mine but came out of me anyway, back when my gift used to take me walking while I slept.

*Down past the wine. Down past the dead. Where the name is kept.*

The dead part, at least, was accurate. Niches lined the lower corridors, each holding an urn, each urn labeled with a name I didn't recognize—which was strange in itself, because I recognized everything. Names, faces, dates, the coat of arms of every minor house in Verenne. These names slid off my mind like water off waxed cloth. I'd look directly at one, *Vessarin*, say, or *Corvane*, and a heartbeat later it was gone, leaving only the shape of having known it.

Which meant the sigil's work reached up here too. Which meant Edmund's name had been unwritten so thoroughly that even the memory of other names had been sanded smooth around the hole he left.

I hated it. I hate being handled by anything, including architecture.

* * *

The cell door was not a door.

It was a wall of script—vertical columns of characters burned into the stone in silver that hadn't tarnished, floor to ceiling, edge to edge, no hinge, no seam, no handle. The writing moved when I wasn't looking at it. I'd stare at a line and it would hold still, obedient, innocent; glance away and back, and the characters had rearranged themselves like courtiers shifting positions around an entering king.

"There you are," I said softly.

The gift roared. Not the polite flicker I used for love charms and false survey readings—the real thing, the thing I'd spent fifteen years sitting on, waking only in dreams I never wrote down. It pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat and said, with terrible clarity: *this is what you are for.*

"I don't recall agreeing to that," I told it, and set my palm against the script anyway.

The reaction was instant. The silver flared white; heat lanced up my arm; something vast turned its attention toward me through seven years of stone—a pressure, a presence, exhausted beyond all measure and yet *aware*, the way a drowning man is aware of light on the surface. And beneath the sigil's roar, faint as a pulse under a wrist:

A voice. Thin, cracked, disbelieving.

"Is someone—no." A pause. "No one comes here. This is a trick. This is the oldest trick there is."

"It's not a trick," I said. "It's a cartographer. Which is arguably worse."

Silence. Then, hoarse and careful, the way a man speaks when hope is a weight he cannot afford to lift: "Say something only a stranger would say."

"The usurper snores," I said. "I've heard him through two walls and a mirror. Also his crown sits crooked and none of the servants will tell him, because telling him means looking at him, and nobody wants to look at him longer than necessary. Even they don't know why."

A sound came through the stone that might have been laughter, if laughter could rust. "That's true," the voice said, wondering. "That's all true. Seven years and the first thing anyone says to me is an insult about my brother's posture."

"Not your brother. Your shape."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Not my brother."

I worked then. I want to be honest about this: I worked like a woman possessed, because I was, in the technical sense—possessed by my own gift, which poured out of me the moment I permitted it, mapping the sigil's architecture faster than I could have drawn it, every binding-line and anchor-point blooming behind my eyes in silver and black. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen and it was built to do the cruelest thing I could imagine: to keep a man alive forever inside a name that had been taken away from him.

And I found the flaw. Of course I found the flaw—I always find the flaw; it's why clients pay. The sigil had one unbound point, a keystone where the whole lattice balanced, and it could be broken. By one thing only.

Power. All of it. Everything I had, given at once, freely, without holding anything back—the way you'd spend not a coin but the mint itself.

Everything meant the charms. Meant the trance-maps. Meant whatever small wild talent lived in me underneath the cartography, the thing I'd never once dared look at straight because I suspected, correctly as it turned out, that once I saw all of it I'd have to admit how much of myself I'd been rationing for fifteen frightened years.

Give it all, and the door opened. Give it all, and I'd never draw true again. I'd be exactly what I pretended to be: a plain woman with ink-stained fingers and an excellent memory.

I sat down on the cold stone with my back against the burning script and did not decide anything for a long time.

* * *

"You should go."

His voice again, after hours. He must have felt me stop working. Prisoners develop senses like that.

"I'm resting," I said.

"You're calculating." A dry note in the wreck of his voice. "I've done seven years of nothing but thinking. I know the sound of someone doing arithmetic they don't like."

"Lord Averil sent me," I said, to change the subject. "He's dead now. The usurper's people got to him. He gave me a map he shouldn't have had, drawn by a woman who apparently sleepwalks her way into treason."

"Averil." Grief moved through the stone like weather. "He held my wedding sword at my coronation. He swore—" The voice stopped itself. Started smaller. "You said a map. Drawn in a trance?"

"By me. Before I knew why."

"Then it was always going to be you." Something in his tone made the hair on my arms stand up—not prophecy, exactly, but the exhaustion of a man who has thought about hope so long it has worn smooth as river stone. "The sigil feeds on the name. On mine. But bindings need a counterweight, and the old rites always demanded one paid willingly. I used to think it would have to be another king. Someone royal. Someone whose gift matched the theft."

"And instead it's a mapmaker who sells love charms to bored noblewomen."

"Instead," he agreed, "it's someone brave enough to come down four forbidden staircases with a candle."

"That's not bravery, that's poor planning."

"It's the same thing. I've had time to learn."

I pressed my forehead against my knees. The script glowed against my spine, patient, certain, waiting for me to be sensible and leave.

"Listen to me," he said, and now the gentleness in his voice was worse than any command. "You can't break it. I felt you find the keystone—I felt you understand the price. Whatever you're considering: don't. Go back up. Take what you know—the truth of me, the rite, the solstice—and give it to the rebels, to the court, to anyone who can use it better than I can. Save the city. That was always the mission, wasn't it? Averil didn't send you to rescue a man. He sent you to rescue a kingdom."

"Yes," I said.

"Then leave me. Please." His voice frayed. "Seven years, I have wanted exactly one thing, and it was not to walk free. It was to know that what was done to me mattered to someone—that the city I failed is worth more than my suffering. If you die here, or burn yourself hollow, then the usurper wins twice. Leave me the dignity of being a cause instead of a cost."

I looked at the wall of moving silver for a long time.

"Do you know what I did before tonight?" I asked. "For fifteen years, I hid. Perfectly. Beautifully. I made invisibility into an art form and I told myself it was wisdom. Every day some part of me went unused, unlived, because using it was dangerous, and I called the fear prudence until I couldn't tell them apart." I stood up. My knees cracked; the sound echoed strangely off the script. "And then a dying man handed me a map of my own dreams and said, *this is what you were for*, and I discovered that I would rather be ruined than accurate about my own smallness ever again."

"So you'll throw your gift away on a stranger in a hole."

"On a king in a hole," I corrected. "On the truth in a hole. On the single most stubborn act of hope in this entire rotten palace." I put my palm flat against the keystone-point, and let the gift rise—all of it, the rationed years, the hidden rooms of myself, everything—and felt it answer like a struck bell. "Besides. You begged me nicely. Nobody begs nicely anymore. It's disarming."

"Sebastian." He knew my name. Of course he did—he'd been listening to my thoughts through the lattice for hours; the sigil ran both ways, and I'd been too busy to guard myself. "Sebastian, wait. Tell me your name again first. If we're doing this, I want to carry it with me, whichever way it ends."

"Ines," I lied automatically, and then laughed, wet and startled. "No. Sebastian. Sebastian Delacroix. If I'm spending everything I am, I'm at least going to be *somebody* when I do it."

"Sebastian Delacroix," he repeated, solemn as an oath, and I felt the words settle over me like a mantle I hadn't earned. "Thank you. For the arithmetic. For refusing to like the answer."

"Bear in mind," I said, gathering power until my fingertips ached and the candle guttered flat, "that if this kills me, I will haunt you specifically."

"Please do," said the buried king of Verenne, and for the first time in seven years, the voice in the dark sounded young. "It would be company."

I broke the keystone.

Light took the corridor. Somewhere above us, far away, a mirror-room shattered, and a thing wearing a king's face screamed—and in the ringing silence after, the wall of script went gray, then dim, then fell inward like ash, opening onto a cell and the man inside it: gaunt, gray-eyed, swaying upright against the far wall by sheer obstinacy, blinking at the first light he had seen in seven years.

He looked at me. I looked at him. Neither of us managed anything clever.

Then, from somewhere above—many floors up, but unmistakable, rolling down the stairs like thunder wearing silk—came the tolling of the palace bells. Not the hour. Not an alarm.

The summons bell. The court, called to assemble.

The solstice rite had begun early.
