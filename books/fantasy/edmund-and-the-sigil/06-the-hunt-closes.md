## 6. The Hunt Closes

*Sebastian*

The third safehouse burned on a Tuesday.

I know it was a Tuesday because I had an appointment that morning—a merchant's wife who wanted a charm against her husband's wandering eye—and I sat in her parlor grinding dried feverfew while smoke rose over the Tanner's Quarter and pretended, with the practiced ease of a woman who has lied to herself for years, that it had nothing to do with me.

It had everything to do with me.

Maren brought the news at noon, sliding into the chair across from me with the particular slouch of a courier carrying bad tidings. "The house on Cordwainer Lane," she said. "Gone by dawn. And the one behind the chandler's—soldiers were there before the embers cooled."

"Anyone taken?"

"Not from ours." She said *ours* the way she said everything now, carefully, as if words might be counted against us later. "But they took the landlady. Old Bess. They walked her out in her nightdress."

Old Bess, who had never known what I kept in the cellar beneath her floors. Who had charged me three coppers a week for a room that smelled of tallow and cats, and had once, unprompted, told me that whatever it was I did with all those papers, she hoped it was worth doing.

"They're following Averil's ledger," Maren said. "Every name he touched, every hand he shook. The inquisitors have his correspondence—we think from his townhouse, before we got there. They're not reading it like soldiers read. They're reading it like scholars."

Of course they were. The thing wearing the king's face did nothing crudely. It had spent seven years learning patience; it could afford a week of careful arithmetic to find one cartographer.

"I should move," I said.

"You should move *tonight*." Maren leaned forward. "Seb. Listen to me. There's a boat out of Greyharbor on the tide after next. Corvane can get you passage. You've done what Averil asked—you found the cell, you know where the sigil sits. Hand the maps to us and go."

"And you'll do what? Break it?" I kept my voice pleasant, the voice I used for selling love charms, because the tea shop was full and walls in this city had learned to listen. "With what?"

"With enough of us and enough powder and enough faith."

"Faith." I stirred my cold tea. "Averil didn't die so you could blow a hole in the palace foundations and call it liberation. The sigil isn't stone, Maren. It's *language*. You can't blast language. You'd just wake it angry."

"Then teach someone the language."

"There isn't time to teach anyone anything." I hadn't meant to say it that loudly. A woman at the next table glanced over, and I smiled at her—the smile that said *nothing here, madam, only two friends discussing fabric prices*—and lowered my voice to almost nothing. "There isn't time," I repeated. "The solstice is nine days off. Whatever the usurper means to do, he means to do it then. I can feel it in the maps. The lines are pulling tighter, like a knot being drawn closed."

Maren sat back. She looked at me for a long moment with those flat grey eyes that missed very little, and I watched her arrive at the conclusion she'd been carrying into the tea shop all along.

"It's him, isn't it," she said quietly. "Not the mission. Him."

"That's absurd."

"You went back to the palace twice since you found the cell. Twice. Corvane thinks you're scouting. I think you're visiting."

I am a good liar. I have made my living at it—selling charms that work because belief does half the labor, drawing maps of places I've never seen because some buried part of me has. But lying to Maren felt like lying to a mirror, and mirrors have never been kind to me.

"He begged me to leave him," I said. "The first time. Did you know that? He's been down there seven years, Maren, in the dark, holding his own name together like a man holding a rope over a drop, and when he finally gets a visitor—when he finally gets *me*—he spends half our time together telling me to go save the city and forget him."

"And the other half?"

"The other half he tells me about rivers." My throat tightened without permission. "Rivers he used to fish with his mother. Hills he rode across when he was fifteen and thought the world was large and kind. He draws them in his mind because they took his hands but not his memory, and he recites them to me like—" I stopped. Like prayers. Like letters. Like the only gifts a prisoner has left to give.

Maren was silent. Then: "Corvane will call this what he always calls it. A liability."

"Corvane calls sunrise a liability."

"Corvane is right more often than sunrise." She stood, dropping coins on the table. "Nine days, Seb. Whatever you choose—choose it fast, and don't let the choosing be done *for* you."

She left. I sat with my cold tea and thought about Old Bess in her nightdress, and about a man underground describing a river he would never see again, and about the difference between loyalty and truth, which I had always assumed was the difference between a map and the territory itself—one drawn, one real—and which I was beginning to suspect were the same thing, seen from different heights.

* * *

*Edmund*

She came on the fourth night, smelling of woodsmoke, and I knew before she spoke.

"The chandler's house," I said. "That was yours."

"One of mine." She knelt at the edge of the sigil-light, close enough that I could see the soot on her jaw, the new cut above her eyebrow that she had not bothered to charm closed—or could no longer be bothered, or dared not, with scrying eyes everywhere. "You can smell it down here?"

"I can smell the city through the stones when the wind comes off the harbor. Smoke especially. Smoke travels." Seven years underground teaches a man the whole vocabulary of air. Rain. Bread. Tar. Fear—I had learned to smell fear coming up through the palace floors during the purge years, a thin acrid thread like singed hair. Lately the air had carried smoke often, and beneath the smoke something worse: ink. Fresh ink, gallons of it, the smell of records being rewritten somewhere above me.

"Tell me about the fires," I said.

She told me. Three safehouses, two couriers fled, one old woman taken whose only crime had been renting a room. She told it plainly, without performance, which is how I knew how much it cost her. Sebastian performs everything—even grief arrives dressed and combed—but plainness, with her, is the naked thing.

"They're using Averil's letters," she finished. "His own hand, turned against everyone he tried to protect. He'd have hated that. He was vain about his correspondence."

"He was vain about everything," I said. "He once told my father that history would remember him as the last honest man at court, and my father said history would remember him as the first honest man to be wrong about everything." The laugh caught in my chest, rusted. "He was right about me, though. That should count for something."

"Edmund." She said my name carefully, the way she handled the sigil-lines—as if too much pressure might set something off. "My people want me to leave. Corvane wants the maps and my silence. Maren wants me on a boat. And they're not wrong. If the usurper takes me, he takes the only person alive who can read your cage, and then it isn't seven years, Edmund. It's forever. It's the solstice and every solstice after, until your name is scrubbed out of the world so thoroughly that even you won't remember it."

"Then go."

"No."

"Sebastian—"

"*No.*" The word cracked against the cell walls. Her hands were fists in her lap. "Don't you dare do that. Don't you sit there in your beautiful ruin and give me permission, like it costs you anything. You've been giving everyone permission to abandon you for seven years. Your guards, your court, your kingdom—they all took it. I'm not taking it."

I lay still in the dark and let the silence do its work. This was another thing seven years had taught me: that a man who cannot move must learn to fight with stillness, and that sometimes stillness wins.

"You should understand what you're choosing," I said at last. "Not the danger—I think you wear danger like perfume. The arithmetic. If you spend yourself on me and fail, the city loses its rite-breaker and gains nothing. If you leave and live, you can come back after the solstice with an army, or a miracle, or both. Coldly reckoned, I am a poor investment."

"I'm a cartographer," she said. "Do you know what we say about cold reckonings? A map that shows you only the safest road is a lie, because it leaves out the river you'd have loved, the pass you needed, the place where the light falls at evening just so. Accuracy isn't safety, Edmund. Accuracy is *everything*, all of it, weighed honestly—including the things that cost you." She leaned closer, and the sigil-light moved on her face. "You are not a poor investment. You are the territory. Everything else is paper."

I wanted to answer her. What I said instead, because seven years strips a man down to his honest materials, was: "Tell me about a river. Any river. One you've actually seen."

So she did—some brown northern water called the Vell, running shallow over red stones, where she'd camped at nineteen with a survey crew and stolen wine—and I held the image of it beside my mother's river, my boyhood river, and the two ran together in the dark of my cell like tributaries finding a common sea.

Above us, faint as a heartbeat, the city smelled of ink and burning.

* * *

*Sebastian*

Corvane ambushed me at the fifth safehouse—which was also, by then, nearly the last—standing in the doorway of the map room with his arms crossed and his face arranged into the expression of a man preparing to be reasonable, which in Corvane's case meant preparing to be cruel efficiently.

"Three houses gone," he said. "Two cells rolled up. Bess in an inquisitor's cellar. And our entire operation now hangs on one woman who keeps walking back into the palace like it's a tavern she's fond of."

"Hello, Corvane. Lovely to see you too."

"Don't." He stepped inside and shut the door. "I liked Averil. I followed Averil. But Averil gave you a mission, and the mission was the *sigil*—the institution of it, the precedent of it. Prove the king is bound, prove the crown is stolen, and every gifted soul in this country breathes free again. That was the arithmetic. Somewhere between that cellar and now, you changed the sum. Now it's the man."

"And if the man *is* the proof?" I spread the maps on the table between us—the trance-maps, the true ones, the palace rendered in lines I'd drawn without knowing why. "Corvane. Look at this. Look at what I found. A living king under a living seal. You want testimony for the court, for the guilds, for the foreign envoys? Testimony needs a *testifier*. Dead men make poor witnesses. Chained ones make worse."

"Then we take him out with force—"

"With force. Through seven wards and a rite-room full of inquisitors. With powder, against language." I tapped the central chamber, where the sigil curled like a sleeping serpent. "No. It breaks the way it was made—by gift, willingly poured. Mine. And I can't pour it if I'm dead, or fled, or standing on a boat in Greyharbor wondering for the rest of my life whether the tide was faster than my conscience."

Corvane studied me for a long moment. He is not a stupid man; that has always been the trouble with him.

"Maren says you talk to him about rivers," he said.

"Maren talks too much."

"He's a king, Sebastian. Or he was. Kings are weather. They pass over you and they change the landscape and they go. Don't build your house on weather."

"He's not weather." I heard my own voice go quiet and certain, the voice I used for the maps, the true voice. "He's the ground. Everything else is built on whether he stands."

Corvane exhaled through his nose. Outside, somewhere in the city, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not an alarm, but the slow measured tolling the crown used for proclamations. We both went still. Three rings. Pause. Three rings. The summons-tone: all persons to hear the royal word.

We gathered at the window with the others, and below, in the square, criers unrolled their scrolls by lantern light, and the proclamation rang out in the usurper's borrowed voice:

*By order of the Crown, on the eve of the solstice, a general amnesty shall be granted to all persons of gifted blood who present themselves for registration before the appointed day.*

Maren made a sound low in her throat. Old Tam swore. I said nothing, because I was reading the proclamation the way I read maps—for what wasn't drawn.

An amnesty. Kindness, from the thing in the mirror room. Registration, from the creature that ate faces.

It wanted us gathered. All of us, in one place, counted and named—because on the solstice it intended to erase a king from every record and every memory, and it could not leave loose threads like us wandering the tapestry.

Or—it wanted *me*. Registered, catalogued, delivered to its doorstep with my own grateful signature.

"Well," said Corvane softly, beside me. "Now it's a race."

Nine days. Eight, by the time the bell finished tolling.

I went upstairs and began packing ink.
