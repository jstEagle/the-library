## 7. The Rite of the Unworn Crown

The candle told me first.

I'd kept it burning in the corner of my workroom for three days—not for light, but for the wax. I had charmed it, back when charming things was still a thing I did with my whole chest instead of in furtive half-measures, to drip in the shape of whatever threatened me most. It had been a slow, boring puddle for a week. Then, on the morning the bells rang wrong, it began to drip letters.

I didn't read them at first. Denial is a cartographer's best tool; we are trained to leave blank spaces where the dragons are and hope no one asks us to sail there.

But the letters kept coming. *S-O-L.* Then *S-T-I-C-E.*

"Well," I said aloud, to the candle, to the empty room, to the ghost of Lord Averil who had died leaving me a map and a mission and not nearly enough instructions. "That's unsubtle."

* * *

Maren found me an hour later, sitting very still at my drafting table with the candle cradled in both hands like something newborn and doomed.

"You look," she said, "like a woman who's received bad news from stationery."

"The candle's writing again."

Her face went through several expressions in rapid succession—disbelief, recognition, and finally the flat gray acceptance of someone who has been in this rebellion long enough that magic behaving badly no longer surprises her. She crossed the room, read the wax, and swore in three dialects.

"Solstice." She said it like a diagnosis. "That's nine days off."

"Eight, if you're pessimistic about how the astronomers count."

"Sebastian."

"I know."

"Do you? Because last month, when the safehouse on Corver Street burned, you said 'we have time.' When they took Odile, you said 'we have time.' You've said 'we have time' so many times I've started using it as a lullaby. And now your *candle* is telling you—"

"I know what it's telling me." I set the candle down before my hands could do something embarrassing, like shake. "But a dripping wax word isn't a plan, Maren. It isn't even a rumor. I need to know what happens at the solstice, or why it matters, or I'm just another madwoman shouting at candles while the city burns around her."

So we went looking. And this is what I learned, pieced together over two days from sources I will not name here because two of them are already dead:

Every seven years, the palace holds a rite called the Binding of the Unworn Crown. Officially—and by officially I mean in the four dusty lines the court chroniclers are permitted—it's a ceremony of renewal. The king renews his oaths. The crown is symbolically offered back to the kingdom and symbolically refused. Very moving. Very dull. Nobody attends who can avoid it.

Unofficially, according to a former palace archivist who now sells eels and paranoia down by the fish market: the rite doesn't renew a binding. It *completes* one.

"He's been feeding it for seven years," the archivist told me, hunched behind his stall, hands never still. "Every oath the false king swears, every decree signed with Edmund's seal, every coin struck with Edmund's face—the rite drinks all of it. Seven years of a reign, poured into the sigil like wine into a wound. At the solstice, it closes. And when it closes—"

He made a gesture I didn't like. Not a cutting motion. An *erasing* one, like wiping condensation off glass.

"—the name goes. From every record. Every memory. The man beneath the palace won't be a prisoner anymore. He'll be nothing. A room with no door. And no one—" here he leaned close, and his breath smelled of brine and terror, "—no one, mistress, will ever have known there was anything to forget. Not the rebels. Not the loyalists. Not you, if you've seen him. Whatever you carry of him now will simply... not be carried."

I walked out of the fish market very calmly. Then I found the nearest alley and was sick against a wall, which I mention only for completeness, since I've decided this account will be honest even where it's humiliating.

Here is the arithmetic of it, laid out plain, the way I lay out any survey: I had spent weeks learning the shape of a prison built from a man's own name. I had traced its corridors on paper, memorized its turning-points, learned the grammar of the sigils that held it shut. I knew—I *knew*, in the way you know the layout of a house you've drawn a hundred times—that Edmund was alive down there. Weak, mortal, half-erased already, but alive, and aware, and waiting in the dark with nothing but maps of remembered places to keep his mind from dissolving.

And in eight days, unless I did something, everything I knew of him would be wiped clean out of the world. Out of me.

You cannot fight an erasure with a sword. You can't even fight it with grief. There's exactly one weapon against being forgotten, and it's the same weapon the usurper is wielding: binding. Except mine would run the other direction. Mine would have to *unmake*.

Which meant going back into the sealed heart of the palace. Alone, more or less. Before the solstice.

Naturally.

* * *

The rebel cell met that night in the undercroft of a tannery, because nothing says "secret meeting" like a room where the smell alone keeps out casual visitors. Maren presided. I brought the maps.

I spread them across a trestle table scarred with salt stains: the palace surveys, my trance-drawn charts of the sigil-rooms, Averil's dying annotations in their careful antique hand. Around the table sat eleven people, and I watched eleven faces do the same calculation at the same moment, the way a flock of birds all turn on the same wind.

"You want to go back in," said Corvus. He was the cell's strategist, a lean gray man who spoke the way accountants write—in columns, in sums. "After Corver Street. After Odile. After they burned the Rookery with three of ours inside it. You want to walk into the seat of the man who ordered all of it, eight days before the biggest rite of his reign, when security will be at its height."

"When it will be at its height *for the rite itself*," I said. "Which means everyone watching the wrong doors."

"That's not courage, Sebastian. That's arithmetic done backwards."

"It's the only arithmetic there is." I put my finger on the chart, on the small spiral of rooms beneath the throne floor that I had drawn without ever seeing, in a trance whose meaning I was only now beginning to understand. "Everything else is secondary. The cells, the safehouses, the network—if the rite completes, none of it matters, because none of us will remember why it mattered. He won't just kill the true king. He'll delete him. Retroactively. Permanently. And the crown becomes his forever, legally, cosmically, with no Edmund left anywhere to contest it."

Silence. Somewhere above us, the tannery vats groaned through their nightly rotation.

Corvus looked at the maps a long time. Then he looked at me, and his voice softened in the particular way men soften when they think they're being kind.

"There's another sum we should do honestly," he said. "Averil recruited you to unbind the king. Fine. But the mission was never about the man. It was about the *crown*. A living Edmund is one path to legitimacy—a good one, I grant you. But if the cost of reaching him is our entire network, our remaining safehouses, every loyalist contact between here and the coast—then we haven't saved the kingdom, we've traded it for one prisoner. The rebellion's cold ledger says: withdraw. Let the rite pass. Rebuild. Find another way."

"And the other way is?"

"We let the name go. We keep the *truth*—documents, testimony, witnesses outside memory's reach. We fight the usurper in the world that remains."

"The world that remains," I repeated, "won't contain him. Don't you understand? It's not that people will believe a lie. It's that there will be no truth left to believe. Every record rewritten, every mind smoothed over. You'd be fighting a war with the enemy holding the only pen."

Maren spoke up from the head of the table, quiet and terrible. "How many of ours have died since Averil came to you, Sebastian?"

"You know the number."

"Say it anyway."

"Fourteen."

"Fourteen. And if you go in and fail, it's fourteen plus however many they sweep up after you, plus you, plus whatever's left of us gets scattered so fine it never reforms." She rubbed her eyes. "And if you don't go in, there is no 'whatever's left,' because there's nothing left to be. Gods." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what I hate most about this? That you're right. That the ledger agrees with you *and* damns us either way."

"Then I go alone," I said. "No cell resources. No names exposed. If I'm taken, I'm a lone surveyor with forged papers and no connections worth torturing. You keep the network. You keep the truth. I keep the appointment."

"That's not—" Corvus started.

"It's the best offer anyone's going to get," Maren said, and something in her voice closed like a door. "Meeting adjourned. Sebastian, stay."

They filed out. The tannery smell rushed in to fill the space they left. Maren waited until the last bootstep faded on the stair, then turned to me with an expression I'd never seen her wear—not commander, not friend, but something older and rawer.

"Averil was my cousin," she said.

I hadn't known that. In fourteen months of rebellion I had apparently not known the single fact that explained almost everything about her.

"He wrote to me the week before he died. Said he'd found the person who could finish it. Said she drew maps in her sleep that charted rooms no surveyor had ever entered, and that either she was the most gifted cartographer in a generation or the palace itself was dreaming through her." Maren's jaw tightened. "I thought he was being poetic. He was always being poetic. But he wasn't, was he. Something chose you for this, and I don't pretend to know what, and I've stopped asking. So here's my answer to your noble little speech about going alone: absolutely not. You go in with a way out. You go in with me on the wall counting minutes. And you go in knowing—" her voice cracked, just once, and she hated it, and I pretended not to notice, "—knowing that if the solstice comes and you're inside, I am coming in after you, ledger be damned. That's the price of my blessing. Take it or take it."

For a moment I couldn't speak. It's strange, the things that break you—not the candle writing doom in wax, not the archivist's erasing hands, but a hard woman in a tannery offering to die for you in the same tone she used for rationing flour.

"Taken," I managed. "Both terms."

* * *

I went home through streets gone quiet with curfew, past bounty notices layered three deep on the posting boards—my own description among them now, third-hand and slightly flattering, which I choose to find funny rather than fatal. The moon was a day from full. Eight days from solstice.

In my workroom, the candle had finished its message. The wax had hardened overnight into a single line of characters, and I stood reading it for a long time before I understood that it wasn't a warning anymore.

It was coordinates.

Rooms beneath the throne floor. The spiral I'd drawn in my sleep, rendered in dripping tallow by a charm I'd cast months ago for a threat I couldn't name—because the threat wasn't to me. The charm had known, somehow, what I was only now learning: that somewhere under the palace, a man made of memories was tracing the same rooms on the walls of his cell, hoping against hope that someone, someday, would trace them back.

Eight days.

I pulled a fresh sheet of vellum toward me, weighted its corners, and began to draw the route in—every corridor, every seal, every second accounted for, because from tonight onward seconds were the only currency I had.

Outside, the bells rang the hour, and for the first time since I'd come to this city, they rang true.
