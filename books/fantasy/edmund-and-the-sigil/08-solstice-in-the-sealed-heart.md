## 8. Solstice in the Sealed Heart

*Sebastian*

The solstice dawn came up the color of old pewter, and I climbed into the palace through a chimney.

Not my finest entrance. I'd planned the aqueduct, but the rebels had burned that bridge behind us—literally, to slow the inquisitors—and so here I was, three days without sleep, soot to the elbows, wedged in a flue above the royal kitchens like a very determined ghost. Below me, cooks were already at work on the feast for the Rite of the Unworn Crown. Roast swan. Honeyed figs. A cake built in tiers to look like the palace itself.

They were baking a monument to a lie, and it smelled wonderful. That's the usurper's whole reign in a pastry, if you ask me.

I dropped down onto the flour-dusted flagstones when the kitchen emptied for the morning delivery, crossed to the servants' stair, and pulled Averil's map from inside my coat. The paper had gone soft as cloth from handling. His last gift to me: the palace drawn not as it stood, but as it *remembered itself*, corridors bending toward the sealed heart the way rivers bend toward the sea.

"Third landing," I whispered to myself. "Left where the wall lies about being a wall."

The palace was already dressed for the rite. Banners of white and gold hung down the great stair, and every mirror in the public halls had been draped in black silk—which told me everything. The usurper hated mirrors on his holy days. He'd shed his face once in front of me by accident, in the corridor of silvered glass, and he did not make accidents twice.

I moved with the servants' traffic, head down, carrying a tray I'd stolen because nothing makes you invisible like looking useful. Twice I passed inquisitors in their grey hoods, and both times the charm sewn into my collar—my last one, ever—went cold against my throat, doing its small work of *nothing to see here*. By noon it would be ash. By midnight, if the rebels' arithmetic held, so would I.

Don't think about the arithmetic. Climb the stairs. Turn left.

The third landing was empty. The wall on the left looked exactly like a wall, smug about it, mortar and all. But Averil's map showed a door, and my own hand—before I understood what I was drawing, back in those trance-nights I still didn't fully remember—had inked a sigil right there, a key drawn in a dead man's blood-red ink.

I pressed my palm to the stone and spoke the word Averil had died trying to finish: "*Vael.*"

Open, in the old tongue. Open, in the language the purge tried to burn out of the world.

The wall sighed and became a door.

Beyond it, the corridor was wrong in the way I'd learned the deep palace was wrong: too long, lit by no windows, the air tasting of candle smoke and cold iron. Somewhere far below, something vast had begun to hum—a sound I felt in my teeth before I heard it with my ears.

The rite had begun.

I ran.

* * *

*Edmund*

Seven years is a long time to be a name in a box.

I had counted them by maps. Every day I drew one place I remembered—the harbor at Vell, my mother's garden, the road to Thornhale where Sebastian's face had first appeared to me like weather—and every day the cell took the drawing and ate it, feeding my memory back into the sigil that bound me. I was a lamp burning my own oil. I had made peace with going dark.

Then she came. Then she put her hands on the sigil and refused to leave, and I learned that peace was just another word for giving up politely.

Now the walls were screaming.

I felt the rite begin the way you feel a bone set wrongly: a grinding, permanent sort of pain, everywhere at once. He was pouring himself into the binding—seven years of stolen shape, all of it meant to seal me shut forever, to close the last seam between Edmund-the-man and Edmund-the-name. After tonight there would be no true king sleeping under the palace. There would only be him, wearing me, wearing the crown, wearing the country, forever.

And there was a second feeling underneath the pain, faint as a heartbeat through a floor: someone tracing the sigil-rooms. Someone moving through the palace's remembering corridors, following lines I had drawn in the dark of my own mind.

Her. It had to be her. She was the only one who read my maps like they were letters addressed to her personally.

"Sebastian," I said, to no one, to the cell, to the name woven into the walls. My voice was a ruin; seven years of talking only to myself will do that. "Hold on. I'm coming to you."

It was an absurd sentence. I could barely stand. When she had loosened the outer bindings—three nights ago, an act I still didn't have words for—I had gained perhaps a tenth of myself. A tenth of a king is a man who can walk badly and bleed easily.

But the corridors were mine. That was the joke of it, the cruel beautiful joke: the palace remembered me even when no one else did, and its memory warped around my cell like water around a stone. If I walked the remembering way—if I let the halls dream me toward her instead of mapping them myself—I might cross the distance.

I pressed my hand to the wall and gave the cell what remained of my name.

"*Vael,*" I said. Open.

The door opened, and I fell through it, and began to crawl.

* * *

*Sebastian*

The inquisitors found me on the Long Gallery, which was supposed to be empty, which was the first thing today that had gone according to anyone's plan but mine.

There were six of them, grey-hooded, carrying the long hooked staves they used to pull magic out of people like teeth. Behind them, through the gallery's tall windows, the solstice sun was setting—no, rising? Time had gone strange below; I'd lost hours in the warping halls. Either way, the light was the color of a wound, and the humming from beneath the floors had become a chord, and my bones knew what my mind kept refusing to say: *there isn't much time.*

"Gently," said the lead inquisitor, in the tone of a man soothing a horse he intends to shoot. "Lord Veyran wants you alive. You're part of the rite now, cartographer. Your maps. Your hands."

My hands. Of course. The usurper had finally worked out what Averil died telling me: that the sigil-rooms answered to drawings made in trance, and that mine were the truest in the kingdom. He didn't need to kill me. He needed to *use* me—one more instrument in the orchestra of his permanence.

"I'm flattered," I said, and threw the tray at him.

I am not a fighter. Let's be honest about that; I'm a woman who spent ten years pretending to sell love charms so no one would look too closely at the real work. But I had spent three days in these corridors, and I had learned their one law: *the palace remembers.* I backed down the gallery shouting the names of rooms that hadn't existed for a century—"the Glass Chapel! the Winter Nursery! the Drowned Court!"—and the palace, dreaming its long dream of Edmund, obliged me. Walls unfolded. Doors appeared where portraits had been. The inquisitors chased me through a ballroom that had burned down forty years ago, and by the time they clawed back into the real palace, I had locked the remembering way behind me with a word.

I leaned against the new-old wall, shaking, laughing a little, hating myself for laughing.

And then I heard the crawling.

He came around the corner of the corridor like something the tide left behind—white as paper, thin as a promise, dragging himself along the stones with arms that shook with every pull. Seven years buried under his own name, and this was what was left of the true king of Vareth: a man who had heard me running and come anyway.

"You're supposed to be resting," I said, and my voice cracked straight down the middle.

"You're supposed," Edmund said, "to be *anywhere else*." He smiled. Even ruined, even gray-lipped, the smile went through me like sunlight through water. "You came back. I told you to save the city."

"The city can save itself for five minutes." I knelt and got my shoulder under his arm. He weighed almost nothing. That was the worst part—not the blood, not the trembling, but how light he was, as if the sigil had been eating him from the edges inward all these years and only the stubborn center remained. "Come on, Your Majesty. We have a rite to ruin."

"It's that way," he whispered, pointing down a corridor that, by every law of architecture, led back the way we'd come.

"That's a wall."

"It's a wall that's lying," he said, "about being a door."

God help me, I loved him. Right then, filthy and half-dead and certain, I loved him, and I stopped pretending otherwise, and the world did not end. There would be time later to be terrified about it.

We went through the lying wall together.

* * *

*Edmund*

She carried me. I want that written somewhere true, since so much else about me has been written false: in the last hour of my binding, the cartographer carried the king.

The sigil-room opened before us like a held breath. I had never seen it from outside—from inside, it had been the whole of the world—but I knew it instantly, the way you know your own heartbeat. A round chamber of black stone. The ceiling open to a shaft of solstice sky, one hour of true light per year, and that hour beginning now. And across the floor, vast as a lake frozen mid-wave, the sigil itself: my name, my binding, seven years of stolen power coiled into letters the size of ships.

Sebastian set me down at the rim of it. Her hands were steady. Hers always were, when mine had failed.

"How do I break it?" she asked.

"You don't," I said. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. It doesn't break. It *transfers*. Whatever power unbinds it pours itself into the space I leave behind." I took her wrist, gently. Her pulse was fast as a bird's. "I won't let you do it. I've had seven years to learn what losing your name feels like. I won't watch it happen to you."

"Edmund—"

"No."

"Yes," she said, wretched and radiant, "because I already know how it ends, and I chose it three nights ago, and you don't get to take this from me, do you understand? Everyone keeps deciding what I should sacrifice—the rebels wanted me to leave you, Averil wanted me to finish his map, the usurper wants my *hands*—and I am so tired of being everyone's instrument. This is the one thing I get to give *freely*."

The doors of the chamber boomed open.

He stood in them, wearing my face.

I will say this for the thing: it wore me well. Better than I ever had, maybe—the shoulders squared, the crown sitting easy, the mouth arranged into mild kingly sorrow. For seven years the court had looked at that face and seen a king. In one hour, if we failed, it would wear that face forever, and no record, no memory, no mother's mourning would ever hold the truth of me again.

"Cartographer," it said warmly, stepping into the light. "And the guest of honor. How fitting. Stay, both of you. Witness. It's only polite, at one's own coronation."

Sebastian's hand found mine in the dark at the rim of the sigil. Her grip said everything her voice couldn't: *with me, then. Together. Now.*

Below us, the sigil began to sing.
