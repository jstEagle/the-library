## 9. Unbinding

The sigil-room smelled of cold iron and old prayers.

I knelt on the floor of the sealed heart of the palace, my palms flat against stone that hummed like a struck bell, and I understood—finally, completely, in the way one understands a thing only when it is far too late to be sensible about it—that the sigil was not drawn *on* the floor. It was drawn through the world. Every line I traced with my fingers ran down into the bedrock and up into the rafters and out through the city's bones, seven years deep, woven from a king's own name by hands that had known exactly what they were doing.

And it was beautiful. That was the worst part. Whoever had bound Edmund had been an artist, and some traitorous corner of me—the part that had spent a lifetime loving lines on paper—wanted nothing more than to study it forever.

"Sebastian." Edmund's voice came from the doorway behind me, ragged as torn cloth. He had dragged himself here through corridors that rearranged themselves around his absence, and he looked it: gray-faced, shaking, leaning against the frame as though standing were a negotiation he was losing. "Don't."

"You're supposed to be resting."

"I'm supposed to be *king*," he said, "and instead I'm a rumor in my own palace. Don't do this. Whatever you're about to do—I can see it in your face. You have that look you get when you're about to lie to someone with a straight line."

He knew me too well for a man I'd met properly three days ago. Seven years of tracing his sigil-rooms, I suppose, counts as acquaintance.

"It's just ink," I said.

"Liar."

*Just ink.* My whole life had been just ink. Charms folded into love letters, wards stitched into maps, a gift kept small and quiet and deniable because small gifts survived purges and large ones got you dragged into the street like Lady Vessane, whose face I still saw sometimes when I closed my eyes. I had spent thirty-one years being careful. Careful was a country I knew every road of.

And now I knelt at the center of the largest working I had ever seen, and I could feel what breaking it would cost, the way you feel the depth of a well by dropping a stone. The sigil held Edmund's name in a knot of seven years' silence. To unknot it I would have to pour myself into the lines—not a trickle, not a charm's worth, but all of it. The gift entire. Every map I would ever draw. Every charm. Everything.

There would be nothing left over. There never is, with workings like this. The magic doesn't take a tithe; it takes the harvest.

"I can save the city another way," Edmund said, coming closer, each step a victory over his own body. "The rebellion has testimony. Averil left letters. We could—"

"Averil is dead. Letters burn. Testimony can be bought—you told me yourself the court sold him years ago and never noticed the difference." I looked up at him. His face was the usurper's face, which was the strangest cruelty of it: I loved a man wearing a stolen mask, and in a few minutes the original owner of that mask would come through that door wearing it better. "They won't believe words, Edmund. They'll believe *him*, because he's worn your face so long they've forgotten there was ever anything underneath. Unless we give them something they can't unsee."

"And that something is you?"

"That something is *you*," I said. "Whole. Named. Standing in front of them. The sigil doesn't just hold you—it holds everyone's memory of you. Break it, and every person in this palace remembers seven years of wrongness all at once. That's not testimony. That's an avalanche."

He crouched beside me then, wincing, and took my hand off the sigil-line and held it between both of his. His fingers were cold. Mortal-cold. I had fallen in love with a half-unbound ghost in a cell made of his own name, and even now, even knowing what I was about to lose, I did not regret a single hour of it.

"There are other cartographers," he said quietly.

"Not like me."

"There are other ways to be happy."

"Not with you in a cell under the world, no, there aren't." I laughed, and it came out wet. "You want to know the truth? I've been hiding my whole life. Hiding the gift, hiding the work, hiding behind a name I invented for selling charms to lonely nobles. And the first time I ever felt like myself was in a dungeon, drawing maps with a man who remembered the coast of a country he'd been stolen from. If I walk away now, I go back to being careful forever. I'd rather be finished."

"Sebastian—"

"Edmund." I said his true name, the one the sigil had been strangling, and felt it vibrate in the stone under my knees. "Hold my hand. Whatever happens, don't let go until it's done. That's not magic. That's just instructions."

He held my hand.

I opened the well.

* * *

People think pouring out a gift feels like burning. It doesn't. It feels like *draining*—like winter coming into a house through every window at once, like blood leaving a limb, like a tide going out and taking the seabed with it. I put my free palm against the first line of the sigil and gave it everything, and the sigil drank.

It drank my charms, all the little folded spells of my careful years. It drank the trance-maps I'd drawn without understanding, Lord Averil's dying inheritance, every hidden door I'd charted in this palace. It drank the thing I'd never dared name, the deep bright vein under my skin that turned ink into intention, and it drank the futures too—all the maps I would never make, all the doors I would never find, all the versions of Sebastian Delacroix who got to keep her secret self.

I felt each one go. Not painfully. That was the obscene mercy of it. It went the way sleep goes, the way warmth leaves a bath, gently and totally, and somewhere in the middle of it I heard Edmund saying my name over and over, and I gripped his hand because that was the instruction, and I thought, absurdly, of the coast of a country neither of us had ever seen, the one he used to draw from memory in the dark.

The last thing I poured in was the very first thing I'd ever charmed—a paper bird, age six, that flew for my mother across our kitchen and made her laugh before she grew afraid of me. I let it go. It flew down the sigil-lines like a spark down a fuse.

Then the knot broke.

Sound came back into the world all at once—a crack like the sky clearing its throat, and beneath it, rising from the stones, from the walls, from the memory of every soul in the palace above, a single word spoken in seven years of accumulated voices:

***Edmund.***

His name, restored. I felt it pass through me and out, felt it ripple up through the floors, and I knew—in the way you know a map is accurate—that in the throne room above, in the great hall, in the kitchens and the guard posts, people were suddenly remembering. Remembering a young king who had gone quiet one autumn and never quite come back. Remembering seven years of small wrongnesses they had explained away. Remembering his face, the real one, the one currently slumped against me with tears cutting tracks through the grime.

Because the sigil hadn't just held him. It had held *them*. And now the avalanche was moving.

"Did it work?" he whispered. "Sebastian. Look at me. Did it—"

I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands. Ink-stained, calloused, empty. I tried to reach for the gift the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore, and found smooth nothing, and something in me keened with a grief I would examine later, much later, possibly never.

But Edmund was rising. Rising without the walls helping him. Standing on his own mortal legs, color coming back into his face like dawn coming into a room, and when he turned to me his eyes were wet and furious and alive.

"It worked," I said. "Go. Your court is remembering you, and the thing wearing your face is about to have a very bad afternoon."

* * *

We came up out of the sealed heart together—he half-carrying me, if we're honest, because whatever triumph feels like, apparently it also feels like being wrung out and hung to dry—and we emerged into the long gallery outside the throne room just as the doors burst open ahead of us.

The usurper stood in the doorway of the hall, crowned, robed in Edmund's ceremonial white, and I watched the exact moment he understood. He felt it the way I'd felt the well drain: the rite he'd been building toward the solstice collapsing under him, his permanence evaporating, and worse—worse—every courtier in that hall turning to look at him with seven years of returned memory in their eyes.

"No," he said. Just that. A spirit that had worn a king for seven years, reduced to one syllable.

And then Edmund walked past me, down the length of the gallery, into the hall, and the crowd parted the way water parts, and I heard someone say it—old Lord Cassel, I think, his voice cracking—"Your Majesty?" and then another voice, and another, the word spreading like fire through dry grass.

The usurper turned to face him. Two identical faces, crown and no crown, and the court looking back and forth between them like spectators at a mirror trial.

"You're dead," the usurper said to Edmund. "You've been dead for seven years. I *kept* this kingdom. I wore your wars and your winters and your—"

"You wore my *face*," Edmund said, and his voice carried the way a king's voice carries, which I suppose it always had; the sigil had only borrowed his throat. "That's all you ever had of me. And now they remember, and looking is all it takes, isn't it? That was always the flaw in you. You can only be what they see."

The usurper looked at the court. The court looked back. Hundreds of eyes, remembering, refusing, and I watched the shape of him start to fail at the edges—the white robe going gray, the crown going dull, the beloved face losing its agreement with itself, features sliding like wet paint. He reached out one dissolving hand toward Edmund, or perhaps toward the crown, and what came out of him wasn't a roar but something smaller and more terrible: a whisper, thin as thread.

"It was a good kingdom. While I wore it."

"It was never yours to wear," Edmund said.

And the usurper came apart—not violently, not with flame or thunder, but the way mist comes apart when the sun insists on morning. He thinned. He frayed. He unraveled into the air of the hall he had ruled, and the last of him went out through a high window like smoke deciding to be honest, and the crown dropped from nothing onto the marble floor and rolled, ringing, to Edmund's feet.

The hall was silent. Then old Lord Cassel sank to one knee, and after him another, and another, a rustling wave of kneeling courtiers, and Edmund stood there looking down at the crown as though it were a snake he was being asked to pet.

I leaned against a pillar, drained to the last drop, ordinary as bread, and watched the man I loved become a king against his will.

He picked up the crown. He did not put it on. He turned, found me in the crowd with unerring accuracy—as if I were the only fixed point on any map—and started walking toward me through all those bowed heads, holding the crown in both hands like a problem he intended to make mine.

Which, knowing him, it absolutely was going to be.
