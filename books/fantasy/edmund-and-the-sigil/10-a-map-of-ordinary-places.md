## 10. A Map of Ordinary Places

### Sebastian

The rite was scheduled for the third hour after noon, which meant, in palace time, that it would begin whenever Edmund finished arguing with the Chancellor about grain tariffs and wandered in looking apologetic.

I had learned this about kingship in the months since the unbinding: it is mostly waiting. Waiting for councils to end, waiting for petitions to be read aloud, waiting for a man who never wanted a crown to decide, daily, all over again, to keep wearing one. I had also learned that the throne room smelled of beeswax and old stone, that the usurper's mirrors had all been taken down except one—the one in the corridor where I'd first seen his face come apart—and that Edmund refused to have it removed because, he said, "it's good to remember what almost happened." He said things like that with such quiet stubbornness that even his ministers had stopped arguing. Mostly.

I stood at the edge of the dais steps while the court assembled around me, and I tried not to fidget like a woman who had once been arrested for less.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Six months ago, standing where I stood now would have been death by slow degrees. A cartographer with ink under her nails and a gift folded up small enough to hide between her ribs. Now the bounty notices were being pulped into paper for school primers—Edmund insisted on that detail personally, and I loved him a little more every time I thought about it—and the gifted walked the streets with their hands bare, and I was the woman who had broken the sigil, invited to court like some kind of hero.

Which was its own kind of lie. Heroes don't stand there wondering if they made a mistake.

"Sebastian." Edmund came down the steps to meet me rather than making me climb to him, which he did every time, and which the court had stopped finding scandalous somewhere around midwinter. He looked tired in the way he always looked tired—seven years under the palace had left marks no rite could lift—but his eyes found mine the way they always did, like I was the fixed point on his map. "You're early."

"You're late."

"The Chancellor believes winter wheat is a personal insult directed at him." He offered his arm. "Shall we?"

The rite itself was simple, as court rites went. No blood, no binding—those days were over, and everyone in that room knew whose days those had been. Just an old ceremony called the Returning, from before the purge: a circle drawn in chalk, a name spoken, and the gift given back to the one who'd poured it out.

A circle drawn in chalk. I watched the court mage kneel and begin it, her hand steady, and something in my chest went tight and cold.

"Edmund," I said, quietly, before she could finish. "Stop."

He turned. The whole court pretended very hard not to watch, which is the court's single greatest talent.

"I can't do this," I said.

"It's your choice," he said. "It was always going to be your choice. That's why I'm offering it instead of simply—" he gestured vaguely at me, "—arranging it, the way my predecessor arranged things."

"That's not—" I exhaled. "That's not what I mean. I mean I've been thinking about it for weeks, and I keep coming back to the same place, and I need you to hear it before I make a fool of myself in front of two hundred nobles."

He waited. He was good at waiting. Seven years in a cell woven from his own name will teach a man patience, or break him, and Edmund had chosen patience the way other men choose swords.

"I don't want it back," I said.

The words came out steadier than I expected. Steadier than I felt.

"Not because I'm noble about it. Not because sacrifice is beautiful—it isn't, it's just loss with better lighting. But Edmund, when I gave it away, I thought I was giving up *me*. The real me. The girl who drew maps in trances and charmed love tokens and hid in plain sight. And then I didn't die, and the city didn't fall, and spring came, and I sat down at my drafting table and I drew—" I laughed, a little helplessly. "I drew the harbor. Just the harbor. The actual harbor, with the wrong number of gulls and a fishmonger's stall I measured myself, on foot, twice. And it was the best map I've ever made. Because every line in it was true, and every line in it was *mine*, earned, not conjured."

The court mage had gone still over her half-finished circle. Somewhere near the doors, someone whispered, and was shushed.

"When I had the gift," I went on, "my maps were always a little bit magic. Perfect. Too perfect. Rivers that bent the way they *should* bend. And I told myself that was craft. It wasn't. It was cheating, and I knew it, and I built my whole life on top of that small dishonesty the way you build a house on sand." I took his hand. His fingers were cold; they were always cold; I kept meaning to knit him gloves and kept not doing it, because some part of me treasured the excuse to warm his hands myself. "I'm a cartographer, Edmund. Not a sorceress who draws maps. A cartographer. I'd like to finally find out how good one I can be."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then he smiled—not the careful public smile, the private one, the crooked one, the one I'd crossed a burning city to reach.

"Well," he said, loud enough for the court to hear, "then we'll need the circle for something else." He turned to the mage. "Erase it. And bring me the surveyor's commission."

### Edmund

There is a particular loneliness to being king that no chronicle mentions. Everyone imagines the loneliness of the crown—the weight, the isolation, the poisoned cup. What no one tells you is that the true loneliness comes afterward, in the evenings, when the council has gone and the candles are burned down and you are alone with the knowledge that seven years of your life are missing, and the world moved on without you, and the only person alive who understands what that silence feels like is downstairs in the map room, arguing with an atlas.

I went down to find her, as I did most evenings.

The map room had been my mother's idea originally—a gallery for painted charts of the realm—and Sebastian had annexed it within a fortnight of the coronation, evicting three portrait painters and a man who catalogued spoons. She worked by lamplight with her sleeves rolled up and ink on her knuckles, and when I entered she didn't look up, which I had come to understand was the highest compliment she paid anyone.

"The northern passes," she said, by way of greeting. "Your surveyors are liars. All of them. The eastern ridge road doesn't switch back four times, it switches back six, and there's a shrine at the top that none of them mentioned, probably because they rode past it instead of climbing to it."

"You climbed to it."

"I climbed to it." She finally looked up, smudged to the elbow, hair escaping its pins, entirely unrepentant. "You cannot map a country from a saddle, Edmund. You have to walk it. You have to let it be ordinary at you."

"Ordinary at you," I repeated.

"It's a technical term."

I crossed the room and stood beside her at the table. Spread out under her hands was the pass country, drawn in her new hand—no trance, no charm, just measurement and patience and that fierce, exacting honesty she carried like a blade. It was, I thought not for the first time, the truest thing in the palace.

"I spoke with the Chancellor today," I said. "About the border survey."

She went carefully still. "And?"

"He wants it done quickly. By summer. He says the northern lords are restless and a completed map would settle them." I paused. "I told him it couldn't be rushed. I told him the royal cartographer would take as long as the work required, and that she would measure every mile herself, on foot, and that the crown would wait."

Sebastian looked at me for a long moment. "That's months."

"It's seasons."

"And you'd be—what. Sitting here. Signing things. While I'm gone."

"That was the plan as the Chancellor understood it, yes."

"But?"

"But," I said, "I spent seven years in a cell drawing maps of places I remembered, telling myself that someday I would see them again. And now I'm king, and kings don't get to go anywhere, and I have begun to suspect that a throne is simply a cell with better furniture." I picked up the corner of the chart, feeling the paper, real paper, honest paper. "So I have decided to be a bad king for one season. The regency council can manage. Halvern has wanted the responsibility for years and pretends to dread it. And I thought—" I heard my own voice go quieter, the way it does when the truth gets close, "—that perhaps the northern passes should be mapped by two people. One to measure. One to remember what open sky looks like."

She didn't answer right away. She set down her pen, which I had learned meant she was taking something seriously, and she looked at the map, and then at the window, where the evening was coming down gold over a city that knew her name now and used it kindly.

"You understand," she said at last, "that I'll make you carry the measuring chain."

"I would carry it gladly."

"And sleep on the ground."

"I have slept on worse ground."

"And when the survey's done, you'll go back to the throne without complaining, because you chose it, freely, the way I chose this—" she touched the map, the whole honest ordinary length of it, "—and neither of us gets to resent the other for the choosing."

"Sebastian," I said, "I spent seven years learning the difference between a cage and a door. This is a door."

She smiled then—all the way, no armor in it—and reached across the table and inked one small mark in the corner of the chart, where cartographers sign their work. Two names, side by side, in her precise, unhurried hand.

"There," she said. "Now it's official. The survey of the northern passes, commissioned jointly by the Crown and by me." She capped the ink. "We leave at dawn."

"Dawn," I agreed.

And for the first time since I had woken beneath the ruined sigil, blinking in a world that had forgotten me, the future looked less like a kingdom and more like a road—unmapped, unmappable, ours—stretching north through ordinary places toward the light.
