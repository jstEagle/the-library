## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with cataloguing impossible things, Lior had learned in their three years at the Guildhall, was that the impossible refused to sit still on a shelf.

Take the jar on the third shelf of the west cabinet, the one labeled — in Master Corvane's cramped, angry handwriting — *Bottled Tuesday (one), slightly used.* It had been trying to become Wednesday since spring. Every morning Lior came down to the archive and found it straining against its wax seal like a cat at a door it knows opens outward. Every morning they wrote in the ledger: *Item 4471, condition stable.* And every morning the word *stable* felt less like an observation and more like a prayer.

"Stop that," Lior told the jar, because talking to the inventory was one of the few habits nobody had managed to break them of. "You're a Tuesday. Act like one."

The jar shivered. Somewhere inside it, faintly, rain began.

Lior wrote: *Item 4471, condition stable-ish,* and moved on.

The Guildhall of Impossible Things occupied a wedge-shaped building between a chandler's shop and a house that everyone agreed was haunted but which paid its taxes promptly, so no one pressed the matter. From the street it looked unremarkable — grey stone, green shutters, a sign with a keyhole through which, if you were very lucky or very unlucky, you could see something you'd been trying to forget. Inside, though, it went back farther than the street allowed. The archive alone ran forty feet past the rear wall, into a space the city surveyors had given up measuring after the third apprentice vanished for a week and returned insisting he'd only stepped out for matches.

Lior had not vanished yet. This was, they felt, a point of professional pride.

They worked the morning shift alone, as always — Sefa was out on the Kettlebridge run, and the junior apprentices didn't come up until the bells rang nine, by which time Lior liked to have the day's first dozen entries done so they could enjoy the look on the children's faces when they saw how much work remained. It was a small cruelty. Lior treasured their small cruelties; they were, Corvane said, what kept a bookkeeper honest. A person who enjoyed nothing would enjoy cheating you most of all.

The morning's arrivals sat in the intake tray where the night porter had left them:

One compass that pointed at whatever the holder missed most, currently trembling between two directions, which meant either the porter was homesick for two places at once or lying about something. One egg, warm, unlaid by any bird known to the guild's bestiary, ticking softly like a clock with somewhere to be. One letter, sealed, addressed to *the person I will be tomorrow* — the fourth such letter this month, and Lior had a theory about a widow in Candlemakers' Row who couldn't stop apologizing to her future self.

And one page. Loose, old, vellum gone soft as cloth at the edges, smelling of dust and iron and — this was the strange part, the part Lior kept coming back to — of nothing else. No age-smell, no mold, no memory of hands. As though the page had been scrubbed of its own history.

Lior did not put the page in the intake ledger.

They had taken it from the oldest ledger eleven days ago, and every day since they had told themselves they would return it tomorrow. The oldest ledger — the First Book, the apprentices called it, in the hushed voice people used for graves — lived chained to its lectern in the restricted vault, and Lior had keys to the restricted vault, because someone had to take the annual inventories and Corvane trusted exactly one person's handwriting more than his own. Trust was a strange thing to be proud of. Lior wore it anyway, like a coat that almost fit.

The page had come free with barely a whisper. That was the wrong part. Vellum that old should have cracked. Instead it had lifted out of its binding as if it had been waiting, as if a hundred years of sitting next to its neighbors had been nothing but a long breath held, and now — finally — someone had said *exhale.*

It was folded now, four times, small enough to hide in the lining of a boot. Lior had hidden many things in that lining over the years: a peach stone from home, a copper coin with a hole in it, a lock of hair they could not remember cutting and could not bring themselves to throw away. The page fit there naturally, among the other orphaned things.

"You're going to get us both dismissed," Lior murmured to the boot, then caught themselves and glanced around the empty archive, embarrassed. Talking to boots was worse than talking to jars.

The bells rang nine. Down in the yard, voices — the juniors arriving, and under them, unmistakably, the particular creaking tread of Master Corvane's boots, which meant the master had slept badly or well, and there was no way to know which until he spoke.

* * *

Corvane's moods were weather, and the whole Guildhall read the sky.

A good day smelled of coffee and ink; he would pass through the archive trailing commentary — *"that shelf is sagging, Dravenmoor, gravity is not an impossible thing, we do not stock it"* — and vanish into his office to argue correspondence. A bad day smelled of nothing at all, which was worse; on bad days he moved through the halls so quietly that apprentices flattened themselves against walls like mice hearing a hawk's shadow. And once a season, roughly, came the terrible days, when he stood at the window of his office facing north — toward the border, toward the mountains, toward the Spire, though nobody said that word in the Guildhall except in whispers and once, memorably, by accident, at dinner, after which the entire table had studied their soup as though it contained instructions.

Today was a window day.

Lior found him there when they brought the morning summaries, standing with his back to the door, hands clasped behind him, looking north through glass that needed cleaning. He did not turn around.

"Item counts," he said.

"Two hundred and six entries current. Three items flagged for review. The Tuesday is still trying to be Wednesday."

"The Tuesday can want in one hand and spit in the other." He said it without heat, an old joke worn smooth. Then, still not turning: "The egg."

"Ticking. Steady. Half a beat fast."

Something shifted in his shoulders. "Half a beat fast," he repeated, as though Lior had reported a fever in the family. "How fast is half a beat?"

"Fast enough that I noticed."

Now he turned. He was not a tall man, Corvane, but he had a way of occupying space that made tallness irrelevant — a compactness, a density, like a stone wall pretending to be a person. His eyes went to the summary in Lior's hands, then to Lior's face, and stayed there a moment too long. Lior had learned not to flinch during the extra moment. Flinching invited questions.

"Fast enough that you noticed," Corvane said. "Good. Noticing is the whole trade. Everything else is penmanship." He took the summary, scanned it, frowned at nothing in particular. "Sefa back today?"

"By evening, if the roads are kind."

"They haven't been kind lately." He said it lightly, but the lightness had a hairline crack down the middle. "Roads out east are slow. Couriers complaining of lost time. Actual lost time — hours gone, pockets lighter, one man swears his beard grew." He snorted. "Drink, more likely. But log it. Everything gets logged."

"Everything gets logged," Lior agreed. It was practically the guild's motto. The actual motto, embroidered above the intake desk, was *We Account For All Things*, which Lior had privately always suspected was a pun the founders hadn't intended and later masters had decided to keep.

Corvane hesitated. Lior watched him hesitate, and something cold walked up their spine, because Corvane never hesitated. Hesitation was for people who hadn't already decided.

"The First Book," he said. "You'll be doing the annual inventory soon."

"Soon as Sefa's back to cover intake."

"Mm." He looked north again, out the dirty window. "Some pages stick, in old bindings. Vellum grips. If you find a page gripping — don't force it. Force tears. Come tell me instead, and I'll show you how it's done."

The cold walked further up. "Of course, Master."

He nodded, dismissed them, and Lior was three steps out the door when he added, conversationally, to the window:

"Noticing is the whole trade, Dravenmoor. But some things, once noticed, can't be unnnoticed. Mind what you notice."

Lior walked back down the stairs with their heart going half a beat fast, and spent the rest of the morning being extremely careful about the boot.

* * *

Lunch in the Guildhall kitchen was bread, hard cheese, pickled something, and gossip, served family-style at the long scarred table where the staff ate while the juniors ate in the yard like ducklings. Lior usually ate with the staff, at the end near the window, close enough to hear everything and far enough away to pretend not to.

Today the topic was the roads again, and the dark.

"It's the stars," said Ottle, the cook, who believed the stars explained everything from weather to her sister's marriages. "My cousin's boy drives the Kettlebridge coach, and he says the stars are dimmer out east. Dimmer, I say. Stars! How does a star get dim?"

"How does a Tuesday get to be Wednesday?" Lior said. "Things drift. It's what things do."

"But a star isn't a *thing*—" Ottle began, and then the argument was off and running, and Lior let it run without them, chewing cheese and watching the window.

The thing was — and Lior had never said this aloud, had barely admitted it in the privacy of their own skull — the thing was that Lior knew how stars got dim. Not the mechanics. The feeling. They'd woken twice this month with a word dissolving off their tongue, some word they'd been about to say about the night sky, gone before it landed. And last week they'd dreamed of a tower — black, impossibly tall, wearing the horizon like a crown — and woken with salt on their face and no idea why, and the dream had felt less like a dream than like a *letter finally delivered.*

Everyone has dreams of towers. Lior knew that. Everyone wakes up sad sometimes, for no reason. That was just having a head.

But everyone didn't keep a stolen page in their boot that smelled of iron and nothing.

The afternoon passed in the ordinary miracle of the work. Lior logged the compass (*Item 4488; points at longing; holder advised to want fewer things or carry a map of everywhere*). Lior soothed the egg by wrapping it in a scarf, on the theory that whatever was in it was nervous, and nervous eggs hatched into difficult birds. Lior taught two juniors the difference between logging a wonder and logging a danger (*a wonder makes you lean closer; a danger makes you lean closer slower*) and sent them off with their heads full. And all the while, the boot waited by the door with its secret in the lining, patient as a debt.

At dusk, Sefa came home.

Lior heard the courier before they saw her — the jingle of her satchel buckles, the specific rhythm of her stride that meant *long miles, bad news, feed me.* She came through the intake door sideways, the way she always did, as if entering rooms head-on was for other people, and dropped her satchel on the counter with a thud that rattled the intake tray.

"You look terrible," Lior said.

"You look like a ledger with legs," Sefa said, which was her highest compliment, and hugged them quickly, hard, the way she hugged — briefly and completely. Up close she smelled of road dust and woodsmoke and, faintly, of something burnt. "Kettlebridge is fine. Roads are strange. Don't ask me strange questions till I've eaten my weight in Ottle's stew."

"I have exactly one question and it's about the egg—"

"Eat first. Questions after. Guild rules." She was already moving toward the kitchen, then stopped, and looked back, and her face had changed — the road-weariness still on it but underneath, something else, something careful. "Actually. One thing. Before I eat and ruin my seriousness."

"Sefa—"

"On the east road. Past the second milestone. There's a field where the fireweed's blooming out of season, all along one line, straight as a fence. Like something burned there once and the ground remembered it wrong." She rubbed her arms as though cold. "And the stars over Kettlebridge — Ottle's cousin wasn't wrong, they're dim, I counted, I'm a courier, counting is what I do — they're dimmer than they were in spring. Not gone. Just... spending faster."

"Spending," Lior repeated. "Stars don't spend."

"No," Sefa agreed. "That's the funny part." She smiled her crooked smile, the one that meant she'd decided not to think about something until tomorrow. "Stew. Now. You can watch me eat and tell me what I missed."

She went. Lior stood alone in the intake room as the light failed, listening to the Guildhall settle around them — Ottle's clatter, the juniors' laughter dying out as the yard emptied, the creak of old Corvane pacing his office overhead, floorboard by floorboard, north side to south side, like a man walking the length of a worry.

Belonging here was the easiest thing Lior had ever done. That was the trap of it. Three years ago they'd arrived with nothing but a name and a talent for numbers, and the Guildhall had simply opened a shelf and set them on it, labeled and dusted and *kept.* They belonged the way Item 4471 belonged: catalogued, stable-ish, straining gently at the wax.

Which was why, every year, on the same unremarkable day, Lior packed a bag and hid it behind the loose panel in their room. Not to leave. Just to prove the door still opened. Maren — no. Not Maren. Some other name rose instead, some other teacher, some other argument, and dissolved before it landed, leaving only the habit: *keep one foot out the door, keep the bag packed, keep the exit warm.*

Lior had stopped asking why the habit survived when so much else hadn't. Habits were like that. They were the bones of a life; the flesh fell away and the bones kept walking.

That night, after the lamps were trimmed and the juniors abed, Lior climbed to the restricted vault one last time before the inventory — ostensibly to check the lectern's chain, officially because sleep was being difficult. The First Book lay open under its glass case, vast and patient, its hundred-year-old columns marching off into the dark of the past.

Lior unlocked the case. Lifted the binding where the page had been.

There was a gap, of course. Eleven days of gap, thin as a knife. But tonight, for the first time, Lior saw what they hadn't seen before: on the page *before* the gap, the final entry ended mid-word. Not mid-sentence. Mid-*word*. The ink stopped clean, as if the pen had been lifted — or as if the rest of the word had been taken, the way a receipt is torn in half.

And on the page *after* the gap, the entry began with the same strange phrase Lior had found on the stolen page, in the same cramped, furious hand — but younger, somehow, a Corvane before he was Master, writing:

*Received in trust, from the account of the Spire:*

Lior read it four times. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, a bell that hadn't been rung in years began to toll — slow, arrhythmic, wrong — and in the vault, in the silence between strokes, the First Book's remaining pages stirred all at once, like a house full of sleepers turning over.

The word that had been missing from the end of the older entry surfaced in Lior's mind unbidden, completing itself the way the Tuesday strained toward Wednesday, and it was:

*Heart.*

Upstairs, Corvane's footsteps stopped.
