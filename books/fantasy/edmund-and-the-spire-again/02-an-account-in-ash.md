## 2. An Account in Ash

The stolen page lived inside Lior's boot for eleven days before they worked up the courage to read it properly.

Not that they hadn't looked at it. They had looked at it the way you look at a locked door in your own house — often, and with growing suspicion. But looking was different from reading, and reading, Lior suspected, would be different from understanding, and understanding was the sort of thing that could not be taken back. You could un-steal a page. You could not un-know what it said.

So for eleven days the page rode against Lior's ankle, wrapped in oilcloth, while Lior went about the ordinary business of the Guild of Impossible Things: cataloguing, cross-referencing, and dodging Master Corvane's moods like weather.

* * *

The Guildhall smelled, on most mornings, of ink and beeswax and something faintly burnt that no one had ever successfully located. Lior had a theory it came from the third-floor storeroom where the guild kept its inventory of things that were technically on fire but refused to admit it. The guild's stock-in-trade defied easy description, which was rather the point. There was a crate of left-handed shadows. A jar of thunder, vintage unknown, slightly flat. A mirror that showed you as a child, which sounded charming until you learned it only did this to people who had forgotten something important, and then it mostly just made people cry in the corridor.

Lior catalogued all of it with a steady hand and a ledger-keeper's neatness, because neatness was the one thing about themselves they had never doubted.

"Dravenmoor." Corvane's voice carried down the stairwell the way cold air does. "The jar of thunder is filed under 'Weather, Ambient.' It should be under 'Sound, Captive.' Do I pay you to be wrong?"

"You don't pay me at all," Lior called back, before their sense of self-preservation could catch up with their mouth. "You pay me in room, board, and the privilege of being shouted at."

There was a pause on the stairs. Lior braced.

Then, unexpectedly, a dry huff that might have been laughter in a better mood. "Room, board, and education," Corvane corrected. "Get the filing right, apprentice."

He descended into the counting-room proper — a tall man gone grey at the temples, dressed in the guild's sombre grey-and-silver, with the permanent expression of someone doing arithmetic he didn't like. He paused by Lior's desk, glanced at the open ledger, and said nothing, which from Corvane was either approval or a held breath.

Lior waited until his footsteps faded toward the vaults before exhaling.

That was the shape of life at the Guildhall. Belonging, but with edges. Lior had run away from Maren's cottage three years ago with nothing but a satchel, a talent for sums, and a hole in their memory shaped exactly like the reason they'd left. The guild had taken them in without questions — which, Lior understood now, should itself have been a question. Guilds asked questions. That was most of what a guild *was*. But Corvane had looked at the runaway apprentice, looked at the ledgers, and said, "You'll do," as if he'd been expecting them.

Some nights Lior lay awake wondering if that was comfort or trap. Most mornings there wasn't time to wonder, because the morning delivery arrived.

* * *

The morning delivery, that twelfth day, included a shipment of bottled dawns from a widow in the hill country who could no longer afford to keep them, and a complaint letter regarding a purchased miracle that had failed to perform ("*It was supposed to be a small miracle,*" the customer wrote, "*a modest one, and instead my well now grants wishes, which is far more than I asked and frankly more than I want.*"). Lior logged both, drafted a refund with a deduction for wish-removal services, and was reaching for the sealing wax when Sefa dropped onto the corner of the desk with the particular thump she reserved for announcements.

Sefa was the guild's fastest courier and its least quiet person, a compact young woman with wind-chapped cheeks and a habit of eating apples down to a perfect spiral of core. She held such a core now, like evidence.

"You're pale," she said.

"I'm always pale. Ink is pale. I'm a professional."

"Paler than usual." She leaned in. "Corvane's been in the vault since before dawn. Alone. And Tam the night clerk says he heard him talking to himself down there. Or—" she lowered her voice with relish— "*to something.*"

"To the ledgers, probably. He talks to the ledgers. He thinks no one notices."

"He talks to the *current* ledgers. This was the old ones. The deep shelf." Sefa's eyes went bright with the specific joy she took in other people's secrets. "Nobody touches the deep shelf. Not even him, hardly ever. Last time was the year I started, and he came up looking like he'd seen a ghost that owed him money."

Lior's hand had gone, without any decision on their part, to their boot.

Eleven days ago, the page had been an impulse. The deep shelf stood in the vault's furthest corner, holding the oldest account book the guild possessed — a ledger so old its leather cover had gone the colour of ash, its spine cracked like riverbed clay. Corvane had been called away mid-inventory; Lior had been alone with it for perhaps four minutes. Four minutes was enough to notice that a page had been cut out. Not torn — cut, cleanly, long ago, with a razor or a very patient knife. And enough to notice, tucked into the binding's broken seam where the cutter had missed it, a single folded leaf that did not belong.

Lior had taken it the way you take a hot coal off a sleeping child: quickly, and without thinking through what to do next.

Now Sefa was watching them watch their own boot, and her eyebrows were performing a slow, interrogative climb.

"Ledger business," Lior said.

"That's your boot."

"Boot business. Ledger-adjacent."

"Lior Dravenmoor." Sefa hopped off the desk, apple core vanishing somewhere into her coat. "I have delivered letters to houses that don't exist and come back with receipts. I know contraband posture when I see it." She tapped the desk twice, a courier's knock, and headed for the door. "Whatever it is — read it or burn it. Secrets go stale faster than bread."

* * *

Lior waited until the evening bell, until the counting-room emptied and the lamps were trimmed low and the only sounds were the settling of the building and the scratch of their own pen winding down.

Then they locked the door, sat on the floor with their back against the desk where no window could catch them, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

The page was old. Older than the current ledgers, older than Lior's apprenticeship, older — by the smell of it, that deep dry smell like a library's bones — than anything Lior had touched before. The paper had gone amber-brown at the folds. The ink had faded to the colour of dried blood, except where it hadn't, and those places seemed almost fresh, as if the words had been waiting.

The handwriting was not Corvane's. It was rounder, older-fashioned, written by someone who had learned their letters from a stricter century. At the top, in a careful clerk's hand:

**RECEIPT OF THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS**

And beneath it, itemized in the manner of any bill of sale Lior had ever copied:

*Received into trust, for keeping and safe return upon demand:*

***the Heart, one (1).***

No description. No dimensions. No condition noted, no valuation given. Every other entry Lior had ever read — every jar of thunder, every left-handed shadow, every impossible thing the guild had ever bought, sold, or brokered — came with particulars. That was the whole discipline of it. An impossible thing without particulars was just a lie with good posture.

This entry had none. As though whoever wrote it had known exactly what it was, and had also known that writing it down plainly would be worse than leaving it blank.

Below the item line, further entries crowded, cramped, added over years by hands that shook:

*Interest accruing.*

*Payment deferred by agreement.*

*Deferred again.*

*We are not able. We ask forbearance.*

And at the bottom, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday, a single line that stopped Lior's breath:

*The debt stands. The debtor remembers.*

Lior read it four times. The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, the building ticked and settled, and the third-floor storeroom did whatever it did with its polite little fires.

A heart. One heart, held in trust. Hearts, in Lior's experience of ledgers, belonged to one of two categories: the anatomical (sold by resurrection-men, brokered rarely, always with particulars) and the metaphorical (which appeared in love-tokens and curse-work and were, frankly, a licensing nightmare). Neither category fit this. This receipt read like a bill for a *thing*, a singular thing, something with weight enough to be received and kept and — the words snagged every time Lior reached them — *returned upon demand.*

Returned to whom?

And the last line. *The debtor remembers.* Debts ran two directions; that was the first thing any ledger-keeper learned. Someone owed the guild a heart. But the phrasing turned it over, like a coin flipped in poor light. Who had written *we are not able*? Who was asking *forbearance*? A guild that held a thing in trust did not beg. Unless—

Unless the guild wasn't the creditor.

Lior sat very still on the cold floorboards, the page trembling faintly in their fingers, and felt the shape of the thought arrive whole, the way bad news does: the guild had cut this page out because the guild was afraid of it. And the guild was afraid of it because the guild was not the one owed.

They were still sitting there, doing the arithmetic of that fear, when the lock turned.

* * *

Lior had a plan for being caught. They had rehearsed it, actually, during the eleven days of looking-not-reading: hide the page, stand up, look busy, produce some plausible piece of work. It was a good plan. It failed completely, because when the door opened and lamplight spilled across the floor, Lior's body simply declined to move. The page stayed in their lap like a confession.

Master Corvane stood in the doorway in his nightclothes and dressing gown, grey-haired, unshaved, holding a candle. His eyes went from Lior's face to the page and back, and something moved across his features that Lior had never seen there before — not anger, not even surprise. Recognition. The look of a man finding, at last, the thing he has been stepping around in his own house for years.

Neither of them spoke for a long moment. The candle flame leaned and steadied.

"You cut the page out," Lior said finally. Their voice came out steadier than they deserved. "Or someone did. Long ago. But you knew it was missing."

Corvane closed the door behind him with a soft click. He crossed the room, set the candle on the desk, and lowered himself into his chair with the care of a much older man. Up close, in the honest light, Lior could see how tired he was — not end-of-day tired, but a tiredness with strata in it, decades deep.

"How long have you had it?" he asked.

"Eleven days."

"Eleven days." He closed his eyes briefly. "In my day, apprentices stole wine."

"In your day, apprentices presumably couldn't read the wine."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth and died. He reached out, and Lior — surprising themselves — let him take the page. He held it the way you hold a bird that might be dead or might be playing dead: gently, and ready for either.

"Do you know what it is?" he asked.

"A receipt. For a heart. Held in trust." Lior swallowed. "Except it doesn't read like we're the ones holding it. It reads like we're the ones who owe. Like the guild took something it couldn't pay for, and has been asking the lender for patience for longer than I've been alive. Longer than *you've* been alive, by the ink." They gestured at the bottom line. "And someone wrote that recently. In this book that nobody opens. Which means somebody's still keeping the accounts."

Corvane listened with his head bowed, the way he listened to difficult figures. When Lior finished, he was quiet for so long that the candle burned down a visible notch.

"You catalogue thunder," he said at last. "You file miracles. You have spent three years learning that the world contains things that do not behave." He looked up. "Are you prepared to learn that it contains things that do not *forgive*?"

"That's not an answer, Master."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. It's a door, and I am asking you not to walk through it yet." He folded the page along its old creases, precisely, reverently, and held it out. Lior took it back. Their fingers brushed his, and his hand was cold.

"What happens if I walk through it anyway?"

Corvane rose. At the door he paused, candle in hand, and for a moment he looked less like a master and more like a man standing at the edge of a very old debt of his own.

"Not yet," he said.

"And when?"

He didn't answer that. The door clicked shut behind him, and his footsteps faded down the corridor, slow and even, a man returning to a sleep he clearly did not expect to find.

Lior sat alone in the dark counting-room, the receipt warm in their hands, and read the last line once more by the guttering lamp.

*The debt stands. The debtor remembers.*

Outside, far to the north, past the city walls and the border wards and everything the guild had ever promised to keep safe, the sky was clear and full of stars. Lior would remember that later — how many stars there had been, that night, and how brightly they burned.

Like a balance about to be called in.
