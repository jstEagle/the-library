## 3. The Night Without Stars

Lior Dravenmoor had catalogued forty-one impossible things before noon, and by the time the evening market bell rang they were ready to swear that number was a personal record.

The day's acquisitions included: one jar of thunder, pickled (the label said *mild*, but the lid rattled when you walked past it); a left shoe that always arrived home three days before its owner; six feet of ribbon that changed color according to the honesty of whoever held it — currently a suspicious, muddy brown in the hands of the silk merchant who'd brought it in; and a hen that laid eggs containing yesterday. Nobody at the guild knew what to do with an egg full of yesterday, so Lior shelved it between the bottled echoes and the clock that ran on regret.

"Careful with that," called Tobbin from the receiving desk, nodding at the thunder jar as Lior carried it past. "If it breaks, we'll be finding glass in our ears for a week."

"If it breaks, we'll be finding *thunder* in our ears for a week," Lior corrected. "The glass is just what's holding it."

"That's the sort of distinction that gets apprentices promoted and then immediately regretted." Tobbin grinned around the apple he was eating. He was a broad, cheerful man built like a door, with ink permanently stained up both forearms from decades of ledger work. "Corvane wants the inventory tallied by morning. And Lior —"

"I know. Don't open the third drawer of his desk."

"Don't open the third drawer of his desk," Tobbin agreed solemnly, "and don't let him catch you humming while you shelve. He says it makes the impossible things nervous."

"The things are jars and shoes."

"The shoes hum back. I've heard them."

This was the shape of Lior's life now, two years into it: ink under the fingernails, dust in the lungs, a warm cluttered Guildhall full of people who had all, in one way or another, run away from somewhere else and been taken in without questions. Master Corvane was sharp-tempered and inscrutable and paid fairly and never asked where Lior had come from or why there were whole seasons missing behind their eyes like rooms with the furniture covered in sheets. The guild fed them, taught them, trusted them with the ledgers.

And still, every night, Lior checked the window latch twice. Still, every time footsteps came down the hall behind them, some animal part of their chest whispered *run*.

One foot out the door. That was the arrangement Lior had with themselves. Belong here, sleep here, eat here — but know which way the door swings.

They were doing the final tally by lamplight when Sefa shouldered through the guild's back door with the smell of cold air and fried onions clinging to her, courier's satchel bouncing against her hip.

"You're missing it," she announced. "The whole city's out. There's a man on the fountain selling predictions for a copper, and half of them are even wrong in interesting ways."

"It's market night?"

"It's *festival* night, you cave-dweller. First clear sky in nine days. Everyone with legs is down at the Nightmarket, and everyone without legs has hired someone to carry them." She dropped onto the edge of Lior's desk, dislodging a small avalanche of receipt slips. "Come on. You've been breathing ledger dust since dawn. One hour. I'll buy you something fried and terrible."

Lior looked at the tally, half-finished. Looked at Corvane's closed office door, behind which lamplight burned and a pen scratched in that slow deliberate rhythm he kept even when writing nothing important, like a man who wanted the world to know he was thinking.

"He'll want this done by morning."

"He'll want it done by morning whether you do it tonight or not. That's the trick of Corvane — the work expands to fill whatever hours you give it, so give it fewer hours and spite him." Sefa was already tugging Lior's sleeve. "Come *on*. The stars are out. All of them. When did you last actually look at them?"

Lior couldn't remember. That was the honest answer, though not the one they gave. "There's a whole column of unaccounted ribbon."

"The ribbon will keep. Ribbon is famously patient." She hauled them up out of the chair, and Lior went, because going along with Sefa was easier than resisting her, and because some restless thing under their ribs — the same thing that kept one foot near the door — leaned toward open sky the way a plant leans toward light.

The Nightmarket swallowed them whole.

It spilled down Copperlane in a river of lanterns and smoke and noise: fry-bread stalls hissing, a fiddler playing something too fast to dance to while people danced to it anyway, children running underfoot with sparklers, fortune-tellers shouting over each other about the auspiciousness of the evening. Above it all, above the chimney pots and the bell towers and the crooked rooftops of the oldest quarter, the sky stood open and enormous and *full*.

Lior stopped in the middle of the street and looked up, and forgot how to walk.

Stars. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Spilled across the dark like salt from a torn sack, dense enough in places to blur together, wheeling around the slow bright anchor of the North Star. Somewhere overhead, faint and green-white, ran the shimmer of the border wards' reflection — the great ward-lattice anchored by the Spire far to the north, painted across the heavens like a second, quieter river.

"There it is," Sefa said softly beside them. "That face. Worth the walk, yes?"

"It's —" Lior hunted for the word. Everything they knew about stars came from books, from columns in old ledgers recording their positions for navigation contracts. It had never occurred to them that the reality would feel like *this*: like standing at the bottom of a well and realizing the water was looking back. "Big," they finished, inadequately.

Sefa laughed. "You're a poet, Dravenmoor. Truly, the guild's money is well spent."

They bought the fried terrible thing — some manner of dough enclosing cheese of aggressive personality — and ate it leaning against the fountain, and for a little while Lior let the festival happen to them. A woman tried to sell Sefa a love charm; Sefa bought it ironically and wore it ironically and blushed entirely sincerely when a pie-seller's daughter winked at her. A child asked Lior if the guild really kept a dragon in the cellar. Lior told him truthfully that the cellar held a very large toad named Chancellor who had once eaten a debt note, and the child ran off delighted, and Lior realized they were smiling and hadn't decided to.

The stars wheeled. The lanterns swayed. The fiddler sawed away.

And then, between one breath and the next, the sky went out.

Not clouded over. Not dimmed. *Out* — all of it, everywhere, at once, the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick. One instant the heavens were a riot of salt-spill fire and the ward-river's green shimmer, and the next there was nothing above the city but flat, total, featureless black, a ceiling instead of a sky.

The sound the crowd made was not a scream. It was worse than a scream: a single vast indrawn breath, ten thousand people inhaling at once, the whole street sucked silent.

Then the silence broke into a thousand pieces — crying children, shouted questions, a stall collapsing as its owner backed into it staring upward, the fiddler's bow dragging off the strings with a sound like a wounded cat.

"What," said Sefa, very quietly, "was that."

Lior didn't answer. They were staring up with their heart slamming against their teeth, and some deep-buried part of them — some part with no memories attached, just a cold animal certainty — was whispering *I have seen this before. I have seen this before and I cannot remember when.*

Because it wasn't only the stars. Looking harder, squinting against the festival glare below, Lior could see that the black wasn't empty. It was *absent*. Where the ward-shimmer had run, there was a darkness with texture to it, a bruised velvet quality, like a hole cut in cloth. Like something had been *removed* rather than merely ended.

"Lior." Sefa had grabbed their sleeve. Her face had gone pale under the lantern-light. "Lior, look north."

North, over the rooftops, where the horizon glowed faintly on any clear night with the distant presence of the Spire — the tower every child in the city learned about before they learned their letters, the anchor-stone, the pillar that held the border wards and, if you believed the old songs, the stars themselves —

The glow was flickering.

It pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat losing its rhythm. Then it steadied. Then it died.

For three full seconds the world held its breath.

Then the sound came.

It rolled down out of the north like weather — a vast, crystalline, splintering roar, the sound of breaking glass magnified a millionfold, except it came not through the air but *through everything*, through the ground and the walls and the bones of everyone standing in Copperlane. Lior felt it in their molars. Felt it in the fountain at their back, the stone shivering like a struck bell. Every lantern flame in the market bent sideways at once, all in the same direction, as if the whole city had flinched.

The wards. Lior's mind supplied the word with terrible calm, the ledger-keeper's part of them filing the entry even as the rest of them shook. *The border wards have fallen.*

Above them, in the sudden absolute dark, something that had hung in the sky for longer than the city had existed finished falling.

* * *

Sefa had been delivering a package to a widow in Tanner's Row when the sky went out, and later she would tell the story differently each time, but always starting there: with the widow, Old Marta, who had paid for express delivery of a tin of plum sweets to be given to her granddaughter on the girl's birthday, which was tomorrow, and who had pressed a copper into Sefa's hand and said, "Run home careful, love, the streets are slick."

Sefa liked courier work for exactly those moments. Anyone could carry a box. What Sefa sold — what the Guild of Impossible Things actually traded in, beneath all the pickled thunder and the lying ribbon — was *certainty*. The certainty that a thing would arrive. That a promise made in one pair of hands would be kept in another. In a city like theirs, wedged against the border like a barnacle on a ship's hull, certainty was the rarest commodity going, and Sefa had never once failed to deliver it.

She was cutting through Cooper's Yard on her way back toward the guildhall, satchel light now, whistling, when the stars went out.

She stopped whistling.

She stood in the middle of the yard with her head tipped back and her mouth open, and the black above her was so complete, so *wrong*, that her mind refused it. Kept trying to correct it. Clouds, her brain offered. Eclipse. Smoke. But clouds moved, and eclipses had edges, and smoke smelled, and this was none of those things. This was the sky being *switched off*, and every star with it, all at once, politely, like candles after a funeral.

Around her, dogs began to howl. Not one or two — every dog in the district, a rising chorus of it, and underneath the howling a low confused murmur from the houses, doors opening, voices calling across yards. *"Do you see it? Tell me you see it—" "Get the children inside—" "Is it the Spire? Has something happened to the Spire?"*

Sefa's brother would be scared.

The thought arrived whole and urgent, the way thoughts about Tam always did, bypassing everything else. Tam was fourteen and brave as a lion in stories and scared of the dark in life, and he was right now somewhere in the refugee quarters by the East Gate where the border folk lodged, and the dark had just gone permanent.

She ran.

She took the courier's routes, the ones she knew blind — the alley behind the chandler's, the steps up and over the tannery wall, the gap in the fence that only fit someone who'd wriggled through it since childhood — and the whole city was coming awake around her, shutters banging, bells beginning to clang in no coordinated pattern, watchmen shouting contradictory instructions. Overhead the black sky pressed down like a lid. Twice she nearly went headlong over things she couldn't see, and twice strangers caught her arm in the dark, and nobody said anything, everybody just gripped and released and stumbled on, the whole city become a crowd of people feeling their way.

She was crossing the Weavers' Bridge when the sound came from the north.

Later, trying to describe it, she would say: you know the sound ice makes on a pond in thaw, that long cracking groan? Now imagine the pond is the size of the sky, and the ice is a wall of magic older than anyone's grandmother, and you are standing inside the glass of it.

It went through her like a spear of cold. She grabbed the bridge rail. Below her the canal water shivered into rings, every ring perfectly round, spreading out from nowhere. Far off, east and west along the city walls, she saw the ward-lamps flare — the great beacon-fires on the gate towers that had burned steady her entire life, that her father had helped tend, that were supposed to burn until the end of the world — flare bright, gutter, flare again, and go out.

All of them. At once.

The border was open.

Sefa stood on the bridge in the dark with her heart going like a courier's feet, and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. Beyond the border lay the Grey Marches, and beyond the Marches lay the places the histories got vague about, the places the last war had supposedly ended a hundred years ago, the places the transformed ones lived — the enemy that wasn't an army anymore, that was something older and stranger, that the wards had held back for a century the way a dam holds back a sea.

The dam had just broken. And between the sea and the city, camped in the fields and sheltering in the border villages, were thousands upon thousands of people.

People like Tam.

She ran the rest of the way. Through the East Gate quarter, where the refugee lodgings crowded up against the wall in a warren of borrowed rooms and lean-tos, where she found the lodging-house by the sound of weeping and prayer and one stubborn voice singing a marching song slightly too loudly, the way Tam sang whenever he was frightened, volume inversely proportional to courage.

He was sitting on the stairs with his knees pulled up, and when he saw her he didn't pretend otherwise, just buried his face against her shoulder the way he had when he was six, and she held him and looked out the doorway past him at the starless dark pressing against the windows, and made herself say, in the firm courier's voice she used for promises:

"I'm here. Nothing's getting through me. Now help me find candles, because we're going to need light, and I know exactly who sells it."

* * *

By midnight the Guildhall was lit like a festival of its own, every lamp burning, every available body hauling stock out of the cellars. Word had spread through the city the way word does — fast, garbled, growing — and the first knock had come within minutes of the wards falling: a chandler wanting to buy anything that gave light without oil. Then a harbor-master wanting maps of coastal waters that had shifted. Then a woman from the border districts asking, in a flat careful voice, whether the guild stocked anything that could keep a promise safe until morning.

Master Corvane had appeared in the middle of it all, coat already on, and issued exactly four sentences: "Open the trade floor. Ledger prices suspended — charity rates for border folk, standard rates for profiteers. Lior, bring me the oldest account book. Tobbin, nobody enters the archive."

Lior fetched the oldest book. Carried it to his office. Set it on the desk between them, and watched Corvane rest his hand on its cover — that cracked leather, that iron clasp, the book so old its title had worn away to a ghost — and for one strange moment the master looked less like a man opening a ledger than like a man laying his palm on a wound.

"Sir," Lior said. "What's happening? The wards — the *stars* —"

Corvane looked at them across the lamplight. His face was unreadable, which was usual; but underneath the unreadability, for the first time in two years, Lior thought they saw something else. Something that might, in a different man, have been grief.

"Not yet," Corvane said.

And opened the book, and turned to a page near the front, and read in silence while outside the window the city cried out in the dark — and Lior stood there, dismissed and not dismissed, one foot out the door as always, watching their master's lips move around numbers, and understanding with cold perfect clarity that he had known this was coming.

That he had known for a very long time.
