## 4. Refugees and Receipts

Sefa Dravenmoor had been a courier for the Guild of Impossible Things for six years, and in that time she had learned three rules of the road: never trust a bridge that hums, never eat anything offered by someone wearing your face, and always, *always* knock twice on the Guildhall's side door, because once was for customers and three times meant the master was in one of his moods.

She knocked twice.

The door opened onto chaos.

Not the ordinary chaos of the Guildhall, which Sefa had long ago learned to read like weather — the soft drizzle of apprentices cataloguing bottled echoes, the occasional thunderstorm of Master Corvane shouting about misfiled miracles. This was different. This was the kind of chaos that came before something broke.

The front hall, usually half-empty at this hour, was packed shoulder to shoulder with people who did not belong there. Farmers with mud still on their boots. A shepherd clutching a crook like it might defend him from whatever had frightened him. Children wrapped in blankets that were too big, their eyes too wide. And through the middle of it all moved Lior, ledger under one arm, quill behind one ear, looking like a person trying to bail out a sinking ship with a teaspoon and good intentions.

"Sefa." Lior spotted her and the relief on their face was so naked it was almost embarrassing. "Thank every star that — " They stopped. Winced. "Bad phrasing."

Sefa looked up. Through the high windows of the hall, where the morning sky should have been doing its usual pale blue business, there was nothing. Not clouds. Not night. Just a flat, starless dark, like the sky had been covered with a cloth.

"I saw," she said quietly. "Halfway back from Millbrook. The stars went out like someone pinched them." She set down her courier's satchel, which was heavier than it should have been, because it contained a compass that pointed at regrets and she'd been meaning to return it for weeks. "Then the wards fell. I felt it in my teeth."

"Everyone felt it in their teeth," said a voice near her elbow. It was Old Wenna, the guild's oldest apprentice — seventy if she was a day, and sharp as a paper cut. She was handing out cups of something steaming to the refugees. "Some of us felt it in our knees. Age has its privileges."

"What happened?" Sefa asked.

Lior and Wenna exchanged a look. It was the kind of look that contained an entire conversation, most of which was *don't say it here*.

"The wards fell," Lior said. "That's what we know. That's what we're telling people."

"And what you're not telling me?"

"Later," Lior said. "Please. I have forty people who need beds and a man over there who insists his cow can see the future and wants the guild to appraise her."

"Can she?"

"He says she predicted the dark. She also ate his hat, so I'm reserving judgment."

Sefa laughed despite herself, and the laugh felt strange in her chest, like a door opening into a cold room. But it helped. It always helped. That was the thing about Sefa that Corvane had seen when he'd hired her off the road six years ago, hungry and seventeen and lying about her age: she could laugh in a burning building, and other people would laugh with her, and then everyone would remember they had hands and could carry buckets.

"All right," she said, rolling up her sleeves. "Put me to work."

* * *

The work, it turned out, was receipts.

This should not have surprised her. It was, after all, the Guild of Impossible Things, and the guild's founding principle — carved into the lintel above the front door in letters that occasionally rearranged themselves when nobody was watching — was *Everything Has a Price, and We Know Most of Them.*

But Sefa had expected the refugees to want bread and blankets, and they did, and the guild provided those without ceremony because Corvane, whatever else he was, was not a monster about bread. What the refugees wanted *besides* bread was stranger.

A woman from the border village of Harrow's End wanted light. Not any light — her children couldn't sleep in the new dark, cried at candleflame because it flickered like something dying. So Sefa walked her down to the storerooms beneath the Guildhall, past shelves of hushed and humming inventory, to the crate marked *DAYLIGHT, ASSORTED, GENTLY USED.*

"We collect it in summer," Sefa explained, lifting out a stoppered bottle full of thick gold warmth. "Long days, when there's more than anyone needs. People leave it out in jars. Half the city does it without knowing why."

The woman held the bottle like a baby bird. "What do I do with it?"

"Uncork it at dusk. It'll remember being afternoon and pour itself out slow. Lasts till morning." Sefa hesitated. "It remembers better than candles. Candles just burn. This *remembers*. That matters, right now."

The woman's hands shook. "How much?"

Sefa looked at her — at the dust of the border road still on her hem, at the way she kept glancing toward the stairs where her children waited — and made the kind of decision that would get her scolded later.

"One memory," she said. "Anything. Something small. Tell me something true about your village that you love, so it gets written down. That's the price. The guild keeps accounts, and lately—" she stopped herself— "lately it seems important to write things down."

So the woman told her about the bell in Harrow's End that rang by itself whenever someone came home after a long absence, even if they snuck in at night, even if they'd left angry. And Sefa wrote it down in the day-book, carefully, in her blunt courier's hand, and the woman carried her bottle of remembered afternoon upstairs like it was made of glass and grace.

By midday, Sefa had traded daylight for eleven memories. She had sold a map of places that no longer existed — actual maps, drawn by the guild's cartographer from the descriptions of refugees, villages rendered in ink and longing — to a man who wanted proof his home had been real. She had priced a jar of birdsong at two stories and a promise. She had refused, firmly, to sell a woman a luck-charm, on the grounds that luck was not currently the problem, and had instead given her a needle and thread and taught her to sew a name into her son's coat collar, because names were holding value these days in ways Sefa didn't fully understand but instinctively trusted.

It was the strangest market day of her life, and she had once sold a bottled thunderstorm to a man who wanted to propose to a storm-witch.

* * *

In the late afternoon, she found Lior on the loading dock behind the Guildhall, sitting on a crate of moonlight (harvested, per the label, from a particularly generous equinox), eating bread like it was a personal enemy.

"You look terrible," Sefa said, sitting beside them.

"I feel organized," Lior said. "It's deceiving. Underneath the organization I am screaming in a very tidy way."

"Forty beds found?"

"Fifty-two. Wenna bullied the innkeepers. She threatened one of them with a haiku and he folded immediately." Lior tore their bread in half and handed her a piece without being asked, which was such an old habit between them that neither remarked on it. "Sefa. The roads east are still coming. Sefa from the waystation says the columns stretch back past the ford. Whole villages walking. And the sky isn't getting better."

"No," Sefa said. "I noticed the sky."

They sat in silence for a moment. Behind them, through the dock doors, the sounds of the guild continued — the clink of inventory, Wenna's voice instructing three apprentices on the proper shelving of hope ("label it, it all looks the same otherwise"), the distant bellowing of the future-cow, which had apparently eaten a second hat.

"Do you know what's happening?" Sefa asked. "Really?"

Lior was quiet long enough that she knew the answer was yes.

"Lior."

"I know some things," they said finally. "I know the guild's oldest ledger has an entry that scares me. I know Corvane knows more than he's saying, and when I asked him, he looked at me like — " Lior shook their head. "Like I was a bill come due. He said 'not yet.' That's all. *Not yet.*"

"Not yet until when?"

"I don't know. But Sefa —" Lior turned to her, and in the grey light their face looked older than it had yesterday, which was impossible, but grief is efficient like that. "The things we're selling downstairs. The daylight, the maps, the memories people trade us. I keep thinking — the guild trades in impossible things. Always has. But I think we've been trading in *one* impossible thing this whole time, and everything else is just packaging."

"What thing?"

"I don't know yet," Lior said. "But I stole a page from the old ledger, and I intend to find out."

Sefa stared at them. "You stole from Master Corvane's ledger? You? You alphabetize your socks."

"My socks are filed chronologically, thank you." But there was no humor behind it. "Someone has to know what's coming. The people downstairs deserve to know what they're trading against."

Downstairs, as if summoned, a bell rang — the front door, twice, which meant customers. Then again. Then a third time, rapid, urgent.

Three times. The mood-bell.

Sefa and Lior looked at each other.

"That's not Corvane's knock," Sefa said slowly. "Corvane doesn't knock in his own house."

They went inside together.

* * *

The front hall had gone quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when something has entered that changes the air. The refugees pressed back against the walls. Wenna stood very straight by the tea table, teapot in hand, positioned — Sefa noted with admiration — exactly where she could throw it.

In the doorway stood a rider in travel-stained court colors, and behind the rider, visible through the open door, the road east was a river of torches in the starless dark, a column of the displaced stretching back beyond sight.

The rider pulled off a gauntlet and produced a letter bearing a wax seal Sefa recognized and did not like: the crown's.

"Word for the Guild of Impossible Things," the rider announced, in the bored voice of someone delivering doom on a schedule. "From the Court of Inquisition. An inquisitor follows behind me. She'll want lodging, cooperation, and a list of everyone who's passed through your doors since the wards fell."

"A list," repeated Wenna, dangerously.

"Names, trades, places of origin." The rider shrugged. "Her words, not mine. She said — " he consulted a card — " 'tell them I only want to help. Help is easier when everyone is accounted for.' "

Sefa felt the words settle over the room like frost. *Accounted for.* She thought of the day-book on the counter downstairs, eleven memories already written in her own hand, names and villages and loves, a perfect record of exactly the people standing in this room.

Beside her, Lior had gone very still, one hand drifting unconsciously toward the inner pocket where, Sefa now understood, a stolen page lived.

"Well," said Wenna brightly, into the silence, pouring tea with the calm of a woman choosing her weapon. "Anyone who wants to help can start by mucking out the cow."

The rider blinked. "The what?"

"There's a cow," Wenna confirmed. "She sees the future. She's in the courtyard. Mind the hats."

And Sefa, watching the rider's composure crack against the immovable object of Old Wenna, laughed again — that door-into-the-cold-room laugh — and reached for the day-book, because whatever was coming down that road full of torches, the guild's work was the same as it had ever been:

Everything has a price. Somebody has to keep the accounts.

She just hoped, turning to the fresh page, that whoever was keeping the accounts on the other side of tonight was kinder than the ones coming up the road.

Behind her, unnoticed, the future-cow lowed once, long and low, at the empty sky.
