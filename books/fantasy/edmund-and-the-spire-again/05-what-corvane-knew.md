## 5. What Corvane Knew

The Guild of Impossible Things kept three kinds of hours, and Lior had learned to tell them apart the way farmers told weather.

There were market hours, when the front hall filled with people wanting small miracles at fair prices—a jar of thunder for a headache, a ribbon that would never fray, a key cut to fit a door that hadn't been built yet. There were ledger hours, which were Lior's own, spent in the counting room with its high narrow windows and its smell of ink and dust and old decisions. And then there were Corvane's hours, which arrived without warning, usually after midnight, and which everyone in the guildhall had learned to survive by becoming very busy and very quiet.

It was during a ledger hour that Lior found the entries.

They'd been working backward through the oldest book—the one bound in grey leather gone soft as moth-wing, the one whose missing page still lived folded in the lining of Lior's coat like a coal that wouldn't cool. The plan had been simple: understand what the page recorded by understanding everything around it. A receipt meant nothing alone. A receipt in context was an answer.

Lior had been copying figures by lamplight, cross-referencing the old hand against the guild's newer conventions, when the dates stopped making sense.

At first they thought it was their own error. Ledger-keepers make errors; it is practically the trade's founding principle. Lior checked the arithmetic twice, then a third time, then set down the pen entirely and pressed both palms flat against the desk as if the wood might confirm or deny.

*Paid to the Warden of the Ninth Gate, in trust: one memory of green. Due: 14th of Harvestmonth, Year 147.*

The current year was 47.

Lior read it again. Then the next entry. *Received from the Custodian of Ashes, on account: one hundred years of borrowed dawn. Interest to accrue. Settlement due: 14th of Harvestmonth, Year 147.*

Then another. And another. Page after page of them, marching forward through the book like soldiers who hadn't been told the war was over—payments scheduled, debts accruing, accounts opened and provisionally closed, all dated in a century that had not yet happened. The handwriting shifted over the pages, older to younger, several hands across decades, but the dates kept going. Whoever had kept this ledger had written past the edge of their own life and kept writing anyway, calm as a miller grinding grain, recording transactions for a future they would never see.

Except one hand appeared again and again, decade after decade, always the same. A tight, precise script that Lior knew intimately, because it signed every order, every wage slip, every instruction that came down from the master's study.

Corvane's hand, dated forty years from now.

Corvane's hand, dated *sixty* years from now.

Lior sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the night porter made his rounds, boots knocking a slow heartbeat through the floorboards.

There were two possible explanations, and Lior examined each with the care owed to any impossible thing. The first: forgery. Someone had faked the master's hand across a century of entries, which would require a forger of genius, patience beyond human measure, and no motive Lior could imagine. The second explanation was worse, and Lior turned it over slowly, the way you turn over a stone knowing what lives underneath.

The second explanation was that Master Corvane could see—or at least *account for*—the future. That the Guild of Impossible Things had been doing business with time itself, on terms, on schedule, with interest.

And at the bottom of the last forward-dated page, in that same tight hand, a single line that was not an entry but a note, underlined twice:

*When she asks—and she will ask—tell her the truth. She has earned worse than the truth.*

No name. But "she" sat alone in the counting room at midnight holding a stolen page, and her hands had begun to shake.

* * *

Lior did not sleep. This was not unusual—ledger hours bled into all the other hours more often than anyone admitted—but tonight the sleeplessness had a different texture. It wasn't restlessness. It was the particular alertness of prey.

By the grey hour before dawn, Lior had made two decisions. The first was to copy nothing. Some knowledge changed weight when you wrote it down, and this felt like knowledge that would burn straight through paper into whoever held it. The second decision was harder, and Lior rehearsed it while washing, while dressing, while eating breakfast standing up in the kitchen doorway because sitting felt impossible.

The second decision was to ask.

Corvane took his morning meal in the small study off the archive, alone, with the door open—an old courtesy he extended to the whole guild, though almost no one ever took advantage of it. Walking in uninvited was the social equivalent of stepping off a roof. Lior stepped off the roof.

He was reading correspondence and did not look up. He was a long man, dry as winter grass, with white hair cropped close and hands that moved with the economy of someone who had never once wasted a gesture. People said Corvane had no moods, only weather systems, and you learned to read the barometer of his silence.

"Master," Lior said. "I need to ask you about the old ledger."

The pen stopped moving.

It stopped cleanly, without a scratch or hesitation-stroke, which somehow was worse than if it had stuttered. Corvane finished the line he was writing—finished it, blotted it, squared the letter with the edge of the desk—and only then raised his eyes.

They were pale eyes, the color of watered milk, and they had always made Lior feel transparent. Today they made Lior feel *read*, which was different and considerably less comfortable.

"Sit down, Keeper."

"I'd rather stand."

"Yes," Corvane said. "I imagine you would." He set the pen down parallel to the letter's edge, precisely, the way he did everything. "You've been in the grey book."

It wasn't a question, so Lior didn't answer it. "There are entries dated a century ahead."

"There are."

"Payments. Scheduled payments. In your hand." Lior's voice came out steadier than expected, which surprised them, because inside they felt like a kettle left too long on the fire. "Dated sixty years from now. Seventy. Your hand, Master. Writing in dates that haven't happened yet."

"No," Corvane said. "Writing about dates that haven't happened yet. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point of my life." He folded his hands on the desk. "Ask your question properly, Keeper. You came here to ask something specific, and you've been circling it since you walked in. I've watched you circle things for six years. You circle like a cat deciding whether the saucer is a trap."

The comparison stung because it was accurate. Lior took a breath.

"What did we buy?" they said. "And what did we pay?"

For a long moment Corvane said nothing at all. Outside the study window, the city was waking up—cart wheels on cobbles, a fishwife arguing cheerfully with nobody, the ordinary music of a morning that believed in itself. Lior would remember that sound afterward, and remember how it sounded like something that could be taken away.

"You have the page," Corvane said quietly. "The one you took from the grey book. Don't look startled; I've known since the week you took it. I let you keep it because a keeper who steals a page to find the truth will guard the truth better than one who's simply handed it." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. "Read me the line."

"'The Heart, one,'" Lior recited, from six years of nightly repetition. "'One, held in trust.'"

"Held in trust," Corvane repeated. "Not stolen. Not bought. Held in trust. Do you know why the wording matters, Lior? Because words are the only thing keeping this city alive, and I chose them the way a surgeon chooses a blade."

"The Heart of *what*?"

Corvane rose. He crossed to the window and stood looking out at the morning with his back to them, and Lior realized, with a strange lurch, that the master was buying himself a moment—that the man who never wasted a gesture was spending several now, deliberately, like a miser forced at last to open the strongbox.

"When I was younger than you are now," he said, "there was a war. Not the kind with battles—not mostly. The kind where two powers stare at each other across a border until the border itself becomes the weapon. On our side of that border stood the Spire. You've seen drawings of it, perhaps. The black tower. The anchor-point." He paused. "The Spire held the wards. The wards held the border. And the border—" his voice went flat and careful, "—the border held back the things the war had made. Things that used to be people. Things that remembered being people, which is the cruelest part."

"And the Heart?"

"The Spire's heart was the wardstone at the center of everything. Every star you've ever navigated by, every ward-line drawn from the Spire outward—it ran through that stone. It was alive, Keeper. Not alive the way you and I are alive. Alive the way a *country* is alive. And the war could not be won, and could not be ended, and the Spire's hunger was growing, because the Spire fed on memory and the war was feeding it a century of grief." He turned around. "So a group of us did the arithmetic. Terrible arithmetic. The kind where every column balances and every sum is monstrous."

Lior's mouth had gone dry. "You took it."

"We *negotiated* for it. That's the distinction I cling to at three in the morning, and I recommend you find your own distinction, because you'll need one." Corvane came back to the desk but didn't sit. "We offered the Spire a trade. Its heart, surrendered willingly, in exchange for a century in which the war would be forgotten on both sides. Not ended—*forgotten*. The enemy beyond the border would forget their grievance. Our side would forget there had ever been an enemy. The wards would run on stored light instead of living heart, like a lamp burning its own oil, and when the oil ran out—" he spread his hands, "—well. A century seemed long enough that it would be someone else's problem. That is what cowardice looks like when it wears good clothes, Keeper. Remember that."

"The stars," Lior said. The word came out small. "The stars are the wards."

"The stars are the wards seen from underneath. Yes." Corvane looked at them steadily. "And the entries you found—the forward-dated ones—are the payment schedule. We borrowed a century of dawn. We borrowed forgetting itself. And the interest has been accruing the whole time, in ways I have spent fifty years trying to track and cannot fully account for. Villages that lose a name. Songs that lose their middle verses. Small hungers, small withdrawals. The Spire has been collecting interest, Lior, the whole time we thought we'd gotten away with something."

The floor seemed very slightly tilted. Lior gripped the back of a chair without letting it show.

"Why tell me?" they asked. "Why now? Why leave a note in the ledger addressed to—"

"*When she asks, and she will ask.*" Corvane nodded. "Because I wrote that note thirty years ago, Keeper, and I wrote it about you, and I did not know your face yet. The ledger told me a keeper would come who would steal a page and ask the right question. The ledger has been telling me things my whole life. That is the last impossible thing this guild does, and I have forbidden myself to speak of it, so consider yourself favored." He sat down at last, heavily, all his economy of motion gone. "The wards are thinning. I can feel it in the accounts—the interest payments growing larger, closer together. We have years, perhaps. Perhaps fewer. And when the century closes, the debt comes due in full, and I will be dead, and someone else will be holding the grey book."

"That's why you've been teaching me the old conventions. The pre-war notation. The—"

"I have been training my successor for thirty years," Corvane said, "and for thirty years I have prayed I was wrong about who it needed to be."

Silence filled the study like water filling a hold. Lior became aware, distantly, that they were angry. It arrived slowly, the way floods arrive—not a wave but a rising, soaking everything.

"A century," they said. "You bought a century and you let everyone believe the sky was just... the sky. You let children be born under borrowed stars. You let me catalogue jars of thunder and ribbon that doesn't fray while the actual account—" their voice cracked and they hated it, "—while the actual account was bleeding out. You knew. All those years I sat in the counting room, and you knew, and you said *not yet*."

"Yes," Corvane said.

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What would you have me say?" For the first time, something moved behind the milk-pale eyes—something old and tired and terribly awake. "That I'm sorry? I am, but sorry is a coin that spends poorly at this scale. That I had no choice? I had choices. I chose the city. I chose a hundred years of weddings and harvests and fishwives arguing with nobody over whatever was coming otherwise, and I would choose it again, and that is either the most defensible act of my life or the least, and I have had fifty years to decide and I *still don't know*." He pressed his palms flat against the desk, mirroring without knowing it the gesture Lior had made alone at midnight. "You wanted the truth. There it is. The guild sold the sky to buy time, the bill is coming due, and the only person I trust to read the accounts when it arrives is standing in my study calling me a thief."

"Are you?"

Corvane considered the question with the seriousness he gave to everything.

"I am the man who balanced the books," he said finally. "Whether the books should ever have been opened—that judgment I leave to you. To the next keeper. To whoever inherits this." He slid a drawer open and did not take anything out; Lior understood, later, that he had been reaching for something and decided against it. "Go and think, Keeper. Hate me if it helps. But do it accurately."

Lior walked out. They made it down the corridor, past the archive, into the counting room, before their legs gave out and they sat down hard on the cold floor among the shelved impossibilities, the grey book open on the desk above them, its forward-dated pages fluttering once in a draft from the window—as if the ledger itself had exhaled.

A century of borrowed dawn. A debt compounding in villages and verses and the spaces between names. And somewhere north, a tower with an empty chest, waiting for the settlement date with the patience of the very old.

Lior put their head in their hands.

That was when they noticed the ink.

Fresh ink—one entry, new since yesterday, in Corvane's tight hand, dated not a century ahead but *tomorrow*: 

*Note to self: the interest has begun to come due early. The first star went out last night. She must be told everything before the sky notices we know.*

Lior looked up, through the high window, toward the brightening east—where, faint and wrong, a gap had opened in the pattern of the fading morning stars, a hole where light should have been.

The sky had already noticed.
