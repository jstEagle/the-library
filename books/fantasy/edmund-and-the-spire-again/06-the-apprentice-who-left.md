## 6. The Apprentice Who Left

Three years before the stars went out, on a morning so ordinary it should have been ashamed of itself, Lior Dravenmoor burned the soup.

Not on purpose. Lior rarely did anything on purpose that ended in disaster; the disasters simply arrived, the way weather arrived, and Lior had learned to stand among them apologizing. The soup burned because Maren had sent them down to the cellar for dried nettle and a jar of pickled ramsons, and in the cellar Lior had found the door.

Everyone has a cellar door they're not supposed to open. Most people's is metaphorical. Maren's was literal, oak-banded, and locked with a knot of braided hair that tightened when you pulled at it — which was, in hindsight, a fairly clear instruction not to pull at it.

Lior pulled at it.

The knot loosened. Not all the way — just enough that a smell came out, green and cold and old, like a forest remembering being young. And underneath the smell, faint as a voice through a wall, something *hummed*.

"Lior!" Maren's voice from above, sharp with the particular edge she reserved for burnt soup, escaped goats, and apprentices standing too long in cellars. "If that broth dies, I'm feeding you the ladle."

"Coming!" Lior called back, and did not come, because the humming had a shape now, and the shape was almost words, and the words were almost *Lior*.

They stood there for the length of one held breath. Then, from upstairs, the soup announced its death with a small wet *whump*, and Maren swore in three languages, two of them dead, and Lior climbed the ladder with their heart hammering and the taste of cold green oldness still on their tongue.

The soup was black. The pot would never be the same. Maren looked at the ruin, then at Lior, then at the faint tracery of cellar dust on Lior's sleeves, and her face did something complicated and closed, like a book snapped shut by wind.

"You went down there," she said.

"I got lost," said Lior, who was a bad liar and knew it, and whose mistress also knew it, and who therefore understood, even then, that this lie cost more than the truth would have.

Maren stirred the corpse of the soup without looking at it. "Eat your bread," she said. "We'll talk tonight."

They didn't talk that night. They didn't talk for six nights, which for a household of two people and one opinionated goat was a very loud silence indeed.

* * *

To understand what happened next, you have to understand what Maren's cottage was.

It sat at the crook of a river valley where the mapmakers had given up and written *hereabouts*, and it was the kind of place travelers found only when they needed to. That was not an accident. Maren had woven the finding into the hedges themselves — a little memory-magic, the harmless kind, or so everyone believed: a hedge that remembered every road that led away from trouble and grew itself along those roads when someone lost came walking. People arrived weeping, or bleeding, or carrying children with fevers no physician could name, and Maren fed them, stitched them, unspooled whatever small spell the moment required, and sent them home lighter than they'd come.

Lior had been one of those arrivals, seven years ago: a scrawny, furious child of eleven whose village had decided that a boy who talked to crows and a girl who dreamed true were both problems, and whose parents had solved the problem by sending both children walking into the winter with a loaf and a blessing. (The girl had made it to an aunt in Corvale. The crows had led Lior to the hedge.) Maren had taken them in the way she took in everything — grumbling, thorough, and completely.

Seven years of mornings. Seven years of learning which herbs argued with each other, how to mend a memory that snagged like a hem, how to hold a dying woman's hand while Maren eased the fear out of it and left the love. Seven years of Maren saying *again, slower* and *no, that's how you burn the soup* and, once, memorably, *if you kiss the cooper's son again I'll have to teach him to forget it, and he's sweet, don't make me do that.*

And in all those years, Lior had never once asked what the magic cost. That was the thing Lior could not forgive, later. Not the asking. The *not asking*.

* * *

On the seventh night of silence, Lior opened the cellar door.

The hair-knot didn't fight them. Later, Lior would wonder if it had ever really been locked, or if it had only ever been waiting — if Maren had braided it knowing that sooner or later curiosity would win, because curiosity always won with Lior, and because some lessons can't be taught, only tripped over.

Inside was not treasure. Inside was a room the size of a generous cupboard, and in it, shelves. And on the shelves, jars. Hundreds of them, stoppered with wax and labeled in Maren's cramped, slanting hand, and each jar held a soft grey-gold light that moved when Lior moved, leaning toward them like a plant toward sun.

Lior lifted the nearest jar. The label read: *Tamsin Hale — her wedding day, kept safe from grief.* Another: *Old Wren — the name of his first dog.* Another: *M. Thackeray — my sister's laugh, 1 of 3.*

And another, lower down, the wax freshly broken, the light inside dimmed to almost nothing:

*L. Dravenmoor — age four to nine. Paid against tuition.*

Lior stood in the cold green dark holding five missing years of their own life in both hands, and the humming in the jars rose around them, and somewhere in the swarm of lights was a child learning to whistle, a mother's face, a reason — the reason — why a furious eleven-year-old had walked into the winter without once looking back, and Lior could not see any of it. It was right there. It was *right there*, glowing gently, and it was not theirs to open.

They carried the jar up the ladder anyway.

Maren was sitting at the kitchen table with a lamp turned low and both hands flat on the wood, and she watched Lior set the jar down between them, and she did not look surprised. That was somehow the worst part.

"How many?" Lior asked. Their voice came out steady, which frightened them.

"Yours? Fourteen jars." Maren said it plainly, the way she said *the fever will break tonight*. "Small ones. Nothing you'd miss."

"*Nothing I'd miss?*" The steadiness cracked. "I don't remember my mother's face. I've known that for years — I thought it was the walking, the cold, I thought I'd *lost* it—"

"You did lose it. To me. For the shielding work, the crow-speech, the first year of herb-lore." Maren's hands stayed flat on the table, but her knuckles had gone white. "Every lesson I ever taught you was paid for out of your own past, because that's what memory-magic *is*, girl. You can't spin something from nothing. Memory is the only thread fine enough, and yours was the only thread that was mine to ask for. I asked. I paid myself, too — look at me, look how much of me I've spent keeping this valley quiet and safe. But you were a child, and children can't consent to a debt, so I kept the accounts, and I meant to give it all back. Every jar. When you were grown. When you could choose."

"When I was grown," Lior repeated. "Like tonight. Like you were going to tell me *tonight*?"

"No," Maren said, and closed her eyes. "I was going to keep finding reasons. That's the truth of it. Because giving it back means telling you what I took it *for*, and most of it went into the hedges, Lior. Into the wards on this valley. Into keeping things asleep that your village's grandparents helped wake. And if you know that, you'll want to help, and helping costs, and I promised your mother—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I promised someone I'd keep you small and safe, and I have never in my life broken a promise so thoroughly by keeping it."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the goat complained about something, on principle.

"There's a war coming," Lior said slowly. "That's what's under the valley. That's what the hedges are for."

"That's what the *Spire* is for," Maren said, and the word came out of her like a stone from a sling. "And the Spire is hungry, and it eats memory, and everything I have done for thirty years — every jar, every ward, every kindness in this valley — has been feeding it quietly so it doesn't feed *loudly*. So it doesn't eat villages whole. So it stays polite." She opened her eyes, and they were wet and fierce and terribly tired. "Now you know. Now you carry it. Are you happy?"

No. Lior was not happy. Lior was fifteen — nearly sixteen, they'd have said, with the pedantry of the young — and the floor of their entire life had just been shown to be a lid.

"You should have told me," Lior said.

"Yes."

"You should have let me choose."

"Yes." Maren reached across the table, toward the jar, toward Lior's hands. "And I'm telling you now, so choose. Stay, and learn it properly — learn it *with* the accounts open, both our names in the columns, everything honest from today. Or go, and I'll return these fourteen jars to you right now, all of them, and you'll walk out of here remembering your mother's face and every reason you ever had to be angry with me, and you'll hate me clearly instead of loving me half-blind. Either way, no more lies. I swear it on the hedge."

And there it was. The choice, offered clean, at last, on a scarred kitchen table smelling of burnt soup.

Here is the thing about Lior Dravenmoor, the thing that would take a guild, a tower, and the loss of everything to teach them: when offered a clean choice, they always, always looked for the third door.

"What about the others?" Lior said. "The jars. Tamsin's wedding. Old Wren's dog. All those people who think they *lost* things. Do they get to choose?"

Maren's hand stopped halfway across the table.

"Some of them can't be told," she said. "Some of them would break."

"That's not yours to decide either!"

"It is exactly mine to decide, because I'm the one still alive who knows how to do it *gently*—"

"Gently!" Lior was on their feet now, and the jar-lights below them were rising, agitated, moths around a struck lamp. "You steal from people *gently*. You feed a hungry god *gently*. You erased *me* gently! Well, I don't want gentle. I want *honest*. I want the ugliest ledger you've got, every entry, every name, and I want to start handing them back tonight—"

"You are not ready—"

"Then teach me until I am! Isn't that what I'm *for*?"

The silence after that was total. Even the goat outside seemed to lean in. And Lior watched the answer move behind Maren's eyes — watched her weigh the apprentice she loved against the war she feared, and watched fear win, and understood, in the same instant Maren did, that it had always been going to win. Thirty years of feeding a hungry dark does something to a person. It teaches you that safety is a debt you pay forever, and that anyone who wants to stop paying is a danger to everyone.

"No," Maren said quietly. "Not yet. You're not ready, and the valley can't afford your mistakes. Ask me again in a year."

Something in Lior went very cold and very clear.

"Not yet," Lior said. "That's what you said about the cellar. That's what you've said about everything, my whole life. *Not yet, not yet, not yet.*" They picked up the jar — their own four-to-nine, dim and patient in their hands. "Then I'll go find someone who says yes."

"Lior—"

"Don't follow me past the hedge." They were already moving, snatching their cloak off its peg, and the terrible thing, the thing they would chew on for three years, was how *easy* it was. Seven years of belonging, and fury fit through the door of it without turning sideways. "Don't weave the roads against me. Don't spend one drop of anything to bring me back. If I'm a debt, let me leave the ledger."

Maren stood. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the herbs hanging from the beams, and for one moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet.

"You'll remember why," she said.

"What?"

"You'll remember *why* you left." Her voice cracked down the middle, and beneath the crack was something that sounded horribly like prophecy, or apology, or both. "One day the account comes due, child. It always does. And when it does — when you finally remember everything — I need you to remember that I loved you the whole time. Even the parts of you I spent."

Lior did not answer. Lior opened the door into the blue cold of evening and walked out, and behind them they heard Maren begin, softly, steadily, to sing — the working-song, the hedge-song, the one that made roads grow toward the lost — and Lior put their hands over their ears and ran, because they knew exactly what she was doing, and they knew, too, that they had no right to stop her, and that she would stop the moment they were truly beyond reach, because underneath everything she kept her promises.

The hedge parted for them anyway. Of course it did. It had spent seven years learning the shape of them.

* * *

Lior walked all night and most of the next day, and the strangest part — the part they hated most, in the years after — was how little they grieved. The anger should have burned down to ash and left mourning under it. Instead it just burned, steady as a lamp, and under it there was only a smooth blank place where the grief should have been, and Lior told themselves that was fury too, that it was all fury, that they'd feel everything properly once they were safe.

They were lying. They knew they were lying. Fourteen jars' worth of a person does not walk out of a valley without leaving a hole, and the hole walked with them, humming faintly, green and cold and old.

In Corvale, three weeks later, broke and hollow-eyed and sleeping in the temple colonnade, Lior saw the notice nailed to the Guildhall gate:

*THE GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS seeks a keeper of accounts. Must be literate, numerate, honest, and unafraid of items that object to being catalogued. Inquire within. No talent required. No questions asked.*

No questions asked. Lior had stood in front of that notice for a long time, reading it over and over, feeling the words rearrange themselves into an answer.

A place that didn't ask questions. A place that wrote everything down instead — every impossible thing weighed, priced, entered, *kept*, nothing vanished into jars, nothing fed to the dark, everything accounted for in ink that anyone could read.

They had gone in. Master Corvane — younger then, iron-grey rather than white, with a smile he wore like a tool — had looked at Lior's hands, then at their face, and asked exactly one question: *"Can you keep a secret you don't understand?"*

"Yes," Lior had said, thinking of a cellar full of light.

"Good," said Corvane. "Neither can I. You're hired."

* * *

Three years later, on the morning the stars went out, Lior would stand in the Guildhall's counting-room with a stolen page hidden in their shirt and think: *not yet. He said not yet too. They all say not yet.*

But that morning was still far away. On this morning — the last ordinary one — Lior woke in the narrow bed above the counting-room, in the city that had taken them in without asking anything, and reached automatically for the memory of a kitchen that smelled of herbs and burnt soup, and found, as always, the smooth blank place where it should have been.

Somewhere north, in a valley the maps called *hereabouts*, a hedge witch was keeping accounts of her own.

And far to the north of that, past the fallen border, something enormous and patient shifted in its sleep, and began, very quietly, to count.
