## 7. Inquisitor Vael Arrives

The city of Emberfall had three gates, and Inquisitor Sera Vael chose to enter through the one nobody used.

The Salt Gate had been closed for a generation—bricked up, according to the guidebooks, after the plague year—and so it was the perfect place for a woman who wished to arrive without being announced. She stood before it at dawn in her traveling cloak, grey as old rain, and waited for the wardens she had sent ahead to unbrick it. They worked in silence. She watched the sun come up over the rooftops and thought, as she always did upon arriving in a new city, *Someone in this place is lying to me. I have only to find out who.*

It was not a cynical thought. It was simply arithmetic. Every city contained liars; every lie, properly followed, led somewhere interesting. Vael had built a career on the premise that truth was not hidden from you—it was merely buried under an inconvenient amount of politeness, and her job was to dig.

"Gate's open, Inquisitor," said Warden Brask, wiping mortar dust from his hands. He was a large man with the face of a friendly ox, which was precisely why she'd brought him. People told friendly oxen things they would never tell a wolf.

"Thank you, Brask. How many people saw us do that?"

"Two beggars. I paid them."

"Generously?"

"Generously enough that they'll remember it happened and forget what we looked like."

Vael smiled. It was a small smile, well-practiced, the kind that made strangers feel included in a joke they hadn't quite heard. "Then we're off to a good start."

She stepped through into Emberfall and immediately disliked it, in the way she disliked every city: too many corners, too many doors, too many places for a guilty conscience to hide. But there was something else here, something specific. The air tasted wrong. Not plague-wrong or smoke-wrong. It tasted like a room where someone had just stopped speaking when you walked in.

Above her, where the sky should have been doing its ordinary morning business—a few clouds, a pale wash of blue—there was nothing. A blankness. She had seen the starless night from two hundred miles away, riding hard, and had told herself it was weather. Weather did not persist through dawn. Weather did not make grown men on the road refuse to look up.

"The wards fell here," she said quietly. "Three nights ago."

"Reports say during market," Brask said. "Midday. Sound like glass breaking, but felt through your boots."

"And the court's response?"

Brask hesitated. He was loyal, which she valued, and honest, which she tolerated, and the combination made him pause before certain answers. "The court's response is you, Inquisitor."

"Yes," Vael said. "I'd noticed." She adjusted the strap of her satchel—the good satchel, the one with the wax seals and the warrant signed in the Lord Chancellor's own hand—and began to walk. "Let's go be polite at someone."

* * *

Her first interview was with a baker, because bakers see everything and are constitutionally incapable of keeping quiet about it.

His name was Odell Pem, his shop sat within sight of the great market square, and when Vael entered he was standing in the middle of his own bakery holding a tray of rolls as though unsure whether bread still mattered in a world without stars.

"Inquisitor Vael," she introduced herself, producing the warrant with a flourish calibrated to impress without intimidating. "Court of the Ember Throne. I'm investigating the incident of three nights past. May I ask you some questions? And may I have one of those? They smell extraordinary."

That was the method, right there, in miniature. Ask permission. Compliment the bread. People relaxed around a woman who wanted a roll more than she wanted their secrets, and relaxed people were talkative people.

Odell talked. He talked about the moment the sky went out—"like someone pinched the wick of the whole world, m'lady"—and the sound that came after, the deep cracking groan that everyone in the square had felt in their teeth. He talked about the panic, the carts overturned, the Guild of Impossible Things throwing open its doors and handing out lamps that burned with remembered daylight.

"The guild," Vael repeated. "They were quick."

"Quick as anything. Master Corvane himself stood on the steps calling orders. Light for the dark, maps for folk cut off from home, charms against—well." The baker lowered his voice. "Against whatever's out past the border now."

"How generous," Vael said, and bit into the roll. It was excellent. Guilty cities baked well; fear demanded comfort. "And no one found this suspicious? That the one institution dealing in impossible things had exactly the impossible things everyone needed, pre-stocked?"

Odell blinked at her. "It's the Guild of Impossible Things, m'lady. Stocking them's rather the point."

"Of course," Vael agreed warmly. "Silly of me."

But she wrote it down anyway, in her neat, terrible hand: *Guild responded within minutes. Either very lucky, or very prepared. Or present at the cause.*

She spent the rest of the day being polite at people. A lamplighter who swore the stars had flickered twice before going out entirely, like a candle deciding whether to stay lit. A widow who insisted her cat had yowled at the sky a full hour beforehand, and while Vael did not put much stock in prophetic cats, she wrote down the time the woman claimed, because it was earlier than anyone else's account and discrepancies were doorways. A drunk in a tavern who grabbed her sleeve and whispered that the Spire had done it, the old Spire up north, the hungry tower of nursery rhymes, and Vael thanked him gravely and bought him a drink and filed him under *useful fools*, because nursery-rhyme explanations were usually the ones people had decided on in advance, and pre-decided explanations were how communities hid what they actually knew.

By evening she had a map of the city drawn in her head, with threads running out of it like loose stitching. Nearly all of them led, sooner or later, to the same building.

"The Guildhall tomorrow," she told Brask, back in the rented rooms above a chandler's shop. She was seated by the window, copying her notes into fair order, and the blank sky beyond the glass kept catching her eye like a stain on a clean shirt. "Send word ahead tonight. Courtesy call. We wouldn't want them thinking we're sneaking about."

"Won't they wonder why an inquisitor came through the Salt Gate, then?"

"Oh, certainly." Vael dipped her pen. "That's why we'll let them wonder overnight. Wonder softens people up. By morning they'll be so busy composing their answers to the question I haven't asked that they'll fumble the questions I have."

Brask considered this. "That seems exhausting, m'lady."

"It is," Vael said, "for everyone. That's rather the design."

* * *

The Guildhall of Impossible Things looked, in daylight, disappointingly sensible.

Vael had expected something crooked and strange—a building that defied its own architecture, doors on the roof, that sort of thing. Instead it was a broad stone hall with a slate roof and a sign depicting a key unlocking a cloud, and clerks coming and going with ledger-books under their arms like any countinghouse in the capital. Only the details were wrong if you looked long enough: the weathervane pointing consistently inward, toward the building itself; the way the shadows of the windows fell at slightly the wrong angle for the sun; the faint sense, walking up the steps, of being *read*.

Master Corvane received her in a study lined floor to ceiling with account books. He was older than she'd expected, silver-haired, with ink-stained fingers and the calm of a man who had made peace with something enormous a long time ago.

"Inquisitor Vael." He bowed precisely the correct depth—not too shallow to insult, not too deep to flatter. "The guild is at the court's disposal. Though I confess we wondered when you'd arrive."

"You wondered?" Vael took the offered chair. "Not feared?"

"Fear is for things whose shape you don't know." Corvane settled behind his desk. "We knew the court would send someone. The wards are the crown's wards. Their falling is the crown's wound. Someone must be seen to hunt for the knife."

"*Seen* to hunt," Vael echoed pleasantly. "You doubt my sincerity, Master Corvane?"

"I doubt nothing about you, Inquisitor. I simply know how courts work. I've been alive a long time, and I've paid enough of their fees." His eyes were pale and steady. "Ask your questions. I'll answer what I can."

So she asked. Where was he when the stars went out? —In this room, alone, working. Did he feel the wards fall? —He felt a sound, yes, like the world clearing its throat. Why did the guild respond so quickly? —Because responding quickly is what the guild is *for*. Each answer was smooth, cooperative, and utterly uninformative, and Vael recognized the texture of it the way a fishmonger recognizes fresh ice: expertly applied, and meant to keep something cold and dead beneath it.

What interested her far more was the clerk.

Corvane had summoned an assistant to fetch a particular volume—one of the guild's older registries, he said, relevant to her questions about the wards' maintenance accounts. And the assistant who brought it was young, sharp-faced, ink to the elbows, with the particular watchfulness of someone perpetually calculating exits. The clerk set the book down with both hands, carefully, the way one handles something fragile or forbidden, and Vael watched those hands the way a hawk watches a hedgerow.

"Your name?" she asked, before the clerk could retreat.

A beat of stillness. "Lior Dravenmoor. Ledger-keeper."

"Ledger-keeper. Then you'll know these volumes intimately." Vael gestured at the registry. "Tell me—is this the complete record of the ward-maintenance accounts?"

Lior glanced at the book. Something crossed the young face—fast, controlled, but not fast enough. Not for Vael. It was the look of a gambit failing silently across a chessboard.

"It's the registry for the last sixty years," Lior said. "The oldest ledger is separate. Restricted."

"Naturally. And this one is complete? No pages missing, no gaps?"

Another beat. Longer this time. Vael counted it the way she counted everything: one, two—

"No gaps," Lior said.

"May I?" Vael held out her hand, and Corvane nodded permission, and she turned the pages slowly, letting the silence stretch, letting them both wonder what she was looking for. She wasn't looking for anything in particular. That was the trick of it. She was giving the book time to tell her what it lacked.

Books, like rooms, had a way of showing their wounds. A page removed left traces: a stub at the spine, a faint offset ghost of ink on the neighboring leaf, a binding that sat differently under the thumb. Vael turned page after page with the gentle attention of a physician palpating for a fracture.

Near the front—near the beginning of the volume, where the entries were oldest and the handwriting most cramped—she felt it. A slight thinness. A stub, trimmed close and skillfully, but a stub nonetheless. And pressed into the facing page, the mirror-ghost of writing that no longer existed: a few words reversed and faded, legible only if you knew to tilt the paper against the light and read the impression left behind.

*...the Heart, one (1)...*

Vael did not tilt the paper. Not yet. She smoothed the page flat, smiled at it as though it had been perfectly satisfactory, and closed the book.

"Well," she said brightly. "Fascinating records. You keep a meticulous house, Master Corvane."

"We try."

"I'll want copies of several sections. And I'll want to speak further with your staff—individually, at leisure. Beginning with your ledger-keeper, I think. Tomorrow, perhaps. Ledger-keepers always know where the bodies are buried. Metaphorically," she added, with the small smile. "Usually."

Corvane's expression did not change at all, which was itself an answer. "Of course. Whatever assists the crown's inquiry."

Everything about the man said *you will find nothing here*, and everything about the room said *we have had a century to prepare for you*, and Vael found, to her private delight, that she was enjoying herself immensely. Polite people were so often hiding the best things.

* * *

That evening she sat by the chandler's window again, fair-copying notes, while Brask ate his way through a meat pie she had expressly told him not to buy from a street vendor.

"The clerk," Brask said, around crust. "Dravenmoor. You liked them."

"'Liked' is a strong word. I *noticed* them." Vael blew ink dry. "Two lies, Brask. Two small, well-made lies in the space of four minutes. 'No gaps,' when there is a gap. And the hesitation before the name—did you catch it? People hesitate before their own names only when they're using it like a shield."

"Maybe they're just nervous. Stars go out, inquisitor comes knocking. I'd be nervous."

"Nervous people fidget. This one *calculated*." She tapped her pen against the page. "And here's the arithmetic that interests me. Corvane answered every question smoothly. Too smoothly. Smoothness like that takes rehearsal, and rehearsal means he knew the questions in advance—which means either the guild is innocent and obsessively prepared, or guilty and obsessively prepared. But the clerk didn't rehearse. The clerk lied *live*. Improvised. Which tells me the clerk has something Corvane doesn't know about—or something Corvane doesn't know the clerk knows about."

Brask chewed thoughtfully. "And the missing page?"

"Ah." Vael set down the pen and looked out at the starless dark, at the city below lighting itself against it with desperate, borrowed brightness. Somewhere out there, past the fallen border, whatever had broken the wards was presumably still breaking things. Somewhere in this city, a young ledger-keeper was probably awake right now, wondering how much she'd noticed.

She had noticed everything. That was the trouble with her.

"A receipt," Vael said softly. "Hidden in the ghost of the page, plain as prayer. '*The Heart, one (1), held in trust.*' Held in trust by whom? For whom? And what does a tower's heart weigh on a guild's scale?" She shook her head. "No. Tomorrow I'll sit across from our ledger-keeper, and I'll be very, very polite, and I won't mention the page at all. I'll talk about bindings and ink and filing customs until our clever clerk forgets to count the seconds between question and answer."

"And then?"

"Then," Vael said, "we'll see whether Lior Dravenmoor lies to me a third time. Liars are like bells, Brask. Strike them gently enough times, and eventually they ring true."

Below, the city glowed against the dark. Above, the empty sky waited, patient as an unpaid debt.

Vael picked up her pen and wrote a single line at the top of tomorrow's page:

*Begin with the clerk.*
