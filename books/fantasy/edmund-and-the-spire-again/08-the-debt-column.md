## 8. The Debt Column

The oldest ledger in the Guild of Impossible Things did not want to be read.

Lior had suspected this for three days now, ever since they'd smuggled it out of the archive under a false cover labeled *Catalogue of Mildly Unusual Buttons*—a book so boring that even the archive moths had given up on it. The disguise had worked. The ledger itself was another matter.

It sat on Lior's desk in the small hours of the morning, breathing dust, and every time Lior reached for it, something went wrong. The candle guttered. A page turned itself with a sound like a sigh. Once, when Lior's fingers were an inch from the cover, the guild bell rang the hour—a full hour early—and by the time the ringing stopped, Lior had entirely forgotten what they'd been about to do.

That was the trick of it, Lior realized on the third night, sitting back and rubbing their eyes. The book wasn't fighting them. It was *distracting* them. Like a child hiding something behind its back while pointing enthusiastically at a window. *Look, over there! Something interesting!*

"Nice try," Lior told it.

The ledger said nothing, which was, in Lior's experience, how most books argued.

They worked in the counting room after hours, which was technically against guild rules and practically against common sense—the counting room at night had a reputation, involving cold spots and the sound of abacuses clicking themselves—but Lior had learned that rules were like the guild's inventory: nominally comprehensive, actually full of holes, and best navigated by someone who kept careful notes.

So Lior kept careful notes.

They spread them across the desk now: three days of transcription, copied in their smallest hand onto scraps of scrap paper because using good paper for this felt like asking to be caught. The stolen page from the archive lay in the center, weighted down with an inkwell. *Received of the Guild of Impossible Things, in trust perpetual: the Heart, one (1). Condition upon receipt: whole. Condition as recorded: whole.* And beneath that, in ink so old it had gone brown, a single word in a column header that Lior had spent two full evenings trying to parse:

*Debitor.*

Not debtor. Debitor. An older form. A formal form. The kind of word lawyers used, and priests, and—Lior's stomach did a slow, cold roll—*accountants*. Someone had opened an account. Someone had borrowed.

And the question that had kept Lior awake, staring at the ceiling beams while the city slept dark around them—darker than it should be, darker than any city had a right to be since the stars went out—was the simplest and worst question in all of accounting:

*Borrowed from whom?*

* * *

"You look terrible," Sefa said the next morning, sliding a tray of breakfast onto Lior's desk without being asked. She did this sometimes. Lior had never decided whether it was kindness or reconnaissance. With Sefa it was usually both. "Terrible in a specific way. I've catalogued your terrible looks. This one is 'hasn't slept and is proud of it.'"

"I slept."

"Liar. You have ink on your face." She pointed. "Two places. One of them is somewhere concerning."

Lior scrubbed at their cheek and found the ink spot had migrated alarmingly close to their eye. "I've been working."

"On buttons?"

"On... adjacent materials."

Sefa leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. She was the guild's fastest courier and its worst liar, which made her excellent company and a constant low-grade danger. Her brother was somewhere in the refugee columns outside the walls—she'd said so once, quietly, the way you say things you can't afford to say twice—and since the night the stars went out she'd had a look Lior recognized from mirrors: the look of someone carrying a question too heavy to set down.

"The whole city's working," she said. "Half of them are working on panic. What's your half?"

Lior looked at her. Looked at the ledger, sitting under its button catalogue disguise. Felt the old reflex rise—the apprentice's reflex, the runaway's reflex, the *keep-one-foot-out-the-door* reflex that had carried them out of Maren's cottage and across half a country and into this guildhall where they belonged, mostly, except for the parts they kept locked.

Then they thought about the stars. All of them. Gone. And the wards falling with that sound like glass breaking inside stone, heard through the soles of your feet before your ears got the news.

"Sefa," Lior said, "if I show you something, will you promise not to ask me questions I can't answer yet?"

"That's the only kind of question anyone can answer lately." She came around the desk and pulled up a stool. "Show me."

* * *

The problem, Lior explained, was the script.

Old ledgers weren't written in plain trade-hand. They were written in *tally-script*, the accountant's cipher, where numbers wore disguises and words were folded into columns like letters tucked into envelopes. Every guild had its own dialect of it. The Guild of Impossible Things' dialect was older than the guild itself—which should have been impossible, and was therefore right at home here.

"I cracked the number substitutions on night one," Lior said, tapping their notes. "Simple offset. Every numeral stands for itself plus the age of the guild in years at the time of writing, mod nine. It's elegant, actually. Self-dating. You can tell when any entry was made by which cipher-key it uses."

"And the words?"

"The words are the problem. Most of them decode fine. *Received. Rendered. Held in trust. Perpetual.* Standard trust-language, very formal, very legal. But there's one column—" Lior turned the stolen page toward her, "—one column that decodes to gibberish no matter which key I apply. Except."

"Except?"

"Except last night I stopped assuming it was encrypted." Lior's finger traced the column of brown-inked characters. "What if it's not cipher at all? What if it's just *old*? Older than the guild. Older than the trade-hand. What if whoever wrote this was writing in a language the rest of the ledger doesn't speak?"

Sefa squinted at the characters. "It looks like tally-marks having a bad day."

"It does. But look—" Lior laid their transcriptions beside it, weeks of frustrated attempts. "Every other column in this ledger balances. In, out, held, returned. Perfectly balanced, for a hundred years and more. That's what ledgers *do*. But this column never balances. It only grows. Entry after entry, century after century, the same figure repeated, and each time it's written, it's written slightly differently. Larger. As if..."

"As if it's accruing interest," Sefa said slowly.

Lior nodded. "On a principal that's never named."

The counting room was quiet around them. Somewhere above, the guild's day was starting—footsteps, the clatter of the manifest carts, Master Corvane's voice rising in irritation at some unlucky junior. Normal sounds. The sounds of a world that still believed it had a floor under it.

"So what is it?" Sefa asked. "The principal. What did someone borrow that takes a hundred years to pay off and never gets smaller?"

"I don't know yet. But I know *who*." Lior took a breath. "Because last night, around the fourth bell, when I was too tired to be clever, I stopped trying to decode the column and started trying to *read* it. Out loud. Sound by sound. The way Maren used to make me read hedge-names—"

They stopped. Saying her name still caught on something in their chest, like a splinter they couldn't find the end of.

"The way I was taught to read old things," Lior amended. "And it's not cipher, Sefa. It's not even language, exactly. It's a *name*. Written over and over. The same name, every entry, growing. It's the debtor's signature. And it's signed in the oldest script I've ever seen—the kind they say predates the border kingdoms. The kind they say the *ward-stones* were cut in."

Sefa had gone very still. "Whose name?"

Lior looked down at their transcription, at the phonetic rendering they'd scratched out at dawn, hands shaking, certain and horrified in equal measure. There was no gentle way to say it. There was possibly no sane way to say it. But Lior had been a ledger-keeper for six years, and the first thing a ledger-keeper learns is that the truth does not care whether you are ready for it. The truth simply enters the column and waits.

"The Spire," they said. "The debtor is the Spire. Listed as a person. Full legal personhood, Sefa—name, account, standing, the works. It's been on the guild's books longer than the guild has existed. And it didn't lend us anything." Their voice came out strange, thin, as if heard from a long way off. "We're in *its* ledger. We always have been. The guild didn't steal from the Spire."

"We borrowed from it."

"A hundred years ago. Something called the Heart. One. Held in trust, perpetual. And the interest—" Lior pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "You remember the village reports? The ones from the border, before the wards fell? Whole hamlets waking up having forgotten their own children's names? Everyone assumed it was ward-failure. Leakage."

"And it wasn't."

"It was *payment collection*." Lior lowered their hands. "Or feeding. Or—I don't know what to call it. But the column's been growing my entire life, Sefa. My entire life, and everyone's, going back and back. The Spire has been owed something enormous for a hundred years, and it has been *patient*, and it has been keeping accounts of its own, and three weeks ago it stopped being patient."

Sefa stared at the ledger as if it might sit up and introduce itself. Then, in a voice much smaller than her usual one: "How big? The debt. How big are we talking?"

Lior had done the arithmetic. They'd done it at dawn, and then they'd checked it, and then they'd checked it again, hoping desperately to find the error that would let them sleep. There was no error.

"If memory is the currency—and everything points that way—then the outstanding balance is roughly equal to the accumulated remembered past of every person living between the Spire and this city." Lior swallowed. "Everyone. Everything they remember. Going back to birth. That's the principal plus a century of interest."

The silence that followed had weight. Sefa broke it eventually, the way she broke most silences—by refusing to respect it.

"That's not a debt," she said. "That's a famine with a paperwork problem."

"Yes."

"And Corvane knows. He has to know. He's master of the guild—he signs the accounts."

"He signs the accounts," Lior agreed quietly. Which meant, though neither of them said it aloud, that every year, for however many years Corvane had held the seal, he had looked at that column—that impossible, growing, hungry column—and initialled it. Carried it forward. *Balance due.*

* * *

They worked through the morning, and it was almost—almost—bearable, having Sefa there. Fear shared out evenly is lighter than fear carried alone; Lior had learned that much from the road, running south with nothing but a satchel and a guilt they couldn't name. Sefa read the decoded entries aloud in her courier's clear voice, and hearing them spoken made them somehow more real and less monstrous at once, the way a storm named is a storm half-tamed.

*Entry 1. Received of the Spire, by agreement of parties: the Heart, one (1), whole. Purpose: the ending of the war. Term: one hundred years. Consideration: [the column]. Witnessed and sealed.*

*Entry 2. Interest rendered: [the column], per schedule.*

*Entry 3. Extension requested by the Guild. Granted. Consideration adjusted: [the column], increased.*

Page after page. Decade after decade. And threaded through them, in the newer hands—younger clerks, Lior realized, generations of clerks who had copied these entries forward without understanding them—little marginal notes that grew increasingly nervous as the decades passed:

*Query: what is the consideration? Answer deferred to Master.*

*Query: the debtor requests early settlement. Is the debtor permitted to request? Answer deferred to Master.*

*Query: villages along the north road report losses. Connection to account? Answer deferred to Master.*

Answer deferred to Master. For a hundred years, answer deferred to Master. Lior felt a kinship with those dead clerks that was nearly painful—every one of them had stood exactly where Lior stood, pen hovering, courage failing, writing the question down and then writing the coward's formula after it. *Deferred.* Filed. Forgotten.

Well. Lior had run away from one teacher rather than ask the question that mattered. They were not going to spend their whole life deferring.

"There," Sefa said suddenly, tapping near the bottom of the current page. "Look. Last entry. Dated—" she frowned, checked the cipher-key, checked it again, "—dated *next month*."

Lior leaned in. The entry was short. Newer ink—not new, but newer, maybe ten years old, written in advance the way the guild wrote all its scheduled payments. But this one wasn't a payment.

This one was a *notice*.

*Notice of default. Upon failure of consideration, the debtor may exercise recovery in kind. Recovery shall be executed against the account holder of record and their assigns.*

"Account holder of record," Lior read aloud, and then their voice failed, because they already knew. Of course they knew. There was only one name it could be, and it was written right there, in the holder line, in the same brown ancient ink as everything else, patient as stone:

*Corvane, Master of the Guild, and his assigns.*

Assigns. Whoever inherited the ledger. Whoever carried the debt forward.

Lior sat back. The counting room seemed very far away and very close at once, the way places do when the ground shifts under them. Outside the window, the sunless grey sky hung over the city like a dropped cloth, and somewhere beyond the walls the refugee fires smoked, and somewhere far north the Spire stood black and hungry and *owed*, and here was the arithmetic of it all, laid out in a dead clerk's careful hand: a tower that had lent its heart to end a war, waiting a century for repayment, now legally entitled to collect from the master of the guild—or from whoever came after him.

From whoever the master chose to carry the debt.

Lior thought of Corvane's moods. His sudden watchfulness. The way he'd said *not yet*—not *never*, not *burn that*, but *not yet*, the words of a man managing a timeline, not a secret.

And underneath the fear, unexpected and unwelcome, a thread of something that felt horribly like understanding. Because Lior knew what it was to owe a debt you hadn't chosen. Knew what it was to have your past spent by someone else's decisions, to discover the bill arriving years later with your name already on it.

Maren had spent Lior's memories like coin. The guild had spent the Spire's heart the same way. And now the Spire intended to collect—from Corvane, or from his assigns, from anyone whose name stood in that column when the term ran out.

Three weeks. Maybe less. The entry was dated next month.

"We have to talk to him," Sefa said.

"I know."

"Today, Lior. Before the inquisitor finishes her interviews and decides for herself what's in this book. You saw how she looked at the archive shelves. She counts things. People like us count things—we should know better than anyone what she'll notice missing."

Lior did see. Had seen, two nights ago, Inquisitor Vael's pale eyes moving along the archive racks with the unhurried precision of a woman reading a ledger written in spines and shelf-gaps. She had smiled at Lior afterward, politely, terribly, and asked one question about the oldest holdings, and Lior had lied, and Lior was fairly sure the lie had gone into whatever ledger Vael kept behind her face.

One debt at a time, though.

Lior closed the old ledger, wrapped it back in its button-catalogue shroud, and stood. Their legs were unsteady—three nights of no sleep and one morning of vertigo at the scale of things—but steady enough. The column didn't care if you were tired. The column only cared that it was read.

"Stay here," they told Sefa. "If I'm not back within the hour—"

"What, hide the book? Run?" Sefa shook her head. "No. Whatever happens, this stays *read*. That's the whole point of you, isn't it? You're the one who reads things." She managed a crooked smile. "Go ask the Master what he owes."

Lior walked the long corridor toward Corvane's study with the ledger's truth sitting in their chest like a swallowed coal. Behind them, in the counting room, the oldest account book in the world waited under its disguise of buttons, its great brown column standing open on the desk—patient, compounding, and, for the first time in a hundred years,

*read*.
