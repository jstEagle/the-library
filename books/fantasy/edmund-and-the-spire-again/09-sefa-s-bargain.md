## 9. Sefa's Bargain

The refugee camp outside the Guildhall had developed its own weather system by the third day: a low grey cloud of woodsmoke, a drizzle of ash from the burnt ledgers, and occasional gusts of rumor that blew through faster than any wind.

Sefa moved through it the way she moved through everything—fast, with a courier's satchel and a courier's habit of counting exits. Two at the north gate. One through the tannery yard, if you didn't mind the smell. One that didn't exist unless you knew to look for the loose brick behind the chandler's stall, which she did, because she'd put it there herself four years ago and had been quietly proud of it ever since.

"Courier!" A woman grabbed her sleeve—a border weaver, by the wool still clinging to her cuffs. "You're guild. You can get things. My daughter needs—she keeps asking for the sky. Just a piece of it. Something blue."

Sefa had learned in three days that this was what people asked for now. Not gold. Not bread, though there was never enough of that either. They asked for pieces of the world that had gone out with the stars.

"A jar of remembered noon," Sefa said. "Third stall past the well. Tell them Sefa sent you, they'll knock a copper off."

"And if they've sold out?"

"Then tell them I said the sun owes me a favor." She smiled the smile that made toll-keepers wave her through and customs men lose their place in their own paperwork. It worked on the weaver too, mostly. The woman let go of her sleeve, but her eyes stayed on Sefa like hands.

Everyone's eyes were like that now. Everyone wanted something carried somewhere, and Sefa was the person whose whole job was carrying things.

She just hadn't expected one of those things to be her brother.

* * *

Tam was sitting where he always sat these days: on the Guildhall steps, in the exact spot where the morning light used to fall before the light stopped being reliable. He'd brought his whittling knife and a block of pine and had produced, so far, a spoon-shaped object that might have been a boat or might have been a very ambitious spoon.

"You're doing it again," Tam said without looking up.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting down next to me like it's an accident. You haven't sat next to me by accident since you were nine."

Sefa sat down next to him anyway. "I'm busy. This is me being efficient. Sitting and visiting simultaneously."

"Mm." He turned the not-spoon over. "The coughing's worse."

And there it was. Three days of careful not-talking about it, undone by a man who whittled spoons and noticed everything.

"It's the smoke," Sefa said. "Half the camp's coughing."

"Half the camp isn't coughing blood into a rag and hiding the rag under their bedroll like I wouldn't find it. I found it, Sefa. I'm slow, not blind."

"You are not slow."

"I'm the brother who took eleven years to notice his sister was running messages for smugglers. Give me my due."

She laughed, because he wanted her to, and then stopped, because the laugh hurt his feelings more than honesty would have. That was the trouble with Tam. He'd followed her out of the borderlands when the wards fell because following her was the only plan he'd ever had, and now he was dying of border-lung in a city that smelled like burnt paper, and every hour she spent running errands for the guild was an hour she wasn't finding him a healer who actually worked.

"The guild has things," she said slowly. "Things people need. Light that remembers daylight. Maps of places that don't exist anymore. And—" she hesitated, because saying it felt like stepping onto ice, "—medicines. Old ones. Things Corvane keeps locked in the cellar vault because they're 'accounting irregularities.'"

"Meaning stolen."

"Meaning *acquired through channels the ledger doesn't describe.*"

"That's stolen with extra words."

"Tam."

"I know." He set down the knife. His hands were steady, which somehow made it worse; hers were the ones shaking. "I know what you're thinking, and I want you to hear me say no first, so you can ignore me properly."

"I wasn't going to ask you."

"You were going to ask me and then do it anyway while I was sleeping. You've got a face for it. You've had that face since the plum incident."

"There was no proof I was involved in the plum incident."

"There were plums in your pockets, Sefa."

She looked out over the camp instead of at him, because looking at him was unbearable in a new way she hadn't budgeted for. The fires. The tarps. The weaver woman buying a jar of noon for her daughter. Somewhere in all of it, an inquisitor in dove-grey robes moving from tent to tent, polite as a hostess, terrible as winter.

Vael had arrived two days ago. Sefa had watched her work. She never raised her voice. She asked questions the way a surgeon asks where it hurts, and by the time you answered you'd already told her three things you didn't mean to. Half the camp called her the Grey Lady and left offerings of bread outside her tent—not worship exactly, more like the way you leave bread out for something that could eat you and might choose not to.

"She's been asking about the guild," Sefa said. "About who has access to the old records."

"Why would you know that?"

"Because couriers know everything. It's the whole job. We're the bloodstream of this city—everything passes through us eventually."

"And what does the Grey Lady want with old records?"

"I don't know yet." Which was a lie, or half of one, because she did know, the way you know a storm is coming before the first drop lands. Vael wanted whoever broke the wards. And everyone in the camp whispered the same name when the wardens weren't close enough to hear: the apprentice. The ledger-keeper. The one Master Corvane burned the current books to protect.

Lior Dravenmoor, who sorted impossible goods with ink-stained fingers and apologized to furniture when they bumped into it. Lior, who had once spent an entire afternoon helping Sefa re-sort a misdelivered crate of singing cheese and had laughed until they cried at the sound of it. Lior, who was the closest thing to a friend Sefa had made since Tam.

Sefa looked at her brother's hands—whittler's hands, farmer's hands, hands that had never done anything to anyone—and felt loyalty bend inside her like a green branch. Not break. Bend. But bending hurts too, and it makes a sound.

* * *

She told herself she was only going to look.

That was the first lie. The second was that she'd decide after. There is no *after* with decisions like this; there is only the moment your feet start moving, and hers started moving toward the dove-grey tent at the edge of the camp before her conscience finished drafting its objection.

Inquisitor Vael received her the way she seemed to receive everyone: seated, unhurried, pouring a second cup of tea before Sefa had finished announcing herself. The tent smelled of cedar and cold iron.

"Courier Sefa," Vael said. "Of the Guild lines. You've carried for the eastern route six years, the northern two. Your delivery record is immaculate." She held out the tea. "Which is why I've been hoping you'd come. Immaculate people always have something they're keeping immaculate."

"That's not how immaculate works," Sefa said, and then wished she hadn't, because Vael smiled like Sefa had just handed her a gift.

"No," Vael agreed. "It isn't. Sit. Drink. I won't poison you—it's bad practice. Poisoned sources stop talking."

Sefa sat. The tea was good, which felt like a trap. Everything about Vael felt like a trap built by someone who genuinely hoped you wouldn't step in it, which was somehow worse than malice.

"I'll come to the point," Vael said, "since you came to mine. I'm hunting whoever broke the wards. The court believes it was sabotage. I believe—" she paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—I believe it was arithmetic. Someone spent something they shouldn't have, and the bill came due. Bills always come due. I want the account book."

"The guild's books burned."

"The *current* books burned. On the same night the wards fell, within hours of my warrant arriving. That is not tidiness, courier. That is a man buying time. And men who buy time always keep the original receipt." Her eyes were pale and patient. "There's an oldest ledger. Pre-founding. Corvane keeps it apart from the others. I want to know who reads it."

The tent was very quiet. Outside, someone's child was practicing a song with only half its notes, and the wrongness of the melody sat in Sefa's chest like a stone.

"If I knew," Sefa said carefully, "why would I tell you?"

"Because you're here." Vael refilled her own cup, unhurried. "People come to me for two reasons. To warn me off, or to trade. Warners stand in the doorway. Traders sit down and drink the tea. You drank the tea."

Sefa looked down at the cup in her hands, traitorously warm, traitorously full.

"My brother's sick," she heard herself say. "Border-lung. The real kind, not smoke. There's a medicine in the guild cellars—amber glass, smells like rain on hot stone. Corvane calls it an accounting irregularity. I call it the only thing in this city that will keep Tam breathing past midwinter. I can't buy it. I can't steal it—I've tried twice and the cellar wards know my face." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway, because stopping would be worse. "So I'm trading. What do you want to know?"

For the first time, something moved behind Vael's pale eyes that wasn't patience. It might have been respect. It might have been pity. With her, Sefa suspected, they were filed under the same heading.

"Who reads the old ledger," Vael said. "Names. Habits. Times."

"Corvane reads it. Always alone, always after midnight, always with the shutters closed." Sefa swallowed. "But there's someone else. An apprentice. Lior Dravenmoor. They were caught with a page torn from it—the Heart page, whatever that means. Corvane caught them reading it and didn't punish them. Didn't report them. Just said *not yet.*" The words came out of her like thread pulled from a hem, each one unraveling something. "They keep the accounts. They have access. If anyone's decoded that book besides Corvane, it's them."

Vael wrote nothing down. Sefa understood, dimly, that this was worse—that whatever went into that perfect memory of hers was going nowhere and could be retrieved forever.

"Lior Dravenmoor," Vael repeated softly. "The runaway apprentice. The one the camp whispers about." She studied Sefa for a long moment. "You care for this person."

"They helped me sort a crate of singing cheese once. It's not important."

"In my experience," Vael said, "it's always the singing cheese that tells you who a person is." She rose, and the audience was clearly over. "Your brother will have his medicine by tomorrow's dusk. Delivered quietly, no debts recorded anywhere but the one between us." At the tent flap she paused. "Courier. When you see the apprentice again—and you will—you may want to consider whether warning them costs you anything you can't afford. Or whether silence does."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means," Vael said, "that I am not the only one hunting them. And the others are less polite than I am." The flap fell shut behind her, and Sefa stood alone in the cedar-and-iron smell, holding an empty teacup, feeling exactly like what she was: a courier who had just delivered the most dangerous parcel of her career and couldn't take it back.

* * *

Tam's medicine arrived at dusk, as promised. Amber glass, smelling of rain on hot stone. No note. No debt recorded anywhere but the one.

He drank it propped against the Guildhall steps and pronounced it "like drinking a thunderstorm that's sorry for you," which was so precisely Tam that Sefa laughed and then had to turn away and pretend to check her satchel straps for a while.

"You traded something," he said. Not a question. He'd known her too long for questions.

"Nothing that matters."

"Sefa."

"A piece of information. About guild business. It's fine. Nobody gets hurt."

He looked at her for a long time, whittling knife idle in his hand, and she watched him decide—deliberately, kindly—to believe her. That was the worst part. That he gave her the belief she hadn't earned, wrapped up like a gift.

Across the camp, the lights that remembered daylight were being lit one by one, little jars of borrowed noon glowing along the tent rows. And beyond them, at the edge of everything, Sefa could see the Guildhall's high window where a lamp still burned past midnight—where a certain apprentice was almost certainly bent over a certain book, ink on their fingers, apologizing to the furniture.

*Tomorrow,* Sefa thought. *Tomorrow I'll find a way to warn them. Tomorrow.*

Tomorrow, she knew, was the oldest lie in any courier's satchel. Parcels didn't wait. Neither did inquisitors.

She picked up her satchel and walked toward the light, telling herself she was only going to look.
