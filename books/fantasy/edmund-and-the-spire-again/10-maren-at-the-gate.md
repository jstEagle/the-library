## 10. Maren at the Gate

The refugee columns had been coming for six days before Lior saw her.

They came up the south road in a slow grey river—carts and handbarrows, goats on ropes, children asleep in grain sacks with the tops folded back like collars. The city gates stayed open past curfew now; the watchmen had stopped checking papers because half the border folk didn't have any, just names they repeated like passwords to themselves. *Brannic. Brannic of the Ash Fords.* The guild had set up a table inside the gatehouse where Sefa's couriers took down what people needed, and the list grew longer every hour: blankets, lamp-oil, news from the east road, light that remembered daylight.

Lior worked the table on the fourth day because Corvane ordered it and because standing still felt worse than anything else. They'd gotten good at this part—the guild part. Ask the name. Write the need. Find the impossible thing that fit inside a possible one. An old shepherd wanted to know if his flock was still alive; Lior sent him to a courier who kept pigeons that flew toward grief. A woman asked for her mother's recipe back, which she'd lost in a fire years ago, and Lior found it in the guild's pantry ledger, copied out by some apprentice a century dead who'd thought it worth keeping. That was the job, really. Deciding what was worth keeping.

On the sixth day, near dusk, a cart came through the gate drawn by a mule so old it seemed to be remembering how walking worked. The driver was a broad woman with sunburnt arms and a boy asleep against her side, and riding in the bed of the cart, wrapped in three shawls despite the mild evening, sat a small figure with white hair and milky eyes turned toward the sky.

Lior knew her before they knew her. That was the strange part. Some part of them stood up from behind the table before their mind caught on, and by the time recognition arrived it arrived all at once, like a dropped plate.

*Maren.*

Their hedge-witch mistress. Their teacher. The woman whose kitchen smelled of rosemary and burnt sugar, whose voice could flay bark off a tree at forty paces, whom Lior had run from in the dark eight years ago without ever quite being able to say why. The last words she'd said to them hung in Lior's chest like a swallowed stone: *you'll remember why.*

She hadn't aged the way people were supposed to age. She'd aged the way candles burn down—all at once, unevenly, as if something had been drinking from her. Her hands, always knotted, were now nearly fists. And her eyes—

Her eyes had been sharp enough to count the whiskers on a mouse across a field. Now they were fogged over, pale as skimmed milk, aimed at nothing.

Lior came around the table too fast and knocked over the inkwell, and didn't stop to right it.

"Maren," they said, and their voice cracked in the middle like an apprentice's, which after eight years felt like an insult added to everything else.

The old woman's head turned toward the sound with terrible precision, the way a compass finds north. For a moment she said nothing. Then: "Well," she said. "The ink's spilled."

That was it. That was the whole greeting. Eight years, a darkened sky, a broken border, and Maren's first words to her runaway apprentice were a complaint about stationery.

"You're blind," Lior said, because they couldn't think of anything else, and immediately wished they'd said literally anything else.

"I'm *economical* with my sight," Maren said. "There's a difference. Sit down, you're looming. You always loomed. Even when you were knee-high you loomed like a tax collector."

"I don't—" Lior started, and then realized they were, in fact, looming, and sat down heavily on the edge of the cart beside her, which creaked in protest. Up close she smelled the same. Rosemary and woodsmoke and something underneath, something mineral and cold, like the air off a deep well. Lior's throat hurt. "You foresaw it. The dark. Didn't you."

"Of course I foresaw it." She said it the way another person might say *of course it rains in autumn*. "I've been packing for six years. Do you know what it's like packing for six years? Every time I put the kettle away I'd have to take it out again. I finally gave the kettle to the Hendersons' girl and told her not to thank me until the stars went out, and she thought I was mad, and now she has my kettle and no stars, which is the sort of arithmetic I've come to expect from the universe."

A laugh escaped Lior before they could stop it—half sob, half snort—and Maren's mouth twitched, satisfied, like she'd banked it somewhere.

But then the twitch faded, and the two of them sat there in the cart while the refugee river flowed around them, and the warmth drained out of the moment as fast as it had come, because there was still the other thing between them. The big thing. The reason Lior had left in the night with one bag and no explanation, and the reason Maren had shouted words after them down the lane that Lior had never been able to fully hear in memory—just the shape of them, furious and frightened.

"You ran," Maren said quietly. Not accusing. Just placing it on the table between them, like a coin.

"You told me to remember why," Lior said. "I never did. I *couldn't*. I've spent eight years—" They stopped. Their hands had made fists without permission. "I built a whole life out of not knowing. And then your letter came, two months ago, saying you'd see me at the gate when the sky went black, and I thought—I thought that was a madwoman's riddle. But here you are."

"Here I am." She reached out and found Lior's arm with surprising accuracy, her fingers closing just above the wrist. Her grip was still strong. It had always been strong; she'd once held a panicked ewe still with one hand while she stitched its leg. "And do you know what I see when I look at you, Lior Dravenmoor? Because I can't see much else these days, so I see a great deal of it."

"What?"

"A debt," she said. "Owing in both directions. And both of us too proud to open the book."

Something cold went through Lior at the word *debt*—the same word that had been following them for weeks through ledgers and ash and receipts written in a dead man's careful hand. "Maren—"

"There's a boy asleep on my feet," she said, "and I haven't eaten since the Ford at Two Willows, and my back has opinions about this cart. Take me somewhere with a chair, apprentice, and we'll do our accounting properly. Or we'll stand here in the mud and trade grievances like fishwives. Your choice. You were always better at choosing than you believed."

* * *

Getting her through the city was an education in humility.

Lior had imagined, in the weeks since the stars went out, a hundred versions of this reunion, and in most of them they said devastating things. In several they forgave her. In one or two she begged *their* forgiveness, which even at the time Lior had recognized as fantasy. What actually happened was that Lior steered a blind old woman through streets full of refugees while she narrated every obstacle a half-second after Lior had already steered around it.

"Curb."

"I know."

"Beggar on the left. He's faking the limp but the hunger's real, give him something."

"I—fine." Lior pressed a copper into the man's palm.

"Now the smell of onions, so a cookshop on the right, and if you're hungry, which you are, you always got thin and silent when you were hungry, we're stopping."

"We're not stopping, we're—"

"Lior." And there it was, the voice, the flaying-bark voice, undimmed by blindness or age or six years of packing kettles. A woman pushing a handcart actually stepped aside reflexively, though the voice hadn't been aimed at her. "I have walked eleven days on a road under a sky with no stars in it, next to a boy who cries in his sleep for a village I could not save, and I will be spoken to with respect or I will sit down in this street and become everyone's problem. Which would you prefer?"

Lior looked at her—at the small, bent, terrifying shape of her, chin lifted toward a voice instead of a face—and felt eight years collapse into about four seconds.

"The Guildhall's this way," they said meekly.

"That's what I thought."

Sefa found them in the courtyard, and Lior watched the two forces meet with the detached awe of someone watching weather systems collide. Sefa, who could charm tolls out of bandits and confessions out of strangers, opened her mouth to deliver one of her easy welcomes—and Maren turned her fogged eyes on her and said, "You're the courier. You bite your nails and you're carrying someone else's secret like a hot coal in your pocket. Sit down, girl, you look exhausted," and Sefa sat down looking vaguely violated, and within ten minutes had told Maren her whole life story up to and including things Lior hadn't known, and had to be physically extracted from the conversation by the promise of supper.

"She's going to be a problem," Sefa whispered in the corridor outside the guest room, delighted.

"She's going to be a *catastrophe*," Lior said, and found they were smiling, and found the smiling hurt.

* * *

Supper was bread, hard cheese, stewed apples, and the good honey Corvane kept locked up for diplomatic occasions, which Lior stole without a flicker of guilt. Maren ate with her fingers and no apology, identified every spice in the stew by scent alone ("cumin, and that's coriander seed burnt, tell your cook shame is free"), and then waved off the offer of more and sat back in her chair with her hands folded on her stick.

The room emptied around them. Sefa went to the evening courier briefing. The fire settled. And then it was just the two of them, and the silence came down like a dropped curtain, and Lior understood that the accounting had begun whether they were ready or not.

"Why did you write to me?" they asked. "Two months ago. Before the wards fell. How did you even know where—"

"I've always known where you were. You leave a trail like a wounded duck." She said it without heat. "And I wrote because I dreamed it. The sky going out. I'd been dreaming it for years, but that winter it changed—it stopped being a dream about the sky and became a dream about *you*, standing under it, holding a book. So I wrote. I'm told you didn't answer."

"I burned it."

"Yes, I assumed. You always burned things you couldn't face. You burned my letters, you burned your own essays when the margins had mistakes in them. Very tidy habit. Terrible for historians." She tilted her head. "Why did you run, Lior? Truly. Not the story you've told yourself. The true one."

And there it was. The question. Eight years old and still sharp.

"I don't know," Lior said. It came out flat and honest and awful. "That's the truth, Maren. I woke up one morning and I couldn't be in that house one more hour. Something in me was screaming and I couldn't hear what it was saying. I packed in the dark and I left before dawn and I have spent eight years waiting to remember why, because you *told* me I would, and I never have. Not once. Not in a dream, not drunk, not sick with fever. There's just a hole where the reason should be, and around the hole, everything else about that house—your kitchen, the smell of the tallow, the way you hummed when you ground herbs—it's all still there. Everything except the one thing I needed."

Maren was very still.

It was a stillness Lior remembered. It was the stillness she went into when a spell was about to go wrong, or when a patient was dying, or when someone asked her a question she'd hoped no one would ever ask. Lior had seen it maybe five times in their childhood, and every time, something bad had followed.

"Maren," they said slowly. "What aren't you telling me?"

"Don't use the voice on me, I invented the voice."

"*Maren.*"

She sighed then, long and low, and turned her blind eyes toward the fire, and for a moment she looked not old but ancient—not a woman grown old but something that had been old for a very long time and was tired of pretending otherwise.

"When you came to me," she said, "you were nine years old, and you had already lost something. Do you remember arriving?"

"Of course. My parents sent me. After the fever."

"After the fever," Maren repeated softly. "Yes. That's what you remember. That's what I let you remember." Her hands tightened on the stick. "Child, I am going to tell you something tomorrow. Properly, in daylight, with tea, because it deserves tea and because tonight I am too tired to catch you when you fall. But I will say this much now, because you've earned this much: the hole in your memory is not a wound. Wounds heal crooked but they heal. A hole is different. A hole is *paid for*."

"Paid for," Lior echoed. The word landed strangely, familiarly, like a key trying locks. "Paid for how? Paid to whom?"

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest smile Lior had ever seen on her face, and she had once smiled at a funeral.

"To whom indeed," she said. "You keep accounts now, don't you? The guild's little keeper of impossible sums. Then here is your first entry for tomorrow's lesson, and mind you copy it exactly: *everything costs, and magic costs most of all, and the price is always paid in the same coin.*" She pushed herself up on her stick, joints cracking like green kindling. "Now help me to bed, apprentice. Tomorrow we open the book."

Lior helped her to bed. At the door, with the candle guttering, Maren spoke once more, so quietly Lior almost missed it.

"You'll remember why," she said. "I promise you, child. By this time tomorrow, you'll wish you never had."

Outside, far to the north, where the Spire stood black against a starless horizon, something vast and hungry turned its attention—briefly, gently, the way a man turns his head toward a familiar voice—toward a small room in a guildhall, where a candle was going out.
