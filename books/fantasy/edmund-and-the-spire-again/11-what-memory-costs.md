## 11. What Memory Costs

Maren Thackeray had spent sixty years learning the shape of other people's forgetting, and she could tell you this much for free: nobody ever misses what they never knew they had.

She sat in the corner of the refugee kitchen behind the Temple of Small Mercies, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's soup, and watched Lior across the room without appearing to watch at all. This was an old skill. Hedge-witches survived by being furniture—by being the woman in the corner stirring something, unremarkable, until the moment came when you needed her and there she was, ladle in hand, as if she'd been waiting all along.

Which, of course, she had been. Waiting was most of the craft.

Lior was arguing with a temple sister about ration tallies, and losing, and enjoying it. That was the thing about Lior that Maren had never managed to put down: they argued like arithmetic was a form of affection. Every correction was a small gift. *No, sister, if you count the barley twice you'll short the third row, and then you'll have hungry people who trust you less than they did yesterday.* The temple sister's ears went pink. Lior softened it with a joke about how even ledgers deserved mercy, and the sister laughed despite herself, and Maren felt the old ache turn over in her chest like a stone in a riverbed.

*You'll remember why,* she had told them, eleven years ago, standing in her doorway while Lior walked out of her life with a pack too big for their shoulders and a face full of a fury they couldn't name.

She had known, even then, exactly why they couldn't name it. She had made sure of it.

That was the part she'd never said out loud to anyone, not to her sister before she died, not to the coven elders, not to the quiet gods of hearth and hedge. She had done it on purpose. And now the stars were out, and the wards were down, and every debt in the world seemed suddenly to be coming due at once—including hers.

* * *

The memory-magic worked like this, and Maren could explain it in one breath if she steeled herself:

Nothing is free. Not light, not luck, not a door that opens when it should be locked. Magic is a trade, always, and the coin it prefers—the only coin it truly respects—is the past. A spell needs somewhere to come *from*. You can pay with your own strength, but strength refills by morning. You can pay with years off your life, but that's just spending yourself slowly. Memory is different. Memory is the only currency that, once spent, stays spent—and the magic knows it. That's why it works so well. The universe trusts a payment that hurts.

A hedge-witch casting a finding-spell might give up the smell of her mother's bread. A weather-worker buying rain for a dry field might lose the name of the first person she ever kissed. The magic takes what it takes; you don't get to choose, not exactly—you choose what you're willing to risk, and the magic chooses from inside that, the way a pickpocket chooses your fullest pocket.

Maren had learned this at her own teacher's knee, which is to say she had learned it the way everyone learns terrible things: gradually, and then all at once. Her teacher, old Sable-Handed Ivet, had shown her the ledger of her own head. *Look,* Ivet had said, tapping Maren's temple like a barrel being checked for leaks. *You've paid me three spells' worth already this season. You no longer remember your seventh summer. You no longer remember the color of your father's coat. You agreed to each one, child. I keep honest accounts.*

Honest accounts. Maren had built her whole life on those two words, and it had taken her fifty years to understand that honesty in the keeping is not the same as honesty in the taking.

Because here was the theft in it, the part that curdled: when you taught someone memory-magic—as she had taught Lior, patiently, lovingly, over six years of kitchens and candlelight—the student paid for their lessons out of their own past. That was the tradition. That was the *economy* of it. The teacher chose the price; the student paid it; and because the payment erased itself in the paying, the student never once felt robbed.

They felt lighter. They felt lucky. They felt, if anything, grateful.

And when enough of your past has gone to pay for your own education, you wake up some mornings with a grief in you the size of a house and no furniture in it anywhere. You feel that something was taken. You cannot say what. You look at the person who took it and you feel—wrongly, or perhaps not wrongly at all—that you must run.

Lior had run. Eleven years ago, at nineteen, furious at nothing they could name, packing in the middle of the night, and Maren had stood in her doorway and given them the only true prophecy of her career: *You'll remember why.*

She hadn't meant it as a curse. She had meant it as a promise—that someday, somehow, the account would be read aloud, and Lior would know what their childhood had cost, and would hate her cleanly instead of foggy, which she had honestly believed would be a mercy.

It had not occurred to her, back then, that the stars themselves might go out first.

* * *

"You're staring," said a voice beside her, and Maren started badly, sloshing tea down her blanket. It was the courier girl—Sefa, the guild badge still pinned crooked to her collar, holding two bowls of stew like a woman who had learned to eat whenever food appeared because it might not appear again.

"I'm resting my eyes," Maren said with dignity.

"Your eyes are pointed directly at Lior."

"My eyes are old. They wander."

Sefa sat down uninvited, which Maren approved of thoroughly. People who waited to be invited starved. "You know them," Sefa said. It wasn't quite a question. "From before. I've seen how you look at them. Like—" she paused, hunting for it, "—like a woman watching someone else spend money she lent."

Maren laughed, a real one, surprised out of her. "That's the most accurate description of hedge-craft anyone's ever given me, and I once heard a bishop try."

"So you *did* lend them something."

Maren looked into her tea. Around them the kitchen roared—refugees, temple sisters, a guild clerk trying to organize blankets by a system only he understood—but between her and the courier girl there was a small pocket of quiet, the kind Maren had spent a lifetime making for hard conversations.

"Girl," she said, "do you know what memory-magic costs?"

"Everyone knows it costs memories."

"Everyone knows the *word*. Nobody knows the *arithmetic*." Maren set down the bowl. "So let me do sums for you, since apparently I raised a bookkeeper. Six years I taught that child. Finding-spells, warding-songs, the little kitchen charms, the big ones too—I taught them everything I had. And every lesson had a price, and the price came out of *their* pockets, not mine. Their seventh birthday party. The name of the dog they loved. The sound of their mother singing. The reason they were angry with me." She stopped. Her hands had gone tight around the bowl, and she made them let go. "The last one especially. I took that one myself, deliberately, the night before they left. It was the largest payment we'd ever negotiated, and I told them it was for a warding strong enough to protect a whole household, and it was, and it worked beautifully, and it cost them the entire argument we'd had that morning. So when they woke up furious with me and couldn't say why—" she spread her hands, "—they ran. Of course they ran. I'd have run too."

Sefa had gone very still. "You *made* them leave?"

"No. I made them able to leave. There's a difference, though I grant you it's a thin one, and I've had eleven years to walk back and forth across it." Maren's voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I thought I was protecting them. There was a thing coming—I saw it, half-blind as I am, I saw the dark years before anyone believed me—and I thought, if they're angry enough to go, they'll go far enough. To the city. Behind walls. Away from the border where the dark lands first." She shook her head. "You tell yourself stories. That's the second theft, the one you do to yourself."

"And now they're here anyway," Sefa said quietly, "and the dark came anyway."

"Now they're here anyway," Maren agreed, "and the dark came anyway, and the only thing I got right was the direction." She looked across the kitchen at Lior, who had won the argument with the temple sister and was now showing her, gently, how to fix the tally. "They don't know. What they paid. They think they had a bad childhood and a worse temper and no reason for either. I have watched them flinch at gaps in their own story for eleven years from a hundred miles away, through letters that got shorter and shorter, and I never once told them, because telling them would take the last thing they had left."

"Which is?"

"The belief that they left for a good reason." Maren smiled, and it hurt. "People can survive almost any loss if they think they chose it."

* * *

Later—much later, when the kitchen had emptied and the candles had burned down to stubs and the temple sisters had given up trying to make her sleep indoors—Lior found her on the step outside, wrapped in her blanket, counting stars that weren't there.

Counting absences, rather. She'd gotten good at that.

"You should be sleeping," Lior said, sitting down beside her. Up close they smelled of ink and road dust and, underneath it, faintly, of rosemary—which had been her soap, once, in her kitchen, and she wondered if Lior knew that or if the knowing had been spent decades ago along with everything else.

"Witches don't sleep during catastrophes," Maren said. "Bad for the reputation."

"You don't have a reputation anymore. You have a blanket."

"I have a blanket *and* a reputation. The blanket is load-bearing."

Lior huffed a laugh, and for a moment they sat in the old rhythm of it, the kitchen rhythm, teacher and student trading small stupidities across a silence that used to be comfortable and was now merely careful. Then Lior said, in the voice they used for difficult ledger entries, "Sefa told me. Some of it. About the lessons. About what they cost."

Ah. So the courier girl had decided to be braver than Maren. Good. Someone had to be.

"Yes," Maren said.

"Why didn't you tell me?" No anger in it. That was the worst part. Just the flat, patient tone of someone reconciling accounts. "Eleven years, Maren. Letters. Visits, even—one visit. You let me think I was just—broken. That I'd lost things the ordinary way. That maybe I was careless with my own life."

"Would it have helped, knowing?"

"I don't know! That's not—" Lior pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "That's not the point. The point is it was *mine*. My dog's name was mine. My mother's voice was mine. Whatever we fought about that last morning—it was *mine*, and you sold it, and you didn't even—" Their voice cracked and reassembled itself. "You didn't even ask me to remember that I'd agreed."

"I asked. Every time. You said yes every time." Maren kept her voice low and level, the way she did for birthing mothers and dying men. "That's the trap of it, Lior. That's the whole rotten architecture. Consent given by someone who can't remember consenting is not consent. I knew that. I told myself the tradition was older than the objection. I told myself I was teaching you survival, and survival is worth any price, and every witch before me had said the same words in the same kitchens going back a thousand years, and every single one of us was lying." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I didn't tell you afterward because the telling would have been another taking. Your ignorance was the only piece of your past you had left that I hadn't touched. I guarded it. Badly. Selfishly. But I guarded it."

Lior was quiet a long time. Somewhere across the yard a baby cried and was hushed. Above them, where the stars should have been, the sky lay flat and black as an unwritten page.

"The village," Lior said finally. "The one near the border. The blank people. Sefa says the Spire did that. Eats memory to live."

"So I'm told."

"And you've known about memory-eating your whole career. You've *practiced* it. Small scale. Kitchen scale." Lior turned to look at her, and their eyes in the dark were wet and terribly clear. "You're the only person alive who understands both ends of it. The eater and the eaten. When we get to the Spire—when I get to the Spire—I'm going to need you to teach me the rest. Even though teaching costs. Even though I now know exactly what I'm paying with."

Maren's chest did something complicated and painful. "You'd let me? After tonight?"

"I'd *insist*." And then Lior did the thing that undid her completely: they reached over and took her hand, the way they had at nine years old, the way they had at fifteen, grip firm around her knuckles. "But on new terms. Written terms. We draft a contract before the first lesson. Price named in advance. Payment witnessed. And Maren—" their voice wobbled and held, "—you don't get to decide what I can afford. Not anymore. That clause goes first."

Maren laughed, and it turned into something else halfway through, and she let it, because she was old and tired and the sky was empty and her student was offering her the one thing she'd never had in sixty years of honest accounts: a chance to practice her craft *without stealing*.

"Agreed," she said. "Clause one. Witnessed by the courier girl, who strikes me as the type to hold a grudge on principle."

"She does it beautifully," Lior said.

They sat together under the starless sky, hand in hand, two thieves reconciling their books, and neither of them saw—because it began too far away, beyond the city walls, in the direction of the Guildhall—the first thin line of lantern-light moving through the night with the unhurried certainty of a hunt that has already found its quarry.

Inquisitor Vael had entered the city at dusk. By morning, everyone at the Temple of Small Mercies would know her name.

By morning, Maren would learn that the court's polite, terrible machinery had already asked its first question: *who kept the oldest ledger, and who has been reading it?*

She squeezed Lior's hand once, and said nothing, and began—quietly, in the dark, the way she did everything—to plan how to stand between her student and the next bill collector.
