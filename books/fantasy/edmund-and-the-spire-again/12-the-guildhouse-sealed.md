## 12. The Guildhouse Sealed

Inquisitor Vael had seized many buildings in her career—a counting-house in Vell, a temple whose god had stopped answering letters, a lighthouse that turned out to be a very committed fraud—and she had developed a theory about them. Every institution, no matter how grand its front doors, revealed its true nature at the moment of seizure. You learned more from what people grabbed as you came through than from a year of depositions.

The Guildhall of Impossible Things, she noted as her wardens breached it at dawn, revealed itself to be a place that kept its tea things by the door.

That was the first thing anyone touched. A junior clerk—seventeen at most, ears still too big for his head—had been sleeping in the entry alcove on a pallet that smelled of chamomile and ink, and when twelve armed wardens poured in wearing grey court livery, his hand shot out and knocked over the kettle stand rather than reach for any weapon. He looked up at them with an expression of pure, betrayed disappointment, as though they had interrupted something sacred.

"The kettle," he said mournfully. "It was *just* coming on."

"Nobody touch the kettle," Vael said gravely, because it never hurt to let people think their small catastrophes were being taken seriously. It made everything after easier. "Master Corvane's study. Now."

She had prepared for this for six days. Six days of polite interviews across the city, of ledgers requested and examined and returned with compliments, of watching faces do small dishonest things. She knew Corvane would not run. Masters who burn their bridges are common; masters who burn their books are rarer, and she had wagered—correctly, she suspected—that he would wait for her.

The guildhouse woke around her wardens as they climbed the stairs. Doors opened. Apprentices peered out in nightshirts. Someone, somewhere above, was already playing a fiddle, badly, which struck Vael as either extraordinary courage or a complete failure to grasp the situation, and both possibilities charmed her against her will.

Corvane's study door stood open.

He sat behind his desk like a man behind a desk, which was somehow the most defiant thing he could have done. He was old in the way of well-kept furniture—nothing loose, nothing wasted, every line earned. His hands were folded over a ledger, and beside the ledger, arranged with terrible neatness, stood a decanter of plum brandy and two glasses.

"Master Inquisitor," he said. "You're early. I'd budgeted another hour."

"For what?"

"Finishing the accounts." He said it without a flicker of irony, and Vael felt the first hairline crack appear in her certainty, because he meant it. Whatever else happened this morning, this man had genuinely intended to finish his accounts.

"You know why I'm here."

"Because the wards fell, the stars went out, and the court needs someone to blame before the refugees start asking why a century of taxes bought them darkness." He gestured at the chair opposite. "Sit. Or don't. But I'll say my piece either way, and I've found standing audiences remember less."

Vael did not sit. "You're under arrest pending inquiry. The charges—"

"Are whatever you need them to be. I read your warrant." He slid a paper across the desk; she took it, and found it was indeed her own warrant, returned, with three errors corrected in red ink. One of the corrections was a date. Another was a legal term she had used loosely, and he had used precisely. "You should be more careful, Master Inquisitor. Sloppy warrants get thrown back by clever advocates, and then where is everyone?"

"Where," Vael said, "is the oldest ledger?"

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing, at last, a question he'd been dreading for so long that its arrival came almost as relief.

"Ah," he said softly. "So you've talked to the Thackeray child."

"I've talked to everyone."

"No, you haven't. Everyone would have told you the ledger was missing a page, but only someone who'd talked to *Lior* would ask about the whole book." He unfolded his hands. "And there it is. The real question."

* * *

Outside the study window, the city was doing what cities do at dawn after catastrophe: pretending. Smoke rose from bakeries. A water-cart creaked past. Somewhere a woman was shouting at a mule with the specific fury of someone who has larger fears and needs somewhere to put them. Beyond the rooftops, northward, the sky hung blank and starless even in daylight hours now—the dark had become a kind of bruise that didn't fade.

Vael had seen the refugee columns from the wall yesterday. She had counted wagons the way other people said prayers. And beneath her professional calm, in a place she did not visit during working hours, something had begun to whisper that the court's questions might be aimed at the wrong end of the problem.

She put that away. It was not yet eleven o'clock, and she had a guildhall to seal.

"Search the study," she ordered. "Then the archive. Then the vaults. Nothing burns, nothing leaves."

Her wardens moved. Corvane watched them with the mild interest of a man observing weather. They pulled books from shelves and found—Vael saw it happen, saw the exact moment—one warden go still over a slim volume bound in grey leather, and look up at her, and shake his head almost imperceptibly.

Not it. Just a decoy. There were decoys everywhere in this room, she realized. False spines, hollow backs, a shelf that swung. This man had spent a lifetime expecting visitors exactly like her.

"Master Corvane," she said. "I'm going to offer you something I rarely offer."

"A fair trial?"

"A chance to cooperate. Tell me where the ledger is, tell me what the guild did, and I will see you treated gently. Refuse, and I take this building apart brick by brick and arrest every soul in it, starting with the boy who loves his kettle."

For the first time, Corvane looked tired. Not defeated—tired, the way a long road looks tired.

"There it is," he murmured. "The polite knife. You do it well. Better than Hask did, gods rest him." He rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves. "You want to know what we did. Very well. Sit down, Master Inquisitor. This takes longer than a warrant."

"I don't have time for—"

"You have less time than you think, which is why you'll listen." He lifted the decanter, poured two measures, and pushed one toward her. When she didn't move, he drank his own, unhurried. "A hundred years ago, the border wards were failing. Not weakening—*failing*, the way a heart fails. And there was a war on, the real one, the one your history books call the Grey War and get wrong in four separate ways. The Spire anchored those wards. The Spire was starving. And a group of people—younger and more frightened than they ever admitted afterward—decided that the only mercy available was forgetting."

"Forgetting what?"

"Everything. That's the part nobody wants to hear. Not the war alone. Everything. Memory was the currency the world ran on before coin, and the Spire was its mint, and when it went hungry it began eating, and what it ate first was the reasons people hated each other." He set down the glass. "We stole its heart to stop the feeding. We bought a hundred years. And we spent the century telling ourselves it was theft when it was surgery, or surgery when it was theft—I've gone back and forth so often I've worn a groove." He smiled, and it was a dreadful, gentle thing. "Now the anesthesia is wearing off, Master Inquisitor. And the patient is waking up hungry."

The study was very quiet. Downstairs, faintly, the fiddle had stopped.

"That's madness," Vael said, and heard how thin it sounded even to herself.

"It's arithmetic. I have the figures somewhere." His eyes glittered. "Or I did."

"You burned them."

"I burned the *current* ledgers, yes." He spread his hands, almost apologetic. "Names of clients, trades of the last fifty years, the location of every smuggler's cache from here to the coast. All of it ash as of midnight. Your wardens will find the cold stove still warm. Let the court chew on that—it'll keep them busy for a season and buy some innocent people a season's peace."

"And the oldest book?"

"Ah." He sat back down. "The oldest book is not mine to burn."

* * *

They found the apprentices assembled in the great hall below, because Corvane rang the assembly bell himself—rang it, Vael noted, with the authority of a man who had not yet been formally dispossessed, and no one stopped him, which told her everything about how this household understood its own loyalties.

Perhaps sixty of them. Children mostly, and near-children, and a handful of adults grown strange and soft around the edges from a lifetime of cataloguing impossible goods. They stood in rows that weren't quite rows, the way guild folk always stood, and Vael walked the length of them reading faces the way she read ledgers: for entries that didn't balance.

There. Third row. A young person with ink-stained fingers and a jaw set like a drawn bowstring—Lior Thackeray, the runaway apprentice, the one whose name had surfaced in three separate interviews like a fish rising. Beside them, a courier girl with wind-burned cheeks and a bruise on her wrist shaped like a gripping hand, positioned just slightly in front of Lior, the way a body positions itself when it has already decided what it will do next. Vael filed that away too.

"People," Corvane said, and his voice carried the way voices only carry when they have spent decades being obeyed. "The court has sealed the Guildhall. As of this hour, the Guild of Impossible Things is suspended. Its accounts are frozen. Its members are detained for questioning." A ripple went through the rows. He waited it out with the patience of a man who had outlasted worse storms. "Some of you will be frightened. Good. Fear means you've understood. Some of you will be angry. Better. But all of you—" and here his voice dropped into something almost tender, "—all of you will remember that impossible things do not stop being needed because a government says so. The dark outside does not care about our warrant problems."

He stepped down from the dais and walked the rows, and Vael followed him, close enough to intervene, far enough to watch.

He paused at each apprentice. A word here, a hand on a shoulder there. To the kettle boy he said something that made the child stand straighter. To the courier girl he said something low that made her flush and glance sideways at Lior. And when he reached Lior Thackeray—

Vael was watching. She would swear to her grave she was watching, and she saw nothing.

His hands went to the boy's shoulders. He leaned close as if to speak privately, the gesture of a master comforting an apprentice, and his sleeve draped forward, and when he straightened again his hands were empty and his gait unchanged and the boy's expression showed only stricken confusion.

But Vael had spent twenty years catching smugglers who palmed gems under the noses of customs officers, and she knew the shape of a transfer. Weight leaves one pair of hands and arrives in another. Something had crossed between them inside that embrace, wrapped in cloth and misdirection and a hundred years of practice.

She said nothing. Not yet. Saying something would make the boy drop it, or run, or swallow it, and none of those outcomes served her. Better to let the debt sit where it sat, marked in her mental column, accruing interest.

"Carry the debt," Corvane said aloud, to Lior, loud enough for the hall to hear and mistake it for benediction. "That's all a keeper is, in the end. Someone who carries what others cannot."

Then he turned to Vael and offered his wrists, palms up, in the old gesture of surrender.

"I believe," he said pleasantly, "you wanted me in irons."

* * *

By midday the Guildhall wore grey. Wardens at every door. Seals on the archive, the vaults, the tea cupboard—which Vael authorized unsealed, purely on humanitarian grounds, and was rewarded by the kettle boy bursting into tears of gratitude, which she chose to find funny rather than alarming.

She interviewed apprentices in the great hall, one by one, in a chair she had placed facing the corner so they wouldn't feel observed by the room. Most of them knew little and volunteered less. The courier girl, Sefa, lied to her twice about her whereabouts three nights ago and was transparent both times, and Vael let her leave believing she hadn't been caught, because a liar who thinks she's skilled will lie again, and the second lie is always more useful than the first.

At dusk she returned to the study, dismissed her wardens, and stood alone among the stripped shelves.

The transfer bothered her. Not the fact of it—she had known within the heartbeat—but the *weight*. She replayed it, the way she replayed everything, and measured. The bundle had been the size of a large book. A thick one. Bound in something that had made no sound against cloth.

She went to the cold stove. Ashes, as promised, still warm at the core. She sifted them with a stylus and found leather fragments, paper fragments, the brass corner of a binding melted into a droplet. Fifty years of guild commerce, destroyed in an evening.

And then, at the bottom, beneath everything, a scrap she nearly missed: a strip of stiff grey vellum, scorched at one edge, bearing a fragment of script in an antique hand. Not guild shorthand. Older. The numerals were wrong—arranged in columns that descended instead of marched—and the few words she could parse included one that raised every hair on her professional arms:

*...debtor: the Spire itself...*

She stood in the emptying study for a long time, holding the scrap, listening to the guildhouse settle around her like a large animal going to sleep in captivity.

Corvane had burned fifty years of records to hide names and caches. Fine. Expected. But the oldest book—the book that mattered, the book containing a debtor's line naming a living tower—he had not burned. He had given it away. To a runaway apprentice with ink-stained hands and a jaw like a bowstring, while an inquisitor stood three feet away.

Which meant Corvane did not consider her the danger. Or did not consider her the audience. Either way, he had chosen his inheritor with the same care he'd used to correct her warrant, and he had done it under her nose, and some cold, precise part of Vael admired it even as the rest of her began composing the report she would *not* send tonight.

She crossed to the window. Below, in the yard, a supply cart was being loaded with confiscated goods under warden supervision—standard procedure, itemized, dull. Two figures were carrying crates. One moved like a courier, quick and economical. The other moved like someone trying very hard to walk naturally with a heavy object under their coat, and had chosen a crate heavy enough to justify the posture, which was clever, and also chose to glance up at the study window, which was not.

Lior Thackeray met her eyes across forty feet of evening air.

Held them for one full second. Then looked away, and hefted the crate, and walked it toward the cart with a performance of boredom so elaborate it constituted a confession.

Vael smiled. It was not, altogether, a stern smile.

"Well," she said to the empty study, to the cold stove, to the scrap of vellum in her pocket, to the debtor whose tower stood waiting in the north. "Let's see where you carry it."

She turned for the door, and was halfway down the stairs before she registered the sound rising from the yard below—the cart wheels, already moving, faster than regulation, and a courier girl's voice ringing off the stones with unmistakable delight:

"*Run!*"

Vael took the rest of the stairs two at a time.
